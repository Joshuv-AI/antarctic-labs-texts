Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) as a predictor of left atrial remodeling in patients with sickle cell anemia
Authors: Fábio Galvão, Felipe Soares Maia, Mabel Rodrigues Rios, Jersey Heitor da Silva Maues, Bruno Deltreggia Benites, Samuel de Souza Medina, Paula de Melo Campos, José Roberto Matos-Souza, Fernando Ferreira Costa, Wilson Nadruz, Sara Teresinha Olalla Saad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In sickle cell anemia (SCA), multiple end-organs are affected by the chronic effects of hemolytic anemia and vaso-occlusive events, with cardiac complications being among the leading causes of death. Cardiac remodeling, including left atrial enlargement, may lead to diastolic dysfunction and increase the risk of atrial arrhythmias. Although hemolysis is an important mechanism in the systemic complications of SCA, the value of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) as an isolated marker of atrial remodeling remains poorly explored. In this context, we evaluated whether this simple marker could predict left atrial enlargement in patients with SCA in a steady state. In a retrospective cross-sectional study, we reviewed medical records, including echocardiographic examinations, of patients with SCA treated at a referral center. LDH elevation, expressed as multiples of the upper limit of normal (LDH/ULN), was used for comparison. Eighty-one patients were included, with a mean age of 41.7 ± 11.4 years, and 60% were female. The median LDH/ULN was 1.39 (range: 0.45–5.4). Forty-four patients (54%) presented an elevated left atrial volume index (LAVi), and 52 patients (64%) had LDH/ULN > 1.0. Receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis identified 1.2 as the optimal LDH/ULN cut-off to detect elevated LAVi, and adjusted logistic regression showed that individuals with LDH/ULN > 1.2 had a 3.70‑fold higher risk of elevated LAVi (confidence interval = 1.33–10.31; p = 0.012). Our study identified an association between LDH and increased LAVi, suggesting a potential role as a simple marker of atrial remodeling, and not solely as a marker of hemolysis.

Dear editor-in-chief,

Although the life expectancy of patients with sickle cell anemia (SCA) remains approximately two decades shorter than that of the general population, advances in care have contributed to a reduction in morbidity and mortality among these patients in recent decades [1, 2]. With the increased longevity of patients with SCA, several end-organs have become affected due to the chronic effects of hemolytic anemia and vaso-occlusive events [3]. Among the affected end organs, the heart stands out, as cardiac complications are among the leading causes of death and are estimated to account for up to one quarter of deaths related to SCA [4]. Cardiac remodeling, including involvement of the left atrium, may lead to diastolic dysfunction and increase the risk of atrial arrhythmias, notably atrial fibrillation. Left atrial enlargement has been frequently identified through echocardiographic evaluation and was reported in approximately 61% of this population in a recent study [5].

Hemolytic processes are major contributors to chronic anemia and, at higher degrees of hemolysis, are responsible for multiple severe end‑organ complications in SCA [6]. Hemoglobin (Hb), a biomarker of hemolysis, has been evaluated in a recent study, together with high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), as a potential predictor of left atrial enlargement in this population [5]. However, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), considered a strong marker of red blood cell (RBC) hemolysis [7], has been scarcely studied as an isolated biomarker in relation to its association with left atrial remodeling. Therefore, our study aimed to evaluate whether LDH assessment, a simple and low-cost test, could serve as a predictor of left atrial enlargement in patients with SCA in a steady state.

Thus, in a retrospective cross-sectional study, we reviewed medical records, including echocardiogram examinations, of patients who were treated at the Hemoglobinopathies Outpatient Clinic of the Hematology and Transfusion Medicine Center (Hemocentro) at the University of Campinas (UNICAMP), located in Campinas, São Paulo, Brazil, between February 2016 and November 2023. The study was approved by the UNICAMP Ethics Committee. Eligible patients were over 18 years of age, diagnosed with the homozygous form of sickle cell disease (HbSS), and had clinical and laboratory data collected during a steady-state condition. Patients with insufficient information regarding clinical, laboratory or echocardiographic variables were excluded. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants whenever possible. In cases where participants had died prior to data collection, a Data Use Commitment Agreement (TCUD) was applied in accordance with the regulations approved by the UNICAMP ethics committee.

LDH levels were assessed using two different biochemical analyzers. Until 2016, the Roche Modular P800 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics Corp., Indianapolis, IN, USA) was employed, and from 2017 to 2023, the Beckman Coulter AU5800 analyzer (Beckman Coulter Inc., Brea, CA, USA) was used, resulting in variations in the upper limit of normal reference values. To ensure consistent comparisons among patients, each LDH result was divided by the maximum normal reference value of the respective analyzer, which used different LDH reagents, thereby generating LDH elevation expressed as multiples of the upper limit of normal (LDH/ULN). Consequently, the upper limit of normal for LDH/ULN was standardized to 1.0.

Laboratory variables were analyzed using the Beckman Coulter AU5800 biochemical analyzer, except for complete blood counts with reticulocytes, which were performed using the Siemens Advia 2120 hematology analyzer (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). Echocardiography evaluation was conducted as previously reported [8, 9], and was used to assess left ventricular (LV) mass index (LVMi), left atrial volume index (LAVi), LV ejection fraction – a marker of systolic function – and tissue-Doppler derived e’ – a marker of LV diastolic function. Elevated LAVi was considered when LAVi > 34 mL/m² [9].

Data are presented and mean±standard deviation, median (range: minimum–maximum) and proportion as appropriate. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Multivariable linear and logistic analysis were used to evaluate the association between echocardiography variables and LDH/ULN. P-values < 0.05 were considered significant. Analyses were performed using Stata 14.2.

Initially, 107 patients diagnosed with SCA were selected. However, 26 were excluded due to insufficient information in their medical records to meet the study criteria. Therefore, 81 patients were included in the study. The clinical, laboratory, and echocardiographic characteristics of the study population [mean age of 41.7 ± 11.4 years, of whom 60% were female] are presented in Table 1. The mean Hb level was 8.8 ± 1.3 g/dL, while the median value of LDH/ULN was 1.39 (range: 0.45–5.4). Forty-four patients (54%) presented elevated LAVi, and 52 patients (64%) had an LDH/ULN > 1.0.

LDH/ULN showed a negative Spearman correlation with Hb (r = − 0.420; p < 0.001; n = 81) and positive correlations with reticulocyte count (r = 0.249; p = 0.025; n = 81) and indirect bilirubin (r = 0.290; p = 0.019; n = 65), supporting a pattern compatible with hemolytic activity.

Linear and logistic regression analyses adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, systemic arterial hypertension, and diabetes showed that LDH/ULN was associated with LAVi (Fig. 1A) and elevated LAVi (Fig. 1B) (both p < 0.05), but not with LVMi (p = 0.11), LV ejection fraction (p = 0.76), and e’ (p = 0.13). However, this association did not remain independent after the inclusion of Hb levels in the multivariable model. Receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis revealed that the optimal cut-off value for LDH/ULN to detect elevated LAVi was 1.2, with a sensitivity of 0.73 and a specificity of 0.62. Further adjusted logistic regression analysis showed that individuals with LDH/ULN > 1.2 had 3.70-fold greater risk of having elevated LAVi (confidence interval = 1.33–10.31; p = 0.012).

These results show that hemolysis, assessed by LDH, may influence left atrial remodeling in patients with SCA. Although cardiac complications may involve both hemolysis and anemia [6], our findings indicate that LDH remained associated with LAVi before adjustment for Hb levels, suggesting a direct role of hemolysis in left atrial remodeling. This effect may be explained by multiple pathophysiological mechanisms directly triggered by hemolysis that contribute to chronic cardiac and vascular dysfunction, thereby favoring atrial remodeling. Intravascular hemolysis releases free Hb, which scavenges nitric oxide (NO), inducing oxidative stress and cardiovascular damage [10, 11]. In addition, heme released during erythrocyte breakdown promotes cardiotoxicity through the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to altered calcium homeostasis and cardiac myocyte dysfunction [12].

LDH elevation, expressed as LDH/ULN, was associated with increased LAVi in models adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, systemic arterial hypertension, and diabetes. This finding reinforces the usefulness of LDH as a clinical marker related to left atrial remodeling in SCA. After additional adjustment for Hb levels, this association was attenuated, which should be interpreted in the context of the pathophysiological relationship between hemolysis, chronic anemia, and the hyperdynamic state in this population. The negative correlation between Hb and LDH, together with the positive correlations with reticulocyte count and indirect bilirubin, supports the interpretation of LDH elevation as a marker of hemolytic burden in this cohort. Thus, LDH elevation may help identify patients with a higher likelihood of increased LAVi.

Our findings are also consistent with those of Njoku et al. [13], who reported that hemolysis independently contributes to left atrial dilation in adults with SCA. Their analysis relied on left atrial diameter and a composite hemolytic index, whereas we evaluated LDH as an isolated marker and used LAVi, a guideline‑recommended volumetric parameter that more accurately reflects true atrial remodeling [14]. This distinction suggests that hemolysis may influence atrial structure more profoundly than previously recognized.

In line with the same pathophysiological rationale, Feugray et al. [5] assessed left atrial remodeling using echocardiographic measures of left atrial size and demonstrated that left atrial dilatation is highly prevalent in patients with SCA, reflecting diastolic dysfunction and increased cardiac oxygen demand. Similarly, Sachdev et al. [15] found increased left atrial volume in adults with SCD and showed that diastolic dysfunction was independently associated with mortality. In this context, our findings add clinical relevance to these observations by demonstrating that LDH levels can discriminate individuals at higher risk of atrial remodeling, with LDH/ULN > 1.2 being associated with a markedly higher likelihood of elevated LAVi. These results reinforce the hypothesis that hemolysis-driven hemodynamic stress contributes to atrial structural remodeling in SCA.

In our study, we did not assess atrial arrhythmias, but the association between higher LDH levels and increased LAVi may have clinical relevance. Recent data in SCA show that early left atrial dilation contributes to atrial hyperexcitability and enhances susceptibility to atrial fibrillation, partly through hemolysis‑related mechanisms such as inflammation, fibrosis, and hypoxemia [16]. These observations raise the possibility that the structural changes associated with higher LDH levels may also contribute to an increased probability of atrial arrhythmias in this population.

A limitation of this study is the unavailability of LDH isoenzymes, which are not part of the routine laboratory assessment of patients followed at our center. However, the correlations observed with Hb, reticulocyte count, and indirect bilirubin support the interpretation of total LDH as a hemolysis-related marker in this cohort.

In conclusion, our study showed an association between LDH and elevated LAVi, highlighting the potential of this simple and low‑cost test as a predictor of atrial remodeling in patients with SCA. In addition, these findings suggest that LDH may not only reflect hemolytic burden but also act as a potential biomarker of structural atrial remodeling.
