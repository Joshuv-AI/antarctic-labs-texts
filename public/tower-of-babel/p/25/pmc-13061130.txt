Genetic and antigenic diversity of rotavirus A in faeces from Danish pigs
Authors: Kasper Pedersen, Nicole B Goecke, Marta Canuti, Pia Ryt-Hansen, Nicolai R Weber, Martí Cortey, Lars Erik Larsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRotavirus A (RVA) causes viral gastroenteritis and is frequently detected in young piglets. These viruses are genotyped according to diversity within the outer capsid coding gene segments designated viral protein 4 (VP4) and 7 (VP7). The present study defines a comprehensive baseline for porcine RVA VP4 and VP7 genotypic diversity in Danish swine herds, important for future monitoring efforts. Disparities between amino acid residues in antigenic domains of the Danish RVA field viruses and the vaccine strain named ‘Ohio State University (OSU) G5P[7]’ were also investigated. RVA nucleic acids from positive faecal samples of 63/84 tested Danish swine herds were amplified using conventional reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction with primers specific for VP7 and VP4 genes and sequenced using Sanger or Illumina technologies. In total, 127 VP7 genes and 110 VP4 genes were sequenced and genotyped. Eight different VP7 genotypes (G2, G3, G4, G5, G9, G10, G11 and G26) and six different VP4 genotypes (P[6], P[7], P[13], P[23], P[26], and P[32]) together with two possibly new VP4 genotypes (P[X1] and P[X2]) were detected, and the most frequent genotype constellations were G9P[23], G9P[13], G5P[13], and G5P[23]. Nine rectal swabs from pigs of nine different herds revealed infections with multiple VP7 genotypes, and four swabs from pigs of four herds presented with various VP4 genotypes. Sampling of several pigs within the same herd revealed co-circulation of different genotypes, which emphasizes that several pigs should be sampled for representative RVA genotyping. Notable differences between the RVA field viruses and the vaccine strain were evident, as on average 11.7/29 and 25.8/37 amino acid residues in immunogenic domains differed in VP7 and VP4 genotypes, respectively. Considering the high RVA field strain diversity and their high divergence from the vaccine strains, future studies are needed to investigate the efficacy of available porcine RVA vaccines.

Introduction

Rotavirus A (RVA, species Rotavirus alphagastroenteritidis) is a common etiological agent of diarrhoea in neonatal piglets (Kumar et al. 2022). RVA belongs to the genus Rotavirus and family Sedoreoviridae, which includes viruses with 11 genome segments (Seg1–11), composed of double-stranded ribonucleic acid (RNA) and enclosed in a triple-layered protein capsid (Estes and Cohen 1989). The genomic segments encode six structural viral proteins [(VPs): VP1-4, VP6, and VP7] and five to six non-structural proteins [(NSPs): NSP1-5/6].

Antibody reactivity against two of the outer viral capsid proteins, VP7 [approximately 330 amino acids (AAs)] and VP4 (approximately 780 AAs, proteolytically cleaved into VP5* and VP8*) designates different serotypes, reflecting neutralizing epitopes (Fields et al. 2013). VP7 is used to divide viruses into G-serotypes (for ‘glycosylated’ protein) and VP4 into P-serotypes (for ‘protease’-sensitive protein). Likewise, nucleotide sequence identity in gene segments categorizes VP7 and VP4 genotypes with nucleotide cut-off demarcation values of 80% (Matthijnssens, Ciarlet, Heiman, et al. 2008). Twelve VP7 genotypes (G1-6, G8-G13, G26) and at least 14 VP4 genotypes (P[1], P[4]-P[8], P[13], P[19], P[23], P[26], P[27], P[32], P[34], P[49]) of RVA have been detected in pigs (Kumar et al. 2022). Additionally, since each gene segment is packaged independently (Fields et al. 2013), reassortment, generating novel genotype constellations, can occur (Iturriza-Gómara et al. 2001). G3, G4, G5 and G9 in combinations with P[6], P[7], P[13], and P[23] form G5P[7], G4P[6], G3P[6], G5P[6], G3P[13], G5P[13], and G9P[23], among the most common constellations detected in pigs (Brnić et al. 2022, Kumar et al. 2022, Monteagudo et al. 2022).

The presence of antibodies in the small intestine plays a significant role in protecting neonatal piglets against gastroenteritis caused by RVA, and therefore, the sows’ delivery of functional lactogenic immunity is an important protector against RVA infection (Fu et al. 1990, Ward et al. 1996). While mortality is usually low, RVA may likely be a part of the post-weaning diarrhoea complex since, at this stage, maternal antibodies no longer protect the piglets from infection (Eriksen et al. 2021). The quality and quantity of the immune response can be enhanced by RVA-antigen exposure, including vaccination of breeding animals before farrowing. In Denmark, the only commercially available porcine RVA vaccine is based on an inactivated Ohio State University (OSU) six prototype (G5P[7]) strain (Rokovac Neo, Emulsion for Injection for Pigs 2021). The strain of this vaccine is old and therefore, there may be divergent strains circulating in swine populations that escape from vaccine-derived immunity with potential detrimental consequences for animal health.

RVA is ubiquitous, and viral particles are relatively stable in the environment (Fields et al. 2013), meaning that viruses can easily spread within a herd, making virus circulation monitoring a crucial aspect for disease control. Additionally, with a genetically labile virus capable of drift, reassortment, recombination, and zoonotic transmission, it is of fundamental importance to have detailed knowledge about the circulating genotypes, both to monitor RVA evolutionary patterns and to support possible epidemiological investigations in cross-species transmissions. RVA is a part of the routine diagnostic panel in Denmark and has been detected in several herds in both pigs with and without diarrhoea (Svensmark, Askaa et al. 1989, Goecke et al. 2020, Eriksen et al. 2023). Nonetheless, the genetic diversity of field RVA in Denmark is unknown, and currently, Danish laboratories do not have a standardized diagnostic assay or approach for RVA genotyping. Therefore, the aims of this study were to investigate the genetic diversity of RVA VP7 and VP4 segments circulating in Danish swine herds, to create a baseline knowledge of the viral genetic make-up for future studies and to investigate the level of identity between the present circulating strains.

Materials & methods

Samples

Faecal samples (n = 333) from domestic pigs (Sus scrofa domestica) aged 1–6 weeks from 84 different Danish commercial herds sampled between 2016 and 2022 were included in the study. The herds were anonymously included using acronyms from A to AQ. Of these, 156 were rectal swabs from individual animals with suspected diarrhoea submitted to routine diagnostics, 91 were rectal swabs from piglets with diarrhoea collected in the framework of other research projects, 33 were pen floor stool samples from farrowing crates without known disease status, and 53 were rectal swabs from healthy piglets collected for other research projects. Of the latter, 51 of the 53 samples were collected from 21 piglets in four different herds as part of a longitudinal cohort study where one to four samples were taken from the same pig at different timepoints. These samples will be referred to as ‘longitudinal samples’ to distinguish them from the other samples, referred to as ‘herd’.

The RVA vaccination schedule in the included herds was not known.

Ribonucleic acid extraction

All rectal swab and pen floor stool samples were stored at ~ 4°C after collection and until arrival at the laboratory, where they were kept at −80°C until testing. For each rectal swab and pen floor stool sample, a 10% solution in PBS was made. A stainless-steel 5 mm bead (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) was added to each sample, which was then processed in a Tissue Lyser II for 30 s at 15 Hz before being subjected to a centrifugation at 10 000 × g for 180 s (rectal swab samples) or 120 s (pen floor stool samples). Afterwards, DNA was isolated from 200 μL of supernatant of each sample using the extraction robot QIAcube HT (QIAGEN) and the Cador Pathogen 96 QIAcube HT Kit (Indical Bioscience GmbH, Leipzig, Germany). In each extraction run, known RVA-positive and negative controls (nuclease-free water) were included. The purified RNA was kept at −80°C until further analysis.

Rotavirus A screening by real-time reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction

Purified RNA was screened for RVA using a real-time reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reactions (RT-PCRs) assay with primers and probe from Pang et al. (Pang et al. 2004). A FAM reporter at the 5′end and a TAMRA quencher at the 3′end of the probe enabled fluoresce-based detection. The Ag-Path-ID One-Step RT-PCR reagents kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) was used, and each reaction tube contained: 4.6 μL nuclease-free water, 7.5 μL 2 × RT-PCR buffer, 0.12 μL of each primer (final concentration 0.4 μM), 0.04 μL probe (final concentration 0.13 μM), 0.6 μL 25 × RT-PCR enzyme mix, and 2.0 μL purified RNA. The thermal cycling was performed on a Rotor Gene Q PCR platform (QIAGEN) with the following thermal conditions: 45°C for 10 min and 95°C for 10 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 45 s. Data were analysed using Rotor–Gene Q software version 2.3.5 (QIAGEN). In each real-time RT-PCR run, positive and negative (nuclease-free water) controls were included. A cut-off value of cycle threshold (Ct) 33 was used based on in-house RT-PCR validation.

Primer design for conventional reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction

The sequences of the primers used for conventional RT-PCR were either from previously published assays or designed for the present study. The primer sequences designed within this study were based on alignments comprising full-length or nearly full-length VP7 and VP4 nucleotide sequences. Sequences used for the alignments were obtained from Database Resources of the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (NCBI Resource Coordinators 2016) and aligned with MUSCLE using CLC Main Workbench version 21.0.5 (QIAGEN). A maximum of three genotype representatives with different sequences were included in the alignment. The specificity of the primers was tested in silico using Nucleotide Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) (Altschul et al. 1990) while melting temperature and basic properties were predicted using OligoCalc (Kibbe 2007). Primer sequences are available in the supplementary material (Table S1).

Conventional reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction and sequencing

For each herd, the RVA-positive sample with the lowest Ct-value in the RT-PCR analysis was selected for Sanger sequencing. Furthermore, 39 RVA-positive ‘longitudinal samples’ were also included in this analysis. RNA was denatured at 95°C for 2 min and VP7- and VP8-* specific RT-PCRs were performed using the Qiagen OneStep RT-PCR kit (QIAGEN) with reaction mixtures containing: 2.0 μL of RNA, 5.0 μL of 5 × Qiagen OneStep RT-PCR buffer, 1.0 μL of nucleotides dNTP mix (0.4 mM), 1.0 μL of Qiagen enzyme mix, and 1.25 μL (0.5 μM, VP7) or 2.0 μL (0.8 μM, VP8*) of each primer, and nuclease-free water to a final reaction volume of 25 μL. The thermal cycling was performed on the PCRmax Alpha thermocycler (Cole-Parmer, St Neots, United Kingdom) with the following thermal cycling conditions: 30 min at 50°C and 95°C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 30 s at 94°C, 45 s at 53°C, and 60 s at 72°C, completed by a final extension step at 72°C for 10 min. After VP8*-based genotyping, a selection of samples were submitted for full length VP4 sequencing using primers specific to four different genotype groups (1:P[24], P[26], P[27], and P[32]; 2: P[6] and P[19]; 3: P[7] and P[13]; 4: P[23]) (Supplementary Table S1). The above-mentioned RT-PCR protocol was used but with the following changes: 2.5 μL primers (0.5 μM) were used for VP7 and 5 μL primers (RVA-VP4-R2-619: 0.5 μM, RVA-VP4-R2-713, -R24262732 and -R2-23: 1.0 μM) were used for VP4, and 120 s were performed during the extension step of the PCR cycling.

The RT-PCR products were purified using the High Pure PCR Product Purification kit (Roche Diagnostics Corporation P.O., Indianapolis, IN, USA) and initially outsourced for Sanger sequencing. Subsequently, amplicons covering the full VP7 (1060 bp) and VP4 (2164-2360 bp) coding sequences were also sequenced using the Illumina® MiSeq platform (Illumina®, San Diego, CA, United States of America) (Bentley et al. 2008). Library preparation was conducted with the Illumina® Nextera DNA Flex Library Prep kit (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Sequence analyses

Raw Sanger sequence data were assembled into contigs using the CLC Main Workbench version 21.0.5 (QIAGEN). Raw MiSeq reads were analysed using CLC Genomics Workbench version 23 (QIAGEN). Specifically, low-quality reads were removed, primers and adaptors were trimmed from the survived reads, and de novo assembly was performed with SPADES (Prjibelski et al. 2020). De novo assembled contigs were submitted to BLAST, and reference genes with the highest identity were used as a reference for mapping and the generation of new consensus sequences. If more than one genotype/variant was detected after assembly, each was blasted independently and used as a reference for mapping. Sequences from samples containing multiple closely related variants that could not be discerned due to the high genetic identity were excluded.

RVA genotyping was performed according to the guidelines of the Rotavirus Classification Working Group (Matthijnssens, Ciarlet, Rahman, et al. 2008, Matthijnssens et al. 2011). VP7 and VP4 genes were compared with sequences available in BLAST, and genotyping was further confirmed with the Rotavirus A Genotype Determination Tool, Virus Pathogen Database and Analysis Resource (ViPR) (Katzel 2021, Pickett et al. 2012). Genotyping results are presented in bar plots made in Graph Pad Prism version 10.

Multiple sequence alignment based on fast Fourier transform was used to perform alignments of obtained and reference sequences and maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees were built with IQ-TREE using the Modelfinder function to determine the best fitting substitution model: a transversion model with empirical base frequencies and nine discrete rate categories (TVM + F + R9) for VP7, TVM + F + R5 for VP8* and a general time reversible with empirical base frequencies and 10 discrete rate categories (GTR + F + R10) for VP4 (Katoh and Standley 2013, Nguyen et al. 2014, Tavaré and Miura 1986, Yang 1994). To evaluate the reliability of the tree topology, bootstrap tests with 1000 pseudo-replicates were used (Felsenstein 1985). Simian SA11 sequences from 1958 were used to root the tree. In the phylogenetic trees and sequence names, the year of detection is indicated, faecal swabs are referred to as ‘S’ whereas stool samples are indicated with ‘F’, and ‘herds’ is referred to as ‘D’, while ‘longitudinal-samples’ is ‘L’ (e.g. SL states for a faecal swab from a longitudinal sample). AN, AQ, CB and CE followed by two- or three-digit number refer to the specific herd and pig in ‘longitudinal-samples’.

Recombination evaluation was performed on the same sequence alignments used for phylogenetic inferences with the recombination detection program 5 (Martin et al. 2021), as we previously performed (Canuti et al. 2016).

Antigenic epitope analysis

Full-length VP7 and VP4 coding sequences were translated into AA sequences, and antigenic epitopes were identified based on previous studies [VP7: (Aoki et al. 2009), VP8* and VP5* (Dormitzer et al. 2002, Dowling et al. 2000, McDonald et al. 2009, Monnier et al. 2006, Mackow et al. 1988)]. Descriptive statistical results are presented in scatter dot plots made in Graph Pad Prism version 10.

Results

Screening and genotyping of VP4 and VP7

Sixty-three (63) of the 84 tested Danish swine herds were found to be RVA RNA positive in the RT-PCR analysis (Table 1). One RVA positive sample from each positive herd (group designation: ‘herds’) was selected for RVA genotyping. Additionally, from four of these herds (group designation: ‘longitudinal-samples’), 21 piglets were also sampled longitudinally (39 samples), and sequences were obtained from the same animals up to 51 days after the first RVA detection.

Overall, a total of 127 VP7 (125 full-length and 2 partial) and 110 VP4 (20 full-length and 90 partial) gene sequences were obtained. In two VP7 and three VP4 positive samples (representing two and three herds, respectively), genotyping was not possible because of the low amplification output.

Of the 127 VP7 sequences, 86 were from 61 herds in the ‘herds’ group, and 41 were from 21 piglets from the ‘longitudinal samples’ group. From the ‘herds’ group, a single VP7 genotype was found in 45 samples (22 pen floor stool samples and 23 rectal swab samples), while in 21 samples (12 pen floor stool samples and nine rectal swab samples) more than one VP7genotype was detected.

Of the 110 VP4 sequences, 70 were from ‘herds’ and 40 were from ‘longitudinal samples’. Among the 63 RVA-positive ‘herds’, a single VP4 genotype was found in 49 herds (26 pen floor stool samples and 23 rectal swab samples), while in nine samples (five pen floor stool samples and four rectal swab samples) more than one VP4 genotype was detected.

For the ‘longitudinal samples’, more than one VP7 or VP4 genotype was detected in two and four samples, respectively.

In total, from ‘herds’, eight different VP7 genotypes (G2, G3, G4, G5, G9, G10, G11 and G26) and eight VP4 genotypes (P[6], P[7], P[13], P[23], P[26], P[32], P[X1] and P[X2]) were detected (Fig. 1a and b and Supplementary Table S2). The most prevalent genotypes in the 63 different herds were G5, G9, and G11 in combinations with P[13] and P[23]. VP7 and VP4 genotypes were observed most frequently in the following constellations: G9P[23], G9P[13], G11P[13], G5/G9P[23], G5P[13], and G9P[32].

Phylogenetic analyses

Sequences from this study were 77.7%–94.1% (VP4) and 88.0%–96.0% (VP7) identical to reference sequences in GenBank (Supplementary Table S3). In the VP7 phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3), the study sequences clustered in eight major clades corresponding to officially established genotypes. Study sequences formed two highly supported subclades (corresponding to lineage I and II) within the G5 cluster, characterized by a pairwise nucleotide distance of 13%–17% (Da Silva et al. 2011). Also, within G11, G9, G8 and G4 different subclades were formed, and all G9 genes from this study, except for one that was in lineage VI of G9, were in the lineage III (Martinez-Laso et al. 2009). One G2 gene differed from previously defined lineages, and another fell into lineage V (Motamedi-Rad et al. 2020). All G3 genes were in the lineage II of these genes (Motamedi-Rad et al. 2020). Interestingly, most of the detected viral variants were found in multiple herds, with five variants being detected only once. This indicates that in Denmark, several different genotypes and variants co-circulate among swine herds. Additionally, in some cases, closely related sequences were observed over the course of a multiple years (e.g. 2016–21 for genotype G9, 2018–21 for genotypes G11or G5), indicating the perpetuation of variants across years. The overall nucleotide and AA diversity of VP7 genes were 71%–100% in both measures (Supplementary Table S2), with G9 being the genotype most distinct from the OSU G5 vaccine strain.

A similar pattern to what is identified for VP7 could be observed in this tree, with study sequences forming multiple sub-clades within most genotypes (e.g. three different variants could be observed in P[13]). The nucleotide identity of VP4 genes was 67%–99%, and the AA identity was 69%–99% (Supplementary Table S2). P[6] appeared to be the genotype most distinct from the OSU P[7] vaccine strain (70%–73% AA identity). Also in this case, no temporal pattern could be observed, and the same variants were found to be circulating over multiple years (e.g. 2017–2021 for P[3], 2016–21 for P[32] or 2018–21 for P[23]).

None of the study sequences were identified as potentially recombinant.

VP7 and VP4 amino acid identities and antigenic sites

The Danish porcine G5 genotype was the most closely related to the OSU vaccine strain (88%–95% and 95%–97% AA identity for each of the two lineages) whereas the G4 was the most distant (73.9%–76.1% AA) in pairwise comparisons. The number of AA differences in the antigenic epitopes of VP7 ranged between 2 and 21 (Fig. 1). Only five residues (98, 104, 190, 201, and 264) within the antigenic epitopes of VP7 were conserved across all study genotypes.

VP4 AA sequences compared to those of the vaccine strain OSU ranged between 70% and 96% in identity in pairwise comparisons. VP4 genotypes had 6–28 AA differences compared to the antigenic epitopes of the vaccine strain. Again, complete conservation was observed only at four residues in VP4 (131, 132, 441, and 434). In almost all VP4 genotype sequences, except for P[7] that included the vaccine strain, the eight to four antigenic domain was different from the OSU vaccine strain.

Results of the antigenic epitope analysis are summarized in Fig. 1c and d, and tables of AA differences in the antigenic sites of VP7 and VP4 are described in supplementary material (Table S4 and S5, respectively). Investigating the antigenic residues in the VP4 proteins of viruses identified in piglets sampled multiple times showed that viruses from two pigs (40 and 280) in herd ‘AN’ differed by 14 AA residues in segment P[13] (8 and 9 days between the samplings). Another pig (120) was shedding RVA at three different sample points with 17 days in between (day 0, 8, and 17): initially and 8 days later, a G5 was detected and afterwards, 17 days after the first detection, we observed a switch to G26. In herd ‘CE’, where three pigs were sampled (2 and 4 days between sampling), no changes were observed in the P[13] sequence, but one pig (222) was co-infected with VP7 G5 and G11. In herd ‘AQ’, three pigs (320R, 240R, and 250R) were infected with the same VP7 G9 genotype with 37 days in between samplings, meanwhile two other pigs were infected with two different VP4 genotypes (P[13] in 320R and P[23] in 250R). Similarly, a VP4 and VP7 shift in genotype was also noted in pig 390R but with 49 days in between and pig (490) in AN with 51 days in between the detection of two different VP7 genotypes. In the last herd (CB), one pig (80) was co-infected with two genotypes (P[13] and P[32]), and one pig was infected with the same genotype with no mutations two days after the first detection. Comparing complete nucleotide segments in piglets (10 piglets with VP7 and four piglets with VP4) with the same genotype detected across two sampling points (from 2 to 37 days) reveals in total one substitution in the VP7 and 431 substitutions in VP4.

Discussion

Rotavirus A detection and genotyping

RVA can be a primary pathogen in young pigs leading to diarrhoea, although its causative role, especially after weaning, is often difficult to resolve, as different pathogens and environmental factors contribute to clinical disease (Eriksen et al. 2021, Dewey et al. 2003, Katsuda et al. 2006, Kongsted et al. 2018, Pettersson et al. 2019, Svensmark, Askaa, et al. 1989). In this study, RVAs were detected in 63 of 84 (75%) investigated herds. Although this study was not designed to accurately determine RVA prevalence, its findings are comparable to another earlier Danish study (Svensmark, Nielsen, et al. 1989) and a more recent Canadian study (Naseer et al. 2017) that found RVA in 75% and 70% of the investigated herds, respectively. On the contrary, a low RVA detection rate (10%) was found in a study including porcine faecal samples from pigs of all ages, but using an in-house antigen detecting enzyme immunoassay (Midgley et al. 2012). This discrepancy may depend on both the diagnostic method and the different age groups included but could also reflect a dissimilar sample collection approach since only 43% (36 of 84) of the samples included in this study were submitted for routine diagnostics. Nonetheless, while the sets of samples included in this study were diverse and did not allow for an accurate virus and genotype prevalence assessment, the results indicate that RVA should be considered ubiquitously present in Danish piglets.

A noticeable overall high genetic diversity was observed in both VP7 and VP4 genes (pairwise identity ranges of 71%–100% and 67%–96%, respectively). The most frequently detected genotypes in this study were G5, G9, and to some extent G11 for VP7, and P[13] and P[23] for VP4. In comparison to other countries, there seems to be a reduced detection of G3, G4, P[6], and P[7] in Denmark (Papp et al. 2013, Vlasova et al. 2017). However, our results are comparable to a recent investigation, with the only exception of higher G3 and G1 detections in Croatia (Brnić et al. 2022). Since Denmark has a very limited import of live pigs, theoretically, no additional RVA genotypes should be introduced from foreign pigs except for potential human-to-swine spillover. However, fluctuations in genotype frequencies are expected, especially if vaccinations are extensively used. Denmark exports ~15 million pigs yearly, which can explain some of the genotypic agreement with other European countries. This possibility is emphasized by the finding of longitudinally sampled piglets being RVA positive 37, 49, and 51 days after weaning, when animals are usually exported (10–12 weeks of age). Indeed, high genetic identity (up to 94.1% and 96.0% identity in VP4 and VP7, respectively) between study viruses and sequences from literature was observed. Transmission between herds is evident in this study by the presence of identical viruses from pigs of different herds across Denmark, and confirmed by the presence of logged pig movements among the herds registered in the Danish ‘The Pig Movement Database’ (www.svineflyt.fvst.dk). With rotavirus being stable in the environment and transmitted by the faecal-oral route, cross-species transmission is likely to occur despite established external biosecurity measures. This is supported by the finding of genotypes mostly identical to human-like RVA strains as one VP7 G2 (RVA/Pig-wt/DK/2019/2SL/AQ370R/G2P[X2]). A similar swine-to-human spillover event has been suggested recently in Croatia (Kunić et al. 2023), and also with porcine G9 genotypes (Fischer et al. 2005). Interestingly, we performed an additional phylogenetic analysis including two Danish human VP7 G9, one G4, one G6 and one G10 sequences, and these belonged to a different subcluster compared to the identified Danish swine sequences, although they shared up to 92.8% sequence identity (Supplementary Fig. S2). However, these were detected in 2010 and 2012—several years before the samples used in this study were collected. Possible zoonotic and reverse zoonotic transmissions highlight the importance of virus surveillance and of having an established RVA genotypic baseline.

Interestingly, sequencing viruses in a rather ‘closed’ population with a ubiquitous virus circulation allowed the identification of two possible new P genotypes. The sequence of one of these was highly identical to a VP4 sequence obtained in Taiwan. The two Danish viruses with this P variant were, like the Taiwanese virus, in a constellation with a known G26, also present in a Danish P[6] virus. This indicates that this potentially novel P genotype is not restricted to Denmark and has potentially been involved in reassortment events. The other potentially novel genotype is the most closely related to genotype P[28], but it diverges from it by ~ 25%. As this is the first detection of this genotype, we cannot speculate about whether this is a genotype that specifically circulates in Denmark or if it has a wider range. Nonetheless, these results highlight how viral RNA sequencing is crucial for identifying new genotypes and how further sequencing efforts, including full genome sequencing, are required to elucidate the origin and distribution of these novel genotypes.

Amplification performed with broad genotype-detecting primers allowed for the detection of multiple VP7 and VP4 genotypes in single samples. Up to four different VP7 genotypes were found in one rectal swab sample, and 23 of 63 samples (37%) from the 63 herds (‘herds’) were positive for more than one genotype. Finding such a high number (33%) of samples with mixed genotypes is comparable (21%, n = 162) to a similar study performed on Danish human population (Fischer et al. 2005). Mixed genotype circulation in Danish swine herds was supported by observations in the longitudinally sampled piglets/pigs (‘longitudinal samples’).

The genotyping of the longitudinal samples revealed up to five genotypes, revealing frequent co-circulation of more than one genotype within a herd that were capable of co-infecting the same pig. We even detected a significant within-genotype variation, with the co-circulation of viruses from multiple lineages of the same genotype and divergent viruses from the same genotype detected in longitudinal samples (e.g. P[13] sequences diverging at 14 AA residues were found in the same pigs in samples collected 8–9 days apart). The co-circulation of more than one genotype within the same herd has previously been reported (Baumann et al. 2022), and this underlines that the definitive determination of the circulating genotype(s) and segment constellation in a given herd can be challenging. Unexpectedly, no intragenic recombination signals were detected in our analysis. This could be due to the inherently low frequency of such events in RVA, but it may also be due to small number of samples examined in this study. Indeed, our results strongly emphasize that genotyping based on a single sample from a specific herd may provide a partial and misleading idea of the overall diversity of circulating RVA viruses.

Due to multiple samples containing more than one genotype, it was not possible to determine the exact genotype constellations, but the combinations G9P[23], G9P[13], G5P[13], and G5P[23] were detected in higher frequencies in the present study. G9P[23] was also the most frequent genotype in a study from Spain (Vidal et al. 2018), but found to a lesser extent and together with G9P[13] or G5P[13] in Belgium (Theuns et al. 2014) and Croatia (Brnić et al. 2022). In contrast, a previous study with a limited sample size showed that G4P[6] and G11P[32] were the most prevalent RVA genotypes in Danish pigs (Midgley et al. 2012).

While Sanger sequencing does not allow to immediately discriminate viruses in case of co-infections, which is only possible after cloning, short-read high-throughput sequencing has the disadvantage of producing reliable consensus sequences only in the case of co-infections by divergent viruses (Brnić et al. 2022). Indeed, some of the sequences obtained with Illumina had to be excluded from our study because of the co-infection in one sample with closely related strains whose sequences could not be resolved (e.g. different variants within one genotype). Further studies aiming at increasing the resolution of the sequence diversity of RVA in these settings should, therefore, focus on obtaining longer sequence reads. Additionally, Sanger sequencing with VP8* specific primers revealed VP4 genotypes not found by VP4-specific primers used for Illumina sequencing in nine samples (rectal swab samples), highlighting the limitation of amplification-based sequencing. Since no or a small number of reference sequences are currently available, this limitation can be overcome by PCR-independent total RNA sequencing.

Antigenic epitope and antibody cross-protection

A high proportion of VP7 sequences (28 of 84 sequenced samples) belonged to the same genotype (G5) as the OSU vaccine while only 2 out of 72 VP4 sequences belonged to the same genotype as the vaccine (P[7]). Nonetheless, we could observe two main G5 lineages among the sequenced viruses, each containing ~ 50% of the sequences, with one lineage being more divergent from the vaccine strain.

Both VP7 and VP4 AA sequences in this study had significant variation compared to the OSU vaccine strain. Only a few antigenic residues in the 7-1a (N = 2), 7-1b (N = 1), and 7-2 (N = 2) antigenic epitopes of VP7 were completely conserved. Similar observations were made with VP4, where only a few residues in the 8–3 (N = 2), 5–1 (N = 1), and 5–2 (N = 1) epitopes were completely conserved. Additionally, we observed a remarkable AA difference between field strains and the vaccine genotype at the level of the antigenic sites (up to 21 and 28 disagreements for VP7 and VP4, respectively). In VP4, half of the antigenic sites were variable in nearly all genotypes. Another study identified 2–10 and 0–13 AAs disagreements between field and vaccine strains in the same epitopes of VP7 and VP8, respectively, as the highest number of AA changes associated with moderate to low vaccine efficacy (Vidal et al. 2018). Considering the amount of variation we observed, we can speculate that vaccine-derived antibodies may have reduced binding affinity to VP7 and VP4 of other genotypes, or even of the same genotype in some cases (e.g. G5), potentially impacting the efficacy of vaccine-derived antibodies in preventing infection. However, only phenotypic changes causing lower antibody affinity towards antigenic sites should be of concern. The antigenic epitope 8–2 (180 and 183) is uncharged as it is composed of neutral AAs (Thr and Asn), and the change from a neutral AA (Asn) to a charged one (Asp) can induce neutralization escape (Zhou et al. 1994). Epitopes investigated in this study might not represent all antigenic domains in VP7 and VP4, as other potential antigenic epitopes have recently been suggested by in silico predictions (Shepherd et al. 2020). Furthermore, the glycosylation of position 238 in VP7 has been shown to block neutralization. Indeed, predictions on antibody–antigen binding affinity cannot be made solely on AA sequences in antigenic epitopes, and should be followed by in vitro experiments or preferably, in vivo challenge studies. However, the difficulties in cultivating RVA in vitro make these experiments challenging and time-consuming.

In this study, two pigs were infected with the same genotype (G5P[13]) at two different points (9 days apart), but in both cases, the sequenced VP4 differed by 14 AA. This is likely to reflect that the animal acquired a new infection with a different variant from the same genotype in those few days, as also supported by the fact that the two different variants were not monophyletic (sequences 90SL and 92SL vs. 91SL and 93SL). Also, pig number 120 from herd AN and three pigs in herd AQ showed prolonged RVA infection of up to 37 days, including a shift in genotype from VP7 G5 to G26 for pig 120, indicating insufficient cross-protection by antibodies developed from the first G5-infection. Thus, future vaccines against RVA should be polyvalent in formulation or target more conserved epitopes to ensure a broader protection.

With RVA being ubiquitous, sows may be naturally exposed to a diverse palette of endemically circulating RVA viruses, which contribute to shaping their immune potential. The match between the vaccine and field strains may, thus, be less significant in preventing infections and clinical signs, especially considering that gilts often are the only pre-farrowing vaccinated animals among Danish sow herds. Unfortunately, in this study, the vaccination status of the herd was unknown, although vaccination could influence virus evolution and circulation dynamics. Virus sequencing in vaccinated and unvaccinated herds should, therefore, be a priority as it would elucidate important aspects related to the immune response against RVA and to the evolution of these viruses under positive selection pressure.

In conclusion, the study identified eight different RVA genotypes of both VP7 and VP4 circulating among Danish pigs, including potentially novel genotypes. Identified viruses were characterized by a high genetic variability, even within single herds, and several cases of co-infections and re-infections by different genotypes were detected. Many of the detected field viruses were divergent from the vaccine strain currently used in Denmark, including at antigenic epitopes, highlighting how virus surveillance can be informative for future vaccine and vaccination strategy development.
