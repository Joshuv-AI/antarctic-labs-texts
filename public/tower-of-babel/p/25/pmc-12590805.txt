Ameliorative effects of biofertilizers on yield, water use efficiency and quality of bean under drought stress conditions
Authors: Halil Demir, Harun Kaman, Andaç Kutay Saka, Ufuk Uçan, Recep Kotan, İsmail Hakkı Akgün
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe use of Plant Growth-Promoting Rhizobacteria (PGPR) is one of the alternative strategies to mitigate the effects of drought. This study aimed to determine the effects of the Partial Root-Zone Drying (PRD) and conventional deficit irrigation techniques on dwarf bean by reducing drought stress through microbial fertilizer applications. The parameters evaluated included yield, plant water consumption, water use efficiency (WUE), irrigation water use efficiency (IWUE), plant growth characteristics, chlorophyll content and biochemical properties. The Gina dwarf bean variety (Phaseolus vulgaris L. cv. Gina) was used in the research conducted at Akdeniz University. Biofertilizers consisting of Pantoea agglomerans RK-92, Paenibacillus polymyxa TV-12E, Bacillus megaterium TV-6D and Pseudomonas fluorescens FDG-37 bacterial strains were supplied by Atatürk University Plant Protection Department. The study was planned with and without biofertilizer (BF) application, and five irrigation treatments were considered. Three conventional irrigation treatments were applied: 100% of the evaporation (I100), 75% of the evaporation (I75), and 50% of the evaporation (I50). And, two treatments were applied to Partial Root Zone Drying (PRD): 75% of the evaporation (PRD75) and 50% of the evaporation (PRD50).ResultsAccording to the research results, remarkable results were obtained from the biofertilizer treatments. The highest first-class (8.44 t ha−1) and total yield (10.50 t ha−1) was recorded from the BF-I100 treatment. Compared to the control treatment (I100) with a first-class yield of 4.40 t ha−1 and a total yield of 9.31 t ha−1, the BF-I100 treatment provided a 14.5% higher first-class yield and a 12.78% higher total yield. Similarly, compared to the control, the BF-I75 resulted in higher first-class (7.36 t ha−1; 6.6%) and total yields (9.34 t ha−1; 9.88%). Among the deficit irrigation treatments, the highest total yield was recorded in the BF-PRD75 (9.54 t ha−1) treatment with biofertilizer. The total yield obtained from BF-PRD75 was 2.47% higher than that of the I100 treatment and was one of the most striking results. The BF-PRD50 treatment exhibited the highest WUE (11.26 kg m−3) and IWUE (13.88 kg m−3) for both first-class and total yield, with no statistically significant differences among BF-I50, BF-I100, and BF-PRD50 treatments.ConclusionsIn conclusion, growth, yield, and quality characteristics were significantly influenced by the irrigation treatments. Biofertilizer applications were effective under both optimized and deficit irrigation conditions. The ameliorative and drought-reducing effects of biofertilizer treatments were clearly observed. This study found that biofertilizers positively impact yield and quality, potentially enabling water savings of 25–50%. They can be effectively used in regions with drought-related water shortages.

Introduction

Bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) is one of the legume vegetables whose seeds and pods are consumed worldwide [1]. Beans, hold an important place in both human nutrition and agricultural production, particularly in crop rotation systems. They are a valuable food source due to their high protein content, richness in minerals, and popular taste. Beans can be cultivated in open fields as well as in greenhouses. Additionally, they are a good dietary protein and important metabolites [2, 3]. While 23 475 187 tn of fresh beans are produced worldwide as of 2023 year, 505 203 tn of this production was obtained from Türkiye [4].

In recent years, global climate change, characterized by rising temperatures, changing precipitation regimes and declining water resources, has made water use and management increasingly important. Water stress has clearly become an international problem, affecting the morphological, functional, and chemical processes of economically important plants such as beans [5].

As in Türkiye, drought stress negatively affects vegetable production in many regions of the world due to insufficient water resources [6]. Beans, which exhibit greater ecological selectivity among food legumes, are particularly sensitive to drought and salinity. Environmental factors such as temperature also significantly impact yield [7]. Due to the increasing effects of drought in recent years, enhancing yield and quality in vegetable production while reducing water consumption has become a key priority [8]. In this context, deficit irrigation regimes have gained attention in areas with limited irrigation water, aiming to irrigate larger areas with less water without compromising optimal yield. Conventional deficit irrigation (DI) is widely used in arid and semi-arid regions to increase water efficiency [9].

Another deficit irrigation strategy that is gaining importance is the “Partial Root-Zone Drying (PRD)” technique. In the PRD technique, water is applied to one half of the plant’s root zone, while the other half is kept relatively dry. Thus, in regions where water is scarce and expensive, it is aimed to use the existing water resources more effectively with less irrigation water, similar to DI. The PRD technique is reported to stimulate root–shoot signaling for water conservation through alternating wet–dry cycles, while maintaining yield. As a result, it may offer higher water-use efficiency compared to DI [10].

Nowadays, new methods and strategies are being developed to protect plants from the effects of drought stress. In recent years, the use of Plant Growth Promoting Rhizobacteria (PGPR) has been suggested as an alternative strategy to reduce the negative impact of drought [11]. The mechanisms of plant growth promoting bacteria are not fully understood. However, it is known that they can produce various growth promoting hormones, especially cytokinin, gibberellic acid, and auxins, and help fix nitrogen and phosphorus [12]. Application of biofertilizers in plant ecosystems is one of the main goals of sustainable agriculture, it reduces or eliminates the need for inorganic fertilizers by up to 50% and saves water [13]. It has been demonstrated that some bacteria living in the rhizosphere support plant growth various mechanisms. PGPR colonize the rhizosphere and promote plant growth via direct and indirect pathways [14]. Studies have shown that PGPR improves the growth of plants under environmental stress conditions and protect them from the harmful environmental effects [13, 15–17].

Biofertilizer is a biological product containing microorganisms that can be used as fertilizer in the soil [18]. It can improve drought tolerance by enriching the soil with macroelements (N, P, K) and microelements (Mn, Zn, Fe) through the degradation of organic compounds [19]. Drought stress affects plant water relations and causes negative effects on many mechanisms of the plant. However, several studies have reported that these adverse effects can be reduced with PGPR [20–25]. PGPRs enhance plant growth through both direct and indirect mechanisms, thereby increasing plant tole to abiotic stress conditions [26]. In a study conducted on beans, it was determined that Bacillus subtilis ameliorated drought stress [27]. In this context, it is evident that the effects of the PRD technique on beans have not been widely investigated, nor has its interaction with beneficial microorganisms been thoroughly examined. Therefore, a significant knowledge gap exists. Integrating PRD with beneficial microorganisms represents a novel approach that could improve a plant’s water use efficiency, drought tolerance, growth, and productivity. This makes the research both timely and innovative.

This research aimed to reveal the effects of different deficit irrigation treatments under DI and PRD techniques on yield, water use, plant water consumption, water use efficiency, plant growth characteristics, chlorophyll and biochemical properties.

Materials and methods

Research area, plant and biofertilizer materials

This research was conducted in a plastic greenhouse with a curved roof at the Faculty of Agriculture, Akdeniz University. The ventilation openings of the greenhouse were covered with mosquito nets to prevent the entry of insects, birds, etc. In addition, mosquito nets were used on the Class-A evaporation pan.

The plant material used in the study was the dwarf bean variety Gina (P. vulgaris cv. Gina). The biofertilizer (BF) used consisted of Pantoea agglomerans RK-92, Paenibacillus polymyxa TV-12E, Bacillus megaterium TV-6D and Pseudomonas fluorescens FDG-37 bacterial strains and was provided by Ataturk University, Faculty of Agriculture, Plant Protection Department. The bacterial strains had been isolated from the rhizosphere of wild or cultivated plants growing in the Eastern Anatolian Region of Türkiye. The bacterial cultures were grown on Nutrient Agar (NA) for routine use, and maintained in Luria Broth (LB) with 15% glycerol at −80 ˚C for long-term storage. The total number of live bacteria in the biofertilizer content is at least 1 × 107 cfu ml−1.

No organic fertilizer and/or materials containing microbiological products were previously used in the greenhouse soil in the research area. Soil samples taken from 0 to 30 cm depth before planting the bean seedlings were analyzed at the accredited LabEN Agricultural Analysis Laboratory according to Kacar [28]. The physical and chemical properties of soil are presented in Table 1. At the start of the study, soil samples were taken for analysis of field capacity, wilting point, electrical conductivity, pH, texture, etc. In addition, an infiltration test and soil water content measurement device (PR2-soil profile water measurement sensor) were carried out.

Climatic characteristics of the research area

The temperature and humidity in the research greenhouse were recorded using a HOBO data logger. Maximum temperatures were observed as 40.91 °C in October, 35.40 °C in November, 32.58 °C in December, 31.32 °C in January, and 35.35 °C in February. The corresponding minimum temperatures were 17.12 °C, 14.14 °C, 9.22 °C, 8.89 °C, and 4.92 °C, respectively. The average monthly temperatures were calculated as 27.56 °C (October), 21.28 °C (November), 16.70 °C (December), 16.13 °C (January), and 16.15 °C (February), indicating a gradual cooling trend from October to January, followed by a slight increase in February. Relative humidity levels varied across the months, with average values of 44.48% in October, 61.62% in November, 68.38% in December, 64.94% in January, and 54.89% in February.

Irrigation treatments and explanations in the study

Fig. 1According to the experimental design (with and without biofertilizer treatments), 5 irrigation treatments with 3 replications

According to the experimental design (with and without biofertilizer treatments), 5 irrigation treatments with 3 replications

The bean seedlings were grown approximately 20 days ago in vials filled with a medium composed of a 70% peat and 30% perlite mixture. Three bean seeds were planted in each cell of the vial. The bean plants were planted on 5 October 2023 as 3 plants with 100 cm between the rows and 40 cm above the rows.

Electrical conductivity, anion and cation contents were analyzed in the irrigation water. The pH of the irrigation water used was determined to be 7.45, the EC value to be 6.30 µmhos cm−1, the bicarbonate content to be 5.25 meq l−1, the calcium content to be 4.45 meq l−1 and the magnesium value to be 1.11 meq l−1.

Soil water content was monitored using the “PR2 soil profile water measurement sensor” from access tubes placed between the dripper and the plant on the row and up to 40 cm deep in each experimental plot. In a study conducted by Kaman and Özbek [29] in the same region, the calibration value of PR2 was found to be quite high (93.05%) and therefore its use was preferred in this study. In addition, gravimetric soil sampling was performed at the beginning and end of the study and the seasonal water balance (ET) was determined for all irrigation treatments. Since the planning of the cover percentage is extremely important for the deep infiltration of irrigation water, the cover percentage was increased according to the soil water content (PR2) observations-measurements. As a result, soil water content was closely monitored before and after irrigation and deep infiltration was prevented in the irrigations.

Water requirement assessment

At first, bean seedlings were evenly supplied with the same amount of irrigation water, after which the irrigation program outlined in the research was applied. Irrigation was applied according to the evaporation amount measured from the Class-A Evaporation Pan. The irrigation interval was set to once a week, as described by Çamoğlu and Genç [30] and Gölgül [31]. It’s worth noting that the amount of irrigation water calculated and applied at one time for the I100 (and BF-I100) treatments did not exceed approximately 40% of the usable water content. However, if the amount of evaporation exceeded 40% of the the usable water content, the irrigation interval was adjusted to be twice a week.

Application of biofertilizers

The biofertilizer was applied twice to the plots defined in the research plan. The first application of biofertilizers was made one week after the bean seedlings were planted, when the plants had began to root in the soil, and the second application was carried out 30 days later. A solution was prepared by mixing 600 cc of biofertilizer with 100 L of chlorine-free well water, and 150 cc of this solution was poured into the root zone of each plant in its respective pit.

Parameters measured

Leaf area (cm2), total chlorophyll content (SPAD), colour of bean leaves (L*, a*, b*), earliness (days to harvest), pod length (cm) and width (mm), pod wall thickness (mm), pod number (number), number of grains per pod (number pod−1), dry weight (g), soluble solids (%), titratable acidity (g citric acid), vitamin C (mg 100 g−1), total phenolic compounds, total flavonoids, 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl free radical scavenging antioxidant activity (DPPH), ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) analysis and yield (t ha−1) criteria were examined.

At harvesting, the fresh weights of the plant samples were weighed and kept in an oven at 70 °C until they reached a constant weight, and their dry weight was determined. In the second harvest period, full-size bean leaves were scanned with the Epson Perfection V370 Photo Scanner and leaf areas were measured in cm2 using the Windias 3 Analysis Systems program. Chlorophyll and colour measurements were taken three times at 15-day intervals, starting 15 days after the seedlings were planted. Chlorophyll contents was measured on full size three leaves for each plant that were not very young and old using a SPAD chlorophyll meter (Konica Minolta, SPAD-502, Japan). Colour measurements on bean leaves were made with a Minolta CR400 model colour chromameter as Light, *a and *b, and C (chroma) and hº (hue) angle values were determined using the obtained *a and *b values [37].

Soluble solids (SS) and biochemical analyses were performed on samples from the second and third harvests. SS was measured using a digital refractometer (Hanna HI 96801). Titratable acidity was expressed as g citric acid 100 mL−1 based on the amount of NaOH used in the titration process in the fruit juice sample obtained for SS [38].

Vitamin C (mg 100 g−1) was examined as described by Uğur et al. [39], the ascorbic acid test kit was immersed in the prepared bean sample solution for 2 s., waited for 8 s. to oxidize externally, and then placing it in the test adapter of the refractometer device for 5 s.

For the analysis of bioactive compounds, broad beans were sliced with a stainless-steel knife, crushed with a blender, and the homogenized samples were stored in Falcon tubes (approximately 30–40 g) at −20 °C until the bioactive analyses were performed. Total phenolic compounds (mg gallic acid equivalent (GAE) kg−1 fresh weight) were determined using a spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 760 nm using the Folin-Ciocalteu chemistry according to Yılmaz et al. [40]. The results for total phenolic compounds were expressed as. Total flavonoid mg (quercetin equivalent (QE) kg−1 fresh weight) was measured using a spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, UV-1280, Japan) at a wavelength of 510 nm. The results of the total flavonoid content are expressed as [41].

The DPPH antioxidant activity was carried out based on the method of Öztürk et al. [42]. The DPPH solution was used as the free radical. The absorbance of the obtained mixture was measured at 517 nm. The absorbance values were calculated using the standard calibration curve of Trolox (10–100 µmol/L). The results of the DPPH antioxidant activity are presented as mmol Trolox equivalents (TE) per kg of fresh fruit. The standard curve formula used in the DPPH calculation is as follows: y = 8.7055x + 5.636; R² = 0.991.

For the FRAP analysis [41], the protocol used resulted in the mixture being read at a wavelength of 593 nm in the spectrophotometer. The results of the FRAP antioxidant activity are given as mmol TE per kg of fresh fruit. The standard curve formula used in calculating FRAP antioxidant power is as follows: y = 0.059x + 0.0739; R² = 0.9942.

Statistical analysis

The experiment was arranged in a randomized block design with 3 replications. Analyzed using JMP Pro 17 (SAS Inc., Cary, NC, USA), a statistical software program. In the statistical analysis, all data were evaluated at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05.

Numerous studies conducted in Türkiye and other countries have explored water-yield relationships using data derived from Class-A evaporation pans [37, 43, 44]. The cumulative evaporation data in Fig. 2 aligns with those reported by [45–47].

During the research, a total of 35 irrigation applications were carried out, with the final one administered on February 6, 2024. The I100 (and BF-I100) treatments acted as controls and are marked as “100%” in the graph. In the I75 and PRD75 treatments (as well as BF-I75 and BF-PRD75), irrigation water was applied at 75% of the evapotranspiration rate and is marked as “75%.” Similarly, the I50 and PRD50 treatments (including BF-I50 and BF-PRD50) were irrigated with 50% of the water volume and are marked accordingly. By the end of the study, the amount of irrigation water applied per plant was 15.41, 13.43, and 11.45 mm for the 100%, 75, and 50% of evapotranspiration treatments, respectively.

The irrigation treatments, 100%, 75%, and 50% of evapotranspiration, were calculated to provide appropriate water volumes to the plant root zones [48;49]. Soil moisture in the plant root zone (cm³/cm³) was monitored using PR2 sensors. Predictably, the highest soil water content was observed in the 100% irrigation group, followed by the 75%, and then the 50% treatments. The temporal variation in root zone moisture followed a descending pattern in accordance with irrigation levels: 100% >75% >50%.

Monitoring root zone soil moisture over time under different irrigation treatments offers valuable insight into water availability dynamics. This trend, as illustrated in Fig. 2, is well documented and consistent with findings from other comparable studies [45, 48, 49].

Table 4 shows the effects of different irrigation levels on first class, second class, total yield (t ha−1) and ET (mm). Significant differences were found among treatments in both yield and ET values, with noticeable increases attributed to biofertilizer (BF). The data obtained from the research indicate that crop performance was directly impacted by irrigation quantity and technique; yield reduced as irrigation levels decreased. However, even under deficit irrigation conditions, BF treatments consistently yielded higher than their non-BF counterparts.

The results obtained are similar to those reported by Köksal et al. [51], who revealed that in dwarf bean deficit irrigations trials, yields decreased with reduced water application. Similarly, Campos et al. [52] observed that moderate deficit irrigation allowed for the successful production of green-shelled beans, whereas severe water stress had a detrimental effect on their yield.

It is thought that the effectiveness of biofertilizers in ameliorating drought stress is related to the mechanisms of action of the bacteria they contain. Yang et al. [53] reported that ethylene is a crucial signaling molecule that regulates various plant functions, and its production increases under both abiotic and biotic stress conditions, ultimately inhibiting plant development. According to Shultana et al. [14], the enzyme 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) deaminase, produced by certain bacteria, slows down ethylene production and mitigates its negative effects by promoting bacterial survival. Through this mechanism, under stress conditions, various plant growth related functions, such as growth hormone production, atmospheric nitrogen fixation, and inorganic phosphate solubilization, are enhanced, thereby promoting plant growth and development.

Al-Amri [5] reported that under drought conditions, mycorrhiza (Glomus mosseae) and bacteria (Bacillus amyloliquefaciens) positively influenced bean yield. In another study, B. subtilis inoculation increased the number of pods (45%) and flowers (70%) under heat stress [54]. Lima et al. [27] reported that the B. subtilis AP-3 strain has an ameliorative effect under water stress. They also found that inoculating bean plants with Rhizobium leguminosarum pv. phaseoli (R), and Pseudomonas brassicacearum subsp. neoaurantiaca (P) resulted in a 16% increase in yield compared to inoculation with Rhizobium leguminosarum pv. phaseoli (R) alone. It is noteworthy that the increase in nodule biomass can be explained by indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production and ACC deaminase activity of the P strain [55]. Shultana et al. [14] reported that P. agglomerans fixes nitrogen, increases IAA production, siderophore formation and phosphate solubilization. By adjusting the irrigation interval in onion cultivation, the same yield was obtained with the non-deficit irrigation treatment, while achieving a 37% reduction in water usage. Additionally, a 50% saving in NPK fertilizer was achieved through the application of biofertilizer [13].

Water-yield relationship

The results revealed a correlation between first-class, second-class, total yield (t ha−1) and the irrigation treatments (Fig. 3). The correlations coefficients between first-class, second-class, and total yield values and irrigation water were recorded as R2 = 0.83, R2 = 1.00, and R2 = 0.86, respectively. The very high correlation between irrigation water and yield indicates that the study was well-designed. The increase in yield values with increasing irrigation water also indicates that t no water loss occurred through surface or subsurface drainage. The results are consistent with findings from other studies on various crops, including beans [46], melons [50], eggplants [47], and cabbages [56].

The most important indicators in the evaluation of water-yield relationships are WUE and IWUE, which reflect plant water consumption. Under current climate change conditions, saving irrigation water is of great importance. Therefore, it is essential to maximize efficiency and quality while minimizing water use. The results of this study are particularly noteworthy in this context. When analyzing the yield values of beans (Table 4), it was observed that with the addition of biofertilizer (BF), treatments that saved 25% and 50% of water were able to achieve yields comparable to optimized irrigation (I100). These results were further confirmed by the WUE and IWUE values.

In some studies involving PRD and DI, WUE and IWUE yielded similar results [44], and the values of WUE were also influenced by whether the research was conducted in pots or in the field. For example, Campos et al. [52] found that in a conventional deficit irrigation study on beans, WUE was higher under field conditions (60% water deficit), while it was lower under pot conditions (70% water deficit). In a study by Simsek et al. [57] using the Gina bean variety, the highest WUE and IWUE values were recorded during the period from seed germination to pod formation, with the highest values observed in treatments with 100%, 75%, 50%, and 25% irrigation. Sezen et al. [58] reported that WUE and IWUE values decreased with increasing irrigation intervals, but their results indicated that a 25% water-saving irrigation regime is important in water-scarce areas. Hussen et al. [59] determined that the highest WUE and IWUE values in mung beans were obtained in treatments where 75% and 50% of optimized irrigation were applied.

The PRD technique aims to increase IWUE without reducing yield [33]. The highest IWUE for total yield, after BF-I50 was calculated in the BF-PRD50 treatment, which was inoculated with biofertilizer (Table 6). There are only a few studies related to the PRD technique in beans. In a previous study, Wakrim et al. [60] reported that under PRD with a 50% water deficit and regulated deficit irrigation, the amount of water lost through transpiration decreased, thereby increasing WUE. Admasu and Tamiru [61] determined that the highest WUE value among alternative, fixed, and conventional deficit irrigation treatments in beans was achieved with alternative irrigation at 75% of optimized irrigation. However, since the yield loss was not tolerable, they recommended conventional deficit irrigation treatments with 75% and 50% water restrictions under water deficit conditions. Ikan et al. [62] reported that irrigation combined with biofertilizer constitutes an effective management strategy to overcome soil drought. In a study similar to our results, Al-Amri [5] demonstrated that in a deficit irrigation study based on irrigation intervals in beans, the application of mycorrhizal fungi and endophytic bacteria increased WUE, and their combination achieved the highest efficiency. In another study, Liu et al. [63] found that Bacillus species bacteria improved WUE in potted tomatoes under deficit irrigation conditions.

In irrigation management planning, the ky value, which is an important indicator of the effect of water deficiency on plant yield during the growing season, can differ depending on the plant species and growth stages [64]. The lower ky values observed in the PRD treatments (BF-PRD50 and BF-PRD75) compared to DI treatments (BF-I50 and BF-I75) indicate that PRD applications may offer a greater yield advantage under certain levels of water deficit. Studies conducted on various plant species and varieties have shown that ky values vary depending on factors such as the irrigation method, the level of irrigation water applied, more [47, 48, 50, 56]. These variations in the yield response factor may be attributed to differences in the study location and period, climate conditions, soil characteristics, irrigation levels, plant species, and variety.

The results varied depending on the amount of water applied. The biofertilizer treated irrigation treatments also yielded similar results among themselves. These results are also supported by some research, as described below. At varying irrigation levels, the plant height, fruit length, and fruit diameter of broad beans increased with the amount of water applied, although changes were occurred depending on the varieties used [66]. Similarly, Simsek et al. [57] observed that the Gina variety exhibited the highest pod length, pod width, and number of seeds per pod under optimized irrigation, a finding that aligns with the results of the present study. Guerrero-Domínguez et al. [67] determined that the number of pods and seeds in beans decreased under deficit irrigation. Another study conducted under both pot and open-field conditions revealed that in water-deficit trials, 60% (open-field trial) and 70% (pot) irrigation levels resulted in a significant reduction in bean biomass and the number of seeds per pod [52]. Sezen et al. [45] found that as water consumption increased in beans, pod width, pod length, and seed count per pod also increased. Similarly, Yıldırım et al. [68] reported that a decrease in irrigation level led to reduced plant growth in curly lettuce.

When irrigation treatments inoculated with biofertilizer were compared to those not inoculated, slightly higher values were obtained from the biofertilizer-treated plots. The impact of biofertilizer is related to the mechanism of action of microorganisms, as emphasized by Shultana et al. [14]. Similarly, Bangash et al. [69] demonstrated that PGPR isolates promoting ACC deaminase activity were effective in enhancing plant growth at different irrigation levels in a drought-prone production region. Al-Amri [5] highlighted that bean pod length decreased as irrigation intervals increased, whereas mycorrhizal fungi, endophytic bacteria, and their combinations relatively increased pod length. In another study, the development of bean plants was positively affected by inoculation with P. polymyxa and Rhizobium tropici under water-deficient conditions [70]. Arshad et al. [71] found that drought stress reduced grain yield in peas, while P. fluorescens application increased grain yield by 48% compared to the control. Ejaz et al. [72] obtained similar results with PGPR applications, despite a 20% reduction in nitrogen and phosphorus fertilization compared to the full dose, in terms of criteria such as pod length and grain number. Based on these results, it has been emphasized that PGPRs save fertilizer by increasing the availability of nutrients and reduce the effects of environmental problems. The ameliorating effects of beneficial microorganisms on the growth characteristics of different plant species under drought conditions have been determined in several studies. For example, Şahin et al. [23] increased growth characteristics such as head length and root collar diameter of lettuce under water stress conditions using PGPRs. In another study, fruit length and diameter of peppers were positively affected by bacterial inoculation [73].

Similar and different results have been reported in previous studies. Anzuma et al. [75] found that under drought stress, properties such as phenolics, flavonoids, and antioxidant activity in beans decreased. However, when B. amyloliquefaciens was applied, the treated plants did not experience stress, suggesting that this bacterium reduces oxidative damage caused by drought. In a study on different stress conditions, Tiryaki et al. [76] determined that bacteria obtained from cold-resistant plants, including P. fragi, P. chlororaphis, P. fluorescens, P. proteolytica, and Brevibacterium frigoritolerans, could be used to increase antioxidant content and mitigate cold stress. Another study involving bacterial applications under varying irrigation levels reported that the total antioxidant content of cabbage was significantly affected by irrigation levels. While bacterial applications had an insignificant effect on total antioxidant content, they were significant in terms of total phenolics [77]. Sarma and Saikia [22] documented the beneficial use of PGPR under water stress conditions, indicating that inoculating mung bean plants with Pseudomonas aeruginosa strains alleviated the effects of water stress through the detoxification of oxidative enzymes. Petropoulos et al. [78] and Fernandes et al. [79] demonstrated that biofertilizers used as biostimulants positively influenced the chemical and bioactive contents of beans depending on the irrigation regime.

Research has indicated a correlation between these criteria and water consumption. Romero-Félix et al. [80] reported that water deficiency in the soil leads to a decrease in the rate of cell production, thereby reducing the elongation of plant organs. Nematollahi et al. [81] demonstrated that the decline in chlorophyll content is associated with reduced availability of nutrients necessary for chlorophyll synthesis, limitations in CO2 diffusion from stomata to intercellular spaces, and constraints in photophosphorylation. A study revealed that leaf area, chlorophyll content, fresh and dry weight values varied among three different bean varieties under DI conditions [82]. Guerrero-Domínguez et al. [67] compared the effects of optimized and deficit irrigation in beans and found that plant height and leaf area decreased ender deficit irrigation.

The results of this study regarding the use of biofertilizers are supported by similar research. For instance, Pastor-Bueis et al. [55] reported that rhizobial inoculants influence growth characteristics in beans. Benito et al. [83] demonstrated that simultaneous infection by rhizobia and other bacteria in nodules can enhance plant growth in legumes. Chavoshi et al. [84] determined that the application of phosphorus-solubilizing bacteria (P. agglomerans and P. putida) and potassium-solubilizing bacteria (Bacillus circulans and B. megatherium), when mixed together, significantly improved bean biomass and chlorophyll content under deficit irrigation conditions in red beans. Al Amri [5] observed that when bean grains were inoculated with mycorrhizal fungi (Glomus mosseae) or pre-soaked with endophytic bacteria (B. amyloliquefaciens), shoot and root dry weights, leaf number, and leaf area increased under 12-day irrigation intervals compared to to the control and reduced irrigation treatments. Similar improvements were also noted in chlorophyll content. Anzuma et al. [75] indicated that B. amyloliquefaciens increased photosynthetic pigments in beans under drought stress. Liu et al. [63] observed that Bacillus species improved leaf surface area and chlorophyll content compared to the control in deficit irrigation pot experiments. Asraf and Iram [85] recorded a decrease in fresh and dry weights and plant height of P. vulgaris and Sesbania aculeata plants in a 60% water deficiency experiment.

Conclusions

The results obtained from the treatments applied with microbial fertilizer were superior to those not applied. Remarkable yield results were obtained through microbial applications. Despite the same irrigation treatments, an increase in yield occurred when the plants were inoculated with BF. For example, compared to the control treatment (I100), BF-I100 provided a 14.05% increase in first-class yield. In terms of total yield, 12.78% more was obtained from BF-I100. The BF-I100 had a 12.78% increase in yield in terms of total yield. Similarly, the first-class and total efficiency of BF-I75 were 6.6% and 9.8% higher than I75, respectively. One of the most striking results was observed in BF-PRD75. In comparison with the I100, both 25% water savings and a 2.47% increase in total yield were recorded. When microbial fertilizer was added to treatments where 75% and 50% of optimized irrigation was applied, the fact that equivalent yields were obtained with optimized irrigation (I100) shows the positive effects of microbial fertilizer treatments. The highest WUE (BF-PRD50, BF-I50) and IWUE (BF-I50, BF-PRD50) values for first-class and total yield were found in water-saving treatments.

Consequently, the yield, growth, and quality characteristics of bean were significantly influenced by irrigation and biofertilizers treatments. In addition, water savings of 25–50% were achieved with the biofertilizer used. The study suggests the use of biofertilizer in production areas with limited water availability.
