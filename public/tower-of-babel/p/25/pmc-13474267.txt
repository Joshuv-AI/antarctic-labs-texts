Parent and child-reported acceptability and preference of pediatric home sleep apnea testing versus In-lab polysomnography: a pilot study
Authors: Anna Scott, Christopher M Cielo, Melissa Xanthopoulos, Ignacio Tapia, Darko Stefanovski, Mahalakshmi Somayaji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIn-lab polysomnography (PSG) remains the gold standard for the diagnosis of pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), but its limited availability has prompted the exploration of home sleep apnea testing (HSAT) with encephalogram (EEG) in children. While the feasibility and accuracy of HSAT have been studied previously in children, parent- and child-reported acceptability and preferences of HSAT compared with PSG are unknown. We aimed to evaluate factors influencing parent and child acceptability and preference of HSAT versus PSG.MethodsIn this pilot study children aged 2–17.9 years completed PSG and comprehensive HSAT with EEG within one week of each other. Parents and older children completed questionnaires assessing feasibility, acceptability, sleep disturbances, and their preference of the two tests. A single structured phone interview with the parent was conducted one day after all study procedures, using ten open-ended questions.ResultsFifteen children participated and all completed testing. HSAT was reported to be feasible by 15 (100%) and doable by 14 (92.8%) parents. Seventy-nine percent of parents and 87.5% children liked the HSAT. HSAT was accepted by 79% of parents and 75% of the children. Overall, 60% of parents and 33% of children preferred HSAT over PSG. During HSAT, 64.3% of the parents needed to adjust sensors, mostly the nasal cannula (42.9%) and the pulse oximeter (66.7%). However, the majority did not find this bothersome.ConclusionHSAT was feasible in children and preferred over PSG by parents but not children. During HSAT, children frequently had to have the sensors adjusted by parents. HSAT has the potential to be more widely acceptable if there is improvement to the stability of the nasal cannula and pulse oximeter. Statement of SignificanceThis pilot study provides an assessment of parental preferences in pediatric sleep diagnostics using a Home Sleep Apnea Testing (HSAT) device with EEG, filling a critical gap in the field. By analyzing the experience of families choosing between HSAT with EEG and polysomnography, we identify that HSAT was feasible in children, welcomed and accepted by the parents. The response from parents during a structured interview captured critical parental feedback regarding optimization of HSAT. Specifically, the sensors from nasal canula and the pulse oximetry were the sources of disturbance, providing insights toward developing next-generation pediatric wearables.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) affects up to 5% [1] of children, with some pediatric subpopulations affected even more, including children with Down syndrome, craniofacial differences, and prematurity [2]. In children, OSA has been linked to multiple medical comorbidities, such as cardiovascular and metabolic dysfunction, including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, hypertension and metabolic syndrome. Furthermore, it has been linked to issues with emotional regulation, neurocognitive, and behavioral consequences [2].

Symptoms of pediatric OSA are generally categorized into nocturnal, including snoring, pauses in breathing, and restless sleep, as well as diurnal, such as daytime sleepiness, inattention, or irritability depending on age, with several cardiovascular and metabolic correlates such as high blood pressure, heart rate variability, risk for insulin resistance, etc. [3–5]. Early diagnosis and management of OSA in children can prevent long-term sequelae [6].

In-laboratory polysomnography (PSG) is the current gold standard for the diagnosis of OSA in children [7]. However, availability of PSG for children is limited, and it can be challenging for some families where overnight medical testing is difficult. Home sleep apnea testing (HSAT) involves diagnostic testing for OSA done in the home environment, which is considered an alternative to PSG in adults [8, 9]. HSAT is not recommended for pediatric OSA evaluation due to a lack of adequate evidence validating its diagnostic accuracy relative to PSG. However, several studies have evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of HSAT compared with PSG in pediatric populations [10–14].

This study uses HSAT with a comprehensive encephalogram (EEG). In addition to being accurate, for HSAT to be an acceptable alternative to PSG for the diagnosis of OSA in children, it must also be feasible and acceptable for the parents who will be responsible for managing the test for their child in their home. Our group also previously found that HSAT with EEG was preferred to polysomnography by nearly three quarters of parents and had very high acceptance rates in a cohort of 43 youth with Down syndrome [13].

This pilot study aimed to evaluate parent- and child-reported feasibility, acceptability, and preference of HSAT compared to PSG in children and assess factors that influence these preferences.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective pilot study recruited children, two to ˂18 years old, referred clinically for diagnostic PSG for suspected OSA to participate in this study. This included HSAT in the home environment and completion of questionnaires and a structured interview. Participants with upcoming scheduled diagnostic polysomnograms were contacted by phone to be recruited for the study. Participants who required respiratory support (supplemental oxygen or positive airway pressure) or who had a tracheostomy or tracheocutaneous fistula were excluded. In addition, families where the parent had limited English proficiency were also excluded as interviews assessing acceptability and preference could only be conducted in English for this pilot study. The study was approved by the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia Institutional Review Board (#19-016737). Informed consent was obtained from the parent of each participant and assent obtained for participants seven years of age and older.

Sleep apnea testing

All participants underwent HSAT in their home environment and in-center PSG in the sleep laboratory. Full methods were previously published [15]. To reduce the risk of bias from a delay between tests, HSAT was scheduled within 5 days either before or after clinical PSG based on the family’s availability for testing.

Home sleep apnea test

HSAT was conducted using Nox A1 monitor (Nox Medical, Reykjavik, Iceland). Participants reported to the sleep laboratory the afternoon of HSAT testing. Technicians applied EEG, bilateral electro-oculogram, chin electromyogram, electrocardiogram, and chest abdominal plethysmography and accelerometry belts. The airflow cannula and pulse oximeter were applied later at home by parents. Parents were given information on troubleshooting any lead dislodging and were provided with a link for an instructional video made for the study. During HSAT testing, the study team were available to contact for additional troubleshooting help.

The HSAT device was collected by courier the following day. Data was downloaded and reviewed by a sleep technologist and final scoring was done by a pediatric sleep medicine physician.

In-laboratory attended polysomnography

Participants reported to the sleep laboratory in the evening and remained with a guardian in a dark quiet room overnight. Data was collected using a Polysmith PSG system (Nihon Kohden, Irvine, CA) using current American Academy of Sleep Medicine specifications, concurrently with the Nox A1 monitor.

During in-laboratory evaluation, all participants underwent concurrent testing with the Nox A1 system (portable lab testing, PLT). Technologists were blinded to PLT data and did not replace displaced PLT leads or cannulas during the study. Thus, each participant had two recordings with the same portable monitor, one at home (HSAT) and one in the sleep laboratory (PLT), with parents managing the equipment overnight in both settings.

Data from PSG and HSAT were scored utilizing the current American Academy of Sleep Medicine pediatric standards [16]. All participants were included in feasibility analysis, regardless of sleep duration or data acquisition.

Assessment of the questionnaires

At the baseline visit, demographics were collected, including sleep disturbance assessed with the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Pediatric Sleep Disturbance item bank (parent proxy). This is a 25-item self-reported survey that evaluates sleep quality, depth, restoration, difficulties falling/staying asleep, adequacy, and satisfaction with sleep over the previous seven days [17]. Raw scores were converted to T-scores (mean = 50, SD = 10) using www.assessmentceter.net. Higher scores indicate greater sleep disturbance. Sleep disturbance was categorized into “normal” (<=55), “mild” (55-59), “moderate” (60-65), and “severe” (>66), based on the T-score.

The day following both PSG and HSAT, child participants and their parent completed questionnaires developed for this study, which evaluated acceptability and feasibility using Likert scales. Parents and children were asked separately about sleep quality, duration, and interruptions. Only parents answered the questionnaires about HSAT ease of use and home applicability.

After completion of both studies, the test preference (either HSAT or PSG) of both parent and child were recorded using dichotomous surveys with a third option of neither, and additional comments were welcomed. For children unable to respond independently, questions were asked to their parent as proxy (n = 7).

A structured interview was conducted where a sequence of 10 open-ended questions (Appendix 1) were asked to the participant’s parent over the telephone, a day after completing all the study procedures. These questions included the parent’s response regarding satisfaction, thoughts and feelings about the testing. The responses were typed into a REDCap form verbatim but not coded. Open-ended questions did not include Likert scales.

For the questionnaire analysis, unless stated, the Likert scale (1: completely agree; 2: somewhat agree; 3: undecided; 4: somewhat disagree; 5: completely disagree) responses from the children and parents were analyzed to determine the percentage of positive and negative responses.

Data analysis

The preference for either HSAT or PSG was analyzed based on the participant’s overall experience as a percentage of total responses for each Likert scale category. Baseline PROMIS T-scores were analyzed using Mann–Whitney U test and the relationship between PROMIS T- score and preference was analyzed using two-tailed Spearman’s rank. Sensor adjustment, age, and sex were analyzed using two-tailed Spearman’s rank.

Results

HSAT feasibility and acceptability

All 15 participants, median (range) age 6 (2–17 years), completed both HSAT and PSG on the first attempt, within 5 days between tests. Participants included 10 (67%) female, 8 (53%) Black, 4 (27%) White, and 3 (20%) not reported; 2 participants (16%) were Latinx (Table 1). Fourteen (93%) parents completed all questionnaires (Supplement Table S1); 8 (53%) children were able to complete the questionnaires. For the 7 remaining children, parents reported that they were too young to answer independently.

All 14 parents who completed questionnaires reported that HSAT was easy to use and feasible for home use (Figure 1A) and 13 (92.8%) of the parents believed they could administer HSAT at home. Twelve parents (86%) strongly agreed that they would welcome the use of HSAT, 11 (78.6%) reported that they liked HSAT and were willing to use it in the future and 11 (78.6%) also said that they approved of HSAT. A few parents (n = 3, 21.4%) were undecided regarding their approval of HSAT (Figure 1B) and if they liked the home sleep test (n = 1, 7.1%). Two (14.3%) of the 14 parents selected “completely disagree” when asked whether they found HSAT likeable and whether they would welcome its use. Similar to the responses from their parents, the children also expressed positive attitudes toward HSAT (Figure 1C). Of the eight children who completed the questionnaire, seven (87.5%) found it likable and six (75.0%) welcomed and approved its use. However, one child (12.5%) completely disagreed that HSAT was acceptable, while one other (12.5%) was unable to decide if they liked using the HSAT.

Test preference and ease of use

All 15 parents and 14 of the children completed the preference questionnaire. Seven parents (46.7%) found HSAT easier compared to six (40%) who found PSG easier with two (13%) who were unsure. Overall, nine (60%) parents preferred HSAT. Interestingly, seven (46.7%) parents reported their child’s sleep was closer to a normal night during HSAT compared to five (33.3%) during PSG and three (20%) were unsure (Figure 2A).For the children (Figure 2B), five (35.7%) found HSAT easier, seven (50%) found PSG easier, and two (14.3%) were unsure. Sleep disturbances

There was no significant difference in PROMIS Sleep Disturbance T-scores based on baseline parent-reported and child-reported questionnaires (p = .097; median (range), parent: 63 (44,75), n = 15; child, 57 (47,68), n = 8). Based on the T-score obtained by the parent PROMIS questionnaire, three children (20%) were in the normal range, one (6.7%) with mild, three (20%) with moderate, and eight (53.3%) with severe sleep disturbance. The response from the child PROMIS questionnaire showed three (37.5%) children with T-score in the normal range, two (25%) with mild, two with moderate (25%) and one (12.5%) with severe sleep disturbance (Table 1).

Figure 3 shows the response from the parent on the number of times the sensors required adjustment during the duration of the HSAT. As age increased, fewer adjustments to sensors were required (Figure 3A, Spearman r = –0.8, p = .003), which was independent of the sex (linear regression: R squared = 0.013). Despite adjusting the sensors multiple times, the majority of the parents and children (Figure 3B) did not find it overly troublesome to manage (sleep disturbance level vs sensor adjustment – Parent: Spearman r = 0.031; p = .92; Child: Spearman r = 0.62; p = .13). Of the fourteen parents, five (36%) parents did not need to adjust any of the sensors throughout the HSAT, however some (21%, n = 3/14) had to adjust the sensors more than five times (Figure 3B, Table 2). Of the HSAT sensors (Table 2), the nasal cannula (43%, 6/14) and pulse oximeter on the finger (43%, 6/14) required the most adjustments from the parent (N = 14). Wire tangles required the least adjustments (parent: 14%, 2/14) during the entire recording period.

Among 14 parents, 5 (36%) reported no adjustments, while some (21%, n = 3/14) adjusted sensors more than five times (Figure 3B, Table 2). The nasal cannula (43%, 6/14) and finger pulse oximeter (43%, 6/14) required the most adjustments; wire tangles required the fewest (parent: 14%, 2/14).

There was no difference between sleep disturbance (assessed using the PROMIS T Score) and the parent preference for HSAT or PSG (Spearman’s rank; rs = –0.26, p = .35). In the 11 participants whose PROMIS T score above 60 (indicating sleep disturbance), 64% (7/11) preferred HSAT and 27% (3/11) preferred PSG and remaining one were unsure. This suggests the parents preferred HSAT when there was moderate to severe sleep disturbance.

Structured interview

Thirteen parents completed the semi-structured interview following completion of both HSAT and PSG. During the structured interview, most of the parents expressed positive feedback regarding HSAT (Figure 4A), while several had negative comments. Although they favored the use of HSAT, parents reported a few methods of how the device could be improved (Figure 4B). Most commonly, parents suggested improving the fitting of the sensors, including the nasal cannula (29%), pulse oximeter (24%) and belts (6%). Additional suggestions included minimizing sensors (12%), fixing adhesive discomfort (12%), stabilizing EEG leads (6%), and upgrading to a device that was “ready to wear” (6%). In addition, four (29%) parents suggested that HSAT could be improved by traveling less or not at all to the hospital for the HSAT set-up. Interestingly, four parents (29%) were inquisitive about the accuracy of the results obtained from HSAT in OSA diagnosis.

Discussion

This pilot study is among the first to assess parent and child-reported acceptability of pediatric HSAT. The results of this pilot study provide insight into the feasibility, acceptability, and preference of HSAT as a diagnostic tool for pediatric OSA. Our findings demonstrate that parents welcomed the use of HSAT, reporting it to be feasible and acceptable. Interestingly, results comparing ease of use and preference for HSAT versus PSG were mixed, with more parents preferring HSAT and finding it easier to use than PSG, but the reverse being true for the child participants. Over 64% of children experienced sleep quality similar to or better than usual nights with HSAT, highlighting its impact on patient experience. Positive feedback from children and parents emphasized the comfort and convenience of HSAT, and many families provided suggestions to improve HSAT testing.

High acceptability of HSAT can be attributed to several key factors: its non-invasive nature and the convenience of home-based administration, the flexibility of HSAT recording time, and the availability of support videos and an available laboratory helpline provided additional support, empowering the parents, and boosting their confidence in using the device. With regard to preference, most parents preferred HSAT while most children preferring PSG. While this discrepancy needs to be further explored with a larger cohort of dyads, we speculate that the difference could be due to the comfort and convenience of home-based testing for the parent, while children might feel this as an intrusion onto their safe space.

All 15 children successfully completed the HSAT [15], aligning with the previous studies reporting a 91% initial completion rate in two larger studies that utilized HSAT with EEG in children [18, 19]. While the small sample size limited the statistical power of the findings, they provide valuable insight for future research.

The questionnaires allowed insight into alterations to improve acceptance of HSAT. This could be addressed through the following approaches that were highlighted by the questionnaires. Firstly, modification to the equipment, such as the nasal cannula and pulse oximeter, can be explored to improve their ability to remain securely in place throughout night, potentially enhancing the test’s acceptance. While most parents (78%) approved HSAT, two of them expressed concerns about wire issues, which might have impacted their overall satisfaction. Two children consistently disapproved of HSAT. In agreement to the reported concerns listed by parents in Table 2, during their structured interview, most parents suggested improvements in the nasal cannula and the pulse oximeter to increase comfort for their child. Few suggested ideas such as – oxygen sensors as gloves so that they don’t fall off or fixing the sensor to the leg, instead of hand. Overall, most of the suggestions that involved HSAT improvement were toward the sensors and a few of them suggested simpler/less bulky/wireless instrument. These outcomes from the sleep disturbance questionnaires resulted in valuable ideas to consider for modifications.

In our cohort, parents of younger children reported more problems with sensors, especially the nasal cannula and pulse oximeter, possibly due to difficulty with fitting the child’s small size [20]. Age of children should be assessed in larger studies of HSAT in terms of feasibility, preference, and the amount of troubleshooting required by parents. Importantly, identification of which subpopulations or factors influence preference, and feasibility could be used when considering which test to choose.

It is important to also note the limitations of this study. Firstly, due to the young age of some participants, it was necessary to rely on parental responses to assess their preferences, which may have influenced the overall results. Future studies could consider child-friendly visual tools or interviews to directly capture the perspectives of younger children. Additional work should randomize the sequence of testing to avoid order bias in responses. Secondly, our protocol also involved dual in-lab setup with HSAT and PSG and in-person setup of comprehensive HSAT with EEG, which could have influenced outcomes compared with more minimal HSAT tests with fewer sensors. Thirdly, the small sample size of this pilot study did not allow for subgroup analysis, and the results should be interpreted with caution. Our study may limit the generalizability of our findings to groups that were not included, especially infants and children with specific medical and developmental disorders. For example, our cohort was predominantly female (75%), while OSA is shown to affect children equally or have a slight predominance for males [21–24]. Larger studies with diverse age groups and specific enquiries about the reported concerns are needed to avoid the outcomes that could be largely influenced by the small cohort size. Additionally, collecting data on other neurobehavioral comorbidities associated with OSA in children [25] could be beneficial. This is particularly relevant considering the challenges of wire placement during the in-laboratory PSG for these children [10]. The convenience of home-based administration may reduce stress and anxiety in these individuals, as well as the flexibility of scheduling allowing it to align with the child’s typical sleep schedule. In addition, utilizing a theoretical framework could have provided qualitative or mixed-methods results to help better inform treatment preferences and acceptability [26, 27].

HSAT is specific for detecting moderate-to-severe OSA in children, but that overall accuracy varies depending on OSA threshold, child’s age, and sensors utilized during testing [12]. Comprehensive reviews of various HSAT devices and their diagnostic accuracy can be found in the following reviews [28–30].

However, questionnaires assessing sleep habits away from home and sleep restlessness may influence the suitability of HSAT. There are no validated instruments evaluating the acceptability and preference questionnaires for a diagnostic device that has been validated for the pediatric population, in the AASM guidelines. These factors could be validated as screening tools to optimize diagnostic choices for individual children. Future studies should investigate lager cohort size, wider age-range, detailed demographic correlations with modality preference, children with intellectual disabilities (e.g.: Down Syndrome).

This study may help guide future use of HSAT in children. Beyond matching PSG accuracy, a pediatric OSA test must also be feasible and acceptable to both parents and children, which are the key factors for manufacturers and policymakers. Although the cohort was small, the findings can still inform HSAT equipment modifications. Families most often reported the nasal cannula and pulse oximeter as bothersome, and these channels also tend to have the most artifact, [13, 18], suggesting a need for more child-friendly sensor designs or evaluation of systems that use alternative channels.

Collecting parent/child feedback is essential to move HSAT from a research tool to a routine pediatric clinical standard, benefiting manufacturers, clinicians, insurers, and hospital administrators. Feedback on cannula adhesion and cable management could drive pediatric-specific redesigns, including for children with special needs or sensory sensitivities. Clinicians could use this information to identify families likely to do well with HSAT versus PSG and to flag those who may need additional pre-test training or video tutorials to improve compliance. Insurers may value evidence of higher HSAT completion rates to support cost-effective first-line testing. Administrators can use feedback about barriers like travel to sleep labs to improve equity for rural or low-income families. Parents also noted lab-based set-up inconvenience; fully remote setups warrant further study for feasibility, preference, and accuracy. In conclusion, HSAT is a valuable tool for diagnosing pediatric OSA, offering numerous advantages over traditional in-laboratory PSG. It is preferred by parents and by addressing issues with wiring, especially in younger children, HSAT can be further optimized for personalized care and patient experience in OSA diagnosis.
