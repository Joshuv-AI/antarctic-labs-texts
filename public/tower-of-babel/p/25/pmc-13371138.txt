Ciliary muscle thickness and intraocular pressure before and after latanoprost
Authors: Emily Slywka, Chiu-Yen Kao, Melissa D. Bailey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To test the hypothesis that a larger, latanoprost-induced intraocular pressure (IOP) reduction is associated with a thicker ciliary muscle.Methods:Participants (N = 31) were healthy with the following details: mean ± SD age 24.8 ± 3.3 years. The ciliary muscle was imaged with anterior segment optical coherence tomography and Goldmann IOP was measured at baseline and 12–18 h after latanoprost instillation. Linear regression models of baseline IOP and IOP reduction as predicted by baseline ciliary muscle thickness were performed. Partial correlation coefficients were calculated for model predictor variables.Results:The mean ± SD latanoprost-induced IOP reduction was −2.6 ± 2.1 mmHg (t = −7.1, p < 0.0001). A larger reduction in IOP was significantly associated with thicker baseline maximum ciliary muscle thickness (CMTMAX) after controlling for age and baseline IOP (R2 = 0.37; CMTMAX: −0.02, p = 0.01; age: 0.23, p = 0.08; baseline IOP: −0.39, p = 0.003), and was also significantly associated with thicker baseline ciliary muscle thickness 1 mm posterior to the scleral spur (CMT1) after controlling for age and baseline IOP (R2 = 0.44; CMT1: −0.02, p = 0.002; age: 0.22, p = 0.06; baseline IOP: −0.43, p = 0.0008). Partial correlation coefficients between IOP reduction and baseline CMTMAX or CMT1 were −0.47 and −0.56, respectively.Conclusions:Thicker baseline CMTMAX/CMT1 was associated with larger latanoprost-induced IOP reductions and lower baseline IOP. Future studies should determine the clinical usefulness of ciliary muscle dimensions as a predictor of the response to latanoprost in glaucoma patients.

INTRODUCTION

Although latanoprost is proven to significantly lower intraocular pressure (IOP) in most patients, it is not effective for everyone.1-5 According to an extensive review by Blondeau et al., 4.1%–51.5% of individuals fail to show a 15% or greater IOP reduction with latanoprost.6 This is well below the typical treatment goal of at least 30%.7

Latanoprost lowers IOP by increasing uveoscleral outflow, a process in which aqueous humor drains from the eye through the ciliary stroma instead of Schlemm's Canal. Uveoscleral outflow was discovered in primate eyes by Bill in 1965 and confirmed in human eyes by Toris et al. in 1993.8,9 Prostaglandin analogs like latanoprost increase this unconventional outflow pathway without affecting conventional outflow.8,10 It is widely accepted that latanoprost-induced increases in uveoscleral outflow are mediated by remodeling of the ciliary body tissue, as the drug increases extracellular spaces between muscle fibers.11 On a cellular level, latanoprost increases matrix metalloproteinase concentrations, which are responsible for collagen turnover in the extracellular matrix of the ciliary muscle.12,13 In turn, several collagen types are reduced after prostaglandin application in human eyes.12-14

Latanoprost's variable efficacy indicates the need for a method to predict which individuals are more or less likely to meaningfully respond to the drug. Currently, there is no way for a clinician to know if an insufficient reduction in IOP is due to drug efficacy or the often poor glaucoma treatment compliance.15 Given that the ciliary muscle is an established part of the uveoscleral outflow pathway, the goal of this study was to evaluate the relationship between latanoprost-induced IOP reduction and ciliary muscle dimensions. A previous study by Marchini et al.1 also explored this topic by measuring the ciliary body thickness with ultrasound biomicroscopy in glaucoma patients before and after a week of latanoprost therapy, but they did not find a statistically significant difference.

Given that imaging the muscle with anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) is now possible, the relationship between ciliary muscle dimensions, IOP, and prostaglandin analogs should be revisited. The present study sought to evaluate the general relationship between IOP and ciliary muscle thickness and to test the following hypothesis: a larger IOP reduction with latanoprost is found in eyes with a thicker ciliary muscle. If this hypothesis is correct, this relationship could be further explored as a method by which ciliary muscle thickness is used as a predictive variable for latanoprost-induced IOP response.1

METHODS

The research adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ohio State University Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained. Written informed consent was obtained from the participants after explanation of the nature and possible consequences of the study prior to enrollment in the study.

Healthy volunteers aged 18–34 years were recruited. This was an unfunded, pilot study, and participants in this age group are more readily available to complete the precisely timed study visits (see below). A previous study by Larsson16 demonstrated a robust IOP reduction with a single latanoprost dose that peaked for 12–18 h following administration, supporting the protocol visit timing and testing of young adults. Additionally, this age group was chosen to avoid the potential atrophy of the ciliary muscle that can occur with aging and disuse17,18 so that a hypothesis specific to the ciliary muscle could be tested. Participants were deemed eligible if they lacked history of ocular surgery or conditions other than refractive error by self-report and were not pregnant by a urine sample test.

The study consisted of three visits over a window of 2 to 7 days. The baseline and final visits were scheduled during the same time of day so that the IOP data were free from the confounding effects of circadian rhythm.19 The second visit was an intervention visit that was completed in the late afternoon prior to the final study visit. At the intervention visit, biological female participants were screened for pregnancy, and a single dose of 0.005% latanoprost at this single intervention visit was instilled into the right eye of all participants. Participants' eyes were closed with puncta occluded for 3 minutes after drop instillation.

All images were captured using AS-OCT (Visante, Carl Zeiss Meditec, https://www.zeiss.com). Two anterior segment images were taken per eye following the manufacturer's protocol. Central corneal thickness was measured from these images because it has been shown to be related to IOP measurements.20,21 Alongside the anterior chamber images that the AS-OCT was designed to capture, AS-OCT was also used to capture four images of cross-sectional, nasal ciliary muscle. This technique for imaging the ciliary muscle with an AS-OCT has been used previously.22-27 To consistently align images, the fixation target was placed at the same height as the participant's fixating eye. The target was also carefully positioned at the participant's far point to minimize accommodation-associated changes in ciliary muscle shape.22 Each cross-sectional ciliary muscle image was imported into a custom software program (MATLAB, MathWorks) for extraction of ciliary muscle dimensions by a semi-automatic algorithm that has been previously described by this lab.22-27 Figure 1 shows an example image with thickness measurements that were made along the length of the muscle. Dimensions included in analysis were the point of maximum thickness (CMTMAX), the thickness at 1 mm (CMT1), 2 mm (CMT2) and 3 mm (CMT3) posterior to the scleral spur, and the cross-sectional area (CMA3) between the scleral spur and CMT3.

IOP was measured for the right and left eyes at both the baseline and final visits via Goldmann tonometry performed by an experienced examiner. This method was chosen as it is the gold standard measure of IOP, demonstrating better repeatability compared to less involved methods such as Tonopen and iCare.28,29 The latanoprost-induced IOP reduction was calculated as final IOP—baseline IOP, so that negative values represent a reduction in IOP.

Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at The Ohio State University.30,31 REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies, providing (1) an intuitive interface for validated data capture; (2) audit trails for tracking data manipulation and export procedures; (3) automated export procedures for seamless data downloads to common statistical packages; and (4) procedures for data integration and interoperability with external sources.

The statistical significance of changes in IOP and ciliary muscle dimensions following latanoprost instillation was determined with paired t-tests. Relationships between various IOP measurements and ciliary muscle dimensions were tested with Pearson correlation coefficients. Linear regression models were performed to evaluate the relationship between baseline IOP and baseline ciliary muscle dimensions while controlling for central corneal thickness. Furthermore, linear regression of the latanoprost-induced IOP reduction and both baseline ciliary muscle dimensions and the change in ciliary muscle dimensions were performed while controlling for baseline IOP and age.

RESULTS

Participant mean ± SD age was 24.8 ± 3.3 years (range 18–34 years). The sample was comprised mostly of biological females (22 biological females and nine biological males, self-reported) and white individuals (26 white, three Asian, two African American). The mean ± SD central corneal thickness for all participants was 531 ± 37 μm. No adverse events were reported aside from eight cases of new, mild conjunctival hyperemia in the right eye at the final visit. Mean ± SD ciliary muscle dimensions and IOP for the baseline and final visits and the change with latanoprost instillation are displayed in Table 1. In univariate analyses, only the latanoprost-induced IOP reduction (right eye) was significantly different from zero (Table 1). Additionally, in univariate analysis, baseline IOP was correlated with the latanoprost-induced IOP reduction (r = −0.44, p = 0.01).

In univariate analysis, baseline CMTMAX and CMT1 were correlated with baseline IOP. Baseline CMT2 and CMT3 were not correlated with baseline IOP (Figure 2), and a sample of approximately 65 participants would be required for the relationship to be statistically significant with 80% power. Additionally, baseline CMA3 was also correlated with baseline IOP (r = 0.51, p = 0.004). For illustration purposes, Figure 3 is an overlay of ciliary muscle images demonstrating the correlations shown in Figure 2. Linear regression models controlling for central corneal thickness did not improve the univariate models of baseline IOP and baseline CMTMAX (R2 = 0.28) nor baseline IOP and baseline CMT1 (R2 = 0.29).

A larger latanoprost-induced IOP reduction was significantly associated with a thicker baseline anterior ciliary muscle (CMTMAX and CMT1) when controlling for age and baseline IOP (Table 2, Figure 4). Models with CMT2 and CMT3 did not reach significance with this sample size (data not shown). Figure 5 shows the partial correlation coefficients showing the individual relationship of each variable with the latanoprost-induced IOP reduction from models in Table 2 to provide the reader with an estimate of the relative contribution of each variable in the models in Table 2. The latanoprost-induced IOP reduction was not significantly associated with changes in ciliary muscle nor anterior scleral thickness measurements (data not shown).

DISCUSSION

This study provided preliminary evidence that a thicker baseline ciliary muscle was associated with a larger latanoprost-induced IOP reduction. Because the ciliary muscle is part of the pathway for uveoscleral outflow, this is consistent with recent work showing that latanoprost increases uveoscleral outflow.32,33 This association was only apparent when controlling for baseline IOP, as the IOP reduction was also significantly correlated with baseline IOP. To further quantify the relative contributions of age, ciliary muscle thickness, and baseline IOP on the latanoprost-induced IOP reduction, we calculated partial correlation coefficients. This analysis revealed relatively equal correlations between IOP reduction versus baseline IOP and IOP reduction versus ciliary muscle thickness, further suggesting an important role for ciliary muscle thickness.

Not all patients respond to latanoprost and previous studies have failed to identify significant predictive factors.1,34,35 Clinicians start patients on latanoprost, evaluate their IOP response after several weeks, and then either continue the treatment or switch medications. Nonetheless, it is currently difficult to distinguish whether latanoprost is ineffective due to poor compliance or because the patient does not respond to the drug. A simple and quick AS-OCT scan of the ciliary muscle to differentiate between responders and nonresponders might be a valuable tool for clinicians. At this time, however, most eye care practices do not have this technology available for use.

Marchini et al. found no relationship between ciliary muscle thickness and IOP reduction with latanoprost,1 although their ultrasound methodology may not have been able to distinguish between the border of the ciliary muscle itself and the ciliary processes, especially anteriorly. The use of AS-OCT imaging facilitated finding an association. AS-OCT is able to distinguish between the ciliary muscle and its processes,27 which is an important technological advancement.

Baseline IOP was associated with latanoprost-induced IOP reduction in our sample. This is likely due to the floor effect of episcleral venous pressure on IOP. IOP cannot drop below episcleral venous pressure, which is typically 8–11 mmHg.36,37 Thus, a lower baseline IOP means less potential for an IOP reduction. For this reason, our study controlled for baseline IOP, which revealed relationships that were not significant in previous work.1

We additionally discovered a novel relationship where lower baseline IOP was associated with a thicker baseline ciliary muscle. To the authors' knowledge, this is the first study to identify such a relationship. This relationship may be due to increased spacing between the ciliary muscle fibers allowing more aqueous humor to escape the globe through the ciliary muscle itself.9,13,38,39 Naturally larger muscles may have more fibers, and therefore more spaces. Alternatively, thicker muscles could be due to wider-than-typical spaces between muscle fibers, and not more muscle fibers. These hypotheses warrant additional study.

Controlling for central corneal thickness did not improve any models in the sample. Compared to the Ocular Hypertension Treatment Study, where the population mean was 551 ± 36 μm, the present study's sample had a relatively thin central corneal thickness of 531 ± 37 μm.40 Due to this unique sample characteristic, the variability in the present study was not sufficient to demonstrate a lack of support for the well-established relationship between central corneal thickness and IOP.20,21 We expect central corneal thickness to be a significant contributor to models in the future, with more diverse samples.

Conversely, participant age did improve the models of IOP prediction in our sample. This is unsurprising considering previous studies have demonstrated a significant relationship between age and IOP, although the details of such a relationship are debated.41,42 Additionally, age seemed to correlate with anatomical differences in the ciliary muscle. In vivo analysis in some studies has demonstrated an antero-inwards displacement of the muscle mass and an increase in maximum width in older individuals, whereas other studies demonstrate ciliary atrophy and decreased area with age.17,18

The present study was an initial exploration into the relationship between ciliary muscle thickness and IOP in young, healthy individuals. In the authors' experience, some of the ciliary muscle thickness data were more variable than expected between images, possibly due to residual uncontrolled accommodation, as participants were not administered cycloplegic drops prior to imaging as has been recommended previously.43 They were instead instructed to view a nonaccommodative target several meters away. Still, we do not know if participants accurately relaxed their blur accommodation, as we did not monitor pupil size or track gaze in this study.

Additionally, their tonic accommodation may have been active and fluctuating during the imaging process.44 Ciliary muscle thickness variations created by any type of accommodation would have appeared randomly across study visit and would be unlikely to create false trends. Still, they may have created noise in this dataset. Despite understanding this limitation, the authors chose to avoid cycloplegia in this study because doing so may artificially influence IOP and ciliary muscle tone, which was an essential outcome measure. Specifically, atropine has been demonstrated to relax ciliary muscles in animal models, phenylephrine can increase IOP in humans, and tropicamide with phenylephrine can increase pachymetry values in humans.9,45-48 In future study of older patients who typically use latanoprost, concerns about accommodation will, of course, be mitigated by presbyopia.

The present study was also limited by its lack of diversity in central corneal thickness, made apparent via comparison with larger samples.40 A more comprehensive study analyzing more individuals may better quantify the relationship between IOP and central corneal thickness that is currently represented in the existing literature.20,21 Nonetheless, in the authors' experience, the ciliary muscle thickness range of the sample does represent the general population, so the models of relationships involving these values and IOP provide reason to further investigate correlations in broader studies.

In the present study, there was a relatively small, overall predictive effect of ciliary muscle thickness on latanoprost-induced reduction in IOP. Considering the singular dose of latanoprost administered to the participants, this was not unexpected. In a future study, where participants use the drug longer, the predictive effect may only increase. In the present study, however, the study population consisted of many women of childbearing age. Thus, examiners did not want to risk potential harmful side effects of the drug in this particular study, even with an initial negative pregnancy test.49,50 Future studies in older individuals, where pregnancy is not a concern, should aim to administer latanoprost for a longer period of time to determine how well ciliary muscle thickness may predict the latanoprost-induced reduction in IOP.

Perhaps most significantly, the participants of the present study do not match the demographic of a typical latanoprost user. Older eyes demonstrate different morphological characteristics compared to the young, healthy eyes studied here. With aging, there is antero-inwards tissue displacement, and muscle atrophy with subsequent connective tissue augmentation.17,18 The present, preliminary study did not have the funding and resources to examine older individuals or people with glaucoma or ocular hypertension for longer periods of time, but these have always been the desired next steps of the investigation. Although the present study trends, which were independent of any pathological or age-related changes in ciliary muscle anatomy, will inform future research, further study on older and diseased eyes is an important next step.

CONCLUSIONS

This study provides evidence for novel relationships involving ciliary muscle thickness, baseline IOP, and the effect of latanoprost on IOP. In the present sample, baseline IOP was inversely correlated with baseline ciliary muscle thickness. Furthermore, baseline ciliary muscle thickness was associated with latanoprost-induced IOP reduction. Specifically, thicker ciliary muscles were associated with larger IOP reductions than ciliary muscles that were thinner. These relationships should be evaluated in larger, older, and more diverse samples. If replicated in those samples, ciliary muscle dimensions may assist in selecting the best candidates for prostaglandin analog glaucoma therapy.
