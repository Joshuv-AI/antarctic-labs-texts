LDL oxidation and cerebrovascular aging: mechanisms of endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and vascular cognitive impairment and dementia
Authors: Jennifer Ihuoma, Madison Milan, Sharon Negri, Eva Troyano-Rodriguez, Rakesh Rudraboina, Aleksandra Kosmider, Shantipriya Awasthi, Anna Csiszar, Zoltan Ungvari, Andriy Yabluchanskiy, Priya Balasubramanian, Stefano Tarantini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vascular cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID) is increasingly recognized as one of the leading causes of cognitive decline in aging populations. VCID is driven by the interplay of endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation. Aging exacerbates cerebrovascular damage by impairing endothelial function, compromising the blood-brain barrier (BBB), and promoting neuroinflammation, all of which contribute to the progression of small vessel disease (SVD) and white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), which are hallmarks of VCID. Epidemiological evidence suggests that aging is associated with modifications in circulating low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and high-density lipoproteins (HDL), particularly oxidation, which further accelerates neurovascular pathology. Oxidized LDL (oxLDL) disrupts endothelial homeostasis, enhances leukocyte adhesion, and triggers chronic inflammatory signaling through microglial and astrocytic activation. Additionally, oxLDL-mediated oxidative stress exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction and epigenetic alterations in cerebrovascular cells, reinforcing a cycle of vascular injury and neurodegeneration. These mechanisms contribute to neurovascular uncoupling, cerebral hypoperfusion, and cognitive decline in aging individuals. This review examines the pathophysiological role of oxLDL in VCID, with a focus on its impact on endothelial integrity, BBB function, neuroinflammation, and lipid-mediated neurodegenerative processes. Furthermore, we discuss emerging therapeutic strategies targeting LDL oxidation, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction as potential interventions to mitigate the burden of VCID in aging populations.

Introduction

Aging and hyperlipidemia are two major risk factors for vascular cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID) in the elderly, yet their combined effects on cerebrovascular function remain poorly understood. Among modifiable vascular risk factors, hypertension consistently demonstrates the strongest association with cerebral small vessel disease, white matter hyperintensities, and vascular dementia, with midlife hypertension conferring approximately a 1.5–2-fold increased risk of later-life dementia in large longitudinal cohorts [1,2]. Diabetes and metabolic syndrome are also associated with increased dementia risk, typically in the range of 1.3–1.6-fold depending on cohort and duration of exposure [3]. By comparison, midlife hypercholesterolemia has been linked to a more modest but significant increase in dementia incidence, with meta-analyses suggesting that each ∼1 mmol/L elevation in LDL-C is associated with approximately 5–10% higher dementia risk [4]. Aging itself remains the dominant non-modifiable determinant of cognitive decline, interacting synergistically with vascular exposures [1]. While hypertension likely carries the greatest population-attributable risk for VCID, hyperlipidemia, and particularly LDL oxidation, may represent a mechanistically distinct and modifiable contributor to microvascular dysfunction through pathways involving oxidative stress, endothelial senescence, and blood–brain barrier vulnerability. While aging leads to progressive cerebrovascular dysfunction through endothelial senescence [5], oxidative stress [6], neuroinflammation [7], and impaired autoregulation [8], hyperlipidemia exacerbates these processes by promoting vascular lipid deposition [9], systemic inflammation, and increased production of oxidized lipids [10] (Fig. 1). Despite their individual contributions to cognitive decline, the synergistic mechanisms through which aging and dyslipidemia accelerate neurovascular damage remain understudied, leaving critical gaps in our understanding of their interplay in the pathogenesis of age-related VCID. The aging vasculature undergoes profound structural and functional changes, including decreased endothelial function [11], increased arterial stiffness [12], reduced nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability [13], and chronic low-grade inflammation [14], that impair cerebral blood flow (CBF) regulation [8,15,16] and contribute to blood–brain barrier (BBB) breakdown [8,17]. In parallel, hyperlipidemia induces vascular oxidative stress and inflammation through mechanisms involving LDL oxidation, foam cell formation, and dysregulated lipid metabolism [18]. Recent data indicate that small dense LDL particles have higher atherogenic potential and greater susceptibility to oxidation than larger LDL [19], while electronegative LDL (L5) has been linked to cognitive impairment in human studies [20]. These harmful subfractions may disproportionately contribute to cerebrovascular injury in aging. Although the separate impacts of aging and dyslipidemia on cerebrovascular health are well-established, the molecular pathways through which they synergistically damage the neurovascular unit (NVU) remain largely unexplored. LDL and its oxidized form, oxLDL, are increasingly recognized as central mediators of cerebrovascular aging and disease [21]. Despite the brain's own cholesterol metabolism, aging-associated BBB vulnerability permits infiltration of oxLDL into the CNS, compromising cerebrovascular function [22]. Peripheral dyslipidemia can thus exert profound effects on cerebral endothelial health [23]. It is important to clarify that under physiological conditions, circulating LDL and oxLDL do not freely cross the BBB [24]. The adult brain relies primarily on locally synthesized cholesterol, and BBB endothelial cells maintain highly regulated transport systems that restrict nonspecific lipoprotein entry. Therefore, oxLDL access to the central nervous system is not a physiological process. Rather, exposure of the NVU to circulating oxLDL occurs predominantly in the context of BBB dysfunction, which may arise from aging, hypertension, metabolic disease, inflammation, or cerebral small vessel pathology. Age-associated endothelial senescence, tight junction disorganization, and increased caveolar or vesicular transcytosis create permissive conditions that allow plasma-derived factors, including modified lipoproteins, to access the perivascular space and potentially the parenchyma. Much of the experimental evidence demonstrating oxLDL accumulation in brain tissue derives from models with established barrier impairment, such as hyperlipidemic or LDL receptor–deficient mice, ischemic injury models, or other pathological states, rather than from healthy, intact systems. Elevated LDL, and especially oxLDL, harms the brain microvasculature by promoting BBB breakdown, chronic inflammation, and neurodegeneration [25]. OxLDL induces endothelial dysfunction, increases barrier permeability, and enhances monocyte adhesion and microglial activation [18]. Mechanistic studies highlight lectin-like oxidized LDL receptor-1 (LOX-1) as a key receptor in oxLDL signaling within endothelial cells, promoting NF-κB activation, oxidative stress, and BBB disruption in aged animals [26]. Moreover, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9), beyond regulating LDL receptor recycling, mediates inflammatory responses and oxLDL uptake, representing another age-dependent driver of neurovascular dysfunction [27]. Despite this growing evidence, longitudinal and mechanistic studies exploring chronic dyslipidemia's impact on cerebrovascular aging and cognition remain sparse. Current therapeutic strategies for managing hyperlipidemia focus primarily on lowering the amount of cholesterol carried within LDL (LDL-C), with statins serving as the cornerstone of both primary and secondary prevention. While these agents effectively reduce LDL-C levels and lower the incidence of major cardiovascular events, a substantial proportion of patients continue to experience adverse vascular outcomes, highlighting the existence of residual risk. This has prompted growing interest in the role of modified lipoproteins, particularly oxidized and electronegative forms of LDL, which exert potent pro-inflammatory and pro-atherogenic effects independent of LDL-C concentration. While VCID and AD are often discussed together, they are mechanistically distinct entities that frequently coexist in aging populations. VCID is primarily driven by cerebrovascular pathology, including small vessel disease, BBB disruption, endothelial dysfunction, hypoperfusion, and white matter injury, whereas AD is neuropathologically defined by amyloid-β plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles. However, these conditions share convergent pathways such as oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and BBB breakdown. In this context, oxLDL-mediated vascular injury most directly aligns with the vascular axis of cognitive decline and VCID pathogenesis through endothelial dysfunction, LOX-1 signaling, and neurovascular uncoupling. Its contribution to AD is likely indirect, acting as a vascular amplifier that may impair amyloid clearance, disrupt APOE-mediated lipid handling, and lower the threshold for neurodegenerative processes in mixed dementia states. This review synthesizes recent high-impact research on LDL/oxLDL effects in brain endothelial cells, focusing on BBB integrity, vascular aging, and links to Alzheimer's disease (AD) and VCID. We explore the synergy between aging and dyslipidemia in accelerating brain endothelial injury and subsequent cognitive decline, summarize preclinical and clinical advancements, including emerging therapeutic interventions, and discuss experimental models, controversies, and future directions for targeting LDL/oxLDL-mediated neurovascular dysfunction. Throughout this review, the term “elevated” or “high” LDL is used with explicit contextual definition. In human studies, elevated LDL-C refers to clinically recognized thresholds (e.g., ≥130 mg/dL [3.4 mmol/L] as borderline high and ≥160 mg/dL [4.1 mmol/L] as high). In murine models, however, LDL levels depend strongly on genotype and diet. ApoE−/− and Ldlr−/− mice maintained on standard chow exhibit moderately elevated circulating cholesterol relative to wild-type controls, whereas Western or high-fat diet feeding markedly amplifies LDL and total cholesterol levels, often several-fold above wild-type values. Persistent vascular risk after lipid-lowering therapies and the case against oxLDL

The current therapeutic landscape for individuals with hyperlipidemia is anchored by statins, which inhibit β-Hydroxy β-methylglutaryl-CoA (HMG-CoA) reductase and effectively lower LDL levels by 30–50%, depending on dose and potency [42]. Statins remain the cornerstone of lipid-lowering therapy and reduce major adverse cardiovascular events by approximately 20–25% per 1.0 mmol/L reduction in LDL-C, as demonstrated by large-scale meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials (Cholesterol Treatment Trialists’ Collaboration) [46,47]. However, the absolute risk reduction varies substantially depending on baseline cardiovascular risk and is generally modest in primary prevention populations. For example, systematic reviews report absolute reductions in all-cause mortality of <1% over approximately five years in lower-risk individuals [70]. Thus, a substantial proportion of treated patients continue to experience vascular events despite optimized LDL-C levels, a phenomenon often referred to as “residual vascular risk.” Importantly, residual risk does not imply inefficacy of statins, but rather reflects the multifactorial nature of vascular disease, including persistent inflammation, oxidative stress, lipoprotein modification, and metabolic dysfunction.

Statins

Statins are the first-line therapy in both primary and secondary prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), supported by robust clinical trial data demonstrating reductions in myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and cardiovascular mortality [71,72]. Despite the clinical success of lipid-lowering therapies, particularly statins, in reducing circulating LDL-C, their capacity to eliminate vascular risk remains incomplete. For instance, the 2022 Cochrane meta-analysis of 62 trials with over 120,000 participants reported that statin therapy reduced all-cause mortality by only 0.5%, with a number needed to treat (NNT) of 217 over five years [45]. Similarly, the Cholesterol Treatment Trialists’ (CTT) Collaboration meta-analysis [46] demonstrated relative cardiovascular risk reductions of ∼20–25%, but modest absolute benefits, especially in low-to intermediate-risk populations. More recently, it has been reported that statin therapy reduced major vascular events by ∼21% per 1.0 mmol/L LDL-C decrease across all ages, although no significant benefit was observed in primary prevention among those >75 years (Residual Risk ≈ 0.92, 95% CI 0.73–1.16) [48]. A growing body of evidence suggests that this “residual risk” may be driven, in part, by incomplete suppression of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL), a pro-inflammatory, pro-atherogenic lipoprotein derivative that is not adequately neutralized by LDL-C lowering alone. Although meta-analytic data confirm that high-intensity statins and statin-ezetimibe combinations reduce circulating oxLDL (Standard Mean Difference (SMD) −0.675 and −0.220, respectively [43], clinical and plaque-level studies suggest that oxLDL often remains elevated in high oxidative/inflammatory states, even when LDL-C is optimized, implying residual oxidative risk persists beyond conventional lipid lowering [49,50]. Notably, oxLDL promotes endothelial dysfunction, monocyte adhesion, and BBB breakdown via LOX-1 and Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling pathways [49,50], mechanisms not directly targeted by conventional lipid-lowering drugs. In the REPRIEVE trial (n = 7769), a statin reduced cardiovascular events in HIV-positive individuals despite low baseline LDL-C, with evidence suggesting that reductions in oxLDL and vascular inflammation, rather than LDL-C per se, may have driven the observed benefit [44]. Furthermore, oxLDL has been implicated in NVU dysfunction and aging-related cognitive decline. Preclinical data demonstrate that oxLDL disrupts tight junctions, activates microglia, and impairs cerebrovascular reactivity, contributing to the pathophysiology of vascular cognitive impairment and dementia [73,74]. This is mechanistically attributed to oxLDL disruption of BBB integrity through oxidative stress–dependent endothelial injury. In mouse cerebrovascular endothelial cells, oxLDL reduces tight junction proteins such as occludin and ZO-1, increases intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and promotes apoptotic signaling, leading to increased barrier permeability [73]. In vivo, oxLDL exposure impairs cerebrovascular reactivity and increases BBB permeability in rats, effects that are prevented by NADPH oxidase inhibition with apocynin, implicating ROS-dependent mechanisms as central mediators of vascular dysfunction [74].

PCSK9-targeted therapies

In addition to statins, PCSK9-targeted therapies have emerged as potent lipid-lowering strategies with established cardiovascular benefit. Monoclonal antibodies such as evolocumab [51] and alirocumab enhance LDL receptor recycling, producing 50–60% additional reductions in LDL-C when added to statin therapy. Large outcome trials, including FOURIER [52] and ODYSSEY OUTCOMES [53], demonstrate significant reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events, without evidence of cognitive harm in dedicated substudies. Beyond LDL lowering, emerging data suggest that PCSK9 may modulate vascular inflammation and interact with scavenger receptor pathways, potentially influencing oxidized lipoprotein biology. However, direct evidence that PCSK9 inhibition reduces circulating oxLDL levels or improves cerebrovascular outcomes remains limited, and its relevance to VCID specifically requires further investigation. Together, these findings support that lowering LDL-C remains essential, but it may not suffice to fully mitigate vascular risk, especially in the context of aging and neurodegenerative disease. Addressing the residual burden of oxLDL and its downstream signaling pathways may be critical for achieving comprehensive cerebrovascular protection to combat VCID. This will require therapeutic strategies that extend beyond traditional lipid-lowering, including combination regimens, antioxidant approaches, metabolic interventions, and emerging therapies designed to neutralize oxLDL or block LOX-1–mediated vascular injury.

Pathophysiological pathways linking LDL oxidation to VCID

Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) originates from the hepatic secretion of very-low-density lipoproteins, which are progressively converted to intermediate-density lipoproteins and then to LDL through the action of lipoprotein lipase and hepatic lipase [75,76]. LDL particles are the principal cholesterol carriers in circulation, delivering cholesterol to peripheral tissues via LDL receptor–mediated endocytosis [76]. LDL can traverse the endothelium and infiltrate the arterial intima through electrostatic interactions with arterial proteoglycans [77,78]. However, it is still unknown whether this mechanism takes place in leptomeningeal or other cerebral vessels and if it is only present in pathophysiological circumstances. As LDL particles circulate in the blood, they can infiltrate the vascular wall and undergo oxidative modification, forming oxLDL, particularly under oxidative stress in the subendothelial space [18]. Mechanistically, oxidative modification of LDL generates bioactive lipid species (including oxidized phospholipids, lysophosphatidylcholine, and reactive aldehydes such as 4-hydroxynonenal) that increase the affinity of apolipoprotein B–containing particles for scavenger receptors such as LOX-1 and CD36 on endothelial cells, rendering it strongly pro-inflammatory and cytotoxic, capable of activating endothelial cells, upregulating adhesion molecules, and recruiting monocytes [18]. Engagement of these receptors activates NADPH oxidase–dependent ROS generation and redox-sensitive signaling pathways, including MAPK and NF-κB, leading to upregulation of adhesion molecules (VCAM-1, ICAM-1, E-selectin), chemokines such as CCL2, and pro-inflammatory cytokines. Concurrently, oxLDL-induced oxidative stress promotes mitochondrial dysfunction and eNOS uncoupling, reducing nitric oxide bioavailability while increasing superoxide and peroxynitrite formation. These events drive endothelial activation, leukocyte adhesion and transmigration, tight junction destabilization, and ultimately apoptotic or ferroptotic cell injury, shifting the endothelium from a quiescent barrier phenotype to a pro-inflammatory and cytotoxic state. In larger peripheral arteries, oxLDL contributes to atherogenesis by injuring the endothelium, promoting monocyte adhesion and infiltration, and transforming macrophages into cholesterol-laden foam cells through scavenger receptor-mediated uptake (LOX-1, CD36) [18,79]. Similar mechanistic pathways operate in the cerebral microvasculature, where oxidative stress, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction initiate and perpetuate tissue injury characteristic of VCID [80,81]. Importantly, oxidized LDL damages the endothelial barrier integrity [82,83], which could implicate the cerebral microvasculature. Patients with hypercholesterolemia or type 2 diabetes, conditions that promote LDL oxidation, exhibit higher risk for developing AD and other neurodegenerative disorders [84]. Even in individuals with normal total cholesterol, a high proportion of small dense LDL, a subfraction highly prone to oxidation, is associated with increased incidence of cerebral small vessel disease, stroke, and white matter damage [85]. Experimental data by Wendt et al. demonstrate that old vessels exhibit heightened sensitivity to oxLDL-mediated endothelial dysfunction in a LOX-1–dependent manner [26]. In human cohorts, elevated oxLDL levels in older ischemic stroke patients correlate with a 26% higher prevalence of cognitive impairment, independent of cholesterol levels or age [86]. Additionally, among healthy elderly individuals, oxLDL inversely associates with endothelial-dependent hyperemia, as assessed using peripheral arterial tonometry, even when LDL-C is normal, suggesting that aging-related LDL oxidative status contributes to vascular dysfunction beyond lipid abundance [87].

Oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation

Oxidative stress is a key driver of cerebrovascular aging and injury, largely through the excessive production of ROS and resultant lipid peroxidation. Cerebral endothelial cells are equipped with abundant mitochondria and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase enzymes, two major sources of ROS in brain vasculature [88]. At physiological levels, ROS serve as an important signaling molecules that are also involved in regulating vascular tone [89,90], but chronic overproduction of ROS in aging and disease overwhelms antioxidant defenses and causes cellular damage. Lipid peroxidation and oxidized lipids (such as oxLDL) play an especially detrimental role in cerebrovascular aging. OxLDL is both a product and propagator of oxidative stress, known for its ability to induce endothelial dysfunction and often serving as a serological marker of oxidative burden [91]. Circulating oxLDL can affect cerebral vessels, where it engages the LOX-1 on endothelial cells. Activation of LOX-1 by oxLDL triggers a cascade of redox-sensitive signaling: it augments intracellular ROS (partly via NADPH oxidases), activates NF-κB and other inflammatory pathways, and causes endothelial apoptosis and inflammation [91,92](Fig. 2). LOX-1-mediated signaling thus couples oxidized lipids to vascular injury. For example, LOX-1 is upregulated in models of ischemic stroke, where it exacerbates neuronal apoptosis, infarct size, and BBB leakage by promoting cytokine release and leukocyte recruitment [93]. when LOX-1 is absent, liver macrophage (Kupffer cells) scavenger receptors and stabilin-1/-2 receptors present in sinusoidal endothelial cells are involved in the uptake and clearance of oxLDL. Consistently, genetic LOX-1 deletion is protective in rodent models: in stroke-prone hypertensive rats, LOX-1 knockout delayed brain lesions and preserved BBB integrity, suggesting that oxLDL–LOX-1 interactions contribute to cerebrovascular damage [91]. Clinically, elevated oxLDL levels correlate with cognitive decline and dementia risk. Hypercholesterolemia creates a pro-oxidant, pro-inflammatory milieu that damages the cerebrovascular endothelium [94,95]. OxLDL contains lipid peroxides and reactive aldehydes (i.e. 4-hydroxynonenal) that can deplete cellular antioxidants and generate ROS. For example, exposure of endothelial cells to oxLDL leads to activation of antioxidant defenses by a rapid synthesis of glutathione (GSH), however, prevention of GSH synthesis during exposure to oxLDL caused extensive cell death [96]. Once antioxidant defenses are overwhelmed, oxLDL triggers a surge of ROS within cells. Mitochondria are a major source and target of this oxidative assault: oxidized lipids disrupt the mitochondrial electron transport chain, causing electrons to leak and form superoxide. Excess ROS then oxidize mitochondrial DNA, proteins, and lipids, compounding cellular injury and possibly leading to cell death [97] (Fig. 2). In brain endothelial cells, oxLDL-induced oxidative stress can uncouple endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) [98], shifting it from NO production to superoxide production, which reduces vasodilatory NO and increases injurious ROS [99](Fig. 2)​. These oxidative reactions damage membranes, modify proteins, and activate redox-sensitive pathways that promote inflammation and cell death. Importantly, the brain is especially susceptible to lipid peroxidation due to its high lipid content and oxygen demand. OxLDL-driven oxidative stress in cerebral vessels and neurons is thought to contribute to amyloid beta (Aβ) accumulation and neurodegeneration over time [100]. Additionally, oxLDL promotes NADPH oxidase activation, contributing further ROS generation. LOX-1 signaling in the aging neurovasculature

LOX-1, encoded by oxidized low-density lipoprotein receptor 1 (OLR1), is a pattern recognition receptor expressed on endothelial cells, including those of the cerebral microvasculature. Although LOX-1 expression is low under physiological conditions, it becomes significantly upregulated with aging, oxidative stress, and systemic inflammation, heightening susceptibility to oxLDL-induced vascular injury [26]. Upon binding oxidized LDL, LOX-1 activates NF-κB signaling, increases ROS production, and promotes endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT), all key processes contributing to endothelial dysfunction and vessel wall remodeling [26]. In the brain, LOX-1 activation leads to BBB disruption by weakening tight junction proteins and enhancing transcytosis, ultimately promoting vascular permeability and neuroinflammation (Fig. 3). Notably, LOX-1 expression is also modulated by PCSK9, a regulator of LDL receptor turnover that is increasingly recognized for its role in vascular inflammation and aging [102,103]. In mouse models of hyperlipidemia and aging, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of LOX-1 reduces BBB leakage, suppresses microglial activation, and protects against cognitive decline [26]. Neuroinflammation and microglial activation

Closely intertwined with oxidative stress is neuroinflammation, a key driver of vascular cognitive impairment. OxLDL is a pro-inflammatory stimulus that activates endothelial cells and immune cells. In blood vessels, oxLDL induces endothelial expression of adhesion molecules such as vascular cell adhesion protein 1 (VCAM-1), intercellular cell adhesion protein 1 (ICAM-1), E-selectin, and chemokines like CCL2 [104]. This causes circulating leukocytes (especially monocytes) to stick to the endothelium and migrate into the vessel wall and perivascular spaces [105]. Under normal conditions, the BBB acts as a selective filter, preventing many immune cells, including macrophages, from entering the brain. However, when the BBB is disrupted or when the brain undergoes neuroinflammation, circulating monocytes can migrate into the brain (Fig. 3). Monocytes then mature into macrophages and, in the brain, can interact with resident microglia [106,107]. Microglia express a repertoire of scavenger receptors, including CD36 and LOX-1, that facilitate the uptake of oxLDL under conditions of systemic dyslipidemia and cerebral oxidative stress [18]. Upon internalization, oxLDL-loaded microglia adopt a pro-inflammatory phenotype, characterized by increased production of ROS, proinflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α) and Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and activation of the NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, in part through TLR4/6–CD36 signaling complexes, creating a vicious cycle of inflammation [79]. In mouse models of cerebral hypoperfusion, LDL extravasation into brain parenchyma leads to localized oxLDL accumulation and perivascular microglial activation, exacerbating white matter damage and cognitive deficits [108]. In aging, microglia that internalize oxLDL often accumulate intracellular lipid droplets, entering a dysfunctional state termed “lipid droplet–accumulating microglia” (LDAM). These cells exhibit impaired phagocytosis, heightened ROS production, and a hyperinflammatory profile (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β), contributing to chronic neuroinflammation and impaired debris clearance. LDAM are abundant in aging brains and have been implicated with myelin damage and white matter degeneration [109]. Recent work reveals that oxLDL uptake by microglia triggers a profound metabolic shift, marked by mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced oxidative phosphorylation, and a compensatory reliance on glycolysis. This metabolic profile supports the adoption of a proinflammatory M1-like phenotype, with increased reactive oxygen species and cytokine production. Mechanistic studies implicate dysregulated lipid-sensitive transcriptional regulators such as sterol regulatory element-binding transcription factor 1 (SREBP1) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), which influence cellular cholesterol handling and inflammatory gene expression, reinforcing this metabolic-inflammation axis [110,111].

Endothelial dysfunction in brain vessels

The inner lining of cerebral blood vessels, the endothelium, plays a crucial role in regulating blood flow, vessel tone, and barrier function [[112], [113], [114], [115]]. Endothelial dysfunction is a hallmark of both cardiovascular disease and cerebral small vessel disease in aging. OxLDL contributes to endothelial dysfunction through multiple pathways. One key effect is reducing the bioavailability of nitric oxide (NO), a vasoprotective molecule [116]. OxLDL induces eNOS uncoupling, transforming it from a NO-producing to a superoxide-generating enzyme. This shift reduces bioavailable NO, impairing endothelium-dependent vasodilation and promoting vasoconstriction and ischemia. In cultured human endothelial cells treated with oxLDL, oxidative stress mediated by the p66Shc pathway was traced directly to eNOS uncoupling and subsequent superoxide generation. Restoration with tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) reversed these effects, illustrating that eNOS uncoupling underlies oxLDL-induced oxidative endothelial injury [117] and is a recognized mechanism in vascular diseases [118].

Ferroptosis

Emerging evidence highlights ferroptosis, a regulated form of cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, as a critical mechanism underlying oxLDL-induced endothelial dysfunction [119]. It should be noted that much of the mechanistic evidence linking oxLDL to ferroptotic signaling derives from studies in systemic vascular endothelium and atherosclerosis models. Direct validation of ferroptosis in brain microvascular endothelial cells remains limited. Thus, while ferroptotic pathways are mechanistically consistent with lipid peroxidation–driven vascular injury, their specific contribution to oxLDL-mediated neurovascular dysfunction in the brain requires further investigation. OxLDL not only generates oxidative stress but also disrupts iron homeostasis within endothelial cells, promoting the accumulation of ROS and peroxidized lipids characteristic of ferroptotic injury. Experimental models have demonstrated that excessive iron loading sensitizes endothelial cells to oxidative stress, leading to compromised viability and loss of barrier integrity. Notably, studies in hyperlipidemic animals have shown that pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis preserves endothelial function and attenuates atherosclerotic lesion development, pointing to ferroptosis as a key downstream effector of oxLDL toxicity in the vasculature. These findings position ferroptosis as a promising therapeutic target for protecting the cerebral microvasculature in the context of dyslipidemia and aging [120]. In hyperlipidemic animal models, recent evidence demonstrates that inhibition of ferroptosis significantly protects endothelial cells and attenuates atherosclerotic lesion development, directly implicating ferroptotic mechanisms in oxLDL-mediated vascular injury. In ApoE−/− mice fed a high-fat diet, treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) markedly reduced vascular iron accumulation, suppressed oxidative lipid peroxidation, and restored expression levels of key ferroptosis regulators such as glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), leading to preserved endothelial function and fewer atherosclerotic lesions [121]. OxLDL further promotes an endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT), wherein endothelial cells lose their normal function and acquire a scar-forming, mesenchymal phenotype. EndoMT contributes to vessel stiffening and rarefaction, hindering brain perfusion [122,123]. Overall, excess LDL/oxLDL accelerates the decline of endothelial health in aging, causing dysregulation of cerebral blood flow, chronic hypoperfusion, and microvascular damage that can manifest as cognitive deficits.

Blood–brain barrier disruption

In addition to the above-mentioned effects that LDL and oxLDL exert on endothelial cells, these lipoproteins are largely excluded from the brain parenchyma under physiological conditions due to the restrictive nature of the BBB. With advancing age, the cerebral endothelium exhibits reduced tight junction integrity and increased transcellular transport [[124], [125], [126]]. Oxidative stress and inflammation greatly exacerbate tight junction proteins damage and increase vesicular trafficking across the BBB. Once the BBB is “leaky,” oxLDL can enter the brain parenchyma [106,107]. Experimental evidence shows that peripheral oxLDL accumulates in aging brains especially after age-related loss of BBB integrity [127]. In aging oxLDL becomes a potent mediator of BBB disruption. A pivotal preclinical study demonstrated that elevated oxLDL promotes LOX-1–dependent BBB breakdown, increasing endothelial permeability by ∼260% in a model of early-onset disease. Blocking LOX-1 preserved tight junction integrity, highlighting a direct pathway linking oxLDL to BBB injury [128]. In vitro studies by Lénárt et al., 2015 using cultured brain endothelial, pericyte, and glial cells from ApoB-100 transgenic and wild-type mice showed that oxLDL, unlike native LDL, induces dose-dependent toxicity, increased ROS generation, NO dysregulation, and transendothelial barrier dysfunction, thus increased permeability. These effects were also accompanied by increased endothelial membrane rigidity and were partially attenuated in cells from transgenic mice [129]. Recent work by Zong et al. shows that transient receptor potential cation channel, subfamily M, member 2 (TRPM2), a redox-sensitive ion channel, is upregulated in dyslipidemia and contributes to BBB dysfunction. In hyperlipidemic Apoe−/− mice, TRPM2 deletion in endothelial and immune cells protected the BBB, reduced infarct size, and preserved neurovascular integrity after ischemic injury [130]. BBB breakdown itself is directly linked to cognitive impairment in the elderly [131,132], even independent of classic Alzheimer's pathology. In fact, BBB leakage is being recognized as an early biomarker of cognitive decline in normal aging and in AD. Here we have gathered evidence suggesting that hypercholesterolemia accelerates this BBB damage. In preclinical studies, LDL receptor–knockout mice (LDLr−/−), which are a widely adopted model of high LDL, develop gliosis and have decreased levels of the tight-junction protein claudin-5 in brain microvessels by a young age, indicating weakened BBB integrity even in the presence of regular chow [23]. Taken together, dyslipidemia and aging act in concert to disrupt the BBB. Once disrupted, a vicious cycle is potentially started: infiltrating oxLDL and inflammatory cells cause further neurovascular damage, leading to worsening leakage. This BBB failure exposes neurons to circulating toxins and contributes to neuronal dysfunction, synaptic loss, and ultimately cognitive decline (Fig. 3).

Mitochondrial dysfunction

Mitochondria are critical for energy production in all cells of the NVU [113,133,134], and they are a highly susceptible to age-related oxidative and metabolic stress [[135], [136], [137]]. With advancing age, cerebral microvessels show marked mitochondrial impairments: aged brain vessels exhibit reduced mitochondrial membrane potential and respiratory capacity alongside an upregulation of mitophagy proteins (Parkin, Nix) [138]. Springo et al. demonstrate that in isolated cerebral arteries, aging dramatically exacerbates pressure-induced mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) production in vascular cells [139]. Elevated LDL and oxLDL cholesterol have been identified as potent drivers of NVU injury via exacerbation of mitochondrial dysfunction: Hypercholesterolemia in rodents recapitulates many features of accelerated brain aging: even in young mice, high plasma LDL causes cortical mitochondrial enzyme deficits (complex I and III activity loss) and increased oxidative damage in the brain, concomitant with memory impairments [140]. A study by de Oliveira et al. provides evidence that chronic hypercholesterolemia induces cognitive deficits through mechanisms involving mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in the brain. In LDLr−/− mice, a model of familial hypercholesterolemia, spatial memory impairments were observed even in the absence of dietary cholesterol supplementation. When fed a cholesterol-enriched diet, these mice exhibited exacerbated deficits, accompanied by decreased activity of mitochondrial complexes I and II in the cerebral cortex. Importantly, these mitochondrial impairments were inversely correlated with circulating cholesterol levels. Hypercholesterolemic mice also displayed pronounced cortical oxidative stress, marked by reduced GSH, increased lipid peroxidation, and disrupted glutathione peroxidase/reductase activity [141]. Notably, in another study, these effects were mitigated by mitochondrial-protective compounds like diphenyl diselenide (PhSe)2, which restores electron transport chain (ETC) function and GSH levels, supporting the role of cholesterol overload in mitochondrial injury [141]. Among the diverse cell types comprising the NVU, brain endothelial cells are uniquely vulnerable to circulating insults such as oxLDL. Unlike neurons or glia, endothelial cells form the interface between the blood and brain and are continuously exposed to systemic oxidative and metabolic stressors. Their high mitochondrial density makes them especially susceptible to redox imbalance and mitochondrial dysfunction [142]. A growing body of evidence shows that oxLDL and hyperlipidemia contribute to endothelial dysfunction by disrupting mitochondrial homeostasis through multiple, converging mechanisms. Li et al. demonstrated that oxLDL induces expression of the nuclear receptor NR4A1 in aortic endothelial cells, which activates the calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII)–Parkin mitophagy pathway, leading to excessive mitochondrial clearance, energy failure, and endothelial apoptosis [143]. Complementing this, another group showed that oxLDL exposure leads to mitochondrial depolarization, calcium overload, and caspase-9/-3 activation in human umbilical vein endothelial cells, while inhibition of PTEN restored AMPK–CREB–Mfn2 signaling and mitophagic flux, thereby improving mitochondrial function and endothelial viability [144]. Yuan et al. further linked mitochondrial oxidative stress to atherosclerosis progression in diabetes, showing that sustained mtROS production impairs eNOS activity, promotes inflammatory gene expression, and exacerbates oxLDL accumulation [145]. Finally, Xie et al. reported that Coenzyme Q10 activates the AMPK–YAP–OPA1 pathway, promoting mitochondrial fusion, enhancing ATP production, and reducing mtROS and inflammatory cytokine release in both oxLDL-treated endothelial cells and hyperlipidemic mice, thus offering a potential mitochondrial-targeted therapeutic approach [146]. Collectively, these findings underscore the central role of mitochondrial dysfunction in oxLDL-induced endothelial injury and support the development of interventions aimed at preserving mitochondrial integrity in the aging vasculature.

Emerging mechanisms and pathways

Recent studies have revealed various mechanisms by which oxLDL can accelerate cerebrovascular aging and NVU dysfunction. One such mechanism is epigenetic reprogramming where oxLDL produces changes in gene regulation. Studies by Su et al. [147] showed that oxLDL upregulated Ash2l, a chromatin-modifying factor responsible for histone methylation in endothelial cells. This promotes a transcriptional program that favors inflammation, scavenger-receptor expression, and impaired endothelial function in high fat diet ApoE mice and ox-LDL treated endothelial cells. This suggests that histone methylation contributes to a feed forward loop in which enhanced oxLDL uptake drives persistent vascular inflammation [147]. OxLDL has been shown to affect the expression of certain microRNAs (miRNA) and long non-coding RNAs (lnc RNA). Hu et al. demonstrated that microRNA-106a-5p contributes to oxLDL-induced endothelial dysfunction by targeting the Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling pathway [148]. Xie et al. reported that treatment of vascular endothelial cells with oxLDL reduced the expression of glutathione perioxidase 4 (GPx4) and eNOS and was associated with the upregulation of miR-214-3p [149]. These findings indicate that epigenetic and non-coding RNA pathways contribute to oxLDL-driven vascular decline, thereby amplifying age-related neurovascular vulnerability. Emerging evidence also implicates oxLDL as a potent inducer of premature senescence in vascular cells, characterized by growth arrest, increased senescence markers, reduced regenerative capacity, and pro-inflammatory secretory signals. In endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs), exposure to sub-toxic levels of oxLDL accelerated the onset of senescence, suppresses telomerase activity by ∼50%, and impairs both proliferation and tube formation [150]. However, these effects were mitigated by LOX-1 receptor inhibition or statin treatment, implying that receptor mediated signaling may be an upstream driver. In a similar way, in mature vascular endothelial cells, oxLDL upregulated cell cycle inhibitors p16 and p21, with associated reductions in proliferation and increases in apoptotic and growth-arrested phenotypes [151,152]. New studies are pointing out the uptake of receptors especially CD36, as important upstream inducers of senescence in vascular pathological conditions [153,154]. While the focus of the present review is on brain microvascular endothelial cells, other BBB cell types have been implicated in the exacerbation of oxLDL effects in the cerebromicrovasculature. Lucarelli et al. demonstrated that astrocytes play a crucial and stimulatory role in LOX-1 expression in endothelial cells [155]. Expression of LOX-1 was increased in co-cultures of rat astrocytes and rat brain microvascular endothelial cells. However, in the absence of astrocytes, LOX-1 receptor was downregulated in those cells, implicating the BBB microenvironment is involved in the regulation of the LOX-1 receptor. It is thought that astrocytes can produce cytokines and growth factors (TNF-α, interleukin-1L, vascular endothelial growth factor, basic-fibroblast growth factor, interleukin-6, monocyte-chemoattractant protein-1) which can presumably act on brain microvascular endothelial cells to enhance the expression of LOX-1 and increase ox-LDL uptake [155]. LOX-1 is also expressed in astrocytes [155] and interaction with ox-LDL induces activation of innate immunity and reduced neuronal survival [156]. Studies by Lenart et al. [157] showed that among three BBB cell types of ApoB-100 transgenic and wild type mice, astrocytes and endothelial cells are most sensitive to oxLDL treatment compared to pericytes. Interestingly, OxLDL treatment induced ROS production in all BBB cell types and even higher oxLDL concentrations increased NO production in all three cell types of the brain microvasculature [157]. Beyond oxidative modification, LDL can undergo glycation in hyperglycemic states through a chemical modification of apolipoprotein −100 by glucose and its metabolites. This is common in dyslipidemic patients who also have an altered glucose metabolism [158]. This glycation of LDL in turn increases LDL susceptibility to oxidation and formation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) in the form of glycoxidized LDL [159,160]. Glycoxidized LDL have been shown to have adverse effect on the vasculature [160] especially in patients with diabetes mellitus. These AGE-LDL bind to receptors of advanced glycation end-products (RAGE) present in endothelial cells, to induce a cascade of pathogenic mediators. AGE-RAGE interactions activate NADPH oxidase to increase oxidative stress, induce mitochondrial dysfunction, inhibit NO bioavailability and increase vascular permeability [161,162]. Glycoxidized-LDL can also inhibit endothelium-dependent vasodilation, activate NF-KB pathway and inflammation, upregulate CCL2 expression and downregulate NOS in endothelial cells [160,163]. Glycoxidized-LDL has also been shown to be more potent than glycated-LDL and oxLDL, as demonstrated by increased expression of LOX-1 receptors [160].

Synergy of dyslipidemia and aging in cognitive decline

The collected emerging evidence from animal models and clinical observations underscores how oxLDL-driven vascular pathology synergizes with aging, thus promoting cognitive decline through networked mechanisms involving BBB disruption, neuroinflammation, vascular rarefaction, and white matter injury. In LDLr−/− mice, high plasma LDL is associated with hippocampal and cortical BBB disruption, manifested by increased permeability, decreased tight junction proteins (claudin-5, occludin), and astrogliosis, even without a high-cholesterol diet [23]. Since BBB integrity is essential for maintaining the brain's microenvironment, its disruption facilitates the entry of neurotoxic plasma components and immune cells, which exacerbates neuronal injury and accelerates cognitive impairment [164]. In middle-aged mice, a Western-style diet altered brain metabolism, increased BBB transport, elevated microglial ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (IBA1) and microvascular factor VIII, and moderately impaired cognitive performance on Y-maze and radial arm water maze (RAWM) tasks. These effects were more pronounced in LDLr−/− mice, which also showed increased brain lactate and proinflammatory lipid metabolites, suggesting that hyperlipidemia synergizes with dietary insults to impair neurovascular health and cognition [165]. In humans, elevated LDL and familial hypercholesterolemia are associated with early BBB leakage and increased risk of cognitive impairment, underscoring BBB dysfunction as a mechanistic bridge between oxLDL and dementia. In a recent study, Rodrigues et al. demonstrated that LDLr−/− mice exhibit early and progressive microgliosis in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, accompanied by reduced claudin-5 expression and cognitive impairment. In their study, these mice showed deficits in hippocampal-dependent memory tasks, including spontaneous alternation in the Y-maze and the object location test. Treatment with minocycline, a pharmacological inhibitor of microglial activation, ameliorated these behavioral deficits and reduced perivascular microglial accumulation, despite not significantly altering total microglial density or phagocytic activity. These findings suggest that microglial morphological changes and perivascular redistribution may also play a central role in BBB dysfunction and memory decline in hyperlipidemic conditions [166]. Taken together, the convergence of dyslipidemia and aging creates a permissive environment for cognitive deterioration, in which oxLDL may act as a central mediator of neurovascular dysfunction. By promoting BBB disruption, chronic neuroinflammation, mitochondrial impairment, and cerebral hypoperfusion, oxLDL compromises critical defense mechanisms of the aging brain. These pathological changes accelerate white matter degeneration and synaptic loss, resulting in cognitive deficits even in the absence of overt atherosclerosis. Preclinical models consistently show that oxLDL exposure impairs hippocampal-dependent memory, while interventions targeting microglial activation or lipid peroxidation can mitigate these effects. Importantly, clinical evidence corroborates these findings: Wang et al. demonstrated that elevated plasma oxLDL levels were significantly associated with cognitive impairment in patients with ischemic stroke, independent of age, total cholesterol, or LDL-C levels. These data suggest that oxLDL may not only be a biomarker, but a driver of cognitive decline in aging and hyperlipidemic individuals, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target in vascular cognitive impairment and dementia [86].

Conclusion

Converging evidence indicates that the interplay of aging, LDL, and especially oxLDL is a critical driver of cerebrovascular injury underlying VCID. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that midlife hypercholesterolemia is associated with an increased risk of dementia, with each ∼1 mmol/L rise in LDL levels linked to an estimated 8% higher incidence of all-cause dementia [167,168]. Mechanistically, atherosclerosis-prone conditions like high LDL promote intracranial arterial disease that compromises cerebral perfusion and precipitates ischemic injury [169]. Beyond large vessels, cholesterol and its oxidized derivatives can accumulate in the cerebral microvasculature, inciting local inflammation and neurodegeneration [169]. In the aging brain, these processes are compounded by an intrinsically fragile vasculature, establishing a strong case that aging, LDL, and oxLDL must be studied synergistically in the context of brain microvascular health and VCID pathogenesis. OxLDL emerges as a particularly deleterious player within the NVU. Once native LDL particles undergo oxidation, they trigger endothelial dysfunction more potently than native LDL [94]. OxLDL engages endothelial cells to upregulate adhesion molecules and pro-inflammatory pathways, while directly degrading the integrity of the endothelial barrier [82,83]. This damage to the BBB permits leakage of neurotoxic blood-derived factors into the brain parenchyma [170], exacerbating oxidative stress and inflammation within brain tissue. Indeed, oxLDL creates a vicious cycle: it is readily taken up by macrophages and brain glia, generating foam cells and further ROS and cytokine release [18,85,94,171]. The result is chronic neurovascular inflammation, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability (impairing vasodilation), and breakdown of microvascular structure, changes that manifest as small vessel disease, microhemorrhages, and ultimately cognitive impairment. Thus, aging-related oxidative stress and oxLDL together destabilize the BBB and cerebral perfusion, linking peripheral dyslipidemia to the hallmark microvascular lesions of VCID. These insights also highlight emerging gaps and complexities. For example, lipid-laden microglia and perivascular macrophages observed in aged or diseased brains mirror the foam cells of peripheral atherosclerosis [169], suggesting common pathways yet underscoring that the brain's unique environment (e.g. BBB selectivity and glial responses) may modulate disease expression. Clinical data on blood lipids and cognition have sometimes been inconsistent, with some cohorts even hint at a U-shaped relationship wherein very low cholesterol might impair neuronal function while high cholesterol clearly promotes vascular injury [172]. This paradox emphasizes that simply measuring LDL levels is not enough; the quality of lipids (e.g. propensity to oxidize) and the context of aging are pivotal. Unresolved mechanisms include precisely how oxLDL interacts with other dementia-related processes (such as amyloid-β accumulation or APOE4-mediated lipid dysregulation) in driving cognitive decline. Addressing these open questions will require translational studies that integrate vascular biology with geroscience, to unravel how age-related lipid toxicity intersects with neurodegenerative cascades in VCID.

Importantly, the detrimental effects of oxLDL point to the need for targeted therapeutic strategies beyond standard LDL-cholesterol reduction. LDL-lowering therapies (e.g. statins) reduce cardiovascular events, but many patients on optimal therapy still experience cerebrovascular complications, a “residual risk” driven in part by ongoing vascular inflammation and oxidative damage [173,174]. In the brain, this residual risk may manifest as continued BBB disruption and microvascular damage despite controlled plasma LDL, because oxLDL and its downstream inflammatory sequelae remain unmitigated. Therefore, future interventions should aim to neutralize oxLDL's harmful actions on the cerebral vasculature. Approaches could include enhancing antioxidant defenses or upregulating endothelial-protective factors to prevent LDL oxidation and endothelial activation. Notably, experimental evidence shows that boosting endothelial expression of anti-inflammatory transcription factors (such as KLF2) can ameliorate oxLDL-induced dysfunction [82,167], illustrating a viable strategy to preserve endothelial health. Another avenue is to inhibit oxLDL's receptors (e.g. LOX-1) or GPER and Scavenger Receptor Class B Type 1 (SR-BI) on brain endothelial cells [175], thereby blocking the cascade of leukocyte adhesion, ROS generation, and tight-junction disruption that follows oxLDL exposure. Enhancing the beneficial arm of lipid transport, for instance, raising HDL or improving its functionality, may also help, as higher HDL is associated with better cognitive outcomes in older adults [167]. By addressing the inflammatory and oxidative components of vascular aging, such therapies would tackle the root causes of ongoing microvascular injury. Recent work further refines the concept of the “leaky” aging BBB by demonstrating that age-related dysfunction is not limited to tight junction disruption but also involves altered endothelial transport dynamics. Studies from Wyss-Coray and colleagues show that aging is associated with increased nonspecific vesicular transcytosis alongside reduced efficiency of selective transporter systems, shifting the BBB from a tightly regulated interface to a more permissive and dysregulated state [176,177]. This transport imbalance provides a mechanistic framework by which circulating factors, including modified lipoproteins, may gain enhanced access to perivascular compartments under aging conditions, even prior to overt structural barrier breakdown. Taken together, these findings suggest that aging, increased circulating LDL, and thus oxidized LDL form a pathological triad that accelerates cerebrovascular damage and cognitive decline. The cumulative literature synthesized here supports the thesis that these factors must be interrogated in concert in future VCID research, with particular attention to their impact on the brain's microvasculature. Recognizing oxLDL as a mediator of residual vascular risk underscores a paradigm shift: successful prevention of VCID will likely require interventions that go beyond lowering LDL alone. Targeting oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation fueled by oxLDL is an auspicious strategy to protect the aging brain's blood vessels. Such an approach – in tandem with traditional risk factor control, may hold promise to reduce the burden of vascular cognitive impairment and dementia in our increasingly aged population, ultimately translating mechanistic insights into preventive and therapeutic breakthroughs.
