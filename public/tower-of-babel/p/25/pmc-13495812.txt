A Modular Liver–Microbial Microfluidic Platform to Evaluate Therapeutic and Adverse Effects of Microbial Metabolites
Authors: Hsih‐Yin Tan, In Young Hwang, Nanthini Jayabalan, Hui Ling Lee, Louis Jun Ye Ong, Chwee Teck Lim, Matthew Wook Chang, Yi‐Chin Toh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGut microbial metabolites, particularly short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like butyrate, play a significant role in modulating non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). While animal studies show that butyrate‐producing microbes can improve liver function, full recovery is hindered by unintended side effects from commensal bacteria. These underlying biomolecular mechanisms remain elusive, due to the lack of in vitro coculture models capable of systematically examine both the therapeutic benefits of engineered microbial metabolites and their potential adverse impacts. To address this, we developed a modular microfluidic platform to study the effects of live microbial metabolites on hepatic steatosis and liver function. We created a microfluidic‐based hepatic steatosis model integrated with a compartmentalized microbial module, facilitating the study of how metabolites produced by live microbes affected the liver model. We compared the effects of synthetic SCFA supplementation with those of coculturing with a control and butyrate‐producing E. coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) strains on hepatic steatosis. Our findings showed that live microbial coculture did not phenocopy exogenous SCFA treatment. While both treatments reduced steatotic lipid accumulation, live microbes induced inflammatory and hepatic metabolic changes, suggesting contributions from additional microbial factors, emphasizing the need to thoroughly assess side effects in liver disease treatment.

Introduction

Hepatic steatosis is a common pathogenic process in many liver diseases, including non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection [1, 2]. Hepatic steatosis is defined as intrahepatic triacylglycerol (TAG) accounting for at least 5% of liver weight, or hepatocytes containing lipid vacuoles in the absence of a secondary contributing factor such as excess alcohol intake, viral infection, or drug treatment [3]. Convincing in vivo evidence shows that factors such as genetics, lipid peroxidation, insulin resistance associated with obesity and diet, may be key contributors to hepatic steatosis, which may ultimately lead to NAFLD [4, 5]. Recent studies suggest that the gut microbiome may play an important role in the metabolic regulation of glucose and lipids, which could ultimately lead to the pathogenesis and progression of hepatic steatosis [6, 7]. This is mainly attributed to an alteration or imbalance of the microbes within the gut. The human intestine harbors a diverse and complex microbiota, which plays a central role in regulating host physiology, including lipid metabolism. Among the metabolites produced by the gut microbiota, short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as acetate, butyrate, and propionate, are particularly critical for maintaining metabolic homeostasis [8, 9]. Disruption in the bacterial phyla, particularly Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, which produce acetate/propionate and butyrate, respectively, can significantly impact host metabolism and liver health. Numerous studies have reported that the availability and concentration of SCFAs may be an important signaling link between the gut‐liver axis [10, 11, 12]. The discovery of such host‐microbe interactions provided opportunities to modulate bacterial phyla as a therapeutic means to address metabolic diseases such as hepatic steatosis.

One avenue researcher have explored is leveraging synthetic biology to engineer microbes to deliver controlled, real‐time, targeted therapeutics [13, 14, 15, 16]. For instance, E. coli Nissle 1917 (EcN), a well‐known probiotic widely used to treat metabolic disorders, has been engineered to control butyrate production to modulate various cellular processes therapeutically [17]. However, how engineered microbes and their metabolites influence steatotic liver phenotypes, and whether live microbial exposure recapitulates the effects of defined metabolites alone, remains poorly understood [18]. Although animal models have been used to study gut microbiome‐liver crosstalk [19, 20], microfluidic multi‐organ culture systems offer a more controlled and human‐relevant platform for evaluating engineered microbes in liver steatosis by enabling compartmentalized coculture, real‐time metabolite exchange, and direct assessment of liver‐specific responses without the confounding effects of whole‐animal physiology. While a growing number of gut–liver coculture models have been reported, their applications remain diverse. Most have been used to show that connecting gut and liver tissues alters tissue phenotype and function relative to monoculture through crosstalk mediated by intestinal metabolites or extracellular vesicles [21, 22, 23], or to investigate gut–liver interactions in other disease contexts, such as anti‐cancer drug metabolism [24], ethanol metabolism [25], and inflammatory bowel disease [26, 27, 28]. In contrast, only a limited number of studies have modeled the gut–liver axis in the context of NAFLD [29, 30, 31]. These models have primarily focused on how intestinal epithelial cells modulate hepatic fatty acid uptake and steatosis progression [29, 30, 31] and have yet to model interactions with live gut microbes.

To address this gap, we developed a modular microfluidic platform that directly links live microbes with steatotic hepatocytes through a shared circulating medium. This reductionist coculture system is designed to overcome a major challenge in host–microbe models, namely the difficulty of maintaining stable interactions between live microbes and mammalian tissues that require markedly different culture environments. By establishing the steatotic liver and microbial compartments independently before coupling them, the platform enables controlled evaluation of how live microbial exposure influences steatotic liver phenotypes and hepatic metabolic responses. Using this simplified modular system, we compared the effects of two auxotrophic mutant strains of E.coli Nissle (EcN), one serving as a control (Ctrl‐EcN) and the other specifically engineered to produce butyrate (But‐EcN), with those of exogenous SCFA supplementation. Our aim was to determine whether live microbial coculture phenocopies exogenous SCFA treatment and to evaluate both the beneficial and unintended hepatic responses elicited by engineered microbial interventions in a steatotic liver context.

Results

Development of Hepatic Steatosis‐On‐Chip

We first establish a hepatic steatosis‐on‐chip model by seeding spheroids of human progenitor cell (HepaRG)‐derived hepatocytes into a previously developed microfluidic 3D culture chip. The chip contained a microchannel with a micropillar array, which served to immobilize HepaRG‐hepatocyte spheroids, facilitating enhanced cell‐cell interactions and maintenance of liver‐specific functions [32].

The establishment of the hepatic steatosis model is summarized in Figure 1A. To induce steatosis, hepatic spheroids in the microfluidic devices were cultured in cell culture medium supplemented with or without free fatty acids (100 µm oleic acid: 50 µm palmitic acid) (FFA) under continuous flow conditions, which has been previously optimized [32]. Within 16 h post‐seeding, the spheroids fused together to form an elongated 3D hepatic tissue. After 5 days of culture in the FFA‐supplemented medium, phase contrast images of the hepatic tissue appeared darker compared to the untreated control (Figure 1B). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the extent of steatosis in the liver tissue. Approximately 97% of hepatocytes exhibited cytoplasmic lipid droplets, with the largest droplets measuring 8 µm (Figure 1B). Data analysis of the captured images revealed that 3D hepatic tissue that was treated with FFA under dynamic conditions showed a 4.3‐fold increment of lipid accumulation (1 196.6 µm3/nucleus) as compared to untreated tissues (272.1 µm3/nucleus). Furthermore, the extent of lipid accumulation was more pronounced in the microfluidic steatosis model than in hepatic spheroids treated with FFA under static conditions, which showed only a modest increase over the untreated control samples (Figure 1C).

Previous reports have suggested that hepatocytes overloaded with FFA can lead to cytotoxic effects [33, 34]. Therefore, we investigated cell death in our hepatic steatosis model. Our data revealed an increased cell death rate (33.9%) in HepaRG hepatic tissues with FFA treatment compared to untreated lean cultures (24.7%) (Figure 1D). To assess the response of hepatocytes to insulin, we evaluated the extent of PI3K‐Akt pathway activation induced by insulin (Figure 1E). To quantify AKT and phospho‐AKT (p‐AKT) protein expression levels, fixed samples were stained with antibodies targeting AKT and p‐AKT and analyzed using a confocal microscope. Immunofluorescence images revealed that in the untreated lean samples, AKT was activated in the presence of insulin, as indicated by the presence of p‐AKT (Figure 1F). Image analysis confirmed that in the presence of insulin, p‐AKT/AKT ratio was 1.2‐fold higher compared to samples without insulin. In contrast, the steatosis‐on‐chip sample showed no activation of AKT, marked by unchanged levels of pAKT/AKT ratios before and after insulin stimulation (Figure 1G). Our data indicated that 3D hepatic tissues cultured under dynamic perfusion of medium supplemented with FFAs displayed a steatosis condition and were unresponsive to insulin treatment.

Effects of Exogenous Butyrate and Acetate on Hepatic Steatosis‐On‐Chip Module

Studies have shown that specific SCFAs positively influence hepatic cells by stimulating gluconeogenesis, antioxidant, and anti‐inflammatory [35, 36, 37]. To investigate the effects of acetate and butyrate on hepatic lipid accumulation in our microfluidic steatosis‐on‐chip model, we monitored the changes of accumulated lipid droplets in response to exogenously applied SCFAs post FFA treatment to see if lipid accumulation and insulin resistance can be reversed (Figure 2A). There were no observable changes in the morphologies of the 3D hepatic tissues when acetate was supplemented to the culture media (Figure 2B). However, butyrate‐treated hepatic tissues showed some signs of dissociation (Figure 2B). This observation was consistent with earlier studies, which have reported that culturing hepatocytes with high concentrations and long durations of butyrate resulted in cell detachment and morphological changes [35]. However, the cell viability assay with Propidium iodide (PI)+ showed no significant difference in cell viability between NaB‐treated and control tissues (Figure 2C), suggesting that the observed dissociation induced by NaB treatment was not associated with significant cytotoxicity. Nonetheless, we observed that lipid accumulation in the FFA‐treated hepatic tissues could be reversed by either butyrate or acetate treatment (Figure 2D). There was a significant reduction of lipid droplets – 50% and 58.3%, respectively, for acetate and butyrate cultures as compared to FFA‐treated (Figure 2D). Moreover, there were fewer large lipid droplets (diameter > 5 µm) as compared to HepaRG spheroids before acetate or butyrate treatment. Our observations concurred with numerous reports showing that lipid droplets in the livers of obese animal models were greatly reduced when they were administered orally or intravenously with butyrate and acetate [38, 39, 40, 41] given their roles in lipogenesis. It was inferred that reducing lipid accumulation in obese animal models consequently improved the animals’ responses to insulin [42]. Hence, we examined whether the insulin response in our microfluidic steatosis‐on‐chip model could be improved by supplementing with these SCFAs. Our data showed no significant differences in insulin responses of steatotic samples after treatment with either butyrate or acetate (Figure 2E,F), as indicated by p‐AKT/AKT ratios (Figure 2F). In summary, our microfluidic steatosis‐on‐chip model successfully enabled the assessment of SCFA‐induced reversal of lipid accumulation caused by FFA treatment. However, it should be noted that we did not observe an improvement in insulin sensitivity in this model.

Development and Characterization of a Microfluidic Microbial Culture Module

To facilitate the assessment of crosstalk effects mediated by microbial metabolites produced by live microbes on the hepatic steatosis model, we designed and developed a microfluidic compartmentalized microbial module, which consisted of a microbial chamber and a systemic chamber separated by a porous membrane (Figure 3A). This design allowed for the culture of commensal or engineered microbes in the microbial chamber and real‐time diffusion of microbial metabolites, such as SCFAs, e.g., butyrate, acetate, into the systemic chamber. When assembled with the hepatic chip, microbial metabolites in the systemic chamber can circulate to hepatic tissues, facilitating the study of gut microbial‐liver crosstalk. The microbial module can be reversibly connected to the 3D liver chip, allowing for independent establishment of microbial and steatotic hepatic cultures before assembling them to assess coculture effects. The separation between the microbial and systemic chambers was achieved using a porous polycarbonate membrane coated with mucin‐agar. We incorporated mucin into the coating to provide essential nutrients for EcN, as this strain utilizes mucin's carbon sources for colonization [43]. To assess the module's ability to contain fluids under continuous flow conditions without leakage, we perfused various colored dyes into the chambers at a flow rate of 10 µl/min and confirmed that there was no leakage (Figure S1). Furthermore, we investigated the permeability of the mucin‐agar coating on the porous membrane, specifically examining whether it allowed for diffusion of soluble factors, as indicated by the dye solution, between chambers. Color changes were observed in the dyes at the outlets of each chamber, and spectrometer analysis confirmed the diffusion of dyes across the mucin‐agar porous membrane (Figure 3B,C). Next, we investigated the mucin‐agar membrane's capability to prevent the translocation of live microbes while allowing diffusion of microbial metabolites across the chambers by culturing green‐fluorescent protein (GFP) labeled wild‐type E. coli on different concentrations of mucin with agarose or mucin with agar. Confocal observations of the fixed microbial cultures on the different coatings showed that using a combination of 10 mg/ml mucin and 2% agar (wt/vol) yielded the best results. Our confocal images showed that GFP‐E. coli adhered to the top surface of the mucin‐agar coating and no GFP‐E. coli penetrated the mucin‐agar coating (Figure 3D). We also ascertained that the microbial chamber was capable of maintaining microbial growth for a period of 48 h without medium replenishment (Figure 3E). Following that, we proceeded to evaluate if the microbial module supported culture and function of control (Ctrl‐EcN) and But‐EcN., which overproduces butyrate [17]. To do so, we collected conditioned media from the systemic chambers of Ctrl‐EcN and But‐EcN 24 h post‐inoculation and analyzed for the concentrations of acetate and butyrate, respectively. From our analysis, acetate was detected from Ctrl‐EcN (1.62 mM) and almost negligible amounts of butyrate (Figure 3F–G), while butyrate (1.4 mm) and negligible amounts of acetate were found in samples from But‐EcN (Figure 3F–G). These values were slightly lower than the concentrations measured in bulk cultures of Ctrl‐EcN and But‐EcN, which we previously reported to be 6 mm [17]. This may be due to diffusion limitations, preventing metabolites from crossing the mucin‐coated membrane into the systemic compartment. Taken together, these data confirmed that the microbial module can provide a suitable environment for both Ctrl‐EcN and But‐EcN to proliferate and produce enough metabolites.

Dynamic Coculture of Live Microbes With Steatotic Liver Tissues

Our earlier studies have shown the effects of synthetic butyrate and acetate on hepatic steatosis and insulin response. To better recapitulate the effects of real‐time microbial metabolites production on steatotic hepatocytes, we cocultured living probiotic microbes (Ctrl‐EcN and But‐EcN) with the microfluidic steatosis‐on‐chip model under dynamic recirculating conditions for 48 h and monitored for any phenotypic changes to the of HepaRG spheroids (Figure 4A). First, we examined the implications of coculturing live microbes on hepatocyte viability by means of staining for necrotic cells (Figure 4B). The percentages of Propidium iodide (PI)+ dead cells when cocultured with either Ctrl‐EcN or But‐EcN were relatively similar to that of FFA‐treated hepatocytes (Figure 4C). Interestingly, we observed a significant drop in lipid droplets for both systems cocultured with Ctrl‐EcN (57.4%) and But‐EcN (76.6%) as compared to steatotic hepatocytes after 5 days of FFA treatment (Figure 4D). Thus, metabolites produced by live microbes, which included SCFAs, could reverse hepatic lipid accumulation to similar levels seen in untreated control. This observation was consistent with previous reports showing that the presence of SCFA, such as butyrate and acetate, can reduce lipids in steatosis hepatocytes [38, 39].

Additionally, we investigated if the coculture with the Ctrl or But‐EcN was capable of reversing insulin resistance elicited by FFA treatment. From our observations and data analysis, there were no significant differences in the ratio of p‐AKT/AKT for both Ctrl‐EcN and But‐EcN (Figure S2), indicating that the steatotic hepatic tissues remained insulin resistant despite receiving a continual supply of acetate and butyrate from the live microbes.

Comparing Live Microbe Coculture with SCFA Supplementation on Hepatocyte Inflammatory Responses

Since both synthetic SCFAs and live EcN microbes could reverse hepatic lipid accumulation caused by FFA treatment in our steatosis‐on‐chip model, we aim to further explore whether live‐microbe cocultures specifically influence hepatic inflammation and metabolic functions. This highlighted the significance of coculturing with live microbes. In particular, we examined changes in the gene expression of inflammatory factors in hepatocytes after FFA induction and subsequent treatment with either synthetic SCFAs or coculture with Ctrl or But‐EcN microbes.

Our results revealed that FFA‐loaded HepaRG hepatocytes cultured in the microfluidic system produced increased levels of inflammation as shown by the upregulation of IL6 (+1.6‐fold), IL8 (+2.64‐fold), and MCP1/CCL2 (+2.5‐fold) as compared to untreated control, except for CXCL10 (‐0.5 fold) (Figure 5A–D). Treatment with synthetic acetate or butyrate did not significantly alter the levels of inflammatory cytokine (IL6 and IL8) expressions (Figure 5A,B) as compared to FFA treatment. However, there was an effect on chemokine expressions. There was a slight increase in CXCL10 with NaB treatment, which was not observed with NaAc treatment (Figure 5C). In contrast, a significant decrease in MCP1/CCL2 expression was observed in steatotic hepatocytes supplemented with both NaB (‐17.5‐fold) and NaAc (‐2.02‐fold) (Figure 5D) as compared to steatotic hepatocytes. This is likely due to the strong association of CCL2 with NAFLD [44], with butyrate being more potent than acetate in reducing FFA‐induced inflammation.

However, when we cocultured the live Ctrl‐EcN and But‐EcN with steatotic hepatocytes in the microfluidic systems, there was a marked difference in hepatic inflammatory responses. Ctrl‐EcN significantly increased IL‐6 (+7.3‐fold) and IL‐8 (+4.2‐fold) expressions compared to steatotic hepatocytes, while But‐EcN upregulated only IL‐8 (+2.9‐fold) (Figure 5A,B). For chemokine expression levels, Ctrl‐EcN slightly increased CXCL10 expression, and MCP1/CCL2 levels remained unchanged as compared to steatotic hepatocytes (Figure 5C,D). Conversely, But‐EcN significantly increased the expression of CXCL10 (+5.9‐fold) but decreased the expression of MCP/CCL2 (‐1.5‐fold). Our data suggest that exogenous SCFAs, particularly butyrate, mitigated inflammation in steatotic hepatocytes. However, cocultures with live microbes, both Ctrl and But‐EcN likely introduced additional factors that counteracted these anti‐inflammatory effects.

One possible contributor to the inflammatory response observed in hepatocytes is endotoxin. Compared with pathogenic E. coli strains, EcN is considered non‐pathogenic because its truncated lipopolysaccharides (LPS) exhibit immunomodulatory properties with reduced immunotoxicity [45]. However, low levels of endotoxin were still detected in both Ctrl and But‐EcN cultures (Figure 5E). While these levels were well below the reported pathogenic threshold (∼100 ng/mL), they may have contributed to the elevated inflammatory responses observed during live microbial coculture. Given that LPS can activate hepatic TLR4 and downstream pro‐inflammatory NF‐κB signaling [46, 47], our findings are consistent with possible involvement of this pathway; however, this mechanism was not directly tested, and other microbe‐derived factors may also contribute.

Comparing Live Microbe Coculture with SCFA Supplementation on Hepatocyte Nuclear Receptors and Metabolic Functions

The cellular signaling pathways mediating induction of hepatocyte steatosis by FFAs and its reversal by SCFAs involve hepatic nuclear receptor transcription factors that regulate lipid metabolism, inflammation, and cellular homeostasis [48]. Many of these transcription factors are also involved in regulating hepatic metabolic enzymes, which would affect the liver's metabolic functions in transforming endogenous compounds and xenobiotics. Therefore, we examined transcriptional changes in nuclear receptor transcription factors and Cytochrome P450 enzymes in hepatocytes following FFA induction and subsequent treatment with either synthetic SCFAs or coculture with live Ctrl or But‐EcN micorbes.

Among the hepatic nuclear receptors, peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor‐alpha (PPARα) and Liver X Receptor (LXR) are the most directly regulated by circulating FFAs and SCFAs given their well‐established roles in lipid metabolism. Indeed, PPARα expressions were the most responsive to the different treatment conditions. The addition of FFA resulted in a 3‐fold reduction in PPARα expression, which was significantly reversed with the addition of NaB or coculture with But‐EcN (Figure 6A). This observation concurred with existing reports that butyrate is a potent activator of PPARα [49]. Liver X receptor (LXR) regulates de novo lipogenesis in the liver [50]. Hence, we observed a slight downregulation of LXR (‐2.2 fold) in the presence of excess exogenous FFA, which were not restored by either synthetic SCFAs or coculture with EcNs (Figure 6B). Constitutive Androstane Receptor (CAR) and Pregnane X Receptor (PXR) are xenobiotic nuclear receptors, which bind to a diverse class of xenobiotics compounds to regulate their metabolic transformation. Their roles in lipid metabolism are only emerging and less well‐understood [51]. Although activation of PXR is not directly linked to FFA, it promotes lipid uptake by hepatocytes through the upregulation of CD36, a fatty acid transporter, alongside repression of PPARα activity [52, 53]. Indeed, we observed a significant upregulation of PXR expression in steatotic hepatocytes, likely to cope with the increased load of circulating FFA by promoting lipid accumulation within the hepatocytes (Figure 6C). Interestingly, treatment with synthetic SCFAs or coculture with live EcNs attenuated PXR expression to levels lower or similar to untreated controls (Figure 6C). CAR is the sister nuclear receptor of PXR, which is constitutively active to protect against bile acid toxicity. Its role in lipid metabolism appeared to be more indirect [51], which is reflected in our result, where CAR expression did not change with FFA addition. However, there was a decrease in CAR expression in the presence of NaAc or live Ctrl or But‐EcN (Figure 6D). Retinoid X Receptor‐α (RXRα) is a heterodimerization partner of PPARα, LXR, PXR, and CAR to modulate genes regulating hepatic lipid metabolism and drug detoxification. Its gene expression was not affected by FFA treatment or exposure to NaB or But‐EcN. However, exposure to either synthetic NaAc or acetate producing Ctrl‐EcN significantly decreased its expression level (∼1.9‐fold) (Figure 6E), as compared to untreated control, likely due to acetate being a substrate for acetyl‐Co‐A, which is involved in the epigenetic regulation of RXRα [54].

Hepatocytes in the liver play an important role in the metabolism of endogenous and xenobiotic compounds, which is mediated by the cytochrome P450 (CYP450) isoenzymes, many of which are regulated by nuclear receptor transcription factors. Therefore, we next assessed the gene expression of key CYP450 isoenzymes to examine how they were differentially affected by the coculture of live probiotic microbes as compared to synthetic SCFAs. CYP2E1 is responsible for metabolizing ethanol and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and there exists an inverse regulatory relationship between CYP2E1 and PPARα [55]. Similarly, we observed that CYP2E1 expression exhibited an inverse trend to PPARα expression, where CYPE2E1 was significantly upregulated (+3.5‐fold compared to untreated control) in the presence of FFA. This elevated expression in the steatotic hepatocytes was reversed in the presence of synthetic SCFAs, NaAc (‐22‐fold) and NaB (‐3.8‐fold), or when cocultured with live microbes, Ctrl‐EcN (‐29.5‐fold) and But‐EcN (‐15.2‐fold) (Figure 7A). CYP7A1 is regulated by LXR and catalyzes the conversion of cholesterol to bile acids. While FFA treatment did not significantly affect LXR expression, FFA markedly increased the expression of CYP7A1, which was downregulated by either synthetic SCFAs and live microbe coculture (Figure 7B). CYP3A4, CYP1A2, and CYP2C19 are key liver CYP450 enzymes regulated by PXR and CAR. Interestingly, their expression did not align with PXR or CAR trends but was consistently downregulated across all treatments compared to untreated hepatocytes. The synthetic SCFAs exhibited selective effects relative to FFA‐treated hepatocytes, with NaAc preferentially reducing CYP3A4 (–37‐fold), while NaB more strongly suppressed CYP1A2 (‐3‐fold) and CYP2C19 (‐9‐fold) (Figure 7C–E). Notably, coculture with live microbes further repressed expression levels beyond those observed in steatotic hepatocytes, with Ctrl‐EcN reducing CYP3A4 by 250‐fold, CYP1A2 by 26‐fold, and CYP2C19 by ‐2.6‐fold, while But‐EcN decreased CYP3A4 by ∼19‐fold, CYP1A2 by 4.2‐fold, and CYP2C19 by ∼19‐fold (Figure 7C–E), likely reflecting the impact of inflammatory factors induced by live microbes on suppressing these drug metabolizing enzymes [56, 57, 58]. When we examined the expression of CYP2D6, which is mainly dependent on genetic regulation [59], we did not see as pronounced a decrease in the expression levels across all conditions (Figure 7F).

Discussion

Modeling complex liver diseases such as NAFLD using organ‐on‐chip platforms is an emerging area of research, particularly given the increasing recognition that gut–liver crosstalk contributes to disease progression and therapeutic response [6, 7]. Previous gut–liver models in the context of NAFLD have largely focused on how intestinal epithelial‐derived factors influence hepatic fatty acid uptake and steatosis progression [29, 30, 31], but have not explicitly examined how live gut microbes modulate steatotic liver tissue. By contrast, models that successfully incorporate live microbes have generally been restricted to gut‐on‐chip systems, in which microbes and intestinal epithelial cells are maintained in compartmentalized microfluidic environments with distinct media and oxygen conditions separated by a porous barrier [60, 61]. While these integrated systems have advanced the study of host–microbe interactions in the intestine, extending them to include an additional organ such as the liver remains challenging because of the need to synchronize multiple tissue types with markedly different culture requirements. In this study, we therefore developed a simplified modular microfluidic platform that enables live microbial and steatotic liver cultures to be established independently under their respective optimal conditions before being coupled through a shared circulating medium. This modular format provides a controlled approach to examining how live microbial exposure influences liver phenotypes and functions in a steatotic context.

Using this approach, we first established a steatosis‐on‐chip model based on HepaRG‐derived 3D hepatic spheroids cultured under dynamic perfusion (Figure 1). HepaRG spheroids exposed to FFAs (PA and OA) under dynamic conditions exhibited steatotic phenotypes, including elevated lipid accumulation (Figure 1B,C), reduced insulin sensitivity (measured by pAKT/AKT ratio upon insulin exposure, (Figure 1E–G), and increased cell death (Figure 1D), likely due to increased generation of ROS from fatty acid oxidation in hepatocyte mitochondria and peroxisomes [62, 63, 64]. The steatotic liver‐on‐chip model also captured biologically relevant changes in inflammatory and metabolic pathways. FFA‐treated HepaRG spheroids showed increased CCL2 expression (Figure 5D), consistent with reports [65, 66] linking lipotoxic stress to chemokine induction and immune cell recruitment during NAFLD progression. However, IL6 expression remained relatively modest (Figure 5A), consistent with prior observations that hepatocyte‐only cultures lacking immune or adipose components display limited inflammatory amplification [67]. The model further recapitulated dysregulation of hepatic nuclear receptors and CYP450 enzymes associated with steatosis (Figures 6 and 7). In particular, we observed the inverse relationship between PPARα and CYP2E1 that has been reported in fatty liver disease [55], supporting the relevance of the model for probing links between steatosis, inflammatory signaling, and metabolic dysfunction.

SCFAs, particularly butyrate, are being explored as therapeutic agents for NAFLD due to their diverse effects, including epigenetic modulation, activation of hepatic nuclear receptors, and anti‐inflammatory properties. Delivering SCFAs via probiotics offers a sustainable and physiological approach through the gut‐liver axis. Using the modular microfluidic coculture system, we could examine the effects of administering synthetic SCFAs (acetate and butyrate) to those of Ctrl or But‐EcN probiotic strains, which preferentially produce acetate and butyrate, respectively, on the steatotic liver‐on‐chip model (Figure 3F,G). Both approaches showed similar anti‐steatotic effects, reducing lipid accumulation with minimal impact on hepatocyte viability, although neither restored insulin sensitivity (Figure 4). Our findings concurred with existing reports on the anti‐steatotic properties of butyrate [41] but do not necessarily reflect GLP‐1R downregulation and insulin sensitivity recovery in NAFLD animal models [68]. However, a limitation of this study is that exogenous SCFA treatment and microbial coculture differ in both concentration and mode of delivery. Specifically, synthetic SCFAs were administered as a single dose (3 mm), whereas microbial coculture resulted in lower, dynamically generated butyrate levels (∼1.4 mm) within the systemic compartment. As such, these conditions are not directly comparable and represent distinct exposure scenarios, which should be considered when interpreting the observed effects.

An important observation in this study was that live microbial coculture did not phenocopy exogenous SCFA treatment. While both reduced lipid accumulation, coculture with Ctrl or But‐EcN elicited additional inflammatory and metabolic changes that were not observed with synthetic SCFAs alone. In particular, steatotic hepatocytes exposed to live microbes showed elevated IL6 and IL8 expression (Figure 5) and more pronounced suppression of selected nuclear receptors and CYP450 genes (Figures 6 and 7). These findings suggest that liver responses to live microbes reflect the combined action of multiple microbe‐derived signals rather than SCFAs alone. For instance, we could detect very low levels of endotoxins in both Ctrl and But‐EcN strains (Figure 5E). Even though EcN lacks pathogenicity due to its truncated form of LPS [45], it can still have proinflammatory properties through the activation of the NF‐kB and MAPK pathways [69]. However, the present study does not directly establish endotoxin as the dominant mechanism, and other microbial products, including non‐SCFA metabolites, may also have contributed. The stronger repression of PXR‐, CAR‐, and CYP450‐associated pathways in the live microbial conditions (Figures 6 and 7) may therefore reflect indirect effects of a more inflammatory microenvironment rather than the direct action of acetate or butyrate alone [56, 57, 58].

This distinction between defined microbial SCFA supplementation and live microbial exposure highlights an important translational point for engineered microbial therapeutics. Although engineering microbes to enhance the production of beneficial microbial metabolites, such as butyrate, is an attractive strategy, whole‐cell microbial interventions introduce a broader spectrum of host‐relevant signals that may alter tissue responses in unintended ways. In our study, the engineered butyrate‐producing EcN strain reproduced some expected beneficial effects on hepatic lipid accumulation and PPARα‐associated responses, yet live microbial coculture also remained associated with inflammatory signaling and broader suppression of drug‐metabolizing pathways. These findings underscore the importance of evaluating both desired and unintended effects of engineered microbes using systems that can capture integrated tissue responses beyond a single metabolite endpoint.

Several limitations of the current microbial‐liver coculture system should be acknowledged. First, the microbial barrier within the microbial module is acellular and consists of only of a mucin‐coated porous membrane. While it could effectively prevent microbial translocation and allow diffusion of microbial‐derived soluble factors into systemic circulation (Figure 3), the physiological relevance of the model could be improved by incorporating intestinal epithelial cells into the barrier. Second, the introduction of nitrogen gas [70, 71] or incorporating oxygen‐scavenging hydrogel materials, as demonstrated in our previous work [72, 73], could better mimic the anaerobic conditions of the gut lumen. Third, the steatotic liver module consisted of HepaRG‐derived hepatocytes and did not include Kupffer cells, stellate cells, liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, all of which are important contributors to model hepatic inflammation and NAFLD progression in vitro [74, 75]. Incorporating these additional cell types would improve the physiological relevance of the model and may enable future studies of steatohepatitis and fibrotic progression. Finally, the present study was not designed to deconvolute the relative contributions of SCFAs, endotoxin, and other microbial products, and additional controls or perturbation studies will be required in future work to define the causal mediators of the hepatic responses observed here.

Despite these limitations, the modular microfluidic platform presented here provides a useful and experimentally tractable system for comparing the effects of exogenous metabolites and live engineered microbes on steatotic liver tissue. Rather than aiming to fully recapitulate the complete gut–liver axis, the platform offers a controlled reductionist framework for studying how live microbial exposure alters hepatic phenotypes and metabolic functions under disease‐relevant conditions.

Conclusions

This study successfully established a microfluidic steatosis‐on‐chip model, which effectively recapitulated key steatotic phenotypes, including lipid accumulation, hepatic inflammation, and insulin resistance. Our findings highlight the hepatoprotective effects of SCFAs, particularly butyrate, in mitigating FFA‐induced steatosis by reducing lipid accumulation, though without restoring insulin sensitivity. While coculturing hepatocytes with butyrate‐producing EcN improved steatosis, it also led to the compromise of xenobiotic CYP450 enzymes, suggesting potential unintended metabolic effects. This study represents one of the first real‐time in vitro models incorporating live microbial cocultures with steatotic hepatocytes, providing valuable insights into the effects of microbial metabolites on liver steatosis. This platform shows that live microbial interventions cannot be inferred from metabolite supplementation alone and may therefore be valuable for the preclinical evaluation of engineered microbial therapeutics intended for metabolic liver disease.

5 Materials and Methods

HepaRG Culture and Spheroid Formation

HepaRG progenitor cells (CVCLO‐9720, Biopredic International, France) were maintained and differentiated as previously reported [32]. Briefly, undifferentiated HepaRG cells were cultured in HepaRG growth media for 14 days; thereupon, cells were maintained in the adaptive medium for 3 days before switching to the differentiation medium for another 14 days. The HepaRG growth and differentiation mediums were prepared by supplementing human William's E (HWE) medium (#W1878, Sigma–Aldrich, USA) with HepaRG growth medium supplement and differentiation medium supplement (#ADD710 and #ADD720, Biopredic, France), respectively. The adaptive medium was prepared by mixing the growth and differentiation media at 1:1 ratio. Once the HepaRG‐hepatocytes were differentiated, they were trypsinized and seeded into AggreWell 400 plates (#34411, StemCell Technologies, Canada) at a density of 2.4 × 105 cells per well to form 3D spheroids. After 48 h of incubation, HepaRG hepatocyte spheroids were harvested from the Aggrewells and collected into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube to allow sedimentation of spheroids for 10 min. Once the spheroids sedimented to the bottom of the tube, medium was removed and replaced with fresh HWE medium to resuspend the HepaRG hepatocyte spheroids prior to seeding them in the prepared microfluidic liver chips.

Microfluidic 3D Liver Chip Fabrication

We employed a previously established microfluidic liver chip. Details of the chip have been described in previous studies [32]. Briefly, the devices were fabricated by pouring a mixture of base and curing agent of Sylgard 184 polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) (#206562, Dow Corning, Midland, MI) onto the DRIE‐etched silicon substrates. The devices consisted of a culture compartment (200 µm wide × 100 µm high), flanked by two perfusion channels (200 µm wide × 100 µm high) at each side. The cell compartment is separated from the perfusion channels by an array of micropillars (50 µm × 30 µm) with a 20 µm gap size, to allow immobilization of cells in high density. Once the replica PDMS is cured, it is removed from the mold, and holes were created for inlet and outlet reservoirs. Next, the prepared replica was irreversibly bonded to a thin film of PDMS (50 µm) by oxygen plasma bonding. The perfusion inlet and outlet of the device were connected to 3 mL fluid dispensing reservoirs (#7366044, Nordson EFD, Westlake, OH) using Tygon tubings fitted with blunt tip needle (#EW‐50106‐20, Cole Parmer) and one‐way stopcocks with Luer connections (#EW‐48523‐26, Cole‐Parmer, Vernon Hills, IL).

Seeding and Perfusion Culture of HepaRG‐Hepatocyte Spheroids in Microfluidic Liver Chips

Cell seeding into the microfluidic device was performed to previously established protocols [32]. First, the outlet of the microfluidic device was attached to a one‐way stop‐cock with Luer connections (#EW‐48523‐26, Cole‐Parmer, USA) via Tygon tubing and connected to a 1 mL syringe attached on a syringe pump (KD scientific, USA). A cell seeding reservoir comprising a one‐way stopcock attached to a 30‐gauge blunt tip needle (#7018462, Nordson EFD, USA) was inserted into the cell seeding port of the microfluidic device. The pre‐formed HepaRG‐hepatocyte spheroids suspended in fresh HWE medium were pipetted into the cell seeding reservoir. The syringe pump was set to withdraw at a 0.1 mL/h flow rate via the perfusion outlet. The micropillar array will immobilize the hepatocyte spheroids, and seeding was stopped when the central cell culture compartment was fully filled with spheroids. Perfusion culture was performed using a gravity‐driven flow assembly previously established in the group [32], where a constant hydrostatic pressure between the inlet and outlet reservoir was used to drive medium flow. The perfusion inlet and outlets were connected to 3 mL fluid dispensing syringe barrels (#7366044, Nordson EFD, USA) which served as medium reservoirs, via 90° Optimum angled dispense tips (#7018274, Nordson EFD, USA) and one‐way stopcock valves. The reservoirs were positioned on a custom 3D‐printed support frame, creating a 3 mm height difference that generated an average flow rate of ∼70 µL/h [32]. Fresh culture medium was added to the inlet reservoir every 24 h.

Microbial Module Fabrication

The microbial module consisted of 2 separate chambers: a microbial chamber and a systemic chamber. The internal dimensions of the microbial chamber were 17 mm (length) × 6 mm (width) × 8 mm (height), while that of the systemic chamber was 15 mm (length) × 6 mm (width) × 6 mm (height). The two chambers were micromilled (Datron) on 10 mm thick polycarbonate (PC) blocks. Designs of the chambers were first drawn with Solidworks software (SolidWorks Corp., Dassault Systèmes) followed by converted to codes using by Fusion 360 (Autodesk) before micromilling the designs on the PC blocks. Next, the interior of the microbial chamber was coated with a PDMS mixture. This allowed better securing of tubings to the inlet and outlet of the chamber and better sealing to the mucin‐agar porous membrane during assembly to the systemic chamber. The mucin‐agar porous membrane was prepared by first cutting out the PET membrane from Transwell inserts (1.0 µm pore size; #353104, Corning, USA) and then placing a PDMS gasket on the apical side of the membrane. The mucin‐agar was prepared by dissolving 200 mg Luria Broth (LB) agar (#L3147, Sigma, Singapore) in 10 mL autoclaved water. This mixture was heated (∼ 60°C) and stirred until all the agar had dissolved. Next, agar, mucin, HWE medium, D‐alanine (#A7377, Sigma, Singapore), and chloramphenicol (#C3175, Sigma, Singapore) were mixed in a centrifuge tube in the ratios of 400:500:100:1:1 (v/v) and subsequently pipetted onto the apical side of the cut‐out porous membrane and allowed to set under UV‐lamp inside the BSC hood to maintain sterility. Once the mucin‐agar coating was set, the mucin‐agar‐coated membrane was assembled with the two chambers of the microbial chambers, with the mucin‐agar facing the microbial chamber and the basal side facing the systemic chamber. M2 screws were used to fix the chambers to ensure a leak‐free module. Finally, Tygon tubing (0.5 mm ID, Cole‐Palmer, USA) was inserted into the inlets and outlets of the chambers and fixed in place by curing PDMS. At the outlet of each chamber, a one‐way valve (ColeParmer, USA) was connected to the tubing.

Functional Characterization of Microbial Module

The functional characterization of the microbial module was performed using a dye diffusion study and microbial growth kinetics (Figure S1). The dye diffusion study was performed by inoculating the two stains of EcN onto mucin‐agar plates. Once growth was established, multiple‐colored dyes were perfused into the chambers at a flow rate of 10 µl/min to ascertain that there were no leakages. Color changes were observed in the dyes at the outlets of each chamber, and spectrometer analysis confirmed the diffusion of dyes across the mucin‐agar porous membrane. To measure the microbial growth kinetics, E.coli was inoculated in LB broth and cultured at the optimal growth temperature, shaking to promote aeration. The growth kinetics of E. coli were measured at regular intervals for over 48 h. The samples from the culture were measured at optical density (OD600) using a spectrophotometer. The OD600 absorbance was normalized to non‐inoculated LB medium.

Microbial Culture and Culturing in Microfluidic Microbial Module

Two auxotrophic mutant strains of E. coli Nissle were used in the studies: a control E.coli Nissle strain with 2 genes deleted for plasmid stability (Ctrl‐EcN); and an engineered butyrate‐producing EcN (But‐EcN) strain, where Ctrl‐EcN was modified to harbor a plasmid encoding a synthetic butyrogenic pathway (pC4), with five additional genes involved in acetate, ethanol, succinate, and lactate synthesis pathways deleted [17]. The GFP‐E.coli strain was a gift from A/Prof Gan Yunn Hwen from the National University of Singapore. Briefly, the EcN strains were maintained on lysogeny agar at 37°C [76]. One colony of each strain of EcN was picked and resuspended in 10 mL of HWE with FFA and supplemented with or without chloramphenicol. After 24 h of incubation at 37°C, shaking at 225 rpm, 300 µl of Ctrl‐ or But‐EcN suspension was diluted in 3 mL of HWE with FFA. Microbes were cultured in the microfluidic microbial module under static conditions for 24–48 h. Microbes were injected into the microbial chamber via Tygon tubing (0.5 mm ID) with a 1 mL syringe. Syringes were left there to prevent any flow of the fluid out of the chamber. The adjacent systemic chamber was connected via Tygon tubing to a three‐way valve (Cole Palmer, USA), which was closed off to prevent any fluid exchange between the two chambers.

Dynamic Recirculatory Coculture of Microbial and Liver Modules

The microbial and liver modules were cocultured by connecting them together using Tygon tubing (0.5 mm ID) to a custom 3D‐printed micro‐peristaltic pump designed for low‐flow microfluidic applications (10–100 µL/h) [77]. The pump was fitted with ethanol‐sterilized silicone tubing (0.5 mm ID, AliExpress, China), chosen for its durability under continuous compression cycles. The pump system supported up to three simultaneous coculture setups, maintaining a recirculatory flow rate of ∼70 µL/h to match the liver chip's pump‐free configuration.

Free‐Fatty Acid Treatment to Induce Hepatic Steatosis

Two fatty acids, oleate acid (O1383, Sigma, Singapore) and palmitic acid (P5585, Sigma, Singapore) were first dissolved in 0.1 M sodium hydroxide at about 70°C in a water bath. Following that, the fatty acids were conjugated to 10% bovine serum albumin (A1595, BSA, Sigma, Singapore) for 2h at room temperature on a rocker (set at 45 rpm). After 2 h, cell culture media were supplemented with 100 µm conjugated oleate acid and 50 µm palmitic acid. Lastly, HepaRG spheroids in the microfluidic device were cultured under perfusion with this FFA‐HWE media. This media devoid of penicillin and streptomycin were also used to culture the Ctrl and But‐EcN in the microbial studies.

Exogenous SCFA Treatment of Steatotic Hepatocytes

To investigate the impact of short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs), 3D hepatocyte spheroids were cultured in a microfluidic device under dynamic flow for 5 days in media with or without free fatty acids (FFAs). On day 6, FFA‐supplemented cultures were treated with either 3 mm sodium butyrate or 3 mm sodium acetate. All conditions were maintained for an additional 48 h under continuous flow prior to analysis.

Lipid Staining and Imaging in Microfluidic Device

HepaRG‐hepatocyte cultures in the microfluidic devices were labeled with fluorescent dyes to visualize lipid accumulation. The device was first rinsed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (#1.00496, Sigma–Aldrich, Singapore) for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were then rinsed with PBS twice. Samples that were stained for lipid droplets were incubated with BODIPY 493/503 (#D3922, ThermoFisher, 1:1000) and DAPI (#D1306, ThermoFisher) diluted in PBS (1:1000) for 2 h at room temperature. After that, cells were rinsed thrice with PBS before putting mounting media and analyzed under a confocal microscope (LSM 800 confocal microscope, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Cell Viability Assessment in the Microfluidic Device

A double‐nuclei staining method reported earlier [78] was applied to assess cell viability by labelling necrotic cells and the total cell population. Briefly, necrotic cells were labelled in live cultures with 4 µM of Ethidium homodimer‐1 (EthD, #E1169, ThermoFisher, Singapore) prepared in cell culture media for 2 h at 37°C. Following that, the HepaRG cultures in the microfluidic devices were rinsed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (#1.00496, Sigma–Aldrich, Singapore) for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were then rinsed with PBS twice followed by incubating with DAPI (#D1306, ThermoFisher, 1:1000) diluted in PBS for 2 h in room temperature. Lastly, cells were rinsed twice with PBS followed by putting mounting medium (FluorShield, Millipore) and analyzed under a LSM 800 confocal microscope). Cell viability was calculated based on the ratio of necrotic cells (stained EthD cells) with total number of cells (stained with DAPI) as follows [78] 1−NectroticcellsTotalnumberofcells×100%

Insulin Response Studies

The insulin response of HepaRG‐hepatocytes was evaluated by incubating untreated or FFA‐treated steatotic HepaRG‐hepatocytes with HWE without insulin for 2 h at 37°C. Following that, samples that were to be stimulated with insulin were incubated for another 30 min at 37°C with clean HWE (devoid of insulin) supplemented with 100 nm insulin (#I9278, Sigma–Aldrich, Singapore). Next, samples were rinsed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (#1.00496, Sigma–Aldrich, Singapore) for 30 min at room temperature before carrying out the subsequent immunofluorescent staining for AKT and p‐AKT. Briefly, cells in the microfluidic device were fixed as described above before they were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 (#93443, Sigma–Aldrich, Singapore) for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were rinsed with PBS twice before incubating with a blocking buffer—PBS supplemented with 2% BSA and 0.1% Tween‐20 for 2 h at room temperature. Next, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies mouse monoclonal anti‐AKT (#2920S, Cell Signaling, 1:250) and rabbit polyclonal anti‐p‐AKT(#4060S, Cell Signalling, 1:400) diluted in blocking buffer at 4°C overnight. The following day, cells were rinsed twice thoroughly with PBS before counter‐stained with secondary antibodies for 2 h in room temperature. Cells were later rinsed twice with PBS and incubated with mounting media before acquiring images with a confocal microscope.

Quantitative Analysis of p‐AKT/AKT Expressions

3D images of the HepaRG samples that were stimulated with or without insulin were captured with a confocal microscope at a slice interval of 2 µm. After the full body imaging, representative slices of the 3D HepaRG spheroids were obtained at half the spheroid depth. Tissue thickness was manually determined by using the live mode and inputting the depth equivalent to half of the tissue thickness in the Imaris software. Image analysis and visualization were performed on Imaris using the built‐in colocaliation function by highlighting the fluorophores of interest. Quantitative PCR Analysis of Liver‐Specific Genes

RNA was extracted from HepaRG cells cultured in controlled conditions, conditioned media, and re‐circulation studies by TRIzoL reagent (#15596026, ThermoFisher, Singapore). Following that, RNA was purified from lysate using a RNeasy Microkit (#74004, Qiagen, USA). Total amount of RNA was determined using the ‘Nanodrop’ function in a Tecan Spark spectrophotometer (Scimed, Singapore) and converted to cDNA using Tetro cDNA synthesis kits (#BIO‐65043, Bioline, UK). Quantitative PCR was performed using FastStart Universal SYBR Green reagent (#04913914001, Roche, Switzerland) on a Real Time PCR system (QuantStudio3, Life Technologies, Thermofisher Scientific, USA). Details of the primers used in the qPCR studies were summarized in Table S1.

Quantification of Microbial Metabolites Using Gas Chromatography Mass Spectrometry (GCMS)

The supernatants were collected and filter‐sterilized to remove any cell debris. 1.5 mL of 10% hydrochloric acid (#320331, Sigma–Aldrich)‐methanol (#34860, Sigma–Aldrich) (v/v) was added to 2 mL of collected supernatants and incubated at 62°C for 3 h. Then, the supernatant mixtures were cooled to room temperature and extracted by vortexing with 2 mL of hexane (#34859, Sigma–Aldrich) for 2 min. After centrifugation at 10 000 g for 10 min, the top hexane layer was collected and used to perform GC‐MS quantification of butyrate (as methyl ester) with the method developed by Yu et al. [79]. Butyric acid (#19215, Sigma–Aldrich) and isovaleric acid (#78651, Sigma–Aldrich) were used as external and internal standards, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analysis were carried out using the software GraphPad Prism‐9. Data in graphs are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise stated. The number of samples examined for the various studies for each data are specified in the corresponding figures. Two‐group comparisons were performed by unpaired Student's t‐test, unless stated otherwise. Comparison among three or more groups was performed by analysis of variance and Tukey‐Kramer multiple range test, unless stated otherwise. Probability value of p < 0.05 was considered significant and indicated with * in the figures. Pearson's correlation coefficient (PCC) calculated by Imaris (Bitplane, Switzerland), was used to express the colocalization of the two fluorophores (AKT and p‐AKT) in the immunofluorescence co‐staining experiment.
