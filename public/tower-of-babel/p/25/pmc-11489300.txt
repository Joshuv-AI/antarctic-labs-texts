CRYAB Promotes Colorectal Cancer Progression by Inhibiting Ferroptosis Through Blocking TRIM55-Mediated β-Catenin Ubiquitination and Degradation
Authors: Haiyan Xia, Jingwen Chen, Wenbo Zhang, Ying Xu, Yongjun Nai, Xiaowei Wei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Backgroundα-Crystallin B (CRYAB) is a chaperone member of the HSPs family that protects proteins with which it interacts from degradation. This study aims to investigate the effect of CRYAB on the progression of colorectal cancer (CRC) and its underlying mechanism.MethodsCRYAB expression was evaluated in CRC tissues. Cell growth was tested by CCK-8 kit. Lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS) assays, lipid peroxidation assays, glutathione assays were used to assess the degree of cellular lipid peroxidation of CRC cells. The potential signal pathways of CRYAB were analyzed and verified by Western blot (WB) and immunoprecipitation (Co-IP).ResultsCRYAB expression was elevated in CRC tissues and exhibited sensitivity and specificity in predicting CRC. Functionally, knockdown of CRYAB induced ferroptosis in CRC cells. Mechanistically, CRYAB binding prevented from β-catenin interacting with TRIM55, leading to an increase in β-catenin protein stability, which desensitized CRC cells to ferroptosis and ultimately accelerated cancer progression.ConclusionsTargeting CRYAB might be a promising strategy to enhance ferroptosis and improve the efficacy of CRC therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10620-024-08584-6.

Introduction

Globally, colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of death in both men and women, with the third highest incidence after lung and breast cancers, and with a trend toward younger adults [1]. Early diagnosis of the disease is a prerequisite for improving the survival of CRC patients, and current diagnostic methods based on carcinoembryonic antigen and colonoscopy are limited by low sensitivity, specificity, and patient discomfort [2, 3]. Considering the highly heterogeneous nature of CRC in different genetic backgrounds, in the era of precision medicine, alterations in molecules associated with the development and progression of CRC should be better understood to reveal their key mechanisms leading to CRC malignancy in order to develop new diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Cell death is critical for normal development, homeostasis, and prevention of hyperproliferative diseases (e.g., cancer) in multicellular lifeforms [4]. In the clinic, therapeutic modalities for malignant tumors (chemotherapy, radiation therapy) usually rely on the activation of cell death pathways to induce cancer cell extinction for therapeutic purposes [5]. However, tumor cells can subtly evade the occurrence of cell death [6], therefore, it is particularly important to explore the specific mechanisms and regulatory targets of tumor cell death. Ferroptosis is an iron- and reactive oxygen species (ROS)-dependent mode of programmed cell death [7], which arises by mechanisms including iron overload, lipid peroxidation, glutathione (GSH) depletion, and decreased glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity [8, 9]. A growing body of research suggested that multiple factors could directly or indirectly modulate the sensitivity of tumor cells to ferroptosis. In CRC, targeting the GPX4/GSH system was an important pathway to trigger or inhibit ferroptosis, and the relevant genes were involved in ATF3, CDKN2A, and GDF7, among others [10]. For example, overexpression of a key protein for iron homeostasis, lipid transporter protein 2, promoted tumor progression and exacerbated chemoresistance through inhibition of ferroptosis, mainly because lipid transporter protein 2 stimulated the expression of GPX4 and xCT [11]. In addition, oncogenes p53 and HO-1 have been identified to be involved in the regulation of ferroptosis in CRC [12]. Activated p53 could induce cellular ferroptosis by blocking SLC7A11, a key molecule in the ferroptosis pathway [13]. Whether other proteins could regulate ferroptosis and influence CRC progression is still unknown.

The ubiquitin (Ub)–proteasome system (UPS) is responsible for the degradation of more than 80% of intracellular proteins and has a wide range of regulatory effects on organismal biological processes [14, 15]. Published studies have indicated that the UPS could dominate ferroptosis susceptibility by directly targeting key ferroptosis molecules. In bladder cancer, PHGDH, an important molecule for serine metabolism, inhibited ubiquitin-dependent degradation of PCBP2, and subsequent high expression of PCBP2-stabilized SLC7A11, thereby blocking tumor ferroptosis and exacerbating malignant tumor progression [16]. In contrast, in hepatocellular carcinoma, suppressor of cytokine signaling 2 (SOCS2) relied on UPS to promote the polyubiquitination degradation of SLC7A11, leading to the sensitization of ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma cells [17]. In CRC, the E3 ubiquitin ligase HECTD3 triggered polyubiquitination and degradation of downstream ferroptosis-associated proteins and enhanced cellular ferroptosis [18]. The above reports supported that UPS-selective ubiquitination or deubiquitylation of proteins contributed to the dynamic regulation of intracellular iron levels, which in turn regulates ferroptosis.

α-B-crystallin (CRYAB), a major member of the HSPs family, is also a chaperone protein, which protects proteins with which it interacts from degradation [19]. In CRC, CRYAB was considered to be a promoter of tumor proliferation and progression and could be used as a diagnostic indicator of CRC prognosis. CRYAB has been reported to associate closely with UPS [20]. CRYAB protects β-catenin, a target protein of UPS, from ubiquitination and degradation. In addition, CRYAB might rely on proteasomal degradation mechanisms to regulate ferroptosis in BMSC cells [21]. However, how CRYAB interacts with β-catenin to regulate Ub levels and how this interaction affects ferroptosis remains unclear.

In this study, using CRYAB as an example, we emphasized the role of specific protein-mediated ferroptosis in CRC. We found that CRYAB was highly expressed in CRC tissues and cells. Functional studies showed that CRYAB knockdown inhibited CRC cell viability and induced ferroptosis, whereas overexpression of CRYAB exhibited the opposite effect. More importantly, CRYAB bonded to β-catenin proteins and impeded TRIM55-mediated ubiquitination and degradation. Together, we identified potential novel mechanisms of CRYAB in promoting CRC progression, which might provide therapeutic targets for CRC patients.

Materials and Methods

Human Tissue Samples and Cell Culture

A total of 19 paired CRC tumor and adjacent noncancerous tissue samples were collected according to standard institutional protocols from Nanjing First Hospital. The study was reviewed and authorized by the Ethics Committee of our institution and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

All cell lines, including a human normal colonic epithelial cell (NCM460) and four CRC cell lines (HCT116, RKO, SW480, SW620), were purchased from the Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The above cell lines were maintained in commercially available DMEM medium (Corning, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco-BRL) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma, USA) at 37 °C and humidified with 5% CO2.

Plasmid and Cell Transfection

The shRNA targeting CRYAB was inserted into the pLKO.1-RFP vector to construct the PLKO.1-sh-CRYAB plasmid (labeled sh-CRYAB, TOP: CCGGGAAGAGCGCCAGGATGAACATCTCGAGATGTTCATCCTGGCGCTCTTCTTTTTG; Bottom: CTTCTCGCGGTCCTACTTGTAGAGCTCTACAAGTAGGACCGCGAGAAGAAAAACTTAA). His-tagged CRYAB plasmid was obtained from Sino Biological (Beijing, China). The corresponding overexpression plasmids (labeled TRIM6, TRIM24, TRIM47, TRIM52, and TRIM55) were constructed by cloning the coding sequences of CRYAB, TRIM6, TRIM24, TRIM47, TRIM52, and TRIM55 into a pcDNA3.0 expression vector. The TRIM55 sequence or β-catenin sequence was cloned into the expression vectors PCMV5-HA or pRK7-Flag, respectively, to generate the following plasmids: HA-TRIM55 and Flag-β-catenin. GST-tagged Ub wild type (GST-Ub WT) and mutant plasmids (GST-Ub K48R, GST-Ub K63R) were obtained from GeneWiz (Nanjing, China). The constructed plasmids were transfected with RKO or SW480 cells for 48 h with the help of Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) reagent.

Cell Viability Assay

Viability of log-phase RKO and SW480 cells was determined using the CCK-8 kit (Dojindo, Japan). Cells (3000 cells/well) were inoculated into 96-well plates containing culture medium and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, CCK-8 reaction reagent (10 μL) was added to the cultures and maintained at 37 °C for 2 h. The cells were then incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm of each well was measured using an automated enzyme marker (Molecular Device, USA) to calculate cell viability.

Determination of Intracellular Iron, Lipid Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), Malondialdehyde (MDA), and Glutathione (GSH) Levels

Intracellular levels of unstable iron (Fe2+) were assayed using an iron assay kit purchased from Abcam. According to the standard experimental protocol, 1.5 × 105 sh-NC, sh-CRYAB, Vector, CRYAB, CRYAB + vector, or CRYAB + TRIM55 group cells were homogenized in iron assay buffer and incubated with 5 µL of assay buffer for 30 min. Immediately after incubation of the above mixtures with 100 µL of iron probe for 60 min at 37 °C protected from light, the optical density (OD) of the reaction mixtures at 593 nm was evaluated on an enzyme labeling instrument.

Intracellular ROS levels were assessed relying on the C11-BODIPY 581/591 (50 ummol, Thermo Fisher) lipid peroxidation fluorescent probe and flow cytometry. Briefly, RKO and SW480 were transfected with specific plasmids and then stained with C11-BODIPY 581/591 as required for 1 h (protected from light, 37 °C). PBS solution was performed to remove excess dye and then changes in cellular fluorescence intensity were analyzed by flow cytometry (Accuri C6, BD Bioscience).

Malondialdehyde assay kit (Abcam, USA) and commercial GSH assay kit (Solarbio, China) were used to determine MDA and GSH content in CRC cell lysates, respectively.

Glutathione Peroxidase (GPx) Activity

GPx activity within cell samples was assessed using the GPx Activity Assay Kit (Solarbio, China). RKO and SW480 cell lysates, preheated GSH-PX assay working solution were sequentially added to a quartz cuvette and mixed, and A340 was read by spectrophotometer immediately after mixing. At this time, the initial value was recorded. After incubation for 3 min at 37 °C, A340 was read again. The blank tubes were operated in the same way as the sample tubes except that distilled water was used in place of the cell lysates. Finally, GPx activity in the cells was calculated according to the formula provided in the manual.

Western Blotting and Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For western blotting, cultured CRC cells were lysed in 300 μL of RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Scientific) mixed with protease inhibitors. After determining the protein concentration (BCA Protein Quantification Kit, Merck), an aliquot of denatured protein samples was loaded and separated on 15% SDS-PAGE and then transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore). Subsequently, the PVDF membrane was incubated with the target antibody (anti-β-catenin, 1:1000, ab224803, Abcam) overnight. After TBST washing, the membrane continued to be incubated with HRP-coupled secondary antibody (1:6500, ab205718, Abcam) for 2 h. Finally, the blot was visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence kit (Millipore).

For immunoprecipitation, lysates from CRC cells transfected with appropriate plasmids were extracted for IP. For IP of HA-tagged, Flag-tagged, or His-tagged proteins, lysates were incubated overnight with Anti-HA magnetic beads (Beyotime, P2121), Anti-Flag M2 magnetic beads (Sigma-Aldrich, M8823), or Anti-His magnetic beads (MedChenExpress, HY-K0209) at 4 °C. Beads were collected to release immunoprecipitated protein samples and used for subsequent western blotting analysis.

In Vivo Ubiquitination Assay

The GST-Ub construct and HA-TRIM55 and Flag-β-catenin were transfected into RKO and SW480 cells. After 24–48 h of transfection, cells were lysed in ubiquitination lysis buffer and sonicated. Immunoprecipitation was performed with anti-Flag antibody (Thermo Fisher), followed by western blot analysis of the precipitated complexes with anti-GST antibody (Merck).

Protein Stability and Degradation Analysis

RKO and SW480 cells transfected with TRIM55 overexpression plasmid or negative control plasmid were co-incubated with MG132 (10 μM, MCE), or incubated with CHX (50 μg/mL, MCE) alone for different times (0, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h) to obtain total cellular proteins and analyzed by western blotting using anti-β-catenin antibody.

RT-qPCR

RNA from CRC clinical tissues and cell lines was isolated by RNAiso Plus kit (TaKaRa, China). After quantification of RNA levels, complementary RNAs were generated using a reverse transcription kit (RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit, Thermo). RNAs for quantification of CRYAB and TRIMAB were isolated by using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, China) and an ABI Prism® Model 7500 Fluorescent Quantitative PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) was applied to perform PCR amplification for quantification of CRYAB, TRIM55, and GAPDH. The relative transcript levels of the target RNAs were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and their expression levels were normalized to the expression of GAPDH. The primers used in this study were as follows: TRIM55, F, 5′-TGGTTTTGGGATAGACATGGGGGT-3′ and R, 5′-CTGGTGGGACTCCTGCTTGTA-3′. CRYAB, F, 5′-CAGCTGGTTTGACACTGGAC-3′ and R, 5′-TGGCGCTCTTCATGTTTTCC-3′.

Statistical Analysis

GraphPad Prism 8.0 software was applied to analyze the data in this study. All experiments used were conducted at least three times independently and measurements were reported as mean ± SD. Statistical differences between two consecutive groups of data were compared using Student’s t test, and comparisons of data between 3 and more groups were made utilizing one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Elevated CRYAB Expression in CRC Promises to Be a Diagnostic Marker for CRC

To explore the possible oncogenic or tumor suppressive role of CRYAB in CRC, we first investigated CRYAB levels in 19 pairs of tumor samples and adjacent non-tumor tissues. As shown in Fig. 1A, CRYAB expression was significantly higher in CRC tumor tissues than in non-tumor tissues. Next, we analyzed the diagnostic efficacy of CRYAB using the ROC curve. The results showed that the area under the ROC curve (AUC) of CRYAB was 0.9501 (Fig. 1B, P < 0.001), suggesting that CRYAB might be a sensitive and specific biomarker for the diagnosis of CRC. In addition, the expression of CRYAB was significantly increased in all four CRC cell lines (HCT116, RKO, SW480, SW620) compared to the NCM460 cell line (Fig. 1C). These results suggest that CRC development might involve differential expression of CRYAB. Knockdown of CRYAB Promotes Ferroptosis in CRC Cells In Vitro

Previous studies have identified CRYAB as a key gene with significant effects on ferroptosis in CRC [22], and to continue to investigate the potential role of CRYAB in ferroptosis, we generated stable CRYAB-knockdown CRC cells using an shRNA in RKO and SW480 cells and quantified CRYAB levels (Fig. 2A). Importantly, we found that knockdown of CRYAB significantly reduced the viability of RKO and SW480 cells (Fig. 2B). Next, we analyzed GPX activity, GSH concentration, lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS), ferrous ion levels, and other indicators of ferroptosis evaluation. It is well known that overloading of ferrous iron (Fe2+) is necessary for ferroptosis, and we found that CRYAB knockdown substantially increased intracellular Fe2+ levels (Fig. 2C). In addition, lowering CRYAB further induced a decrease in GSH concentration and GPX activity (Fig. 2D and E), and GSH depletion and its induced GPX inactivation disrupted redox homeostasis in cells and induced ferroptosis [23]. We then detected that CRYAB knockdown increased the accumulation of lipid ROS, as well as the content of the lipid peroxidation product MDA in CRC cells (Fig. 2F and G). CRYAB Induces β-Catenin Protein Degradation in CRC Cells

Next, we attempted to decipher the possible molecular mechanisms by which CRYAB functions in CRC. The top 50 genes with the highest connectivity to CRYAB were screened using the STRING database, and CTNNB1 (a gene encoding a β-catenin protein) was identified as a key gene associated with CRYAB (Fig. 3A). To characterize CRYAB-mediated protein–protein interactions, we immunoprecipitated CRC cells transfected with Flag-β-catenin and HA-CRYAB with anti-Flag or anti-HA antibodies. The results showed that CRYAB had a strong physical interaction with β-catenin (Fig. 3B). Next, we investigated the effect of CRYAB on β-catenin expression, and the results showed that β-catenin protein levels decreased with depletion of CRYAB in sh-CRYAB stably transfected CRC cells (Fig. 3C). To determine whether sh-CRYAB decreased β-catenin expression by promoting β-catenin degradation, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 was added to CRC cells transfected with sh-NC or sh-CRYAB. The results presented that sh-CRYAB was unable to promote degradation of β-catenin under the inhibitory effect of the proteasome, and as a result, the level of β-catenin protein was accumulated (Fig. 3C). TRIM55 Is a K48-Specific Ubiquitin Ligase for β-Catenin

Considering that CRYAB silencing promotes β-catenin degradation, we hypothesized that inhibition of CRYAB might activate the UPS and thereby destabilize β-catenin. Accordingly, we screened for potential E3 ubiquitin ligases that might interact with β-catenin in CRC cells. Published studies have indicated that TRIM family proteins depended on the UPS to accelerate the degradation of their substrates. Therefore, we transfected overexpression plasmids of TRIM6, TRIM24, TRIM47, TRIM52, and TRIM55 into RKO cells and performed western blot experiments, which showed that only TRIM55 overexpression inhibited β-catenin protein levels (Figs. 4A, S1A, and B). Subsequently, we immunoprecipitated TRIM55 from CRC cells and detected β-catenin by western blotting. As shown in Fig. 4B, the interaction between TRIM55 protein and β-catenin protein was observed. Consistently, immunoprecipitation of β-catenin from CRC cells also detected TRIM55 protein by western blotting. We next tested whether TRIM55 affected the stability of β-catenin protein. Cycloheximide (CHX) was incubated with control and TRIM55 overexpressing CRC cells, and protein levels of β-catenin were texted at specific time points. The results presented that TRIM55 overexpression promoted the rate of β-catenin degradation (Fig. 4C). Subsequently, we investigated the direct effect of TRIM55 on ubiquitination of β-catenin. In vivo ubiquitination results demonstrated the ubiquitination of β-catenin accompanied by an increase in TRIM55 overexpression (Fig. 4E). To further investigate the type of TRIM55-mediated ubiquitination of β-catenin, we used a series of K (lysine)-to-R (arginine) ubiquitin mutants (K63R and K48R). K63R or K48R were co-expressed with HA-TRIM55 and Flag-β-catenin in CRC cells, and then analyzed the changes of β-catenin ubiquitination. As shown in Fig. 4E, only the K48R mutant had impaired ubiquitination modifications, indicating that TRIM55 mediated K48-linked β-catenin ubiquitination.

We also explored whether the ubiquitin ligase activity of TRIM55 on β-catenin depends on the presence of CRYAB. As expected, CRYAB overexpression increased β-catenin expression in CRC cells, whereas enforced expression of TRIM55 partially reversed this effect, possibly because the presence of CRYAB impeded the binding of TRIM55 to β-catenin (Figs. 4F, S1C). Then, we performed a Co-IP assay, and the results presented that TRIM55 binding to β-catenin was decreased in CRYAB overexpressing CRC cells (Figs. 5, S1D). TRIM55 Overexpression Induces Ferroptosis in CRC Cells

Given that β-catenin was a common target of TRIM55 and CRYAB, we sought to determine whether this juxtaposed mechanism contributes to ferroptosis. Therefore, RKO and SW480 cells were transfected with CRYAB and/or TRIM55 overexpression plasmids (Figs. 6A, B, S1C, and D). As expected, CRYAB overexpression increased CRC cell viability, and the addition of the TRIM55 overexpression plasmid partially overcame this effect (Fig. 6C). Importantly, CRYAB overexpression depleted lipid ROS levels, MDA content, and Fe2+ levels, upregulated GSH content and GPX activity, obstructing ferroptosis, which was reversible by TRIM55 overexpression in CRC cells (Fig. 6D–H). Discussion

CRYAB has been recognized as a novel oncoprotein due to its protective effects on cancer cells, and its impact in several cancers (breast, lung, gastric, prostate, CRC) has received much attention [24]. In gastrointestinal tumors, overexpression of CRYAB activated some aspects of epithelial–mesenchymal transition in vitro (cell invasion and metastasis as well as the expression of two core products, MMP7 and E-cadherin), possibly by targeting the ERK signaling pathway [19]. In addition, based on its high expression profile, CRYAB can be used as a clinical diagnostic marker for early metastasis of CRC [25]. Similarly, immortalized human mammary epithelial cells induced aberrant cell proliferation after CRYAB overexpression, meeting the characteristics of malignant tumor growth [26]. Importantly, CRYAB provided additional prognostic information for breast cancer patients to help predict prognosis and clinical drug response. Clearly, these findings revealed critical evidence for CRYAB as an oncoprotein. However less attention has been paid to whether CRYAB drove other signaling to play its pathological role in CRC.

Consistent with the published studies, we found that CRYAB was highly expressed in CRC tissues and cells. The role of CRYAB in CRC cell growth was also validated, and CRYAB-deficient tumor cell growth was effectively restricted. Activation of ferroptosis effectively resists tumor progression, and CRYAB has been suggested to be a ferroptosis-related gene in CRC and bladder cancer, playing a key role in the ferroptosis process [27]. After CRYAB silencing, we observed evidence of enhanced ferroptosis in CRC cells, including, higher ROS and MDA levels, lower GSH content, and GPx activity. To explore by what mechanism CRYAB regulated ferroptosis sensitivity, we screened potential interacting proteins with CRYAB using online databases. We verified that CRYAB bonded directly to β-catenin and restricted its degradation by inhibiting the interaction between β-catenin and TRIM55. β-catenin is a key factor in the Wnt signaling pathway [28]. Over-activated Wnt/β-catenin signaling is a major cause of the development of many cancers, including CRC, and 90% of CRC patients have abnormalities in this pathway [29]. β-catenin accumulation in the cytoplasm is a feature of Wnt signaling pathway activation, and therefore activation of β-catenin degradation mechanisms to control β-catenin levels may be effective for inhibiting the CRC progression. Wu et al., reported that TRAF6 triggered a selective autophagic β-catenin degradation mechanism and inhibited CRC metastasis [30]. Here, we found that β-catenin protein levels decreased after CRYAB knockdown. More importantly, CRYAB-silenced CRC cells did not reveal differential changes in intracellular β-catenin protein levels after treatment with MG132. We hypothesized that β-catenin might undergo protein degradation at the post-translational level. Considering that β-catenin was a target of UPS, we conjectured that β-catenin might be degraded by ubiquitination in response to CRYAB. Indeed, it has been reported that ubiquitination contributed to the aberrant degradation of β-catenin [28, 31]. Similarly, our Co-IP results confirmed that the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM55 interacted with β-catenin and degraded β-catenin by catalyzing K48-linked polyubiquitination and β-catenin degradation. We also found that CRYAB bonded to β-catenin and prevented its binding to TRIM55, resulting in reduced ubiquitination of β-catenin by TRIM55. These data provided a new mechanism by which CRYAB regulated β-catenin protein stability.

In CRC, blockade of Wnt/β-catenin signaling could function as a role in inducing ferroptosis [32]. Next, we set out to explore the relationship between TRIM55-mediated β-catenin ubiquitin degradation and ferroptosis in CRC cells. We found that TRIM55 overexpressing CRC cells had higher levels of unsaturated iron ions, lipid ROS, and lower GSH content and GPx activity, suggesting the presence of ferroptosis sensitization by TRIM55. The reason for this effect might be due to the binding of TRIM55 to β-catenin promoting its ubiquitination degradation, which in turn blocked β-catenin signaling and accelerated ferroptosis. Notably, the presence of CRYAB impeded TRIM55-mediated sensitization to ferroptosis, suggesting that CRYAB disrupted TRIM55 binding to β-catenin, resulting in activation of β-catenin signaling and increased ferroptosis resistance (Fig. 7).Fig. 7CRYAB promotes CRC progression by stabilizing β-catenin and inhibiting TRIM55-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of β-catenin

In conclusion, our study revealed a novel mechanism for the role of CRYAB in CRC, i.e., CRYAB-enhanced β-catenin stability through the formation of the CRYAB/β-catenin complex, which hindered TRIM55-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of β-catenin and ultimately inhibited ferroptosis in CRC. Based on these findings, enhancing TRIM55 expression to accelerate β-catenin degradation, or targeting CRYAB, may provide a new entry point for improving the treatment of CRC.
