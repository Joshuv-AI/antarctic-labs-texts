The roles of ginseng and ginsenosides for immune regulation: Relation to immunometabolic responses
Authors: Baicheng Chen, Mengjiao Huang, Jun Hong Park, Seung Yeol Nah, Jong-Hoon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGinseng, a traditional medicinal plant with a long history of clinical application has garnered increasing attention due to its broad immunomodulatory properties. Its principal bioactive component ginsenosides regulate diverse immune activity through complex molecular and cellular mechanisms. Despite growing evidence supporting the role of ginseng in modulating innate and adaptive immunity, current studies remain largely focused on individual immune cell types or isolated signaling pathways. A unifying framework that integrates ginseng-mediated immunometabolic regulation with coordinated immune cell interactions is still lacking. In this review, we provide a comprehensive summary of the immunomodulatory effects of ginseng and its saponins across innate and adaptive immune compartments. We further delineate the underlying mechanisms with a particular emphasis on key signaling pathways and immunometabolic reprogramming. Notably, we propose that ginseng functions as an immunometabolic regulator that bridges innate and adaptive immunity by orchestrating immune cell crosstalk and restoring immune homeostasis. This conceptual framework not only advances current understanding of ginseng-mediated immune regulation but also highlight its potential as a metabolic modulator in immunotherapy for immune-related diseases.Keywords: Ginseng, Immune activation, Immune metabolism, Immune regulation

Introduction

The immune system is increasingly recognized as a highly complicated and elaborate network, through which an interplay between innate and adaptive immunity occurs in a way that helps to achieve homeostasis and coordinate responses to infection, inflammation and cancer in the host [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Innate and adaptive immune components are highly connected through extensive communication networks and intercellular networking that influences the size, specificity, and duration of immune response [5]. Over the last few years, there has been increased interest in the development of natural products as immunomodulatory agents. Among these, ginseng has become of great interest owing to its extensive record of medical application and wide pharmacological actions [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Growing evidence suggests that ginseng exerts potent immunomodulatory activities through a wide range of bioactive constituents, including ginsenosides (such as Rb1, Rh1, Rk1, and Rg3) and non-saponin fractions enriched in polysaccharides. These components are capable of orchestrating both innate and adaptive immune responses by regulating the activation, differentiation, trafficking, and functional properties of various immune cell subsets [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. The growing body of evidence shows that ginseng has pleiotropic effects on both innate immune cells such as NK cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells, as well as on T and B lymphocytes (as a part of adaptive immunity) [[15], [16], [17]]. However, reports has been highly targeting particular immune cell types, or pathways in immune signaling, and not addressed in a systematic manner regarding how ginseng treatment is able to regulate innate and adaptive immunity into a functional scale.

In this review, a thorough overview of immunomodulatory activities of ginseng and their active constituents are discussed, especially the role of ginseng and its active components to bridge the innate and adaptive immunity (Fig. 1). We also discussed the underlying molecular and cellular mechanisms, with the developing evidence of immunometabolic regulation, which might suggest a conceptual framework to explain how ginseng orchestrates immune system crosstalk both physiologically and pathologically. This combined approach can offer new findings on the therapeutic potential of ginseng in the disease related to immunity.

Coordinating innate and adaptive immunity: Roles of immunometabolism and homeostasis

The immune system is a greatly orchestrated biological network, whereby the dynamic fusion between the innate and adaptive immunity is the foundation behind the successful host defense [[18], [19], [20]]. Immunometabolism is the key to the organization of this process through regulating the activation, differentiation, and communication of immune cells [21,22].

These metabolic pathways not only provide energy, but also essential biosynthetic sources that control immune cell fate and function in an active manner [[23], [24], [25]]. Innate immune cells, e.g. natural killer cells, macrophages and dendritic cells, the metabolic homeostasis is swift and adaptive to meet the high energetic and biosynthetic requirements of cytokine production and cytotoxic activity [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. Such responses occur very early and they are important when it comes to establishing the extent of innate immunity and also in eliciting the adaptive immune reactions that follow. An example is that the metabolic condition of macrophages affects the polarization of the cells, where M1-type macrophages secrete cytokine like IL-12 and IL-18 that stimulate natural killer cell activation and triggering the activation of T cell responses [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. On the other hand, adaptive immune cells too are under strict metabolic control, which aid in immune homeostasis [34,35].

Activated T cells depend mostly on glycolysis to facilitate quick proliferation and effector capabilities, and memory T cells depend on oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation to maintain long-term survival. In the same way, B cell activation and antibody level are highly associated with metabolic remodeling systems which sustain gene rearrangement and differentiation [[36], [37], [38]]. A combination of these mechanisms ensures good immune responses and helps to resolve inflammation and restore immune balance. Besides providing nutrients to the cell, other metabolites, including lactate, succinate, and α-ketoglutarate, are signaling molecules that can be used to regulate cellular communication and gene expression [[39], [40], [41]]. In pathological conditions, this coordinated network may be disturbed by metabolic dysregulation that gives rise to impaired immunological responses and loss of homeostasis [42]. Together, these results point to immunometabolism and immune homeostasis as key processes that govern the dynamic combination of innate and adaptive immunity. Interestingly, natural compounds like ginseng have become a possible regulator of immunometabolic processes and this implies that they will be able to reestablish the immunometabolic balance and have future therapeutic opportunities in regard to immune-associated disorders [43,44].

Ginseng modulates AMPK/mTOR balance and mitochondrial function

AMPK is one of the major metabolic metabolic sensors that ensure cellular energy homeostasis in state of metabolic stress [45,46]. AMPK activation in Natural killer cells (NK cells) leads to catabolic activities, including fatty acid oxidation and mitophagy, thus supporting the survival of nutrient-deprived and hypoxic conditions. Through downstream effectors such as ULK1 and PGC-1α, AMPK coordinates mitochondrial quality control and biogenesis, enabling NK cells to maintain metabolic fitness in adverse microenvironments [47]. However, sustained AMPK activation may simultaneously suppress mTORC1-dependent anabolic metabolism, resulting in reduced protein synthesis, cytokine production, and cytotoxic activity. Therefore, the biological outcome of AMPK signaling in NK cells is highly context-dependent and relies on its dynamic interplay with mTOR signaling [[48], [49], [50]].

A growing body of evidence suggests that this balance can be altered with compounds derived out of ginseng. It has been suggested that protopanaxadiol and protopanaxatriol could act to regulate AMPK activity, but at the same time it may mediate the enhancement of mTOR signaling, implying that it may facilitate metabolic fitness optimalisation. Besides that, ginsenoside compound K has also been identified to inhibit plasma cell differentiation through SIRT1 and AMPK-mediated signalings [[51], [52], [53]]. Moreover, 20(S)-protopanaxatriol was found to lead to the activation of AMPK/PGC-1α/PPARγ signaling axis linked directly with mitochondrial biogenesis and metabolic response [54].

Rather than acting as a simple activator of AMPK, ginseng may optimize the balance between AMPK-driven energy conservation and mTOR-mediated effector responses. By coordinating mitochondrial turnover, metabolic flexibility, and anabolic capacity, ginseng could preserve NK-cell functionality under metabolic stress while preventing excessive energetic expenditure. This metabolic fine-tuning may represent an important mechanism through which ginseng enhances immune resilience and NK-cell-mediated immunity (Fig. 2).

Ginseng counteracts TGF-β-mediated metabolic suppression

Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β) is a very powerful immunosuppressive regulator of NK cells, and can mediate impairment in cytotoxicity, cytokine production and metabolic adaptation of NK cell, as well as affect differentiation programs [55]. TGF-β mechanistically inhibits mTOR signaling and protein synthesis, inhibits Glycolysis and prevents IFN-γ production [56,57]. High concentrations of TGF-β in the tumor microenvironment help to impair the functionality of NK cells and promote tumor immune evasion [58]. Evidence suggests that ginseng active ingredients such as Compound K, Rk1, and Rg5 can regulate the TGF-β signaling pathway or alter its production [[59], [60], [61]]. Since TGF-β plays a key role in the suppression of NK-cell metabolic activity and activity, the ginseng-TGF-β interactions could present a potential mechanism of NK cell activity restoration. Our hypothesis is based on the fact that ginseng could reverse the metabolic suppression induced by TGF-β.

Exploring the potential of ginseng to regulate NK cell function through metabolic reprogramming

Emerging evidence suggests that specific ginseng-derived constituents, including gintonin and ginsenoside 20(R)-Rg3, regulate NK-cell immunometabolism through signaling pathways such as MAPK/ERK1/2, thereby promoting activating receptor expression, metabolic fitness, and effector functions [62,63]. Instead of employing a unicellular mechanism of action, ginseng-derived compounds play a role in a complex interrelationship with various signaling cascades, such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK, and NF-κB pathways [62,64]. All these signaling re-join at metabolic reprogramming which allows NK cells to maintain cytotoxicity and cytokine production. These effects are strongly linked with metabolic reprogramming, which is a mechanistic effect.

Gintonin is reported to increase glycolysis, mitochondrial activity and nutrient utilization to help support the energetic and biosynthetic requirements [[65], [66], [67]]. The activation of lysophosphatidic acid receptors by gintonin, which activates MAPK-ERK1/2 signaling and turns on activating receptors (like NKG2D) and influences oxidative phosphorylation and lipid metabolism to maintain metabolic fitness [62]. Besides direct actions that affect the NK cells, ginseng can indirectly boost the NK cells by regulating the upstream cytokines, such as IL-2 and IL-12 [68]. Together, the results give an idea that ginseng modulates the abilities of the NK cell to restore the metabolic homeostasis and the interdependence of the metabolic and immune signal transduction due to which the specific mechanism.

Emerging evidence suggests that fatty acid oxidation (FAO) also contributes to NK-cell metabolic adaptation, particularly under nutrient-restricted conditions [69]. AMPK-driven activation of the PGC-1α/PPAR signaling axis enhances mitochondrial fatty acid utilization and supports long-term cellular fitness. Since several ginseng-derived compounds activate AMPK- and PGC-1α-related pathways, modulation of FAO may represent an additional mechanism through which ginseng preserves NK-cell functionality.

Mechanisms of ginseng-mediated regulation of T cell function

Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that ginseng and its bioactive constituents modulate T-cell activation, differentiation, and effector functions through diverse molecular, cellular, and metabolic pathways. [66]. Instead of pursuing one pathway, ginseng appears to be faithful by organizing multiple signaling networks that control T cell activation, differentiation, and effector functions [15]. The growing body of evidence indicates that ginseng inhibits physiological and pathological T cell subsets and functional polarization. Indicatively, Korean ginseng ranks as having the ability to reverse the inability of cyclophosphamide to cause acute loss of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and to reinstat the percentage of Foxp3+ cells in the spleen [64]. Moreover, 20(S)-ginsenoside Rh2 has therapeutic effects in T cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signal transduction [70]. Ginsenoside F2 reduces alcohol-induced liver damage by stimulating Treg differentiation and inhibiting Th17 responses and ginsenoside F2 improves CD4+ T cell response and can increase the Th1/Th2 balance [71,72].

Ginseng has been shown to regulate key signaling pathways involved in T-cell activation, including the PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK, and NF-κB pathways [73,74]. These signal cascades combine extracellular signals and control T cell growth, production of cytokines, and survival. An example is that mTOR stimulation enhances glycolytic metabolism and effector T cell-mediated responses, and its suppression leads to the production of regulatory and memory T cell-subsets [75]. In particular, it has been also revealed that Korean red ginseng can improve the mitochondrion functionality and relieve T cell immunosenescence symptoms [76]. New evidence also shows that Ginsenoside F3 controls T cell activities by immunometabolic reprogramming [77]. Activated T cells rely on aerobic glycolysis to support rapid proliferation and effector behaviors, whilst memory and regulatory T cells incline to rely on oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation [78]. T cell fate choices and functional polarization can be affected by modulating these metabolic programs by ginseng. Alongside its direct action on T cells, ginseng can also indirectly control T cell responses in other ways like controlling the production and presentation of antigens and cytokines. Ginseng alters the microenvironment that regulates T cell priming and differentiation by modulating the production of dendritic cells and the secretion of cytokines like IL-2, IL-6 and IL-12 [64]. In contrast effector and regulatory T-cell subsets exhibit distinct metabolic preferences. While activated effector T cells predominantly rely on aerobic glycolysis, memory T cells and Tregs preferentially utilize FAO and mitochondrial respiration. Through modulation of AMPK and mTOR signaling, ginseng-derived compounds may influence this metabolic reprogramming and consequently affect T-cell differentiation and function.

Ginseng modulates immune responses through macrophage polarization and metabolic regulation

Ginseng is well known to control immune responses by attenuating macrophage polarization [79]. Macrophage is characterized by significant plasticity and events to various functional phenotypes, i.e, classically activated (M1), and alternatively activated (M2) macrophages, respectively. Ginseng has been reported to have bidirectional regulatory activity; for example, Rg1 and Rg2 can induce increased polarization of M2 macrophages and decreased polarization of M1 macrophages, or vice versa, thereby promoting inflammation resolution and immune homeostasis; while other components can induce polarization of M1 and M2 macrophages in the immune system, thereby promoting host defense and inflammatory response [80,81]. It is worth noting that metabolic reprogramming is more or less associated with the polarization of macrophages [82]. Enhanced glycolysis and impaired mitochondrial metabolism characterize M1 macrophages and oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation are favored by M2 macrophages. These programs of unique metabolism have now been established as determinants of macrophage function and phenotype [83,84]. Given that several ginsenosides regulate AMPK signaling and mitochondrial function, metabolic reprogramming may contribute to the ability of ginseng to promote M2 polarization. Moreover, ginseng can indirectly regulate the macrophage activity by altering the microenvironment of cytokines, thus, playing a role in dynamic regulation of polarization in macrophages. Taken together, these results indicate that ginseng controls immune reactions via macrophage polarization, and it is possible that metabolic pathways play a role in this process that requires the investigation further [85].

Metabolic convergence across immune cell subsets

Although distinct immune cell populations exhibit specialized metabolic requirements, several conserved metabolic programs are shared across NK cells, T cells, and macrophages. Glycolysis predominantly supports rapid effector responses, including cytokine production and cytotoxic activity, whereas oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) are associated with long-term survival, memory formation, and immune regulation. In addition, glutaminolysis and amino acid metabolism provide biosynthetic intermediates essential for immune cell proliferation and differentiation.

Central signaling hubs, including AMPK, PI3K-AKT-mTOR, and PGC-1α, coordinate these metabolic transitions in response to environmental cues. Emerging evidence suggests that specific ginseng-derived constituents target these conserved pathways. For example, compound K regulates SIRT1/AMPK signaling, 20(S)-protopanaxatriol activates the AMPK/PGC-1α axis to enhance mitochondrial function, whereas ginsenoside F3 alleviates T-cell exhaustion through RIPOR2-mediated immunometabolic reprogramming. In addition, gintonin and ginsenoside 20(R)-Rg3 regulate NK-cell function via MAPK/ERK signaling and metabolic adaptation [62,77].

Collectively, these findings suggest that ginseng-derived compounds may orchestrate immune-cell-specific functions by modulating shared immunometabolic networks, thereby promoting innate-adaptive immune crosstalk and maintaining systemic immune homeostasis [Table 1]. Nevertheless, comparative studies systematically evaluating the metabolic responses of distinct immune cell subsets to individual ginseng constituents remain limited. Future studies integrating multi-omics approaches and immune cell-specific metabolic profiling will be essential to elucidate the compound-specific and cell-type-specific mechanisms underlying ginseng-mediated immunometabolic regulation.

Ginseng-mediated innate–adaptive immune crosstalk

The interactive mechanisms of innate and adaptive immunity are a base of natural immunity surveillance and clearance of pathogens. This two-way communication is mediated by direct cell-cell interactions and indirect signaling by cytokines and chemokines [87]. Mounting evidence indicates ginseng constituents that may alter this immunological network by altering cytokine and chemokine profiles, thus impacting immune coordination instead of a single immune pathway. Indicatively, Korean ginseng and its bioactive fraction gintonin have been documented to increase the levels of immunoregulatory cytokines, such as IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-12, IL-18 and IFN-γ, regardless of immunosuppression caused by cyclophosphamide [64,68]. Simultaneously, these therapies increase chemokine and chemokine receptor expressions linked with the activation of NK cells, which indicate their involvement in fostering innate immune responsiveness [62]. It is also noteworthy that ginseng too shows context-specific immunomodulatory actions by stimulating the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines in the case of excessive inflammation, underscoring its dual-regulating potential [88,89]. Mechanistically, ginseng seems to promote innate-adaptive immune crosstalk via cytokine-mediated negative feedback. Early production of IFN-γ by NK-cell-derived IFN-γ is important for macrophage activation, which produce IL-12 and IL-18 to activate NK cells thereby creating a positive feedback loop. This 2-way communication does not only strengthen innate immune responses but also develops poised adaptive immunity. New information on SARS-CoV-2 infection modelling also reaffirms the importance of ginseng in the organization of multi-cellular immunity in a pathologic state [90]. Significantly, the immunomodulatory effects of ginseng do not arise from isolated cellular interactions, but are characterized by the dynamic interplay among multiple cell types. On the one hand, ginseng inhibits excessive immune response as uncontrolled NK and T cell responses, thus suppressing immunopathology. Conversely, it maintains the adequate immune signaling in order to clear the pathogens appropriately. This dual regulatory attribute helps in restoring immune homeostasis especially in the contraction phase after activation of immunity. Nevertheless, despite the increasing amount of evidence that ginseng is immunomodulatory, direct mechanistic data to link ginseng-induced metabolic reprogramming to intercellular immune communication are lacking, which highlights an essential gap and a key direction to be taken in future studies.

Ginseng in immunometabolic regulation of cell-based immunotherapy

Cell-based immunotherapy is now the promising approach in the treatment of cancer and other diseases associated with immunity. The success of these strategies such as T cell- and natural killer (NK) cell-based therapies heavily relies on metabolic fitness and functional preservation of transferred immune cells [91]. Nonetheless, in the tumor microenvironment and other immunosuppressive environments, immune cells often spontaneously experience metabolic exhaustion, and this can relate to impaired glycolysis, mitochondrial impairment and interrupted signaling networks, resulting in a reduced response to therapy [[92], [93], [94]]. Based on this, the immunometabolism has emerged as a focus in streamlining the cell-based immunotherapy. Metabolic reprogramming can satisfy the energetic and biosynthetic needs of immune cells, and control their differentiation, effector abilities and long-term survival. Basic regulatory routes such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR, AMPK, and NF-κB signaling are essential to the process of staying active and exhausted in immune cells that are used in therapy [[95], [96], [97]].

Ginseng and its bioactive components, in this regard, have gained momentum of interest as a possible regulator of immunometabolism. There is a growing body of evidence indicating that ginseng is likely to increase metabolic fitness by supporting mitochondrial activities, controlling glycolysis, and maximizing the use of nutrients. The ginseng could aid in normalizing metabolic homeostasis of the immune cells exposed to immunosuppressive conditions through the coordinated control of the AMPK -mTOR axis and related signaling pathways on coordinated regulation of homeostasis in the immune cells [51,52]. Additionally, ginseng could positively affect the action of cell-based immunotherapies by increasing immune cell growth, cytokine generation, and cytotoxic functions. It can also indirectly transform the tumor microenvironment through cytokinergic network regulation and suppression of immunosuppressive signals, which in turn facilitates an improved immune response environment. Though existing results indicate the possible use of ginseng in immunometabolic regulation, there are few direct studies on ginseng in cell-based immunotherapy. More research is needed to clarify the exact molecular process and how ginseng could be used successfully as a part of immunotherapeutic practices.

Limitations, challenges, and future perspectives

Cellular metabolism and immune functioning have become key regulators of the immune system, as highlighted by their inter-communication. Immunometabolic reprogramming controls sufficient energy provision and also modulate the dynamic equilibrium between innate and adaptive immunity by the concerted transcriptional and epigenetic control [98,99]. Breakage of this regulatory network, especially in tumor microenvironment or in viral infection, are significant obstacles for successful immune surveillance and clearance. In that regard, there is increasing evidence to indicate that ginseng and its biomolecular components might also act as immunometabolic modulators [100,101]. Mechanistically, ginseng affects the regulation of major signaling pathways, such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR and AMPK and potentially override immunosuppressive response, such as those generated by TGF-β [51,52,[59], [60], [61]]. Moreover, ginseng shows promise of having epigenetic effect on the regulation of metabolism, growing evidence shows that ginseng might have a similar impact on the functional plasticity of immune cells, which include the activation of NK cells and development of memory T cells.These reports suggest a potential metabolic–epigenetic micro-axis that may underlie the long-term immunomodulatory effects of ginseng, representing an under-investigated aspect of its biological activity [86,102]. Although these studies are promising, there are certain limitations that requires further discussion. One major challenge is the heterogeneity of ginseng preparations, such as whole extracts, purified ginsenosides, and particular fractions like gintonin, which adds considerable variability to the composition, bioavailability, that alters the reproducibility and cross-study comparability [103,104]. Second, there is a relative lack of evidence on well-designed clinical studies with most information available on an in vitro experimental or animal model, and thus translation of the results to human immune regulation has not been determined well. Third, the mechanism by which ginseng causes immunoregulation is not fully elucidated. Direct evidence of how metabolic reprogramming can connect to intercellular immune communication is rarely reported, however its impact on signaling pathways, and cytokine production are well documented. Moreover, dose effect and context effect also increases difficulty in interpreting the data because ginseng can have bidirectional immunomodulatory effect regarding physiologic or pathologic conditions. Lastly, the ginsenosides are not well-characterized in terms of diversity and their interactions are largely not studied as synergistic or antagonistic, but rather, as single compounds. Future studies with standardized experimental designs and well-controlled clinical trials could help in better defining the therapeutic potential of ginseng. Notably, integrated multi-omics technologies, such as metabolomics and epigenomics, will be required to understand the mechanisms of ginseng-triggered immunometabolic activity, which governs innate-adaptive immune-crosstalk.

Overall, ginseng is a promising natural immunometabolic modulator that can be utilized in immunomodulatory and NK cell-based immunotherapies. Greater mechanistic insight into its activities could offer a conceptual guideline to the derivation of new therapeutic approaches that focuses on immunometabolic response.
