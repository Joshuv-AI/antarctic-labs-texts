Predicting fertilizer treating of maize using digital image processing and deep learning approaches
Authors: Eshete Derb Emiru, Kassie Bishaw
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe quality and quantity of maize yields are declining as a result of several structural issues with Ethiopia’s traditional maize producing system. The lack of soil fertility, which is frequently hard to see visually from the maize leaves, is a major reason for this decline. An automated approach to identify and categorize fertility problems in maize plants is desperately needed to address this issue. The goal of this study is to develop a model for the recognition and classification of fertilizer treatment for maize based on maize leaf images, using deep learning algorithms to facilitate and improve the recognition and early control of fertilizer treatment for maize. The datasets utilized for this study were collected from various farming areas in the East Gojjam Zone, specifically the Hulet Ejju Enessie Woreda, comprising 4000 images of normal and deficient maize leaves. Through data augmentation techniques, this dataset was expanded to 16,000 images. A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) with VGG16 and VGG19 architectures, along with a SoftMax classifier, was employed to analyze and classify the images into eight distinct categories based on their characteristics. Image enhancement and segmentation were performed using Gaussian filtering and Canny edge detection techniques. Hyperparameters, including image size, epoch number, batch size, dataset training and testing split ratio, and learning rate, were used to enhance the model’s performance. The experiments with an image size of 224 × 224, 60 epochs, a batch size of 32, an 80/20 dataset split ratio, and a learning rate of 0.001 showed significant improvements in the classification model’s performance. Ultimately, the best result was achieved with an accuracy of 95% in VGG16. VGG16 performed better than VGG19 in predicting fertilizer treatment for maize due to its lower complexity, which minimizes the risk of overfitting and enhances generalization, especially with smaller datasets.Keywords: CNN, Image processing, Maize leaf lack of fertility, VGG16, VGG19Subject terms: Computational science, Information technology, Scientific data

Introduction

Ethiopia is one of the developing countries in the world, and its economy depends on both agriculture and industry1. The word “agriculture” is the most comprehensive term used to indicate the many ways in which crop plants and domestic animals sustain the global human population by providing food and other products2. Agriculture is the backbone of the Ethiopian economy, with more than 85% of the population gaining their livelihood directly or indirectly from agricultural production, including livestock. Forty-five percent of the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) of the country comes from agricultural products. Besides, this sector is also the backbone of the Ethiopian economy, determining the growth of all other sectors and, eventually, of the entire national economy. There are many agricultural products found in Ethiopia; some are coffee, maize, teff, wheat, malt-barley, and tea. Maize is the single most important cereal, accounting for 17% of the per capita calorie intake, followed by sorghum (14%) and teff (11%). Maize is one of the most significant cereal crops used in the human diet in large parts of the world3, and it is an important food component for livestock. It is produced throughout Ethiopia under diverse environments4. Maize is the most produced and consumed crop in Ethiopia. It is common in our country that most crops suffer from various factors contributing to low productivity5. Soil fertility is a major concern for crop production in the area6. The fertilizer treatment of maize for silage should lead to high nutrient efficiency. The availability of nutrients in the soil is a key factor influencing nutrient content in maize and yields. Maize responds well to less nitrogen (N), less phosphorus (P)7, less sulfur (S), less boron (B), less magnesium (Mg)8, less zinc (Zn), and less potassium (K); these are highly limiting nutrients that support good crop growth and development due to the practice of multiple and continuous cropping systems using low fertilizer. These factors are significant in Ethiopia, which is now considered one of the most challenging environments for maize crops in most of the country where maize is grown. Such factors affect various sections and stages of the crop, significantly decreasing its production. Therefore, identifying and recognizing these factors through different methods, including manual and computer-based systems, is critical.

Due to the fertilizer treatment of maize, the outcomes are highly affected in both the quality and quantity of the results. Currently, computer technologies are used to improve agricultural productivity in various ways. Deep learning approaches are increasingly successful in leaf image-based analysis9, such as predicting different fertilizer treatments and assessing levels of risk. One way to reduce this issue is through the early detection of fertilization needs using current technology like deep convolutional neural networks, which achieve better results in detecting deficiencies related to specific features. Deep learning simplifies the complex feature extraction problem and hence is used for image recognition.

Several studies have explored the classification and prediction of nutrient deficiencies in maize, showcasing various methodologies and algorithms. For instance, in10, researchers aimed to classify maize leaf image data into four categories of fertility deficiency: less nitrogen (N), less phosphorus (P), less potassium (K), and deficiencies in NPK using Support Vector Machine (SVM) and decision tree algorithms. This study achieved a commendable classification accuracy of 93%, highlighting the efficacy of decision tree algorithms in this context. In another study11, the focus was on predicting simulated maize yield and nitrogen loss through a meta-model that utilized metrics such as Mean Square Error (RMSE) and Relative Root Mean Square Error (RRMSE). The algorithms implemented included Extreme Gradient Boosting, Ridge Regression, and Random Forests, resulting in impressive accuracies of 98.3%, 85.4%, and 54.5%, respectively, particularly in addressing nitrogen deficiency. Additionally, in12, researchers classified nitrogen deficiency in maize using features derived from Hu moments, Haralick texture, and color histograms with algorithms such as Random Forest Classifier, Support Vector Machine, and Gaussian Naive Bayes, resulting in classification accuracies of 72.74%, 63.33%, and 50.78%, respectively. Collectively, these studies underline the importance of using advanced computational techniques for diagnosing nutrient deficiencies in maize, which is relevant to our research.

Nowadays, by using deep convolutional neural networks, we can diagnose and detect different leaf deficiencies that have very similar features. In this study, we propose image-based data analysis with deep learning techniques such as CNN with VGG16 and VGG19 applied to the fertilizer treatment of maize. This study introduces a deep learning model for the automated detection and classification of maize leaf fertility deficiencies, built upon a novel dataset and image preprocessing. A comprehensive, augmented dataset created from diverse farming areas facilitated robust model training. A CNN with a VGG16 architecture, optimized through systematic hyperparameter adjustments, was implemented. The resulting model achieved a 95% accuracy in classifying fertility deficiencies, demonstrating superior performance.

Maize leaf image acquisition

Ethiopia is home to various agriculture research centers across different regions, each staffed with experts focused on maintaining standard data quality and addressing biases and fairness issues. Engaging with these centers and their specialists is crucial before data collection. Notably, the East Gojam Zone in the Amhara region, known for its agricultural fertility, hosts one such agriculture research center that can provide valuable insights. Data was collected through open-ended interviews and technical observations. Researchers prepared interview questions in advance and interviewed farmers, agricultural sector representatives, and domain experts, including staff from the East Gojam Zone Agricultural Office’s plant protection directorate. These discussions focused on common maize leaf fertility deficiencies and classification methods. Additionally, researchers observed farmers’ fields to verify issues and identify symptoms of deficiencies in Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Sulfur, Boron, Magnesium, Zinc, and Potassium, enhancing their domain knowledge and documenting their findings. Image acquisition is the process of obtaining a digital image of an object in the real world using a digital camera or scanner, which is then converted to the desired output format13. The collected samples of maize leaves exhibiting each type of fertilizer deficiency are presented in Fig. 2.

Sample maize leaf images of each fertilizer deficiency.

During the acquisition process of each image, random changes may be introduced in the pixel values of the image, which is referred to as noise. Atmospheric degradation, motion of the image or camera, scanner quality, and the distance between the camera lens and the image are some of the causes of changes in pixel values during image acquisition. There is not enough acquired data available for this research in our country, so the researcher obtained additional maize leaf images using an appropriate camera. Since there is no ready-made dataset for this type of research in our country, we prepared our own dataset for training and testing. To do this, normal and fertility-deficient maize leaves were collected from various farming areas in the East Gojjam Zone, Huletijuworeda, in the Amhara region. The collected maize leaves were verified by experts from the farming area. All sampled maize leaf images are from the 2014/2015 production year. The details regarding the quantities and images of sample normal maize leaves and fertility-deficient maize leaves have been used in our experimentation.

The collected data contains both normal and infected/abnormal maize due to lack of fertility. Initially, the researcher acquired 2,000 images of normal and fertility-deficient maize leaves in each class, as shown in Table 2. However, this data was not sufficient, so image augmentation techniques were applied to increase the dataset, ultimately resulting in a total of 16,000 maize leaf images.

Image preprocessing

After the collection of the maize leaf images, the researcher applied different preprocessing techniques to the maize leaf images, such as resizing, noise removal, and augmentation. The purpose of preprocessing is to improve the quality of the maize leaf images by removing noise; for this reason, the model can examine the images in a better way. Preprocessing is used to decrease complexity and increase the accuracy of the applied algorithm by suppressing undesired distortions and enhancing the features that are necessary for image segmentation and feature extraction techniques13.

Image resizing

Image resizing is the first image preprocessing task. Image resizing involves preserving an important region of an image, minimizing distortions, and improving efficiency. Hence, state-of-the-art models take input images sized at 224 × 224. Therefore, image resizing helps to reduce the training time of a neural network; the more pixels there are in an image, the more input nodes there are, which in turn increases the complexity of the model14. This fixed size is used for all imported images because the accuracy of the feature extraction process is affected if the images are of different sizes. Due to a significant limitation in certain deep learning algorithms, such as CNNs, we have harmonized the size of the maize leaf images in our dataset. This means that before being sent to the learning system, photos need to be preprocessed and resized to have the same widths and heights. The algorithm used to process the dataset determines the image size. Image resizing contributes to the continuous performance improvement of common vision models22. At the preprocessing phase, the image size was changed to 224 × 224. In state-of-the-art models, an input image size of 224 × 224 is standard. Here, we experimented with several image sizes, including 64 × 64, 128 × 128, and 224 × 224. Therefore, it is practical to compare the network with state-of-the-art models using an image size of 224 × 224.

Data augmentation

Data augmentation is the process of increasing the number of training data points in a dataset by generating more data from the original dataset. In addition to this, it helps the network learn different degrees of orientation of the image. Image data augmentation includes creating altered forms of images in the training dataset that belong to the same class as the original image. Generally, different researchers apply data augmentation for data enlargement and to help the network learn different orientations of the image. In this study, the researcher used data augmentation techniques for both dataset enlargement due to the insufficient number of images collected for the dataset and the prevention of overfitting problems. The principal approaches to data augmentation are flipping, rotation, translation, and zooming.

Data augmentation is an effective technique that can improve overall performance, reduce overfitting, help models generalize to new data more effectively, and increase the diversity of training datasets. It can multiply the amount of accessible training data without requiring additional manual labor, making it a crucial technique in situations where obtaining large labeled datasets is challenging or costly23. Image augmentation comes in many forms; here are some of the most common: vertical shift, horizontal shift, vertical flip, horizontal flip, rotation, brightness adjustment, and zoom in/out. Therefore, this paper applies data augmentation techniques to the maize leaf image dataset to increase the number of relevant images. Image augmentation is used to create different variations of identical content to expose the model to diverse training examples. This procedure aids in the resolution of overfitting issues and improves the model’s capacity to generalize during training. In general, data augmentation using Keras can be used to artificially increase the size of a dataset and improve the robustness of a machine learning model. In this case, we include vertical shift, horizontal shift, vertical flip, horizontal flip, and rotation. We also specify that any empty pixels should be filled with the nearest pixel value24,25.

Image enhancement

Image enhancement is a common approach to improving the interoperability of data in image viewers and significantly contributes to other automated image processing methods. The primary goal of image enhancement is to modify the attributes or features of an image, making it more suitable than the original for a specific task and observer26. The quality of images in a dataset plays a vital role in the performance of a model. Various elements, such as illumination, can lead to insufficient contrast in images, resulting in inadequate feature representation27.

Image enhancement methods can emphasize general or local features of images, visualize unclear images, highlight specific features of interest, suppress irrelevant features, and increase the contrast between different objects within the images. These methods can enhance image quality and information, improve image interpretation and recognition, and make images more suitable for visual systems and analysis5. To enhance model performance, the quality of images is crucial. In this study, considering the factors described above, the researcher employed various image enhancement algorithms, including the median filter, Gaussian filter, and non-local means image enhancement algorithm. Image segmentation

Image segmentation is a technique in digital image processing that focuses on separating an image into different parts based on its features and properties28,29. The primary goal of image segmentation is to simplify the image for easier analysis. Edge-based segmentation is one of the most widely used methods in image processing. This approach emphasizes identifying the edges of various objects within an image, which is crucial for uncovering the features of those objects, as edges contain significant data. Edge detection is popular because it helps eliminate unnecessary and superfluous information from an image, significantly reducing its size and facilitating analysis. In this research, the Canny edge detection method is employed for image segmentation, specifically for detecting the shape and boundary of maize leaf images.

Feature extraction

One of the critical concepts in image analysis is the extraction of relevant information, which helps describe the type and nature of an image30. Feature extraction is the most common approach to processing a raw image, aiming to reduce the number of features by creating new features from the existing ones and then discarding the original features. These newly reduced sets of features should summarize most of the information contained in the original set. The goal of feature extraction is to improve classification rates by representing images using newly generated features from raw pixel data. This stage is crucial for image detection and classification31. Various algorithms and procedures are employed to identify and separate specific desired areas within the image32. A feature is a significant piece of information derived from an image that enhances understanding of its content. To extract texture features from an image, the researcher utilized the Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) and Gabor filtering techniques. These methods are among the most popular for texture feature extraction and analysis. Textural features within images can enhance the accuracy of detection and classification. A set of Gabor filters with varying frequencies and orientations is used to extract textural features such as orientation and frequency from the image.

Experiment results

Experimental setup

The experiments for this research were primarily conducted using Python, leveraging several libraries to facilitate various tasks. OpenCV and NumPy were the most frequently used libraries for image processing and numerical computations, respectively. For data visualization and exploration, we utilized additional libraries alongside Keras, which employed a TensorFlow backend for data modeling. The research was conducted using Google Colaboratory (Google Colab), a free cloud-based Jupyter notebook environment that enabled model training on GPUs, independent of local hardware capabilities. The data was partitioned into training and testing datasets, with 80% allocated for training and 20% for testing. We also experimented with other splits: 70% training and 30% testing, 60% training and 40% testing, and 90% training and 10% testing.

Deep learning models

We chose VGG16 and VGG19 because they are well-established and widely used CNN architectures known for their strong performance in image classification tasks. Their relatively simple architecture makes them suitable for transfer learning, and we aimed to investigate the performance of these classic models in classifying maize leaf diseases as a baseline before attempting more complex models. We selected these architectures due to their popularity and the availability of pre-trained weights, which simplify implementation. After feature extraction, classification is performed based on the information learned during training, specifically after the distinctive features are identified. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are commonly utilized for image classification tasks33. The VGG-16 model, which consists of 16 layers, is capable of performing typical image classification tasks effectively34.The model was trained multiple times using the CNN architecture with datasets of varying image sizes, including 224 × 224 and 127 × 127, while adjusting the training, validation, and testing ratios. Additionally, different hyperparameter values, such as the learning rate and batch size, were tested. The researcher also compared the results of the model with other CNN architectures, including VGG19.

In this study, the focus was on fine-tuning various hyperparameters to enhance classification performance. The values for several hyperparameters were determined experimentally, emphasizing the importance of careful selection in achieving an effective model design. Each hyperparameter was meticulously tested to minimize loss and improve evaluation metrics35. The model optimization employed a gradient descent algorithm combined with the Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) optimizer, which adjusts the learning rates for each parameter and incorporates moving averages of the gradients. The learning rate, crucial for weight updates, was assessed at values of 0.001, 0.01, and 0.1, with 0.001 identified as optimal. The Categorical Cross-Entropy (CCE) loss function was selected, paired with the SoftMax activation function in the output layer to address the multiclass classification problem. Moreover, the model training explored various epochs and batch sizes, determining that 60 epochs and a batch size of 32 yielded the best results. Dropout was also implemented as a regularization technique to mitigate overfitting during training.

Results discussion

In the results section, the model was trained multiple times by adjusting hyperparameter values to improve performance. The first experimental scenario involved changing the image size. In this experiment, the model was trained using image sizes of 224 × 224 and 127 × 127 pixels. The researcher achieved 99% training accuracy and 95% testing accuracy with the 224 × 224 image size, while the 127 × 127 image size resulted in 95% training accuracy and 85% testing accuracy.

The second experimental scenario focused on changing the training and testing dataset split ratio. The ratios applied were 60/40, 70/30, 80/20, and 90/10. The results showed 95% training accuracy and 93% testing accuracy at the 60/40 ratio, 98% training accuracy and 93% testing accuracy at the 70/30 ratio, 99% training accuracy and 95% testing accuracy at the 80/20 ratio, and 95% training accuracy and 92% testing accuracy at the 90/10 ratio.

The third experimental scenario examined the effects of batch size. It is challenging to generalize the impact of batch size; however, when a larger batch size is used, computational speed improves. Two batch sizes were tested: 32 and 16. The results yielded 99% training accuracy and 95% testing accuracy with a batch size of 32, while a batch size of 16 resulted in 97% training accuracy and 93% testing accuracy.

The fourth experimental scenario involved varying learning rate values. The model was trained using learning rates of 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001. The researcher achieved 91% training accuracy and 85% testing accuracy with a learning rate of 0.1, 94% training accuracy and 93% testing accuracy with a learning rate of 0.01, and 99% training accuracy and 95% testing accuracy with a learning rate of 0.001. From these experiments, the optimal hyperparameter values identified were a batch size of 32, an image size of 224 × 224, and a learning rate of 0.001.

When comparing the VGG16 model with the VGG19 model, the results indicated that the VGG19 model achieved 97% training accuracy and 92% testing accuracy, while the VGG16 model achieved 99% training accuracy and 95% testing accuracy. Consequently, the VGG16 model outperformed VGG19 due to its lower training parameters. Thus, VGG16 can accurately identify maize leaf fertility deficiencies 95% of the time. The proposed deep learning model for detecting and classifying maize leaf fertility deficiencies achieved an impressive accuracy of 95% using the VGG16 architecture, outperforming comparable models such as VGG19. The experimental analysis involved adjusting various hyperparameters, including image size, dataset split ratio, batch size, and learning rate, significantly enhancing the model’s performance. The results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach in addressing the structural challenges of traditional maize production in Ethiopia.

In comparison to existing works, such as the decision tree algorithm10 and Random Forest Classifier12, which achieved lower accuracy rates of 93% and 72.74% respectively, the proposed model demonstrates superior performance. Additionally, this work contributes to the existing literature by addressing the specific challenge of maize leaf fertility deficiencies and providing a viable solution for improved maize production in Ethiopia. VGG16 outperformed VGG19 in predicting fertilizer treatment for maize due to its simpler architecture, which reduces the risk of overfitting and enhances generalization, particularly when working with smaller datasets that may lack sufficient diversity for a more complex model. The fewer layers in VGG16 can effectively capture pertinent features in the images, making it well-suited for this specific task. In contrast, the additional layers of VGG19 may complicate the learning process without delivering proportional benefits in feature extraction. Furthermore, if hyperparameter tuning and training resources are limited, the advantages of VGG19’s complexity may not be fully realized, favoring the more efficient VGG16 in this context.

To effectively extend the VGG16 and VGG19 models for analyzing different crops and other fertilizer treatments, it is essential to leverage transfer learning by fine-tuning pretrained models on new datasets specific to the crops in question, implementing data augmentation to account for variability in growth conditions, and customizing the architecture by adding specific layers or adjusting the output layer for multi-class classification. Incorporating regularization techniques and conducting comprehensive hyperparameter tuning will enhance model generalization, while ensemble methods may further improve prediction accuracy across varied agricultural contexts.

Conclusion and recommendation

In conclusion, maize production faces numerous challenges, with a significant issue being nutrient deficiencies, including low levels of nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, boron, magnesium, zinc, and potassium, which contribute to reduced crop yields. Additionally, the lack of effective problem-solving tools in developing countries such as Ethiopia severely hampers development and quality of life. Consequently, there is an urgent need for affordable and accessible technological solutions to detect nutrient deficiencies at an early stage. To address this issue, the researcher developed a deep learning model utilizing convolutional neural networks (CNN) and image processing techniques to identify and classify nutrient deficiencies in maize leaves based on leaf images. The dataset was collected from the Hulet Ejju Enessie Woreda farming area in East Gojjam and consisted of 40,000 images of both healthy and deficient maize leaves. Given that this initial data volume was insufficient, image augmentation techniques increased the dataset to 16,000 images. The researcher employed Gaussian blurring and Canny edge detection methods to enhance and segment the images.

This study utilized a VGG16 deep learning model for feature extraction and classification, using the SoftMax activation function in the fully connected layer. The VGG16 model was trained with various hyperparameter values, including image sizes of 224 × 224 and 127 × 127 pixels, batch sizes of 32 and 16, a total of 60 epochs, and learning rates of 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001. The optimal hyperparameter values identified were an image size of 224 × 224, 60 epochs, a batch size of 32, an 80/20 dataset split ratio, and a learning rate of 0.001, which resulted in an average accuracy of 95%. The findings indicated that the VGG16 model outperformed the VGG19 model in terms of accuracy. Moreover, improvements in model performance were observed as the number of epochs and batch size increased, alongside the application of augmentation techniques. While this study specifically focused on nutrient deficiencies in maize leaves, it encourages future researchers to expand their scope to include other crops and types of nutrient deficiencies, such as those affecting stems and roots. There is potential for developing models that provide specific treatment recommendations based on varying levels of maize leaf nutrient deficiencies. Although this research utilized CNNs with VGG16 and VGG19 models, future studies could explore alternative image classification algorithms for comparative performance analysis.

Furthermore, converting this research into a mobile application could greatly enhance accessibility and usability for stakeholders, facilitating the implementation of findings and recommendations. This study also acknowledges the impact of environmental factors on model performance, particularly concerning soil types and lighting conditions. The model was tested on images captured under various lighting conditions, underscoring the importance of generalization across different environments. Future research will focus on evaluating the model’s robustness and ability to generalize by testing its performance on images taken under diverse environmental conditions.
