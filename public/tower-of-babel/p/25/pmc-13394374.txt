Antimicrobial Resistance in Escherichia coli From Diagnostic Fecal and Uterine Samples of Swine in Northern Vietnam
Authors: Nguyen Tuan Anh Mai, Van Phan Le, Thanh H. Nguyen, Csaba Varga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) in swine poses a threat to animal health and food safety. Escherichia coli is commonly used as an indicator organism for AMR surveillance; however, comparative data across anatomical sites in swine remain limited in countries with intensive antimicrobial use, such as Vietnam.ObjectivesThis study aims to characterize phenotypic AMR and multidrug resistance (MDR) in E. coli isolated from faecal and uterine samples of swine in Northern Vietnam and to compare AMR patterns between sample types and across seasons.MethodsSamples submitted to a veterinary diagnostic laboratory in Northern Vietnam between February 2023 and November 2024 were cultured for E. coli. Isolates were tested for antimicrobial susceptibility using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method. The tested antimicrobials represented five major classes: β‐lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, and folate‐pathway inhibitors. Descriptive analyses and logistic regression models assessed AMR and MDR profiles and associations with sample type and season.ResultsA total of 211 E. coli isolates (96 faecal and 115 uterine) were identified. High resistance was observed to ampicillin (96.7%), tetracycline (95.7%), gentamicin (80.1%), sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (80.1%), and enrofloxacin (73.5%). Significantly higher odds of AMR were observed in faecal isolates for ceftiofur, enrofloxacin, norfloxacin, and sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (odds ratios: 1.38–4.62). Higher odds of resistance to ceftiofur and gentamicin were observed during the winter months.ConclusionsHigh AMR was observed in E. coli from swine in Northern Vietnam, with faecal isolates having a significantly higher resistance than uterine isolates, likely reflecting selection pressure in the intestinal tract or differences between commensal and extraintestinal E. coli populations.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in commensal and pathogenic Escherichia coli (E. coli) from swine remains a major global health problem, as it could affect animals, humans, and their surrounding environment (McEwen and Collignon 2018).

The rise of AMR in Vietnam could be influenced by the availability of antimicrobials without prescription and the overuse and misuse of antibiotics in large commercial and small‐scale livestock production, including swine (Coyne et al. 2019; Lhermie et al. 2016). Swine production and consumption of pork are important in Vietnam (Ruengjirachuporn 2017), highlighting the likelihood of transmission of multidrug‐resistant commensal or pathogenic bacteria to humans via the food chain (Huong et al. 2014).

In 2017, Vietnam's Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development (MARD) approved a National Action Plan to address AMR in livestock and aquaculture. The plan aimed to improve antimicrobial use (AMU) in livestock by restricting non‐therapeutic AMU, including the use of antibiotics for growth promotion, while strengthening AMR surveillance and awareness (taking effect from 2020) (MARD 2017). However, the implementation of this policy has faced challenges because antibiotics are frequently used for prophylactic purposes on swine farms to account for the poor animal productivity and high disease incidence (Carrique‐Mas et al. 2015). On swine farms, the majority (approximately 90%) of antimicrobials are administered orally at the herd level through feed or water, exposing large groups of animals and their intestinal microbiota to antimicrobial selection pressure (Chauvin et al. 2002; Lekagul et al. 2020; Merle et al. 2012; van Rennings et al. 2015; Wallinga et al. 2006). These administration routes increase the selection of multidrug‐resistant bacteria in the intestinal tract of animals (L. Zhang et al. 2013).

Both pathogenic and commensal E. coli are relevant for AMR surveillance, but they provide different information. Pathogenic E. coli isolates cause infections, and understanding AMR in disease‐causing bacteria is useful in informing therapeutic decisions. In contrast, commensal E. coli is the dominant aerobic bacterium in the gastrointestinal microbiota of vertebrates (Savageau 1983; Tenaillon et al. 2010) and is used to monitor AMR in swine production to identify emerging AMR trends (Sodagari et al. 2023). In addition, commensal E. coli are considered an indicator for the selection pressure of AMU (Varga et al. 2009). Moreover, multidrug‐resistant E. coli within the gut can transfer resistance genes to other commensal or pathogenic bacteria, posing a risk to animal and human health (Y.‐Y. Liu et al. 2016).

Previous studies assessed AMR and AMU in Vietnamese commercial and small‐scale swine production (Chao et al. 2025; Nguyen et al. 2016). However, no study compared AMR patterns between faecal and uterine E. coli isolates in swine. Fecal E. coli AMR patterns may indicate herd‐level antimicrobial exposure and environmental persistence of resistant bacteria, whereas uterine isolates are more likely linked with reproductive tract infections. Also, it was previously described that AMR on swine farms was seasonal, with a higher prevalence in spring and summer than in winter (X. Liu et al. 2022).

This study conducts an epidemiological analysis of AMR in swine E. coli, focusing on resistance profiles, differences between faecal‐ and uterine‐origin isolates, and seasonal variation to inform antimicrobial stewardship strategies. We hypothesize that faecal E. coli isolates will exhibit higher resistance to antimicrobials commonly used on swine farms. We further hypothesize that AMR patterns in E. coli are influenced by season, with higher resistance in warmer months. To test these hypotheses, the objectives of this study are to (1) describe the antimicrobial resistance profiles of E. coli isolates from swine to antimicrobials commonly used in swine production, (2) compare the AMR between faecal‐ and uterine‐origin E. coli isolates, and (3) assess the effect of season on AMR levels.

Materials and Methods

Collection of Samples

Samples were collected by field veterinarians and farm owners between February 2023 and November 2024 across provinces in Northern Vietnam and submitted to a veterinary diagnostic laboratory. Uterine samples were obtained using sterile swabs coated with a mixture of agarose or a sterile syringe, whereas faecal samples were collected using sterile swabs. The samples were kept on ice and sent to the veterinary diagnostic laboratory for analysis on the same day.

Bacterial Isolation and Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

For each submission, a swab containing uterine or faeces was plated onto CHROMagar ECC (CHROMagar Microbiology, Paris, France). Culture plates were incubated under aerobic conditions at 37°C, and bacterial growth was assessed after 24 h of incubation. The suspected E. coli colony was identified according to instructions from the manufacturer. The colony was enriched in Nutrient broth (Thermo Scientific, Cat. No. CM0001B) and 15% glycerol and stored at −80°C.

These colonies were transferred to Mueller–Hinton agar (Thermo Scientific, Cat. No. CM0337B) for antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Bacterial suspensions were adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard in 0.9% saline and spread onto agar plates. Disks were applied within 15 min, and plates were incubated at 37 ± 1°C for 18–24 h. Zone diameters were measured in millimetres and interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (M100, 2024; VET01S, 2023). Antibiotics included in the antimicrobial susceptibility testing panel were selected according to customer requirements. These antibiotics were purchased from Oxoid, United Kingdom: ampicillin (AMP) 10 µg, kanamycin (KAN) 30 µg, gentamicin (GEN) 10 µg, tetracycline (TET) 30 µg, doxycycline (DOX) 30 µg, norfloxacin (NOR) 10 µg, sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (SXT) 25 µg, ceftiofur (TIO) 30 µg, and enrofloxacin (ENR) 5 µg.

Isolates were categorized as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant based on interpretive zone diameter breakpoints established by the CLSI in M100 (35th Edition) and VET01S (7th edition). Among the tested agents, ceftiofur was the only antimicrobial with swine‐specific breakpoints available in CLSI VET01S. For enrofloxacin, poultry breakpoints were applied. Breakpoints for all remaining antimicrobials were derived from human interpretive breakpoints in CLSI M100 (35th edition).

Intermediate isolates were considered resistant. The level of antimicrobial resistance was defined as rare: <0.1%, very low: 0.1%–1.0%, low: >1.0–10.0%, moderate: >10.0%–20.0%, high: >20.0%–50.0%, very high: >50.0%–70.0%, and extremely high: >70.0% (European Food Safety Authority and European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control 2021).

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.1; R Core Team, 2024) in RStudio (version 1.4.1106; RStudio, PBC, 2009–2021). For each sample type, we calculated the prevalence of antimicrobial resistance as the proportion of resistant isolates out of all isolates of that sample type that were tested for each drug. Binomial 95% confidence intervals (CI) for each proportion were calculated using the Clopper–Pearson method. Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as resistance to three or more antimicrobial classes. For each class, an isolate was considered resistant to an antimicrobial class if it was resistant to at least one drug in that class.

Antimicrobial Resistance Pattern Analysis

A dendrogram was constructed using Ward's hierarchical clustering method with Euclidean distance to evaluate co‐resistance patterns among isolates. The resulting clusters were visualized as a heatmap created with the heatmap.2 function from the gplots package (Warnes et al. 2005).

Pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients between antimicrobial resistances (yes/no) were calculated to assess the strength of the association and visualized as correlograms using the ggcorrplot package (Kassambara and Patil 2016).

Logistic Regression

Logistic regression models were constructed to assess the association between resistance to individual antimicrobials and selected predictor variables. The outcome variable was binary (yes/no), representing resistance (yes/no) to individual antimicrobials. Intermediate isolates were included in the resistant category. Two categorical predictors were included: sample origin (faecal samples, compared to uterine samples as the reference) and season (winter as the reference). The seasons were grouped into spring (March–May), summer (June–August), fall (September–November), and winter (December–February).

First, univariable models were fitted for each antimicrobial and one predictor individually, followed by a multivariable model incorporating both predictors to evaluate their combined effects. Model parameters were estimated using the glm function with a logit link, and the results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs and corresponding p values. A p value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. An OR >1 indicated an increased odds of resistance relative to the reference category, whereas an OR <1 indicated a decreased odds of resistance.

Resistance was widespread across nearly all antimicrobial classes tested. The highest levels of resistance were observed for ampicillin (96.7%) and tetracycline (95.7%). High resistance was also found for gentamicin (80.1%), trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole (80.1%), and enrofloxacin (73.5%), while moderate resistance occurred for kanamycin (63.0%), doxycycline (62.6%), ceftiofur (56.4%), and norfloxacin (52.1%). These results showed that high resistance to several antibiotic classes commonly used on swine farms remains a concern.

In faecal isolates, almost all were resistant to ampicillin (97.9%) and tetracycline (96.9%). Very high resistance was also seen for trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole (91.7%) and enrofloxacin (82.3%), followed by gentamicin (84.4%) and kanamycin (68.8%). Moderate levels were observed for doxycycline (66.7%), ceftiofur (64.6%), and norfloxacin (67.7%).

Among uterine isolates, nearly all isolates were resistant to ampicillin (95.7%) and tetracycline (94.8%). High resistance was also found for gentamicin (76.5%), trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole (70.4%), and enrofloxacin (66.1%). Moderate resistance levels were observed for kanamycin (58.3%), doxycycline (59.1%), ceftiofur (49.6%), and norfloxacin (39.1%).

Overall, the distribution of MDR profiles varied between the two sample types. The most frequent MDR pattern was AG | BL | FI | FQ | TC, representing resistance to aminoglycosides (AG), β‐lactams (BL), folate‐pathway inhibitors, fluoroquinolones, and tetracyclines (TC). This MDR profile, which included resistance to five classes, was found in 69 faecal isolates (71.9%) and 50 uterine isolates (43.5%), making it the predominant MDR pattern in both groups.

Among faecal isolates, other common MDR profiles included AG | BL | FI | TC (12.5%), AG | BL | FQ | TC (4.2%), and BL | FI | FQ | TC (3.1%), each representing narrower four‐class combinations. In contrast, uterine isolates showed greater diversity, with frequent profiles such as AG | BL | FQ | TC (13.9%), AG | BL | FI | TC (13.0%), and AG | BL | TC (8.7%). Several additional profiles, BL | FI | TC, AG | BL | FQ, and AG | BL | FI, occurred at low frequencies (<5%) and together accounted for only a small portion of isolates in each group.

Across all MDR combinations, BL were the most consistently represented drug class, appearing in nearly every resistance pattern, followed closely by AG and TC.

Discussion

In this study, we applied a comprehensive epidemiological approach, combining descriptive analyses and logistic regression modelling, to characterize antimicrobial resistance patterns in swine E. coli and evaluate differences by isolate source and season. A high rate of AMR in E. coli isolated from faecal and uterine samples of swine in Northern Vietnam was identified. Resistance was the highest for BL (ampicillin > 95%) and TC (> 94%), followed by gentamicin, sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim, and enrofloxacin. Fecal isolates had higher AMR than uterine isolates, including resistance to aminoglycosides (AG), BL, folate‐pathway inhibitors, fluoroquinolones, and TC. Strong co‐resistance between enrofloxacin and norfloxacin, antimicrobials in the same class (fluoroquinolones), was observed. Significantly higher odds of resistance in faecal isolates compared to uterine isolates were detected for ceftiofur, enrofloxacin, norfloxacin, and sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim. Lower odds of ceftiofur and gentamicin resistance were observed during non‐winter months. These findings indicate that the intestinal tract serves as a reservoir of multidrug‐resistant E. coli in pigs. Antimicrobial stewardship and improved health management practices on swine farms are needed, which consider the sample source and seasonal influences on AMR emergence.

Resistance to BL was widespread in both faecal and uterine E. coli isolates in this study, with ampicillin resistance exceeding 95%. This agrees with earlier reports from Vietnam, where commensal E. coli from pigs and chickens showed extremely high resistance to BL and a predominance of multidrug‐resistant phenotypes (Nhung et al. 2016; Tuat et al. 2021). Similar levels have been observed in international surveys of livestock and companion animals, reflecting sustained selection pressure from antimicrobial use (Y.‐Y. Liu et al. 2016; Yudhanto et al. 2024). The detection of resistance to third‐generation cephalosporins, particularly ceftiofur, supports previous evidence of extended‐spectrum β‐lactamase (ESBL)–producing E. coli harbouring bla CTX‐M and bla TEM genes in Vietnamese pig farms and among farm workers (Kim et al. 2025; Truong et al. 2021). However, the ceftiofur resistance observed in our faecal isolates (∼50–60%) was higher than levels previously reported in Vietnamese livestock (∼10%) (Tuat et al. 2021) and substantially greater than the 0%–5.5% reported in healthy pigs in Europe and North America (Gibbons et al. 2016; Smith et al. 2016; Sodagari and Varga 2023; Song et al. 2022). This suggests that third‐generation cephalosporins may be used more intensively in Vietnamese swine farms or persist longer in local environments, maintaining strong selective pressure for ceftiofur resistance.

Resistance to AG was also high for gentamicin (63%–78%) and kanamycin (50%–54%) in faecal isolates. These values are more than double those previously reported in Vietnam (∼30%) (Nhung et al. 2016) and surpass resistance levels seen in North America and Europe (O'Neill et al. 2023; Sodagari et al. 2023). Comparable results were observed in pathogenic E. coli from South Korea, where aminoglycoside resistance exceeded 60% (Do et al. 2022). The consistently high aminoglycoside resistance observed here may be linked to routine in‐feed or in‐water antimicrobial use and limited stewardship implementation in smallholder and semi‐intensive farms.

Fluoroquinolone resistance was higher in faecal than in uterine isolates, with enrofloxacin and norfloxacin resistance correlating strongly in the E. coli isolated from faecal samples. This pattern was observed in previous studies reporting fluoroquinolone resistance exceeding 70% in swine E. coli across Asia (Fang et al. 2019; X. Zhang et al. 2020). The strong cross‐resistance between enrofloxacin and norfloxacin is consistent with their shared mode of action, being part of the same antimicrobial class, and the spread of mutations in gyrA and parC genes, or plasmid‐mediated qnr genes (Seo et al. 2022). By contrast, the weaker association in uterine isolates likely reflects lower and less consistent drug exposure in the reproductive tract.

Resistance to sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim was high in both sample types and aligned with the findings from China, South Korea, and Vietnam, where resistance to folate‐pathway inhibitors remains common in swine and poultry E. coli (Fang et al. 2019; Song et al. 2022; Tuat et al. 2021). Tetracycline resistance was nearly universal in both faecal and uterine isolates, consistent with its long‐term and heavy use in pig production worldwide (O'Neill et al. 2023; Varga et al. 2009). Similar levels were reported by Abdelwahab et al. (2022), who also detected concurrent resistance to gentamicin, sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim, tetracycline, and ampicillin in swine isolates. Doxycycline resistance in our isolates, however, was lower compared to Chinese pig farms (X. Liu et al. 2022), indicating possible differences in dosing practices or drug exposure frequency.

MDR pattern analysis revealed distinct anatomical differences: faecal isolates frequently displayed five‐class resistance profiles typically including BL, AG, TC, folate‐pathway inhibitors, and fluoroquinolones, whereas uterine isolates tended to include three or four classes. This trend was consistent with previous observations that commensal E. coli from faecal samples serve as reservoirs for multidrug resistance and gene exchange within herds (Nhung et al. 2016; Peng et al. 2022; Truong et al. 2021). Cluster analysis also confirmed that the faecal isolates exhibited more consistent co‐resistance patterns, whereas uterine isolates were more heterogeneous; these findings are consistent with global AMR network studies showing denser co‐selection matrices in enteric bacteria (von Wintersdorff et al. 2016).

The logistic regression findings showed significantly higher odds of resistance to ceftiofur, enrofloxacin, norfloxacin, and sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim in faecal isolates, supporting earlier studies highlighting the gut as the main site of antimicrobial exposure in pigs (Nhung et al. 2016; Tuat et al. 2021). The seasonal differences observed, such as lower ceftiofur and gentamicin resistance outside winter, are consistent with a previous study, which found winter peaks in AMR rates across respiratory and enteric bacteria (Martinez et al. 2019). These patterns indicate that environmental and management‐related factors, such as seasonal antibiotic use and disease occurrence, might likely contribute to the difference in AMR across seasons. Collectively, our results confirm that the intestinal microbiota of pigs in Northern Vietnam act as a reservoir for MDR E. coli, paralleling the resistance trends observed in recent regional and international studies (Do et al. 2022; Kim et al. 2025; Sodagari and Varga 2023; Truong et al. 2021).

The higher resistance levels observed in enteric E. coli are consistent with the strong selection pressure exerted by orally administered antimicrobials. These high drug levels put strong pressure on gut bacteria to survive (L. Zhang et al. 2013). Most of the drugs used on farms are mixed into feed or water (Chauvin et al. 2002; Lekagul et al. 2020; Merle et al. 2012; van Rennings et al. 2015; Wallinga et al. 2006). This prolonged contact favours the persistence and exchange of resistance genes, explaining the tighter clustering and more extensive multidrug resistance (MDR) profiles detected in faecal isolates (von Wintersdorff et al. 2016). In contrast, the uterus is less often exposed to antibiotics due to targeted treatment. This limited drug exposure likely explains the varied and lower resistance levels found in bacteria from the uterus. The high resistance to ceftiofur might be due to the spread of ESBL genes, particularly blaCTX‐M variants, which are being reported in livestock (Kim et al. 2025). ESBL‐producing E. coli can persist in manure and farm environments, facilitating horizontal transmission and maintaining cephalosporin resistance reservoirs (Aslam et al. 2018). Co‐selection also contributes to the strong MDR profiles. Many resistance genes for drugs such as fluoroquinolones, AG, and beta‐lactams are all located on the same mobile genetic elements. Therefore, giving one drug can keep the resistance to the other drugs (Baindara 2019). Seasonal differences in resistance may reflect changes in how antibiotics are used and how often diseases occur throughout the year. Cold months often bring more respiratory diseases, which can lead to higher antibiotic use and, consequently, higher resistance levels in winter (Martinez et al. 2019). This explains the lower ceftiofur and kanamycin resistance outside of winter in our data.

According to the World Health Organization's (WHO) Medically Important Antimicrobials List for Human Medicine, nine antibiotics used in our study belong to these categories: highest priority critically important antimicrobials (ceftiofur, enrofloxacin, norfloxacin), critically important antimicrobials (gentamicin, kanamycin), and highly important antimicrobials (ampicillin, sulfamethoxazole‐trimethoprim, doxycycline, tetracycline) (World Health Organization 2024). Meat products serve as a potential vector for antimicrobial‐resistant pathogens, posing a significant threat to public health and compromising the efficacy of clinical treatments (World Health Organization 2022). Antibiotic‐contaminated water and improper disposal of animal manure could also contribute to the reservoirs of antibiotics and antibiotic‐resistant bacteria (Ghimpețeanu et al. 2022). The residual antimicrobial agents and resistant bacterial populations in food products such as meat could potentially pose a threat to public health (Arsène et al. 2022).

Comparing the results of our study to previous studies should be done with caution due to differences in study design, sample source, location, and antimicrobial susceptibility tests used (disk diffusion vs. broth microdilution). In addition, before interpreting our study, several limitations should be considered. First, samples were collected from a specific geographic region and may not be representative of swine populations in other parts of Vietnam or different production systems. Second, differences observed between faecal and uterine isolates may reflect ecological and physiological differences between intestinal and reproductive tract environments rather than antimicrobial exposure alone. Additionally, information on prior AMU, animal age, production stage, farm type, husbandry practices, disease status, and reason for sample submission was not available, limiting our ability to link observed resistance phenotypes to specific risk factors. Also, the isolates were obtained from passively submitted diagnostic samples rather than from a randomized surveillance study. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as a descriptive assessment of AMR among the tested isolates and not as population‐level prevalence estimates. Finally, the samples analyzed were from a diagnostic laboratory submitted from diseased swine with possible previous AMU; therefore, the AMR prevalence might be overestimated.

Future studies should focus on the molecular characterization of resistance genes to confirm the genetic basis of the co‐selection of antimicrobial resistance observed in this study. Understanding these underlying mechanisms in commensal E. coli is critical for identifying which drugs drive specific resistance patterns, thereby guiding more effective antibiotic usage and treatment protocols. Implementation of an antimicrobial stewardship programme is also important, as it was found to help decrease the prevalence of antimicrobial resistance in E. coli (Shrestha et al. 2023).

Conclusions

This study used a rigorous epidemiological analysis and identified higher rates of AMR and MDR in faecal isolates than in uterine isolates, suggesting that orally administered antimicrobials might impose selection pressure on the intestinal microbiota of pigs. The high prevalence of resistance, including to WHO‐classified highest‐priority critically important antimicrobials such as ceftiofur and enrofloxacin, is concerning. Seasonal variation in AMR suggests that temporal factors may influence AMR patterns. The transmission of multidrug‐resistant bacteria through foodborne and environmental pathways poses a food safety and public health risk. Integrated AMR surveillance, molecular characterization of resistance determinants, and strengthened antimicrobial stewardship strategies are needed to reduce the burden of AMR.
