Expanding outpatient eligibility using a narrow definition of cancer in the Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index
Authors: Aidan R Campbell, Camille M Smith, Grace V Heringer, Javeria Khader, Madeline J Somers, Pierre-Marie Roy, David R Vinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGuidelines recommend the Pulmonary Embolism (PE) Severity Index (PESI) or the simplified PESI (sPESI) to identify low-risk patients eligible for outpatient care. Cancer is a high-scoring variable in both scores, but definitions vary: “any” cancer (active/recent as well as remote/nonactive) vs “active” cancer (only active or recent).ObjectivesThis study evaluated the effect of narrowing the cancer definition (from any to active) on outpatient eligibility.MethodsWe conducted a secondary analysis of a retrospective cohort study across 21 US community hospitals. Included were ambulatory adults diagnosed with PE in primary care who underwent vital sign measurements. Active-cancer PESI accrued cancer points only for metastatic disease or diagnosis, treatment, or palliative care within 12 months. We compared proportions of low-risk classification and 30-day adverse events (recurrent venous thromboembolism, major bleeding, and all-cause mortality) between any- and active-cancer definitions in both PESI and sPESI.ResultsAmong 636 included patients, 132 (20.8%) had any cancer and 49 (7.7%) had active cancer. Active-cancer PESI classified more patients as low risk than any-cancer PESI: 69.7% vs 61.9%; difference, 7.7% (95% CI, 5.8%-10.1%; P < .001). Findings were similar with sPESI, although smaller: 52.8% vs 47.0%; difference, 5.8% (95% CI, 4.1%-7.9%; P < .001). Thirty-day outcomes among low-risk patients were rare (0.5%) and similar between groups.ConclusionAn active-only cancer definition (vs any cancer) in PESI and sPESI classified a larger proportion of PE patients as low risk. No differences in 30-day outcomes were observed in this very low-risk cohort. Studies in higher-risk cohorts are needed.Keywords: ambulatory care, cancer, outpatient, pulmonary embolism, retrospective studies, risk assessment

Introduction

Society guidelines recommend validated risk stratification tools, such as the Pulmonary Embolism (PE) Severity Index (PESI) and its simplified counterpart (sPESI), to identify low-risk patients who may be eligible for outpatient care [[1], [2], [3]]. Using these indices, outpatient eligibility requires either a class I or II score on the PESI (with points totaling <86) or 0 points on the sPESI (Table 1) [4,5]. Cancer is a high-scoring component in both indices and can have significant implications for early site-of-care decision making, ie, discharge home vs hospitalization. On the PESI, cancer adds 30 points, enough to preclude outpatient care of an otherwise healthy 46-year-old man or a 56-year-old woman with near normal vital signs. By adding 1 point to the sPESI, cancer alone places patients of any age outside the low-risk class often required for outpatient care (Table 1).

The definition of cancer used to calculate the PESI and sPESI scores varies. Many studies have used a broader definition that includes history of cancer and active cancer [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Others have used a narrower definition, restricted to active or recent cancer, assigning no points to cancer that is only remote or long in remission [[8], [9], [10]]. Even the definition of “recent cancer” varies from a 6-month threshold [11,12] to a 12-month threshold [9,13,14]. Professional society guidelines have not commented on the varying cancer definitions used in PESI and sPESI scoring, nor its implications for PE management decisions. Guideline recommendations from the 2026 American Heart Association and the 2019 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) have placed the PESI and sPESI at the base of their PE risk stratification algorithms used to guide PE treatment [1,2]. Both guidelines define cancer only as “cancer,” without qualification.

Little research has been undertaken comparing the effects of differing cancer definitions in the PESI or sPESI on the proportion of patients classified as low risk and potentially eligible for outpatient care. We aimed to address this knowledge gap by evaluating low-risk classification differences when using a broad (any cancer) vs a narrow (only active or recent) definition of cancer in PESI and sPESI calculations in a cohort of adults with acute PE diagnosed in primary care.

Methods

Design and setting

This retrospective study was a secondary analysis of the PE in Primary Care (PEPC) study [15], which was conducted across Kaiser Permanente (KP) Northern California, a large, US integrated health care system providing comprehensive care for >4.5 million members via 21 medical centers and >260 associated clinics. The study was approved by the KP Northern California institutional review board with waiver of informed consent.

Study population

The PEPC study included health plan members aged ≥18 years with objectively-confirmed PE diagnosed in the outpatient primary care setting from 2013 through 2019. Patients were excluded if they were already taking anticoagulants on presentation, were residents of a skilled nursing facility, were receiving only comfort-focused care, were initially transferred to an out-of-system facility, or were known to be pregnant. Details of cohort assembly are reported elsewhere [15]. Patients from PEPC were included in this current analysis if at least 1 set of vital signs (even if incomplete) were documented in the electronic health record during their primary care or emergency department (ED) evaluation.

Data collection and study definitions

Data were collected for PEPC by electronic extraction from administrative and clinical databases combined with a manual chart review [15]. For this current analysis, we included variables needed to calculate the PESI and sPESI (Table 1). These included patient age, sex at birth, primary care and ED vital signs, mental status, and the following 3 comorbidities: cancer (using 2 definitions described further), heart failure (either systolic or diastolic), and chronic lung disease (including asthma). For our score calculations, we used the worst value: the lowest temperature, lowest systolic blood pressure, highest pulse rate, highest respiratory rate, and lowest oxygen saturation documented. Missing vital sign values were uncommon (except clinic-based respiratory rate) and were assumed to be nonscoring values (eg, respiratory rate < 30 breaths/min and oxygen saturation ≥90%) (Table 1) [15].

The narrow definition of cancer (“active cancer”) used in the PEPC study was active, recurrent, metastatic, or was diagnosed or treated (eg, surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, hormonal therapy, or palliative care) within the prior 12 months [15]. Cancer that failed to meet active criteria (eg, was in remission and not receiving treatment within 12 months) was categorized as nonactive. “Any cancer” included active and nonactive cancers. Consistent with other studies, we excluded nonmetastatic nonmelanoma skin cancer (eg, cutaneous basal or squamous cell carcinoma) from both active and any cancer categories [16,17].

PESI and sPESI scores and risk classification were calculated using both definitions of cancer. In any-cancer PESI and sPESI, cancer points were assigned for patients with either active or nonactive cancer. In active-cancer PESI and sPESI, cancer points were assigned only to patients whose cancer was active, as defined earlier.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the percent change in patients classified as low risk by subtracting the smaller percentage of low-risk any-cancer patients from the larger percentage of low-risk active-cancer patients. We reported the percent change in low-risk classification with both PESI and sPESI. Our secondary (or safety) outcomes were 30-day adverse events. Adverse events included major bleeding using the definition of the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) [18], objectively-confirmed new or worsening venous thromboembolism, and all-cause mortality, as in the parent PEPC study [15]. We used the KP Northern California claims database to identify 30-day outcomes that occurred outside the closed health system. To identify death, we used a health system mortality database that links to the Social Security death master file and the California State Department of Vital Statistics.

Statistical analysis

We compared proportions of low-risk patients (active-cancer PESI vs any-cancer PESI and active-cancer sPESI vs any-cancer sPESI) using McNemar exact test and calculated marginal Wald-type CIs on the differences in proportions. We considered a 2-sided P value of < .05 to indicate statistical significance. Analyses were conducted in R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), version 4.3.1.

Results and Discussion

Study cohort

Of 652 patients in the parent PEPC study, 16 were excluded for lacking documented vital signs. The remaining 636 constitute our study cohort. Their median age was 65 years (IQR, 51-74 years), and 52% were male. One hundred thirty-two patients (20.8%) had any cancer, and 49 (7.7%) met the active cancer definition.

Outcomes

Active-cancer PESI classified more patients as low risk than any-cancer PESI (+7.7%) (Table 2). Findings were similar with sPESI, although the magnitude of the difference was slightly smaller (+5.8%). Thirty-day adverse events were rare (0.5%) among those classified as low risk (Table 3). No patient who was reclassified from higher risk to low risk experienced a 30-day adverse event.

Discussion and next steps

We found that active-cancer PESI classified a statistically significantly greater proportion of ambulatory patients with PE as low risk compared with any-cancer PESI. Active-cancer sPESI also classified a greater proportion of patients as low risk than any-cancer sPESI. Patients reclassified from higher risk to low risk did not experience any 30-day adverse outcomes. We are unaware of other studies that have calculated the magnitude of reclassification between active-cancer PESI or sPESI and any-cancer PESI or sPESI.

The broader definition of cancer commonly used in PESI scoring (ie, any cancer) originated from the nonspecific definition of cancer found in the first derivation and validation study of PESI: history of cancer (Table 4) [[19], [20], [21]] . This is also the definition of cancer used in the online calculator of MDCalc [22]. The original PESI investigators clarified their definition of cancer in their second external validation study: defined as a history of cancer or active cancer [6]. This definition has been widely used [21], including in PESI-based multicenter trials of outpatient PE management [4,7].

The more narrow cancer definition, on the other hand, is limited to active or recent cancer. Active cancer has been used to calculate PESI in some outpatient PE management studies and to guide PE management in some institutional care pathways [8,23]. Active cancer is used to calculate PESI and sPESI scores by the Registro Informatizado de la Enfermedad TromboEmbólica (RIETE), the world’s largest prospective PE registry. RIETE defines cancer as “newly diagnosed cancer or cancer undergoing treatment [in the last year] (ie, surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hormonal, or support therapy),” the same definition of active cancer used in PEPC [9]. A recent study of the PESI-based risk stratification algorithm from the ESC used a narrow definition of cancer in their calculation [10]. Distinguishing patients with only remote, nonactive cancer from those with active or recent cancer makes sense when estimating prognosis as these 2 groups carry different risks for 30-day adverse events [24].

The differences in cancer definitions in PESI and sPESI calculations present challenges to those interpreting the literature. Readers need to be mindful of which version of PESI or sPESI was being studied. Questions also confront clinicians and health care institutions who want to use PESI-based risk stratification instruments (eg, that of the American Heart Association [1] and of ESC [2]) in patient care: which definition of cancer (active or any) is best for their PESI and sPESI calculations?

We also found that PESI classified more patients as low risk compared with sPESI (Table 2). This pattern has been described in other studies [[25], [26], [27]]. The sPESI is easier to calculate than its longer counterpart, although it has disadvantages not found in PESI. Unlike PESI, sPESI automatically excludes from low-risk classification and outpatient care patients aged >80 years, those with certain cardiopulmonary conditions, and those with cancer. The 5-tiered classification strata of PESI is more nuanced than the simplistic binary of sPESI (Table 1). Calculation of the 11-variable PESI is not difficult if one uses an online calculator, such as MDCalc [22,25].

Next steps should include reclassification studies in different populations and different settings. Our study included patients diagnosed with PE in primary care, a highly selected low-risk population [15]. We suspect that the magnitude of reclassification would be lower in unselected ED patients, who have a greater comorbidity burden and a higher prevalence of abnormal vital signs than our primary care patients [7,28]. Also needed are studies that compare performance metrics between any-cancer PESI and active-cancer PESI in predicting 30-day outcomes. We suspect that active-cancer PESI would perform at least as well as any-cancer PESI.

Society guidelines could serve their readers by discussing the variation in cancer definitions used in the PESIs. When evidence allows, recommendations on which cancer definition is the best would be helpful to clinicians and researchers alike. A similar argument on the value of definitional uniformity has been made by researchers of cancer-associated PE [24].

Limitations

Missing vital sign values were uncommon (except clinic-based respiratory rate) and were assumed to be nonscoring values (eg, respiratory rate < 30 breaths/min and oxygen saturation ≥ 90%) (Table 1) [15]. This was not an unreasonable assumption as the primary care clinicians believed these ambulatory patients were suited to an outpatient diagnostic evaluation for suspected PE rather than urgent transfer to the ED, as would have been indicated in an adult clinic outpatient with a respiratory rate of ≥ 30 breaths/min or an oxygen saturation of <90%. Our results may not be generalizable to different cohorts of patients in different settings. Given the very low adverse event rate observed (<1%), the study was underpowered to detect differences in 30-day outcomes between classification strategies. Even assuming higher combined event rates reported in ED cohorts (∼2%-3% [2.8% in patients with PESI classes I-II in our setting [7]), substantially larger samples would be required to meaningfully compare serious adverse outcomes across PESI categorizations using different cancer definitions. Thus, larger studies among ED patients (who are generally higher risk than our primary care population) are needed to better understand the effect of cancer-modified PESIs on outpatient eligibility and on 30-day outcomes.

Conclusion

Active-cancer PESI and sPESI classified a statistically significantly greater proportion of ambulatory patients with PE as low risk than any-cancer PESI and sPESI, respectively. This could have site-of-care implications when evaluating patients with acute PE who might be eligible for outpatient care.
