Self-reinforcing nanoplatform uniting pemetrexed, magnetic hyperthermia and PD-L1 blockade reshapes the tumor microenvironment and elicits combined antitumor immunity in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Zhengjun Guo, Xulin Xie, Wenxiu Li, Shouhui Yi, Emmanuel Koomson, Renardi Gunawan, Zhihang Zhou, Md Kowsar Alam, Li Zhou, Tongxu Si, Zesheng Wang, Xiaoyu Zhou, Yuan Peng, Xiaoyue Zhang, Zhenzhou Yang, Mengsu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, and immune checkpoint blockade is often limited by an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). To address these challenges, we developed a novel multifunctional nanoplatform, based on a mesoporous Fe3O4 core and silica shell, co-loading pemetrexed and an anti-PD-L1 antibody (PD-L1&Pem@msNPs). The construct enables checkpoint-targeted delivery, alternating magnetic field (AMF)-triggered hyperthermia and controlled drug release, enabling a single-system chemo–immuno–magnetothermal regimen. We comprehensively investigated the physicochemical properties, magnetothermal performance, loading/conjugation, and release behavior, and validated cellular uptake and cytotoxicity in vitro. In subcutaneous and orthotopic lung tumor models, PD-L1&Pem@msNPs achieved superior tumor suppression and extended survival compared with control formulations. Mechanistically, transcriptomic profiling together with immunophenotyping demonstrated marked TME remodeling, with increased intratumoral T-cell representation accompanied by coordinated rewiring of macrophages status. Notably, longitudinal flow cytometry revealed a shift in T-cell states from an exhausted-intermediate (Tex-int) toward a progenitor-like (Tex-prog) phenotype, consistent with restoration of T-cell functionality under treatment. Collectively, PD-L1&Pem@msNPs provides an externally activatable, modular platform to remodel the TME and improve the therapeutic impact of PD-L1 blockade in lung cancer.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the most prevalent form of cancer worldwide and remains a major public health challenge due to its high incidence and mortality rates [1,2]. Despite notable advances in therapeutic approaches over decades, the clinical outcomes are limited by low overall response rates and the emergence of acquired resistance, particularly in patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors [3,4].

Immunotherapy, as recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, has emerged as a promising approach for advanced NSCLC without driver mutations, achieving durable responses in a subset of patients [5,6]. However, monotherapy yields response rates of only 20-30%, and many responders eventually progress due to adaptive immune resistance [5,7]. Conventional chemotherapy, such as pemetrexed, exerts cytotoxicity against proliferating tumor cells but suffers from systemic toxicities (myelosuppression, mucositis) and non‐selective biodistribution [8,9]. External hyperthermia enhances tumor perfusion and drug uptake but lacks spatial precision, risking damage to adjacent normal tissues [10]. Monotherapies and dual combinations therefore remain suboptimal because they cannot fully address the immunosuppressive TME or minimize off‐target effects [8,11].

To overcome these challenges, we have engineered a nanotechnology-based Fe3O4-core msNPs that combine magnetic hyperthermia, on-demand chemotherapy drug pemetrexed release, and immune checkpoint blockade in a single, self-reinforcing platform (Scheme 1). Under an alternating magnetic field, the Fe3O4 core heats the tumor, collapses the silica shell to release pemetrexed and PD-L1ab on surviving cells. This will enhance antibody binding, T cell cytotoxicity. Materials and methods

Preparation of msNPs (Fe3O4@SiO2 nanoparticles)

FeCl2•4H2O and FeCl3•6H2O were dissolved in 1 mL of deionized water at a molar ratio of 1:2 to form an iron salt solution. Subsequently, 8 mL of ethylene glycol was added to this solution, and the mixture was stirred under a water bath at 60 °C. Following this, a 0.003 mol/L solution of sodium dodecylbenzenesulfonate (SDBS) was introduced, and stirring was continued for 20 min. Subsequently, the pH of the solution was adjusted to 8 b y dropwise addition of diethanolamine, at which point a rapid formation of black colloidal particles was observed. The resulting mixture was then transferred to an autoclave and reacted at 120 °C for 4 h. After the reaction, the product was cooled to 60 °C using a programmed cooling method, followed by centrifugation (25 °C, 10000 rpm, 10 min) and washing for subsequent use.

The Fe3O4 nanoparticles obtained from the previous step were dispersed in 200 mL of deionized water and mechanically stirred under a water bath. Subsequently, 0.002 mol/L of CTAB (cetyltrimethylammonium bromide) was added to the system as a surfactant and structure-directing agent, and stirring was continued for 5 min. The pH of the solution was then adjusted to 10 b y dropwise addition of diethanolamine, and stirring was maintained for an additional 15 min to ensure sufficient adsorption of CTAB onto the Fe3O4 surface. Following this, 1 mL of ethyl silicate diluted with ethanol was slowly added dropwise, and the mixture was stirred continuously to allow for the aging reaction. Upon completion of the reaction, the product was centrifuged, washed, and dried under natural conditions, ultimately yielding msNPs (mesoporous silica-coated Fe3O4 nanoparticles).

Preparation of PD-L1&Pem@mesoporous nanoparticles

In the process of fabricating PD-L1&Pem@msNPs, Pemetrexed is first dissolved in an appropriate solvent to create a supersaturated solution. This solution is then slowly added dropwise to a pre-dispersed and uniform msNPs suspension, with stirring to ensure thorough adsorption of Pem molecules into the mesoporous structure of msNPs. Following this, the supernatant is removed via centrifugation, and the resultant product is freeze-dried to obtain Pem@msNPs solids. Subsequently, the Pem@msNPs solids are introduced into a PD-L1 antibody solution and stirred for half an hour at low temperature, enabling the physical adsorption and coating of PD-L1 antibody molecules onto the Pem@msNPs surface, thus forming PD-L1&Pem@msNPs. Finally, stable PD-L1&Pem@msNPs nanoparticles are obtained through centrifugation.

Pem loading was quantified indirectly by measuring the amount of free pemetrexed remaining in the combined supernatants solutions after nanoparticle collection, as described in a previous report [12]. Similarly, the absorption efficiency of PD-L1 antibodies was assessed indirectly. The unbound antibody in the collected supernatant was quantified using a BCA protein assay. The absorption efficiency and antibody density were then calculated by subtracting unbound antibody from the total antibody added and expressing the incorporated antibody as μg antibody per mg nanoparticles, according to this report [13].

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

SEM was employed to examine the surface morphology and roughness of the nanoparticles. Prior to analysis, a small amount of the sample was dispersed on a carbon-coated copper grid and dried under ambient conditions. The SEM instrument was operated at an appropriate accelerating voltage (typically ranging from 5 kV to 20 kV) to obtain high-resolution images. These images provided valuable insights into the shape, size distribution, and surface texture of the nanoparticles.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM was used to further elucidate the internal structure and core-shell morphology of the nanoparticles. A drop of the nanoparticle suspension was placed on a copper grid coated with a thin carbon film and allowed it to dry. The TEM instrument was operated at a higher accelerating voltage (often exceeding 200 kV) to enable the transmission of electrons through the sample. High-magnification TEM images were acquired, revealing the precise shape, size, and core-shell interface of the nanoparticles. In addition, selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns could be obtained to confirm the crystalline nature of the Fe3O4 core and SiO2 shell.

Particle size analysis

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was utilized to determine the hydrodynamic diameter and size distribution of the nanoparticles in solution. A dilute suspension of the nanoparticles was prepared, and the sample was placed in a cuvette for analysis. The DLS instrument measured the fluctuations in scattered light intensity over time, which were then converted into particle size distributions based on the Stokes-Einstein equation. This method provided an estimate of the average particle size and its polydispersity index. Time-dependent hydrodynamic size stability was investigated at 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h, and measurements were performed in triplicate(n = 3, technical replicates). Data are reported as mean ± SD.

Zeta potential measurements

Zeta potential measurements were performed to assess the surface charge of the nanoparticles, which is crucial for understanding their colloidal stability and interaction with other molecules or surfaces. The nanoparticle suspension was diluted to an appropriate concentration and placed in a zeta potential cell. An electric field was applied across the cell, and the resulting electrophoretic mobility of the particles was measured. The zeta potential was then calculated using the Helmholtz-Smoluchowski equation. A high absolute value of zeta potential typically indicates good colloidal stability.

Magnetic analysis

Magnetic properties of the nanoparticles were investigated using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM). A small amount of dried nanoparticle powder was placed in a non-magnetic holder and subjected to a varying magnetic field. The magnetization of the sample was measured as a function of the applied field, yielding a hysteresis loop. From this curve, key magnetic parameters such as saturation magnetization, remanence, and coercivity could be extracted. These parameters provided insights into the magnetic strength, reversibility of magnetization, and energy required to change the magnetic state of the nanoparticles.

Animals, xenografts and in vivo imaging

Six-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Research Unit (LARU, City University of Hong Kong). Subcutaneous xenografts were established by injecting LLC cells (5 × 106 cells/mouse) into the right flank of mice. Eight weeks after implantation, mice were euthanized. Subcutaneous tumors were excised, weighed, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for paraffin embedding. Tumor volume was calculated as volume (V, mm3) = width2 × length/2. Fixed tumors were sectioned into 5-μm slices on an Olympus CX41 rotary microtome, then stained with Gill's hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological evaluation. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs) were processed identically to assess biosafety, focusing on inflammatory infiltration and tissue architecture.

For the orthotopic lung xenograft model, luciferase-expressing LLC cells (Luc-LLC) were harvested at > 90% viability and resuspended in a 1:1 mixture of PBS and Matrigel to a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/20 μL. The chest wall was shaved, disinfected, and injected percutaneously between the 6th and 7th ribs using a 30 G needle. Six-week-old C57BL/6 mice (n = 3 per group) were anesthetized with isoflurane, and the left chest wall was shaved and disinfected. Using a 30-G needle, 1 × 106 Luc-LLC cells in 20 μL PBS/Matrigel mixture (1:1) were injected percutaneously into the pleural cavity under aseptic conditions between the 6th and 7th ribs. The mice were recovered on heating pads and monitored daily. Bioluminescence imaging was performed on post-injection days 12, and 32 after administration of D-luciferin (150 mg/kg). Total photon flux was quantified using Living Image System (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer). Signal intensity (photons/sec/cm2/sr) was quantified with Living Image software (v4.5.1). Humane endpoints were predefined as: ≥15% body weight loss, severe dyspnea, inability to feed or moribund state. Animals reaching humane endpoints were euthanized and recorded as events in survival analyses. Necropsies were performed on all animals that died or were euthanized; cause of death was assigned based on macroscopic findings and corroborative histology and serum biochemistry.

All animal studies were carried out following the Guidelines on Ethical Review of Research Experiments Involving Animal Subjects and approved by Department of Health, Hong Kong.

Results

Characterization of msNPs materials

The successful fabrication of PD-L1&Pem@msNPs nanoparticles validates the self-assembly approach for creating multifunctional nanomaterials. The supersaturated Pem solution effectively loads Pem into the mesopores, crucial for drug delivery (Pem@msNPs). The gentle physical adsorption of PD-L1 onto Pem@msNPs at low temperatures preserves its biological activity, enhancing targeting and enabling further surface modifications. The SEM and TEM images demonstrated the msNPs were stably distributed in PBS buffer for weeks without aggregation in a uniform spherical shape (Fig. 1A and B). The sizes of the msNPs were narrowly distributed about 131.1 ± 42.2 nm (Fig. 1C). The particle diameter distributions of msNPs, Pem@msNPs, and PD-L1&Pem@msNPs were detected (Fig. S1A). As shown in Fig. S1B, both formulations maintained relatively stable hydrodynamic diameters across 1-48 h, indicating satisfactory colloidal stability during the observation period. The zeta potential of msNPs was 27.9 ± 0.8 mV (Fig. 1D). The zeta potential of msNPs, Pem@msNPs, and PD-L1&Pem@msNPs were measured (Fig. S1C).

To evaluate the magnetic properties of nanoparticles, the magnetization curve was obtained. And the magnetization curve demonstrates a typical superparamagnetic behavior with negligible coercivity and remanence, indicating that msNPs exhibit no magnetic hysteresis (Fig. 1E). This result confirms the high magnetic susceptibility of the msNPs and the potential application in magnetic hyperthermia and controlled drug delivery. To test the thermal properties of msNPs, different concentrations of msNPs were put into the AMF for a period, and the resulting temperature changes were recorded accordingly. PBS was set as control. As shown in Fig. 1F, the temperature of msNPs at concentrations of 0.5 mg/mL and 1.0 mg/mL increased from 27 °C to a plateau of 43 °C and 46 °C, respectively, while the temperature of the PBS control remained constant at 27 °C. This result indicates that the temperature rise of msNPs under an AMF is concentration-dependent, with higher concentrations leading to faster heating rates and higher maximum temperatures. Additionally, the magnetic hyperthermal response of msNPs remains stable after three consecutive cycles of heating (AMF ON) and natural cooling (AMF OFF), demonstrating the thermal stability of msNPs (Fig. 1G). The hyperthermal response of msNPs, Pem@msNPs, and PD-L1&Pem@msNPs under AMF ON and OFF conditions were systematically evaluated (Fig. S1C). These findings highlight the potential of msNPs as stable thermal therapeutic agents for future biomedical applications. UV-vis absorption spectroscopy was utilized to evaluate the concentration profiles of controlled Pem released from Pem@msNPs over varying incubation times, ranging from 0 to 60 min (Fig. 1H). The results demonstrated that all samples exhibited a maximum absorption peak at a wavelength of 280 nm (Fig. 1H). And under AMF, the released Pem concentration increases with incubation time (Fig. 1H). The Pem@msNPs demonstrated a robust and controlled drug release profile under an AMF, achieving over 50% drug release within the first 100 min and approximately 70% release after 250 min of incubation (Fig. 1I). The Pem loading capacity was 825.2 μg/mL for Pem@msNPs and 782.4 μg/mL for PD-L1&Pem@msNPs (Fig. S2A), indicating that PD-L1 surface modification did not materially compromise drug loading. Anti-PD-L1 surface absorption quantified by BCA assay (Fig. S2B) showed a conjugation efficiency of 38.64%, corresponding to an antibody density of 19.32 μg/mg nanoparticles. In parallel, zeta-potential analysis revealed a stepwise surface-charge shift after drug loading and PD-L1 decoration (Fig. S1C), and together with the stable hydrodynamic size over time (Fig. S1B), supports the colloidal stability of the formulated nanoparticles.

Furthermore, Bio-TEM imaging revealed clear localization of msNPs beneath the plasma membrane following incubation with A549 lung cancer cells (Fig. 1J), and immunofluorescence confirmed abundant Rhodamine B-labeled msNPs (RhB@msNPs) in the cytoplasm (Fig. 1K), indicating efficient cellular uptake and enabling controlled release of Pem/PD-L1ab. To evaluate whether PD-L1 antibody decoration improves cellular targeting, we performed a time-dependent uptake assay using RhB labeled nanoparticles. Cells were incubated with RhB@msNPs or RhB&PD-L1ab@msNPs and imaged at 1, 3, 6, and 12 h (Fig. S3A). Quantification by ImageJ revealed a consistently higher RhB uptake per cell in the PD-L1ab-modified group compared with the unmodified nanoparticles across the entire time course (Fig. S3B). These data indicate that PD-L1 antibody modification enhances cellular uptake, supporting improved targeting capability toward the target cells.

These observations collectively demonstrate that the msNP-based system integrates favorable physicochemical attributes with magnetic hyperthermia capability and controlled drug release, which may collaboratively enhance therapeutic efficacy in cancer treatment [14]. The superparamagnetic nature of msNPs allows them to generate localized heat under AMF without residual magnetization, minimizing risks of particle aggregation in vivo [15]. Furthermore, the observed AMF-triggered Pem release profile reported in this study corroborates previous findings that magnetic hyperthermia can accelerate drug diffusion from mesoporous matrices by increasing molecular mobility and potentially enhancing tumor penetration [16]. Also, under physiological conditions, the positive zeta potential of msNPs may promote initial electrostatic interaction with negatively charged cancer cell membranes, facilitating uptake, whereas PD-L1ab functionalization could further mediate targeted binding to PD-L1-expressing tumor cells, improving selectivity [17,18]. The robust thermal cycling performance observed here suggests that msNPs can withstand repeated hyperthermia sessions without loss of functionality, an important consideration for multi-session cancer therapy regimens [19]. Under magnetic hyperthermia the intratumoral temperature can reach ∼46 °C. While such temperatures are within the therapeutic hyperthermia range and are sufficient to enhance drug release and tumor penetration, they are below the typical irreversible denaturation temperatures reported for many full-length IgG antibodies [20]. Consequently, PD-L1ab immobilized on the msNPs surface are not expected to undergo rapid inactivation during short AMF exposures.

In vitro combined thermotherapy and chemotherapy property

The cytotoxic effects of msNP-based formulations were assessed both in A549 and LLC lung cancer cells under AMF ON and OFF conditions. Flow cytometry analysis of Annexin V staining revealed a significantly higher percentage of apoptotic cells in groups treated with msNPs, Pem@msNPs, and PD-L1&Pem@msNPs compared to the control group (Fig. 2A). Statistical analysis confirmed a marked increase in apoptosis in both A549 and LLC cells when treated with Pem@msNPs or PD-L1&Pem@msNPs with AMF ON (P < 0.01, Fig. 2B). No statistically significant difference was observed between these two groups (P > 0.05, Fig. 2B). To further validate anti-proliferative activity, we performed CCK-8 assays in A549 and LLC cells treated with Ctl, msNPs, Pem@msNPs, or PD-L1ab&Pem@msNPs. As shown in Fig. S4A, msNPs alone reduced viability to 40.5% (A549) and 34.4% (LLC) versus controls (99.3% and 100%). Pem-loaded nanoparticles produced markedly stronger inhibition, with viability of 21.6%/13.5% (Pem@msNPs) and 23.5%/15.8% (PD-L1ab&Pem@msNPs) in A549/LLC cells, respectively. Collectively, these results provide additional quantitative support for the pronounced anti-proliferative effect of the Pem-loaded nanoplatform in vitro (Fig. S4). Furthermore, transwell assays showed that the invasion and migration capabilities of A549 and LLC cells were significantly reduced following treatment with msNPs, Pem@msNPs, or PD-L1&Pem@msNPs compared to the control group (P < 0.05, Fig. 2C and D). Similarly, no significant difference was detected between the Pem@msNPs and PD-L1&Pem@msNPs groups in suppressing cellular invasion and migration (P > 0.05, Fig. 2D). Additionally, colony formation assays confirmed a substantial decrease in clonogenic potential in all treatment groups compared to the control (P < 0.05, Fig. 2E and F). Although Pem@msNPs exhibited a slightly greater inhibitory effect than PD-L1&Pem@msNPs, the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05, Fig. 2F). These findings indicate that integrating magnetic hyperthermia with chemotherapy produces combined tumoricidal effects, consistent with reports on other multimodal nanoparticle platforms [18,21]. Mechanistically, AMF-triggered localized heating accelerates the release of pemetrexed from the mesoporous shell and increases tumor-cell membrane permeability, thereby promoting intracellular drug accumulation and enhanced cytotoxicity [18].

In vivo combined nanoparticle therapy in subcutaneous xenograft mouse model

In vivo evaluation of the nanoparticle formulation in C57BL/6 mice bearing subcutaneous xenografts revealed potent cooperative antitumor efficacy. Tumor growth inhibition was evident following the treatment schedule (Fig. 3A), with AMF-triggered therapy leading to marked reductions in tumor volume and weight. Representative photographs of excised tumors show visibly smaller masses in the nanoparticle-treated group versus the control group (Fig. 3B). Tumor volumes were measured and calculated every 4 days. Control mice displayed exponential tumor growth, reaching an average volume of 1853 mm3 by day 32. Treatment with msNPs reduced tumor volume to 916 mm3, while Pem@msNPs further decreased to 459 mm3, and the combination PD-L1&Pem@msNPs demonstrated the most pronounced efficacy, suppressing tumor volume to 66 mm3 (P < 0.05, Fig. 3C). Also, at day 32, mean tumor weights were 2.48 g in controls versus 1.66 g for msNPs, 0.88 g for Pem@msNPs, and 0.54 g for the PD-L1&Pem@msNPs group (P < 0.01, Fig. 3D). Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining of subcutaneous tumors was preformed to test the corroborated antitumor effects (Fig. 3E). The Ctl (Fig. 3Ei) and msNPs (Fig. 3Eii) groups showed no appreciable histologic differences, tumor cells were densely packed and hyperchromatic with sparse vasculature and only minimal necrosis, consistent with an immune-excluded/desert architecture. In contrast, the Pem@msNPs group exhibited scattered foci of coagulative necrosis, fragmentation of tumor nests, interspersed stromal components between tumor cells, and patchy intratumoral hemorrhage (Fig. 3Eiii). The PD-L1ab&Pem@msNPs group displayed the most pronounced changes, with extensive disruption of tumor nests, geographic necrosis (Fig. 3Eiv). Moreover, the PD-L1&Pem@msNPs group exhibited a clear increase in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) on H&E, with dense intratumoral lymphoid aggregates relative to other groups (Fig. 3Eiv). These results showed a stepwise reduction in tumor volume and mass with each successive formulation, in line with H&E evidence of fragmented tumor nests, geographic necrosis, and conspicuous inflammatory infiltrates, consistent with an “inflamed” microenvironment. The reduction suggests that the ‘locate-and-detonate’ design, in which PD-L1ab direct nanoparticles to tumor sites and AMF triggers rapid drug release, yields superior intratumoral drug accumulation and cytotoxicity. Moreover, the antibody-mediated targeting likely minimized off-target distribution, translating into enhanced biosafety and tolerability in vivo. While PD-L1ab guided AMF-triggered release improved intratumoral accumulation and tumor control in our models, tumor heterogeneity and AMF penetration depth represent critical translational challenges that require further optimization. Notably, tumor cell-intrinsic heterogeneity may influence therapeutic responsiveness across different genetic backgrounds and 3D architectures [22,23]. In vivo combined nanoparticle therapy in intrapleural xenograft mouse model

In an orthotopic pleural xenograft model using immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice, intrapleural injection of tumor cells under direct visualization achieved consistent tumor engraftment with minimal surgical trauma (streamlined procedural detail in Fig. 4A). In the control and msNPs groups, the bioluminescence signal (Avg radiance) increased significantly, indicating active tumor growth, while the Pem@msNPs group did not exhibit a marked difference, the PD-L1&Pem@msNPs group displayed a significant reduction in bioluminescence signal intensity (P < 0.01, Fig. 4B and C), confirming its superior ability to suppress lung tumor proliferation. The PD-L1&Pem@msNPs cohort demonstrated a 15-fold reduction in radiance (p < 0.01, 1.31 vs. Ctl 19.78), with intermediate effects in Pem@msNPs (13.56) and msNPs (16.32) groups (Fig. 4D). Finally, the survival analysis of the treated mice was performed (Fig. 4E). The Pem@msNPs and PD-L1&Pem@msNPs treatment groups exhibited prolonged survival, with the PD-L1&Pem@msNPs group achieving a median survival time of 47 days (P < 0.0001, Fig. 4E). This significant extension of lifespan highlights the durable antitumor benefit conferred by the ‘locate-and-detonate’ nanoplatform. To delineate the contribution of PD-L1 blockade alone, we included a free anti-PD-L1 antibody monotherapy control in the orthotopic LLC-Luc model and monitored tumor progression longitudinally by IVIS bioluminescence imaging. Consistent with a limited response to checkpoint blockade in this setting, anti-PD-L1 monotherapy did not significantly reduce tumor bioluminescence compared with baseline, whereas PD-L1&Pem@msNPs markedly suppressed tumor burden over the same observation window (Fig. S6A and B). These results indicate that the improved efficacy of PD-L1&Pem@msNPs cannot be attributed to PD-L1 antibody treatment alone, and support a sensitization/enhancement effect of the chemo-(hyperthermia) nanoplatform [24].

Based on necropsy findings and clinical records, the predominant cause of death across groups was tumor progression, most commonly large pleural effusions leading to respiratory compromise. Importantly, serum biochemistry remained within normal ranges and H&E examination of major organs revealed no treatment-related pathology (Fig. S7), indicating that the extended overall survival observed with PD-L1&Pem@msNPs is attributable to antitumor efficacy rather than reduced systemic toxicity. Serum biochemical analysis showed no significant differences in albumin (ALB), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CREA), total protein (TP), uric acid (UA), and urea (UREA) between groups (Fig. S7A). Additional assessments revealed comparable levels of cystatin C (CysC), direct bilirubin (DBIL), γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT), and total bilirubin (TBIL) across groups (Fig. S7B). Histological analysis via H&E staining showed no discernible pathology in heart, liver, spleen, or lung tissues (Fig. S7C). The favorable safety profile supports the biocompatibility of these nanoparticles and their suitability for repeated in vivo applications. Maintaining normal biochemical parameters and organ integrity is critical for clinical translation, particularly in therapies requiring multiple AMF exposures and systemic nanoparticle distributions.

Immune microenvironment remodeling induced by PD-L1&Pem@msNPs treatments

To investigate how the PD-L1&Pem@msNPs treatments reshape the tumor microenvironment beyond simple cytotoxic effects, we performed transcriptomic profiling of murine transplant tumors across the 4 groups (Fig. 5A and Fig. S9). Unsupervised k-means clustering of differentially expressed immune- and stroma-related genes identified four distinct transcriptional modules (Fig. 5A): Cluster 1, enriched for lipid and steroid metabolism; Cluster 2, annotated as a neuro-immune interface and sensation module; Cluster 3, related to cardiac function and systemic regulation; and Cluster 4, comprising genes involved in immune-inflammatory responses. Functional enrichment analysis further clarified the biological roles of these clusters. KEGG analysis showed that Cluster 2 was significantly enriched for “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” and “IL-17 signaling pathway” pointing to enhanced cytokine-driven communication and IL-17–associated inflammatory signaling within the neuro-immune interface module (Fig. 5B). Cluster 4 was enriched for canonical immune-effector pathways, including “chemokine signaling pathway,” “complement and coagulation cascades,” “T cell receptor signaling pathway” and “natural killer cell mediated cytotoxicity” (Fig. 5B). These pathways collectively support a microenvironment with heightened chemokine-driven leukocyte recruitment, complement activation, and T/NK cell effector engagement, consistent with a more inflamed, immune-active tumor niche. In line with these annotations, module-score analysis demonstrated that Cluster 2 and Cluster 4 scores were markedly higher in the PD-L1&Pem@msNPs group than in the Pem@msNPs group, whereas cardiac/systemic (Cluster 3) modules showed minimal changes (Fig. 5C). Thus, PD-L1&Pem@msNPs preferentially activate transcriptional programs linked to neuro-immune signaling and immune-inflammatory responses.

Furthermore, functional enrichment analyses provided a detailed characterization of molecular alterations. GO annotation analysis revealed significant activation of immune and inflammatory responses in PD-L1&Pem@msNPs-treated group (Fig. S9A). Activation of such programs suggests enhanced antigen presentation and leukocyte recruitment, thereby linking local tumor injury to systemic immune engagement [25,26]. KEGG pathway analysis identified notable upregulation in pathways critical to immune modulation, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, NF-κB signaling, hematopoietic cell lineage, Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, Th17 cell differentiation, chemokine signaling, and T cell receptor signaling (Fig. S9B). The concomitant rise in cytokine and chemokine pathways together with NF-κB signatures implies a microenvironment that favors APC maturation and T cell trafficking. In practical terms, this would increase the chance that tumor neoantigens released by chemotherapy and thermotherapy are captured and cross-presented to effector T cells [[27], [28], [29]].

GSEA provided additional functional resolution on specific immune cell-related signatures. Comparing PD-L1&Pem@msNPs with Pem@msNPs, we observed significant positive enrichment of gene sets such as “WT vs Pparg knockout macrophage up”, “NKT cell vs αα CD8 T cell up” and “germ-free vs SPF mouse CD4 T cell down” (Fig. 5D). The macrophage-related signatures indicate a shift in macrophage activation and metabolic programming in PD-L1&Pem@msNPs-treated tumors, toward transcriptional states that favor antigen processing, cytokine secretion and antigen-presenting capacity [30,31]. In extended GSEA analyses, additional immune pathways were differentially enriched between PD-L1&Pem@msNPs and other treatment groups (Fig. S9C and D), including the downregulation of lupus CD4+ T cell-biased signatures relative to lupus myeloid cell programs and the upregulation of gene sets representing WT versus MyD88-deficient macrophages. Given that the MyD88/NF-κB axis is a central innate sensing pathway, its activation pattern here suggests that nanoparticle-induced tumor stress may stimulate antigen-presenting cells via MyD88-dependent signals, thereby facilitating more effective priming of adaptive immune responses [28,32]. Together, these macrophage- and CD4+ T cell-associated gene sets point to coordinated activation of innate and helper T-cell circuits rather than isolated changes in a single compartment.

Changes in memory CD8+ T cell states were also evident, with GSEA revealing preferential enrichment of secondary memory CD8+ T cell signatures over quaternary memory T cell-like programs in the PD-L1&Pem@msNPs group (Fig. 5D). A bias toward secondary memory CD8+ T cells has important functional implications: these populations typically display more robust recall capacity, rapid effector re-expansion and superior long-term tumor surveillance compared with terminally differentiated or deeply exhausted counterparts [33,34]. In the context of PD-L1 blockade, such a memory-skewed CD8+ repertoire is likely to support sustained checkpoint responsiveness rather than a transient burst of short-lived effector activity. Collectively, the macrophage, NKT, CD4+ and memory CD8+ T cell-related GSEA signatures reinforce a model in which PD-L1&Pem@msNPs promote a coordinated reprogramming of both innate and adaptive immunity-steering the tumor microenvironment toward a more inflamed, antigen-presenting and memory-oriented state that is favorable for durable antitumor control.

Also, to complement cytotoxicity assays with immune-relevant characterization, we measured surface PD-L1 on LLC cells by flow cytometry. PD-L1 positivity increased from 8.58% in Ctl to 17.68% in msNPs and 30.68% in Pem@msNPs, while the PD-L1ab-decorated formulation showed 15.5% positivity, potentially underestimated due to antibody occupancy during staining (Fig. S11A). While our findings demonstrate enhanced antitumor efficacy of the triple-modality nanoplatform compared with selected controls, the lack of exhaustive single-/dual-agent comparisons and formal synergy analyses means that our results should be interpreted as evidence of combination efficacy rather than proven synergy. To further assess whether these immune-related changes observed at the single-cell level were reflected at the whole-sample protein level, we next performed bulk proteomic analyses.

To complement the single-cell analyses, we performed whole-sample proteomic profiling to validate protein-level changes between the Pem@msNPs and PD-L1&Pem@msNPs groups. Differentially expressed proteins were identified and visualized using a volcano plot, with immune-related proteins, including PD-L1, highlighted (Fig. S14A). Integration of transcriptomic and proteomic datasets across overlapping genes (n = 6373) revealed a near-zero global Spearman correlation between mRNA and protein log2 fold changes (ρ = −0.040, p = 0.00136), indicating limited genome-wide concordance. This observation is consistent with previous reports showing that mRNA abundance is often a poor predictor of protein-level changes in cancer and immune-related contexts due to extensive post-transcriptional and post-translational regulation [[35], [36], [37]]. Directional concordance analysis showed that approximately 42% of genes exhibited consistent mRNA-protein regulation, as illustrated by a concordance scatter plot (Fig. S14B). Protein-based immune biological process enrichment analysis further supported immune-associated alterations at the proteome level (Fig. S14C). Together, these results support the notion that immune modulation induced by the nanoplatform is detectable at the functional protein level, while also underscoring the necessity of direct proteomic measurements rather than relying solely on transcriptomic inference.

While our findings demonstrate enhanced antitumor efficacy of the triple-modality nanoplatform compared with selected controls, the absence of exhaustive single- or dual-agent comparisons and formal synergy modeling precludes definitive conclusions regarding pharmacological synergy. Therefore, the present data should be interpreted as evidence of combination efficacy rather than proven synergistic interaction.

Immune cell composition changes induced by PD-L1&Pem@msNPs treatment

We utilized the CIBERSORT algorithm to determine the immune cell composition from the bulk transcriptome data of each sample. This analysis revealed that treatment with PD-L1&Pem@msNPs induced pronounced and coordinated shifts within the tumor immune compartment. In the PD-L1&Pem@msNPs group, activated mast cells were reduced to 0%, accompanied by a significant increase in resting mast cells compared to other groups (Fig. 6A), suggesting a shift toward a less inflammatory and suppressive TME [38,39]. The observed reduction of activated mast cell signatures here is consistent with a move away from a chronically inflammatory, pro-tumor milieu [38,39]. Also, a decrease in M1 macrophage percentages was observed in this group (Fig. 6A). The observed reduction in classically pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages following therapy may seem counterintuitive, particularly when overall antitumor activity remains high [40]. However, our transcriptional evidence might suggest that it is not a loss of function or a simple conversion to an M2-like state. Instead, it reflects a dynamic metabolic rewiring and phenotypic switching within the macrophage population. This reprogramming gives rise to macrophage subsets whose gene expression profiles no longer match the canonical M1 signature [41]. Crucially, these metabolic shifts enhance their capacity for antigen processing and cross-presentation, allowing them to sustain or even boost a potent anti-tumor immune response despite the down-regulation of classical M1 markers [42]. Because the macrophage states inferred in Fig. 6A may not fully capture intermediate phenotypes, we profiled tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells using TIMCs. Flow cytometry of F4/80+ cells revealed treatment-associated shifts among M1-like (CD86 + CD206-), M2-like (CD86−CD206+), and mixed/transition (CD86 + CD206+) subsets, with a prominent enrichment of the mixed population in the PD-L1&Pem@msNPs group (Fig. S12A and B). T cell compartment analysis demonstrated a clear expansion of total T cells in the PD-L1&Pem@msNPs group, whereas proportions of naive B cells, memory B cells, CD4+ memory (resting) T cells, Tregs, and activated NK cells were reduced (Fig. 6B). Immunofluorescence performed on tumor sections confirmed a significant increase in CD3ε+ T cell infiltration in PD-L1&Pem@msNPs-treated tumors relative to other groups (Fig. 6C and D), supporting the flow-based composition data and indicating enhanced lymphocyte recruitment to the tumor bed. This concordance between bulk composition changes and spatial IF suggests that PD-L1&Pem@msNPs not only expand T cell numbers systemically but also promote their physical entry into tumor parenchyma, a key step for effective cytotoxic responses. To probe T cell functional states, we adopted the CD69/Slamf6 exhaustion framework described by Wherry and colleagues [43]. CD69 and Slamf6 were utilized as reliable markers of T cell exhaustion. Specifically, in the PD-L1&Pem@msNPs group, from a Tex-int phenotype (Slamf6-/CD69-; transitional exhaustion state with partial loss of function) to a Tex-prog phenotype (Slamf6+/CD69-; progenitor-exhausted cells retaining proliferative and recovery capacity), indicative of progressive reversal of exhaustion (Fig. 6E). In contrast, the Pem@msNPs-treated group maintained predominantly in the Tex-int phenotype, with a slight shift toward the Tex-term phenotype (Slamf6-/CD69+; terminally exhausted cells refractory to immunotherapy), suggesting no significant recovery from exhaustion (Fig. 6E). Consistent with these phenotypic shifts, we further quantified the Slamf6/CD69-defined subsets across multiple time points (D12, D16, D20, and D32). As shown in Fig. 6F, the frequency of Slamf6-/CD69- (Tex-int) T cells progressively decreased in the PD-L1&Pem@msNPs group and was significantly lower than that in the Pem@msNPs group. Conversely, a concomitant increase in Slamf6+/CD69- (Tex-prog) T cells in the PD-L1&Pem@msNPs group compared with Pem@msNPs, quantitatively supporting the time-dependent emergence of a progenitor-exhausted population and the reversal trajectory observed in the representative plots (Fig. 6G). The emergence of a Slamf6+ progenitor-exhausted population is highly relevant therapeutically because these cells can proliferate and respond to checkpoint blockade, providing a cellular substrate for sustained antitumor immunity when combined with PD-1/PD-L1 axis interruption [44]. In a T cell-LLC co-culture system, ELISA of supernatants showed increased IFN-γ and TNF-α secretion under the indicated treatment conditions, supporting enhanced T-cell effector activation (Fig. S11B).

To strengthen exhaustion-state annotation beyond Slamf6/CD69 phenotyping, we incorporated the canonical exhaustion-associated markers PD-1, TOX, and TCF1 into the flow cytometry workflow and quantified their distribution within the four Slamf6 × CD69 CD8 T-cell compartments (Fig. S13). PD-1 expression showed a highly polarized pattern, with marked enrichment in the Slamf6-/CD69+ subset (61.13% PD-1+), whereas PD-1 positivity remained low in Slamf6-/CD69- (2.38%), Slamf6+/CD69- (0.47%), and Slamf6+/CD69+ (4.25%) subsets (Fig. S11A). We next profiled TCF1 and TOX to further contextualize exhaustion-associated transcriptional programs across these subsets (Fig. S11B). The Slamf6-/CD69-compartment was TCF1-/TOX- (98.95%), consistent with a largely non-exhausted or intermediate-like phenotype. In contrast, the CD69+ compartments exhibited increased TOX positivity, with TOX + fractions of 38.82% in Slamf6-/CD69+ (TOX+) and 60% in Slamf6+/CD69+ subset. Notably, TCF1+ cells were retained within Slamf6+ subsets (TCF1+ fractions 25.34% in Slamf6+/CD69-and 25% in Slamf6+/CD69+), supporting the presence of a progenitor-like/stem-like population within the exhaustion-associated population. Together, the PD-1, TCF1 and TOX profiles support that the Slamf6-/CD69+ subset is enriched for a PD-1+/TOX + Tex term phenotype, whereas Slamf6+ subsets retain a TCF1+ fraction, suggesting a more progenitor-like phenotype.

Taken together, these results indicate that PD-L1&Pem@msNPs induce a coordinated remodeling of the tumor niche, with markedly increased intratumoral T cell infiltration, evidence of reversal of T cell exhaustion as shown by a shift toward progenitor-like phenotypes, and a reorganization of myeloid and lymphoid compartments consistent with enhanced antigen presentation and immune activation. The concomitant reductions in B cell subsets, Tregs, and certain NK cell fractions may reflect redistribution within lymphoid pools, selective depletion of less functional populations, or a transition toward more antigen-specific and functionally active lymphocytes; clarification of these mechanisms will require detailed single-cell phenotyping and functional verification.

Conclusion

In conclusion, PD-L1&Pem@msNPs represent an innovative multimodal therapeutic platform for lung cancer that combine thermotherapy, chemotherapy, and PD-L1 blockade to overcome immunotherapy resistance. By integrating localized hyperthermia-enhanced drug delivery, immunogenic cell death, and TME reprogramming, this strategy effectively remodels the immunosuppressive TME, reverses T cell exhaustion, and converts immunologically ‘cold’ tumors into therapy-responsive ‘hot’ lesions. The ability of the platform to combine existing and emerging therapies underscores its potential as a cornerstone for advancing combinatorial cancer immunotherapy.
