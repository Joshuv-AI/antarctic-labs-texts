Dental implant complications on YouTube™: an evaluation of video informativeness
Authors: Mahide Büşra Başkan, Meltem Koray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental implant therapy is a highly predictable and widely utilized modality for the rehabilitation of partial and complete edentulism, spanning various configurations including single crowns, fixed dental prostheses, and overdentures. However, diverse surgical, biological, and mechanical complications can occur across all rehabilitation types, potentially compromising clinical outcomes and patient satisfaction. YouTube™ is a widely used source of health-related information due to its accessibility, but the lack of peer review raises concerns regarding the accuracy and completeness of its content. Although previous studies have evaluated YouTube™ videos related to dental implants, those specifically focusing on implant-related complications remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to critically evaluate the informativeness, quality, and demographic characteristics of YouTube™ videos related to dental implant complications and to assess the information they provide to patients and dental professionals.MethodsThis study evaluated the informativeness of YouTube™ videos on dental implant complications using a structured assessment framework derived from Newman and Carranza’s Clinical Periodontology and Implantology. Using the keyword “dental implant complications” the first 300 videos were screened, and 139 eligible videos were included. Videos were assessed based on the presence of key complication-related topics defined in the reference textbook. In addition, video duration, number of likes, interaction rate, and audiovisual quality were analyzed.ResultsMost videos were classified as having a “moderate” level of information, with only 15.1% rated as “very good.” Significant differences were observed in video duration, number of likes, interaction rates, and visual quality across different information levels. Video duration also varied significantly according to the upload source, while audiovisual quality differed based on publication year (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe limited informativeness of YouTube™ videos on dental implant complications may contribute to inadequate patient understanding and unrealistic expectations. These findings highlight the need to improve the quality of online patient education and emphasize the role of dental professionals in providing accurate and comprehensive digital health information.Keywords: Dental implants, Health education, Health information, Social media, Surgical complications

Introduction

Dental implant therapy is a widely accepted and predictable treatment modality for rehabilitating partial and complete edentulism, encompassing a broad range of restorations, from single crowns and fixed dental prostheses to implant-supported overdentures [1]. Despite its high success rates and increasing popularity, complications associated with dental implants are a significant clinical concern [2, 3]. These surgical, biological, prosthetic, and mechanical complications can compromise functional outcomes and patient satisfaction, often necessitating additional procedures or even implant removal [4–6].

A comprehensive understanding of the etiology, risk factors, and management strategies of implant-related complications is essential for ensuring long-term treatment success [3]. Moreover, the early identification of such complications and the implementation of appropriate clinical interventions are critical, particularly in the face of growing patient expectations and the expanding demand for implant-based rehabilitation [4, 5].

While most complications are relatively rare and manageable, certain cases have been reported with severe, even life-threatening outcomes. For instance, Özmarasalı et al. report a case of implant migration into the anterior cranial fossa, requiring emergency neurosurgical intervention [7]. Cascio et al. describe migration into the ethmoid sinus [8], and Barragán-Reyes et al. present a rare case series in which a screwdriver used during implant placement was aspirated into the bronchial tree, necessitating bronchoscopic removal [9]. These cases underscore the need for meticulous surgical planning, anatomical awareness, and preparedness for unexpected complications that may extend beyond the oral cavity.

Such dramatic cases occasionally attract media attention, raising public awareness not only of the benefits of implant procedures but also of their associated complications. As a result, patients increasingly seek further information from online sources. In this context, the quality, accuracy, and educational value of online health information play a pivotal role in shaping patient perceptions and influencing their clinical decisions [10, 11].

The advent of digital health technologies has expanded public access to medical information and facilitated communication between patients and healthcare providers. However, the effective and ethical use of these resources depends on clear regulatory frameworks and scientific validation [12, 13]. According to surveys such as the Health Information National Trends Survey (HINTS), the majority of internet users now rely on the web to obtain health information, with usage patterns varying by age, education level, and health status [11, 14].

YouTube™ (Google® LLC, San Bruno, California), a free video-sharing platform launched in 2005, is currently the most visited website worldwide after Google® [15, 16]. Its large user base and ease of content publication make it a powerful resource for disseminating health-related information [17]. Numerous studies explore YouTube™’s role in patient education, treatment adherence, and healthcare decision-making [18]. Nevertheless, the absence of peer-review mechanisms allows the proliferation of misleading or scientifically inaccurate content [17]. This is particularly concerning in complex domains such as dental implant complications, where misinformation can significantly affect patient understanding and expectations.

Previous investigations have evaluated the overall quality of YouTube™ videos concerning dental implants, peri-implant diseases, and implant failure [19–23]. A comprehensive literature search using the keywords “dental implants,” “complications,” and “YouTube” was conducted across PubMed/MEDLINE, Google Scholar, Web of Science, and Scopus. No previous study specifically evaluating the broad spectrum of dental implant complications on YouTube™ was identified. Therefore, this study addresses an underexplored area within the current literature. Given the clinical importance of this subject and the increasing reliance on online information by patients, a critical assessment of the available video content is warranted. The objective of this study is to evaluate the informativeness and quality of YouTube™ videos regarding dental implant complications.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and informed consent

As this study involved publicly available YouTube™ content and did not include human participants or identifiable personal information, ethical approval was not required, in accordance with institutional and journal guidelines.

Screening of YouTube™ videos

A search was performed on the YouTube™ platform on September 25, 2024, using the keyword “dental implant complications”. The use of a single keyword was intended to reflect typical user search behavior and to ensure consistency in the search strategy, as multiple keywords may introduce variability in the retrieved content. Browser history and cache were cleared, and the search was conducted in incognito mode without logging into any user account. Videos were sorted by relevance, with no additional filters applied.

The URLs and titles of the first 300 videos were recorded. All videos were screened by a single researcher (MBB) under standardized conditions between September 26 and 30, 2024, between 10:00 and 17:00 each day.

Selection and analysis of YouTube™ videos

Among the initial 300 videos, those with durations shorter than 1 min (n = 47) or longer than 15 min (n = 51) were excluded. This 1-to-15-minute filtration framework was applied to eliminate very short videos that may not provide sufficient time to present detailed information regarding complex implant complications (< 1 min), as well as extended video formats that may differ substantially from standard patient-oriented educational content (> 15 min). Utilizing these specific duration boundaries is consistent with similar screening thresholds in recent dental social media research [24, 25], ensuring a balanced and homogenous video cohort for analysis. From the remaining 202 videos, the following were further excluded: non-English videos (n = 11), those with no or unintelligible audio (n = 16), advertisements or irrelevant content (n = 16), academic lectures, courses, or conference recordings (n = 16), and duplicates (n = 4). Ultimately, 139 videos met the inclusion criteria and were subjected to analysis (Fig. 1).

Scoring system for informativeness

The informativeness of the videos was evaluated using a structured scoring framework derived from the classification of implant-related complications presented in Newman and Carranza’s Clinical Periodontology and Implantology [3]. Ten key complication-related topics were identified based on this reference. Each video was assessed for the presence of these predefined topics, with 1 point assigned if a topic was explicitly addressed and 0 if it was not mentioned (Table 2). The total score for each video ranged from 0 to 10. Based on the total scores, videos were categorized into five levels of informativeness: very poor (0–2), poor (3–4), moderate (5–6), good (7–8), and very good (9–10).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported for continuous variables, while categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. As continuous variables did not meet the assumption of normality, non-parametric tests were applied. The Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney U tests were used for group comparisons, and Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction was employed for post-hoc analyses. Associations between categorical variables and knowledge levels were assessed using the Chi-square test. Additionally, to minimize period effects and facilitate chronological comparison, publication years were grouped into four categories and recoded as follows: 2008–2016 (Code 1), 2017–2019 (Code 2), 2020–2022 (Code 3), and 2022–2024 (Code 4). The associations of these chronological codes with visual quality, audio quality, upload source, target audience, and video type were analyzed using the Chi-square test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 139 videos were included in the study following a YouTube™ search using the keyword “dental implant complications”. For researcher calibration, 21 randomly selected videos constituting 15% of the total sample size were re-evaluated after 3 weeks. As a result of the Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test analysis, no significant difference was found between the evaluations (p > 0.05).

Frequency and percentage distribution of videos according to criteria

The analysis of video distribution based on various evaluation criteria revealed that the proportion of videos meeting the third (highest) criterion, which is the highest score in the visual quality and audio quality categories, is 71.9% and 60.4%, respectively. When evaluated in terms of upload source, it was found that 59.7% of the videos are uploaded within the scope of the 1st criterion, namely by healthcare institutions.

In terms of video type, 90.6% of the videos meet the 2nd criterion, and it is understood that these videos mostly convey physician experiences or contain educational information. In the target audience analysis, it was found that 52.5% of the videos are included in the 2nd criterion and are mainly prepared for the patient audience.

When the geographical distribution is examined, it was found that 39.57% of the total 139 videos originate from the United States. When classified in terms of information level, it is seen that 38.8% of the videos provide “moderate” level of information, and no videos were classified as “very poor”.

Descriptive statistics of variables

The descriptive analysis of the videos revealed wide variability in variables such as duration, number of views, likes, and dislikes. The average video duration was 4.54 min, with an average of 90,748 views, 802 likes, and 27.5 dislikes. The number of subscribers showed a wide distribution (mean: 183,329; median: 5,230; range: 2–2,170,000). The view rate reached up to 409,837, and the interaction rate ranged from − 0.03 to 37.50. The number of days since upload varied between 18 and 5,795, while the average number of comments was 120.85.

Post-hoc pairwise comparisons (Bonferroni-adjusted) revealed statistically significant differences only in video duration. Specifically, videos in knowledge levels 2 and 3 were significantly shorter than those in levels 4 and 5 (p < 0.01). No significant pairwise differences were found for number of likes or interaction rate (adjusted p > 0.05).

Cross-tabulation and Chi-square analysis for knowledge level comparisons

The variables of visual quality, audio quality, upload source, video type, and target audience were analyzed using the Chi-square test in relation to knowledge level classifications. A statistically significant association was found between knowledge level and visual quality (χ² = 14.072, df = 6, p = 0.029), suggesting that higher knowledge levels are associated with better visual quality.

In contrast, no statistically significant relationships were observed between knowledge level and audio quality, upload source, video type, or target audience (p > 0.05). Detailed results for the visual quality variable are presented in Table 4.

Distribution of the year of publication, recoding and analysis by year code

In the distribution of videos by year of publication, the highest frequency was observed in 2022, accounting for 15.1% of all videos. In contrast, the lowest frequency occurred in 2008, with only one video uploaded (0.7%). When analyzing the distribution of the videos across the year codes, the largest share of video content was observed during the 2020–2022 period (Code 3), representing 36.7% of the total sample. Statistically significant associations were found between year code and visual quality (χ² = 42.419, df = 6, p < 0.001), as well as year code and audio quality (χ² = 36.681, df = 6, p < 0.001). Visual quality was predominantly rated at level 2 during the 2008–2016 period, whereas it shifted toward level 3 in the period after 2017. A similar trend was observed for audio quality, which showed substantial improvement over time. Notably, 95.2% of the videos published between 2022 and 2024 were rated as level 3 in terms of audio quality. No statistically significant differences were found across year codes for the remaining variables (p > 0.05).

Distribution of variables according to upload source

Videos uploaded by healthcare institutions were significantly shorter in duration compared to those uploaded by dentists (KW = 7.335, p = 0.026; Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.025), while no significant differences were found between other pairs.

Discussion

Since its inception in 2005, YouTube™ has become a cornerstone of digital engagement and currently ranks as the second most visited website globally, following Google [15, 16]. Video content has emerged as a significant tool for accessing information and entertainment, making YouTube™ a subject of research across disciplines such as health sciences, engineering, and the social sciences [26]. Particularly in healthcare, studies have frequently examined the impact of video content on patient education, treatment adherence, and information dissemination [10]. In the fields of dentistry and oral and maxillofacial surgery, content analyses of YouTube™ videos are increasingly common [27–30].

Patients often turn to the internet to learn about dental implant options and complications [20]. Mohamed et al. [31] reported that YouTube™ serves not only as a health information source but also as a decision-making aid. Highlighting this evolving digital shift, Ceylan Şen et al. [32] recently demonstrated that even advanced AI chatbots exhibit significant variations in accuracy and reliability when handling dental implant failure queries. These findings further highlight the importance of evaluating the quality and reliability of online information sources related to dental implant complications. Beyond the inherent risks of surgical procedures, evaluating online content is important because of its potential influence on patient education, public understanding of health information, and clinician–patient communication. Digital platforms like YouTube™ are heavily utilized by the public for post-operative guidance and by clinicians as supplementary educational resources. Inaccurate or low-quality information regarding implant complications may contribute to misunderstanding, unrealistic expectations, and challenges in patient education. Therefore, evaluating the quality and informativeness of such content is important for understanding the information available to patients and healthcare professionals. Earlier investigations focusing on general dental implant videos revealed that complications were addressed in only 15.4% [19] and 7.78% [20] of the reviewed content. Alongside these general evaluations, the current dental literature also includes studies targeting more specific adverse outcomes. For instance, an evaluation of implant failure videos demonstrated that the available online information generally provides poor content quality for patients [21]. Similarly, the quality of videos dedicated exclusively to peri-implantitis has been scrutinized, with researchers concluding that while general quality scores may present acceptable reliability, the overall clinical depth remains limited and highly heterogeneous for patient guidance [22, 23]. Corroborating the variability observed across digital educational resources, Gaikwad et al. [33] reported that although YouTube™ videos on peri-implant diseases and conditions often provide accurate preliminary information, their overall reliability remains uncertain. The authors further noted considerable heterogeneity in content quality, indicating limitations in their usefulness as patient educational resources. Conversely, Bulut et al. [34] reported a more positive outlook, noting that nearly half of the top-ranked peri-implantitis videos serve as valuable resources, with high-content videos attracting greater popularity and quality. However, a literature search reveals that these recent efforts remain confined to isolated disease entities or terminal outcomes like implant loss. In contrast, our work broadens this perspective by contextualizing the entire multi-factorial framework of implant complications. By covering surgical, biological, and prosthetic complications simultaneously, this study contributes to addressing a gap in the current social media literature by evaluating surgical, biological, and prosthetic complications within a single analytical framework.

From the first 300 videos retrieved using the keyword “dental implant complications”, 139 were selected for analysis based on predefined exclusion criteria. Previous studies have shown that most users focus on the first 30 results [35], and similar studies typically analyze around 100–120 videos [36]. The total video duration in our study was 631 min, with the most common video length being under two minutes. These videos collectively accumulated over 12 million views, with substantial engagement in the form of likes and comments, indicating considerable public interest in the topic.

In the present study, YouTube™ Shorts (< 1 min) and videos exceeding 15 min were deliberately excluded from the final analysis. Methodologically, very short video formats may not provide sufficient opportunity to explain the multifactorial surgical, biological, and prosthetic complications associated with implant therapy in adequate detail. Conversely, videos extending beyond 15 min often represent a different educational format, such as extended lectures, webinars, or procedural demonstrations, making direct comparison with standard educational videos more difficult. Utilizing this specific duration filtration window is documented in recent independent dental literature [24, 25], and was adopted to improve comparability across the analyzed videos. Our findings showed that videos categorized as having a “very good” level of information were significantly longer than those with “poor” or “moderate” levels (p = 0.001 and p = 0.025, respectively). This suggests that longer videos tend to contain more comprehensive content, although video length alone is not a definitive indicator of quality. Similar results were reported by Lena et al. [37] and Zincir [38]. However, Ayrancı et al. [30] cautioned that long videos on genioplasty may still lack informative value. Our study supports this view, highlighting that while longer videos often provide more information, this relationship is not absolute.

Video upload sources were also analyzed, and videos uploaded by healthcare institutions were found to be significantly shorter than those uploaded by individual dentists (p = 0.025). This difference may reflect individual practitioners’ preference for more detailed explanations. Although 59.7% of the videos were uploaded by healthcare institutions, no statistically significant association was found between upload source and information level. This result is consistent with previous findings by Gaş et al. [28] and Erdinç et al. [39] on other dental topics.

Due to the use of an English-language search term, 39.57% of the videos originated from the United States, aligning with similar studies [19, 40] and the country’s global ranking in internet usage [41]. Other countries accounted for 30.94% of the videos, with the United Kingdom contributing 14.39%. Turkey was represented by only one video (0.72%).

In terms of content type, 90.6% of the videos focused on physician experience or educational information. This contrasts with the study by Hegarty et al. [27], which found that 77.5% of orthognathic surgery videos were uploaded by patients and 41.67% were based on patient experiences. While patient-generated content is more expected in procedures with aesthetic concerns, such as orthognathic surgery, our findings suggest that videos on dental implant complications tend to come from professional and educational sources. Among the complications, biological issues were the most frequently addressed (76.3%), while esthetic and phonetic complications were the least mentioned (25.2%). From a clinical standpoint, this uneven distribution can be attributed to the acute and severe nature of biological complications, such as peri-implantitis, progressive bone loss, or implant mobility, which directly jeopardize the long-term survival of the fixture and cause tangible patient discomfort [3, 5]. Because biological failure often requires extensive surgical revision or explantation, content creators—primarily clinicians and healthcare institutions—prioritize educating patients on these high-stakes risks. On the other hand, aesthetic and phonetic complications are largely restricted to the anterior maxilla and are often perceived as subjective or prosthetic-driven concerns rather than absolute surgical failures. These issues may be perceived as less severe than biological complications and therefore receive less attention in online educational content. This explains the lower demand for, and availability of, digital video content dedicated to aesthetic and phonetic implant challenges.

In YouTube™ health-related content, the most critical factor is the accuracy of the information presented. When evaluating specific dental conditions, Atagün et al. [42] reported a more positive trend, indicating that patients can access watchable, reliable, and highly informative video content regarding dentin hypersensitivity on YouTube™. In contrast to the findings of the present study, these results suggest that the quality and educational value of online health information may vary considerably depending on the specific dental topic being evaluated. Previous studies in various dental topics, such as root canal therapy [40], prosthetic care [29], and tooth whitening [43], have reported generally insufficient or moderate information levels. Similarly, our findings showed that the majority of videos were categorized as “moderate” in terms of informativeness, and only 15.1% achieved a “very good” score.

Although the number of likes varied significantly across knowledge levels (KW = 8.739, p = 0.033), this difference lost significance after post-hoc analysis. A similar pattern was observed in interaction rates (KW = 8.010, p = 0.046). These results suggest that while higher-quality videos tend to receive more engagement, the relationship is not consistently statistically significant, supporting findings from previous research [44, 45].

A significant association was found between information level and visual quality (χ² = 14.072, p = 0.029), with higher-quality visuals more common in videos classified as “good” or “very good.” No such relationship was observed with audio quality (χ² = 7.169, p = 0.305), although a slight trend toward improvement was noted. Notably, both visual and audio quality showed statistically significant improvement over time, likely reflecting advances in technology (χ² = 42.419 and χ² = 36.681, respectively; p < 0.001).

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was limited to English-language videos, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Second, the evaluation of video content and the subsequent grading of informativeness levels were conducted by a single primary researcher, which introduces a potential risk of subjectivity and observer bias. Although an intra-examiner calibration protocol was strictly implemented within a designated washout period to safeguard data consistency, the inherent reliance on predefined criteria and individual interpretation remains a limitation that should be considered when generalizing these findings. Third, YouTube™ utilizes a dynamic and proprietary search algorithm, meaning that search outputs and video ranking positions are constantly fluctuating based on temporal factors, user search histories, and geographic regions. Consequently, the 139 videos analyzed in this cross-sectional study reflect a specific snapshot in time (September 2024). Although strict protocols were implemented to maximize reproducibility—such as clearing the browser cache, utilizing incognito mode, and avoiding user login—the dynamic nature of the platform implies that replicating this search on a different date or from a separate geographical jurisdiction may yield non-identical video selections or altered interaction metrics. This structural volatility is an inherent limitation of social media content analyses. Finally, some subgroup analyses may be affected by relatively small sample sizes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, most YouTube™ videos addressing dental implant complications offer only a moderate level of informativeness. Higher informativeness was generally associated with longer video duration and better visual quality, whereas likes and interaction rates did not consistently reflect content quality. Biological complications were discussed more frequently than aesthetic or phonetic complications. These findings highlight the need for more comprehensive and reliable educational content on dental implant complications and support the involvement of dental professionals and academic institutions in providing high-quality digital health information.
