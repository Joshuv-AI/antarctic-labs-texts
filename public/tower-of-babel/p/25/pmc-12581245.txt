Introduction

Globally, stroke represents a major public health challenge, ranking as the second leading cause of mortality and a primary contributor to long-term disability [1]. Recent epidemiological data reveal the substantial impact of stroke, with approximately 7.25 million deaths and 160.46 million DALYs attributed to this condition in 2021 alone. The economic burden is equally staggering, with annual direct and indirect costs of stroke treatment estimated at $73.7 billion [2]. Prognostic care for stroke accounts for approximately 34% of total global healthcare expenditure, highlighting the significant financial implications of this disease [3]. In China, the situation is particularly severe, as stroke has emerged as the leading cause of national mortality [4]. The year 2019 witnessed approximately 3.94 million new stroke cases in China, underscoring the urgent need for effective prevention and management strategies [5].

The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified key modifiable risk factors for stroke prevention, including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, smoking, physical inactivity, unhealthy dietary patterns, and abdominal obesity [6]. Among these, five major risk factors-hypertension, smoking, abdominal obesity, poor diet, and physical inactivity-collectively account for over 80% of the global stroke risk. Hypertension, particularly, stands out as the most significant treatable risk factor for stroke [6, 7]. Clinically, hypertension manifests in three distinct patterns: isolated systolic hypertension (elevated SBP), isolated diastolic hypertension (elevated DBP), and combined systolic-diastolic hypertension [8]. Current WHO estimates indicate that approximately 1.3 billion individuals worldwide are affected by hypertension, with 1 billion cases specifically attributed to elevated systolic blood pressure [9]. This prevalence continues to escalate, driven by demographic aging and evolving lifestyle patterns. Despite significant advancements in hypertension diagnosis and management, global control rates remain suboptimal, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [10, 11]. Elevated systolic blood pressure exerts the most pronounced impact on adverse cardiovascular outcomes.

Previous studies revealed that HSBP accounted for 56.8% of all stroke-related DALYs in 2021, with its relative contribution rate had significantly increased compared to 1990 [12]. However, there is a paucity of research that delineates the temporal trends and subnational heterogeneity of this attributable burden, particularly in relation to socioeconomic development levels, although the association between HSBP and the risk of stroke is well known, and the global trend in hypertension management has also been described [13, 14]. Our study builds upon this groundwork by providing updated estimates through 2021 and a detailed analysis of the spatiotemporal disparities in HSBP-related stroke burden across SDI regions. We further integrate economic and geopolitical contexts to highlight inequalities, offering a more precise understanding for resource allocation and policy formulation.

Methods

Study design

This is a secondary longitudinal analysis of the disease burden of stroke caused by HSBP in different regions of the world from 1990 to 2021. The data used all come from the GBD 2021.

Data sources

The data for this study were obtained from the GBD 2021 database, published by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) at the University of Washington. This comprehensive database evaluates the burden of disease and health loss for 369 diseases, injuries, and 87 risk factors across 204 countries and territories. The dataset includes age-standardized estimates of the attributable burden of various risk factors, enabling cross-country comparisons [15]. Socio-demographic data were primarily derived from the Socio-demographic Index (SDI), a composite measure that includes total fertility rate under the age of 25 (TFU25), mean education for individuals aged 15 and older (EDU15+), and lag-distributed income (LDI) per capita [16]. Based on SDI values, the GBD categorizes countries into five regions: low SDI, low-middle SDI, middle SDI, high-middle SDI, and high SDI [15, 16]. Therefore, our analysis of regional disease burden encompassed the five SDI regions as well as the United States and China—the world’s two largest economies by gross domestic product (GDP).

Evaluation indicators

The study employed several disease burden indicators, including the number of new cases, prevalent cases, deaths, DALYs, and their corresponding 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). Crude rates (incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALY rates) were also calculated. To account for variations in population age structures across regions, age-standardized rates were used. These rates were computed by multiplying disease burden indicators for each age group by the corresponding age-specific weights from the GBD standard population and summing the results across all age groups [17–19]. DALYs, a key metric in this study, quantify the combined impact of years lived with disability (YLDs) and years of life lost (YLLs) due to premature death, expressed as DALYs = YLDs + YLLs [20].

In addition to HSBP, other risk factors exceeding the theoretical minimum risk exposure level (TMREL) were included to assess their contribution to stroke burden [21]. The GBD methodology employs a comparative risk assessment framework, which organizes risk factors into hierarchical, potentially combinable causal networks. For each risk factor, the TMREL represents the exposure level associated with the lowest possible disease burden, serving as a benchmark for risk minimization [22]. Subgroup analyses were conducted by gender and age. Given the disproportionately high burden of stroke among older populations, the analysis focused on individuals aged 40 years and older. Age subgroups were defined as 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, 85–89, 90–94, and 95 + years, consistent with the GBD subgrouping methodology.

Statistical analysis

Data organization and preliminary calculations, including the rate of change in stroke burden indicators, were performed using Microsoft Office Excel 2021. The rate of change was calculated as:

rate of change = (rate in 2021- rate in 1990)/rate in 1990 × 100%.

Trends in age-standardized disease burden rates were analyzed using Joinpoint Regression Software (Version 5.1.0.0, National Cancer Institute). This model identifies inflection points in temporal trends through Monte Carlo permutation tests and calculates the annual percentage change (APC) and average annual percentage change (AAPC) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). APC quantifies the change during a specific time interval. AAPC represents the overall change across the entire time period. An APC < 0 indicates a year-over-year decrease, while an APC > 0 indicates a year-over-year increase. An APC value equal to the AAPC (APC = AAPC) suggests the absence of significant turning points in the trend. Similarly, an AAPC < 0 signifies an overall decreasing trend, whereas an AAPC > 0 indicates an overall increasing trend. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 [23]. Data visualization was performed using R 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Key R packages included ggmap, rgdal, ggplot2, maps, dplyr, and sp for mapping and graphical representation of results.

Results

Trends in stroke disease burden globally and across SDI regions, 1990–2021

In 1990, the global burden of stroke was substantial, with 7,019,100 new cases (95%UI: 634.56–779.42), 50,415,600 prevalent cases (95%UI: 4,779.35–5,322.72), 5,033,600 deaths (95%UI: 4,712,471–5,291.61), and 121,405,500 DALYs (95%UI: 11,472.22–12,762.51). By 2021, these figures had risen to 11,946,300 new cases (95%UI: 1,077.21–1,321.98), 93,816,300 prevalent cases (95%UI: 89,030.02–99,335.55), 7,252,700 deaths (95%UI: 656.69–780.82), and 160,457,200 DALYs (95%UI: 14,778.14–17,164.26).

Overall, from 1990 to 2021, global age-standardized mortality and DALY rates for stroke exhibited a consistent downward trend. In contrast, the age-standardized incidence rate initially decreased, then increased, and subsequently declined again, while the prevalence rate decreased before showing a gradual upward trend. Overall, the age-standardized incidence rate (AAPC = −0.81, 95%CI: −0.86 to −0.75), mortality rate (AAPC = −1.60, 95%CI: −1.77 to −1.43), prevalence rate (AAPC = −0.29, 95% CI: −0.30 to −0.28), and DALY rate (AAPC = −1.57, 95%CI: −1.67 to −1.47) demonstrated significant declines. When calculating APC between consecutive years, from 2005 to 2014, the age-standardized incidence rate of stroke declined relatively rapidly. However, between 2014 and 2019, it showed an opposite upward trend. The age-standardized prevalence of stroke declined most rapidly during 1999–2009 but increased during 2019–2021. The fastest rate of decline in both the age-standardized mortality rate and the age-standardized DALY rate for stroke occurred during 2004–2007, respectively (Fig. 1).

Across SDI regions, age-standardized incidence, mortality, and DALY rates for stroke also decreased from 1990 to 2021 (Fig. 2). High SDI regions consistently exhibited the lowest rates, significantly below the global average. Globally, the age-standardized incidence rate declined by 21.78%, with the most pronounced reduction observed in high SDI regions (37.27% decrease from 143.79/100,000 in 1990 to 90.20/100,000 in 2021). In contrast, the slowest decline occurred in middle SDI regions (14.17% decrease). Similarly, the global age-standardized mortality rate decreased by 39.40%, with high SDI regions experiencing the largest decline (59.43%) and low-middle SDI regions the smallest (23.58%). The age-standardized DALY rate declined by 38.74% globally, with high SDI regions again showing the most significant reduction (54.52%) and low-middle SDI regions the least (26.44%).

Age-standardized prevalence trends varied slightly by region. Globally, as well as in low, low-middle, and high-middle SDI countries, prevalence rates initially declined before showing a slight increase in later years. In high SDI countries, the trend was more variable, with a wave-like pattern but an overall decline. Over the past three decades, the global age-standardized prevalence rate decreased by 8.48%, with the fastest decline in low SDI regions (13.65%) and the slowest in middle SDI regions (1.99%). In the United States, prevalence rates peaked in 2002 before declining, resulting in an overall decrease of 1.66%. Conversely, China experienced a continuous increase in prevalence rates since 1990, rising by 11.48%.

Global and regional stroke burden attributable to risk factors by SDI

In 2021, HSBP was the leading risk factor for stroke-related DALY rate globally and across all SDI regions (Fig. 3). The burden attributable to HSBP was inversely correlated with SDI levels, with lower SDI regions exhibiting higher DALYs. The age-standardized DALY rate for stroke attributable to HSBP in low SDI regions was 1,359.65/100,000 (95%UI: 1,024.88–1,686.56), compared to 380.27/100,000 (95%UI: 280.22–469.94) in high SDI regions. The United States recorded a rate of 335.94/100,000 (95%UI: 241.27–424.52), while China’s rate was significantly higher at 1,484.39/100,000 (95%UI: 1,055.29–1,956.98).

Other major risk factors for stroke included air pollution, tobacco, dietary risks, and high LDL cholesterol. High LDL cholesterol was the second-leading risk factor in high SDI regions, while air pollution ranked second in middle, low-middle, and low SDI regions, as well as globally. In China, air pollution and tobacco were the second and third leading risk factors, respectively, whereas in the United States, high LDL cholesterol and high fasting plasma glucose held these positions.

In 2021, HSBP accounted for approximately 4.21 million stroke deaths (95%UI: 3,175,180) and 91.85 million DALYs (95%UI: 69,070,000–112,220,000), representing 58.08% of total stroke deaths and 57.25% of total DALYs globally. The age-standardized DALY and mortality rates for stroke attributable to HSBP exhibited significant regional disparities, with the highest burden observed in East and Central Asia (Fig. 4).

From 1990 to 2021, age-standardized mortality and DALY rates for stroke attributable to HSBP declined significantly worldwide (Fig. 6). The global age-standardized mortality rate decreased by 37.39%, with high SDI, high-middle SDI countries, and the United States exceeding this decline (64.61%, 45.76%, and 40.22%, respectively). In contrast, low, low-middle, and middle SDI regions, as well as China, experienced smaller reductions (20.13%, 16.96%, 29.80%, and 33.57%, respectively). Similarly, the global age-standardized DALY rate declined by 34.64%, with high SDI, high-middle SDI countries, and the United States again showing greater reductions (60.20%, 43.48%, and 37.47%, respectively).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of global stroke prevalence trends from 1990 to 2021 and examines the complex patterns of stroke burden attributable to HSBP across spatiotemporal, gender, and age groups. While global age-standardized incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALY rates for stroke have shown a declining trend over the past three decades, the absolute burden of stroke has increased significantly. This rise is largely driven by population growth and aging, particularly in low- to upper-middle-income countries, which continue to bear the largest share of the global stroke burden [24].

The distribution of the burden of stroke shows that in regions with a higher SDI, the disease burden is lower, while in regions with a lower SDI, the disease burden is higher. There persists a persistent social and economic disparity in the global burden of stroke diseases [25]. The observed disparities may be attributable to a confluence of multiple interrelated factors, including suboptimal efficacy of existing primary prevention protocols for stroke and cardiovascular diseases, inequitable access to healthcare resources, suboptimal management of modifiable risk factors, and deficiencies in comprehensive secondary prevention frameworks [26–28]. Cross-national analyses reveal heterogeneous patterns in healthcare utilization: high-income nations demonstrate minimal educational gradient in therapeutic interventions, whereas low- and middle-income countries exhibit a significant inverse association between educational attainment and treatment adherence - a phenomenon potentially reflecting the inverse care law in resource-constrained settings [29]. In higher SDI regions, populations typically benefit from superior healthcare infrastructure, health-conscious dietary patterns, and enhanced preventive care initiatives, thereby attenuating the disease burden [30]. Conversely, in low-SDI settings (exemplified by Haiti), stroke burden is aggravated by fragile health systems, resource constraints, and suboptimal management of antihypertensive therapies—factors that collectively compromise prevention efficacy and therapeutic outcomes [31]. Despite advancements in stroke prevention and treatment—such as the establishment of stroke centers and improvements in therapies like intravenous thrombolysis and interventional procedures—the global crude rates of stroke incidence and prevalence have continued to rise slowly. This suggests that while progress has been made, it has been insufficient to counteract the growing burden imposed by population expansion and aging [32]. This trend is particularly evident in China, where rapid aging, inadequate control of risk factors like hypertension and diabetes, and a large population have contributed to a worsening stroke burden [5, 33]. Moreover, extant evidence has documented pronounced urban-rural disparities in stroke burden across China, primarily driven by persistent gaps in socioeconomic development and differential allocation of healthcare resources [34].

The total number of stroke-related DALYs attributable to risk factors increased from 100.11 million in 1990 to 134.97 million in 2021, reflecting a rise of approximately 34.86 million. This increase is not only due to demographic changes but also to the growing impact of key risk factors such as high BMI, ambient particulate pollution, high fasting glucose, HSBP, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, renal dysfunction, and high temperatures [12]. Existing literature confirms that despite a downward trajectory in the global burden of stroke attributable to air pollution through 2021, the magnitude of this burden remains significant. The prevalence of air pollution-associated stroke rises progressively with age and varies considerably across geographic regions [35]. The distribution of stroke risk factors varies by SDI region; for example, high LDL cholesterol is more prominent in high-SDI regions and the United States, likely due to divergent economic development and dietary patterns [36–38]. However, HSBP remains the leading risk factor for stroke globally, accounting for over half of the stroke burden in 2021. Regional disparities in HSBP-related burden are stark, with higher burdens observed in Asia and Africa and lower burdens in Europe, the Americas, and Oceania [15]. Subgroup analyses revealed that the stroke burden attributable to HSBP is higher among men than women, This phenomenon reflects the confluence of both biological and socio-cultural factors. This disparity may be driven by higher exposure to risk factors such as smoking and a greater prevalence of chronic inflammatory vascular diseases like atherosclerosis in men [39, 40]. On the other hand, Estrogen exerts cardioprotective effects by regulating lipid metabolism, enhancing endothelial function, preventing atherosclerosis, and attenuating myocardial ischemia. Collectively, these mechanisms predispose men to stroke [41].

Among age groups, the burden increases significantly with age, peaking in the 90–94 age group for men and the 95 + age group for women. The progression of aging intrinsically amplifies stroke risk through diminished physiological resilience and accrual of chronic comorbidities. Consequently, observed gender and age disparities in health outcomes extend beyond biological mechanisms to encompass key social determinants—including lifestyle behaviors, socioeconomic positioning, and differential healthcare access [42].The slight decline in burden among men over 95 may be attributed to competing risks from other fatal diseases [43]. Elderly patients are often accompanied by multiple comorbidities (such as diabetes and chronic kidney disease), the presence of comorbidities like diabetes and chronic kidney disease further complicates treatment, necessitating a careful balance between efficacy and safety [44, 45].

The global decline in HSBP-related stroke burden (1990–2021) reflects advances in hypertension prevention and management. Updated guidelines from organizations like the American Heart Association (AHA) and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) have emphasized individualized treatment, lifestyle interventions, and lower diagnostic thresholds for hypertension [46, 47]. Enhanced screening methods, including home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) and ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM), have improved diagnostic accuracy [48, 49]. Public health initiatives, such as salt reduction programs and food labeling regulations, have also contributed to better hypertension control [50]. High-income countries have further leveraged health insurance systems and public health programs to improve hypertension treatment and control rates [51]. WHO global target of achieving a 50% hypertension control rate by 2025 has spurred collaborative efforts worldwide [9]. Additionally, advancements in mobile health (mHealth) and telemedicine have facilitated real-time blood pressure monitoring and personalized treatment, making hypertension management more accessible and effective [52–54].

Regional differences in HSBP-related stroke burden underscore the role of socioeconomic factors in shaping health outcomes. High SDI regions, with their emphasis on health education, better healthcare infrastructure, and higher public awareness, have achieved greater success in reducing stroke burden [55, 56]. For example, the implementation of the 2017 ACC/AHA hypertension guidelines in the United States has led to significant improvements in hypertension management, contributing to lower stroke burdens compared to other regions [57, 58]. This guideline expands the hypertensive patient population meeting diagnostic criteria and broadens treatment indications to mitigate major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) incidence [59]. In contrast, low- and middle-SDI regions face challenges such as limited healthcare access, inadequate risk factor control, and insufficient public health resources. These regions also contend with the unique challenges of managing HSBP in aging populations, where “isolated systolic hypertension” is prevalent due to age-related arterial stiffness [60]. Furthermore, distinct geographical and sociocultural contexts engender country-specific stroke burden challenges even within equivalent SDI tiers. China exemplifies this through persistently excessive sodium consumption—a primary modifiable risk factor for hypertension—where population-wide salt reduction constitutes a critical stroke prevention strategy [34]. Conversely, India faces systemic barriers including healthcare access disparities and health literacy gaps, which impede timely stroke management. Targeted enhancement of medical infrastructure in underserved regions represents the most effective approach to mitigate stroke burden attributable to uncontrolled systolic hypertension in the Indian context [61].

This study underscores the imperative for precision public health interventions. Gender-stratified approaches demonstrate differential efficacy: interventions targeting women effectively elevate health literacy and promote behavioral modifications, with observed burden reduction attributable to enhanced risk awareness, healthier lifestyles, and proactive preventive measures [62]. Conversely, mitigating the male stroke burden necessitates intensifying gender-specific health education—particularly among elderly males—through heightened risk perception campaigns and implementation of protective protocols. These include enhanced follow-up systems and routine stroke screening for men with multi-morbidity risk profiles.

Policy formulation must account for regional healthcare infrastructures and sociocultural determinants influencing stroke epidemiology. As evidenced by ACC/AHA guidelines reclassifying 31 million U.S. adults with Stage 1 hypertension, evidence-based risk stratification is critical: pharmacotherapy is now indicated for individuals ≥ 65 years or with established CVD, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or ≥ 10% 10-year CVD risk. For lower-risk cohorts, clinician-delivered behavioral interventions targeting lifestyle modification are recommended [63].

Projections indicate that sustained systolic blood pressure control (< 130 mmHg) could avert >26,000 cerebrovascular events annually [64]. This substantiates the clinical utility of updated diagnostic thresholds in primary stroke prevention, with guideline implementation potentially reducing systolic hypertension-attributable stroke burden.

Limitations

This study acknowledges several methodological constraints. (i) GBD database limitations: Despite sophisticated bias-correction algorithms, inherent constraints in source data quality and completeness persist; (ii) Undercaptured stroke burden in low-to-middle SDI regions—attributable to insufficient screening, diagnostic resource deficits, and healthcare access barriers—may underestimate true disease prevalence and preclude granular geographic analyses (e.g., urban-rural stratification). Future iterations would benefit from higher-resolution spatial data; (iii) Joinpoint regression assumptions: The model’s segmentation-dependent architecture cannot fully accommodate stochastic epidemiological perturbations driven by dynamic demographic shifts, environmental exposures, healthcare system fluctuations, and economic volatility; (iv) Unmeasured confounding: Exclusive focus on hypertension neglects material confounding from genetic predisposition, environmental toxins, psychological stressors, and dietary patterns, potentially compromising causal inference validity; (v) Insufficient research design: The study only relied on the estimated values derived from global disease burden studies for the secondary longitudinal analysis, without conducting validation with actual clinical cohorts. This may lead to inconsistencies between the model and the data. Future research should prioritize: (i) standardized data collection protocols that integrate occupational exposures and healthcare accessibility metrics; (ii) strengthened surveillance in underserved populations; (iii) refined disease subtyping for greater accuracy; (iv) the development of transdisciplinary predictive frameworks incorporating genetic, environmental, and behavioral determinants. These efforts should be ideally supported by external validation cohorts to ensure predictive validity.

Conclusion

The findings underscore the persistent disparities in stroke burden attributable to higher SBP across different regions. While high SDI countries have made significant progress in reducing stroke burden through comprehensive hypertension control programs, our findings highlight the highest burden observed in lower SDI regions. The reasons for this disparity warrant further investigation. Targeted interventions, including improved access to affordable antihypertensive medications, public health education, and lifestyle modifications, are crucial to reducing the global burden of stroke.
