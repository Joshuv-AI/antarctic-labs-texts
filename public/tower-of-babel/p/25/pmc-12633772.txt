Mfn2 Regulates Scleral Remodeling in Myopia by Maintaining Endoplasmic Reticulum Homeostasis in Scleral Fibroblasts
Authors: Yiyan Wang, Shuting Liu, Qiong Huang, Yang Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeEndoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress participates in the development of various disorders by regulating tissue remodeling and apoptosis. This study aimed to explore the regulatory role of mitofusin 2 (Mfn2)-mediated ER stability in scleral remodeling in myopia.MethodsMyopia was induced in rats by form deprivation and hyperopic defocus. Scleral remodeling, ER stress, oxidative stress, and Mfn2 protein expression were examined in vivo. ER and mitochondrial ultrastructure were assessed by electron microscopy, and tissue apoptosis was evaluated. Primary rat scleral fibroblasts (SFs) were exposed to hypoxia to establish an in vitro myopia model, and Mfn2 expression in SFs was interfered by cell transfection. The extent of SFs transdifferentiation, extracellular-matrix remodeling, ER stress, mitochondrial damage, and cell apoptosis were assessed.ResultsIn this study, we discovered that tissue remodeling was accompanied by ER stress and oxidative stress in the sclera of myopic rats. Concomitantly, Mfn2 expression levels were significantly decreased, and the structures of the ER and mitochondria were damaged, along with tissue apoptosis. In vitro study showed that both hypoxia induction and MFN2 knockdown significantly increased hypoxia-inducible factor-1α expression in SFs. Mfn2 overexpression inhibited the transdifferentiation of hypoxia-induced SFs into myofibroblasts and upregulated the expression of collagen Iα1. Additionally, Mfn2 overexpression alleviated ER stress and mitochondrial damage in hypoxia-induced SFs, and reduced apoptosis of SFs.ConclusionsOur study revealed a critical role of Mfn2 in maintaining ER homeostasis in SFs, which conferred protective effects during scleral remodeling and provided a new therapeutic target for myopia.

Myopia is the most prevalent refractive error, and poses a substantial challenge to public health worldwide. By 2050, the global prevalences of myopia and high myopia are projected to reach 49.8% and 9.8%, respectively.1 The complications of myopia, including vitreous, retinal, and choroidal damage, are primary contributors to irreversible visual loss.2,3 These pathological changes are associated with progressive elongation of the ocular globe and scleral remodeling, characterized by reduced collagen deposition in the sclera, loss of elasticity, and diminished resistance to intraocular pressure-induced expansion.4 The core processes of scleral remodeling involve fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transdifferentiation and extracellular matrix (ECM) modifications. However, the molecular mechanisms triggering this process remain unclear.

Recent studies demonstrated a link between tissue hypoxia and scleral remodeling in myopia.5,6 Palladium nanocrystals endowed with antioxidant properties effectively reduce ECM remodeling and oxidative stress in sclera, thereby decelerating myopia progression in guinea pigs.7 Conversely, hypoxia triggers myopia onset by upregulating hypoxia-inducible factor-2α and activating matrix metalloproteinase2 (MMP2), leading to collagen degradation.8 Furthermore, hypoxia-induced oxidative stress and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress have been found to play a synergistic role in the upregulation of MMP2.9,10

ER stress is an adaptive program initiated when unfolded or misfolded proteins accumulate, restoring ER homeostasis via the unfolded-protein response (UPR) or triggering apoptosis.11 Studies have shown that ER stress is associated with myopia.9,10,12 The expression of ER stress markers is significantly upregulated in the sclera of lens-induced myopia models.13 Using ER stress inducers can induce axial elongation and refractive errors in mice.10 Mechanistically, activation of the ER-associated PERK and ATF6 signaling pathways mediates the degradation of type I collagen and the consequent axial elongation.9 Mitofusin-2 (Mfn2) is essential for maintaining ER homeostasis and sustaining an adaptive UPR. It is a direct physical tether between the ER and mitochondria, and regulates ER morphology through its mitochondrial fusion activity.14 Loss of Mfn2 exacerbates ER stress, manifested by elevated expression of ER chaperones and CHOP-mediated apoptosis.15 Conversely, Mfn2 can restrain ER stress by inhibiting the PERK/ATF4 pathway and downregulate α-SMA expression in cardiac fibroblasts and hepatic stellate cells.16,17 However, whether Mfn2-mediated ER stability exerts a similar regulatory effect on myopia remains unclear. Nonetheless, α-SMA, a characteristic protein of myofibroblasts, has been proven to have a positive correlation with the degree of myopia in the sclera.18,19 Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the role of Mfn2 in maintaining ER stability in scleral fibroblasts (SFs) and regulating sclera remodeling in myopia.

Here, we report decreased Mfn2 expression and associated ER dysfunction in the sclera of rats with myopia. Mfn2 enhanced the resistance of SFs to hypoxia-induced ER stress in vitro, inhibited their transdifferentiation into myofibroblasts, and prevented apoptosis. Concurrently, Mfn2 restored ECM structural stability through upregulation of collagen Iα1 expression. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the role of Mfn2-mediated ER homeostasis in myopia.

Methods

Animals

Animal experiments were conducted according to the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. The research protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. Male Sprague-Dawley rats aged 6 weeks were purchased from SiPeiFu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). Animals were housed in specific pathogen-free conditions in rat colony cages (length × width × height = 475 mm × 350 mm × 200 mm) at a maximum density of five animals per cage. The temperature was maintained at 20°C to 26°C and the humidity at 40% to 70%. A 12-hour light cycle (350 lux, 8:00 AM to 8:00 PM) and a 12-hour dark cycle (8:00 PM to 8:00 AM) were implemented. Standard irradiated rodent chow and purified water were available ad libitum. All rats underwent adaptive feeding for 3 to 5 days before the experiment.

Establishment of the Myopia Models and Ocular Measurements

Rats were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 5 per group): normal control (NC), form deprivation (FD) model, and hyperopic defocus (HD) model. In the FD and HD model groups, the oculus dexter (OD) of each rat served as the experimental eye, whereas the oculus sinister (OS) acted as the contralateral control. Monocular FD myopia was induced by covering the right eye with a translucent latex occluder (Supplementary Fig. S1A). The position of the cover was checked daily, and the size of the cover was changed as necessary to ensure that it was suitable for the growth needs of the animals. Monocular HD myopia was induced by positioning a –10.0 diopter (D) lens in a custom-made holder that was sutured to the upper and lower eyelids and to the medial and lateral canthus of the right eye (Supplementary Fig. S1B). During the induction period the lens surface was cleaned daily.

The refractive status and axial length were measured after 8 weeks. For refraction examination, cycloplegia was induced with 5 g/L tropicamide/phenylephrine hydrochloride eye drops (Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shiga Plant), administered once every 5 minutes for a total of 3 to 4 doses. In a darkened room, refraction was assessed by the same experienced examiner using a streak retinoscope (YZ24B; 66 Vision Technologies Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). Axial length was measured using A-scan ultrasonography (SW-2100; Suoer, Tianjin, China) operating at 10 megahertz (MHz) and representative A-scan traces are shown in Supplementary Figure S2. Topical anesthesia was administered by instilling 4 g/L bupivacaine hydrochloride (Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shiga Plant) into the eye, once every 5 minutes, for a total of 2 to 3 times until the corneal reflex disappeared. The ultrasound probe was oriented perpendicular to the visual axis and gently applanated against the central cornea without indentation.

Cultivation and Treatments of SFs

Scleral tissue was isolated from the eyeballs of uninduced rats and was minced into 1 mm × 1 mm fragments. A cell culture dish was pre-wetted with culture medium and the scleral pieces were evenly distributed on the dish surface. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle medium/F12 medium (DMEM/F12; G4610; Servicebio) containing 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 164210, Pricella) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (G4003; Servicebio) was added, and the dish was incubated at 37°C with 5% CO₂ for 24 to 48 hours. When the tissue blocks adhered to the wall, the medium was changed every 3 to 5 days. Subsequently, the cells were digested using 0.25% trypsin digestion solution (G4012; Servicebio) and cultured in DMEM medium (G4511; Servicebio) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Fibroblasts were identified by immunofluorescence as vimentin-positive and keratin-negative (Supplementary Fig. S3). Cells from passages 3 to 6 were used for the subsequent experiments. To establish an in vitro myopia model, SFs were exposed to a hypoxic environment (90% N2, 5% CO2, and 5% O2) for 24 hours. Concurrently, gene expression was modulated using overexpression (OE) vectors and short hairpin RNA (shRNA). Specifically, oe-NC and oe-MFN2 plasmids were transfected into SFs under hypoxic conditions using Lipofectamine 2000 (Life Technologies). In contrast, sh-NC and sh-MFN2 plasmids were transfected into SFs under normoxic conditions. Transfection efficiency was assessed by measuring the mRNA and protein expression levels of Mfn2 (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Cell Viability Assay

The Cell Counting Kit-8 assay (CCK-8; BS350A; Biosharp) was used to assess cell viability. SFs were subjected to hypoxic conditions and transfected with plasmids for 24 hours. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 hours. Cell viability was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader (DR-3518GL; Diatek).

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (BS259A; Biosharp). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (KCD-M1003; Cronda). The primer sequences used are listed in the Supplementary Table S1. Gene expression levels were assessed by qRT-PCR using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) on a real-time PCR system (MA-6000; Molarray Co. Ltd.). Relative mRNA levels were analyzed by 2-△△CT with β-actin as the internal control.

Western Blotting

Cells and tissue homogenates were lysed on ice using RIPA lysis buffer (BL504A; Biosharp) supplemented with phosphatase (G2007; Servicebio) and protease inhibitors (A32955; Thermo). The protein concentration was measured using BCA assay kit (BL521A; Biosharp). Subsequently, 30 to 50 µg of protein were subjected to Western blotting (WB) with primary antibodies. The primary antibody information used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table S2. β-actin served as an internal control. Membranes were visualized using a chemiluminescence substrate kit (WBKLS0100; Millipore) and imaged on an integrated chemiluminescence imaging system (5200; Tanon, Shanghai, China).

Transmission Electron Microscopy

Scleral tissue was fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 6 hours, dehydrated with graded ethanol and acetone, and embedded in a mixture of half-epoxy propane. Subsequently, the sections were stained with 2% acetate hydrogen peroxide in uranium for 15 to 30 minutes and with 0.5% calcium citrate for 3 to 15 minutes. Finally, the ultrastructure of the sclera was observed and photographed using a TEM (HT-7800; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Morphometric analyses of mitochondria and ER was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Histology

Scleral tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin for tissue sectioning (4 µm). Following dewaxing and rehydration using a graded ethanol series, the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and examined under a light microscope (IX71; Olympus).

Immunohistochemical Staining

Scleral tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded, and sectioned (4 µm). Subsequently, tissue sections were dewaxed and rehydrated using a graded ethanol series. They were then treated with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 minutes and immersed in citrate buffer at 96°C for 5 minutes. After blocking, antibodies to α-SMA (1:2000; Proteintech), Mfn1 (1:200; Proteintech), Mfn2 (1:200; Proteintech), and DRP1 (1:200; Proteintech) were then added and incubated at 4°C overnight. After incubation with a goat anti-rabbit IgG for 20 minutes, the sections were stained with freshly prepared DAB chromogen solution, followed by washing and hematoxylin staining for 1 minute. Finally, the tissues were observed under a microscope (IX71; Olympus).

Immunofluorescence

SFs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA. Then, the cells were incubated with anti-α-SMA (1:1600; Proteintech) and incubated at 4°C overnight. After washing, these cells were incubated with Cy3-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (GB21303; Servicebio) at 37°C for 1 hour. Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 minutes. Finally, anti-fluorescence quenching sealing solution (P0126; Beyotime) was used to seal the glass slide, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (IX71; Olympus).

Confocal Microscopy

Co-expression of PERK, Mfn2, and mitochondria with Calnexin protein were examined. For PERK and Mfn2 detection, SFs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and sealed with 5% BSA. Then, the cells were paired with anti-PERK (1:50; Proteintech), Mfn2 (1:400; Proteintech), and Calnexin antibodies (1:400; Proteintech) and incubated at 4°C overnight. For mitochondria detection, SFs were incubated in MitoTracker Red (40741ES50; Yeasen) at 37°C in the dark for 20 to 30 minutes. Subsequently, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 minutes, and incubated with ice methanol at –20°C for 10 minutes. After washing, the cells were blocked with a blocking solution containing BSA and Triton X-100 for 1 hour. Then, the cells were incubated overnight with the anti-calnexin antibody (1:400; Proteintech) at 4°C.

After washing, all cells were incubated with Cy3-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (GB21303; Servicebio) or FITC-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (GB22301; Servicebio) at 37°C for 1 hour. Hoechst 33258 (C1017; Beyotime) was then added, and the cells were stained in the dark for 10 minutes. Finally, the images of PERK, Mfn2, MitoTracker Red, and Calnexin were captured using a laser confocal microscope (TCS-SP8; Leica, Germany).

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Detection

SFs were seeded into glass-bottom dishes at a density of 1 × 105/mL. Mitochondrial membrane potential was measured with the JC-1 assay kit (M8650; Solarbio). Dilute JC-1 (200 ×) with ultrapure water and JC-1 buffer (5 ×) to prepare the working solution. After washing the dishes with PBS, the cells were incubated with the JC-1 working solution at 37°C for 20 minutes. After washing with JC-1 buffer (1 ×), fluorescence images were acquired on a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Flow Cytometry Analysis of Reactive Oxygen Species

SFs from each experimental group were collected and assessed for the level of reactive oxygen species (ROS) using the ROS Assay Kit (G1706; Servicebio). We prepared the working solution by diluting DCFH-DA in serum-free cell culture medium at a ratio of 1:1000. After 30 minutes of staining with the working solution at 37°C, the cells were analyzed on a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and the data were processed with FlowJo software version 10.

Flow Cytometry Analysis of Apoptosis

SFs from each experimental group were collected and assessed for apoptosis using the Annexin V–FITC/ propidium iodide (PI) Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (G1511; Servicebio). After 15 minutes of incubation with Annexin V–FITC and PI at room temperature, the cells were analyzed on a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and the data were processed with FlowJo software version 10. Early apoptotic cells were identified in the lower-right quadrant (Q3), whereas late apoptotic cells were identified in the upper-right quadrant (Q2).

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay

According to the manufacturer’s protocol, the rat ELISA kits for ROS, MDA, and SOD (JILID Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and TNF-α, IL-1β, collagen Iα1, MMP2, and MMP9 (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were used to quantify the corresponding proteins in scleral tissue homogenates or cell supernatants. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (DR-3518GL; Diatek).

Statistical Analyses

GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 was used to perform graphing and statistical analysis. All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. If normality was confirmed, 1-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc multiple comparisons was performed for multi-group comparisons; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric test with Dunn's post hoc test was applied. Two-way ANOVA combined with Bonferroni post hoc analysis was used to evaluate the effects of MFN2 knockdown or overexpression on SFs under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. Independent sample t-test was used to analyze the eye axis lengths of the rats in the experimental group and the control group. Paired sample t-test was used to analyze the differences between the two eyes of the rats in the experimental group. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

FD and HD Induced Scleral Remodeling in Rats

FD and HD both induced significant increases in refractive error and axial length in the right eyes of rats (Supplementary Fig. S5), confirming the successful establishment of a myopia model.20,21 H&E staining showed that collagen fibers in the sclera of myopic eyes exhibited a disordered arrangement, increased spacing, and fiber fractures compared with that of the NC group (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. S6). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining revealed that the myofibroblast marker α-SMA was upregulated in myopic sclera (Figs. 1B, 1C), whereas WB results confirmed a significant decrease in collagen Iα1 expression and increased expression of MMP2 and MMP9 in myopic sclera (Figs. 1D, 1E). These findings suggest that FD and HD induce myopia in rats, accompanied by scleral tissue remodeling and the transdifferentiation of SFs into myofibroblasts.

ER Stress Occurred in the Sclera of Myopic Eyes

Previous studies have demonstrated that ER stress reduces collagen expression in the sclera by activating the PERK/ATF6 pathway, contributing to axial elongation of the ocular globe.9,12 In this study, WB analysis revealed that the expression of ER stress initiation factors PERK and GRP78, as well as the downstream apoptotic signaling molecules CHOP and caspase-12, were significantly elevated in myopic sclera compared with those in controls (Figs. 2A, 2B). Oxidative stress is a common trigger of ER stress, and inflammatory responses represent a typical tissue reaction under ER stress conditions.22 In our study, levels of hypoxia marker HIF-1α were upregulated in myopic sclera (see Figs. 2A, 2B). Concurrently, oxidative stress indicators ROS and MDA, as well as the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β, were significantly increased, whereas the antioxidant enzyme SOD was significantly reduced (Fig. 2C). These findings indicate the presence of hypoxia and oxidative stress in the myopic sclera, accompanied by ER stress and tissue inflammatory response.

Mfn2 Expression was Reduced and ER Homeostasis was Disrupted in the Sclera of Myopic Eyes

Mfn2 is a critical protein for maintaining ER homeostasis and enabling its adaptive stress responses.14,15 We examined the expression of proteins involved in regulating ER and mitochondrial morphology in the sclera and found that Mfn1 and Mfn2 levels were significantly decreased, whereas the DRP1 level was significantly increased (Figs. 3A–3D). TED further revealed mitochondrial swelling and structural disruption in the myopic sclera, accompanied by significant increases in mitochondrial length, width, perimeter, and area. Concurrently, the ER showed extensive vesiculation and a marked increase in width (Figs. 3E, 3F). Previous studies have shown that ER-stress signals can be transmitted directly to mitochondria, provoking mitochondrial dysfunction and initiating apoptosis.23 Consistently, in the sclera of myopic eyes, we observed activation of the apoptotic program, evidenced by elevated pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and caspase-3 together with reduced anti-apoptotic proteins Bcl-2 and Pro-caspase-3 (Figs. 3G, 3H). Collectively, these results indicate that myopia-induced down-regulation of Mfn2 in the rat sclera is accompanied by dysregulation of the ER–mitochondria axis and activation of the apoptotic pathway.

Mfn2 Inhibited Hypoxia-Induced SFs Transdifferentiation and Upregulated Collagen Iα1 Expression

Hypoxia-induced SFs are widely used to simulate ECM remodeling in myopic cellular models.5,24 Therefore, we established an in vitro myopia model by exposing rat SFs to hypoxia and used sh-MFN2 or oe-MFN2 to modulate Mfn2 expression. CCK-8 results showed that both hypoxia and Mfn2 knockdown decreased SFs viability, whereas Mfn2 overexpression alleviated the cell viability inhibition caused by hypoxia (Fig. 4A). Hypoxia and Mfn2 knockdown also significantly decreased collagen Iα1 expression and secretion, increased MMP2 and MMP9 production (Figs. 4B–D), and enhanced α-SMA fluorescence intensity (Figs. 4E, 4F). Conversely, Mfn2 overexpression reversed these effects. These findings indicate that Mfn2 maintains ECM homeostasis by suppressing hypoxia-induced SFs transdifferentiation and collagen loss.

Mfn2 Inhibited Hypoxia-Induced ER Stress in SFs

Considering the association between ER stress and myopic scleral remodeling, we studied the role of Mfn2 in regulating ER stress in SFs. Results showed that both hypoxia and Mfn2 knockdown significantly increased HIF-α expression in SFs (Figs. 5A, 5B) and elevated the secretion of TNF-α and IL-1β (Fig. 5C). Concurrently, hypoxia and Mfn2 knockdown induced ER stress, manifested by increased levels of PERK, CHOP, GRP78, and caspase-12 in SFs (Figs. 5D, 5E). PERK is an ER stress-sensitive protein, whereas Calnexin participates in protein folding and quality control within the ER.25,26 Confocal microscopy analysis showed that hypoxia and Mfn2 knockdown enhanced the fluorescence intensities of PERK and calnexin (Fig. 5F), indicating activation of the unfolded protein response and heightened ER stress in SFs. Conversely, MFN2 overexpression significantly increased Mfn1 and Mfn2 protein levels, reduced DRP1 and p-DRP1 protein levels (Figs. 5G, 5H), and concurrently decreased calnexin expression (Fig. 5I). Collectively, these results suggest that Mfn2 overexpression enhances the resistance of SFs to hypoxia-induced ER stress.

Mfn2 Alleviated Hypoxia-Induced Mitochondrial Damage and Apoptosis in SFs

ER stress transmits signals directly to mitochondria via mitochondria-associated ER membranes (MAMs), leading to mitochondrial structural and functional damage.27,28 In the present study, fluorescence staining revealed that under hypoxic or Mfn2 knockdown conditions the fluorescence intensity of the calnexin was increased, whereas the distribution of MitoTracker Red-labeled mitochondria was reduced (Fig. 6A). Concomitantly, elevated monomeric JC-1 staining indicated a decline in mitochondrial membrane potential (Figs. 6B, 6C). Flow cytometry analysis further demonstrated that hypoxia or Mfn2 knockdown elevated intracellular ROS levels (Figs. 6D, 6E). Notably, Mfn2 overexpression reversed all these alterations.

Apoptosis represents one of the terminal response programs that cells undergo in response to ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction.23 Flow cytometry revealed that Mfn2 overexpression significantly reduced the proportion of hypoxia-induced apoptotic SFs (Figs. 6F, 6G). WB results further demonstrated that Mfn2 overexpression promoted the expression of the anti-apoptotic proteins Bcl-2 and pro-caspase-3 whereas decreasing the pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and caspase-3 (Figs. 6H, 6I). Collectively, these data indicate that Mfn2 restores hypoxia-induced mitochondrial damage, suppresses activation of the Bcl-2/Bax apoptotic pathway, and thereby reduces apoptosis in SFs.

Discussion

Scleral remodeling is widely believed to be associated with the development of myopia. The transdifferentiation of SFs into myofibroblasts and ECM alteration are main manifestations of this process.4,29,30 However, specific regulatory mechanisms underlying these changes remain unclear. This study aimed to explore the role of Mfn2 in maintaining ER homeostasis in SFs and its implications in myopic scleral remodeling. Our findings revealed an unreported function of Mfn2 in regulating scleral remodeling during the progression of myopia. Specifically, we observed that Mfn2 inhibited cell transdifferentiation and apoptosis by enhancing the resilience of SFs to oxidative and ER stress. Additionally, Mfn2 promoted collagen Iα1 secretion while downregulating MMP2 and MMP9 expressions, thereby preserving ECM structural integrity. Based on these results, we confirmed the important protective role of ER homeostasis mediated by Mfn2 in myopic scleral remodeling.

Hypoxia is a major contributor to myopic scleral remodeling,5,6 and a critical factor in inducing ER stress.11 Recent studies highlighted a strong correlation between ER stress and myopia.9,10,12 In this study, we observed that ER stress and tissue remodeling co-occurred in the sclera of myopic animal models. Mitochondria are the organelles most intimately linked to the ER, forming physical contacts via MAMs.31 Mfn2 is an essential protein that stabilizes the MAMs architecture.32,33 Reduced Mfn2 diminishes the adaptive response to ER stress and weakens the physical connection between the ER and mitochondria.27 This impairs calcium transfer and energy metabolism, leading to structural and functional damage of mitochondria and ultimately promoting apoptosis.28,34 Considering the growing importance of Mfn2 in regulating ER homeostasis, we investigated whether Mfn2 dysregulation occurs during scleral remodeling in myopia. Through animal experiments, we confirmed that Mfn2 expression was significantly downregulated in the sclera of myopic rats, accompanied by impaired ER stress and activation of the apoptotic pathway.

Previous studies showed that Mfn2 overexpression ameliorated renal injury by inhibiting ER stress and apoptosis.35,36 Conversely, other research found that inhibiting Mfn2 reduced LPS-induced rat intestinal oxidative stress.37 However, the role of Mfn2 in myopia remains unclear. In the present study, Mfn2 knockdown induced stress responses in SFs, consistent with those observed in the scleral tissue of myopic rats. Notably, Mfn2 overexpression reversed these adverse effects, even under hypoxic conditions in vitro. Consistent with previous studies, the present study showed that Mfn2 overexpression restored hypoxia-induced ER stress in SFs by upregulating Mfn1 and Mfn2 and downregulating DRP1 and p-DRP1. These proteins simultaneously regulate the structure and function of mitochondria. Mfn1 and Mfn2 regulate mitochondrial fusion, whereas DRP1 mediates mitochondrial fission.38 In our study, Mfn2 overexpression restored the fluorescence intensity of MitoTracker Red-labeled mitochondria and reduced intracellular ROS levels. Previous studies indicated that Mfn2 regulated the mitochondrial-mediated apoptotic pathway by modulating the expressions of Bax and Caspase family proteins.39 Our cellular experiments further confirmed that Mfn2 overexpression inhibited the activation of the ER stress-related PERK/GRP78-CHOP and the mitochondrial damage-mediated Bcl-2/Bax pathway, thereby reducing the caspase cascade reaction and decreasing SFs apoptosis.

In addition, another focus of this study was to investigate the role of Mfn2 in ECM remodeling. Previous studies demonstrated that hypoxia induces a reduction in type I collagen expression and an elevation in MMP2 and MMP9 expressions in SFs.8,40 Our findings corroborated these observations. Moreover, we discovered that Mfn2 overexpression effectively reversed the altered the expression of these proteins under hypoxic conditions. Because MMP2 and MMP9 are capable of degrading collagen within the ECM,41 inhibiting their expression via Mfn2 overexpression contributes to the maintenance of ECM structural stability. On the other hand, previous research indicated that Mfn2 can downregulate α-SMA expression in cardiac fibroblasts and hepatic stellate cells.16,17 Consistent with these reports, our study revealed that Mfn2 overexpression attenuates the hypoxia-induced increase in α-SMA expression. Consequently, we hypothesized that Mfn2 overexpression may suppress the differentiation of SFs into myofibroblasts by enhancing their stress resistance. SFs represent a critical source of type I collagen in the sclera, and an increased proportion of these cells is beneficial for the accumulation of type I collagen in tissues.7,42

In conclusion, this is the first study to elucidate the link between Mfn2-mediated ER homeostasis and myopic scleral remodeling. Our findings demonstrated that Mfn2 levels were decreased in the scleral tissue of myopic rats, accompanied by ER stress. Mfn2-mediated stabilization of ER enhanced the resistance of SFs to ER stress. This effect further inhibited the transdifferentiation of SFs into myofibroblasts or apoptosis, and ultimately regulating scleral remodeling by upregulating the expression of collagen Iα1, thereby exerting a protective effect against myopia.
