Repeated low-level red-light therapy for myopia control: An update on efficacy and safety
Authors: Yue Wu, Jijing Li, Yanxian Chen, Mingguang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Repeated low-level red-light (RLRL) therapy has emerged as an effective intervention for myopia control, yet concerns regarding its safety remain. This review summarizes the current high-level evidence on the efficacy and safety of RLRL therapy. Randomized controlled trials consistently demonstrate that RLRL therapy significantly reduces axial elongation and slows myopic onset and progression in children, with network meta-analyses indicating superior efficacy compared with established interventions. The short-term safety profile appears favorable, and no irreversible damage has been reported. However, uncertainties persist regarding the clinical significance of observed structural changes, such as transient foveal hyperreflectivity and cystoid abnormality on optical coherence tomography, as well as functional changes on multifocal electroretinogram. Addressing critical knowledge gaps, including long-term monitoring, dose optimization, and device design refinement, will be essential for realizing the full potential of this therapy while ensuring its safety.

Introduction

Myopia is a global health issue, and its prevalence has increased dramatically over recent decades, especially in East Asian population.[12] Current myopia control strategies include pharmacological interventions and optical approaches, which face limitations in variable efficacy and potential side effects.[3] Low-dose atropine (0.01%–0.05%) typically reduces axial length (AL) elongation by 0.13 mm/year and spherical equivalent refraction (SER) progression by 0.33 D/year. High-dose atropine (0.5%–1.0%) can yield nearly twice the efficacy, but it carries a higher risk of complications, including allergic conjunctivitis, photophobia, near vision blurriness, and glare. Orthokeratology inhibits AL elongation by 0.28 mm/year, but it requires lens wear, has infection risk, and depends heavily on nightly compliance.[4]

Repeated low-level red-light (RLRL) therapy has recently emerged as a promising alternative. Originally introduced in China as a treatment for amblyopia more than three decades ago, RLRL has been repurposed as a novel approach for myopia treatment. The treatment typically involves a 3-min exposure to red light (approximately 650 nm wavelength) administered twice daily using a desktop device. Early clinical trials have demonstrated remarkable efficacy, with several studies reporting not only a reduction in myopic progression but also axial shortening in a proportion of treated children, a phenomenon rarely observed with other interventions.[5678] Despite its widespread clinical adoption in China and recent regulatory approval in some other countries, questions regarding its overall safety are growing.[9]

This review aims to synthesize the latest evidence on the efficacy of RLRL, as well as to evaluate the safety profile on structural and functional aspects. Finally, we identify and discuss several critical knowledge gaps from clinical perspectives to suggest future research directions.

Literature Search

We conducted thorough literature searches in PubMed, Scopus, and EMBASE to discover relevant articles on RLRL therapy for myopia (latest search performed on November 1, 2025). Both medical subject heading terms and general keywords related to “red light” and “myopia” were used as search terms. Full-text articles and the relevant papers were retrieved subsequently to evaluate the possible eligibility. Published studies were considered eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) involved human subjects; (2) included at least one group treated with RLRL therapy, with or without single vision spectacles; (3) reported outcomes related to myopia progression (e.g., AL and SER) or safety evaluation; and (4) were available as full-text articles published in English. To provide a comprehensive overview of the current evidence, we included original research articles (including randomized controlled trials [RCTs], prospective and retrospective studies, and case reports) as well as meta-analyses. Animal studies and publications not written in English were excluded. All eligible and relevant studies were included in the present review.

Efficacy of Repeated Low-level Red-light Therapy

Myopia control

The evidence for RLRL efficacy in myopic children is substantial and compelling. Figure 1 shows the absolute efficacy of RLRL in myopia control reported in the published RCTs to date.[568101112131415161718192021222324] The landmark multicenter RCT was conducted by Jiang et al., which enrolled 264 children aged 8–13 years with myopia ranging from − 1.00 to − 5.00 D.[5] After 12 months, the RLRL group showed only 0.13 mm AL elongation compared to 0.38 mm in controls, representing a 66% reduction in axial elongation. The RCT by Cao et al. enrolled the largest number of participants to date (n = 336) and reported similar efficacy, with the RLRL group showing a mean AL change of −0.11 mm compared to an elongation of 0.26 mm in the control group after 12 months.[10] As illustrated in the plot, the treatment effect of RLRL increased rapidly within the first 6 months, reaching a peak indicative of a strong initial suppression of myopia progression, and was durable during the continued therapy. In the 24-month post-RCT follow-up study by Xiong et al., children who maintained RLRL treatment for the year showed a cumulative AL elongation of 0.16 mm over 2 years, compared to 0.64 mm in the untreated children who were wearing single vision spectacles.[16] An important subset of RLRL research has been its application to high myopia, conventionally considered more challenging to control. Xu et al. specifically targeted children and adolescents with high myopia (defined as SER≤−4.0D), and they claimed that RLRL demonstrated greater treatment effects than observed in mild-to-moderate myopia by Jiang et al.[18] Another RCT updated its efficacy in high myopic children aged 7–12 years (defined as SER≤−6.0D).[8] Recently, the application of RLRL has been further explored in the pediatric extreme high myopia subgroup, where children with a mean SER of −13.72 D demonstrated modest but significant 12-month improvements (−0.06 mm in AL change and 0.61 D in SER change), according to a cohort study.[25] However, it is important to acknowledge that the comparisons across studies are complicated by variations in patient age, treatment frequency and device protocol, and the maintenance effect was not yet clear.

The consistency of findings across multiple centers and investigators strengthens confidence in the treatment effect. Several meta-analyses have successively synthesized data showing pooled mean differences of approximately −0.21 to −0.35 mm for AL and 0.40–0.68 D for SER in favor of RLRL therapy.[272829] Nevertheless, it should be noted that nearly all trials have been conducted in the East Asian population, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other ethnic groups. Furthermore, the open-label designs employed in most previous studies may overestimate the efficacy due to the lack of masking. To date, the study by Dong et al. remains the only double-blind trial that utilized a sham device emitting light at 10% of the therapeutic power as a control.[6]

Myopia prevention

Beyond treating myopes, RLRL has also been studied as a prophylactic intervention. Two RCTs that enrolled premyopic children are represented by dashed lines in Figures 1 and 2.[1223] Premyopia is defined as SER ranging from −0.50 D to +0.50 D or +0.75 D, and it often targets individuals considered at elevated risk for incident myopia, such as those with at least one parent having SER≤−3.00 D in either eye. Unlike myopia control, the primary outcome in myopia prevention is the incidence rate of myopia. In these trials, RLRL demonstrated a substantial reduction in myopia incidence. He et al. reported that the cumulative incidence of new-onset myopia was lowered from 61.3% to 40.8% over 12 months, alongside differences of 0.17 mm in AL and −0.41 D in SER between groups.[23] Similarly, Liu et al. observed a decrease from 19.4% to 2.5%, with corresponding group differences of 0.15 mm and −0.34 D in the first 12 months,[12] and cumulative differences of 0.17 mm and −0.45 D over 24 months.[24] These data suggest that RLRL may delay the onset of myopia, effectively shifting some eyes from a “high-risk trajectory” toward a “stable or slower-growth trajectory.”[30] From a clinical standpoint, it is valuable because delaying the onset of myopia by even 1–2 years can meaningfully reduce eventual peak AL and the lifetime risk of high myopia-related complications.[31]

Comparison with established interventions

While numerous reviews have compared the efficacy of conventional myopia control strategies,[3233] studies on RLRL emerged mainly between 2022 and 2024 and it is important to conduct updated evidence-based analysis. In 2025, Yam et al. conducted a pairwise meta-synthesis of the myopia control interventions.[4] RLRL demonstrated a pooled treatment effect with a mean change in SER of 0.64 D (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.57–0.71) and in AL of −0.32 mm (95% CI −0.36 to −0.27) per year. These effects were comparable to or greater than those achieved by high-dose atropine (0.64 D for SER and-0.24 mm for AL), and were greater than orthokeratology, moderate and low-dose atropine, peripheral plus spectacles, and multifocal spectacles. Expanding upon these pairwise findings, three network meta-analyses (NMAs) established a definitive efficacy ranking in myopia control.[343536] At the 12-month follow-up, RLRL achieved an optimal cumulative probability of effectiveness in slowing AL and SER progression (SUCRA = 98.6% and 93.8%), greater than those for high-dose atropine, orthokeratology, middle-dose atropine, and low-dose atropine.[36] Similar superiority was observed in pediatric high-myopia over 1 year, where RLRL emerged as the most likely best treatment option, when compared against posterior scleral reinforcement, rigid gas permeable lenses, orthokeratology, and multifocal lenses.[37]

Although indirect comparisons in NMA are informative, the current body of evidence is limited regarding direct head-to-head, multi-arm RCTs that compare RLRL and other interventions within the same study design. Two RCTs randomized children to compare the efficacy of RLRL with 0.01% atropine, and found lower myopia progression in the RLRL therapy group at 12-month follow-up.[3839] A more recent cohort study extended these similar conclusions to a 2-year follow-up period, confirming sustained superiority of RLRL over low-dose atropine.[40] Beyond its role as a monotherapy, several small-scale RCTs also indicate that RLRL may act synergistically with optical interventions (orthokeratology, defocus incorporated multiple segment lenses, or distant-image screen). However, the evidence is preliminary or ongoing.[414243] Looking forward, RLRL is creating new possibilities for enhancing the efficacy of existing ophthalmic treatments. Moreover, as a noncontact and nonpharmacological intervention, RLRL may improve adherence in younger children, and it is suitable for a broader age group, compared to regimens involving eye drops or overnight lens wear.

Safety of Repeated Low-level Red-light

Overview of reported adverse events

Currently, evaluations of the safety profile are focusing on self-reported discomfort, visual function, and ocular structures. Visual function is typically assessed using measures such as best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and multifocal electroretinogram (mfERG), while ocular structures are often examined via optical coherence tomography (OCT). The most common discomfort after RLRL includes intolerance, dry eye, and after image, but these symptoms are usually transient and typically alleviated in a short period. Liu et al. reported intolerance to bright light and dry eye symptoms in two children, which were resolved either spontaneously or with minimal intervention.[13] Another trial reported a mean afterimage duration of 3.2 ± 1.2 min in 20 participants.[19] Chen et al. conducted a thorough safety systematic review including evidence up to February 10, 2024.[44] The incidence of adverse events is 0.088 per 100 patient-years (95% CI: 0.02–0.50). No severe adverse events, defined as sudden vision loss ≥2 lines or scotoma, were reported in these retrospective, cohort, or RCT studies. However, there is one case of vision loss reported in a case study.[45] In general, the rate of adverse events is low, but functional and structural damage may still occur.

Best-corrected visual acuity

BCVA is considered the key safety endpoint. Most studies report no significant changes in BCVA during RLRL treatment, with mean BCVA remaining stable or showing slight improvement.[2244] However, a 12-year-old girl who experienced a two-line decline in BCVA in both eyes during RLRL therapy was reported by Liu et al. and Yu et al.[4647] Her BCVA initially improved from 20/25 to 20/20 after 3 months of treatment. However, by the 5th month, it declined to 20/30, accompanied by prolonged afterimages occasionally exceeding 8 min. After discontinuation of therapy for 3 months, her BCVA was improved to 20/25.

Retinal structure changes in optical coherence tomography

Ellipsoid zone alteration and foveal hyperreflectivity

The ellipsoid zone (EZ) is a highly reflective layer in the outer retina, visible on OCT, and is associated with the integrity of photoreceptor cells. EZ alteration has been occasionally reported in RLRL-eyes, but its characteristics and clinical relevance remain poorly defined. This finding manifests as mild irregularity and altered reflectivity of the EZ band while the overall integrity of the layer remains preserved. In a cohort study, Zhu et al. observed a transient increase in the relative EZ reflectivity ratio at 6 months after RLRL treatment, which was no longer significant at 12 months.[21] Meanwhile, foveal hyperreflectivity was found at 6 and 12 months. In a recent RCT, Zhang et al. reported that 7% (three cases) of RLRL-treated children showed transient dome-shaped hyperreflectivity between the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) layer and foveal EZ on OCT.[22] These hyperreflective signals appeared during treatment but disappeared after 1 month of discontinuation, without any associated impairment of visual function.

The EZ corresponds to the inner segments of photoreceptors, a region densely packed with mitochondria-rich cone cells. In contrast, the region between the EZ and RPE corresponds to the photoreceptor outer segments (POS). Under physiological conditions, the POS undergoes continuous renewal: new discs are synthesized distally, while old discs are shed and phagocytosed by the RPE cells.[48] Disruption of this renewal cycle, either through excessive disc synthesis or impaired phagocytosis, may lead to transient accumulation of outer-segment debris and thereby increase optical reflectivity.[214950] Alterations in the reflectivity of the outer retinal layers have been reported in conditions such as commotio retinae and retinal inflammation, where metabolic stress or interface structural disturbance is present.[5152] Given that red light therapy is known to enhance mitochondrial function and RPE phagocytosis,[53] the transient hyperreflective changes observed during RLRL treatment may reflect increased metabolic activity and accelerated POS renewal.

Ellipsoid zone discontinuity

A more severe manifestation is disruption of EZ integrity. In the aforementioned case report, OCT showed band-like discontinuity in the foveal EZ of both eyes following 5-month RLRL therapy. Accompanied image findings included bilaterally darkened foveae on fundus photography, hypoautofluorescent plaque on autofluorescence imaging, and moderately to mildly decreased responses on the mfERG in the macular and paramacular areas.[46] Although this presentation shares some similarity with laser-induced maculopathy,[5455] the lesion boundary of the reported RLRL-associated case is not distinct and is reversible on treatment cessation. Nevertheless, extensive EZ discontinuity should be viewed as a significant adverse event requiring discontinuation, particularly when associated with functional vision loss.

Inner retinal cystoid abnormality

Cystoid spaces appear as small, well-defined hyporeflective areas with high contrast to the surrounding tissue within the retinal layers on OCT. In the cohort study by Liao et al., one cystoid cavity within the ganglion cell layer was identified in 1 of 97 RLRL-treated eyes, and this abnormality resolved within 3 months after treatment discontinuation.[56] However, the interpretation of such imaging findings varies across researchers.[57] Similar microcystic changes have been documented in healthy eyes; for example, Osaka et al. reported paravascular inner retinal cystoid or fissure-like spaces without connection to the vitreous cavity in 71 of 178 healthy eyes (39.9%) without ocular disease or any history of RLRL exposure.[58] Furthermore, the absence of baseline OCT scans in Liao et al.’s report limits the causality establishment. Whether these abnormalities reflect benign anatomical variation or treatment-related change remains uncertain.

Retinal layer thickening

Several studies reported increases in retinal thickness after RLRL therapy within the parafoveal and perifoveal regions, whereas other studies have found no significant change.[8135960] Because the observed variations are well below the threshold typically associated with retinal edema or pathological swelling, retinal thickness may serve as a useful safety indicator. The subtle magnitude of changes reported in previous studies may suggest that RLRL does not induce clinically relevant retinal edema.

Multifocal electroretinogram

Published data on the electrophysiological changes following RLRL therapy remain limited. Zhu et al. discussed the utility of mfERG in quantitatively assessing retinal function.[21] During the 12-month follow-up, the only significant change was the mild increase in the P1-wave response density and amplitude in the central ring at 6 months. Because P1 activity mainly reflects the ON-bipolar pathway, these findings suggest preserved photoreceptor integrity with potential functional enhancement of the inner retinal activity during RLRL treatment. MfERG can also demonstrate localized functional deficits when retinal injury has occurred. In the case reported by Liu et al., P1-wave response density was notably decreased in the central (15.9 and 16.8 nV/degree2) and paracentral ring (9.2–14.6 nV/degree2), and the corresponding three-dimensional response-density plots lacked the typical central white peak. In these contexts, mfERG serves as a sensitive tool both for indicating potential improvements in retinal electrophysiological function and for detecting localized retinal dysfunction. Future longitudinal studies with a larger sample size are warranted to better understand the mfERG changes during RLRL therapy.

Summary

Overall, current key safety indicators, including BCVA, OCT-based retinal structure, and mfERG, suggest that RLRL is generally well tolerated under clinically supervised, protocol-adherent conditions. Most prospective and randomized studies report stable vision, minimal structural changes, and normal or mildly enhanced electrophysiological responses. Mild foveal alterations such as transient outer-retinal reflectivity have been observed on OCT. However, it is currently unknown whether these changes represent physiological adaptive responses to photobiomodulation as part of the therapeutic mechanism or being early indicators of cumulative light damage. The emergence of one case with BCVA decline and EZ disruption underscores the need for close monitoring. Continued structural and functional surveillance is essential to determine the long-term safety margin of RLRL and to identify changes that warrant treatment modification or cessation.

Knowledge Gaps and Future Directions

The accumulated evidence supports RLRL as an efficient, generally well-tolerated intervention for pediatric myopia control; however, the mechanisms involved are not clear. RLRL therapy is hypothesized to enhance retinal metabolism, increase choroidal blood flow, and possibly alleviate scleral hypoxia.[61] At the same time, the therapy raises important unanswered questions that will define its long-term role in clinical practice. Its biological effects, including both photobiomodulation and potential phototoxicity, are influenced by multiple factors such as irradiance, spatial distribution, cumulative exposure, and retinal susceptibility. Therefore, achieving optimal therapeutic benefit while minimizing retinal risk requires more understanding of these parameters. In this context, the knowledge gaps in the clinical application of RLRL can be framed around the following six key questions.

Who will derive the greater benefit?

Individual variability in treatment response has been observed, with some children experiencing dramatic axial shortening while others show minimal effect. Age, baseline myopia severity, treatment compliance, choroidal thickness changes, and afterimage duration were considered the potential predictors of response;[6263] however, their relative contributions remain unclear. Development of the predictive models incorporating baseline characteristics and early treatment responses could enable personalized treatment approaches and improve patient selection. Extending the application to broader population, such as multi-ethnic pediatric groups,[64] adults,[65] and those with pathological myopia,[66] is also a crucial step, but requires careful efficacy and safety evaluation before clinical recommendation.

What is the long-term efficacy?

While short-term efficacy is well-established, evidence on the long-term durability is limited.[1116] Currently, the longest available observational follow-up extends to 24 months.[1624] Strictly designed RCTs with extended follow-up, washout period, and crossover arms are needed to determine the long-term benefit and rebound effect, and explore tapering strategies to mitigate progression after discontinuation.

What is the optimal treatment dosage?

Variations in intensity and frequency (5–7 sessions per week) exist across studies. Ostrin et al. evaluated two RLRL devices and showed that although the total output power of commercially available devices is low, the coherent beam is focused onto a very small retinal area, and the irradiance can approach or exceed maximum permissible exposure limits for photochemical and thermal retinal injury.[67] Recently, in response to safety concerns, Chinese regulatory authorities have reclassified RLRL instruments as high-risk (Class III) medical devices.[9] Against this backdrop, reducing delivered power is an important direction for balancing safety and efficacy. To date, two studies directly compared the effect of different RLRL dosages.[2068] Zhou et al. reported that low (0.37 mW), medium (0.6 mW), and high intensity (1.2 mW) RLRL all effectively slowed myopia progression in children, with a slightly greater benefit observed at 1.2 mW.[20] In adults, choroidal thickening, a biomarker of treatment efficacy, was similar after 1 month of RLRL therapy at 50% and 100% intensity (calculated as power through a 4 mm pupil of 0.145 mW and 0.29 mW, respectively).[68] The potential for lower doses to maintain efficacy while improving safety supports the need for further research to define an optimal dosing strategy.

Where should the retina be stimulated?

Currently, RLRL devices direct light centrally aligned with the fovea. As Schaeffel mentioned in the editorial, it remains unclear whether therapeutic benefit requires direct foveal irradiation, or whether peripheral or annular exposure that spares the fovea could achieve equivalent efficacy while reducing macular safety concerns.[69] Comparative studies of different illumination patterns would help to answer this question.

How do retinal cells respond to repeated low-level red-light?

Although OCT/OCT angiography offers high-resolution structural and vascular assessment, they cannot resolve cellular-scale alterations. Adaptive optics scanning laser ophthalmoscopy (AOSLO) allows visualization of the cone mosaic and can capture subtle photoreceptor changes. A retrospective AOSLO study reported localized cone density alterations in some RLRL-treated children. However, the absence of baseline data, RLRL device heterogeneity, and the limited stability and repeatability of commercial imaging equipment constrain its interpretation and generalizability.[455670] Additional concerns have also been raised regarding whether the hyporeflective region visualized on AOSLO necessarily corresponds to the cystoid cavity seen on OCT.[71] Nonetheless, future studies should incorporate advanced and multimodal imaging techniques to detect cellular-level changes, including compartment-specific morphology, longitudinal dynamics of retinal cells, within multi-center, prospective cohorts.

Is repeated low-level red-light a cost-effective intervention?

Economic evaluation is essential for the broad clinical adoption of RLRL therapy yet remains underexplored. In the comparative cost-effective analysis of 13 myopia control interventions, Agyekum et al. estimated that RLRL yielded an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of approximately US $846 per diopter in SER reduction over a 5-year time horizon from the societal perspective.[72] Despite higher upfront device costs, RLRL is still cost-effective for children compared to single vision spectacles. However, existing models rely on short-term clinical data, and several barriers to real-world implementation remain. High equipment and maintenance costs limit accessibility in low-resource settings, while the lack of reimbursement coverage necessitates heavy reliance on out-of-pocket payments. Additional challenges include the need for standardized training, daily treatment adherence, and regional regulatory inconsistencies. To better inform policymaking, future cost-effectiveness analyses should incorporate quality-adjusted life years, lifetime horizons, and diverse perspectives (e.g., healthcare systems and payers) to assess the affordability and scalability of RLRL more accurately.

Conclusion

RLRL is a highly effective intervention for myopia control in children, with evidence demonstrating superior efficacy compared to established treatments. The intervention shows promise not only for controlling myopic progression but also for preventing myopia onset in premyopic children and potentially reversing myopic changes in high myopia. Short-term safety appears favorable, although some structural changes have been observed, and its long-term effect remains uncertain.

Critical knowledge gaps persist across both efficacy and safety. Future research should prioritize long-term follow-up studies with comprehensive structural and functional monitoring in different population, dose-optimization trials, and comparative studies of different illumination patterns to optimize device design. Addressing these knowledge gaps will help this promising intervention to achieve its full potential while maintaining the safety standards.
