The adjustment disorder is not a wastebasket diagnosis: a grounded theory study of psychiatrists’ and psychologists’ clinical reasoning
Authors: Juan Pablo Zapata-Ospina, Jhon Styven Sierra-Muñoz, Pablo Madrid Martínez, Carlos Enrique Yepes-Delgado
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: the aim of this study is to understand the diagnostic process undertaken by psychiatrists and psychologists regarding adjustment disorder (AD) in their clinical practice and how they differentiate it from major depressive episode (MDE).Methods: A hermeneutic study using grounded theory techniques was carried out. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with twelve psychiatrists and eight psychologists in Colombia, and transcribed verbatim. Initial line-by-line coding was performed, followed by focused and axial coding to construct categories explaining the professionals’ reasoning process.Results: The clinical reasoning of professionals regarding AD was understood through four major categories. (1) Difficulty in addressing the experience of stressful events, as there is a risk of pathologizing and medicalizing them. (2) Mental health diagnoses are necessary but not apodictic. (3) The diagnostic category of AD allows for the description of a fluctuating depressive and anxious syndrome occurring in reaction to a stressful event, whose abnormality criteria are based on intersubjective knowledge of the patient's life history and consequential reasoning regarding the need for professional support. (4) The AD label could potentially protect against overdiagnosis of MDE and overuse of antidepressants. Many clinicians in their practice thus subordinate the diagnosis of MDE to ensuring it is not AD, contrary to what is outlined in diagnostic manuals.Conclusion: This study allowed us to understand the clinical reasoning of psychiatrists and psychologists about AD as a diagnosis that inherently indicates the need to work on coping and intervene in the stressor and should be considered as a diagnostic possibility in the same hierarchy as MDE in reactive syndromes, rather than a residual category.KEYWORDS: Adjustment disorders, emotional adjustment, depressive disorder, clinical reasoning, diagnosis, differential

Background

The adjustment disorder (AD), according to diagnostic manuals, encompasses affective and behavioural symptoms related to a perceived stressful event, as long as these symptoms do not reach the threshold for another disorder (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; O’Donnell et al., 2019). This definition has elicited criticism regarding its theoretical foundation, since it would be considered a diagnosis subordinate to other disorders, leading to consider it as a disorder without a theoretical model or a category of little use or a wastebasket (Ben-Ezra et al., 2018; Kazlauskas et al., 2017; Semprini et al., 2010). However, clinical experience and empirical data show that AD is frequent (Andreasen, 1982; Balon, 2010; Despland et al., 1995; Kovács et al., 2010; Osborn et al., 2014). It is the seventh most utilized category by psychiatrists (Maercker et al., 2013) and the eighth in the case of psychologists (First, 2016), representing up to 12% of diagnoses in outpatient psychiatric services and up to 18.5% of referrals in general hospitals (Foster & Oxman, 1994; Pintor-Pérez et al., 2018; Strain et al., 1998), and it was one of the most used during the COVID-19 pandemic (Lotzin et al., 2021; Turan et al., 2021). It is also the most common diagnosis following a suicide attempt attended in emergency departments (Taggart et al., 2006); in fact, it has a higher mortality rate from any cause and from suicide compared to a population without psychiatric diagnosis after a stressful event (Gradus, 2017; Gradus et al., 2015).

Despite its importance, the coding of AD in clinical records has decreased over time while major depressive episodes (MDE) have increased, accompanied by an increase in the use of psychotropic medications (Diefenbacher & Strain, 2002). Differentiating between these two disorders is crucial because AD tends to be self-limiting as long as the stressor ceases (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; Morgan et al., 2022). There is little evidence to support treatment with antidepressants (Casey, 2014; Hoffman & Stein, 2022), and psychotherapy and psychosocial interventions are privileged (O’Keeffe & Ranjith, 2007). Administering antidepressants to patients who will not benefit from them leads to unnecessary exposure with potential adverse effects which is a concern in predisposed patients, such as the medically ill (Rackley & Bostwick, 2012). Distinguishing between AD and MDE can be challenging because the psychopathological characteristics may be indistinguishable if the latter is related to a stressor. Therefore, there is a call for further research to allow for the distinction between these diagnoses (Takei & Sugihara, 2006).

Some research has found that patients with AD less frequently exhibit perfectionist personality traits (Doherty et al., 2014) and have fewer personal and family histories of mental disorders (Anastasia et al., 2016) compared to patients with MDE. Additionally, they more commonly report maintenance insomnia (Doherty et al., 2019) and engage in suicidal behaviour earlier following the occurrence of the stressful event (Rackley & Bostwick, 2012), unlike patients with MDE. However, these differences are based on the frequency distribution of variables in two independent samples and may be of limited utility in clinical practice because they can be present in both AD and MDE patients. Therefore, some authors have pointed out, based on their experience, the existence of qualitative differences between both diagnoses that are not covered by current diagnostic manuals. For example, although in both disorders the mood is sad, in MDE it is persistently poorly modulated, whereas in AD it depends on the cognitive presence of the stressor, leading to greater affective reactivity (Semprini et al., 2010).

There is moderate agreement between routine assessments by psychiatrists (which favour the diagnosis of AD), and structured assessment with an instrument such as the Structured Clinical Interview (SCI), based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) (which favours the diagnosis of MDE) (Taggart et al., 2006). This could indicate that in routine psychiatric evaluation, clinicians may differentiate between both disorders based on criteria other than the quantitative fulfilment proposed by diagnostic manuals. Therefore, the aim of this study is to understand the clinical reasoning conducted by psychiatrists and psychologists for AD and how they differentiate it from MDE.

Methods

Study design

Hermeneutic study incorporating some techniques from constructivist grounded theory (Charmaz, 2006), which is a systematic, flexible, and iterative method that allows for the development of theory from the analysis of qualitative data. Grounded theory enables the construction of new analytical frameworks in the field of mental health, as it starts from an understanding of the meanings and actions of participants (Charmaz, 2017). The theoretical framework of this research is symbolic interactionism (Blumer 1986), as we consider clinicians to be human beings who act in accordance with the meaning they attribute to an object. In this context, the clinician interacts both with the patient, who expresses symptoms, and with the construct of the psychiatric diagnosis of AD. This perspective emphasizes the dynamic and interpretative processes clinicians use in diagnosing and understanding the patient experiences.

Researchers

The interviews were conducted by two of the researchers with training in psychiatry and qualitative research, who also perform assessments of patients with mental health issues, with analysis and supervision provided by an epidemiologist expert in qualitative research. Additionally, we had the support of a medical student for the analysis and transcription of the interviews and literature search.

Context

The interviews were conducted over a period of 12 months during 2020 and 2021. Direct contact was made with participants who were attending patients in high-complexity institutions in the city of Medellín, Colombia, where liaison psychiatry and clinical psychology services available.

Sampling

Initially, we employed snowball sampling to identify psychiatrists and clinical psychologists with at least four years of experience in patient care. It was decided a priori to include at least 20 clinicians (Sim et al., 2018), appealing to expert criteria to achieve theoretical saturation (Guest et al., 2006). Subsequently, we utilized theoretical sampling (Charmaz, 2006) based on the emerging categories and information needs that arose during the first ten interviews. It is worth noting that no clinician declined to participate in the interviews during the recruitment process.

Data collection and processing

Initially, a form was administered to collect the sociodemographic characteristics of participants. Subsequently, individual semi-structured interviews were conducted based on a script consisting of 46 open-ended questions that explored the personal diagnostic process of AD, MDE, and the differentiation between both diagnoses (Table 1). The script underwent an initial pilot test with the first five clinicians to determine the need for adjustments, which were made primarily in the formulation of questions. The information from this pilot test was considered crucial and included in the analysis. Field notes were taken during the interviews, which were also included in the analysis. At the time of the study, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the interviews were conducted via video calls, which were recorded and then transcribed verbatim into documents. Proper names were removed from the transcripts to ensure confidentiality.

Analysis

An initial coding process was conducted, during which two researchers independently performed line-by-line coding. Subsequently, they met weekly to discuss the codes they had individually identified, and researcher triangulation was performed (Risjord et al., 2002). Following this, focused coding was carried out, wherein codes were grouped into emerging descriptive categories, which were then further grouped into analytical categories that explained the professionals’ reasoning process. Phenomena were identified to comprehend the context, causes, interactions, and consequences involved in the reasoning, which were organized into a paradigmatic matrix (Corbin & Strauss, 2015). The analysis was conducted concurrently and iteratively using the constant comparative method (Glaser & Strauss, 2017), allowing for the integration of emerging categories and the delineation of a theoretical model. We align with the notion that Grounded Theory can be used to develop both descriptive, non-theoretical outcomes and comprehensive explanations of phenomena, as suggested by Charmaz (Charmaz & Belgrave, 2015). Theoretical saturation was considered achieved when a robust and comprehensive explanation of the clinical reasoning process was attained and satisfied the researchers (Low, 2019). As a form of member-checking, a focus group was conducted with the same participants, where the results were presented and discussed, thereby strengthening the understanding reached by the researchers and supporting the notion of increased saturation (Birt et al., 2016).

Reflexivity

Throughout the process, we became increasingly aware of our roles as researchers, acknowledging the complexity of the subject matter and recognizing that, as clinicians ourselves, our own opinions may influence our interpretation. When contacting participants, we presented ourselves as individuals eager to learn and did not inhibit any viewpoints. We meticulously analyzed all interviews, actively searching for potential outliers or codes that did not fit (Engward & Davis, 2015). During our weekly meetings, we reflected on our own perceptions and realized theoretical saturation when no new perspectives emerged. Additionally, we view ourselves as researchers in training, and this study represents a learning process through which our skills in qualitative research have been strengthened.

Ethical Considerations

This study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with Colombian regulations. It received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, University of Antioquia (Approval Act 008 of May 21, 2020). Each clinician participated voluntarily and signed informed consent.

Results

We interviewed a total of twelve psychiatrists and eight psychologists, who engage in clinical activities in the cities of Medellín and Bogotá (Colombia) with 5–35 years of experience in patient care (Table 2). The interviews lasted on average 104 minutes (range of 59–170 minutes). From 507 initial codes, we comprehended the clinicians’ clinical reasoning about AD into four final broad categories (Table 3) and subsequently presented the paradigmatic matrix.

Category 1: the difficulty in addressing the subjective experience of stressful events

Stressful events were understood as discrete or ongoing situations or occurrences that disrupt and alter an individual's life. These situations are perceived as demands or challenges to maintain life functioning and, as a result, trigger a physiological, emotional, and/or behavioural stress reaction. Sometimes, it is not the events themselves, but their consequences, that are experienced as stressful. Stressful events can occur in various domains, such as one's own health or that of close individuals, medical care, economic or work situations, academy, and social relationships, including family and partner relationships, and situations of violence, thar is prevalent in our country. Given that human life is uncertain and complex, our own vulnerability as finite beings and interaction with a constantly changing environment and others make stressful events ubiquitous in our lives. Experiencing stressful events is a highly dynamic and interactive process, motivated by the desire to recover, or even improve, the functioning one had before the occurrence of the stressful event.

Human beings experience life events and typically have emotional reactions to them, it's inevitable … We will all have them and there will be a range of reactions we have to them. Some will be expected, and others will cause more distress, but they will happen to us. (Psychiatrist 11)

Therefore, in the clinical practice of mental health professionals, contact with patients experiencing a stressful event inevitably occurs. However, despite being so common, understanding that experience proves to be extremely difficult for the participants. On one hand, during the assessment of patients, the question always arises about the normality of the experience and the search for the proportionality of the reaction to the event, for which there are no external parameters that dictate it. In this search, the clinician's own emotions and experiences arise, who even puts themselves in the patient's situation to try to better understand the reaction.

We, the evaluators, the outsiders, are the ones who have to, with that life story, weigh whether what the patient is experiencing falls within the normal range, and there lies the serious problem, as defining the normal range is too difficult … Who has a ‘proportionometer'? (Psychiatrist 3)

At times, one puts oneself in the evaluation because, depending on one's life story and also how one feels on the day they evaluate the patient, certain things will be given more weight to determine if what the patient is experiencing is normal, which is quite complicated. (Psychiatrist 7)

On the other hand, there is the concern of not pathologizing the experience of adversity and prescribing a single way to react to it. Clinicians shared their experience of having seen a wide spectrum of reactions to stressful situations, and rather than adopting a prescriptive view of the situation, what should be sought is a comprehensive understanding. In fact, the assessment of a situation as stressful is entirely subjective, and each human being, based on their experience and the context surrounding them, may attribute the adjective ‘stressful’ to it.

There are people who, after very traumatic events, can have calm responses that one would never expect, and others, on the contrary … It's worth noting that human nature is so diverse that there are really people who achieve incredible adaptations to changes, and others who struggle more … But normality shouldn't be demanded from the outside, it should be linked to the resources that [the patient] has built for themselves. (Psychologist 1)

The clinicians address the difficulty of this understanding through an idiographic approach, where during the patient and family interviews, the individual's life history is reconstructed to understand how they have coped with stressful situations in the past, what the current stressors represent, and how they impact different areas of life. Thus, the assessment of proportionality and the criterion of normality are based on intra-individual perceptions of the stressful event itself, how one reacts to it, and how it is managed. This implies an intersubjective reasoning where the professional, influenced by their own history, constructs the life story of the other to understand how they have experienced and lived through a stressful event.

How do we identify in the subject's history which resources they have normally used in similar situations? I think that's very important to be able to evaluate … The clinical perspective has to dialogue with the history of that subject to know and assess whether what they are experiencing is adaptive or not. (Psychologist 4)

Clinicians pointed out an additional difficulty related to adversity intolerance that can be observed in society, in the sense that there is a quick extinction of the discomfort brought by stressful situations and avoiding recognizing them because they may signify failure. In a culture of immediate gratification, there is no room for acknowledging adversities as an unavoidable part of human life or as sources of personal growth. This is reflected in medicine, as many times it is not allowed to experience discomfort in the face of stressful events such as a diagnosis of illnesses. Consequently, there is an excessive demand placed on mental health professionals who are expected to propose quick solutions, but instead end up explaining that it may be a normal or expected reaction. However, at times, this demand can alter the assessment of normality and proportionality as it conditions mental health professionals to diagnose a mental disorder.

Sometimes there's also this idea that it's bad for a lady to start crying because she got some news, like she was diagnosed with de novo diabetes, and it's like if she couldn't cry, as if that reaction wasn't allowed at that moment. And what do [doctors] do? They consult psychology or psychiatry, and we end up putting some diagnosis. (Psychologist 2)

Category 2: psychiatric diagnoses are essential, but not apodictic

Clinicians aim to understand the patient's lived experience, and in that exercise, although not their ultimate goal, assigning a diagnostic category is crucial because: (1) The use of a category is required to navigate the healthcare system to administratively guarantee access to services, including the care provided by mental health professionals. (2) They allow for the description of commonalities among patients, which can facilitate the organization of care protocols, clinical research on groups, and the description of population epidemiology. (3) They enable communication among different healthcare professionals and even with patients themselves, as for some, a denomination of the process they are going through is important. Therefore, as the construction of the individual's life history progresses in the clinical interview, the constellation of signs and symptoms ends up being encompassed with a label that seeks to summarize the clinical picture, but not replace the understanding reached of the patient's experience.

We work in a healthcare institution, that's something that needs to be understood. It's not possible to detach oneself from the institutional requirements regarding diagnosis, whether it's a personal or school stance. Initially, I didn't feel comfortable because when you're freshly graduated, you might think that labeling patients is harmful, but when you work, you realize the opposite. It's actually a beautiful thing because if I don't assign a diagnosis to the patient, we won't be able to provide them with proper care. (Psychologist 1)

I can guarantee you that if I start not assigning a name [a diagnosis], or I just put [in the medical record] grief, the auditors [of the clinic] will start asking me why I'm still seeing those patients and why I'm doing so much with them, or why I'm giving them outpatient appointments. So, in the end, you end up giving it a name, [a name of a disorder]. (Psychiatrist 3)

However, in the clinicians’ practice, the designation of a diagnostic category does not imply its recognition as natural classes or apodictic matters, as there is currently no foolproof method to determine the presence or absence of a mental disorder; there are no pathognomonic symptoms or a gold standard, such as a biopsy or a tangible diagnostic test available in practice, and therefore there may be uncertainty and disparities in the assigned diagnostic label.

I believe all diagnoses in psychiatry generate uncertainty. I mean, I don't think one can say, with certainty: ‘This is it; the patient has this diagnosis. It's not like being an orthopedist who has X-rays and sees the broken bone, there's no uncertainty there, everyone would say it's a fracture. (Psychiatrist 12)

These are constructs or abstract objects that attempt to summarize a person's experience. The choice of label to name the object is largely based on anticipating the consequences that arise from labelling it. Although there may be an underlying pathophysiology that is part of the commonality among patients with the same label, the clinician places great weight on the effects that derive from or should derive from its categorization because there is no certainty in the ultimate causes or the absolute presence of stress-related mental disorders. The consequences are part of the diagnostic argumentation in such a way that the question is not only why the diagnosis is present but also for what purpose it will be designated. In this regard, the clinical judgment of what is defined as a mental disorder in the experience of a stressful event is based on consequential reasoning, on the question of the resources that should be made available to help the patient.

Rather than explaining to the individual that they have a diagnosis because they have some symptoms, it's about considering whether anything needs to be done about those symptoms. If someone is feeling sad because of something, but they don't need help, they can continue with their activities in a normal way, then nothing needs to be don and no diagnosis needs to be given. (Psychiatrist 8)

As the interview with the patient progresses, each clinician gathers information from the constructed medical history, mental examination, and the projection of actions that should be taken to help the patient, until they reach a kind of personal threshold, after which they decide whether to assign a diagnostic label or not, and to determine which one best describes the patient's experience. Often, the threshold is guided by the criteria outlined in diagnostic manuals, which serve as a guide to orient the assignment of the label agreed upon by a scientific community and to project the treatments described in the scientific literature for that label, which often form the focus of academic training. In this way, there is a theory-laden observation derived from the manuals, as the clinician considers the various available labels and groups the information from the clinical picture following the thread of diagnostic criteria. However, in clinical practice, these manuals have led to a reification of mental disorders that erases the patient and have erroneously operated as a judge or truth of the order of natural classes, which is not the case for mental disorders, nor the intention sought by clinicians with the assignment of a diagnosis.

I believe that diagnoses are our fallacy, and what ultimately matters is whether the patients receive the management that I, after the technical evaluation, determine they should receive. The manuals with the diagnostic criteria we have defined are important for there to be correspondence in what we see in various places and for us to communicate, but in practice, they operate very little. I focus more on the semiology and history I find in patients; I am not eager to classify them because it's not about knowing a disease but knowing a person. (Psychiatrist 4)

For me, diagnostic manuals are handy because there are moments when I have a diagnostic hypothesis, and I look at the manual to guide me. I was educated more with the DSM and trained to orient myself by it. So, I do think, ‘What is this patient presenting? How long have they been presenting it?’ Those are the questions I always ask myself, and they are in the manuals. But undoubtedly, what concerns me is understanding the situation [of the patient] and if they need specific treatment. (Psychologist 5)

Category 3: adjustment disorder as a useful and necessary diagnostic category

Participants perceive the use of the diagnostic label of AD to be common in outpatient, hospital, and emergency settings. With it, they seek to describe the depressive and anxious syndrome that a person presents as a reaction to a perceived stressful event. AD is a diagnosis that demands understanding from the clinician itself, as it directly prompts one to think about the interaction the patient is having with an event; the emphasis is on the reaction the patient is having and invariably forces one to focus on the stressful events and their coping mechanisms. Therefore, it requires a thorough clinical evaluation of the life history to have that intersubjective knowledge of the stress experience, which in clinical practice requires dedicated time and conditions of privacy.

[AD] is a common diagnosis, it may be a bit more common in private practice. This diagnosis is not easy; it requires a lot of thinking, a lot of conversation … It's a diagnosis that obliges you to reconstruct previous situations [of the patient] or how they have dealt with other moments in their history and the problem-solving capacities they have. (Psychiatrist 1)

I diagnose it quite frequently because the patients I attend to are patients in a hospital context, and often we are with them from the initial moment of an organic diagnosis, and the first thing that comes to mind is to inquire about the concept of the subject's coping capacity. When I realize that they have difficulties in those coping resources, that's when the idea of ⁣⁣adjustment disorder starts to appear. (Psychologist 6)

The label of AD refers to various depressive and anxious symptoms that are attributed by the patient to the occurrence of the stressful event. Often, this attribution is established through a counterfactual imaginary exercise of what would happen if the event or its consequences disappeared. Professionals also described that the clinical presentation is characterized by a rapid onset of symptoms after the event, so their onset can be placed very close to the occurrence of the event on the timeline; high variability and great dependency on the stressor, so the mood fluctuates throughout the day depending on what happens with the event and ceases if the event ends or is resolved; and also preserved affective reactivity, so that the patient retains the ability to experience positive emotions such as joy. Within the symptoms, clinicians warn that suicidal behaviour can be included, which, in the face of experiencing a stressful event, justifies the designation as a mental disorder because it requires actions to be taken for its containment.

I believe that adjustment disorder can be of such severity that it can lead to suicide. In fact, I believe that the only person who committed suicide at the clinic [where I work] did so during an adjustment disorder. This can be so severe that a person can die because of a situation he/she is experiencing. We must be very careful. (Psychiatrist 2)

The use of the AD label implies that the experience of the stressful event must be disproportionate, which can only be constructed with the patient, who feels overwhelmed. Additionally, it is also assigned when there is a need for the individual to receive help to get out of the situation or to alleviate the discomfort of the symptoms, rather than to a normative criterion of disturbed adaptation. At this point, the diagnosis of AD fills a gap experienced in clinical practice to be able to accompany and follow up on the experiences of some individuals who, if not labelled, could be excluded from health services:

The difference between a normal stress reaction and an adjustment disorder is the proportionality of the response to the stressor; the issue is that determining that proportionality is entirely subjective and according to the patient's context … Everything is very much oriented toward the context and how much the situation can be improved, and the diagnosis allows for that monitoring, because the course of symptoms is uncertain; it can be adverse and increasingly worse, or it can improve. The patients move according to the stressful events, and with the diagnosis, I can follow them. (Psychiatrist 5)

In this regard, AD is seen as a working diagnosis that leads to organizing healthcare services to support the patient in their experience and in seeking resolution of the event or its consequences, and to monitor their progress. Clinicians mainly referred to interdisciplinary work involving medicine, psychiatry, psychology, and social work to provide psychoeducation, psychotherapy, psychosocial interventions, and even spiritual support that can improve the patient's coping with the event.

If we say that a patient has an adjustment disorder, that's where we need to start working. Perhaps the stressor will persist, bring new things, so it's crucial to identify coping strategies, look at personality traits, their context, everything that can help them cope with the situation. (Psychologist 3)

The use of psychotropic medication is limited to relieving symptoms such as insomnia; in fact, the label of AD seems to explicitly discourage and restrict the use of antidepressants, as it emphasizes coping with the stressful experience. In this sense, AD serves as a safeguard against over-medicalization or indiscriminate use of antidepressants. However, there are occasions when doctors may end up prescribing antidepressants not so much as an intentional therapeutic action but rather as a safety behaviour, particularly in cases where there is uncertainty about being able to follow up with patients or when there is no interdisciplinary team available for mental health care, especially in the presence of suicidal risk.

Actually, a pharmacological approach is not typically required; what is done is more of a psychotherapeutic support. In the event medication is necessary, insomnia is treated for insomnia. If there are significant anxiety symptoms present, specific treatment is provided for those symptoms. (Psychiatrist 6)

Another thing that sometimes happens is due to the lack of access to outpatient psychology. This leads one to make decisions that are probably not correct, but one ends up prescribing antidepressants to these patients due to the limitation in access. (Psychiatrist 7)

A relevant aspect regarding the use of the AD label is that it requires training not only in gathering information but also in diagnostic reasoning. For clinicians, evaluating these patients can be a challenge, especially for untrained staff. Moreover, for some of them, the diagnosis of AD represented a discovery after being students, as they did not receive training in understanding the experience of stress, and it was through clinical practice that they found this way of describing these types of patients.

With practice, my perception of adjustment disorder changed radically. I was taught to see it as a diagnosis of exclusion, like a trash bin; it was imparted with much less importance than it has today after seeing so many patients. (Psychiatrist 5)

For me, there was a big shock because in university, not much emphasis is placed on this diagnosis, and when you enter the real world, you realize that it is a very commonly used diagnosis and that it is very necessary because it explains much better the symptomatology associated with a triggering factor. (Psychologist 5)

Category 4: the overdiagnosis of depression in clinical practice: ‘It's either depression or nothing’

A central issue in the reasoning of AD is to differentiate it from MDE, as in practice the boundaries are blurred and at times AD can be seen as a phenocopy of MDE. However, if depressive symptoms can be attributed to a stressful event, the diagnosis privileged is AD. Even in a patient with all the symptoms of the diagnostic criteria for MDD according to the DSM-5 after a stressful event, clinicians preferred to assign the label of AD because it better described the clinical picture and prevented the therapeutic resources from focusing on the use of antidepressants, instead centreing the specific work on the stressful event and its coping. While there may be symptomatic overlap and AD cannot be differentiated from MDD under an apodictic logic, the consequences of each label are different.

Even in the typical case of a patient with anhedonia, sadness, sleep problems, and suicidal ideation, one might think it's a major depressive episode, but if there's a trigger, then one questions because there's a life situation explaining that clinical presentation. I would think that if there's a precipitating event or a core event, one should probably first consider an adjustment disorder because what's needed is the proposal for psychological support and close monitoring by psychiatry to see how the patient adapts. (Psychiatrist 1)

We can't view adjustment disorder as a diagnosis of exclusion. What we must ultimately see is that perception of threat and the stress the patient is facing, not being able to identify resources to cope with it, and that, even if it seems like depression, requires initiating specific and different psychotherapeutic management. (Psychologist 8)

We don't have blood levels to determine which disorder it is when there's a stressful event; we don't have an ‘adjustemia’ or ‘depressionemia’ in the clinic, so we'll always be uncertain. Therefore, it seems to me that one has adjustment disorder, which is a good working diagnosis because it indicates that one as a clinician has a lot to do. (Psychiatrist 5)

This is how many clinicians in their practice prioritize the diagnosis of MDE over it being a TA, contrary to what is proposed in diagnostic manuals, where AD is ruled out. They even perceive that in their work, AD is more frequent than MDE. A different issue arises when symptoms were present before the event or when symptoms are not attributed by the patient to a stressful event, in which case the assigned label is MDE. Clinicians also see the transition from AD to MDE as feasible when symptoms initially were reactive to the event but later lose variability and become independent of the stressor (as presented in Figure 1).

There are patients who started with an adjustment disorder and then the symptoms increased in severity; the symptoms no longer relate to a specific event but to many different things. The sadness becomes overwhelming, so we have to change the diagnosis to depression. (Psychiatrist 11)

However, there is a perception in the clinical field that there is an overdiagnosis of MDE, leading to an exaggerated prescription of antidepressants. Clinicians believe there is a tendency to oversimplify the evaluation of patients experiencing a stressful event, resulting in the overdiagnosis of MDE, especially when the time for assessment is brief. An approach focused on quickly determining a checklist of diagnostic criteria symptoms makes it impossible to construct the patient's life history and thus eliminates the AD label from clinical reasoning.

I'm very disillusioned that a large portion of doctors and psychiatrists opt for the simplest diagnosis. If you meet all the criteria, you have depression and then antidepressants. It seems to me that we need to talk to the patients, inquire about their lives, try to put ourselves in their shoes to understand what's going on because if it's due to an event that has them feeling this way, will that patient improve with an antidepressant? No, an antidepressant won't help them improve. (Psychiatrist 9)

You see a lot of diagnoses of depression and anxiety, without a very clear specification. You encounter that a lot, but it's because we don't have the time to differentiate anything absolutely, so we go for the easiest or quickest option. (Psychologist 5)

The label of AD is also switched to MDE with the consequent use of antidepressants as a new safety behaviour by clinicians when there is no administrative guarantee of follow-up or no way to access interdisciplinary care. It's as if mental health professionals are expected or pressured to diagnose an MDE as if it were the only valid diagnosis for the healthcare system, and the prescription of psychotropic medications were the only therapeutic approach in medicine. In this sense, AD implies the recognition that medicine can address suffering in general through collaborative work with other healthcare professionals.

It's complex because it's either depression or nothing. In clinical practice, one feels that if at the end of the evaluation, it's determined to be an adjustment disorder, and the patient should not be medicated, that we'll wait, that we'll provide psychotherapy, then other clinicians perceive that nothing was found because there's no depressed patient on medication. (Psychiatrist 7)

Due to this overdiagnosis, the term ‘depression’ has become polysemous, referring to different negative mood states, including those that are reactions to stressful events. Since labels affect patients and condition future healthcare, if an MDD is diagnosed in a reactive case, the emphasis on the triggering event may be lost when the patient is seen by another clinician. Additionally, the use of MDD or AD labels has consequences for patients, as their understanding of the process will be different. While in AD, the need to address the stressor is emphasized, in MDE there may be an emphasis on pathophysiological hypotheses and the need to normalize neurotransmission with antidepressants.

A diagnosis of depression can create the false impression that the low mood is caused by an internal pathology, similar to diabetes or emphysema. This could lead us to ignore other options, such as when it is an adjustment disorder, which is totally different because it is external. (Psychiatrist 2)

Conceptualization of professionals regarding Adjustment Disorder (AD) and Major Depressive Episode (MDE) based on the clinical presentation of symptoms. A. The label of AD describes the depressive (and anxious) syndrome attributed to a stressful event, with high variability and fluctuations in its course. B. The label of MDE describes the depressive syndrome with low variability and without attribution to a stressful event or when symptoms were present before the occurrence of the event. C. Transition from AD to MDE is possible when depressive symptoms become independent of the stressful event.

Towards a theoretical model of adjustment disorder diagnosis

In a healthcare system that operates with diagnostic codes, the label of AD is frequently utilized by clinicians. With it, the aim is to comprehend a patient presenting with a depressive and/or anxious syndrome, which includes depressed or anxious mood, preserved affective reactivity that fluctuates over time, accompanied by cognitive rumination, excessive worry, thoughts of death, and even suicidal ideation; insomnia, decreased appetite, and behavioural disturbances such as risk-taking behaviours, substance abuse, and aggression towards others. The distinguishing feature of this syndrome is the presence of a stressful event to which the symptoms can be attributed, with an onset of symptoms closely related to the appearance of the stressor. In fact, the mere presence of a stressful event prompts a diagnosis of AD and necessitates its exclusion before proposing another diagnostic label. This process involves an idiographic approach, the application of intersubjective reasoning, and consideration of consequences. However, in the context in which our interviewed clinicians operate, there is an intolerance to adversity that may pressure clinicians to medicate emotional reactions stemming from the adversity. Even in medical services, it appears that psychiatrists and psychologists are expected to quickly resolve the syndrome, which can lead to pressure to assign the label of MDE and prescribe antidepressants, especially among personnel with limited training (Figure 2). There are also systemic and administrative pressures, such as reimbursement policies, and limited time for assessment, which often favour the diagnosis of MDE over AD. Clinicians feel compelled to label symptoms as MDE to ensure that patients receive necessary treatments and services.

Paradigmatic matrix of the diagnostic process of AD conducted by clinicians. AD: adjustment disorder. MDE: Major Depressive Episode.

Constructing the diagnosis of AD requires time and conditions of privacy that are often not guaranteed in the Colombian healthcare system, which operates under a profit-driven logic focused on cost-saving measures achieved by attending to a large number of patients in the shortest possible time. Consequently, there may be a diagnostic simplification where any depressive syndrome is labelled as MDE because the conditions for a deeper understanding are not met. As obstacles also exist in the system for providing services such as intensive psychotherapy for coping or close patient follow-up, clinicians may feel pressured to assign a different label, one that may be seen as a ‘true illness’ like MDE because it would be a safer path to navigate the healthcare system, even if AD may better capture the patient's experience. In this sense, the diagnosis of MDE and the prescription of antidepressants would be safety behaviour for the clinician who fears the patient may not receive the services they require.

All of this leads to an overdiagnosis of depression and the consequent overuse of antidepressants, which is convenient for the pharmaceutical industry as it focuses attention on pharmacological measures. As attention is focused on altered neurobiology, the close relationship with a stressful event is erased, and consequently, the need to intervene in the event is forgotten. Many stressful events are related to health determinants, so focusing solely on the individual experiencing the event and their neurobiology excludes the responsibility of the State regarding those determinants. This could promote the psychologization of determinants, with the belief that the solution lies in neurobiology and there is no need to address external social and economic factors that influence health, such as poverty, unemployment, lack of public services, or discrimination. With the overdiagnosis of depression, it is possible that true cases, those that do require the use of antidepressants for moderate to severe cases, may encounter limitations in accessing specific treatment for their condition (Figure 2).

Discussion

In this study, we found that clinicians prioritize the diagnosis of AD when faced with a depressive and/or anxious syndrome attributed to a stressful event. However, within the clinical setting of the Colombian healthcare system, there are pressures that favour the overdiagnosis MDE and, consequently, the overuse of antidepressants.

This work aimed at identifying clinical problems, considering and ruling out diagnostic categories, and subsequently assigning a diagnostic label and proposing a treatment, requires a cognitive process known as diagnostic reasoning (Croskerry, 2009; Lally et al., 2019). There are multiple approaches that attempt to explain diagnostic reasoning. One of the most popular is the dual processing model, which posits the existence of a pattern recognition-based processing system for making quick and automatic decisions (Type 1), and another, much more analytical and methodical system that allows for solving complex problems or those with which the clinician lacks sufficient experience or familiarity (Type 2) (Croskerry, 2009). While this model is widely accepted, in this study we observed that clinicians not only described a cognitive process of identifying depressive and anxious symptoms but also the construction of a life history in which these symptoms appeared. That is, diagnostic reasoning in mental health requires abstraction of information: how symptoms and their attributes are identified (Fernando et al., 2013).

The process of creating this abstraction involves the exchange of complex meanings between the two individuals, which allows for a deeper understanding of the patient's experiences and emotions, known as intersubjective reasoning (Harrison & Tronick, 2022). We observed the reciprocal influence of first-person knowledge (the individual perception of reality: ‘I believe, I think’ of the patient) and third-person knowledge (impartial and removed from the first person, ‘the other, the object,’ of the clinician) (Gupta et al., 2019). Through this constructed knowledge, professionals can understand how symptoms manifest, such as whether their onset is related to a stressful event, if they change with the improvement of the stressor, and if the symptoms can be attributed to the stressor. This intersubjective reasoning helps assess the proportionality of the response to the stressful event and the criterion of normality, ultimately allowing for the diagnosis of AD based on the patient's experiences.

We also understood that clinicians do not engage in a separate process of diagnostic reasoning and therapeutic reasoning but rather integrate the two, which has been described as a process of synergy between thinking about diagnosing and treating (Patel & Bergl, 2018). We outlined a consequential reasoning where assigning the AD diagnosis involves considering the impact of choosing one diagnostic label over another. When AD is assigned, the focus shifts from initiating psychotropic medication to managing symptoms through non-pharmacological interventions while the patient adapts. AD is essential for capturing the difficulties patients face in adjusting to stressors, emphasizing the adaptation process. This makes it a valuable diagnosis, not as a natural category but for addressing an experience that needs support without defaulting to psychotropic medication. Diagnoses in mental health have a particularity described by our clinicians, which is that they cannot be taken as ontological objects. Categories in mental health are not objects or natural classes but rather socially constructed abstractions or practical classes designed to fulfil pragmatic functions (Zachar, 2014). They do not reflect the ontology of the natural world but rather establish boundaries within a theoretical framework to be used by scientific groups such as the medical community in our case (McGrath, 2005).

Indeed, we observed how in the evaluation of patients in clinical practice, it is the teleological sense rather than the ontological one that leads to the use of diagnostic labels. In patients meeting MDE criteria, clinicians preferred diagnosing AD when a clear link to a stressful event was evident, as it emphasizes the need for adaptation rather than focusing on neurobiological normalization. Currently, diagnostic manuals for mental disorders subordinate the diagnosis of AD to the non-fulfilment of diagnostic criteria for other disorders such as MDE (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; World Health Organization, 2022). But in practice, since the diagnosis of AD implies, as an object, an emphasis on psychotherapeutic interventions rather than the need for specific psychopharmacological treatment, a reorganization of the hierarchy is proposed in which AD can be used, even with symptoms of MDE, if one considers the emphasis on causality with a stressful event. Diagnosing MDE instead of AD in a reactive stress syndrome might lead to antidepressant prescription and could shift focus away from addressing the stressor in the treatment plan. Precisely psychology students participating in a field study to determine the impact of poverty on access to mental health services transformed the expression ‘distress’ of some interviewed women into ‘depression’ (Stolkiner, 2013). Thus, the distressing experience of living in poverty became a specific diagnosis for which there is a whole medical model supporting the use of psychotropic medications to restore altered neurobiology (Phillips, 2018). If these women were to seek health services for their ‘distress,’ they should receive help in a way that preserves the essential link between their symptoms and poverty. Appropriately encompassing this experience with a consequential reasoning would allow efforts to be directed, for example, towards financial and governmental aid, counselling, and social work, which becomes explicit with the AD label. Therefore, AD in practice does not operate as a diagnosis of ‘exclusion’ (Patra & Sarkar, 2013), residual, or subordinate (Fernández et al., 2012), but as a primary diagnosis under the conditions described in our results.

The proper definition and amendment in the hierarchy of AD are important in contexts where there is an overdiagnosis of depression (Parker, 2007), a phenomenon perceived by the clinicians in this study and by clinicians in other regions. Some studies have found that the diagnosis of depression and the use of antidepressants have increased in recent years (Thombs et al., 2019). In countries like the United States, antidepressants are one of the most prescribed medications (Safer, 2019), with the aggravating factor that many of their prescriptions are inappropriate. In adults over 65 years of age who have been prescribed antidepressants, only 15% of them meet diagnostic criteria for MDE, which suggests that they are used indiscriminately for subsyndromal or reactive symptoms to events, and therefore are exposed to adverse events without possible benefit (Mojtabai, 2014). In our study, one of the reasons for incorrectly assigning the diagnosis MDE is when intersubjective reasoning and idiographic evaluation are hindered, as happens in healthcare systems or services where there is not enough time for the interview. Thus, decisions in mental health are affected not only by clinician factors, such as their experience, but also by external factors that act as sources of bias (Bhugra et al., 2011). Or this context leads to the adoption of safety behaviours (Sharp, 2001); our participants noted that when the clinical and healthcare system context limits idiographic evaluation or the provision of adequate psychosocial treatment for patients with affective symptoms reactive to stressors, they often prioritize diagnosing MDE and prescribing antidepressants. This is done as safety behaviour driven by fears of severe outcomes like suicide or the inability to resolve the patient's problem. Then, having guaranteed access to appropriate psychosocial treatment could change patterns of antidepressant prescribing in mental health (Lam et al., 2022).

It is likely that the differentiation between AD from other stress-related disorders, particularly post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is clearer for clinicians, as the component of autonomic hyperarousal and the life-threatening nature of the stressful event seem distinctive for the latter. While AD is primarily characterized by emotional distress in response to a non-life-threatening stressful event, PTSD involves specific symptoms such as re-experiencing the trauma, persistent avoidance of trauma-related stimuli, and marked autonomic hyperarousal (Karatzias et al., 2022). This hyperarousal, which includes intense physiological responses like hypervigilance and exaggerated startle reactions, coupled with the life-threatening nature of the event, helps healthcare professionals more accurately identify PTSD, since there may be an overlap of depressive and anxious symptoms (O’Donnell et al., 2016).

It is also necessary to mention the social implications of mental health diagnoses. In the narratives of the patients, we noticed the risk of medicalization (Holmqvist, 2009) or psychologization (Mulvale, 2016) of social life, understood here as the phenomenon of attributing social problems to an individual's illness or disorder. This phenomenon, also covered in this case as ‘stressism’ (Becker, 2013), involves interpreting problems that are primarily of socio-economic nature as mental disorders, with the consequent search for clinical solutions instead of addressing the underlying broader causes. This perspective hinders the adoption of a biopsychosocial paradigm for addressing mental health, which explicitly acknowledges the interaction of biological, psychological, and social systems (Roache 2019). However, it also downplays the role of States in ensuring the basic needs, including mental health, of their citizens, making it less likely that comprehensive public policies will be implemented to address the social determinants of health (Leiva- Peña et al., 2021). It is essential not to lose sight of the need for structural interventions that act as stressors, especially in developing countries, and to ensure the coverage of health-related expenses in the event of a diagnosis of ADT, that is in the same hierarchy of the MDE, and thus reducing the pressure to make a clinical diagnosis of MDE only for administrative purposes.

Our research provides a description of AD that could enrich current diagnostic criteria and measurement tools to truly capture its essence and give it the importance it deserves, which is a knowledge gap identified in the field of stress disorders (Morgan et al., 2022). Further research on the diagnostic process of AD is necessary to determine if it is perceived similarly in other contexts and to develop new measurement instruments that can identify it based on this reasoning model. Additionally, this information can be used to design medical education models that enable healthcare professionals to make this diagnosis with greater precision and confidence (Fei et al., 2014). As some participants explained, there is a late discovery of AD as a diagnostic possibility, which is why we need to enhance the training of psychiatrists and clinical psychologists by integrating the new diagnostic logic for AD into their curricula. This will include developing specialized training modules, workshops, and continuing education programmes focused on the accurate diagnosis and management of stress-related disorders and differentiation from MDE.

It is important to acknowledge some limitations of this study. The assessment of reasoning through recollection and hypothetical outcome formulation may not fully reflect the reasoning that occurs with a real patient in practice. Also, the perceptions of the clinicians included may reflect what happens at high-complexity care levels and specialized liaison psychiatry services, but extrapolation to the primary level could be partial. Thus, studies addressing actors at this level, where the prevalence of AD may be even higher but training in its management is lower, are needed. Furthermore, we view ourselves as researchers in training, and this research is the product of learning through which qualitative research skills were strengthened. In this sense, emerging theory should rather be seen as a nascent understanding, with gaps that could be completed and improved over time.

Conclusion

This study allowed us to understand the clinical reasoning of psychiatrists and psychologists, who narrated that in their clinical practice, they use a consequential and intersubjective knowledge-based reasoning to understand the experience of a stressful event and assign the label of AD when they consider it disproportionate. However, there is pressure that favours the overdiagnosis of MDE, which diverts therapeutic efforts towards the overuse of antidepressants. In this scenario, AD becomes necessary as it denotes the need to work on coping and intervene in the stressor itself and should be considered as a diagnostic possibility in the same hierarchy as MDE and not as a residual category.
