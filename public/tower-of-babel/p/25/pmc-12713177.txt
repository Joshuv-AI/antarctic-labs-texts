Impact of Sedentary Time and Physical Function on Health-Related Quality of Life in Hospitalized Patients with Cardiovascular Disease
Authors: Kazuhiro P. Izawa, Masahiro Kitamura, Asami Ogura, Ikko Kubo, Kodai Ishihara, Yuji Kanejima, Koichiro Oka, Shinichi Noto, Koichi Naito, Peter H. Brubaker, Hitomi Nagashima, Hideto Tawa, Daisuke Matsumoto, Ikki Shimizu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigated the association between health-related quality of life (HRQOL), sedentary time, and physical function in hospitalized patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD) undergoing phase I cardiac rehabilitation (CR).MethodsThis observational study enrolled CVD patients between October 2020 and September 2024. Participants were classified into low-HRQOL ( < 0.83) and high-HRQOL ( ≥ 0.83) groups based on the median EuroQol 5-Dimensions 5-Levels score. Clinical characteristics, physical function measures, and sedentary time were compared between groups. Multiple regression analysis was performed with the score as the dependent variable to identify predictors of HRQOL.ResultsThe final analysis included 544 patients (mean age: 69.1 years; male: 74.8%), of whom 49.0% were classified into the low-HRQOL group. Patients with low HRQOL showed significantly longer sedentary time and poorer physical function. Multiple regression identified employment status (unstandardized coefficient [B] = 0.068, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.007-0.130, P = 0.030), angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor and/pr angiotensin II receptor blocker use (B = 0.076, 95% CI: 0.026-0.126, P = 0.003), gait speed (B = 0.233, 95% CI: 0.109-0.358, P <0.001), and sedentary time (B = –0.0004, 95% CI: –0.0005to –0.0003, P <0.001) as significant predictors of HRQOL.ConclusionsExcessive sedentary time during hospitalization was significantly associated with lower HRQOL in CVD patients undergoing phase I CR. Reducing sedentary time, along with formulating functional and clinical strategies, may be vital for improving HRQOL in inpatient CR programs. These findings highlight the need for future studies to explore personalized CR interventions to improve long-term outcomes.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains a dominant global health concern that significantly contributes to morbidity, mortality, and escalating healthcare costs.1, 2, 3 Patients diagnosed as having conditions such as heart failure, ischemic heart disease, and valvular disorders frequently experience marked reductions in physical activity levels that can impair health-related quality of life (HRQOL).4, 5, 6 This decline in HRQOL exacerbates the burden of CVD, complicates clinical management, and worsens long-term prognoses. Thus, improving HRQOL is critical to enhancing patient satisfaction, reducing readmission rates, and promoting better survival outcomes.4,5

Being sedentary, defined as expending energy of ≤ 1.5 metabolic equivalents while sitting, reclining, or lying down,7,8 has emerged as an independent risk factor for adverse health outcomes, including obesity, diabetes, and CVD progression.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 Among hospitalized CVD patients, particularly older adults with mobility limitations, excessive sedentary time accelerates physical decline and critically impairs HRQOL.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 Despite its recognized impact, sedentary time remains an overlooked factor in current cardiac rehabilitation (CR) programs, highlighting the need for targeted interventions during hospitalization.15 Current CR programs have yet to sufficiently address sedentary time as a key modifiable factor during hospitalization.

Our previous multicentre cohort study investigated the relationship between sedentary time and physical function in hospitalized CVD patients undergoing phase I CR.15 Among 353 patients (mean age: 69.6 years; 75.6% male), excessive sedentary time ( ≥ 480 min/d) was significantly associated with a lower level of physical function, as evidenced by reduced Short Performance Physical Battery scores at discharge. Patients with excessive sedentary time scored 10.5 ± 2.4 points, compared to 11.2 ± 1.6 points among those with shorter sedentary time durations ( < 480 min/d, P = 0.001).15 These findings underscore the urgent need for strategies to reduce sedentary time during CR to enhance physical recovery and functional independence.15

Although CR programs incorporating exercise and education have demonstrated effectiveness in improving cardiovascular fitness, psychological well-being, and mortality risk,1,4,5 the specific impact of sedentary time on HRQOL during phase I CR remains insufficiently explored. This study hypothesized that among hospitalized CVD patients undergoing phase I CR, individuals with lower HRQOL would exhibit diminished physical function and an extended duration of sedentary time compared to patients with higher HRQOL.15

By elucidating the relationship between HRQOL, sedentary time, and physical function, this study aimed to provide valuable insights for optimizing CR strategies and promoting patient-centred care. Addressing sedentary time as a modifiable factor could improve HRQOL, reduce hospital readmissions, and foster better long-term outcomes in vulnerable CVD patient populations.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15

Methods

Study design and subjects

This prospective, multicentre cohort study (The Kobe Cardiac Rehabilitation Project for People Around the World [K-CREW]) was conducted at 4 affiliated hospitals (200-580 beds), each offering CR. Patients admitted primarily for cardiovascular indications between October 2020 and September 2024 were eligible for inclusion if they completed phase I CR, remained hospitalized for > 5 days, and were not admitted solely for catheter-based procedures, such as coronary angiography or percutaneous coronary intervention, without CR. The selection criteria for CR participation were determined based on physician recommendations and institutional protocols, which took into account factors such as clinical stability and the ability to participate in rehabilitation activities. These measures ensured that only patients with appropriate indications and sufficient physical and cognitive capacity were included in the study. This process may have introduced a potential selection bias, which is discussed in the limitations section.

Patients admitted for < 5 days were excluded to ensure sufficient time for comprehensive assessment and participation in phase I CR. Patients also were excluded if they had probable dementia (documented diagnosis or Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) score < 24), could not walk independently, declined to provide informed consent, died during the index admission, or had incomplete data.15,16 Further details regarding the selection process of study participants are provided in Figure 1.Figure 1Patient flow in the present study. Comprehensive phase I CR

Phase I CR was implemented in accordance with the Japanese Circulation Society guidelines for managing and rehabilitating CVD patients.1,2 CR sessions commenced within 3 days of hospital admission or cardiac surgery.1,2,15,16 Exercise programs were conducted 5-7 times per week, with intensity levels maintained between 11 and 13 on the Borg scale or adjusted to stay slightly below each patient’s anaerobic metabolism threshold. Each session comprised upper and lower body stretching for 10-20 minutes, up to 25 minutes of aerobic activities, such as warmup, aerobic exercises, and cool-down, and 10-20 minutes of resistance training, as supported by previous studies.1,2,15,16 All exercises were personalized to suit the condition of each patient.

Patients were provided with guidance and education on their disease, lifestyle modifications, nutritional improvements, medication adherence, and exercise routines. This multidisciplinary care was delivered by a team comprising physicians, nurses, registered dietitians, pharmacists, and physical or occupational therapists.1,2,15,16

Patient clinical characteristics

Clinical data, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), employment status, living arrangements, smoking habits, marital status, long-term care (LTC) level, primary diagnosis, comorbidities, Charlson Comorbidity Index,17 left ventricular ejection fraction, biochemical test data such as albumin and hemoglobin, and medications prescribed upon admission, were extracted from medical records by a designated researcher. In addition to the medical record review, at hospital discharge, patients were actively evaluated via completion of the MMSE questionnaire under the guidance of the study team, to assess their cognitive function. This data collection, including both medical record extraction and direct patient interaction for MMSE assessment, was conducted by a single trained researcher, thereby ensuring consistency in the process.

EuroQol 5 Dimensions 5 Levels score

The EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L) as an index of HRQOL is a standardized, self-administered questionnaire developed to assess a patient’s current health status. Owing to its strong validity and reliability, the EQ-5D-5L is widely utilized in both clinical practice and research settings.18, 19, 20 The EQ-5D-5L encompasses the following 5 dimensions of HRQOL: mobility (ability to move and perform physical activities); self-care (capacity to manage personal care tasks, such as bathing and dressing); usual activities (engagement in daily routines, including work, study, household tasks, and leisure activities); pain/discomfort (experience of physical pain or discomfort); and anxiety/depression (emotional state related to worry or sadness). Each dimension is rated on 5 levels of severity, ranging from having no problems to having extreme problems.18, 19, 20 Participants evaluate their current health condition, and the responses are used to generate a HRQOL index score ranging from 0 (equivalent to death) to 1 (representing full health). This index is derived from a predetermined value set that reflects societal preferences for various health states.18, 19, 20 We assessed this outcome at discharge.

Gait speed (GS) and Five-Times Sit-to-Stand Test (FTSST) to assess physical function

As indices of physical function, GS and the FTSST were evaluated at discharge in the present study.16,21, 22, 23 These measures were chosen for their established validity and reliability in assessing mobility and lower-extremity strength, which are essential components of physical function in CVD patients.

For GS, each participant was instructed to walk a 6-meter distance as fast as possible without falling. The time required to walk the 4-meter distance in the middle of the 6-meter-long walking path was measured by stopwatch. Two measurements were taken, with the shorter time being used.16,21, 22, 23 GS is widely recognized as a strong predictor of overall functional capacity and cardiovascular health, as well as adverse outcomes such as frailty and mortality.

For the FTSST, the patients were seated in a chair with their arms crossed across their chest. They were then instructed to stand up from the chair and sit down again 5 times as quickly as possible.16,21, 22, 23 The FTSST specifically evaluates lower body strength, balance, and functional independence, making it highly relevant for this patient population.

Sedentary time

Sedentary time was assessed with the Workforce Sitting Questionnaire24; the reliability and validity of this assessment have been established in Japan.25 This tool evaluates sedentary time over a 1-week period across 6 categories, as follows: driving, transportation, work, television viewing, personal computer or smartphone use, and other leisure activities.15,16 In this study, hospitalized CVD patients were not medically cleared to drive or use transportation during their hospital stay due to clinical and safety considerations. Consequently, these categories were assigned a value of 0 minutes.15,16 Responses for each category were gathered separately for workdays and non-workdays over a 7-day span. One researcher then calculated the total daily sedentary time by averaging the total time in minutes spent being sedentary across the 7 days (total sedentary time in minutes ÷ 7 days).15,16 Sedentary time, reflecting sedentary activity throughout the entire day during hospitalization, was used as the basis for classification.

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation or number (%). To examine differences between the low- and high-HRQOL groups, participants were divided into 2 groups according to the median EQ-5D-5L score,26 with both parametric tests and χ2 tests conducted. Additionally, variations in clinical characteristics between the 2 patient groups were evaluated with an unpaired t-test. To identify factors influencing HRQOL, multiple regression analysis was performed using the forced entry method, with the EQ-5D-5L score as the dependent variable. Clinically significant factors (age, sex, BMI) and variables significant in the 2-group comparison were included as explanatory variables. The explanatory variables were selected as multicollinear if the variance inflation factor (VIF) was ≥ 10; items with a VIF of < 10 were selected as explanatory variables.27 To assess the relationship between HRQOL, sedentary time, and physical function, linear regression models were applied. HRQOL (measured by EQ-5D-5L) was the dependent variable, and sedentary time and physical function (GS and FTSST) were included as independent variables.

Adjustments were made for potential confounding factors, such as age, sex, BMI, comorbidities, left ventricular ejection fraction, and biochemical markers. Statistical significance for all analyses was set at a P-value of < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using SPSS 29.0 statistical software (IBM SPSS, Armonk, NY).

Results

Flowchart of study participants

During the study period, 13,587 CVD patients were admitted to the affiliated hospitals. The inclusion criteria, which required hospitalization for > 5 days and participation in phase I CR, were met by 4641 of these patients. Following the exclusion of 4097 patients who met the exclusion criteria, 544 patients were ultimately enrolled in the study. The final cohort consisted of patients with a mean age of 69.1 ± 12.6 years, of whom 74.8% (407 patients) were male. Median outcomes (range) were an FTSST of 8.6 seconds (7.3-10.4), a GS of 0.99 m/sec (0.83-1.13), sedentary time of 480 min/d (300-720), and EQ-5D-5L score of 0.83 (0.70-1.00). Discussion

This study underscores the complex interplay among sedentary time, physical function, employment status, and medical management in influencing HRQOL of hospitalized CVD patients undergoing phase I CR. Interpretation of findings

This study supports the hypothesis that excessive sedentary time and reduced physical function significantly impair HRQOL in hospitalized CVD patients. Patients in the low-HRQOL group showed excessive durations of sedentary time, averaging 510.3 ± 246.9 min/d, compared to 464.6 ± 216.8 min/d in the high-HRQOL group (P = 0.022). Such prolonged sedentary behaviour is associated with adverse outcomes, including muscle atrophy and cardiovascular health deterioration, as shown in previous studies.15,16,28,29 Structured interventions to reduce sedentary time, such as incorporating scheduled activity breaks during hospitalization, can mitigate these effects and enhance functional recovery.28

Riopel-Meunier et al. highlighted in their review that sedentary time consists of not only "sitting" but also other sedentary activities, and its association with cardiovascular health outcomes is significant. Their findings emphasize sedentary time as an independent cardiovascular risk factor, suggesting that strategies to reduce and interrupt prolonged sitting are valuable managing CVD and enhancing HRQOL.30 This finding aligns closely with those of the current study, underscoring the critical role of sedentary time reduction in CR protocols.

However, the possibility of reverse causation cannot be excluded. Plausibly, patients with low HRQOL engage in prolonged sedentary activities and exhibit reduced physical function due to their compromised overall health status. This bidirectional relationship highlights the complex interplay among HRQOL, sedentary time, and physical functioning, underscoring the need for future longitudinal studies to explore causal pathways and provide a deeper understanding of these interactions.

Additionally, GS, a reliable predictor of frailty and mortality, was significantly slower in the low-HRQOL group (0.915 ± 0.260 m/sec) compared to the high-HRQOL group (1.040 ± 0.233 m/sec, P < 0.001). Reduced GS suggests compromised mobility and independence, reinforcing the importance of mobility-focused interventions, such as walking programs and early ambulation strategies during hospitalization, to enhance HRQOL outcomes.31, 32, 33

The FTSST, which evaluates lower-limb strength and functional endurance, also indicated significant deficits in the low-HRQOL group, emphasizing the need for resistance training within CR programs to improve physical function and daily independence.1,34, 35, 36 These findings collectively highlight the necessity of adopting a holistic approach in CR programs that addresses sedentary time reduction, physical function improvement, and targeted interventions to support long-term recovery.

Employment emerged as a significant contributor to HRQOL (B = 0.068, 95% CI: 0.007-0.130, P = 0.030). Employment provides psychological and social benefits, including enhanced self-efficacy and a sense of purpose.37,38 Employed patients are likely to experience better mental well-being, which positively impacts their overall recovery.37,38 CR programs should incorporate reintegration strategies, such as vocational counselling, to support patients in returning to their professional roles.

Use of ACE inhibitors and ARBs showed significant positive associations with HRQOL (B = 0.076, 95% CI: 0.026-0.126, P = 0.003). These medications play a dual role by alleviating symptoms and preventing cardiac and renal remodelling, thereby slowing disease progression.39, 40, 41 Their side effect profiles—such as manageable incidences of cough and potassium shifts—allow patients to maintain better daily functioning without substantial lifestyle disruption.40,41 This finding underscores the importance of effective medication management within CR protocols to enhance both immediate recovery and long-term outcomes.

These findings all highlight the need for a holistic approach to CR. By addressing sedentary time reduction, physical function, employment reintegration, and ACE-I and ARB management, CR programs can significantly improve patient-centred outcomes.

Implications for CR programs

Emerging technologies offer innovative solutions for improving CR protocols. Wearable activity trackers and mHealth tools provide real-time monitoring and feedback to patients, thus promoting adherence to sedentary time reduction strategies. These technologies can remind patients to engage in short bouts of physical activity that help to reduce sedentary time and enhance physical function.42, 43, 44, 45

Riopel-Meunier et al. emphasized in their review that sedentary time is a significant cardiovascular risk factor, comparable to other major risk factors. They highlighted the importance of adopting strategies to reduce and interrupt sedentary time, in alignment with the integration of wearable technologies discussed in this study.46 Incorporating these tools into CR programs aligns with the present study’s findings and holds the potential to revolutionize patient-centred care.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s strength lies in its robust design. The large sample size of 544 participants ensures the findings are well-powered and applicable across a diversity of ospitalized CVD patients. The use of validated HRQOL assessments and objective measurements of sedentary time and physical function enhance the study’s internal validity and provide reliable data to inform clinical practice.

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, as it is an observational study, causal relationships between sedentary time, physical function, and HRQOL could not be established. Although correlations were identified, the lack of causal evidence limits the overall strength of these findings. Second, potential confounding factors, such as psychological stress, nutritional status, and prehospitalization activity levels were not accounted for, and these may have influenced HRQOL outcomes. Future studies should include these variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of factors impacting HRQOL.

Third, sedentary time was estimated using the Workforce Sitting Questionnaire rather than being directly measured through accelerometry, which introduces potential measurement bias. Using wearable activity trackers, such as accelerometers, in future research would allow for more precise and granular data on sedentary time, enabling stronger correlations with functional recovery. Fourth, the study population consisted predominantly of male patients, thereby limiting the generalizability to female patients. This sex discrepancy reflects broader trends in cardiovascular rehabilitation (CR) programs and cardiovascular research, in which female patients are often underrepresented. Further studies are needed to investigate barriers to participation for female patients in CR and explore strategies to improve inclusivity.

Finally, although HRQOL was analyzed based on a median EQ-5D-5L score (0.83) for group classification, this approach simplifies the analysis and may reduce statistical power, compared to treating EQ-5D-5L as a continuous variable. Additionally, the chosen cut point may not fully capture the clinical significance of HRQOL variation, especially compared to the HRQOL in the general population not undergoing CR, as highlighted in the literature. Modeling EQ-5D-5L as a continuous variable in future studies could refine analysis and provide more-nuanced insights. Future research also should consider leveraging wearable technologies and conducting long-term follow-up studies to evaluate how postdischarge sedentary time and changes in functional activity impact HRQOL outcomes over time.

Conclusion

This study substantiates the hypothesis that excessive sedentary time and reduced physical function significantly impair HRQOL in hospitalized CVD patients. By addressing these factors through targeted interventions, such as early ambulation, resistance training, sedentary time reduction strategies, and advanced wearable technologies, CR programs can optimize functional recovery and patient-centred outcomes. Future research should further explore personalized and technology-driven CR strategies to ensure sustained improvements in post-discharge HRQOL.
