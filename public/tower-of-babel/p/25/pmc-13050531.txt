Pragmatic approaches for studying workflow in health informatics
Authors: Mustafa Ozkaynak, Saira Haque, Kim M Unertl, Craig Kuziemsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop recommendations to inform the development and use of pragmatic workflow approaches.Problem DescriptionWorkflow analysis in research is rigorous but also resource-intensive, requiring extensive expertise, labor, and time. However, workflow analysis is needed in clinics during technology implementation or evaluation over time in which such labor and time are not necessarily readily available. Pragmatic workflow approaches are required to enable individuals and organizations to conduct workflow analysis as needed in a timely and efficient way.ResultsWe adapted five principles to guide pragmatic workflow studies: relevance, actionability, comprehensibility, ethical reasoning, and iterative assessment. We also detailed a six-step guideline.Discussion and ConclusionPragmatic workflow approaches use fewer resources by appropriately trading off rigor to create empirically relevant workflow analysis and recommendations. However, managing this trade-off can be challenging. We can learn from case studies that apply these approaches to determine strategies for future modifications.

Introduction

Workflow is defined as the flow of work through space and time.1 Various components of workflow include temporal features (such as sequence and concurrency), activities, actors, information, and additional resources (eg, technology, materials). Lack of alignment between health information technology (HIT) and workflow is well-recognized as a reason that informatics interventions are not sustainable in practice.2,3 Much of the literature on HIT implementation is pilot studies or takes place in a research setting, with less emphasis on scaling up. This is problematic for examining workflows, since pilot studies and funded research often have additional resources such as personnel and expertise that are not available in clinical practice settings. However, when moving to broader implementation and use of HIT, health care organizations need a good understanding of workflow in practice at different times in a fluctuating unpredictable environment.

Analyzing workflow is an essential step to ensure that informatics interventions (including clinical decision support and large language models) are (1) congruent with ongoing practices,4–6 (2) effective,3 and most importantly (3) adopted.7 Workflow studies should be conducted both before implementing HIT to understand practice patterns so that HIT can be optimally implemented or following implementation to evaluate the fit between the workflow and HIT to avoid unintended consequences. One reason why workflow analyses are not consistently done is that they are not pragmatic outside of the research context. Thus, it is important to identify a set of approaches that can work in a real-world setting.

The purpose of this paper is to develop recommendations to inform the development and use of pragmatic workflow approaches to (1) support HIT implementation and (2) facilitate the generation of knowledge for HIT implementation. We define pragmatic as practical (needs reasonable resources) and useful (produce findings that are easy to use). Pragmatic workflow approaches can also facilitate learning health system (LHS) efforts.8 While these types of studies provide a snapshot of workflow at one moment in time, organizations must recognize that workflows are constantly evolving and iterative assessments over time are needed to stay updated on current practices and unexpected changes in the system. Building time, effort, and resources to allow iterative assessment are critically important to continuing to respond to ongoing changes in LHSs.

Need for pragmatism in workflow studies

The informatics literature and peripheral disciplines such as human factors engineering includes many approaches to study workflow.9 However, alternate approaches to workflow analysis are needed in contexts where operational constraints and resource availability present challenges to research-focused approaches, while still ensuring a rigorous assessment of workflow-related components. Pragmatic workflow approaches are therefore needed to enable individuals and organizations to conduct workflow analysis more often. Pragmatic workflow analysis refers to analyzing workflow with limited resources and by people who do not necessarily have extensive expertise in workflow research. Pragmatic workflow approaches also yield results that are directly applicable to the existing clinical situation. We envision that pragmatic approaches will be beneficial by (1) expanding workflow studies and making them routine by making novices in the field workflow data collectors and analyzers; (2) facilitating implementation efforts by making more workflow studies possible. Therefore, workflow from various clinical perspectives and under various conditions (such as shifts, individual care needs) could be captured.

To sustain changes related to HIT and associated workflows, it is important to understand both the ostensive and performative aspects.10 The ostensive aspects outline how workflows are supposed to work, and the performative ones are how they occur in practice. Understanding these, the differences between them, and why the differences occur is helpful for facilitation.11,12 In addition, workflows may change over time due to internal or external factors and understanding these changes and reasons for the changes can help inform modifications to HIT systems.13

Summary of literature on pragmatic approaches to study workflow

Pragmatic approaches have been used in studying IT implementation both inside and outside healthcare.14–16 Pragmatism has been advocated as a method to support other research paradigms such as interpretivism by creating a dyad of how end users interpret a health system and how they actually work within the system.15 Pragmatic approaches support the integration of data, user experiences, and methodological rigor.17 Pragmatic approaches differ from exploratory ones in that the former provides evidence about the adoption of an intervention into a real-life setting.18

Much of the work on pragmatic approaches in health informatics has been to develop pragmatic clinical trials.16,17 While workflow has been well studied in health informatics, it has not been looked at from a pragmatic lens. We posit that pragmatic approaches for studying workflow will allow us to balance methodological rigor with efficiency and context sensitivity for the settings where informatics applications are used. The availability of automated data collection (EHR audit logs and sensors) and analysis19 gives an opportunity to leverage large sample sizes with reasonable effort.

Recommendations for pragmatic workflow studies

We adapted the following principles to guide pragmatic workflow studies: relevance, actionability, comprehensibility, and ethical reasoning.20 Relevance refers to the connection between workflow analysis and a set of problems that are deemed to be important by stakeholders (patients, clinicians or healthcare administrators). Actionability refers to the extent to which workflow analysis informs informatics and other related interventions. Comprehensibility refers to the extent to which workflow study findings are disseminated at a level appropriate to the intended audience. Ethical reasoning refers to the degree to which the workflow study considers the range of roles (patients, nurses and physicians) affected and equitably weighs the consequences to all stakeholders. We added another principle, iterative assessment, which is common in human factors engineering. The first four principles can guide and serve as evaluation criteria for pragmatic workflow studies. A holistic view of stakeholders and workflows must be taken into account alongside all four principles. In addition, following an iterative approach to gather stakeholder feedback and integrate it into the understanding of the workflow is critically important for pragmatism.

We developed a six-step approach for pragmatic workflow studies (Figure 1) that utilizes the five principles explained in the previous paragraph. Our approach incorporates workflow analysis in day-to-day HIT usage, which ties back to the background literature in human factors engineering, workflow research, and implementation science.9 Our approach also translates pragmatic workflow approaches from quality improvement (QI) into HIT implementation. The five guiding principles ensure the sustainability of implementation. The novelty of this approach is placing workflow studies at the intersection of implementation science and health informatics and bringing the strengths and opportunities of each discipline.

Six-step approach for pragmatic workflow studies.

1. Identify the purpose and audience

1. Identify the purpose and audience

Workflow is a concept that can be used to examine any work phenomena including a specific task (eg, order entry or provider assessment), individual job or patient visit (eg, from entering an emergency department to being discharged home). The scope of the workflow study and its boundaries should be clear and relevant and congruent with ethical reasoning. The audience and purpose as well as relevance will identify the level of details of the studies. Many workflows are interrelated and encompass several components. Identifying the priorities is important at this stage to set boundaries on the workflows to be assessed. Then, the associated stakeholders and audiences can be identified.

2. Plan workflow elements to capture

2. Plan workflow elements to capture

Because so many workflows are interrelated, turning to the priorities identified in step 1 can be useful when establishing starting and stopping points for analysis. Then, we must define processes and identify relevant building blocks. While there are many aspects that are important, focusing on the components that inform priorities will eliminate collecting data on unnecessary elements. Possible building blocks of workflow (at any level such as individual, unit or hospital) may include activities, roles, temporality, material, information, documents. The main criteria in selecting building blocks are actionability and comprehensibility. Workflow can be defined in four levels.9

2.1. Individuals using technology: We must understand the interplay between people and technology. It is important to understand the users, their role and how they use technology. Studying workflow related to the use of technology (such as using a specific clinician decision support tool within EHR) can help identify user considerations and ultimately improve usability.

3. Gather resources for workflow study

3. Gather resources for workflow study

Studying workflow can feel daunting and labor-intensive. However, there may be existing data sources or processes that can be used to reduce this burden. Leveraging available data, processes, and other resources to capture building blocks identified in step 2 will minimize the requirement of additional resources to examine workflow. For example, EHR logs or RFID devices can be used to trace activity and provide a proxy for workflows.27 In addition, data around actions taken from alerts can help shed light on how they fit into workflows.28 If there are existing quality improvement efforts, population health initiatives or research projects, insights from those can be used to inform workflow study. This information can be augmented by EHR data such as self-service EHR reporting (eg, Epic SlicerDicer) and dashboards. This can help to narrow the focus for qualitative data collection, such as rapid cycle interviews.

4. Collect data

4. Collect data

Once data sources and methods are identified, data collection can occur. Using existing data can help inform new data collection that is needed such as additional reporting, interviews or observations. The investigator’s embeddedness in the process must also be considered. For example, if the investigator is part of the workflow, that might inform their perspectives on data collection. Depending on the findings, the stakeholders’ needs and purpose, there may be multiple modes of capturing qualitative data, such as interviews, observations, and focus groups.

5. Analyze data

5. Analyze data

Data analysis and capturing should occur iteratively. This way, findings from one aspect can inform the other. For example, if EHR reporting on alerts suggests that a specific alert is closed without being reviewed by 50% of users or 50% of the time, questions can be generated to explore reasons with the users or the system. The rule for stopping the iteration should balance pragmatism and rigor. One possible rule could be findings sufficiently informing implementation and operational goals. Workflow data could be analyzed visually, qualitatively and quantitatively9 to guide further questions or observations.

6. Communicate findings and gather additional feedback from stakeholders

6. Communicate findings and gather additional feedback from stakeholders

Reporting findings must include implications for different audiences. Reporting the results also provides a key opportunity to gather additional feedback to further inform the understanding of workflow. For example, administrators, technical staff and providers have different priorities and will do different things with the report. Pragmatic reporting should support operational decisions to inform identifying problems and finding solutions to informatics problems. Discussion

Pragmatic workflow approaches are needed to make workflow analysis more accessible to broader partners and purposes. We provided a guideline (Figure 1) in this paper for pragmatic workflow studies. Guidelines and tutorials are useful to facilitate workflow studies and help by identifying sub-steps to reach the aim in the most efficient way.29 Our guideline promotes a balance between methodological rigor, efficiency and context sensitivity by providing six general steps and five principles (relevance, actionability, comprehensibility, ethical reasoning and iterative assessment). These steps can organize workflow studies, and principles can promote the sustainability of HIT implementation.

While pragmatic approaches have been widely applied in implementation research, they have not been systematically extended to workflow analysis in health informatics. Our contribution lies in applying a pragmatic lens to workflow studies. This paper can inform workflow studies by providing action driven insights as opposed to sole interpretive descriptions of workflow.15 We offer a practical, scalable approach that enables non-experts to conduct workflow analysis, thereby democratizing and routinizing workflow assessment in clinical settings. This contribution moves workflow studies beyond research-intensive methods toward a model that is feasible, actionable, and sustainable in real-world healthcare environments. Future studies could expand case studies to include more context-specific challenges and pragmatic solutions.

Analyzing workflow should not be a one-time process but occur over time so that we can understand changing contexts. Then, pragmatic approaches can be useful for longitudinal analysis and inform the adaptation of HIT. Pragmatic workflow approaches can afford less resources by appropriately trading off rigor. However, identifying this trade-off can be challenging. We can learn from case studies that apply these approaches and decide what to take forward. Our guidelines can support individual efforts as well as the participation of multiple partners (eg, patients, nurses, providers) for co-analysis of workflow.

Our guidelines can also facilitate knowledge for HIT implementation by revealing changes in the workflow after implementation as well as the workflow that involves HIT. Such information can be used to identify adaptations, effective implementation strategies, and factors for sustainability. Our pragmatic workflow approach is scalable and adaptable to different scales and complexities of HIT implementation. Implementation of both small (eg, implementing a single pathway within an EHR) and major applications (eg, switching to a new EHR) can benefit from this approach.

Systematic evaluation of pragmatic workflow studies can utilize pragmatic approaches. As described in Step 6 (Figure 1), feedback can be collected from relevant parties. Already available data sources can be utilized for triangulation. As workflow studies are conducted at multiple time points, longitudinal comparisons inform systematic evaluations.

Our pragmatic workflow approach focuses on the operational level; however, decision-making around HIT procurement is frequently centralized at the executive level. Our approach cannot necessarily retroactively fix vendor selection decisions that are misaligned with user needs, but it can mitigate downstream risks by informing configuration, training, local adaptation, communication plans, and governance structures.

Workflow analysis is useful both pre-contracting (uncommon) of a HIT and post-contracting (more common), with unique implications and constraints in each phase. A pre-contract workflow analysis is ideal for ensuring alignment between vendor capabilities and actual work practices. A post-contract workflow analysis is still highly valuable for implementation planning, change management, risk mitigation, adaptation, and evaluation. Pragmatic workflow analysis helps organizations detect emerging fit problems early and identify feasible local workarounds or design adaptations within the constraints of the contracted system.

Our pragmatic workflow approach can be applied to both (1) In-house developed tools (where iterative co-design may be feasible) and (2) vendor-based systems (where configuration, governance, and local adaptation are the primary levers). While the degree of influence over system modification differs, the need for workflow alignment remains the same.
