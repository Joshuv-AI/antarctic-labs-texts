Detection of camel milk adulteration with buffalo milk during semi hard cheese production
Authors: Lívia Darnay, Annamária Barkó, Barnabás Juhász, József Surányi, Miklós Süth, Karina Ilona Hidas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Appearance of β-lactoglobulin refers to camel milk adulteration.•Ripened camel cheese is darker, yellower and firmer if mixed with buffalo milk.•Addition of buffalo milk led to cheese holes development during ripening.Keywords: Camel milk, Buffalo milk, Adulteration, Semi-hard cheese, ELISA, Cheese holes

Introduction

Camel milk is an excellent source of nutrition, especially for desert communities, as it has high concentration of calcium, protein, and Vitamin C, (Alhaj et al., 2022). Furthermore, it is known for its possible effect on treating autism, allergies, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases (Wajahat et al., 2022). Camel milk is difficult to process into dairy products due to its special casein composition, but it can be used to produce yoghurt, butter and soft cheese (Berhe et al., 2017). Mixing camel's milk with buffalo milk helps in the production of soft cheese and increases the dry matter content and yield of the cheese (Shahein et al., 2014). Camel milk is expensive as it poses excellent nutritional value, which however are difficult to access on an international level. However, it has a growing interest from consumer points of view, which is unfortunately encouraging the attempts for milk adulteration. Based our research review, the most researchers studying camel milk adulteration are from China (40 %), second most publications are deriving from Near-East (25 %) followed by other countries from all over the world (USA, Canada, Great Britain, Netherland, Australia), which are all listed in our reference list. Researchers suggest that camel milk powder can be altered with low price alternatives such as cow or ovine milk powders (Wu et al., 2022). Cow means cattle throughout the manuscript.

The methods for detection of camel milk adulteration are mainly different DNA based methods such as ordinary PCR (Wu et al., 2022), real-time PCR (Wajahat et al., 2022), triplex PCR (Hai et al., 2020). The other determination procedure is based on spectroscopy, such as near infrared (Mabood et al., 2017), and fluorescence-based methods (Ghohestani et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2021). Moreover, hyperspectral imaging (Ruan et al., 2024) and ELISA tests (Ringbio, 2025) are recently arisen alternatives. Based on our best knowledge, among the above-mentioned methods the most could detect 0.1 v/v % cows’ milk in camel milk with Triplex real-time PCR method based on using mitochondrial 12S ribosomal DNA. (Hai et al., 2020). Although in most cases camel milk adulteration is tested with cows and goats’ milk, there can be also other species in question.

Camel and buffalo are both present and used for dairy in several countries in the Near-East, however studies are rarely discussing the effect of mixing of these milk types on cheese production. Although there are few publications examining the influence of mixing these milk types on cheese properties (Shahein et al., 2014; Batool et al., 2024; Quadeer et al., 2024), but they are focusing exclusively on soft cheese. According to our best knowledge, no research was demonstrating the effects of buffalo milk on camel milk in case of long-ripened semi-hard cheese. Our study aims to determine the effect of buffalo milk on camel milk both in milk and in 6 months long-ripened cheese. This information may be important for tracing adulteration of camel cheese, but also for the understanding of advantages of declared application of buffalo milk on camel milk to widen its application range to semi-hard cheese products.

Materials and methods

Materials

Trappist cheese manufacturing and ripening

Semi-hard Trappist cheese was made from 5 liters of pasteurized (72 °C, 15 s) camel and buffalo’s milk in different ratios (CB50: camel:buffalo = 5:0; CB41: camel:buffalo = 4:1, CB32: camel:buffalo = 3:2; CB23: camel:buffalo = 2:3, CB14: camel:buffalo = 1:4; CB05: camel:buffalo = 0:5). The milk constituents of mixed milks were analyzed with Lactoscan MCC WS (Milkotronic Ltd, Nova Zagora, Bulgaria /homepage) and results are presented in Table 1. Pasteurized camel milk was sent by cold transportation from a German dromedary camel farm (Kamelfarm Marquard, Hittingen /homepage). Buffalo milk derived from a Hungarian organic farm (Virágoskút Ltd.., Balmazújváros /homepage), and cheese manufacturing was done in our Food Technology Laboratory following the manufacturing procedure of our previous study (Darnay et al., 2024). Trappist starter culture (BiaRia Ltd., Biatorbágy, Hungary) containing Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis, Lactococcus lactis subsp. cremoris, Lactococcus lactis subsp. Lactis diacetylactis, Streptococcus thermophilus was used for the inoculation of cheese milk. The applied dosage of calf rennet enzyme (BiaRia Ltd, Budapest, Hungary /homepage) was 0.21 IMCU/L (IMCU = International Milk-Clotting Unit), neither calcium chloride nor microbial transglutaminase was added during vat work. Ripening of cheese samples lasted 6 months using at same ripening conditions (T = 14 °C, RH= 85 %) as in the above referred publication. Cheese maturation was executed in Italian designed aging cabinet (Fimar Ltd, Via Del Tesoro /homepage). Ripened cheeses were subjected to analysis right after the ripening (see on Fig. 1). The results are averages of 3 replicated experiments.

Methods

ELISA test of adulterated milk

Camel Milk Adulteration Rapid Test Kit (Ring Biotechnology Co., Beijing, China) invented for detection of cow (0.3–0.5 %) and goat (0.1 - 0.2 %) milk in raw camel milk and was validated by Flanders Research Institute for Agriculture, Fisheries and Food (Duquenne et al., 2021). The ELISA kit was kindly provided for this research to define its detection limit in case of camel milk adulterated with buffalo milk after pasteurization.

Gel electrophoresis of adulterated milk

The protein analysis of buffalo and camel milk samples and mixtures of different proportions of these samples was performed by sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Handcast gels 6–15 % (w/v) (acrylamide/bis-acrylamide, 83 mm × 73 mm × 1.0 mm) were used for the electrophoresis and a vertical system was applied by SDS-PAGE during the measurement (Bio-Rad mini-Protean Tetra System, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). To prepare 15 % resolving gel solution the following were added: 6 mL 30 % acrylamide/Bis Solution (29:1), 2.7 mL 2 M Tris–HCl (pH 8.8), 75 μL of SDS solution 10 % (w/v), 3.09 mL deionized water, 75 μL of 10 % APS (ammonium persulphate), 9 μL N, N, N′, N′-Tetramethylethylenediamine. To prepare 6 % stacking gel solution the following were added: 0,75 mL 30 % acrylamide/Bis Solution (29:1, v/v), 495 μL 0.5 M Tris–HCl (pH 6.8), 42 μL SDS solution 10 % (w/v), 2,4 mL deionized water, 38 μL 10 % APS, 5 μL TEMED. 5 µl of each sample was added to 150 µl of 2 × Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad). The sample and sample buffer mixture were heated in a temperature-controlled water bath for 2 min at 100 °C. From the dilutions, 7 μL molecular standard and 5 μL of protein solutions were loaded into the wells and run for 45 min at 200 V. The gels were stained for 30 min with 0.2 % Coomassie brilliant blue (R-250, Bio-Rad). The range of molecular standard (Precision Plus Protein All Blue Standards, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) was 250–10 kDa. The stained gel images were captured using a Gel Doc XR+ System (Bio-Rad). The identification and the densities of the bands were quantified by using Quantity One and Image Lab 6.1 software programs (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Chemical and physical properties of ripened cheese

Physicochemical properties of cheese samples were determined after 6 months of ripening. Fat content was measured with gravimetric method according to ISO 23319 (2022). Protein level was determined by Kjeldahl method according to MSZ 1385–1987 Hungarian Standard. Manual pH meter (Testo AG., Titisee-Neustadt, Germany) was used to determine the pH level. The dry matter content was determined with an MB 160 moisture analyzer (VWR, Debrecen, Hungary) at 105 °C until constant weight (weight loss < 0.1 %/min). Histamine determination was based on HPLC-DAD according to EUR 26,605 (Stroka et al., 2014).

Color analysis of ripened cheese

Konica Minolta CHROMA METER CHR-400 tristimulus color measuring system (Konica Minolta Sensing Europe B.V., Nieuwegein, Holland) was used to measure the surface color of samples in CIELAB values (lightness, L*; redness, a* and yellowness, b*) with standard illuminant D65. Sample preparation and measurement conditions were strictly following the methodology given by our former publication (Darnay et al., 2022).

Texture analysis of ripened cheese

Texture Profile Analysis was performed with TA.XTPlus (Stable Micro Systems, Godalming, UK) Texture Analyser using a 500 N load cell. The cheese core samples were axially compressed two times to 70 % of their original height with 35 mm cylinder probe at a crosshead speed of 2 mm s−1. Detailed description of the sample preparation and measuring method can be found in our previous publication. (Darnay et al., 2019)

Sensory analysis of cheese holes of ripened cheese

Sensory analysis was organized with trained panellists with experience in cheese tasting (number: 15 persons; age: 23–70 years), both by academic stuff of University of Veterinary Medicine Budapest and cheese manufacturers from countryside (Körösladány, Ménfőcsanak, Mónosbél). Panellists had to evaluate the number of round shaped ripening cheese holes of 6-months-long ripened on 2 × 2 cm cheese samples on 1–5 scale (1: none- blind cheese,2: few -1–2 holes 3: ideal - 3–6 holes; 4: many (7–12) holes; 5: too many - > 12 holes)

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and visualization were conducted using Microsoft® Excel® (version 16.45) for chart preparation, while statistical evaluations were performed with SPSS Statistics software (version 27, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to identify statistically significant effects. The normality of residuals was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (with p > 0.05 indicating normal distribution), and variance homogeneity was examined using Levene’s test. When ANOVA indicated significant differences, post hoc analyses were conducted. Tukey’s HSD test was applied when variances were homogeneous, whereas the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used when variance homogeneity was not met. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Relationships between measured variables were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient.

Results and discussion

Results of SDS-PAGE analysis of adulterated milk

Fig. 2 shows the peptide profiles of pasteurized buffalo milk and camel milk, as well as their mixtures in different proportions. The first column is the molecular standard, which allows the identification of proteins between 250 and 10 kDa. Alpha-lactalbumin (α-la) was identified at slightly different molecular weights in buffalo milk (∼12 kDa) and camel milk (∼13 kDa), so the bands appearing close to each other in the mixture both represent alpha-lactalbumin. These values differ slightly from the results obtained by Hinz et al. (2012) who identified alpha-lactalbumin at 14.2–14.4 kDa. β-Lg was not detected in camel milk (El-Agamy et al., 2009; Kappeler et al., 2003) and since β-lg is a common allergen in cow milk and is not contained in human milk, camel milk appears to be a good alternative. In buffalo milk and mixtures, the 16 kDa molecular weight bands correspond to β-lg. By reducing the proportion of buffalo milk in milk mixtures, the amount of β-lg decreases proportionally and thus its color intensity. Accordingly a previous research reported a considerable band thickness change already by the addition of 10 % buffalo milk to camel milk (Qadeer et al., 2024).

Protein pattern of camel and buffalo milk samples based on gel electrophoresis.

1. CB05: 0 % camel milk - 100 % buffalo milk; 2. CB14: 20 % camel milk - 80 % buffalo milk; 3. CB23: 40 % camel milk - 60 % buffalo milk; 4. CB32: 60 % camel milk - 40 % buffalo milk, 5. CB41: 80 % camel milk - 20 % buffalo milk; 6. CB50: 100 % camel milk - 0 % buffalo milk.

The amount of total casein is higher in buffalo milk than in camel milk, especially with regard to the amount of β- and α-casein. The αS1- casein (28.4 kDa), αS2-casein (31.5 kDa), β-casein (30.2 kDa), κ-casein (19 kDa) were identified at slightly higher molecular weights than reported by El-Agamy et al. (2009); Hailu et al. (2016); Hinz et al. (2012). The present study also shows that camel milk is deficient in αS1- casein, which is also a major allergen, making it an excellent choice for the development of various allergy-free protein products (Yasmin et al., 2020). In camel and buffalo milk, as well as in their mixture, the 69 kDa molecular weight bands correspond to serum albumin (Sa), which is consistent with the results of Buffoni et al. (2011) identified using a chromatographic technique. Lactoferrin (Lf), identified in buffalo milk at a molecular weight of 76 kDa, was not identified in camel milk, similarly to β-lg and αS1- casein, and in the case of mixtures, the amount of lactoferrin decreases with increasing the proportion of camel milk.

Results of ELISA test of adulterated milk

The possibility of using ELISA test to detect cows milk in camel milk is known as it was established by selected β-Lg as the antigenic indicator (Chi et al., 2024). The immunosorbent assay applied in this study has been already validated for testing camel milk adulteration with cows milk, and also for goat milk. However the manufacturer was interested about the effect of buffalo milk on the developed ELISA test kit, which could be analyzed in detail in this study. Camel milk was mixed with known concentration of buffalo milk, which allowed us to be able to investigate the sensitivity of Ringbio Camel Adulteration test kit also for this type of camel milk aduteration. Results of ELISA based rapid adulteration test with different concentration of camel and buffalo milk are shown on Fig. 3.

Analytical results of ripened cheese

Physicochemical properties of ripened camel cheeses affected by buffalo milk additions are peresented on Table 2. Fat concentration of ripened cheese is affected by the proportion of buffalo milk in the cheese samples, increase of buffalo milk content resulted in elevated fat level in the final cheese. This linear correlation (R2=0.9856) is also visible from the significant difference in fat concentration between 100 % camel cheese (CB50) and samples made from minimum 60 % buffalo milk (CB 23, CB14, CB05). This tendency can be easily understood by the fact, that buffalo milk is rich in fat and this parameter is one of the main differences among the 2 applied milk types. In comparison to fat level, the protein concentration of final cheeses was not differing significantly in the range of 100–40 % camel milk based samples (CB50, CB41, CB32, CB23), however cheese made of 100 % buffalo milk was significantly different from all other samples. In case of total solids level, 3 statistically distinct groups were identified as cheeses made of 100–80 % camel milk (Group A: CB50, CB41), 60–40 % camel milk (Group B: CB32, CB23) and 20–0 % camel milk (Group C: CB14, CB05) according to Tukey post hoc test.

Although a significant decrease of pH was first observed with the addition 40 % buffalo milk, the pH values decreased slightly with increasing ratio of buffalo milk, therefore the lowest pH cheese was made of 100 % buffalo milk and was significantly different from all other samples based on Tukey test. Previous researchers (Shahein et al., 2014) found comparable linear correlation between the concentration of fat/protein and the increasing proportion of buffalo milk even after 2 months. Due to the long ripening time (6 months) the concentration of histamine was also measured in cheese samples. Based on our results only slight (1–2 mg/kg) concentration increase could be realised by the increased level of buffalo milk, but there is no significant difference between the experimental cheese samples. These results are not comparable with the related scientific studies as according to our best knowledge the effect of biogenic amine formation due to the addition of buffalo milk into camel milk was not yet studied.

The measured concentrations of histamine cannot be judged as no comparable study could be found in the scientific literature. The cheese yield of camel milk increased significantly with the addition of buffalo milk (Fig. 1). This is due to the high fat and protein level of buffalo milk (Table 1) which could be incorporated during camel cheese manufacture. Comparable study showed improvement in cheese yield of buffalo cheese if 4–8 mg/ml camel whey protein concentrate was added instead of camel milk (Elbarbary et al., 2019)

Results of color measurement of ripened cheese

Lightness (L*), redness (a*) and yellowness (b*) values in CIELAB color space were used to define the color difference between the ripened cheese samples due to the different ratio of camel and buffalo milk applied by manufacturing (Table 3). A statistically significant difference was observed among the different cheese samples for all the three color attributes. Lightness (L*) showed a decreasing tendency with the addition of buffalo milk to camel milk, indicating that increasing buffalo milk concentration in camel cheese may lead to darkening effect to the ripened cheese. According to the results of the Tukey test, even the addition of 20 % buffalo milk (CB41) caused a significant change in lightness. Redness (a*) values increased with the addition of buffalo milk, but significant change was only detected if at least 60 % buffalo milk was applied. If cheese was made exclusively from buffalo milk, it was resulting the most intense red hue and redness differed significantly from all other cheese samples.

Yellowness (b*) values showed also a consistent and significant increase as the proportion of buffalo milk increased. Furthermore in all cases, the differences between cheese samples were statistically significant, indicating that the application of buffalo milk has a remarkable effect on yellow color component of the final cheese. Color difference (ΔELab) was defined from the Lab values with equation (Mokrzycki et al., 2011) to see the perception of human eyes (Table 2). Based on our results, cheeses made of 20–40 % buffalo milk (CB41, CB32) resulted in clear difference in color compared to 100 % camel milk (CB50). Furthermore application of >60 % buffalo milk (CB23, CB14, CB05) led to color differences in which observer notices already 2 different colors.

Results of texture analysis of ripened cheese

Hardness and springiness were measured to define the influence of different ratios of camel and buffalo milk on ripened cheese samples as shown on Fig. 4, Fig. 5. Hardness values showed a significant linear correlation (R2 = 0.9655) with buffalo milk concentration, the higher the proportion of buffalo milk, the harder was the ripened cheese (Fig. 4). The observed clear trend is also confirmed by the significant difference between each sample. This indicates that buffalo milk had a consistent and significant firming effect on the texture of the samples. Springiness values also tended to increase (R2 = 0.9024) with the addition of buffalo milk (Fig. 5). However, in contrast to hardness, not all differences between sample groups reached statistical significance. Samples containing at least 40 % of buffalo milk exhibited significantly higher elasticity compared to the cheese made of 100 % camel milk. Furthermore, if the cheese was made of 100 % buffalo milk (CB05) it was significantly more elastic after ripening, than all other samples, suggesting a dose-dependent response in textural flexibility.

Results of sensory evaluation of cheese holes of ripened cheese

Sensory evaluation of apperance of ripening holes was based on perception of trained panellists and result is shown on Fig. 6. Specifically, samples containing at least 40 % buffalo milk (CB32) differed significantly from those semi-hard cheeses made of 100 % camel milk (CB50). This observation is unique and could be an initiative to be used to develop a methodology to control camel milk adulteration in given cheese types by trained human eyes and/or computers.

Conclusions

In this study, the aim was to find suitable methods to control camel milk adulteration with buffalo milk throughout the whole cheese manufacturing process, from the start (cheese milk) to the end (ripened cheese). Our research opened a new approach by following the changes of milk with both well-known classical analytical methods and new emerging approaches (ELISA kit) with understanding also the proteomic changes (SDS-PAGE). Moreover, the cheese samples were allowed to ripen for 6 months, which allowed us to follow the effect of aging on wide range of factors as physicochemical parameters, color, texture and cheese hole development. Based on our study, it can be concluded that even the addition of 20 % buffalo milk to camel milk can be traced after 6 months ripening period with objective measurement (lightness, hardness). Post hoc analysis using the Tukey test revealed a clear trend: increasing the proportion of buffalo milk in camel milk led to higher numbers of ripening holes in cheese samples. Therefore, the subjective judgement of trained panellists may help to recognize the use of 40 % of buffalo milk in ripened camel cheese based on number of appearing ripening holes.
