Using Cenobamate for managing idiopathic generalized epilepsy: A single‐center experience and systematic literature review
Authors: Nicole Woodrich, Andreas Alexopoulos, Dileep Nair, Imad M. Najm, Vineet Punia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the single‐center experience of Cenobamate's (CNB) off‐label use in adults with idiopathic generalized epilepsy (IGE).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective review of electronic health records within a large healthcare system to identify IGE patients prescribed CNB. Extensive clinical and medication‐related data were extracted, including monthly seizure frequency at baseline, 3, 6, and 12 months, and at last follow‐up. We used CNB retention, a marker of its potential effectiveness and tolerability, for 12 months as the primary outcome of interest. Statistical analysis, including Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, was performed to estimate CNB retention over time. We also performed a systematic literature review investigating seizure reduction after CNB use in IGE.ResultsSixteen IGE patients (median age = 36 years; 11 females) treated with CNB were included. Twelve (75%) had JME. Median epilepsy duration was 18 years, and a median of 8 ASMs were tried before CNB use. Among the 14 patients with at least a 12‐month follow‐up, 8 (57%) achieved the primary outcome. Median CNB retention based on survival analysis was 34 months, and 31% of patients stopped CNB within 3 months. At 3, 6, and 12 months, ≥50% seizure reduction was seen in 75%, 86%, and 63% of patients, respectively. Of 8 patients on CNB at last follow‐up (median CNB use of 21 months), 3 (38%) were seizure‐free. Median ER visits declined significantly in the 6 months post‐CNB (p = 0.028; Cohen's d = 0.66). Fatigue, dizziness, and cognitive symptoms were the most frequently reported adverse effects. Systematic review revealed 3 articles with 4 IGE patients whose seizure outcomes were similar to our findings.SignificanceThe study found CNB's use in IGE to be effective, including decreased ER visits. These findings provide preliminary evidence to suggest that off‐label use of CNB can be considered in drug‐resistant IGE patients.Plain Language SummaryCenobamate (CNB), an anti‐seizure medicine, was studied for off‐label use in 16 adults with drug‐resistant idiopathic generalized epilepsy (IGE). Most had juvenile myoclonic epilepsy and long‐standing seizures despite many prior treatments. Over half continued CNB for at least 12 months, and around 30% stopped within 3 months. At 3, 6, and 12 months, 63%–86% had at least 50% fewer seizures; three patients became seizure‐free. Emergency visits dropped significantly after starting CNB. Common side effects were fatigue, dizziness, and cognitive issues. These findings suggest CNB can help reduce seizures and healthcare use in adults with difficult‐to‐treat IGE, warranting further study.

INTRODUCTION

Idiopathic generalized epilepsies (IGE) comprise a clinically and genetically defined subgroup of generalized epilepsies, encompassing four classic syndromes—childhood absence epilepsy (CAE), juvenile absence epilepsy (JAE), juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME), and generalized tonic–clonic seizures alone (GTCA)—that share overlapping features and typically carry a favorable long‐term prognosis. 1 Overall, around 10%–15% of the people with IGE develop drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE). 2 Among adults with IGE, depending on the age of onset and subtype, drug‐resistant seizures occur in up to a third of patients, a proportion comparable to focal epilepsy. 3 , 4 , 5 Despite comprising 20% of the epilepsy burden, less than 1% of scientific literature is dedicated to IGE, partly because of poor awareness about DRE in this epilepsy type. 5 There is a dire need to explore newer therapies in IGE, especially in patients with drug‐resistant seizures.

Cenobamate (CNB) is a novel antiseizure medication (ASM) approved for the treatment of focal‐onset seizures in adults. CNB's dual mechanism of action—preferential inhibition of persistent sodium currents and positive allosteric modulation of γ‐aminobutyric acid‐A (GABA‐A) receptors—contributes to its broad‐spectrum antiseizure potential. While clinical trials have demonstrated its efficacy and safety in focal epilepsy, preclinical, and emerging real‐world evidence suggest that CNB may also be effective in treating generalized epilepsies, especially developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs). Several single case studies, small case series, and a systematic review of literature evaluating CNB's use in DEE published in the past two years show that CNB can be a well‐tolerated, efficacious ASM for seizure control in this population. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 However, CNB's use in IGE remains poorly documented. We aimed to fill this knowledge gap by evaluating a single center's experience of CNB's off‐label use regarding its effectiveness, safety, and retention in adult patients with drug‐resistant IGE. Our findings seek to inform CNB's potential role beyond its current indication and guide clinical management in IGE.

METHODS

After Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board approval, we screened the electronic health record (EHR) system of a large healthcare system for all adults (≥18 years of age) who started CNB in outpatient care from January 2020 to November 2024. Subsequently, a board‐certified epileptologist (VP) examined each patient's EHR to identify adults with IGE as defined by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) criteria. 1

Data collection

We extracted the following data elements from the EHR: demographics (age, sex, race), epilepsy features (type, age of onset, duration, seizure type, monthly seizure frequency at baseline, and below‐mentioned timepoints), ASM management (historically tried and concomitantly used ASMs), Vagus Nerve stimulation (VNS) use, CNB use (duration, adverse effects, maximum dose used), and treatment‐emergent adverse effects (TEAEs) at each epilepsy follow‐up visit. We also extracted the number of emergency room (ER) visits for breakthrough seizures in the 6 months preceding and following CNB initiation. We used the Care Everywhere feature of the EMR system Epic to find out ER visits outside of our health system. Epic accounts for more than 40% of the EMR market, including 43 hospitals in the state of Ohio, and connects 100% of all the US‐based hospitals that use Epic. 12 We also extracted information on patients' attendance in the pharmacist‐led clinic. This clinic at the Cleveland Clinic Epilepsy center aids the treating epileptologists in managing patients by allowing more frequent outpatient visits in patients starting an ASM with major drug–drug interactions, already on ≥3 ASMs, or who have poor ASM adherence.

Seizure reduction

Apart from baseline, monthly seizure frequency at 3, 6, and 12 months after starting CNB and at the last follow‐up visit was extracted. To minimize variability in seizure reduction calculations, we analyzed ≥50% seizure reduction at the abovementioned fixed time points compared to the baseline seizure frequency of GTC seizures, and those meeting the criteria were marked as 50% responders at these time points. We also calculated the total percentage seizure reduction at the last visit compared to the baseline monthly count. Although myoclonic seizures were not used for responder rate calculation when GTC seizures were present, we tried to quantify the myoclonic seizure burden based on the number of days with myoclonic seizures per month. 13

Primary outcome

Since ASM retention—defined as the duration of continuous use—reflects both efficacy and tolerability, we selected 12‐month retention after CNB initiation as the primary outcome. 14 We also estimated CNB retention over time for the study population.

Systematic literature review

We conducted a systematic literature review to identify published data on CNB's use in IGE patients. To maximize sensitivity, the search strategy included only the term “Cenobamate” without combining it with Boolean operators linked to IGE‐specific terms. Four databases were searched—OVID, Scopus, Web of Science, and MEDLINE. PRISMA guidelines were followed. 15 Eligible studies included case reports or case series where seizure outcomes after CNB use in IGE patients could be determined. The title and abstract screening step excluded meeting abstracts, book chapters, review articles, animal studies, and non‐English articles. A full‐text review followed. We also examined the reference list of these articles to find relevant articles.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized the patient and clinical characteristics of the study population. Given the small sample size and potential for non‐normal distributions, we summarized continuous variables using median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized using proportions and percentages. To explore factors associated with 12‐month CNB retention (primary outcome), we performed univariable comparisons between patients who remained and those who discontinued CNB before 12 months. Covariates analyzed included: age, sex, epilepsy type (JME vs. other), family history of epilepsy, epilepsy duration, seizure frequency, number of concomitant antiseizure medications (ASMs), maximum CNB dose, pharmacist clinic attendance, and concomitant clobazam use. The latter was included based on empirical evidence of increased adverse effects seen during their concomitant use due to pharmacokinetic interactions. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using univariable logistic regression. p‐values were derived using Wilcoxon rank‐sum or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. We used the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test to compare paired counts of ER visits before and after CNB initiation. The effect size was estimated using Cohen's d. p‐values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. We did not adjust for multiple comparisons due to the exploratory nature of the analysis.

To evaluate CNB retention over time, we used Kaplan–Meier survival curves, where the primary event was CNB discontinuation. Patients still on CNB at their last follow‐up were censored at that time point. We estimated survival probabilities, i.e., population percentage on CNB, at 3, 6, and 12 months, and final follow‐up, with 95% confidence intervals derived from the Greenwood formula. Apart from Kaplan–Meier‐based retention estimates, we performed a post‐hoc analysis to calculate restricted mean survival time (RMST) to quantify the average time patients remained on CNB during the first 12 months of treatment. RMST calculates the area under the survival curve up to a prespecified time horizon (τ), providing an interpretable estimate of average treatment duration that accommodates right censoring. We chose τ = 12 months to reflect the primary outcome of interest. This method is particularly robust in small samples or when median survival or confidence intervals are not estimable. All analyses were conducted using R (v2024.12.1 build 563). 16 The analysis used the following packages: ggplot2, table one, and survival.

CNB use and seizure response

Figure 2 shows a 50% responder rate status for each patient at 3, 6, and 12 months after the start of CNB use. It was achieved by 9 out of 12 patients (75%; data unavailable for patient #1) at 3 months, 7 out of 10 patients (70%; data unavailable for patients #2 and 13) at 6 months, and 5 of 8 patients (63%) at 12 months Figure 4. Eight patients were continuing CNB at the last follow‐up (Figure 2), with a median CNB use of 21 (11.5–37) months. Their monthly seizure reduction compared to baseline was 25% in 1, 50% in 2, 75% in 2, and 100% in 3 patients Figure 4. ER visits significantly decreased from a median of 1 (0–2.5) in the 6 months preceding CNB initiation to a median of 0 (0–1) 6 months after initiation (p = 0.028). The standardized mean difference (Cohen's d) was 0.66, indicating a medium‐to‐large effect on reducing ER visits.

Myoclonic seizure burden ranged from every other day (patient #2, 12) to daily (patients #9–11, and 14, who exclusively had myoclonic seizures). Improvement in myoclonic seizure control couldn't be quantified in these patients, except for patient #14, who exclusively had myoclonic seizures (see Table 1, Figure 2). None of the patients with myoclonic seizures had worsening of this seizure type.

Adverse drug effects

All but two patients reported TEAEs from CNB use. Figure S3 shows a heatmap of each patient's TEAE. The three most reported adverse effects were fatigue/drowsiness (n = 7; 44%), imbalance/dizziness (n = 5; 31%), and cognitive impairments (n = 3; 19%). Among the 7 patients reporting fatigue/drowsiness, 5 (71%) concomitantly took clobazam. One patient had DRESS (patient #10). She was in the third week of the CNB titration pack and was taking a 25 mg daily dose. She presented with complaints of sore throat, right ear pain, and headache for 3 days. The rapid Streptococcal test was positive in the clinic, and she was started on Amoxicillin. She was recommended to watch out for any rashes, which she developed overnight. She had a prior history of DRESS 4 years ago while taking lamotrigine. Like the current event, she was started on Amoxicillin after a sore throat and rash due to concern for a streptococcal infection, but a rapid Streptococcal test was not performed at that time. The next day, she was seen by the same dermatologist who treated her for DRESS 4 years ago. She was found to have a “worsening red and itchy rash,” which mainly affected her face and bilateral upper extremities. CNB was immediately stopped and she was started on a prednisone taper. On follow‐up 3 weeks later, she had fully recovered (Figure 4).

Systematic literature review

Figure S4 presents the PRISMA flowchart. After removing duplicates, 726 articles were screened; 22 underwent full‐text reviews, and 3 met the inclusion criteria. As detailed in Table 3, these studies reported on three patients with JAE and one adult with JME. 8 , 17 , 18 Agashe et al. found a 50%–83% reduction in GTCS and an 83% reduction in absence seizures in JAE cases, with one patient showing no change. Another JAE case, described as an incidental IGE patient included in a single‐center experience of using CNB in 121 patients, was reported to be “seizure‐free after 4 months of follow‐up.” The JME case reported seizure freedom from GTCS and marked reduction of myoclonic seizures. These patients had tried 8–10 ASM before starting CNB.

DISCUSSION

In this real‐world, single‐center study, we present the largest clinical case series evaluating the off‐label use of CNB in adults with drug‐resistant IGE. We found that CNB could be an effective ASM option in this population. In our cohort, patients had failed a median of 8 ASMs during a median epilepsy duration of 18 years. Notably, almost three‐fifths (57%) of the cohort achieved the primary outcome of 12 months CNB retention, like the retention estimated by the survival analysis. Seizure improvement was also notable, with more than 60% of patients showing ≥50% seizure reduction at 3, 6, and 12 months after starting CNB. With an estimated median CNB use of 34 months, all but one patient on the medication at the last follow‐up had ≥50% seizure reduction, including three who were completely seizure‐free. In conjunction with a significant decrease in ER visits, these findings support CNB's potential as a viable therapeutic option for treatment‐resistant IGE.

The favorable seizure outcomes observed in our cohort align with preclinical studies showing CNB's broad‐spectrum efficacy across multiple seizure types, including generalized seizures. Its ability to suppress photoparoxysmal responses in photosensitive epilepsy—a hallmark EEG feature of IGE—strongly indicates its potential utility in this population. 19 CNB shows effectiveness in animal models of generalized seizures, including the maximal electroshock seizure test for convulsive seizures and the subcutaneous pentylenetetrazol test for nonconvulsive seizures. 20 Its unique dual mechanism of action putatively offers a possible pharmacodynamic rationale for its efficacy in IGE. 21 Unsurprisingly, an ongoing randomized controlled trial (C025) and its open‐label extension (C033) are underway to evaluate CNB in IGE patients with GTCS. 22 , 23 Our findings provide complementary real‐world data that support the trials' scientific rationale and clinical feasibility. In the meantime, our findings suggest that CNB's off‐label use could become a valuable ASM option in managing drug‐resistant IGE patients, for whom the FDA has only approved two ASMs in the last 10–12 years.

A notable observation in our cohort was the high 50% responder rate at 3 months (75% of patients taking CNB at the 3‐month timepoint). However, this period also coincided with a sharp drop in retention, which fell by almost a third (31.2%; Figure 3). Nonetheless, this observation provides evidence of early efficacy of CNB in IGE patients with potential for long‐term seizure reduction in patients who can tolerate and continue this medication. The most common reasons for discontinuation were TEAEs, particularly fatigue and imbalance. These TEAEs are consistent with those reported in CNB trials and real‐world studies. 10 , 24 , 25 Among patients experiencing fatigue or drowsiness, 71% were on concomitant clobazam. In recent years, we have learned of CNB's significant interaction with clobazam, which increases N‐desmethylclobazam's serum concentration and causes significant fatigue and drowsiness. 26 Clobazam was co‐administered in 67% (4/6) of patients who discontinued CNB before 12 months and in 38% (3/8) of those who achieved 12‐month retention. This difference was not statistically significant, likely due to the small sample size. Nonetheless, clobazam, as in our cohort, is commonly used in this population. Based on empirical evidence, cautious CNB titration with timely clobazam reduction under close clinical monitoring could be the key to improving its retention and effectiveness. Serum drug level monitoring of clobazam is performed at our center based on the provider's discretion, which is typically during CNB up‐titration when drowsiness is encountered or at the end of titration. This strategy likely contributed to the 100% 12‐month retention among patients followed in the pharmacist‐led clinic.

Emerging evidence supports CNB's benefit in reducing healthcare utilization and costs in patients with drug‐resistant focal epilepsy. 27 Laskier et al. found improved quality‐adjusted life‐years with reduced cost in patients using CNB in the UK National Health Service compared to other third‐generation ASMs. 28 We observed a significant reduction in emergency room visits following CNB initiation. While our sample size limits definitive conclusions, the observed medium‐to‐large effect size (Cohen's d = 0.66) suggests that CNB may reduce seizure‐related healthcare utilization, especially in patients with a history of frequent GTCS. Given that almost two‐thirds of patients in our cohort had monthly GTCS and long disease duration, such reduced ER visits may also translate to improved quality of life. There are potentially other, even more critical benefits of reducing GTCS, given that the occurrence of GTCs is one of the highest risk factors for sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP). 29 A recent analysis of patients enrolled in CNB clinical trials suggested that its use may reduce the increased mortality risk seen in uncontrolled epilepsy patients. 30

Our systematic review found isolated case reports of CNB use in IGE patients, including one patient contained in a larger real‐world use series. 8 , 17 , 18 The 3 IGE patients had tried 8–10 ASMs before CNB initiation, which is similar to our cohort. 8 , 18 Seizure outcomes in those reports are also consistent with our findings, with marked reductions in seizures, including seizure freedom. Some studies incidentally report CNB use in IGE patients but do not provide meaningful details. In a German multicenter survey, Lauxman et al. identified 4 out of 121 (3.3%) patients with IGE on CNB and reported group‐level reduction in tonic–clonic seizures, including generalized and focal onset. 9 A retrospective US claims‐based study assessing CNB retention rates included 195 patients, of whom 7 (3.6%) had exclusively used ICD‐10 codes for generalized epilepsy. 31 An analysis of 2132 patients who received one or more CNB doses in phase 2 and 3 clinical studies included 114 (5.3%) patients with IGE, all of whom had GTC seizures. 30 Combined, these studies suggest that CNB is rarely used in IGE. While encouraging, these reports lack the clinical information and generalizability needed to guide clinical practice. Our series thus provides much‐needed data, bridging a critical knowledge gap and offering practical insights into the real‐world, off‐label use of CNB in IGE patients. The one patient who developed DRESS had a history of a similar adverse event in the setting of use of lamotrigine and amoxicillin prescription. The concomitant use of amoxicillin both times makes it difficult to ascribe a clear causality due to CNB. However, given that more than half of patients with DRESS can have a relapse and a majority are due to new drugs, it is advisable to avoid the use of CNB in patients with such a history. 32

Our study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, which may especially affect seizure frequency counts. Its sample size is modest, although several times more than the total number of cases published to date. The study only allows for univariate analysis, providing a simplified view of data, which can be helpful for initial exploration, but lacks the rigor and depth of multivariable methods. The study's single‐center design limits generalizability. Most of our patients had a potential 12‐month follow‐up, yet longer‐term efficacy and tolerability need future investigation. Moreover, we did not account for specific seizure subtypes beyond GTCs when considering responder rate. It is well known that absence seizures can be difficult for patients or caregivers to record accurately, but there was no suggestion of an increase in absence seizures or exacerbation of myoclonic seizures in the course of this real‐life empirical observation. Our experience does not shed light on the early CNB use in IGE, as patients tried it after a median of 8 ASMs. Additionally, although we used the CareEverywhere feature of the EMR to cast a wide net in capturing ER visits, we cannot rule out the possibility of missing some during the pre‐ and post‐CNS use period. Despite these limitations, our study provides compelling preliminary evidence supporting the off‐label use of CNB in IGE, particularly in patients with drug‐resistant epilepsy. These findings are hypothesis‐generating and provide real‐world data in a patient population with limited ASM options at high SUDEP risk. Future studies should aim to validate these findings in larger, multicenter cohorts and assess the long‐term impact of CNB on seizure control, quality of life, cognitive outcomes, and healthcare utilization in IGE.
