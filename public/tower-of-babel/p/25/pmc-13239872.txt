A Refined Method for Fecal Microbiota Transplantation Using Nutella
Authors: Ida W. Henriksen, Camilla H. F. Hansen, Janne Koch, Dorte B. Sørensen, Dennis S. Nielsen, Christina Bartholdy, Axel K. Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) in mice is an evolving research field, often employing oral gavage, which can be stressful and harmful for the animals and time‐consuming for technicians. Voluntary intake methods, such as delivery in Nutella, have successfully been used for drug administration in mice and may likewise be a promising tool to refine traditional FMT techniques. Nutella‐based FMT may improve animal welfare, reduce workload, and enhance animal safety without compromising microbiota engraftment rates. This study compared microbiota engraftment after mouse‐to‐mouse FMT in antibiotic‐treated BALB/c mice using three methods: oral gavage, pipette delivery, and voluntary intake of a Nutella‐inoculum mixture. Feces collected before and after FMT were analyzed using GridION Nanopore 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. Nutella‐based FMT showed similar engraftment rates as traditional methods i.e., oral gavage and pipette delivery. Oral gavage delivery resulted in greater variability and divergence from the donor composition compared to pipette delivery. Notably, the oral gavage group had the highest number of genera differing from the donor. Single‐dose FMT restored microbial diversity in one study, while it was less effective in another, suggesting to add a second dose of FMT. Nutella‐based FMT appears to be an efficient, reproducible, and less stressful alternative to forced‐feeding methods, warranting further exploration.

Introduction

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is an evolving technology where the entire gut microbial community is transferred from a donor to a recipient, leading to the introduction of a distinct microbial enterotype that can impact the physiological phenotype [1]. Clinically, this technique has gained valuable attention due to its outstanding therapeutic efficiency in Clostridioides difficile infections, where FMT from a healthy donor to an infected recipient increased the resolution rate from 23% to 94% compared to traditional vancomycin treatment [2]. Since then, the clinical interest of gut microbiota manipulation has expanded further to other disease areas such as anorexia [3, 4, 5], food or alcohol addiction [6, 7, 8], or metabolic disorders [9, 10]. Preclinically, FMT can be used as a valuable tool for studying microbiota‐dependent mechanisms and understanding how specific microbial compositions impact disease risks [11, 12, 13]. It can likewise be used to modulate phenotypes in animal models [14] or in data evaluation to control for variation introduced by microbiota differences and thereby improve statistical power [15].

This expanding field of research has resulted in increasing numbers of mice being subjected to forced oral administration, usually by oral gavage which is considered the “golden standard” method for FMT in mice [16]. Oral gavage requires restraining, which is time‐consuming for technicians and stressful as well as potentially harmful for the animal [17, 18, 19], and therefore, may compromise experimental results [20, 21]. Previous studies have successfully demonstrated oral administration of drugs through voluntary intake, with Nutella being a favorable enabler [22, 23], as it has also been shown to be less stressful [24]. This technique may have the potential to be utilized for administration of other substances, which are naturally ingested through the oral route such as fecal microorganisms in mice but it remains to be demonstrated.

We hypothesized that FMT, through Nutella administration, was (1) practically possible by habituating mice to the taste, and (2) resulted in similar engraftment success compared to traditional methods. The primary read‐out was engraftment rate (frequency of shared operational taxonomic units (OTUs) between recipient group and donor) as well as similarity in microbiota composition between recipient group and donor (Bray Curtice distance to donor).

Methods

This study is reported according to the ARRIVE guidelines [25]. The animal experiments received approval from the Animal Experiments Inspectorate, Ministry of Food, Fisheries and Agriculture, Denmark, and it was planned according to the PREPARE guidelines [26]. All housing, maintenance, and experimental procedures were carried out in compliance with the EU directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes and The Danish Animal Experimentation Act (LBK 474 from 15/05/2014).

Experimental Design

A first study (study 1) was conducted testing the success rate for FMT in mice using two different methods: Pipette vs. Nutella. Ten female and ten male BALB/cJBomTac mice (Taconic Biosciences, Lille Skensved, Denmark), aged 4 weeks at arrival, were block‐randomized, according to sex, into two groups. The pipette group was given FMT donor material by dropping the inoculum into the oral cavity using a pipette, while scruffing the animal (Figure 1A); the Nutella group was given the same donor material mixed in Nutella, by voluntary intake (Figure 1B). A second study (study 2) was conducted similarly but with different donor material and including an additional group where FMT material was allocated by oral gavage (Figure 1C), the “golden standard” for this procedure in mice [16]. In this study, 12 female and 12 male BALB/cJBomTac mice (Taconic Biosciences, Lille Skendsved, Denmark), aged 4 weeks at arrival, were randomly allocated in the three treatment groups: pipette, Nutella, or oral gavage. Both studies followed the same experimental timeline as illustrated in Figure 1D. During the 7 days acclimatization period, the mice received 1 g ampicillin/1 L drinking water. For 4 days prior to FMT, all mice were offered Nutella on an aspen gnawing brick in the cages to familiarize the mice with the Nutella taste (approx. 2.5 g/cage). 14 days after FMT, the mice were terminated by cervical dislocation. Feces were collected before antibiotic treatment, before FMT, and at termination.

Housing

The mice were housed in single‐sex groups (4–5 mice per cage) in open‐type 1284 L Eurostandard II L cages (Techniplast; Varese, Italy) with aspen bedding, an aspen gnawing brick (Tapvei, Harjumaa, Estonia), disposable shelters, mini fun tunnels, Nestlet nesting material, and EnviroDri (Brogaarden, Lynge, Denmark). The mice had ad libitum access to Altromin 1324 (Altromin, Sage, Germany) and fresh water. They were housed in our AAALAC accredited barrier‐protected facility, which maintained an average temperature of 21.8°C, an average relative humidity of 39.2%, and a 12‐h light/dark cycle (University of Copenhagen, Copenhagen, Denmark).

Fecal Microbiota Transplantation

The donor material for the first study comprised freshly collected feces pellets from two Swiss Webster mice (Taconic Biosciences, Lille Skendsved, Denmark) housed in our AAALAC accredited teaching facility (University of Copenhagen, Frederiksberg, Denmark). In an anaerobic chamber one pellet was vortexed in 1 mL sterile saline with 10% glycerol. This was done with three pellets in total, giving a total pooled value of 3 mL. The inoculum was transferred to the animal facility on wet ice. In contrast to the first study, the donor material for the second study was not prepared anaerobically and comprised caecum content from one BALB/cAnNRj female mouse (Janvier Labs, Le Genest‐Saint‐Isle, France) diluted approx. 1:20 directly after sampling in a 10% glycerol/PBS mixture and stored at −80 degrees for approx. a year until use. For the Nutella group, the inoculum (the same as for the other groups) was mixed 1:4 in Nutella in a weighing tray and transferred to a 2 mL syringe with a spoon, making it possible to allocate a precise amount.

The oral gavage group and the pipette group received 100 μL of the inoculum using a metal gavage or a pipette tip respectively. The Nutella group received 400 μL Nutella‐inoculum mixture, resulting in 100 μL inoculum allocated on the inside of a plastic tunnel in a clean empty procedure cage without a lid.

Fecal Microbiota Analysis

DNA Purification and 16S rRNA Amplification

Fecal sample DNA from recipient mice was isolated using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (47,016; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. For the donor material in the first study, DNA extraction was performed on 100 μL of the FMT inoculum, whereas in the second study, DNA was isolated from three separate replicates of 100 μL inoculum and three replicates of fecal samples collected from the donor mouse. The quality and concentration of the extracted DNA were evaluated using a Varioskan Flash Multimode Reader alongside an Invitrogen Qubit 1X dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A near‐full length of 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene amplicon was sequenced with GridION (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) as previously descibed [27].

16S rRNA Gene Amplicon Sequencing and Bioinformatics

The sequencing library was processed on a GridIONX5 platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) utilizing an R10.4.1 flow cell. Sequencing data acquisition was executed with MinKnow software (v23.07.12), followed by base calling using Guppy (v3.3.3) in high‐accuracy mode. Subsequent analysis included generating an abundance table from the raw FASTQ files using the Long Amplicon Consensus Analysis (LACA) pipeline [28], available on GitHub (https://github.com/yanhui09/laca). Taxonomic assignment of quality‐filtered reads was performed utilizing the SILVA database [29].

Data processing for the analysis was conducted in R, focusing exclusively on bacterial domain sequences, while singletons were excluded. To calculate the relative abundance of various taxa, the number of reads assigned to specific taxa in each sample was divided by the total number of reads, and operational taxonomic units (OTUs) below 0.01% relative abundance were omitted from the phyloseq object for downstream analyses. Rarefaction was carried out using the phyloseq [30] R package (sampling without replacement) to standardize the read depth to 10,000 reads per sample across both studies (Figs. S1A and S2A).

Following data preprocessing, downstream analyses were carried out based on a workflow adapted from Panah [31]. Alpha diversity was determined using Shannon diversity indices on rarefied data, excluding singletons. Beta diversity was assessed by calculating Bray–Curtis dissimilarity indices using the distance function in the phyloseq package. Engraftment rate was assessed by the frequency of shared OTUs between the donor and recipients. This was calculated using non‐rarefied data to preserve the presence of rare taxa. Singleton OTUs were removed to reduce noise.

To determine taxa that were differentially abundant at the genus level, the DESeq2 [32] R package was employed. Geometric means of sequencing read counts for each sample were first calculated to normalize the data and estimate the variance (dispersion) in the dataset. These normalized counts were used to evaluate the effect sizes for various factors in the analysis. To account for the False Discovery Rate, p‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini‐Hochberg (BH) method. Only taxa with adjusted p‐values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant and included in the visualizations.

Statistics

Data analysis and visualization were performed using the statistical software R. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used for statistical comparisons. In the first study, alpha diversity between FMT groups was compared using Welch's unpaired t‐test within each timepoint. Beta diversity indices, including the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix, were analyzed using Distance‐based Redundancy Analysis (dbRDA) implemented through the dbrda function in the vegan package [33] with 999 permutations. “Time” was used as a blocking variable, while FMT, time, and sex were incorporated as explanatory variables. The Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix was generated from log‐transformed rarefied OTU counts and evaluated for homogeneity of variance around centroids. A variance dispersion test was conducted using the betadisper function from the vegan package [33], with “time” applied as a constraining block for the 999 permutations.

Results

FMT Using Nutella Was Practically Successful and Time Saving

Study 1 was conducted to test whether it was possible to perform FMT by mixing the inoculum into Nutella for voluntary intake by the mice. It was observed that most of the mice were very interested in the Nutella‐inoculum suspension and ate the amount within approx. 5–15 min, whereas a few mice spent up to 30 min. It was also possible to dose the entire group at once (n = 10) using empty procedure cages and fun tunnels. Following dosing, fecal pellets could be collected from the procedure cage. Hence, it was very straightforward to collect feces samples, which is otherwise time‐consuming and potentially stressful for the mice. However, the Nutella method generated more equipment cleaning afterwards, and it is necessary to habituate the mice to the taste in advance.

FMT Using Nutella Resulted in Similar Engraftment Rate as With Forced Feeding

In study 1, FMT using either Nutella or a pipette resulted in similar restoration of the gut microbial alpha diversity (Shannon index) after antibiotics treatment (Figure 2A). The engraftment rate (frequency of shared OTUs with the donor) was significantly increased from before FMT to after FMT for both groups, whereas no significant differences in engraftment rate were observed between the two groups at any timepoint (Figure 2B). When analyzing the gut microbiota composition by a distance‐based redundancy analysis (dbRDA) using Bray–Curtis Dissimilarity indices, it was clear that both groups had a composition more similar to the donor after FMT; however, they still displayed differences from each other (Figure 2C). Sampling time (before ABX vs. after FMT) explained far the most variation between the samples (inertia = 1.55, F = 19.4, p = 0.002), whereas the FMT method only explained a small part of the variation (inertia = 0.16, F = 2.04, p = 0.039). There was also a notable interaction between the sampling time and the FMT method. Specifically, the FMT method had a significant effect only after FMT (Figure 2C). Variation around the centroid was similar between both groups (Figure 2C and S1B). To more precisely assess whether the two groups were equally close to the donor composition, we measured the Bray‐Curtis distance to the donor sample. Both groups were equally close to the donor composition (p = 0.7, Figure 2D). Despite differences in the dbRDA model, a network analysis including a permutation test for pure edges indicated that the two groups were not significantly distinct from each other after FMT (Figure 2E).

A stacked barplot at family level indicated that the recipients got a more similar profile to the donor sample after FMT: for instance reduced levels of Akkermansiaceae and Tannerellaceae but increased levels of Lachnospiraceae (Figure 2F). To deepen more into significant changes between the two groups, a differential abundance analysis was used. Only two families were differentially abundant between the pipette and the Nutella group (not graphed but indicated with * in Figure 1F legends), whereas four genera were differentially abundant (Figure 1G): Alloprevotellaceae and Muribaculaceae were downregulated in Nutella mice, whereas Rikenellaceae and an unspecified Prevotellaceae were upregulated compared to pipette mice.

FMT Using Oral Gavage Resulted in Higher Variability and a Gut Microbiota Composition More Distinct From the Donor Profile

Since oral gavage is a very commonly used method for FMT in mice, we aimed to reproduce the results from the first study, now also including a group receiving the material through oral gavage. Despite similar experimental design, it was not possible in this study to fully restore the gut microbial diversity after antibiotics treatment, as in the first study. This resulted in reduced alpha diversity (Shannon index) 14 days after FMT; however, not different among treatment groups (Figure 3A). Similarly, the engraftment rate (frequency of shared OTUs with the donor) was equal between treatment groups (Figure 3B).

When analyzing the gut microbiota composition by a distance‐based redundancy analysis (dbRDA) using Bray–Curtis Dissimilarity indices, it was clear that the microbiota composition changed in response to FMT. However, the microbiota composition was significantly different from the donor profile at both timepoints (Figure 3C). Again, sampling time (before ABX vs. after FMT) accounted for the most variation between samples (inertia = 0.51, F = 33.2, p < 0.001). Next, sex explained some variation significantly (inertia = 0.16, F = 10.4, p < 0.001). Interactions between sampling time and FMT method existed, where FMT method (as expected) only explained variation significantly after FMT. Interestingly, the variation around the centroid was larger for the oral gavage group compared to the pipette group (Figures 3C and S2B). Additionally, the oral gavage group had a significantly longer distance to the donor composition when compared to the pipette group, whereas the Nutella group had a similar distance to the donor as the other two groups (Figure 3D). Despite the dbRDA analysis showing significant differences in gut microbiota composition between the treatment groups after FMT, a network analysis including a permutation test for pure edges analyzed for each timepoint separately, showed that the groups did not differentiate significantly from each other (Figure 3E,F).

We next wanted to assess the differences in microbiota composition between donor inoculum (from caecum content) and feces sampled from the donor, and we analyzed it in triplicates. Visualized on the dbRDA plot, the triplicates clustered quite similarly with minor distance between the inoculum and the donor group (Figure 3C). A differential abundance analysis showed that eight genera were more abundant in the feces samples compared to the inoculum (Figure 4A).

We also compared the relative abundance after FMT between FMT groups and the donor (all donor replicates grouped) (Figures 4B and S3). Anova tests revealed FDR corrected significance for 14 out of 22 families as indicated by stars on the heatmap (Figure 4B). For instance, the donor samples had more Marinifilaceae, Prevotellaceae, and Bacteroidaceae but less Lactobaccilaceae than the other groups. The oral gavage group had reduced levels of Rikenellaceae and Ruminococcaceae compared to the donor and the other two groups. To dig more into which genera differentiated from each FMT group and the donor feces respectively, differential abundance analyses were conducted. The oral gavage group differentiated the most from the donor samples with 15 genera. Nutella mice differentiated with 13 genera and pipette mice differentiated the least from the donor samples with only 10 genera (Figure 4C–E). Compared to the donor feces samples, all three groups had fewer Muribaculum, Alloprevotella, an unspecified group of Prevotellaceae, and Butyricicoccus but more Anaeroplasma, Marvinbryantia, and Erysipelatoclostridium (Figure 4C–E). Monoglobus and Agathobacter were reduced in Nutella mice but not in the other groups, whereas oral gavage mice had less Ruminococcus and Subdoligranulum but more Limosilactobacillus compared to the donor, which was not the case for the other groups.

Discussion

We investigated whether FMT, administered through Nutella, was practically feasible and would lead to similar engraftment rates compared to traditional methods. The Nutella method resulted in engraftment rates comparable to traditional methods in both studies. We reported engraftment rates at around 70%–75%, which is within the range of observed rates from other studies [16, 34, 35]. However, reported engraftment rates vary substantially depending on taxa level, as well as experimental factors such as donor‐recipient compatibility, microbiota depletion strategies, age, strain, inoculum preparation techniques, route of delivery, and dosing strategies [16, 36].

We observed that FMT through oral gavage resulted in a higher degree of inter‐individual variation and exhibited a greater divergence from donor microbial composition when assessing distance‐to‐donor measures and number of genera differentiating from the donor. Prior studies have demonstrated that oral gavage is a highly stressful procedure for mice [20, 21] and that stress influences gut microbiota likely through the gut‐brain axis [37, 38]. Wang et al. [39] compared gut microbiota in male mice subjected to either chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) or kept as controls for 4 weeks. In stressed mice, reduced levels of Clostridiales and increased levels of Lactobacillaceae were observed. This is in agreement with the observations in our study in which the oral gavage group had reduced levels of Ruminococcus and Subdoligranulum belonging to the Clostridiales order and increased levels of Limosilactobacillus belonging to the Lactobacillaceae family. These observations were restricted to the oral gavage group and not to the other two groups and may explain the higher variation and reduced success rates observed in this group. Notably, the increased variation and distance‐to‐donor metrics were only statistically different between the oral gavage group and the pipette group, while the Nutella group showed no significant differences when compared to either of the other groups. Furthermore, analyses of engraftment rates, alpha diversity, dbRDA, and network analyses indicated no significant differences among the three groups. Therefore, the slightly reduced success rate observed in oral gavage mice may be interpreted as preliminary evidence, warranting further detailed investigation.

In general, the microbiota composition was altered following FMT in all mice, with minor differences observed between methods. The genera that were lost during the FMT procedure (i.e., reduced differential abundance compared to donor) were all strict anaerobic and may have been lost during aerobic handling of the donor material. All three groups lost Muribaculum, Alloprevotella and Prevotellaceae UCG‐001 as top three in the differential abundance test. Nutella mice, by contrast to the other groups, also lost the genera Monoglobus and Agathobacter, which could have been affected by the nutritional factors in Nutella. The high sugar and fat content in Nutella may have had a negative influence on fiber‐dependent taxa like Monoglobulus and Agathobacter [40, 41].

In the first study, alpha diversity was fully restored 14 days after FMT for all groups. However, alpha diversity was reduced in the second study, despite similar experimental procedures and environmental conditions. This discrepancy between the two studies may be attributed to differences in donor material. In the first study, freshly collected feces from two mice were mixed anaerobically with PBS and glycerol. In contrast, the second study utilized caecum content from only one mouse and was stored at −80°C in PBS and glycerol for a year, potentially compromising the vitality and quality of the donor material [42]. However, the Shannon index was not substantially different in the inoculum from the two studies (cf. the green line Figures 2A and 3A), indicating that the lower restoration of alpha diversity in study 2 was unlikely caused by differences in bacterial load between the two inoculums used but rather due to specific differences in the microbial composition. When comparing the relative abundance of some of the most abundant bacterial families in the different donor inoculums from the two studies (Figures 2F and S3), it appeared that the donor inoculum from study 1, in contrast to study 2, did not contain Prevotellaceae and Bifidobacteriaceae, which were in fact some of the families that failed to colonize in study 2. Additionally, Bacteroidaceae failed to colonize in study 2, but successfully colonized in recipients of study 1. Other studies have claimed Prevotellaceae and Bifidobacteriaceae are challenging to colonize in mice [43, 44]. So, the reason for the lower alpha diversity in study 2 may rather be a result of the presence of bacteria in that particular donor material that are difficult to colonize, which were not present in the inoculum from study 1. In study 2, a second FMT dose may have ensured complete restoration of the recipients' alpha diversity.

We observed in the second study that the alpha diversity in the donor feces was lower than the caecum inoculum but that the feces contained higher abundances of eight genera compared to the inoculum. The reason for this discrepancy may be that the Shannon index assessed alpha diversity is a measure of abundance and evenness. So, even though the inoculum has lower abundance of eight genera than the feces, the evenness may be higher. The caecum contents were directly stored in PBS and glycerol and snap frozen directly after sampling, which may have preserved more bacteria than the feces, that was longer time in aerobic conditions and stored in empty tubes [16]. Although Nutella proved to be practically feasible without compromising FMT engraftment rates, it has certain limitations. Nutella contains sugar, fat, and additives, which should be taken into account for studies focusing on nutritional parameters and metabolic measurements. In mouse experiments, however, FMT is given to pregnant dams, which is then passed on to their offspring, allowing these offspring to be used for experiments without any direct impact from Nutella or preliminary antibiotic treatment. Nutella might also influence the quality of the inoculum in different ways. On one hand, Nutella ingredients could influence the bacteria in the inoculum, which is why we aimed to mix it immediately before use to limit the bacteria's contact time with Nutella. On the other hand, Nutella could have protective benefits for the bacteria by increasing transit time, as chocolate is known to protect probiotics from stomach acid [45, 46]. As all groups were fed Nutella 3 days prior to the FMT, it is not possible here to investigate the broader microbial and physiological impact of Nutella intake. Future experiments should include a control group that receives no Nutella exposure, supplemented by in vitro studies to directly assess the influence of Nutella on microbial viability. Additionally, exploring other palatable, non‐nutritive media as potential alternatives could provide valuable insights for optimizing FMT mixing agents.

A practical limitation to microbiota studies is a potential cage‐bias. It was chosen not to co‐house mice from different FMT groups to avoid masking effects of FMT method.

FMT using forced feeding i.e., oral gavage or pipette requires restraint of the animal which had shown to increase stress‐like responses in rats such as heart rate, mean arterial blood pressure and movement in the cage [21]. Future studies, should directly aim to assess welfare parameters in mice engrafted using the Nutella method compared to forced‐fed methods to confirm these speculations. As researchers, we are obliged to constantly refine experimental procedures when conducting animal experiments [47]. Therefore, these preliminary results strongly encourage for further exploration.

This study specifically examined the success of engraftment in mouse‐to‐mouse FMT. In the field of translational medicine, there is significant interest in human‐to‐mouse FMT. However, such approaches present greater challenges and frequently result in lower engraftment rates [35]. For this investigation, mouse‐to‐mouse FMT was selected over human‐to‐mouse FMT due to its comparative simplicity and the ability to achieve higher statistical power, allowing for a focused evaluation of the methodologies. The findings of this study prompt compelling questions about whether these results could be extrapolated to human‐to‐mouse FMT studies, with potential implications for improving experimental animal welfare in translational research.

In conclusion, Nutella administration for FMT was practically feasible and resulted in engraftment rates comparable with traditional forced‐fed methods. However, the potential microbial and physiological impact of Nutella feeding persists as an area for further investigation, and its welfare implications remain to be clarified.
