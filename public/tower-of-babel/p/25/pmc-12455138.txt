Enzymatic characterization and proteomic profiling of venoms from the medically important Androctonus species
Authors: Alhussin MA Megaly, Masahiro Miyashita, Abdulaziz R Alqahtani, Mohammed Abdel-Wahab
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEgyptian scorpions of the Androctonus genus (family Buthidae) produce life-threatening stings owing to their neurotoxic venom. However, the composition and enzymatic activities of their venoms remain poorly understood: We used electrophoresis to analyze the protein components of venoms collected from three Androctonus species: Androctonus amoreuxi, Androctonus australis, and Androctonus bicolor. Mass spectrometric analysis was performed to characterize the peptides present in these venoms. The phospholipase A2 (PLA2), hyaluronidase, and protease activities of the venoms were examined to gauge their potential contribution to venom toxicity. Finally, the antibacterial and hemolytic activities of the venoms were evaluated. The electrophoretic profiles of the three venoms showed features specific to each species, with distinct protein bands observed at 75, 74, 67, 48, 46, 40, and 28 kDa, along with a notable band above the 15-kDa mark. Liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry analyses were used to detect the presence of 369, 324, and 351 components in with molecular masses in the range of 500–10,000 Da in the venoms of A. amoreuxi, A. australis, and A. bicolor, respectively. Disulfide-rich peptides (three disulfide bridges) were abundant, but peptides without disulfide bonds were also detected in all venom samples. All three venoms exhibited hyaluronidase activities, whereas protease and PLA2 activities were either weak (at 1 µg and 10 µg) or undetectable, even at higher concentrations (up to 20 µg). All assays were performed using venoms standardized by dry weight to ensure consistent protein quantities. Crude venoms of A. amoreuxi and A. australis showed antibacterial activity against E. coli and B. subtilis (5–10 μg), whereas A. bicolor required 10 μg. Hydrophobic fractions (40–55 min) of A. australis alone retained this activity. This work furthers our knowledge of the enzymatic and peptide composition of Androctonus venoms, unveiling their potential in drug delivery enhancement and other biomedical applications. These findings will inform the development of better strategies for the treatment and prevention of scorpion envenomation.Keywords: Scorpion venom, Androctonus scorpion genus, Enzymes, Hyaluronidase, MS analysis

Introduction

Scorpion venom is a complex blend of various components, including enzymes, peptides, lipids, and other unidentified substances. “These venoms can trigger toxic reactions in humans, and understanding their makeup could play a valuable role in guiding the discovery and development of novel therapeutic drugs.1” Proteomic research indicates that scorpion venom from a single species can contain more than 100 unique peptides, usually falling within a molecular weight range of 1–9 kDa.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Additionally, some enzyme proteins in the venom may be composed of 120–370 amino acid residues.7

Scorpion venom peptides are generally divided into two primary groups based on their structure and function: those containing disulfide bonds, known as disulfide-bridged peptides (DBPs), and those lacking these bonds, referred to as non-disulfide-bridged peptides (NDBPs).8 DBPs, which are typically composed of 13–70 amino acids and stabilized by three or four disulfide bonds, represent a significant portion of scorpion venom.9, 10, 11, 12, 13 These peptides are primarily known for their neurotoxic effects, which they exert by targeting specific ion channels, including channels of sodium,14 potassium,15 calcium,16 chloride,17 and transient receptor potential (TRP) channels.18

(NDBPs), which usually consist of 13–56 amino acids, often adopt amphipathic α-helical structures, resembling those found in various cationic antimicrobial peptides, and display a remarkable structural flexibility. This structural versatility enables their diverse biological functions, including enhancing bradykinin activity, fighting microbial infections, influencing cell signaling pathways, causing red blood cell lysis, and modulating immune responses.8, 19, 20, 21 Consequently, they have attracted great interest because of their potential pharmaceutical applications, including use as antibacterial, antiviral, and anticancer therapeutics.18

While scorpion venom peptides have been widely researched for their broad range of biological activities;22 the protein constituents of the venom, particularly enzymes, remain relatively understudied despite their important roles in various physiological processes.23

Scorpion venom enzymes include proteases, PLA2, hyaluronidases, phosphatases, acetylcholinesterases, lysozymes, and metalloproteinases.24, 25, 26, 27 Although the functions of some of these enzymes remain unexplored, their presence has been verified through transcriptomic and/or proteomic analyses.25, 28, 29 Studying these enzymes is valuable not only for understanding their role in scorpion venom but also for discovering their potential biomedical applications. 30, 31, 32 Specifically, these proteins are believed to significantly contribute to several harmful effects, such as tissue necrosis at the sting site, destruction of red blood cells within blood vessels, and acute kidney injury.33 These outcomes are linked to their interactions with the skin, blood components, as well as the cardiovascular and central nervous systems.34

Scorpion venoms are enzymatic arsenals,23 and their enzymatic profiles vary according to the scorpion species. Some scorpion species contain PLA2, which is toxic to mammals and causes edema and/or hemolysis in mammalian erythrocytes.35, 36, 37 Hyaluronidases usually have an indirect effect; by increasing the absorption of active peptides, facilitating the rapid diffusion of toxic components, and inducing systemic reactions.38, 39 Except for snake venom enzymes, enzymes in the venoms of other animals have hardly been studied, and their biotechnological potential has not been fully exploited.23, 40

Of the 930 scorpion species inhabiting Africa, most belonging to the family Buthidae, 31 species are classified as Egyptian scorpions.41 These 31 species are classified into 15 genera encompassing four families: Buthidae, Euscorpiidae, Hemiscorpiidae, and Scorpionidae.42 The Buthidae family includes A. amoreuxi, A. australis, and A. bicolor (fat-tailed scorpions), which are widely distributed in the Middle East and North Africa. This family also includes Leiurus quinquestriatus, the most dangerous species with regard to human scorpionism. L. quinquestriatus has been extensively studied because its venom possesses toxic compounds of great medical value.43 Androctonus species are among the most medically significant scorpion species and are responsible for deadly or potentially deadly scorpion bites in North Africa.44 However, the venom of Androctonus species is poorly characterized, and little information is available about the enzymes and NDBPs in Androctonus venom.45, 46, 47, 48

This study aims to generally characterize the enzymatic and peptide components of venom of the three aforementioned scorpion species, using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and mass spectrometry (MS). We will identify the unique biochemical characteristics of these scorpions by comparing their enzymatic activities (hyaluronidase, protease, and phospholipase A2) and peptide profiles with those of other scorpions of the Buthidae family. A dual approach combining functional enzymatic assays with peptide fingerprinting will aid in identifying genus-specific venom properties that impact toxicity and potential biomedical applications.

Materials and methods

Extraction of venom from scorpions

Scorpions belonging to the species A. amoreuxi (Audouin, 1825), A. australis (Linnaeus, 1758), and A. bicolor (Ehrenberg, 1828) were collected from different sites in Egypt. Their venoms were obtained by applying gentle pressure to the venom glands with fine forceps and manually homogenizing the glands in purified water. The collected venoms were then spun at 14,000 rpm for a duration of 10 min in at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected, freeze-dried, and stored at (−20 °C) for potential future use.

Electrophoretic profile

Crude venoms (30 µg in 12 µL) were analyzed using sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) on 15 % (w/v) gels under nonreducing conditions, following the method of Laemmli (1970).49 Electrophoresis was performed alongside a prestained protein ladder marker (range 10–245 kDa, nacalai tesque, Kyoto, Japan) for determination of molecular weight. The gels were subsequently stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue G250 (1.25 g/L) and subsequently imaged. Protein band analysis was conducted using Gel Analyzer (GelAnalyzer 23.1.1 by Istvan Lazar Jr., PhD and Istvan Lazar Sr., PhD, CSc).50.

Liquid chromatography profile

Crude venom samples (10 μg) were injected into a reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) column (Grace Vydac Everest; 5 μm, 250 × 1.0 mm). Solvent A (0.1 % TFA in water) and solvent B (0.08 % TFA in acetonitrile) were pumped through the column at a flow rate of 0.05 mL/min, and elution was performed using a linear gradient of 5–60 % of solvent B over 55 min. Elution was monitored by recording the ultraviolet absorbance of the eluate at 215 nm.

Hyaluronidase activity

Hyaluronidase activity within the venom samples was assessed by SDS–PAGE, following the methodology outlined in a prior study.51 To facilitate this analysis, hyaluronic acid was incorporated into a 10 % resolving gel at a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. The crude venom was prepared by dissolving it in a 2 × Laemmli buffer under non-reducing conditions. This buffer contained 4 % SDS, 20 % glycerol, 0.004 % bromophenol blue, and 0.125 M Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), with a venom concentration of 10 µg. Electrophoresis was carried out at room temperature under a constant voltage of 90 V until the tracking dye had completely migrated to the gel's lower edge.

Upon completion of electrophoresis, the gel, measuring 10 × 8 × 0.1 cm, was subjected to two consecutive washes in 50 mL of 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 5.8) containing 0.15 M NaCl and 5 % Triton X-100. Each wash lasted for one hour. The gel was then transferred to 50 mL of the same phosphate buffer with a reduced Triton X-100 concentration of 0.05 % for an additional hour. Subsequently, it was immersed for 10 min in 50 mL of phosphate buffer at pH 5.8 without Triton X-100. All these steps were executed at ambient temperature with continuous agitation on a rotary shaker. This treatment facilitated the replacement of SDS with Triton X-100 and enabled gel equilibration in a buffer suitable for enzymatic activity assessment.

After equilibration, the gel was removed from the phosphate buffer, sealed in a container, and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. It was then washed twice, each wash lasting 15 min, in 50 mL of 0.015 M Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.95). Following this, the gel was incubated in a staining solution under gentle agitation for a minimum of 5 h. Finally, it was kept in the dark until it was ready for imaging.51, 52 Just before use, the staining solution was freshly prepared by mixing 5 mL of 0.1 % Stains-All (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in pure formamide—previously stored in a light-protected container—with an additional 5 mL of formamide, 20 mL of isopropanol, 1.5 mL of 1 M Tris-HCl buffer at pH 7.95, and enough deionized water to reach a final volume of 100 mL.53 Following the staining process, the gel was immersed in a destaining solution composed of 5 % formamide, 20 % isopropanol, and 0.015 M Tris-HCl (pH 7.95) to enhance contrast. This treatment allowed enzymatic activity bands to appear distinctly against a deep blue background formed by undigested hyaluronic acid. The molecular weight of the active enzyme band was estimated using prestained protein molecular weight markers, which remained clearly visible against the dark-stained background of hyaluronan.

Protease activity

The proteolytic activity of these venom samples was assessed using fluorescein thiocarbamoyl–casein (Thermo Fisher Scientific, located in San Jose, California, USA) as a substrate.54, 55 Each venom sample was thoroughly mixed with the substrate in individual 1.5-mL disposable polypropylene microtubes, which were sealed and incubated in a shaking water bath at 37 °C for 1 h. To terminate the enzymatic reaction, 120 μL of 5 % trichloroacetic acid was added to each tube. The mixtures were then left to stand at room temperature for a minimum of 15 min to allow protein precipitation. Following incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 3 min to pellet the insoluble proteins. From the resulting supernatant, a 30-μL aliquot was removed and diluted to a final volume of 2 mL using 500 mM Tris buffer at pH 8.5. Fluorescence measurements were carried out using a spectrofluorometer (RF-1500, Shimadzu), with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 490 nm and 525 nm, respectively. Caseinolytic activity was quantified by comparing the fluorescence intensity of each venom sample against that of trypsin, which served as a positive control. All measurements were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Phospholipase A2 (PLA2) activity

To evaluate the PLA2 activity in crude venom, 240 mg of agarose were dissolved in 30 ml of phosphate-buffered saline with a pH of 8.1 in boiling water bath. After that, the solution was cooled to 50 °C before adding 0.28 ml of the egg yolk (source of lecithin) diluted 1:4 with saline solution and 0.28 ml of 0.01 M CaC12 solution. This mixture (13 mL) was applied to plastic plates (90 × 80 mm), preheated to 50 °C, and allowed to gel. Subsequently, Hollows with a diameter of 3 mm were excavated and filled with 20 µl of venom samples. The plates were kept at 37 °C for 18 h and stained with Stains-All dye to easily detect the diameter of the cleared egg yolk substrate zone. The control sample was prepared at a volume of 20 µL using PBS. The experiment was carried out in duplicate form.51, 56, 57, 58, 59

Hemolytic activity

Sheep red blood cells (sRBCs) were isolated via centrifugation of sheep blood, washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), which comprised 35 mM phosphate buffer and 150 mM sodium chloride at a pH of 7.2, and then pelleted through centrifugation at 2000g for five minutes before being resuspended in PBS. A 50-micro-liter portion of crude venom in PBS was combined with 50 micro-liters of a suspension of sheep red blood cells at a concentration of 4 % (v/v) in a micro-tube, then incubated for one hour at 37 °C. Subsequently, the samples were spun at 2000g for 5 min, and then the resulting supernatants were poured into a 96-well plate where the release of hemoglobin was tracked by reading the absorbance of the liquid at 450 nm with a Benchmark microplate reader. Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity was assessed via a plate growth inhibition test conducted on a thin layer of agar. The bacteria Escherichia coli NBRC 3972 (a Gram-negative species), Staphylococcus aureus NBRC 13276 (Gram-positive), and Bacillus subtilis NBRC 3009, all housed at the NITE Biological Resource Center in Chiba, Japan, were employed in this test. The bacteria were grown overnight at 37 degrees Celsius in a Petri dish under aerobic conditions, followed by culture in liquid Luria Bertani broth consisting of 1 % tryptone, 0.5 % yeast extract, and 1 % NaCl. A culture was incorporated into warm (∼50 °C) Luria Bertani broth supplemented with 2 % agar at a concentration of 20 μL of bacterial culture per milliliter of medium. The resulting mixture was then poured into a Petri dish (with a diameter of 9 cm) to achieve a thin layer of agar approximately 1 mm in thickness. Between 1 and 2 µl of the venom samples, which contained approximately 5 µg per microliter, were applied to the agar surface. The plate was incubated at 37 degrees Celsius for 12 h, with the suppression of bacterial growth being documented.

Mass spectrometric analysis

The crude venom samples (2.0 µg) were analyzed using a high-resolution LC/MS system specifically an Orbitrap Exploris 240 instrument from Thermo Scientific. First, the venom samples were separated using a reversed-phase C18 column with specifications of 5 µm, C18, and 250 × 1.0 mm, which was provided by Vydac Everset. The column was eluted using a mixture of 0.1 % formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B) with a gradual increase in solvent B from 5 % to 50 % over a period of 50 min at a flow rate of 0.05 mL/min. All the raw data from the spectra acquisition were extracted from the files.

Alkylation and reduction of cysteine residues

Crude venom (100 μg) was dissolved in a buffer containing 0.13 M NaHCO3 (pH 8.5), 2.7 M urea, and 35 mM dithiothreitol. The mixture was first incubated for 1 h at 50 degrees Celsius, then it was treated with iodoacetamide, which was used at a final concentration of 125 millimolar, for another 1 h at 25 degrees Celsius. The samples were analyzed by LC/MS directly without prior purification.

Results

Electrophoretic profile

The SDS–PAGE Analysis of venom samples from A. amoreuxi, A. australis, and A. bicolor revealed proteins ranging in size from 7 to 91 kDa (Fig. 1). A total of 11 distinct protein bands were identified across a range of molecular weights: 91, 75, 74, 67, 48, 46, 40, 28, 12, 8, and 7 kDa (Table S1). A few faint bands over the 75-kDa mark were observed in the venom samples from A. amoreuxi and A. bicolor (Fig. 1). The venoms of A. amoreuxi, A. australis, and A. bicolor exhibited similar electrophoretic profiles, featuring protein bands in three distinct molecular weight ranges: low (7–8 kDa), intermediate (∼25–40 kDa), and high (∼75 kDa). Specifically, all three species shared common bands at 7 and 8 kDa in the low molecular weight range, along with bands near 25 kDa and 40 kDa in the intermediate range, and a prominent band at 75 kDa. Interestingly, the bands below the 10-kDa mark exhibited the highest intensity and were prominent across all three electrophoretic profiles. These bands likely correspond to the peptides.

Hyaluronidase activity

Hyaluronidase is commonly referred to as a “spreading factor” because it hydrolyzes hyaluronic acid, a major component of the extracellular matrix. This facilitates the diffusion of venom toxins through tissues, promoting their spread, leading to symptoms such as pain, inflammation, and diffuse edema at the envenomation site. The role of hyaluronidase in venom diffusion was assessed using zymography (SDS-PAGE with 10 % gel co-polymerized with hyaluronic acid). Interestingly, hyaluronidase activity was detected in all three Androctonus venoms, as evidenced by clear digestion zones at ∼ 40 kDa (Fig. 3). Notably, A. amoreuxi and A. bicolor exhibited strong activity, suggesting efficient venom spread. While A. australis exhibited slightly weaker activity, possibly due to lower enzyme abundance or structural variants (e.g., glycosylation differences). Remarkably, the consistent detection of hyaluronidase activity across A. amoreuxi, A. australis, and A. bicolor venoms suggests that this enzyme is a conserved spreading factor within the genus.

Proteolytic activity

Proteolytic enzymes in scorpion venoms contribute to prey digestion and venom spread, though their role in Buthidae venoms remains poorly characterized. Although viperid venoms contain potent metalloproteases that cause hemorrhage, scorpion venom proteases are typically less active. This study quantified protease activity in Androctonus venoms using ultra-sensitive fluorescence-based assays to evaluate their potential roles in envenomation pathology. The proteolytic activity of Androctonus species crude venom was measured, for the first time, by using fluorescein thiocarbamoyl–casein as a substrate material (Fig. 4). Fluorescence intensity was measured at 490 nm and compared against a trypsin positive control (1 µg). While all three Androctonus venoms exhibited caseinolytic activity, the observed effects were remarkably weak. Specifically, absorbance readings at 490 nm revealed minimal proteolysis at both tested concentrations (1 µg and 10 µg crude venom). This consistently low activity across species suggests that proteases have limited ecological or pathological roles in the envenomation of Androctonus.

Phospholipase A2 activity

Phospholipase A2 enzymes are key venom toxins that induce hemolysis and inflammation through membrane phospholipid hydrolysis. While they are prevalent in many scorpion venoms (e.g., Scorpio maurus), their presence in Androctonus species remains controversial. In the present study, the activity of PLA2 in crude venom samples was assessed via agarose gel incorporating egg yolk as a lecithin source and CaCl2 (Fig. 5, Table 1), where enzymatic hydrolysis produces clear halos around active samples. Under standardized conditions (20 µg venom, 0.01 M CaCl2, 37 °C/18 h), neither A. amoreuxi, A. australis, nor A. bicolor crude venoms exhibited detectable PLA2 activity (halo diameter = 0 mm). In contrast, the positive controls of bee venom at 1 μg and Scorpio maurus venom at 20 μg exhibited strong PLA2 activity, with distinct halo formation observed for both venoms. The absence of halos in Androctonus samples was consistent across three biological replicates, suggesting these species lack functional PLA2 enzymes. The consistent lack of PLA2 activity across the three Androctonus species (Fig. 5) underscores a unique venom strategy favoring neurotoxin dominance over broad-spectrum cytotoxicity. This adaptation likely enhances predatory efficiency against their preferred prey while shaping the distinct clinical profile of Androctonus envenomations.

Hemolytic activity

Hemolytic activity is a common feature of many scorpion venoms, often attributed to PLA2 and cytolytic peptides. In the present study, crude venom samples from all three Androctonus species were tested for hemolytic activity using fresh sheep red blood cells (sRBCs).51, 59, 60 After a 1-hour incubation at 37 °C with 4 % sRBC suspensions, none of the venoms induced significant hemolysis, even at the highest tested dose (100 µg). Spectrophotometric analysis (450 nm) of released hemoglobin revealed only 3 ± 0.5 % hemolysis (Fig. 6), a negligible effect compared with the positive control (1 % Triton X-100, 100 % hemolysis) and negative control (PBS, 0 % hemolysis). These findings strongly suggest that the observed lack of hemolytic activity is likely due to the absence or extremely low concentration of functional PLA2 isoforms, underscoring their neurotoxic prioritization.

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of the crude venom samples were tested against E. coli, B. subtilis, and S. aureus through the agar diffusion technique. In addition, venom fractions obtained between 40 and 55 min were also assayed; these fractions represent the hydrophobic components of the venom, likely including a high concentration of linear peptides with antibacterial activity (Fig. 2). The crude venoms from A. amoreuxi and A. australis exhibited antibacterial activity against E. coli at both 5 and 10 μg and against B. subtilis at 10 μg (Table 2). By contrast, A. bicolor venom exhibited antibacterial properties against both (E. coli & B. subtilis) only at 10 μg. No antibacterial activity was observed against S. aureus for all three venom samples at the tested doses. Moreover, only the fractions of A. australis venom collected between 40 and 55 mins exhibited antibacterial activity.

Mass spectrometric analysis

The total number of peptides found within the venom of each Androctonus species was estimated by LC/MS analysis (Fig. S1,2,3). Molecular mass distributions of the components found in A. amoreuxi, A. australis, and A. bicolor venoms are displayed in Fig. 7A. In the molecular mass range of 500–10,000 Da, 369, 324, and 351 components probable to be peptides were identified in the venoms of A. amoreuxi, A. australis, and A. bicolor, respectively (Tables S2, S4, S6). A. amoreuxi venom had a relatively higher number of components within a molecular mass range of 6001–7000 Da, whereas A. australis and A. bicolor venoms had a relatively higher number of components within a molecular mass range of 7001–8000 Da.

(A) LC/MS was used to detect the molecular mass distribution of venom components in A. amoreuxi, A. australis, and A. bicolor was detected using. (B) The number disulfide bridges was estimated by measuring the mass shift observed in LC/MS following the reduction and alkylation of cysteine residues. (C) Evaluation of disulfide-rich and −poor peptides in each species.

All venom constituents were treated with a reducing agent (Dithiothreitol) to determine the number of peptides containing disulfide bonds, and the resulting free thiol groups of cysteine residues were then modified with an alkylating agent (Iodoacetamide). These venom component derivatives were subjected to LC/MS analysis. The results revealed an elevated in molecular mass by 116.058 × n Daltons, due to the chemical modification of disulfide bonds via carboxyamidomethylation of two unbound thiol groups. A total of 109, 88, and 96 peptides from A. amoreuxi, A. australis, and A. bicolor venoms, respectively, were found to possess disulfide bonds (Tables S3, S5, S7). Interestingly, most of the DBPs across the three scorpion species were found to possess a framework of six cysteines, corresponding to three disulfide bridges (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, 19.9 %, 18.5 %, and 15.0 % of the components from the venoms of A. amoreuxi, A. australis, and A. bicolor, respectively, were found to be linear peptides without disulfide bonds (Fig. 7C).

Discussion

Animal venoms have become a key focus in pharmaceutical research because they are a rich source of bioactive compounds that can be developed into promising drug candidates. Various bioactive substances found in scorpion venom have been identified for their ability to combat microbial threats, suppress viral activity, treat malaria, and counteract cancer development.61, 62 In this study, we investigated the enzymatic activities of venom from three Egyptian scorpion species, namely A. amoreuxi, A. australis, and A. bicolor. Electrophoretic and mass spectrometric profiles of the three venom samples revealed distinct patterns unique to each species. SDS–PAGE was used as the initial method to fractionate the venom, showing that most proteins ranged between 10 and 75 kDa in size. Earlier research analyzing scorpion venom has frequently identified a predominance of proteins with molecular weights below 15 kDa.63, 64, 65, 66, 67, 68 These small proteins, primarily toxins mediate the main toxic effects during human envenomation.69, 70, 71 Additionally, recent studies have highlighted the potential of these small proteins as bioactive compounds with diverse pharmacological properties.72 The HPLC profiles of the three crude venom samples highlighted clear differences in the positions and intensities of peaks across the three scorpion species (Fig. 7). A higher number of compounds from A. amoreuxi venom eluted between 35 % and 40 % acetonitrile. By contrast, the HPLC profile of A. australis venom displayed a predominant peak between 38 % and 43 % acetonitrile, whereas a higher number of compounds from A. bicolor venom eluted between 40 % and 45 % acetonitrile. Notably, the HPLC profile of A. bicolor venom exhibited three distinct peaks between 42 % and 45 % acetonitrile, which were absent in the profiles of both A. amoreuxi and A. australis venom. These results suggest that the concentration of some venom peptides varies among the three scorpion species.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that high-molecular-weight enzymes, such as hyaluronidases, are universally present in scorpion venoms.34, 38, 39, 73 These enzymes function as spreading factors, enhancing venom diffusion through the tissues of the prey by breaking down the glycosaminoglycans in the connective tissue, thereby promoting systemic envenomation.39 Notably, scorpion venoms exhibit significantly higher hyaluronidase activity compared with snake venoms74, 75 and spider venoms.73, 74, 76, 77 The activity of hyaluronidase has been detected in the venoms of various scorpion species, with the molecular weights of the responsible enzymes ranging from 40 to 60 kDa.38, 39, 51, 68, 73, 78, 79 This enzymatic activity contributes to one of the main obstacles to effective patient treatment is rapid venom dissemination and, consequently, the quick onset of symptoms in patients with scorpion bite, both of which may be caused by the high hyaluronidase activity in scorpion venoms.22 The venom of Androctonus scorpions contains neurotoxins that primarily target sodium and potassium channels in nerve and muscle cells, disrupting normal nerve signaling and causing systemic symptoms.80, 81 Moreover, the potent hyaluronidase activity in all three venoms is associated with the rapid onset and systemic spread of these venom neurotoxins, leading to severe systemic symptoms, such as cardiovascular and neurological disturbances, which are common in acute Androctonus envenomations.75, 82 Mendoza-Tobar (2024) recently expanded this catalog by confirming hyaluronidase activity in both whole and soluble venoms of Centruroides margaritatus and T. pachyurus using zymogram assays. Consistent with these findings, our study detected hyaluronidase activity in all three Egyptian scorpion species examined (Fig. 2). These findings not only corroborate previous reports documenting the ubiquitous presence of hyaluronidases in scorpion venoms but also extend our knowledge about the venoms of previously unstudied North African scorpion species.

On the other hand, as known proteases degrade proteins and peptides, leading to tissue damage, hemorrhage, and disruption of cellular functions. Phospholipids in cell membranes are hydrolyzed by PLA2 enzymes, leading to cell lysis, inflammation, and the release of arachidonic acid, which is a precursor for pro-inflammatory.83, 84 The reduced protease and PLA2 activity likely result in less severe local tissue damage, but the presence of these enzymes in the venom still contributes to inflammation and pain.85, 86

Using fluorescein thiocarbamoyl–casein as a substrate, we demonstrated that the venoms of the three Androctonus species possessed weak proteolytic activity (Fig. 4). This result is consistent with previous studies, which have reported a variety of proteolytic activities in scorpion venoms.25, 34, 51, 61, 87 It is speculated that proteolytic enzymes in scorpion venom cause necrosis, hemolysis, and gangrene.88 Necrosis has also been documented as a symptom in humans stung by Hemiscorpius lepturus, and enzymes are directly implicated in this phenomenon.33 The venom of Tityus discrepans contains metalloproteases and serine proteases that demonstrate significant fibrinogenolytic properties, correlating with clinical manifestations of envenomation.87 Using zymography, T. serrulatus venom was shown to possess gelatinolytic activity.25 Some venom proteases have been implicated in hypotension and bradycardia following scorpion stings.28 Moreover, these proteases may undergo post-translational modifications, potentially activating toxin precursors.25 Although the precise function of proteolytic enzymes within scorpion venom has yet to be fully elucidated, current hypotheses suggest that they may increase tissue permeability, thereby promoting venom dissemination. Furthermore, these enzymes may exert direct cytotoxic effects and could contribute to the pathogenesis of pancreatitis observed in some cases of envenomation.25, 89

In our analysis, we found no signs of PLA2 activity in the venoms of A. amoreuxi, A. australis, or A. bicolor, even at the highest doses tested. The absence of detectable phospholipase A2 (PLA2) activity in the venom of the three Androctonus scorpion species suggests that hemolysis and membrane disruption may not be a primary toxic mechanisms in these species. This lack of activity is consistent with what has been seen in several other scorpion species,25, 28, 37, 68, 90 reinforcing the notion that PLA2-dependent toxicity is not a universal feature among scorpions. Interestingly, researchers recently isolated a PLA2 enzyme from the venom of the Egyptian scorpion Scorpio maurus palmatus.91 This particular enzyme showed the ability to break down red blood cells from humans, rabbits, and rats, likely through its interaction with phospholipid membranes—a mechanism also seen in similar venom components.91 Since PLA2 enzymes typically disrupt cell membranes by hydrolyzing phospholipids, their absence in Androctonus venoms implies alternative envenomation strategies, possibly favoring neurotoxins or other cytolytic peptides. Notably, certain alternative components may exert antimicrobial effects, since venom-derived peptides often exhibit dual functions in immobilizing prey and defending against pathogens.

In line with this idea, our study examined the antibacterial potential of crude venom from three Androctonus species against representative strains of Gram-negative (Escherichia coli) and Gram-positive (Bacillus subtilis and Staphylococcus aureus) bacteria. Venoms from A. amoreuxi and A. australis displayed dose-responsive inhibitory activity against E. coli at both 5 µg and 10 µg concentrations and showed measurable effects against B. subtilis at 10 µg (see Table 2). In contrast, the venom of A. bicolor demonstrated a more restricted antibacterial profile, exerting inhibition of E. coli and B. subtilis only at the higher 10 µg dose. Notably, only the venom fractions of A. australis collected between 40 and 55 min during chromatographic separation exhibited detectable antibacterial action. Fractions within this same time window from A. amoreuxi and A. bicolor lacked similar activity, suggesting that the linear antimicrobial peptides in their venoms elute earlier than 40 min, while the compounds active in A. australis likely correspond to disulfide-bridged peptides (DBPs). None of the tested venoms showed efficacy against S. aureus at the concentrations used. Although the overall antibacterial effects were relatively modest, these findings confirm the presence of bioactive molecules in Androctonus venoms with activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial species. This supports the increasing interest in scorpion venom–derived antimicrobial peptides due to their potent activity, reduced risk of resistance development, and novel biological mechanisms.92, 93, 94, 95, 96, 97 The differential activity patterns exhibited by the venoms from the three species suggest possible differences in venom compositions, warranting further investigation into the specific antimicrobial components in their venoms.

LC/MS analysis revealed a range of peptide profiles in the studied species, aligning with earlier reports.41, 98 and supporting the well-documented peptide complexity identified in MS studies of Buthidae venoms.41, 99, 100, 101, 102

A relatively high number of peptides had molecular masses ranging from 6001 to 8000 Da. These peptides are most likely long-chain scorpion neurotoxins with insecticidal activity, which have been detected in other Buthidae venoms.41, 103, 104 Linear peptides without disulfide bonds accounted for 10 %–15 % of all three venoms. These peptides are thought to have amphiphilic α-helical structures and may be responsible for the antibacterial activity observed in this study.

Conclusion

The venoms from A. amoreuxi, A. australis, and A. bicolor had distinct electrophoretic and chromatographic profiles, with shared weak proteolytic but unexpectedly strong hyaluronidase activities. The absence of PLA2 activity correlated with low hemolysis, while zymography confirmed hyaluronidase as a potential key toxin. Notably, all venoms exhibited dose-dependent antibacterial effects against Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains, with A. australis hydrophobic fractions (40–55 min) displaying particular potency. As far as we are aware, this work provides the first in-depth analysis of Androctonus venom composition using mass spectrometry. Through this approach, we identified a diverse set of peptides across all three venoms, with molecular weights ranging from approximately 500 to 10,000 Da. NDBPs, which accounted for 15–20 % of the total peptides, may be responsible for the antibacterial activity of the venoms. Furthermore, most of the DBPs contained six cysteine residues (forming three disulfide bridges), a characteristic feature of scorpion venom peptides. This study represents a masterful integration of classical biochemistry and modern proteomics to unravel the hidden complexities of Androctonus venoms. Our analytical strategy combines electrophoretic separation, functional enzymology, antimicrobial profiling, and high-resolution mass spectrometry to produce the first detailed molecular profile of these medically significant venoms. These findings provide critical insights into species-specific enzyme diversity and their potential roles in venom toxicity-knowledge that could inform the development of improved treatments and prevention strategies for scorpion envenomation.
