The global, regional, and national burden of acute lymphoblastic leukemia, 1990 to 2021: a cross-sectional analysis from the 2021 global burden of disease study
Authors: Chen Yuan, Meiping Zhu, Yi Shi, Qian Chen, Hongzhou Ye, Yinjie Ling, Jing Fang, Wenyuan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) remains a major public health challenge, yet its global burden and epidemiological trends are inadequately characterized. This study evaluates the global burden of ALL from 1990 to 2021 and projects trends through 2040.​​MethodsUsing data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2021, we analyzed prevalence, incidence, mortality, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and age-standardized rates (ASRs) for ALL, stratified by time, region, age, and Socio-Demographic Index (SDI). Advanced methods included estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), Joinpoint regression, decomposition and frontier analyses, and Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) modeling to quantify trends, inequalities, and projections.ResultsIn 2021, ALL caused 386,813 prevalent cases globally (10.3% increase since 1990) and 71,221 deaths (41.8% decline). Age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) rose from 4.1 to 5.4 per 100,000 (EAPC = 1.4), while mortality rate (ASMR) decreased significantly (EAPC = − 1.73). High SDI regions exhibited the highest ASPR (16.1 vs. 1.3 per 100,000 in low-SDI areas) but lower ASMR (0.5 vs. 1.3) and DALY rates (23.7 vs. 47.9). A bimodal age distribution peaked in children < 5 years (21.5 per 100,000), with male predominance across ages. Modifiable risks included high BMI (5.8% of global deaths) and smoking (15.4% in high-income North America). Health inequalities widened: the slope index of inequality for prevalence increased from 3.22 to 4.23 (1990–2021), and ASDR disparity reached − 30.97. Frontier analysis highlighted untapped ASDR reduction potential in low-SDI nations (97.17–136.34 per 100,000). Projections suggest a 50% global ASPR decline by 2040 (2.84 per 100,000), though regional disparities persist.ConclusionThe global ALL burden reflects progress and disparity: high-SDI regions face rising prevalence and aging-related challenges despite mortality declines, while low-SDI areas grapple with entrenched inequities. Age- and sx-specific patterns, alongside modifiable risks, necessitate targeted prevention and equity-driven policies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03683-w.Keywords: ALL, DALYs, GBD 2021, Health inequalities, Risk decomposition, Age-standardized rates

Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is a malignant clonal disorder arising from B- or T-lineage lymphoid progenitor cells, characterized by the abnormal proliferation of immature lymphoblasts in the bone marrow [1, 2]. Over the past four decades, treatment outcomes for leukemia have improved substantially, with the 5-year overall survival rate increasing from approximately 31% in 1975 to nearly 70% by 2009. However, this overall progress conceals significant disparities: while the 5-year survival rate for pediatric patients has approached 90%, it remains below 25% among individuals aged 50 years and older, underscoring the need for further optimization of treatment strategies in older patients (≥ 40 years) [3–5]. Moreover, although survival rates have improved across countries with varying income levels between 1995 and 2009, basic therapeutic regimens remain underutilized in several low- and middle-income regions, resulting in persistent disparities in treatment outcomes between countries [6].

Understanding the epidemiology of ALL is critical for informing healthcare planning and guiding resource allocation [7]. Historically, comprehensive global assessments of the epidemiology of ALL have been limited [8]. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study offers a standardized and systematic approach to quantifying the burden of disease, associated risk factors, and clinical outcomes across multiple countries [9]. However, since the release of the 2021 GBD update, no recent studies have addressed the global epidemiology of ALL. This study aims to address this gap by analyzing global epidemiological trends of ALL from 1990 to 2021 and forecasting disease burden through 2040.

Methods

The data for this study are derived from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 Study (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/), which estimates health indicators related to 371 diseases and injuries across 204 countries and regions from 1990 to 2021 [10]. The GBD 2021 emphasizes the necessity of prioritizing emerging and existing health threats in the global public health agenda. Data on acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) used in this study were obtained from this database.

The GBD study divides the world into seven super-regions, further subdivided into 21 GBD regions [10]. Additionally, the Sociodemographic Index (SDI) is an important composite indicator for assessing the socioeconomic development level of a country or region [11]. Based on SDI scores, GBD classifies countries and regions into high SDI, high-middle SDI, middle SDI, low-middle SDI, and low SDI regions to reflect health disparities across different socioeconomic levels.

Statistical analysis

Joinpoint regression analysis was applied to detect shifts in temporal trends. This approach divides the time series into multiple segments and pinpoints statistically meaningful turning points, allowing for the estimation of trend directions within each segment [12]. The main metrics include the Average Annual Percentage Change (AAPC), which summarizes the overall trend over an extended period, and the Annual Percentage Change (APC), which captures variations over shorter intervals. A positive or negative APC or AAPC signifies an increasing or decreasing trend, respectively. We utilized the Joinpoint software (version 5.3.0; National Cancer Institute, Rockville, MD, USA) to analyze global trends in age-standardized prevalence (ASPR), incidence (ASIR), mortality (ASMR), and DALYs (ASDR) from 1990 to 2021.

Health inequality refers to measurable differences in health outcomes among various population groups categorized by social, economic, geographic, or demographic factors [13, 14]. To evaluate the disparities in the prevalence, incidence, mortality, and DALYs of ALL, we employed two indicators: the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and the Concentration Index (CIX), which represent absolute and relative inequality, respectively [14]. The SII is obtained through a weighted regression model, capturing the gradient in health outcomes across the full spectrum of SDI rankings [15]. In contrast, the CIX is calculated based on the area between the concentration curve and the line of equality, using SDI to reflect how health outcomes are distributed among groups with varying development levels.

Decomposition analysis serves as an important statistical method for examining the underlying factors and their interactions that drive changes in health outcomes over time. It is frequently utilized in the fields of epidemiology and public health research [16]. In this study, to explore the primary contributors to the variation in ALL-related DALYs between 1990 and 2021, we conducted a decomposition analysis to separately quantify the effects of population increase, demographic aging, and shifts in disease patterns on the overall change in disease burden.

Frontier analysis is a valuable approach for evaluating performance by benchmarking against top-performing counterparts, often used to assess healthcare system efficiency and guide optimal resource use [17]. In this research, we utilized the non-parametric Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) to model a non-linear frontier curve based on development levels, reflecting the theoretically lowest achievable ALL burden [18]. By integrating ASDR with SDI data from 1990 to 2021, we conducted frontier analysis to explore the potential for burden reduction across countries at varying development stages. To improve the reliability of our estimates, we also carried out 1,000 bootstrap replications for validation.

The disease burden of ALL was primarily estimated through prevalence, incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). We downloaded data from the GBD database, including 20 age groups (grouped in 5-year intervals), age-standardized rates (ASR), and estimates for all age groups, along with corresponding 95% uncertainty intervals (UI).

Assuming the natural logarithm of ASR changes linearly over time, the annual trend in ASR can effectively represent stable changes in the indicator over a period. Thus, we applied estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) analysis to assess trends in ASR changes during specific periods.

Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess the association between SDI and ALL burden. Furthermore, we applied the Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model, integrated with the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) method, to predict future trends in the ALL burden [19]. All computations were conducted using the R software (version 4.3.3).

Global level

As of 2021, ALL remains a major global public health concern. The total number of cases reached 386,812.6 (95% UI: 266,572.8–486,274), marking a 10.3% increase compared to 1990. However, the ASPR showed only a modest rise, increasing from 4.1 per 100,000 population in 1990 (95% UI: 3.5–4.9) to 5.4 per 100,000 population in 2021 (95% UI: 3.7–6.9). The EAPC for ASPR was 1.4 (95% CI: 1.21–1.59), indicating a slow but steady upward trend (Table 1; Fig. 1). In contrast, the global number of new ALL cases in 2021 was estimated at 103,727.2 (95% UI: 71,606.7–122,516.7), representing a 28.57% decline from 1990 levels. Correspondingly, ASIR declined from 1.8 per 100,000 population in 1990 (95% UI: 1.4–2.2) to 1.4 per 100,000 in 2021 (95% UI: 0.9–1.6), with an EAPC of −0.64 (95% CI: −0.74 to −0.54), reflecting a downward incidence trend. Mortality also declined substantially.

Global and regional trends in acute lymphoblastic leukemia burden: prevalence, incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (1990–2021)

Trends in acute lymphoblastic leukemia prevalence, incidence, mortality and disability-adjusted life-years from 1990 to 2021

The number of ALL-related deaths in 2021 was estimated at 71,220.7 (95% UI: 49,169.2–83,167.3), a 41.84% decrease from 1990. The ASMR declined from 1.6 per 100,000 in 1990 (95% UI: 1.2–2.0) to 0.9 per 100,000 in 2021 (95% UI: 0.6–1.0), with an EAPC of −1.73 (95% CI: −1.76 to −1.70), indicating a consistent decline in mortality. In terms of DALYs, the burden attributable to ALL in 2021 was 3,719,776.7 (95% UI: 2,587,454.9–4,353,159). The ASDR declined from 94.9 per 100,000 in 1990 (95% UI: 72.5–123.3) to 48.9 per 100,000 in 2021 (95% UI: 34.1–57.5), with an EAPC of −2.11 (95% CI: −2.15 to −2.06), indicating a substantial reduction in global disease burden over the past three decades (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Regional level

The global burden of ALL exhibits substantial regional disparities and is closely associated with SDI levels. The highest ASPR was observed in high-middle SDI regions, reaching 16.1 per 100,000 (95% UI: 9.4–22.7), while the lowest occurred in low SDI regions at 1.3 (95% UI: 0.8–1.6) (Table 1; Fig. 1). Temporal trends in ASPR across SDI levels indicated varying stages of epidemiological transition. In low SDI regions, despite a decreasing trend (EAPC: − 0.89; 95% CI: − 0.96 to − 0.82), the absolute number of prevalent cases increased from 9,329.8 in 1990 (95% UI: 5,355.7–15,542.2) to 15,460.6 in 2021 (95% UI: 9,593.3–19,874.1), likely due to population growth and improved case detection (Table 1; Fig. 1). In contrast, high-middle SDI regions experienced the most notable increase in ASPR, with an EAPC of 3.66 (95% CI: 3.36–3.95). ASPR rose from 65,921.7 (95% UI: 55,319.2–78,491.0) in 1990 to 131,718.5 (95% UI: 78,703.1–179,580.4) in 2021 (Table 1, Supplementary Figure S1A), potentially reflecting enhanced diagnostic capacity, increasing life expectancy, urbanization, and shifts in lifestyle-related risk factors such as diet, stress, obesity, and exposure to carcinogens. Regarding mortality and disease burden, both ASMR and ASDR varied significantly by SDI. Middle SDI regions reported an ASMR of 1.1 (95% UI: 0.7–1.3) and an ASDR of 58.8 (95% UI: 40.3–68.8), while high SDI regions showed lower values of 0.5 (95% UI: 0.4–0.5) and 23.7 (95% UI: 21.4–25.5), respectively (Table 1; Figs. 1 and 2). The highest ASDR was recorded in upper-middle SDI regions at 2.6 (95% UI: 1.6–3.5), whereas the lowest was in low SDI regions at 0.8 (95% UI: 0.5–1.1) (Table 1; Figs. 1 and 2).

At the regional level, Australasia bore the highest global burden of ALL, with an ASPR of 110.7 per 100,000 (95% UI: 97.6–125.4), followed by Western Europe (97.2; 95% UI: 91.5–103.8) and High-income North America (92.1; 95% UI: 86.5–97.3). The elevated ASPR in Australasia may be attributed to genetic susceptibility, increased carcinogen exposure, limited access to high-quality healthcare, and insufficient awareness of risk factors. In contrast, the high ASPR in High-income North America may be primarily linked to environmental pollution and rapid urbanization.

In Asia, the burden of ALL is also considerable. East Asia reported a relatively high ASIR of 3.6 per 100,000 (95% UI: 2.0–5.0), followed by Andean Latin America (2.3; 95% UI: 1.5–2.9) and Central Latin America (2.2; 95% UI: 2.0–2.5), whereas the lowest ASIR was recorded in Western Sub-Saharan Africa (0.4; 95% UI: 0.2–0.6) (Table 1; Fig. 2B). From 1990 to 2021, ASIR trends in Asia varied markedly. East Asia experienced the greatest increase (EAPC = 0.64; 95% CI: 0.35–0.93), while Central Asia had the steepest decline (EAPC = − 1.62; 95% CI: − 1.74 to − 1.50) (Table 1, Supplementary Figure S1B).

In terms of ASMR, Central Latin America, Andean Latin America, East Asia, the Caribbean, and Southeast Asia all reported rates exceeding 1 per 100,000. The sharpest increase in ASMR was observed in Southern Sub-Saharan Africa (EAPC = 0.47; 95% CI: 0.22–0.72), whereas Eastern Europe saw the most significant reduction (EAPC = − 2.91; 95% CI: − 3.14 to − 2.69) (Table 1, Supplementary Fig S1). With respect to ASDR, the highest values were reported in Central Latin America (101.4; 95% UI: 90.6–115.4) and Andean Latin America (99.9; 95% UI: 68.0–127.2), followed by East Asia (73.6; 95% UI: 42.8–94.1) and the Caribbean (68.4; 95% UI: 47.3–103.8) (Table 1; Fig. 2). From 1990 to 2021, Southern Sub-Saharan Africa experienced the greatest increase in ASDR (EAPC = 0.38; 95% CI: 0.14–0.62), while Eastern Europe achieved the largest decline (EAPC = − 3.61; 95% CI: − 3.84 to − 3.38) (Table 1, Supplementary Fig S1).

Correlation analysis revealed that ASPR and ASIR were positively correlated with SDI (ρ = 0.698, p < 0.001; ρ = 0.448, p < 0.001), whereas ASMR and ASDR showed negative correlations (ρ = − 0.228, p < 0.001; ρ = − 0.108, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig S2). Regions with higher SDI typically possess stronger healthcare infrastructure, screening programs, and early diagnostic capabilities, contributing to elevated prevalence and incidence. In contrast, lower-SDI regions may underestimate cases due to limited medical resources, despite facing a relatively higher mortality and disease burden. Furthermore, high-SDI regions reported a greater proportion of pediatric ALL cases, with children under 5 years old consistently representing the largest patient group in both 1990 and 2021 (Fig. 3). From 1990 to 2019, ALL incidence increased across all age groups, with pediatric populations exhibiting substantially higher rates than adults (Supplementary Fig S3).

National level

At the national level, the ASPR of ALL exhibited substantial variation, ranging from 0.2 to 69.8 per 100,000 population. Monaco reported the highest ASPR globally (69.8 per 100,000; 95% UI: 38.2–104.6), followed by San Marino (45.5 per 100,000; 95% UI: 26.5–68.9) and Italy (23.6 per 100,000; 95% UI: 20.3–27.3) (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S1). Notably, these three countries are all located in Western Europe, consistent with regional findings that highlight Western Europe as bearing a high burden of ALL. This alignment between national and regional trends underscores the ongoing challenges European countries face in the prevention and control of ALL. Trend analysis revealed that Jamaica experienced the most pronounced increase in ASPR (EAPC = 5.33, 95% CI: 4.6–6.07), whereas Georgia showed the largest decrease (EAPC = − 3.2, 95% CI: − 3.8 to − 2.6) (Supplementary Fig S1A and Table S1).

In terms of ASIR, Monaco also ranked first (8.8 per 100,000; 95% UI: 4.9–13), while the Cook Islands recorded the lowest incidence (0.1 per 100,000; 95% UI: 0.1–0.3) (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table S1). Notably, countries such as Cabo Verde showed a broad ASIR range and a significant upward trend (EAPC = 2.57, 95% CI: − 1.01 to 6.28), while others like Albania exhibited a significant decline (EAPC = − 2.99, 95% CI: − 3.49 to − 2.49), reflecting variations in healthcare access, risk exposure, and preventive strategies across nations.

In 2021, Afghanistan reported the highest ASMR (2.9 per 100,000; 95% UI: 1.2–6.1) and ASDR (148.1 per 100,000; 95% UI: 66.4–291.4), followed by Bolivia (ASMR: 2.2 per 100,000; 95% UI: 1.3–3.1; ASDR: 122.8 per 100,000; 95% UI: 72.1–169.3) and Ecuador (ASMR: 2.2 per 100,000; 95% UI: 1.8–2.6; ASDR: 114.9 per 100,000; 95% UI: 74.5–162.8) (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table S1). The overlap in countries with high mortality and burden highlights the considerable impact of ALL in these regions. Regarding ASDR trends, Belarus (EAPC = − 4.81, 95% CI: − 5.35 to − 4.26), Luxembourg (EAPC = − 4.64, 95% CI: − 4.84 to − 4.45), and Estonia (EAPC = − 4.32, 95% CI: − 4.59 to − 4.04) recorded the most significant declines, whereas Zimbabwe (EAPC = 2.63, 95% CI: 2.11–3.14), Lesotho (EAPC = 2.41, 95% CI: 2.07–2.76), and Mauritius (EAPC = 2.1, 95% CI: − 1.75 to 6.11) exhibited increasing ASDR trends (Supplementary Fig S1D, Table S1). Similarly, ASMR trends showed the most notable declines in Belarus (EAPC = − 4.13, 95% CI: − 4.67 to − 3.58), Luxembourg (EAPC = − 4.01, 95% CI: − 4.13 to − 3.89), and the Republic of Korea (EAPC = − 3.85, 95% CI: − 4.03 to − 3.67), while Mauritius (EAPC = 3.04, 95% CI: − 1.39 to 7.67), Lesotho (EAPC = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.96–2.66), and Zimbabwe (EAPC = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.72–2.66) demonstrated increasing mortality trends.

In terms of changes in case numbers between 1990 and 2021, Tokelau experienced the most substantial increase in ASPR, rising by 373%, whereas Georgia saw a slight decline of 0.57% (Fig. 4A). Incidence rates also increased most in Tokelau (168%) and declined most in the United Arab Emirates (–57%) (Fig. 4B). For mortality, Tokelau recorded a 117% increase in deaths, while the Maldives experienced a 68% reduction (Fig. 4C). DALYs similarly rose by 139% in Tokelau but decreased by 76% in the Republic of Korea (Fig. 4D).

Correlation analysis showed that ASMR was negatively associated with SDI (ρ = − 0.294, p < 0.001), indicating that mortality is lower in high-SDI countries, likely due to better healthcare access and earlier diagnosis. ASIR demonstrated a positive association with SDI (ρ = 0.42, p < 0.001), suggesting higher incidence in more developed countries, potentially linked to better detection systems and lifestyle-related risk factors. ASPR also correlated positively with SDI (ρ = 0.71, p < 0.001), reflecting longer survival and higher disease prevalence in high-SDI regions. Conversely, ASDR showed a negative correlation with SDI (ρ = − 0.413, p < 0.001), indicating a relatively lower disease burden in economically advanced regions due to improved treatment outcomes (Supplementary Fig S4).

Age and gender patterns

Data from 2021 reveal that the ASPR of ALL increases as age decreases, reaching its peak in the < 5 years age group (Fig. 5A). Across most age groups, males consistently exhibited higher ASPRs than females, with the gender disparity being particularly marked during childhood. The ASIR of ALL in 2021 displayed a distinct “bimodal” distribution (Fig. 5B), with prominent peaks in early childhood and older age. The highest ASIR was observed in the < 5 years age group. Compared with 1990, the ASIR in 2021 declined across various age groups. Notably, the incidence rates for males and females were similar up to the 45–49 years age group; however, beyond this point, male ASIRs remained consistently higher than those of females. Similarly, the ASMR in 2021 also followed a bimodal pattern, with higher mortality rates observed in childhood and older adulthood, peaking in the 90–94 years age group. Compared to 1990, ASMRs (Supplementary Fig S5A, S6) declined across all age groups. Regarding gender differences, male ASMRs were generally comparable to female ASMRs up to the 45–49 years age group. Beyond this age, male mortality rates surpassed those of females in corresponding age groups. The ASDR in 2021 also showed an inverse association with age, with the highest disability-adjusted life years observed in the < 5 years age group. Overall, male ASDRs (Supplementary Fig S5B, S6) were higher than those of females across most age groups, with the greatest gender gap again seen in early childhood. These findings highlight substantial age- and gender-related disparities in ALL burden. The high burden in young children and the consistent male predominance across indicators suggest that age- and sex-specific factors should be considered in future research, as well as in the development of targeted prevention, diagnosis, and treatment strategies.

Joinpoint regression analysis

Global Joinpoint regression analysis shows that, over the past 32 years, the ASPR for ALL has generally increased (AAPC = 0.84%; 95% CI: 0.59%–1.10%; P < 0.001), although the upward trend has gradually flattened. Notably, between 2003 and 2008, the ASPR increased significantly (APC = 3.45%; 95% CI: 2.89%–4.00%; P < 0.001), followed by a notable decline (APC = − 2.57%; 95% CI: − 3.18% to − 1.94%; P < 0.001). Meanwhile, the global ASIR shows a downward trend (AAPC = − 0.89%; 95% CI: − 1.02% to − 0.76%; P < 0.001), with the most significant decrease observed between 1993 and 2003 (APC = − 1.49%; 95% CI: − 1.78% to − 1.20%; P < 0.001). Additionally, the global ASMR demonstrates a consistent decline (AAPC = − 1.77%; 95% CI: − 1.85% to − 1.69%; P < 0.001), with the most significant decrease occurring between 2018 and 2021 (APC = − 2.57%; 95% CI: − 3.18% to − 1.94%; P < 0.001). The ASDR exhibits an even steeper downward trend (AAPC = − 2.15%; 95% CI: − 2.26% to − 2.04%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 6).

Risk factors for acute lymphoblastic leukemia

This study collected and analyzed data on four risk factors associated with mortality and DALY attributed to ALL from the GBD 2021 classification: Smoking, High body-mass index, Occupational exposure to formaldehyde, and Occupational exposure to benzene. These factors were stratified by geographic region (Supplementary Fig S7). Globally, in 2021, high body-mass index was the leading risk factor for ALL-related mortality and DALYs, accounting for 5.8% of global deaths. By geographic region, High-income North America had the highest proportion of deaths attributed to high body-mass index, at 11.6%, while Western Sub-Saharan Africa had the lowest, at 1.2%. Additionally, smoking was also a significant risk factor for ALL-related mortality, with a smoking-attributed death rate of 15.4% in High-income North America, compared to just 0.1% in Western Sub-Saharan Africa. Overall, in different SDI regions, the death rates attributed to smoking and high body-mass index were significantly higher in high and high-middle SDI regions compared to low SDI regions. In terms of DALYs, high body-mass index was again the primary risk factor globally, contributing 4.0%. Among geographic regions, High-income North America had the highest proportion of DALYs attributed to high body-mass index, at 9.6%, while Western Sub-Saharan Africa had the lowest, at 0.8%. Smoking also contributed significantly to DALYs, with a smoking-attributed DALY rate of 9.4% in High-income North America. When the analysis was expanded to include five SDI regions, high body-mass index accounted for the highest DALY proportion in high SDI regions, at 8%, while the lowest proportion was observed in low SDI regions, at just 1.3%. The contributions of other risk factors to DALYs also exhibited regional heterogeneity across SDI levels, further highlighting the complex interplay of these factors in the global burden of ALL (Supplementary Fig S7).

Health inequality analysis

From 1990 to 2021, the slope of the ASPR related to ALL increased from 3.22 to 4.23, while the ASIR rose from 0.39 to 0.41, indicating a slight upward trend in both the prevalence and incidence rates across different socioeconomic groups. At the same time, the slope index for ASMR decreased from 0.04 to −0.14, and the absolute difference index (SII) for ASDR significantly expanded from − 22.13 to −30.97, indicating that although mortality disparities have slightly improved, low-income groups continue to experience more severe health loss due to the disease. This phenomenon may reflect that higher-income groups benefit from better healthcare resources, leading to higher disease diagnosis rates, while the increase in high-risk behaviors and environmental exposures also positively contributes to the overall prevalence. In the concentration index analysis from 1990 to 2021, both ASIR and ASMR showed significant declines, from 0.05 to −0.04 and from 0.13 to 0.06, respectively. However, the ASPR changed only slightly from − 0.19 to −0.20, and the ASDR shifted from 0.17 to 0.15. These data suggest that while health inequalities related to ALL have alleviated over the past few decades, with overall improvements in disease management, the substantial change in ASDR indicates that challenges remain in further reducing health loss. The persistent gap may stem from disparities in disease management, rehabilitation resources, and social security distribution (Supplementary Fig S8).

Decomposition analysis

From 1990 to 2021, the DALYs associated with ALL showed a significant net decrease, with an overall change of −1,735,338.32. The driving mechanisms of this change exhibit notable spatiotemporal heterogeneity. On a global scale, age effect, population effect, and DALY rate change effect contributed − 31.5%, + 107.9%, and − 176.4%, respectively. This indicates that changes in age structure (such as adjustments in the proportion of the elderly population) and improvements in disease risk (i.e., the reduction in DALY rates) were the core drivers of alleviating disease burden, while the expansion of population size had a reverse driving effect. In gender-specific analysis, the decrease in DALYs for both males and females was mainly driven by risk improvement effects (with a negative contribution of −176.4% in both genders). However, the positive pressure due to population growth was significantly higher in males (+ 107.9%) compared to females (+ 84.2%), suggesting that gender-specific population dynamics may influence the prioritization of intervention strategies. From the SDI stratification results, the decline in disease burden in high-SDI and upper-middle-SDI regions was primarily driven by DALY rate change effects, contributing − 194.4% and − 172.9%, respectively, confirming the key role of advanced diagnostic and treatment technologies, as well as optimized healthcare systems, in mitigating leukemia burden. Notably, although the age effect contributed only − 7.3% in high-SDI regions, it showed a significant negative impact (−41.8%) in middle-SDI regions, reflecting the potential mitigating effect of the demographic transition (such as the increase in the working-age population) on disease burden in these areas. In stark contrast, the population effect in low-SDI and lower-middle-SDI regions showed extreme positive contributions (+ 276.8% to + 1271.4%), which not only fully offset the benefits of risk improvements (−152.1% to −833.7%) but even led to a net increase in DALYs (Supplementary Fig S9).

Frontier analysis

There is a clear trend between ASDR values and SDI across different countries/regions. Overall, countries/regions with an SDI below 0.3 show significant differences, indicating heavier disease burdens and greater potential for improvement. As SDI increases to between 0.3 and 0.6, the distribution of ASDR remains relatively scattered, with some countries still showing substantial variations. However, in countries/regions with an SDI above 0.6, the variation in ASDR decreases significantly, suggesting that the disease burden in these countries has stabilized. Furthermore, from 1990 to 2021, countries with an SDI below 0.3 exhibited more dramatic changes in ASDR, while the trend stabilized once SDI exceeded 0.3. The top 10 countries/regions with the largest effective differences (effective difference: 97.17–136.34) span various SDI regions, including Afghanistan, Bolivia (Plurinational State of), Mexico, Ecuador, Dominica, Haiti, Ethiopia, Central Latin America, Andean Latin America, and Guatemala, indicating substantial room for improvement. In contrast, the top 5 countries/regions with the smallest effective differences, all of which have an effective difference of less than 2, are from low-SDI and lower-middle-SDI regions. These include São Tomé and Príncipe (low-SDI), Cook Islands (lower-middle-SDI), Gambia (low-SDI), Guinea (low-SDI), and Somalia (low-SDI), suggesting that they are at the forefront of reducing the burden of acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Overall, high-SDI countries have made significant progress in alleviating disease burden, while low-SDI countries still have substantial improvement potential (Supplementary Fig S10).

Global burden of acute lymphoblastic leukemia: future projections

It is projected that from 2021 to 2040, the global burden of ALL will undergo significant changes, although the trends for different indicators will vary. According to the forecast, the global combined ASPR for both sexes will decrease from approximately 5.39/100,000 in 2021 to about 2.84/100,000 in 2040, a reduction of approximately 50%. Specifically, the male ASPR is expected to decline slightly from about 6.19/100,000 in 2021 to approximately 4.86/100,000 in 2040, while the female ASPR will experience a steeper decline, from about 4.56/100,000 in 2021 to about 1.91/100,000 in 2040. The global ASIR for acute lymphoblastic leukemia is also expected to show an overall decline, from around 1.37/100,000 in 2021 to about 0.81/100,000 in 2040. Additionally, the ASMR is expected to decrease from about 0.90/100,000 in 2021 to approximately 0.53/100,000 in 2040, reflecting possible improvements in disease treatment and management. Notably, the DALY rate is also projected to significantly decrease, from 48.80/100,000 in 2021 to approximately 24.61/100,000 in 2040. Among all indicators, although the incidence rate for males will remain higher than for females, the gender gap is expected to narrow gradually over time, particularly in terms of the incidence rate. This trend suggests that future public health interventions and healthcare resource allocation may need to further focus on gender-specific needs to achieve more balanced health improvements (Supplementary Fig S11).

Discussion

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia poses a significant challenge to global public health, with its distinct epidemiological characteristics attracting widespread research attention. This comprehensive analysis spanning 32 years and covering 204 countries across six continents systematically reveals the heterogeneous disease burden across populations and time periods.

Quantitative indicators demonstrate that the ALL burden increased between 2021 and 1990. Although global ASIR and ASMR showed significant downward trends (EAPC: −0.64 and − 1.73, respectively), the ASPR rose by 10.3%. This paradox may reflect two factors: first, advances in medical technology have prolonged survival among ALL patients, increasing cumulative case numbers (e.g., high SDI regions achieved a 5-year survival rate improvement through precision diagnostics); second, regional improvements in diagnostic capacity (particularly in low SDI areas) have reduced historical underreporting [20]. For instance, ASPR in low SDI regions increased from 9,329.8/100,000 in 1990 to 15,460.6/100,000 in 2021, suggesting enhanced disease surveillance may partially explain this rise. These findings align with global cancer survival trends, where therapeutic advancements are transitioning ALL toward chronic disease management [21].

Strong positive correlations were observed between SDI and ASPR/ASIR (ρ = 0.698 and 0.448), while SDI inversely correlated with ASMR/ASDR (ρ = −0.228 and − 0.108), highlighting the dual impacts of socioeconomic development. High SDI regions (e.g., Western Europe, Australasia) achieve high detection rates (ASPR >90/100,000) through robust screening systems (e.g., flow cytometry and molecular diagnostics) and aging populations, yet their low mortality (ASMR 0.5/100,000) reflects the success of multidisciplinary care. Conversely, limited healthcare resources in low/middle SDI regions (e.g., Afghanistan, Bolivia) result in delayed diagnosis and inadequate treatment, with ASMR as high as 2.9/100,000 and ASDR (148.1/100,000) approximately sixfold higher than in high SDI regions [22]. A significant modifiable factor influencing ALL outcomes in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is the limited capacity for early diagnosis and access to optimal treatment. Delays in detection, shortages of trained personnel, inadequate infrastructure, and lack of essential medications all contribute to poorer survival rates. Strengthening health systems in these regions is imperative to reduce the global inequality in ALL outcomes.

The rising ALL burden in high/middle SDI regions (e.g., EAPC of 3.66 for ASPR in middle-high SDI areas) may correlate with environmental exposures. Rapid urbanization-driven air pollution, Westernized diets, and obesity epidemics are identified as potential drivers [23]. ALL’s pediatric predominance (< 5 years) persists across all SDI regions, though high SDI areas show increasing childhood cases, potentially reflecting interactions between genetic susceptibility and environmental exposures. Genome-wide association studies have identified CDKN2A locus variants tripling childhood ALL risk, while assisted reproductive technologies in high-income countries may exacerbate risks via epigenetic changes (e.g., abnormal DNA methylation) [24, 25]. The incidence of ALL in Latin American regions is notably higher than in most other low- and middle-income regions. This may be partly attributed to biological and genetic predispositions, as populations with a high proportion of Indigenous American ancestry—common in Latin America—more frequently carry germline variants in GATA3, IKZF1, and ARID5B, which are associated with an increased risk of childhood ALL [26].

ALL’s burden exhibits pronounced age and gender heterogeneity, shaped by biological and environmental interplay. Age-specific trends reveal a bimodal pattern: incidence and mortality peak in children < 5 years and adults >90 years. The pediatric peak is linked to fetal hematopoietic stem cell genetic abnormalities (e.g., ETV6-RUNX1 fusion genes) and postnatal immune dysregulation, while the elderly peak arises from immunosenescence and cumulative carcinogen exposure (e.g., benzene derivatives) [27–29]. Notably, despite improved survival in children, treatment-related complications make this group the most affected by lost healthy life years, underscoring the need for lifelong management [30]. Gender disparities persist across all ages, with males consistently burdened more severely, particularly in childhood—a phenomenon potentially explained by X-chromosome dosage compensation in females and androgen-driven leukemic stem cell proliferation [31]. These findings emphasize the need for age- and gender-specific strategies: environmental carcinogen control and survivorship programs in children, enhanced immune surveillance in the elderly, and sex-differentiated early screening to reduce disparities.

High body mass index (BMI) has emerged as the leading modifiable risk factor for ALL-related DALYs globally, particularly in high SDI regions like North America. Overweight patients face elevated risks of treatment-related mortality [32]. While occupational benzene/formaldehyde exposure is better controlled in high SDI areas, lifestyle-related metabolic risks (high BMI, smoking) now dominate, reflecting industrialized societies’ shifting risk profiles. Paternal smoking preconception correlates with reduced 5-year survival in children with AML, potentially via germline damage affecting immune/oxidative pathways or epigenetic programming of offspring metabolism [33–35]. Occupational benzene exposure during pregnancy induces genetic/toxic events linked to childhood leukemia [36], while benzene metabolites trigger oxidative stress, epigenetic dysregulation, and hematotoxicity in murine models of preleukemia [37]. Collectively, high BMI, formaldehyde/benzene exposure, and smoking are critical ALL determinants, particularly in high-burden regions. Targeted interventions addressing these factors are essential for burden reduction.

Health inequity monitoring reveals persistent disparities in ALL burden across socioeconomic groups. From 1990 to 2021, ASPR and ASIR slope indices rose from 3.22 to 4.23 and 0.39 to 0.41, respectively, indicating widening risk gaps between high- and low-income populations. This divergence may reflect improved diagnostic access in wealthier regions alongside socioeconomic gradients in environmental exposures and risky behaviors [38]. Although ASMR slope index improved from 0.04 to −0.14, ASDR slope index deteriorated sharply from − 22.13 to −30.97, highlighting growing health loss disparities due to uneven rehabilitation resource allocation and systemic social protection gaps [39]. Concentration index trends further illustrate inequity complexities: ASIR/ASMR indices improved (from 0.05 to −0.04 and 0.13 to 0.06), signaling enhanced service accessibility, but ASDR stagnation (0.17 to 0.15) underscores persistent disability-related gaps. These findings stress that reducing mortality alone is insufficient for health equity—post-disability management and equitable social support systems are equally vital.

In conclusion, the evolving burden of ALL presents a dual dynamic: in high Socio-Demographic Index regions, advances in medical technology have led to decreased mortality rates, yet population aging and improved diagnostic capacity have contributed to increased prevalence. In contrast, low-SDI regions continue to face health inequities due to limited healthcare resources. The age-specific bimodal pattern of disease burden—peaking in early childhood and older adulthood—along with sex differences and modifiable metabolic/environmental risk factors, highlights the necessity for precision prevention and control strategies. This study provides evidence for the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Cancer Control Blueprint and supports the health equity agenda outlined in the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals.

Limitations and implications

Despite the comprehensive nature of this study, several limitations merit acknowledgment. First, the accuracy of the GBD estimates is inherently constrained in countries lacking robust national cancer registries. In such settings—particularly in low-SDI regions—GBD relies on statistical modeling and data extrapolation from neighboring countries or similar settings. While these methods enhance global comparability, they may introduce uncertainty, and the resulting estimates should be interpreted with caution. In addition, differences in the initiation time, update frequency, and diagnostic criteria of cancer registries across countries may introduce bias into temporal trend estimates. Furthermore, the GBD provides population-level estimates and cannot capture individual-level clinical or molecular heterogeneity, such as specific subtypes, treatment regimens, or genetic background. Finally, potential biological and socioeconomic determinants—such as racial or genetic susceptibility, healthcare accessibility, and treatment adherence—cannot be fully quantified using GBD data.
