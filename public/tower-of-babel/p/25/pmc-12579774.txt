Rate of prenatal diagnosis of critical congenital heart disease continued to improve despite COVID‐19 pandemic: multicenter Canadian study
Authors: L Eckersley, M Lad, H Rose, J Lougheed, H Poolsaar, A Szabo, L Nield, D Fruitman, G Mansuskani, L K Hornberger, B Arya, D Dover, E Blagdon, L Freud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTravel restrictions, reallocation of health resources and physical distancing during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic caused extraordinary strain on healthcare systems. The overall impact of public health measures during COVID‐19 on the prenatal diagnosis of congenital heart disease (CHD) has received limited attention. We sought to determine the rate of prenatal diagnosis and the pregnancy and postnatal outcomes of critical CHD prior to and during the COVID‐19 pandemic.MethodsCases of critical CHD with estimated due date between 1 January 2016 and 31 March 2022 that required or were expected to require neonatal intervention were identified from all tertiary fetal cardiac centers in Ontario and Alberta, Canada. Pregnancies were stratified based on reaching 18 weeks' gestation before (pre‐COVID‐19) or after 1 March 2020 (during COVID‐19), as the latter group would require ultrasound scanning under COVID‐19 pandemic restrictions. Outcomes included timing of critical CHD diagnosis (prenatal or postnatal), gestational age at prenatal diagnosis, pregnancy outcome and infant mortality at 30 days and 1 year after birth. The rate of prenatal diagnosis was assessed using Cox proportional hazard modeling with time‐varying and time‐invariant cofactors.ResultsPrenatal diagnosis occurred in 1238/1774 (69.8%) cases of critical CHD overall, of which 858/1257 (68.3%) cases were in the pre‐COVID‐19 group and 380/517 (73.5%) cases were in the COVID‐19 exposure group (P = 0.03). There was no difference between the pre‐COVID‐19 group and the COVID‐19 exposure group in median gestational age at obstetric ultrasound (20.1 (interquartile range (IQR), 19.0–22.0) weeks vs 19.8 (IQR, 19.1–21.3) weeks; P = 0.07), median time from obstetric ultrasound to diagnosis of critical CHD (1.2 (IQR, 0.6–2.2) weeks vs 1.2 (IQR, 0.6–2.2) weeks; P = 0.59) and prenatal diagnosis < 22 weeks' gestation (454/848 (53.5%) vs 221/378 (58.5%); P = 0.11). Termination of pregnancy was more common in the COVID‐19 exposure group (pre‐COVID‐19, 20.9% vs during COVID‐19, 27.5%; P = 0.01). Infant mortality at 30 days (pre‐COVID‐19, 2.5% vs COVID‐19, 1.8%; P = 0.44) and at 1 year after birth (pre‐COVID‐19, 8.9% vs COVID‐19, 8.6%; P = 0.86) did not differ between eras. Once adjusted for province, trends in calendar time, week of gestation, distance to closest tertiary fetal cardiac service and genetic diagnosis, the rate of prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD continued to increase, with a trend towards an increased rate during the COVID‐19 pandemic compared with the pre‐COVID‐19 era.ConclusionsDespite the reduction in clinical services during the COVID‐19 pandemic, the rate of prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD continued to increase during the pandemic in two of Canada's largest provinces. These findings provide reassurance regarding the impact of refined fetal cardiology referral indications for resource‐limited scenarios. © 2025 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

From its onset in March 2020, the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic was a major disruptor of usual practice in medicine. To minimize the risk of COVID‐19 transmission, many services developed modified referral criteria and limited the requirement for in‐person contact in a hospital setting to only the highest‐risk cases. Maternal–fetal medicine and fetal cardiology services were not an exception to this change in care, resulting in reduced in‐person surveillance of pregnancies by tertiary services 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 .

The detection of fetal congenital heart disease (CHD) occurs mostly at routine obstetric anatomical ultrasound examination. While referrals for routine anatomical ultrasound were accepted as usual during the pandemic, it is unclear what effect the pandemic may have had on their timing and frequency, with adverse outcomes in some jurisdictions 5 . Disincentives to attending routine care appointments included the risk of COVID‐19 infection and associated anxiety. There were also intermittent legislatively mandated barriers to travel and/or accommodation for both the providers performing ultrasound examinations and the patients attending appointments. The potential for missed or late diagnosis of fetal CHD is concerning, given the importance of prenatal diagnosis for appropriate parental counseling 6 , genetic investigations, pregnancy surveillance, decision‐making, delivery and perinatal management. For critical CHD, defined as CHD requiring, or expected to require, intervention within 30 days after birth, there could also be additional adverse neonatal outcomes if a prenatal diagnosis was not made.

The extent to which the COVID‐19 pandemic affected the rate and timing of the prenatal diagnosis of CHD is unknown. Moreover, whether this translated to worse neonatal outcomes compared with the era preceding the COVID‐19 pandemic has not been explored. We aimed to investigate whether the COVID‐19 pandemic was associated with a reduction in the rate of prenatal diagnosis, later prenatal diagnosis, and/or worse pregnancy and postnatal outcomes for cases of critical CHD.

METHODS

Study population

This was a multicenter retrospective study of the timing of diagnosis of critical CHD, conducted at all tertiary fetal cardiac centers in Alberta and Ontario, Canada (Ontario: The Hospital for Sick Children, Children's Hospital of Eastern Ontario, Sunnybrook Health Sciences Center, McMaster Children's Hospital, Children's Hospital at London Health Sciences; Alberta: Stollery Children's Hospital, Alberta Children's Hospital), of pregnant patients with an estimated due date between 1 January 2016 and 31 March 2022 and a diagnosis of critical CHD. Cases were identified from the records of participating hospitals, including medical and surgical records and fetal cardiac program databases.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Critical CHD was defined as CHD that required cardiac intervention within 30 days after birth, or in the case of termination of pregnancy (TOP) or fetal demise, CHD that was anticipated to require such an intervention. Critical CHD was categorized as follows: transposition of the great arteries (TGA) with or without ventricular septal defect (VSD); truncus arteriosus; single ventricle lesions, including hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS); critical aortic stenosis or coarctation or interruption of the aorta; pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum; tetralogy of Fallot or pulmonary atresia with VSD; critical pulmonary stenosis; severe Ebstein's anomaly or tricuspid valve dysplasia; total anomalous pulmonary venous return; heterotaxy; other complex CHD with ductal dependence for systemic or pulmonary blood flow; or complete heart block. Where applicable, we used the timing of neonatal cardiac intervention to define critical CHD, rather than the type of lesion. Singleton and multiple pregnancies were included, as were fetuses with extracardiac anomalies and/or genetic syndromes.

Cases with a diagnosis of non‐critical CHD (i.e. CHD that did not require, or was not expected to require, cardiac intervention within 30 days after birth), such as single ventricle lesions without outflow tract obstruction, tetralogy of Fallot with pulmonary stenosis, balanced atrioventricular septal defects or isolated VSD, were excluded. Cases in which the timing of diagnosis could not be determined from retrospective chart review were also excluded.

Data collection

We collected data for all patients regarding singleton or multiple pregnancy, birth weight, gestational age (GA) at birth, presence of extracardiac anomalies and/or genetic syndromes, critical CHD diagnosis and timing of the diagnosis (prenatal vs postnatal). If a prenatal diagnosis was made, GA at diagnosis, GA at first obstetric anatomical ultrasound examination, indication for referral for fetal echocardiogram, GA at critical CHD diagnosis (defined as the first fetal cardiology visit at which the diagnosis was made) and pregnancy outcome (TOP, intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD) or live birth) were noted. To classify cases according to the likelihood of prenatal detection, CHD diagnosis was also categorized according to the obstetric ultrasound screening view on which an anomaly would be evident, on a case‐by‐case basis (Table S1). For all liveborn patients, whether compassionate care vs active medical and surgical management was implemented was noted. The length of neonatal hospital stay and infant mortality at 30 days and 1 year after birth were noted for liveborn patients managed with an intention‐to‐treat approach.

We also included the Canadian three‐digit postal code (forward sortation area (FSA)) of residence at the time of referral for prenatally diagnosed patients or that at birth for postnatally diagnosed patients. The minimum distance (in km) from each FSA to the closest participant tertiary fetal cardiac service was calculated as a measure of remoteness.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the rate of prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD. Secondary outcomes included GA at prenatal diagnosis, rates of TOP and IUFD, and infant mortality at 30 days and 1 year after birth. Exposure to the COVID‐19 pandemic was considered as reaching 18 weeks' gestation after 1 March 2020, as these pregnancies would have required the second‐trimester obstetric anatomical screening ultrasound under restrictions in place owing to the COVID‐19 pandemic. In a sensitivity analysis, we also categorized cases that reached 12 weeks' gestation after 1 March 2020, as these pregnancies would have required both first‐trimester and second‐trimester ultrasound examinations under COVID‐19 pandemic conditions.

Crude rates of prenatal diagnosis were aggregated into 25 quarterly periods from January 2016 to March 2022 and modeled, identifying time variance over the corresponding period. We estimated the univariable risk of missed prenatal diagnosis and late prenatal diagnosis (defined as diagnosis > 22 weeks) and compared the group reaching 18 weeks during the COVID‐19 pandemic with those reaching 18 weeks in the period prior to the pandemic. Pregnancy outcomes and infant mortality rates at 30 days and 1 year after birth were compared between the pre‐COVID‐19 period (1 January 2016 to 28 February 2020) and the COVID‐19 pandemic era (1 March 2020 to 31 March 2022). The outcome of interest was GA at the prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD, with censoring at birth, TOP or IUFD. A Cox proportional hazards model with time‐varying covariates and piecewise constant hazards was used to model the likelihood of a prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD, using gestational week from conception as the timescale and a constant hazard during each week, censored at birth, TOP or IUFD. Time‐varying covariates were updated at the start of each week. The time‐varying covariates were number of months since the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic (1 March 2020) and calendar time (in months). The number of months since the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic was entered into the model as an indicator for each month, since there was no information on the functional form they would follow during a pandemic. Calendar time was entered into the model as linear. A second model entered COVID‐19 exposure as a binary variable. Distance to the closest tertiary fetal cardiac service (< 100 km or ≥ 100 km) and diagnosis of a genetic syndrome (yes or no) were also adjusted for in the model. In addition, a logistic regression model for prenatal diagnosis outcome was modeled using average FSA linear distance (per 100 km) from the closest tertiary fetal cardiac service, genetic diagnosis, province, exposure to the COVID‐19 pandemic < 18 weeks and calendar time (in months) as predictors. A multivariable logistic regression model was used in an exploratory analysis with TOP as the outcome, given the presence of an association with the COVID‐19 pandemic on univariable analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

RESULTS

Of the 1774 overall cases of critical CHD included, 1238 (69.8%) were diagnosed prenatally. In the prenatally diagnosed cohort, TOP occurred in 404/1235 (32.7%) cases and IUFD occurred in 71/1235 (5.7%) cases, yielding 760/1235 (61.5%) liveborn cases; the outcome was unknown in three cases. Among the total live births (n = 1296), 48 (3.7%) were managed with compassionate care owing to cardiac and non‐cardiac disease incompatible with intervention (10 and 19 cases, respectively) or because of poor prognosis (19 cases), after a shared decision‐making process, all of which were diagnosed prenatally.

A genetic diagnosis was identified in 333/1459 (22.8%) of patients who were tested. At least one extracardiac congenital anomaly was identified in 325/1491 (21.8%) of cases for which this information was available. The median length of neonatal hospital stay was 17 (interquartile range (IQR), 10–34) days. Neonatal mortality at 30 days occurred in 29/1247 (2.3%) cases and mortality at 1 year occurred in 106/1202 (8.8%) cases among liveborn neonates managed with an intention‐to‐treat approach.

Table 1 displays the characteristics of the groups with prenatal and postnatal diagnosis. Prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD was significantly associated with diagnosis of a genetic syndrome, at least one extracardiac congenital anomaly and abnormality seen in the four‐chamber view on ultrasound. Prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD was least reported among cases with venous abnormalities only, which were largely isolated total anomalous pulmonary venous drainage. The crude rate of prenatal critical CHD diagnosis was higher in the group reaching 18 weeks' gestation after 1 March 2020 (COVID‐19 exposure group). There was no difference in the prenatal diagnosis rate according to strata of distance from residence to closest tertiary fetal cardiac service (< 100 km vs ≥ 100 km), although average FSA linear distance (per 100 km) was associated with reduced odds of prenatal diagnosis (odds ratio (OR), 0.91 (95% CI, 0.85–0.97), P = 0.008; adjusted OR, 0.87 (95% CI, 0.79–0.96), P = 0.006). The primary reason for referral was suspected CHD in 82% of cases and any fetal abnormality detected on obstetric ultrasound in 93% of cases, with no differences between the pre‐COVID‐19 group and the COVID‐19 exposure group. The crude rate of prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD over the study period is shown in Figure 1. The odds of a prenatal critical CHD diagnosis was positively associated with the year of birth during the study (OR, 1.08 (95% CI, 1.02–1.14); P = 0.004).

Among pregnancies diagnosed prenatally with critical CHD, the rate of diagnosis < 22 weeks varied according to the distance to the closest tertiary fetal cardiac service, with the group residing ≥ 100 km from such a service having a lower rate of diagnosis < 22 weeks (Table 2). The rate of TOP was higher in those diagnosed < 22 weeks compared with those diagnosed ≥ 22 weeks, and management with compassionate care was more common among those with a prenatal diagnosis ≥ 22 weeks (< 22 weeks, 20/400 (5.0%) vs ≥ 22 weeks, 28/351 (8.0%); P = 0.04). There was no difference in the rate of prenatal diagnosis < 22 weeks according to COVID‐19 exposure, when considered both as reaching 18 weeks or 12 weeks after 1 March 2020. Those diagnosed < 22 weeks had a higher number of abnormalities seen in the four‐chamber view on ultrasound compared with those diagnosed ≥ 22 weeks (Table 2).

On comparison of pregnancies that reached 18 weeks' gestation before or after the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic, there was a difference observed in pregnancy outcome, with a reduced proportion of live births and an increased proportion of TOP in the COVID‐19 exposure group (Table 3). When limited to those with a prenatal critical CHD diagnosis, the rate of TOP was 37% in the COVID‐19 exposure group and 30% in the pre‐COVID‐19 group (P = 0.02). In a sensitivity analysis restricted to pregnancies reaching 12 weeks' gestation after 1 March 2020, the findings were similar (COVID‐19 exposure, 37% vs pre‐COVID‐19, 31%; P = 0.04). On an exploratory analysis, a more recent calendar month of due date, province and distance < 100 km to the closest tertiary fetal cardiac service were predictive of TOP, whereas COVID‐19 exposure was not (Table S2). A higher rate of TOP was seen for those with abnormalities of the tricuspid valve, coarctation of the aorta and HLHS (Table S3).

There was no difference between the COVID‐19 exposure and pre‐COVID‐19 groups in infant mortality at 30 days or 1 year after birth for liveborn patients managed with an intention‐to‐treat approach. At the two largest tertiary fetal cardiac programs at surgical centers in Ontario and Alberta (The Hospital for Sick Children and Stollery Children's Hospital), the number of individual patients seen in 2020 fell by 20% compared with that in 2019. In 2021 there was an 11% increase in the number of patients seen compared with 2020 (Table S4).

With adjustment for trends in calendar month (with an underlying increasing rate of prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD), GA at diagnosis, province, distance to the closest tertiary fetal cardiac service and diagnosis of a genetic syndrome, there were no timepoints during the 25 months following the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic when the likelihood of prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD (GA at diagnosis censored at birth) differed from that before the COVID‐19 pandemic (Figures 1 and S1). When COVID‐19 exposure was considered a binary variable, there were statistically significant differences in the likelihood of prenatal diagnosis over time (GA, censoring at birth (postnatal diagnosis)) according to category of distance to the closest tertiary fetal cardiac service, genetic diagnosis status and province, when adjusting for all other variables. There was a trend toward an increased likelihood of prenatal diagnosis over time during the COVID‐19 period (hazard ratio, 1.21 (95% CI, 0.92–1.37); P = 0.051). There was no statistically significant difference in the likelihood of prenatal diagnosis by calendar month when adjusting for all other variables (Table 4, Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

The COVID‐19 pandemic disrupted society and healthcare delivery in a manner unprecedented in the modern era. It is incumbent on healthcare providers to analyze the impact of this unique period in specific areas of practice. Despite a 10–20% reduction in fetal cardiac service provision, the time to prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD did not differ during the COVID‐19 pandemic compared with prior to the pandemic in analyses adjusted for time‐varying and time‐invariant cofactors. The proportion of cases of TOP increased during the pandemic; however, among liveborn infants managed with an intention‐to‐treat approach, there was no significant difference between COVID‐19 pandemic eras in infant mortality at both 30 days and 1 year after birth.

Our findings mimic those of Gupta et al. 7 , who found a higher rate of prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD during the COVID‐19 pandemic. However, our approach of time‐series analysis, rather than treating pandemic eras categorically, is novel, and reveals that the findings are consistent with a continued overall improvement in detection at a population level. These findings are encouraging and probably reflect multiple different strategies aimed at improving the prenatal detection of critical CHD, such as enhanced sonography and interpretation skills 8 , and more stringent guidelines for obstetric ultrasound in recent years 9 , 10 . Not surprisingly, the prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD occurred more commonly when an abnormality was seen in the four‐chamber view (four‐chamber; outflow, VSD), followed by those seen in the outflow tract (outflow only; TGA) and those in the three‐vessel (great artery only) views, compared with postnatal diagnosis.

The conduct of our study in a country with universal access to healthcare is unique and such access may have facilitated an ongoing increase in the rate of prenatal diagnosis over the study period. However, we still observed signs that residents of rural and remote communities that reside ≥ 100 km from a tertiary fetal cardiac service were disadvantaged, with lower rates of prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD and a later GA at diagnosis among those that were prenatally diagnosed. We were unable to measure individual or neighborhood socioeconomic status in this study, but our prior work found that, in Alberta, the timing of prenatal diagnosis was also influenced by these metrics 8 .

In June 2020, the American Society of Echocardiography published a statement to specifically address service provision in pediatric and fetal echocardiography during the COVID‐19 pandemic 11 . This document suggested a reduction in offering fetal echocardiography for low‐risk cases (e.g. those conceived by in‐vitro fertilization, family history of some forms of CHD, dichorionic twins) and a delay in fetal echocardiography to a later date for moderate‐risk cases (e.g. fetal cardiac anatomy not seen well, family history of CHD with higher recurrence risk, poorly controlled maternal diabetes mellitus, increased nuchal translucency thickness), a decision made at a time when the pandemic wave persistence was unknown 11 . These recommendations were designed to reduce the demand for non‐urgent clinical services, but the impact on rates of prenatal diagnosis has not been thoroughly evaluated.

Consistent with previous studies 12 , 13 , we found that most cases of prenatally diagnosed critical CHD were referred owing to abnormalities detected on obstetric ultrasound examination rather than for fetal cardiac screening of low‐ or moderate‐risk pregnancies. Given that obstetric ultrasound screening was deemed a necessary practice for all pregnancies during COVID‐19, it seems reasonable that the prenatal diagnosis rate of critical CHD could be maintained, despite fewer accepted indications for referral. This is, in fact, what our study found. Not only was the timing of anatomical obstetric ultrasound examination maintained during the COVID‐19 pandemic, but the rate of prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD was similar, if not higher, despite the challenges of the pandemic. The implication of this finding is significant. Instead of resources being directed to fetal cardiac screening of low‐to‐moderate‐risk pregnancies, the expertise may have been better utilized in providing fetal cardiac training to community sonographers and physicians performing and interpreting anatomical obstetric ultrasound examinations.

Although the pandemic exerted a profoundly negative impact on quality of life, as in many situations of adversity innovations rapidly developed, some of which may result in lasting benefits. In the context of the COVID‐19 pandemic and changes to fetal cardiac screening recommendations, some investigators have suggested that tele‐echocardiography may constitute an alternative approach 14 . This approach would involve expert review of anatomical obstetric screening images, either in real time or retrospectively, by maternal–fetal medicine specialists or fetal cardiologists. The advantages of this approach would be, to the healthcare system, the potential to reduce scanning time and resource utilization associated with duplicate normal scans, and to the patient, elimination of the time, travel, anxiety and costs associated with a secondary fetal cardiac scan. The disadvantage would be the inability of a fetal cardiologist to perform a hands‐on ultrasound scan to ensure an accurate diagnosis. In cases with possible pathology, secondary review would be required to confirm the findings and counsel parents.

This multiprovincial study of critical CHD demonstrated similar findings to our prior work in Alberta, which showed a relationship between distance from care and timing of prenatal diagnosis of major CHD 8 . Rural and remote regions may experience healthcare disadvantages because of many factors 15 . In the current study, the obstetric ultrasound scan leading to referral was performed later in those diagnosed > 22 weeks' gestation, and the time from obstetric ultrasound examination to fetal cardiac diagnosis of critical CHD was also longer. The timing of diagnosis may be important for pregnancy management, including the decision regarding TOP, as illustrated in the present study. In some regions, access to obstetric sonographers may be limited, delaying anatomical screening. A bias towards rescanning locally rather than early referral to a tertiary fetal cardiac center may exist when the tertiary center is far away from the residence of the patient. It is reassuring, however, that this disadvantage did not worsen during the COVID‐19 pandemic. There is potential for the innovative telehealth solutions that emerged during the COVID‐19 pandemic to benefit regional and remote patients. Telehealth may serve not only to provide expert review, but also as a platform for educating local providers. However, none of the participating centers utilized telehealth during the study period.

Our study was also unique in that it was population‐based, including all cases of critical CHD rather than only those referred for surgery 7 . This enabled us to examine differences in the proportion of live births before and during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Although the proportion of IUFD remained the same, there was a surprisingly higher proportion of TOP. Decision‐making in pregnancy is complex, and factors contributing to specific decisions are often difficult to elucidate. Stress and anxiety regarding job security, the economy and the future of a post‐pandemic society may have played a role in the observed increase in TOP rate. Since residential postal code was ascertained for all patients, the increase does not represent movement of patients from the USA, where abortion restrictions have impacted prenatal care 16 .

Reliability of data

This study captured data from healthcare providers at all tertiary care centers providing fetal cardiology and pediatric cardiology surgical services in Ontario and Alberta, Canada, with a population of approximately 19 million and an estimated birth rate of 190 000 per year. Our case number of 1774 patients with critical CHD is approximately 1.29/1000 births, which corresponds well with the prior estimated prevalence of critical CHD of 1.3/1000 10 , suggesting good case acquisition at a population level. Nevertheless, we were unable to include the rare cases of critical CHD that are only diagnosed postmortem, owing to a lack of sufficient access to medical examiner records. The cardiac and non‐cardiac diagnostic data were derived directly from patient chart review with oversight by our study team, all of whom have subspecialty training in pediatric and/or fetal cardiology. We are therefore confident that our findings are more robust than the data derived from administrative records.

Limitations

This study is one of the largest to examine the contemporary rate of prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD across provinces in a universal healthcare system. Although the rate of prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD continued to rise during the COVID‐19 pandemic, our findings do not imply that individual pregnant families were not profoundly affected by the pandemic. Owing to the retrospective nature of the study, data were missing for some variables. In addition, we were unable to collect data regarding how and why pregnancy decisions were made. We used a measure of remoteness to explore possible relationships, but we did not have data available regarding other measures of access to care, including race/ethnicity, indigenous status, maternal education and income. The 10–20% reduction in cases seen in the two major centers during the COVID‐19 pandemic suggests that some low‐ and moderate‐risk cases were still seen, and our results do not imply that a system based purely on referral for suspected critical CHD would or would not be equally effective as the current system.

Conclusions

In a large population‐based study, we found that the rate of prenatal diagnosis of critical CHD continued to increase despite the COVID‐19 pandemic and reduction in fetal cardiology case volumes. These encouraging findings reflect not only the resilience of the Canadian healthcare system, but also the improved training of obstetric ultrasound providers in recent years. Further investigation is warranted to identify specific factors that have contributed to this success.
