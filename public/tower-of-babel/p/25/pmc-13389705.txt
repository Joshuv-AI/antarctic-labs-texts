In vivo tolerability of topical telavancin versus vancomycin in a murine ocular model
Authors: Inès Fenniri, Paulo J M Bispo, Camille Andre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMulticentre surveillance studies show increasing resistance among ocular MDR bacteria causing sight-threatening infections, especially MRSA. MRSA is associated with poor visual outcomes despite treatment with topical fortified vancomycin (25–50 mg/mL), the current standard of care. We previously demonstrated that telavancin exhibits approximately 16-fold greater in vitro potency than vancomycin against ocular MRSA isolates, with lower cytotoxicity.ObjectivesTo compare the in vivo ocular tolerability of topical telavancin and vancomycin at 25 and 50 mg/mL in a murine maximum-tolerated-dose model.MethodsImmunocompetent female BALB/c mice (n = 24; six groups, four/group) received 20 instillations, 15 min apart over 5 h, of telavancin or vancomycin (25 or 50 mg/mL) or matching vehicles [250 or 500 mg/mL hydroxy-propyl-β-cyclodextrin (HPβCD) for telavancin; 0.9% NaCl for vancomycin]. Ocular irritation was graded 30 min after the final dose and cage-side observations were recorded 1, 3 and 5 h.ResultsVancomycin showed dose-dependent toxicity. At 25 mg/mL, all mice (4/4) exhibited hyperaemia, chemosis and eyelid closure; at 50 mg/mL, all had eyelid closure, chemosis, hyperaemia and corneal dryness, and 2/4 had white discharge. Telavancin 25 mg/mL was well tolerated: only 1/4 displayed mild chemosis and squinting, with no eyelid closure. Telavancin 50 mg/mL caused eyelid closure, chemosis and hyperaemia in 4/4 mice. The 250 mg/mL vehicle caused no clinically relevant ocular changes, although 1/4 mice showed squinting; 500 mg/mL vehicle induced mild chemosis and hyperaemia in 4/4 mice.ConclusionsThe telavancin 25 mg/mL formulation was better tolerated than the fortified vancomycin formulation tested in this model. These findings support further preclinical evaluation for MDR Gram-positive ocular infections.

Introduction

Gram-positive bacteria, especially S. aureus, CoNS and S. pneumoniae are leading causes of ocular infections.1,2 Nationwide multicentre surveillance studies show increasing and remarkably high antibiotic resistance levels among ocular pathogens, especially for MRSA,3,4 resulting in treatment failures with serious consequences, such as visual loss and eye evisceration.5,6

For decades, vancomycin was viewed by many as the gold standard for the treatment of severe infections caused by MDR MRSA isolates and remains the standard of care in ophthalmology to treat such infections.7 However, vancomycin frequently induces ocular irritation,8 has slow bactericidal activity9 and exhibits creeping minimum inhibitory concentrations against resistant isolates.10 Moreover, even when patients are treated with fortified vancomycin eye-drops (25–50 mg/mL), MRSA infections engender a high risk of poor visual outcomes.11,12

Telavancin is a lipoglycopeptide derivative of vancomycin used for systemic MRSA infections.13–15 This new molecule contains a lipophilic side chain that is not present in vancomycin, increasing its half-life and enhancing membrane anchorage, resulting in enhanced antimicrobial potency.16 Because this lipophilic side chain limits aqueous solubility, an ophthalmic formulation requires a solubilizing vehicle. We therefore used hydroxy-propyl-β-cyclodextrin (HPβCD), a solubilizer commonly used for intravenous preparations of telavancin, with a well-tolerated ocular profile.17,18 In our recent in vitro study of ocular Staphylococcus isolates, telavancin showed greater potency against ocular MRSA (MIC90 0.12 mg/L versus 2 mg/L) and lower cytotoxicity to human corneal epithelial cells than vancomycin.19 We therefore conducted an exploratory in vivo maximum-tolerated-dose (MTD) study comparing topical telavancin and vancomycin, the results of which are presented in this brief report.

Materials and methods

Ethics

All procedures complied with the International Guiding Principles for Biomedical Research Involving Animals (CIOMS), the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and were approved by the PDS Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol CNS002-02232022).

Study design

A MTD assessment was performed to compare the tolerability of topical telavancin (Cumberland Pharmaceuticals, USA) and vancomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) formulations. Solubility enhancer hydroxy-propyl-β-cyclodextrin (HPβCD) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), commonly used for intravenous preparations of telavancin, was used to dissolve the compound instead of DMSO to avoid its cytotoxicity. Six groups of immunocompetent, uninfected female BALB/c mice (n = 4 per group; total n = 24) received 20 instillations of drugs or vehicle into the right eye at 15-min intervals over 5 h (Table S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC Online), mimicking the loading phase used clinically for sight-threatening keratitis.7 Groups 1 and 2 received vehicle only [250 or 500 mg/mL (HPβCD) in 0.9% NaCl], Groups 3 and 4 received vancomycin (25 or 50 mg/mL in 0.9% NaCl), and Groups 5 and 6 received telavancin (25 or 50 mg/mL in the HPβCD vehicle). Each instillation volume was 5 µL per mouse. All groups were dosed concurrently under identical conditions. The right eye of each animal was treated to standardize instillation and the untreated contralateral (left) eye served as an internal control.

Dosing solutions were prepared fresh daily, filtered (0.22 µm) and stored at 4°C until use. Humane endpoints were assessed prior to each instillation. Cage-side observations were performed at 1, 3 and 5 h after the first dose to monitor systemic toxicity and general health. Ocular scoring was conducted before dosing and 30 min after the final instillation. Photographs of both eyes were taken at these same time points.

Source of mice

Female BALB/c mice (7–8 weeks old; n = 24) were obtained from BioLASCO Taiwan (AAALAC-accredited Charles River licensee). Animals were housed in individually ventilated cages (GM500 IVC seal safe plus; Tecniplast) at 20°C–24°C and 30%–70% humidity under a 12 h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to sterilized standard laboratory diet (Oriental Yeast) and autoclaved tap water. Cages contained sterile aspen chip bedding and enrichment (autoclaved tunnels). Room parameters were monitored continuously and recorded twice daily. Twenty-four female BALB/c mice (7–8 weeks old) were randomized into six groups (n = 4 mice per group).

Formulation and preparation of dosing solutions

Vancomycin hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and telavancin (Cumberland Pharmaceuticals, USA) powder were stored at −20°C and HPβCD (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) powder at room temperature. On each study day, dosing solutions were prepared 30 min before the first instillation. For vancomycin, 17.2 or 35.3 mg of powder (corresponding to 16.1 and 33.0 mg active vancomycin, respectively) were dissolved in 0.9% NaCl to yield 25 and 50 mg/mL solutions. For telavancin, 17.4 or 35.0 mg of powder (14.7 and 29.7 mg active telavancin) were dissolved in HPβCD vehicle (250 or 500 mg/mL HPβCD in 0.9% NaCl) to yield 25 and 50 mg/mL solutions (Table S5). Each formulation was vortexed for 30 s, pH measured using a calibrated pH metre and sterile filtered [0.22 µm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) syringe filter] into labelled, prechilled vials. Prepared solutions were kept at 4°C and used within 4 h of preparation. The pH values of all formulations were measured and recorded (Table S5).

Treatment and evaluation

Vancomycin, telavancin and their respective vehicles were instilled (5 µL per dose) into the right eye of each mouse every 15 min (Q15 min) over a 5-h period, for a total of 20 applications as detailed in Table S1. This intensive schedule was chosen to mimic the early clinical management of sight-threatening bacterial keratitis:7 compressing the regimen into 5 h yields a cumulative number of instillations comparable to one day of hourly therapy while enabling a MTD assessment in mice. General health and systemic signs (piloerection, response to gentle touch, posture, aggressiveness, pain and distress, hyper/hyposensitivity, eye appearance, eye scratch behaviour and lacrimation) were evaluated by cage-side observation at 1, 3 and 5 h after the first dose. Eyes were examined immediately before dosing and 30 min after the final instillation according to predefined criteria (Table S2). Ocular scoring was performed under high-intensity illumination as previously described.20 Assessments were performed by trained personnel and not under blinded conditions. Photographs of both eyes were taken at each ocular observation time point.

Results

Vancomycin 50 mg/mL and telavancin 50 mg/mL demonstrate comparable irritation

In both high concentration groups, vancomycin and telavancin produced marked ocular toxicity. At 50 mg/mL, vancomycin caused eyelid closure in all mice (4/4) from the fourth dose onward, whereas telavancin 50 mg/mL induced partial or complete closure in three of four mice at the fourth dose (3/4, Table S3) and in two of four mice at the 12th dose (2/4, Table S4). One animal developed white ocular discharge after the 12th instillation of 50 mg/mL vancomycin (Table S4). Thirty minutes after the final instillation, all mice in the 50 mg/mL groups exhibited marked piloerection and hypo-responsiveness to external stimuli (Table 1). In addition, all vancomycin treated mice showed a hunched posture (Table 1). Eyes were closed or squinting, with chemosis and conjunctival hyperaemia in both groups (Table 1). In the 50 mg/mL vancomycin group, corneal dryness (n = 4) and white discharge (n = 2) were observed in some animals (Table 1). No corneal ulceration was observed (Figure 1). After the last dose, the 500 mg/mL vehicle induced conjunctival hyperaemia and chemosis in all mice (4/4), without squinting, corneal dryness, discharge or ulceration (Table 1).

The tested telavancin 25 mg/mL formulation appeared better tolerated than the tested vancomycin 25 mg/mL formulation

At the lower concentration, vancomycin elicited early ocular signs (4/4 mice) and systemic discomfort (1/4 mice) by the fourth instillation, characterized by reduced activity and eyelid closure (Table S3). By the 20th dose, all mice exhibited eyelid closure, piloerection and persistent hypoactivity (Table 1). Slit-lamp examination revealed chemosis and conjunctival hyperaemia in all mice (4/4), without corneal ulceration (Table 1; Figure 1). In contrast, telavancin 25 mg/mL was well tolerated: up to the fourth dose no behavioural abnormalities or ocular changes were observed, except for one mouse that showed squinting (Table S3). By the 12th instillation, piloerection was noted in all animals, and eyelid closure was observed in one mouse (1/4, Table S4). By the 20th dose, only one mouse showed mild chemosis and squinting (1/4); the remaining mice appeared normal (Table 1; Figure 1). In the 250 mg/mL HPβCD vehicle group, no ocular irritation was recorded after the last dose; one mouse exhibited slight squinting (1/4) (Table 1).

Discussion

Antimicrobial resistance within and beyond ophthalmology is a costly pandemic that poses a serious public health threat worldwide.21 Nationwide surveillance studies, such as the ARMOR study, found that approximately 70% of the ocular MRSA isolates in the U.S. were resistant to at least one fluoroquinolone antibiotic.22 In our hospital, we found that more than 60% of MRSA causing ocular infections were MDR.2 These findings underscore the urgent need for novel, better-tolerated topical agents with sustained potency against MDR staphylococci. To fill this gap, we are working on the development of novel topical formulations using newer lipoglycopeptides with enhanced anti-staphylococcal activity.19

Using a rigorous murine MTD protocol, we showed that telavancin 25 mg/mL eye drops were better tolerated than vancomycin 25 mg/mL. Only one of four telavancin-treated eyes showed mild conjunctival changes, whereas 25 mg/mL vancomycin inflamed all eyes and triggered systemic stress behaviours. The 250 mg/mL HPβCD vehicle produced no systemic signs and no clinically relevant ocular findings (1/4 mice squinting), indicating that the vehicle alone did not account for the difference observed at 25 mg/mL. However, the vancomycin and telavancin 25 mg/mL formulations differed in pH (3.65 versus 5.00; Table S5). At 50 mg/mL, both antibiotics caused severe chemosis and eyelid closure. The acidic pH (3.34–5.00; Table S5) and cumulative exposure may contribute to the high-dose toxicity observed. These in vivo data extend our previous in vitro observations of lower telavancin cytotoxicity on human corneal epithelial cells.19 Although telavancin’s dual mechanism of action (cell–wall inhibition plus membrane depolarization)15 has been associated with greater antimicrobial potency, its relationship to ocular tolerability was not evaluated here.

This study has several limitations. It is an exploratory, descriptive MTD assessment in a small number of animals (n = 4 per group). The telavancin and vancomycin formulations differed in vehicle and pH and osmolality were not measured. Tolerability was assessed over a short observation period, in uninfected eyes. The findings should therefore be interpreted as specific to the formulations and conditions tested. Further studies should evaluate the ocular PK/PD profile of topical telavancin and its efficacy in relevant infectious models.

In summary, the tested topical telavancin 25 mg/mL formulation appeared better tolerated in vivo than the tested fortified vancomycin formulation in this murine model and supports further formulation optimization and efficacy studies.
