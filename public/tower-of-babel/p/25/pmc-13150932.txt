Endothelial TRPV1 Drives Choroidal Neovascularization in Wet Age-Related Macular Degeneration via NF-κB Signaling
Authors: Na Sun, Jing Li, Jianhui Wang, Jiajing Wang, Tianxing Chen, Yani Wang, Cheng Liu, Shuai Yang, Tengteng Yao, Huazhang Feng, Zhaoyang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeWe investigated the effects of endothelial cell transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) activation on neovascularization and endothelial cell–microglial crosstalk, aiming to clarify the mechanisms underlying choroidal neovascularization (CNV) in wet age-related macular degeneration (wAMD).MethodsWe performed bioinformatics analysis on transcriptomes of retinal pigment epithelium–choroidal tissues from wAMD patients. Vascular leakage was assessed in laser-induced CNV and TRPV1 knockout (TRPV1−/−) mice using fundus fluorescein angiography, optical coherence tomography, hematoxylin and eosin staining, and isolectin B4 staining. TRPV1 expression in CNV mice was determined via immunofluorescence, western blotting, and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction analyses. Angiogenesis and microglial activation were examined via pharmacological intervention, siRNA silencing, and co-culture assays. Physiological TRPV1 agonists were analyzed by public metabolomics datasets.ResultsTRPV1 was upregulated in patients with wAMD and mice with laser-induced CNV. TRPV1 activation exacerbated vascular leakage in CNV mice, whereas inhibition or knockout had the opposite effects. In human umbilical vein and retinal microvascular endothelial cells, TRPV1 activation promoted tube formation, with no measurable effects on proliferation or migration. TRPV1 promoted the release of inflammatory factors from endothelial cells by regulating the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway, which in turn induced pro-inflammatory polarization of microglia and the secretion of angiogenic factors from microglia, further facilitating angiogenesis. Moreover, linoleic acid, a physiological ligand of TRPV1, activated the endothelial TRPV1/NF-κB pathway to promote angiogenesis.ConclusionsEndothelial TRPV1 mediated endothelial dysfunction and microglial activation via NF-κB, thereby promoting CNV, with linoleic acid serving as a key activator. TRPV1 can be considered a potential therapeutic target for treating wAMD.

Choroidal neovascularization (CNV) is a defining feature of wet age-related macular degeneration (wAMD).1 This abnormal angiogenesis disrupts normal tissue architecture and function, leading to severe visual impairment. Currently, the primary treatment for wAMD involves intravitreal injection of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) agents.2–4 Notwithstanding, these therapies require regular retreatment, as many patients develop resistance over time.5,6 Prolonged use may also lead to complications such as retinal atrophy and fibrotic changes,7 underscoring the urgent need for novel treatment strategies.

Previous studies have revealed that CNV has a pathogenic positive feedback loop: Angiogenesis and inflammation mutually reinforce. Excessive vascular outgrowth amplifies inflammation, which accelerates angiogenesis. Vascular endothelial cell–microglia crosstalk is a key regulatory node that sustains this deleterious cycle.8–10 Specifically, diverse inflammatory stimuli can activate endothelial cells (ECs), and activated ECs upregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β),11 which directly promote angiogenesis and promote immune cell recruitment.12 Microglia, the resident immune cells in the retina,13 not only perform traditional biological functions such as synaptic pruning, phagocytosis, and surveillance of neuronal activity but also are involved in blood vessel formation.14 Previous literature has indicated a close relationship between microglia and retinal vascular development. Microglia can help guide the direction of vessel growth.15–17 For example, a study found that, in a model of oxygen-induced retinopathy, microglia could secrete VEGF to promote the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells.17 Therefore, blocking the vicious cycle between endothelial cells and microglia represents a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of CNV.

Transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1), also known as the capsaicin receptor, is a non-selective cation channel belonging to the transient receptor potential superfamily. When activated, TRPV1 mediates calcium ion influx, and it can trigger a cascade of intracellular signaling events, including angiogenesis.18 Previous studies have shown that TRPV1 plays a crucial role in the regulation of vascular functions,19 which can enhance cell proliferation, migration, and tubulogenesis of endothelial cells. However, the precise molecular mechanisms linking endothelial TRPV1 to angiogenic regulation and EC–microglia crosstalk remain unclear. We hypothesize that targeting endothelial TRPV1 exerts dual effects: It directly inhibits EC-driven angiogenesis, and it disrupts the EC–microglia vicious cycle, thereby suppressing CNV progression.

Collectively, our study identified endothelial TRPV1 as a key mediator of CNV in wAMD. TRPV1 was upregulated in wAMD patients, and TRPV1−/− mice exhibited attenuated CNV, confirming its pro-angiogenic role in vivo. In co-cultures, TRPV1 agonist–pretreated human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) induced human microglia clone 3 (HMC3) to upregulate inflammatory/angiogenic factors, which enhanced HUVEC proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis. Mechanistically, TRPV1 activated the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway in HUVECs to drive angiogenesis. Clinically, linoleic acid (LA), a physiological TRPV1 agonist,20 was significantly elevated in wAMD aqueous humor. Together, endothelial TRPV1 promoted CNV via NF-κB signaling and endothelial–microglial crosstalk, supporting TRPV1 as a promising therapeutic target for wAMD.

Materials and Methods

Gene Expression Omnibus Database Analysis

The GSE29801 dataset was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (Supplementary Table S1) and analyzed using R Studio (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted at thresholds of |log2FC|) > 1 and P < 0.05. Relevant data were obtained in compliance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.21

Cell Culture and Treatment

HUVECs were purchased from Fu Heng Biotechnology. (Shanghai, China), human retinal microvascular endothelial cells (HRMECs) were obtained from BioWing Biotechnology (Shanghai, China), and HMC3 cells were obtained from Camel Bio-Tech Rock (Shanghai, China). HUVECs and HRMECs were cultured in endothelial cell medium (ScienCell Research Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. HMC3 cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM; Pricella, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% Gibco fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and 1% Gibco penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Details of cell treatment are presented in the Supplementary Material.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed as previously described.22 RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using SYBR Green (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Target gene expression was quantified using the 2–ΔΔCt method. The sequences of primers used for amplification are shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Western Blotting

Western blotting was performed using a previously described procedure.23 Proteins were extracted from samples using Invitrogen radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA) lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA), blocked, and incubated with primary and secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table S3). Protein bands were visualized using ECL developing solution (FuShenbio Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and quantified using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Determination of Intracellular Ca2+ Levels

Intracellular Ca2+ levels were measured using a Fluo-4 AM Calcium Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. After staining, photographs were recorded using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and the images were analyzed using ImageJ.

Live/Dead Assay

Cell viability was assessed using an Invitrogen live/dead staining kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Photographs were obtained using a Nikon fluorescence microscope and analyzed using ImageJ.

Evaluation of Angiogenic Capacity

The angiogenic capacity of cells and tissues was evaluated using tube formation and choroidal sprouting assays, the procedures of which are described in the Supplementary Materials.

Evaluation of Cell Migration

Cell migratory capacity was evaluated using scratch and Transwell assays, the procedures of which are described in the Supplementary Materials.

Immunocytochemistry

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, blocked with 10% goat serum, and incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S3) at 4°C overnight. Following incubation with Invitrogen anti-mouse/anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (1:800; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 hour at room temperature, immune-positive cells were imaged using a DM6 B Upright Microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), with signal intensity quantified using ImageJ.

Animal Treatment

We established a laser-induced CNV model in mice and evaluated CNV-related phenotypes via multiple methods, including fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA), optical coherence tomography (OCT), and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, immunofluorescence, and choroid–retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) flatmount staining, the procedures of which are described in the Supplementary Materials.

Small Interfering RNA Construction and Cell Transfection

siTRPV1 (5′→3′-GGAAGACCUGUCUGCUGAATT; Zuorun Biotech, Shanghai, China) was transfected into cells (2 × 105/well in six-well plates, pre-cultured for 24 hours) using Invitrogen Lipofectamine 2000 and Invitrogen Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 6 hours, the medium was replaced with complete medium, followed by culturing for a further 2 days prior to sampling.

RNA Sequencing

RNA sequencing was performed as described previously.24 RNA libraries were sequenced using a NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), gene counts normalized via DESeq2, and differentially expressed genes screened at thresholds of q < 0.05 and |log2FC| >1.0 (fold change > 2).

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay

Cytokine levels were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; ABclonal, Woburn, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions, with absorbance measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax iD5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Molecular Docking Analysis

Molecular docking (PyMOL, Chem3D, and AutoDockTools) was used to assess the affinity of binding between LA and TRPV1. The protein structure of TRPV1 (ID: Q8NER1) was obtained from UniProt, and the structure of LA (Compound CID: 5280450) was obtained from PubChem.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using Prism (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA). Results are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA.

Results

TRPV1 Promoted the CNV in a Laser-Induced CNV Mouse Model

Compared with age-matched controls, transcriptomic analysis of RPE–choroidal tissues from the GEO database revealed a significant upregulation of TRPV1 in wAMD patients with CNV (Figs. 1A, 1B; Supplementary Table S1). Immunofluorescence staining showed the co-localization of TRPV1 with vascular endothelial cells in CNV lesions (Figs. 1C, 1D; Supplementary Fig. S1), and, consistently, TRPV1 protein and mRNA levels were significantly elevated in laser-induced CNV model mice (Figs. 1E–H). To elucidate the role of TRPV1 in CNV development, we intravitreally injected laser-induced CNV mice with a TRPV1 agonist (Cap) or antagonist (CPZ). H&E staining revealed that Cap promoted significant CNV development with an enlarged neovascular area (Figs. 1I–K), whereas CPZ caused a marked inhibition of neovascularization. Isolectin B4 (IB4) staining confirmed that Cap enhances, whereas CPZ suppresses, CNV development (Figs. 1L, 1M). Evaluation of vascular function via FFA analysis revealed clear vascular leakage in laser-induced CNV mice, which was exacerbated by Cap and rescued by CPZ (Figs. 1N–Q). Consistently, OCT assessment of retinal thickening (a key CNV feature) in laser-induced CNV mice revealed that neovascular thickening was exacerbated by Cap and attenuated by CPZ (Figs. 1R, 1S). Collectively, these findings indicate that TRPV1 activation promotes significant CNV in a laser-induced CNV mouse model, identifying this protein as a potentially key regulator in CNV pathogenesis.

Endothelial TRPV1 Promoted Angiogenesis In Vitro

Given that endothelial dysfunction is a direct pathogenic basis for the development of CNV,25 we investigated the regulatory role of endothelial TRPV1 in angiogenesis. Compared with non–tube-forming HUVECs, under tube-forming conditions we detected a significant increase in calcium influx, a hallmark of TRPV1 activation26 (Figs. 2A, 2B). To clarify the regulatory role of TRPV1 in HUVEC function in vitro, we subsequently treated HUVECs with Cap or CPZ (Fig. 2C), and, consistent with expectations, Cap promoted calcium influx in HUVECs whereas CPZ abrogated this effect (Figs. 2D, 2E).

Interestingly, although neither the activation nor inhibition of TRPV1 influenced HUVEC migration (Figs. 2F–I) or proliferation (Figs. 2J, 2K; Supplementary Fig. S2), Cap significantly promoted tube formation in HUVECs and HRMECs, whereas CPZ inhibited angiogenic activity (Fig. 2L; Supplementary Figs. S3, S4). Subsequently, we isolated mouse choroidal explants and treated these with Cap or CPZ. Consistently, Cap treatment promoted a significant increase in the area of vascular sprouting in choroidal explants, whereas CPZ reduced angiogenesis (Figs. 2M, 2N).

We subsequently sought to determine the effects of endothelial TRPV1 on tube formation associated with VEGF, a key cytokine regulating angiogenesis, and found that neither the activation nor inhibition of TRPV1 altered the gene or protein levels of VEGF in HUVECs (Supplementary Figs. S5A–S5C). Moreover, we detected no significant difference in cell tube-forming capacity between the VEGF-alone and VEGF+CPZ co-treatment groups (Supplementary Figs. S5D, S5E). Collectively, these findings indicate that TRPV1 can contribute to regulating the tube formation of endothelial cells.

Endothelial TRPV1 Modulated the Inflammatory Polarization of HMC3 Cells

Previous studies have identified dysfunctional EC–microglia crosstalk as a key mechanism exacerbating wAMD.27,28 To investigate whether activated endothelial TRPV1 regulates microglia, we collected conditioned media (CM) from Cap-treated ECs (Cap-EC-CM) and CPZ-treated ECs (CPZ-EC-CM) and exposed microglia to these conditioned media to mimic EC–microglia crosstalk in vitro (Fig. 3A). Compared with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)-EC-CM, Cap-EC-CM promoted and CPZ-EC-CM significantly inhibited HMC3 proliferation (Figs. 3B, 3C). Similarly, wound healing and Transwell assays revealed that Cap-EC-CM promoted and CPZ-EC-CM suppressed HMC3 migratory capacity (Figs. 3D–G).

Subsequently, we assessed microglial inflammation induced by different EC-CMs. Compared with DMSO-EC-CM, Cap-EC-CM induced a marked upregulation of inflammatory cytokines in HMC3 cells, as evidenced by qPCR, western blotting, and immunocytochemical staining (Figs. 3H–L). In contrast, CPZ-EC-CM substantially reduced HMC3 inflammation (Figs. 3H–L). Using ELISA, we measured the levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, which play key roles in microglial activation, in CMs, which revealed elevated and reduced levels of these cytokines in Cap-EC-CM and CPZ-EC-CM, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Moreover, intravitreal Cap injection significantly enhanced microglial inflammatory activation in CNV mice, as evidenced by a significantly stronger intensity of cluster of differentiation 86 (CD86) and ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (IBA-1) fluorescence compared with the control group. In contrast, intravitreal injection of CPZ attenuated microglial activation (Figs. 3M, 3N; Supplementary Fig. S7). Collectively, these findings indicate that TRPV1-activated ECs induce microglial polarization toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype via the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby exacerbating wAMD.

Inflammatory-Polarized HMC3 Regulated the Tube Formation, Migration, and Proliferation of HUVEC via the Secretion of VEGF

Previous evidence has indicated that microglia play essential roles in maintaining endothelial cell function.29,30 By co-culturing ECs and EC-CM–treated HMC3 (Fig. 4A), we found that, compared with the control group, treatment of HMC3 with Cap-EC-CM promoted significant HUVEC and HRMEC tube formation, whereas CPZ-EC-CM-treated HMC3 inhibited this capacity (Figs. 4B, 4C; Supplementary Fig. S8). Consistently, sprouting assays revealed that, whereas Cap-EC-CM–treated HMC3 promoted increases in the area of vascular sprouting, this expansion was inhibited in CPZ-EC-CM–treated HMC3 cells (Figs. 4D, 4E). Moreover, compared with the control group, Cap-EC-CM–pretreated HMC3 promoted a significant increase in migration (Figs. 4F–I) and proliferation of HUVECs (Figs. 4J, 4K), and HUVEC migration and proliferation were inhibited by CPZ-EC-CM–treated HMC3 (Figs. 4F–K).

Given that activated microglia secrete large amounts of VEGF,31 which can regulate the proliferation, migration, and tube formation of ECs in a paracrine manner, we determined VEGF concentrations in different CMs. In line with expectations, compared with the control group, ELISA revealed significantly higher concentrations of VEGF in the supernatant of Cap-EC-CM–treated HMC3 at 48 hours and corresponding reductions in CPZ-EC-CM–treated HMC3 (Supplementary Fig. S9). Thus, microglia activated by dysfunctional ECs secrete larger amounts of VEGF, which in turn modulate endothelial function via paracrine signaling.

Inhibition of TRPV1 Ameliorated Neovascularization

To further characterize the regulation of endothelial TRPV1, we used siRNA (siTRPV1) to knockdown TRPV1 expression in HUVECs and HRMECs, and knockdown efficiency was verified (Figs. 5A–C; Supplementary Fig. S10). siTRPV1 significantly impaired calcium ion influx in HUVECs (Figs. 5D, 5E), and, similar to pharmacological inhibition, siTRPV1 did not affect the proliferation or migration of HUVECs but significantly impaired their tube formation (Figs. 5F, 5G; Supplementary Fig. S11). These inhibitory effects on tube formation were also observed in HRMECs (Supplementary Fig. S12).

To further confirm the TRPV1-mediated regulation of neovascularization, we established laser-induced CNV in TRPV1−/− mice. Compared with wild-type (WT) mice, choroidal sprouting capacity was significantly impaired in TRPV1−/− mice (Figs. 5H, 5I). FFA (Figs. 5J–L) and OCT (Figs. 5M, 5N) images further revealed significant reductions in CNV area and lesion thickness in TRPV1−/− mice, and these observations were consistent with H&E staining (Figs. 5O–Q). Furthermore, IB4 immunofluorescence staining revealed significant suppression of CNV angiogenesis in TRPV1−/− mice (Figs. 5R, 5S). Collectively, these findings indicate that inhibition of TRPV1 had beneficial effects in ameliorating neovascularization.

TRPV1 Regulated Neovascularization via the NF-κB Signaling Pathway

To elucidate the mechanisms whereby TRPV1 regulates angiogenesis, we performed RNA sequencing for HUVECs transfected with siTRPV1 or siNC (small interfering negative control RNA). Principal component analysis and sample correlation coefficients revealed that siTRPV1 altered gene expression profiles in HUVECs (Supplementary Fig. S13A–S13C). Compared with siNC, siTRPV1 promoted an up- and downregulation of 676 and 677 genes, respectively, in HUVECs (Supplementary Fig. S14). Volcano plot and heatmap analyses indicated that TRPV1 knockdown significantly downregulated inflammation-related pathway genes (Figs. 6A, 6B), and Reactome and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses provided further evidence that siTRPV1 downregulates inflammation-related pathways in HUVECs (Figs. 6C, 6D), whereas Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed that TRPV1 knockdown significantly downregulated the NF-κB signaling pathway in these cells (Figs. 6E). This was validated by western blotting, revealing substantially reduced phosphorylation of p65 and IκBα in the siTRPV1 group (Figs. 6F–H). Collectively, these findings indicate that TRPV1 regulates the NF-κB pathway.

To verify whether TRPV1 regulates HUVEC tube formation via the NF-κB pathway, we co-treated HUVECs with Cap and the NF-κB pathway inhibitor IKK-16.32 Western blotting revealed that Cap enhanced the levels of phosphorylated p65 and IκBα, which was markedly blocked by IKK-16 co-treatment (Figs. 6I–K). Moreover, IKK-16 significantly inhibited Cap-induced tube formation in HUVECs and HRMECs (Figs. 6L, 6M; Supplementary Fig. S15), whereas blocking the NF-κB pathway inhibited Cap-induced TNF-α secretion by HUVECs (Supplementary Fig. S16), and HUVECs co-treated with Cap and IKK-16 were characterized by significantly weaker activation of HMC3 compared with HUVECs treated with Cap (Supplementary Fig. S17). These findings indicate that TRPV1-mediated regulation of endothelial tube formation and microglial inflammatory activation are dependent on the NF-κB pathway.

TRPV1 Was Activated by LA in the Aqueous Humor

Previously, it has been established that in humans, different metabolites can activate TRPV1.20,33 To identify the main ligands regulating TRPV1, we analyzed upregulated metabolites in the aqueous humor of wAMD patients (Supplementary material 2).34 LA, a potential TRPV1 agonist, is significantly upregulated in the aqueous humor of wAMD patients.20 Our molecular docking analysis revealed a binding energy of –5.9 kcal/mol between TRPV1 and LA, thereby indicating a favorable interaction (Fig. 7A). In vitro, LA promoted increases in calcium influx in HUVECs, an effect inhibited by siTRPV1 (Figs. 7B, 7C). Consistently, siTRPV1 impaired LA-promoted tube formation in HUVECs and HRMECs (Figs. 7D, 7E; Supplementary Fig. S18). Whereas LA also increased choroidal vascular sprouting area in WT mice, this effect was impaired in TRPV1-knockout mice (Figs. 7F, 7G), indicating that LA effectively promotes angiogenesis by activating endothelial TRPV1.

To investigate whether LA regulates CNV development via TRPV1 in vivo, we intravitreally injected LA into TRPV1−/− laser-induced CNV mice. IB4 immunofluorescence staining revealed that, whereas LA promoted neovascularization in WT mice, TRPV1 knockout abrogated this effect (Figs. 7H, 7I). OCT (Figs. 7J, 7K) and FFA (Figs. 7L–N) analyses revealed that intravitreal LA exacerbated vascular leakage and enhanced CNV thickness in WT mice, whereas LA-aggravated neovascularization was significantly inhibited in TRPV1−/− mice.

Furthermore, we examined whether LA activates the NF-κB signaling pathway via endothelial TRPV1. Western blotting revealed that LA significantly increased p65 and IκBα phosphorylation in HUVECs, an effect that was impaired following TRPV1 knockdown (Figs. 7O–Q). Collectively, these findings indicate that LA regulates angiogenesis and inflammation via TRPV1.

Discussion

CNV plays a predominant role in the pathogenesis of wAMD. In this study, we found that, in wAMD patients, abnormally activated endothelial TRPV1 promotes angiogenesis by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway and mediates microglial activation via the release of inflammatory factors, thereby facilitating CNV development and inflammatory responses. LA, a free fatty acid abnormally elevated in the aqueous humor of wAMD patients, was identified as a key ligand responsible for activating endothelial TRPV1 (Fig. 8). Notably, interventions targeting TRPV1 expression and activity suppressed angiogenesis and ameliorated vascular hyperpermeability. Collectively, our findings provide compelling evidence that endothelial TRPV1 is a key factor inducing CNV development and is a promising therapeutic target for the treatment of wAMD.

Scheme of the detailed mechanism by which TRPV1 promoted pathological neovascularization. The deficiency of endothelial TRPV1 inhibited pathological neovascularization in the CNV model. Mechanistically, elevated LA levels in the aqueous humor of patients with wAMD activated TRPV1, which promoted angiogenesis by triggering the NF-κB pathway in HUVECs. Meanwhile, the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines in TRPV1-activated HUVECs induced microglia to secrete both pro-inflammatory and angiogenic factors, which in turn facilitated angiogenesis. Therefore, targeting the TRPV1/NF-κB pathway may offer a novel therapeutic approach for treating pathological neovascular diseases.

TRPV1 has been implicated in multiple ocular neovascular diseases, including corneal neovascularization and retinal neovascularization.35,36 In this study, we found that the expression of TRPV1 was upregulated in the CNV mouse model and HUVECs during tube formation. Previous studies have demonstrated that TRPV1 contributes to pathological angiogenesis.35 Consistently, our results also showed that intravitreal injection of a TRPV1 agonist significantly increased neovascularization and vascular leakage in the laser-induced CNV mouse model, whereas a TRPV1 antagonist alleviated disease symptoms. Moreover, laser-induced TRPV1−/− mice exhibited reduced vascular permeability and neovascularization. These data extend our understanding of TRPV1 as a critical mediator of pathological ocular angiogenesis and further support its potential as a target for wAMD therapy.

VEGF, a key regulator of ECs, modulates cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis and plays essential roles in the development of laser-induced CNV.37,38 We investigated whether TRPV1 regulates EC function via VEGF and found that, whereas TRPV1 activation promoted tube formation in HUVECs, it has no significant effect on cell proliferation or migration. Moreover, we established that TRPV1 antagonists did not impede VEGF-induced angiogenesis, cell migration, or cell proliferation, findings that are consistent with those reported by O'Leary et al.,35 indicating that endothelial TRPV1 may regulate vascular processes independently of VEGF. Notably, although intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy confers significant clinical benefits, a subset of retinal vascular diseases remains unresponsive to this therapeutic strategy; thus, endothelial TRPV1 may serve as a novel therapeutic target for non-responsive patients.

Chronic inflammation is a key factor promoting the development of CNV and accelerating the pathological progression of wAMD.39,40 The findings of numerous studies have indicated that, by regulating the NF-κB signaling pathway, TRPV1 can contribute to the regulation of inflammation in diverse cell types.41,42 Consequently, we further examined whether TRPV1 is involved in regulating the inflammatory responses of endothelial cells. The findings of our transcriptomic analysis of TRPV1-knocked down HUVECs revealed that cells transfected with siTRPV1 had an impaired tube-forming capacity, along with significantly downregulated expression of NF-κB signaling pathway constituents. Previous studies have shown that EC dysfunction induces the abnormal activation of microglia,43–45 and we confirmed that, by releasing inflammatory factors, TRPV1-activated ECs promote the polarization of microglia to a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Such pro-inflammatory microglia have been proven to play a predominant role in promoting the formation of an intraocular inflammatory microenvironment in wAMD patients.46 Moreover, the inflammatory microenvironment mediated by microglia also provides favorable conditions for angiogenesis, thereby promoting the progression of CNV.47–49 Consistently, we found that EC-activated pro-inflammatory microglia can secrete VEGF and promote EC function via paracrine activity. Our findings thus provide evidence that TRPV1-activated ECs and activated microglia can contribute to a positive “inflammation–angiogenesis” feedback loop.

It has been established that LA can act as a potential angiogenesis-inducing metabolite in vascular-related diseases,50,51 and levels of this fatty acid have been found to be significantly elevated in the aqueous humor of patients with wAMD.34 By activating the NF-κB pathway in microvascular ECs, LA promotes the secretion of inflammatory factors.52 However, although previous studies have indicated that LA may serve as a physiological agonist of TRPV1,20,33 the mechanisms whereby it induces pro-angiogenic effects via endothelial TRPV1 have yet to be established. On the basis of molecular dynamics studies, we demonstrated the favorable binding between LA and TRPV1. Accordingly, our findings indicating that LA can activate the NF-κB pathway by binding to endothelial TRPV1, thereby promoting angiogenesis both in vitro and in vivo, indicate that targeting the LA–TRPV1–NF-κB axis may provide a novel therapeutic strategy for treating wAMD.

To summarize, in this study, we demonstrated that inhibition of TRPV1 significantly attenuated the development of CNV, indicating that TRPV1 may serve as a potential therapeutic target. Mechanistically, LA in the aqueous humor of wAMD patients binds to endothelial TRPV1, thereby activating the intracellular NF-κB signaling pathway to promote vascular tube formation and the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, ultimately inducing CNV. In further research, we will focus on evaluating the efficacy of combined anti-TRPV1 and anti-VEGF therapy, which may represent a multifaceted therapeutic strategy for CNV. However, given that mouse models of CNV have limitations in accurately recapitulating the complex pathological progression of CNV in humans, it will inevitably be necessary to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of TRPV1-targeted interventions in non–human primate models.
