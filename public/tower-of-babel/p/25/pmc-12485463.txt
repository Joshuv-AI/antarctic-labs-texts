Erector spinae plane block for postoperative analgesia in vertebral surgery: an umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses
Authors: Alessandro De Cassai, Maria Bisi, Marco Nardelli, Irene Paiusco, Serafino Talarico, Valentina Fincati, Burhan Dost, Annalisa Boscolo, Paolo Navalesi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe erector spinae plane (ESP) block has gained attention as a regional anesthesia technique for pain management in vertebral surgeries. This umbrella review synthesizes data from systematic reviews (SRs) and meta-analyses to evaluate the effectiveness of the ESP block in reducing postoperative opioid consumption, pain, and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) in patients undergoing vertebral surgeries.MethodsA search was conducted in CENTRAL, Embase, PubMed Central, and Scopus from 2016 to 2025. The authors included SRs and meta-analyses that investigated the use of the ESP block in vertebral surgeries. Primary outcomes were opioid consumption at 24 postoperative hours (measured as milligrams of morphine equivalent), pain scores at 12 and 24 hours, PONV incidence, and the need for additional analgesics. Quality was assessed using the AMSTAR 2 tool.ResultsThirteen SRs were included. The ESP block reduced opioid consumption at 24 postoperative hours (mean morphine equivalents difference, –8.70 to –18.69), although high heterogeneity was observed. Pain reduction at 12 and 24 hours was statistically significant but clinically modest, with most SRs reporting reductions of less than one point in Numeric Rating Scale or Visual Analog Scale pain scales. The ESP block also significantly reduced PONV and additional analgesic use. However, most SRs were rated as low quality due to inadequate pre-registration and justification for excluding studies.ConclusionsThe ESP block demonstrates potential as a multimodal analgesia component in vertebral surgeries, reducing opioid consumption, pain intensity, and PONV. However, high heterogeneity and low methodological quality highlight the need for further research.Keywords: Analgesics, Opioid; Anesthesia, Conduction; Erector Spinae Plane; Meta-Analysis; Nerve Block; Pain, Postoperative; Systematic Review

INTRODUCTION

Vertebral surgeries are associated with significant postoperative pain lasting several days after surgery [1], which can negatively impact recovery, rehabilitation, and overall patient outcomes [2]. For this reason, it is crucial in these procedures to minimize opioid consumption, reduce complications, and enhance patient comfort. In this framework regional anesthesia, when incorporated into a multimodal pain management strategy, appears to be an optimal option for managing pain following vertebral surgery with techniques such as paravertebral block, interfacial plane block, neuraxial anesthesia, and local anesthetic wound infiltration [3,4]. The erector spinae plane (ESP) block, an interfascial plane block first described in 2016 [5], which has emerged as a promising regional anesthesia technique for managing pain in vertebral surgeries, involves injecting local anesthetic into the fascial plane between the erector spinae muscle group and the transverse processes of the underlying vertebrae. Its mechanism of action remains a topic of debate, though the most widely accepted theory suggests a paravertebral-by-proxy effect [6]. However, while paravertebral spread does not occur in all patients, studies have consistently demonstrated that the ESP block reliably affects the dorsal rami of the spinal nerves in all cases [6]. Additionally, its rapid learning curve [7] and low complication rate [8] have contributed to the growing interest in ESP block for vertebral surgery.

The ESP block for vertebral surgeries has been evaluated in randomized controlled trials, with overall evidence synthesized in multiple systematic reviews (SRs) and meta-analyses. It was decided to perform a systematic review of systematic reviews (umbrella review) [9] to consolidate the available evidence and provide a clearer assessment of the analgesic efficacy of the ESP block in vertebral surgery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This manuscript follows the PRIOR [10] reporting guideline for umbrella reviews to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting.

1. Literature search

A study protocol was written before conducting the study and registered on the Open Science Framework (registration reference: 2KTJF) on March 3, 2025.

A systematic literature review was conducted across multiple databases, including the CENTRAL (Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials), Embase, PubMed Central, and Scopus. The search was restricted to articles published in English and the results were limited to articles published since 2016, as the ESP block was first described that year [5]. The search was last updated on March 6, 2025, and the detailed search strategies for each database are available in Supplementary Digital Content (SDC). Additionally, to identify potentially relevant articles not captured by the authors’ search, the reference lists of the included studies were screened. A formal search of the gray literature was not conducted; however, gray literature may have been indirectly included if such studies were cited within the included reviews.

2. Study selection

Two researchers independently screened titles and abstracts of the identified papers in order to select relevant and not-relevant papers. Each citation was reviewed with full-text retrieval of any citation considered potentially relevant. All studies including patients undergoing vertebral surgery (P) receiving ESP block (I), sham block, or no intervention (C) evaluating milligram of morphine equivalent (MME) at 24 hours (main outcome), pain scores at 12 and 24 hours, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) incidence (%) and use of rescue analgesics (%) were included in the analysis; the authors included SRs with or without meta-analysis (S).

3. Data extraction and data retrieval

Two team members independently reviewed and assessed each included study for data extraction. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third author or by contacting the corresponding author.

The extracted data included study characteristics such as first author, year of publication, country, conflict of interest disclosure, prospective protocol registration, and the PICOS criteria—comprising the population studied (P), the intervention evaluated (I) and its comparator (C), the outcomes investigated (O), and the type of studies included (S), the systematic search strategy (databases, registries, reference evaluation, search restrictions, and final included studies), and investigated outcomes (primary and secondary outcomes, additional retrieved outcomes, subgroup and sensitivity analyses).

Additionally, information was collected on the tools used to assess the robustness of the findings, including subgroup and sensitivity analyses, GRADE, trial sequential analysis, and publication bias assessment.

4. Risk of bias assessment

To assess the risk of bias in the included SRs, the AMSTAR 2 (A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews) tool was used, a validated instrument for evaluating the methodological quality of SRs [11].

AMSTAR 2 consists of 16 domains covering critical aspects such as protocol registration, comprehensive literature search, risk of bias assessment in primary studies, and the appropriateness of statistical methods. Each domain is rated as “yes,” “partial yes,” or “no,” and the overall confidence in a review’s quality is categorized as:

- High: No or one non-critical weakness

- Moderate: More than one but less than three non-critical weaknesses, no critical weakness

- Low: One critical flaw with or without non-critical weaknesses

- Critically low: More than one critical flaw with or without non-critical weaknesses

Three authors independently conducted the evaluation. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion.

The risk of bias for the primary studies was extracted from each SR and summarized by evaluating the assessment tool used, the number of authors involved in the risk of bias evaluation, and the overall judgment reported.

5. Statistical analysis

Data synthesis was visualized using R 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). To quantify the degree of overlap in primary studies among the included SRs, the Corrected Covered Area (CCA) was calculated. CCA is a quantitative metric used in umbrella reviews to assess the degree of overlap in primary studies across included SRs.

The treatment effect for continuous outcomes was expressed as mean difference (MD) or standardized mean difference with a 95% confidence interval (CI). The treatment effect for dichotomous outcomes was expressed as an odds ratio, risk ratio, or risk difference with 95% CI according to the retrieved SRs.

3. Study overlap

To analyze the overlap of primary studies included in different meta-analyses, an UpSet plot was generated (Fig. 2) [25].

UpSet plot illustrating the overlap of primary studies across the included systematic reviews. Each set of connected filled dots on the bottom axis represents a unique combination of reviews, while the bars above indicate the number of primary studies shared among those reviews.

An UpSet plot is a data visualization tool that helps display the intersections of multiple sets, especially when dealing with a large number of groups. It represents combinations of sets along the left side with vertical bars, while the size of each intersection is shown on the right side with horizontal bars. This graph makes it easier to visualize complex overlaps, as opposed to a Venn diagram, which becomes difficult to interpret with more than three or four sets.

The authors chose an UpSet plot over a Venn diagram because it is better suited for visualizing intersections between many sets—like the 13 meta-analyses in this analysis. Venn diagrams become increasingly complex and hard to read as the number of groups grows, while the UpSet plot provides a clear, scalable way to display and interpret these overlaps.

The UpSet plot shows that while some primary studies were identified in most of the SRs there are some studies that were retrieved only in some meta-analyses (for example Ma et al. [12] and Wu et al. [24] included five primary studies not included in the remaining SRs).

The authors assessed the quantitative overlap of primary studies across the included SRs using the CCA. With 13 SRs, 42 unique primary studies, and a total of 170 study occurrences, the CCA was calculated as 24.0%, indicating a very high degree of overlap. This substantial redundancy suggests that the findings of the SRs cannot be considered fully independent, limiting the feasibility of pooling their results directly.

While the redundancy is understandable given that the SRs were conducted within a relatively short timeframe (2021–2024), which likely led to similar studies being retrieved during the literature search, Ma et al. [12], Wu et al. [24], and He et al. [22] contributed unique studies to the analysis (five studies, five studies, and three studies, respectively). This can be explained by differences in their search strategies, including variations in search terms and methodologies.

4. Systematic reviews risk of bias assessment

According to the AMSTAR 2 assessment, seven studies were rated as critically low quality, and six as low quality. A detailed evaluation of each domain for each study is available in SDC. The overall low-quality rating was primarily due to two critical factors: the lack of justification for excluding individual studies (item 7, happening in 12 out of 13 included studies; 92.3%) and the failure to pre-register the SR (item 2) (happening in seven out of 13 studies; 53.8%).

5. Primary studies risk of bias assessment

In this paragraph the authors will summarize the risk of bias evaluation of primary studies performed in each of the SRs. All but one of the included SRs used the original Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) tool as Liang et al. [13] applied the updated version, RoB 2. Interestingly, one meta-analysis [19] used both the Cochrane RoB tool and the Jadad scale for risk of bias assessment.

In most SRs, the risk of bias assessment was conducted independently by two investigators. However, in several reviews [15,16,21,23,24], the number of assessors involved in the risk of bias evaluation was not explicitly reported, even though the authors used plural terms when describing their methods. Notably, although all of the studies included a summary table or discussion of the risk of bias findings, none offered a detailed justification for the risk of bias ratings assigned to each individual study.

Regarding the findings, most SRs did not provide an overall judgment of the risk of bias. However, domain-specific analyses revealed that many primary studies were judged to have a high risk of bias—or at least some concerns—particularly in the domain of the blinding of participants and personnel.

Notably, two meta-analyses reached differing conclusions. Viderman et al. [19] reported a high risk of bias in nearly all domains except randomization, while Fu et al. [20] found no issues related to blinding but identified a high risk of bias in the domain of incomplete outcome data for half of the included studies.

6. Outcomes

The results of all the investigated outcomes for each included SR are available for consultation as SDC.

7. Milligram of morphine equivalent at 24 hours

Eight SRs evaluated MME consumption at 24 postoperative hours. However, two recent meta-analyses [23,24] assessed postoperative opioid consumption without specifying the time of analysis and were therefore excluded from the current evaluation.

All included SRs consistently identified the efficacy of the ESP block in reducing opioid consumption at 24 postoperative hours (Fig. 3), with a mean reduction ranging from –8.70 [21] to an upper MD of –18.69 [13]. Statistical heterogeneity was high across all SRs and remained unexplained.

Modified forest plot showing the calculated effect of ESP on MME in the included SRs. MD represents the absolute difference in means between groups, expressed in the original units of measurement (e.g., milligrams of morphine consumption), while SMD standardizes this difference by dividing it by the pooled standard deviation, allowing comparisons across studies with different measurement scales. Please note that MD and SMD reflect effect sizes in different ways and are not directly comparable. Notably, Oh et al. [17] conducted a subgroup analysis comparing the effect of ESP block at the thoracic and lumbar levels but found no significant differences. Similarly, Sun et al. [21] performed a subgroup analysis based on the type of surgery (decompression, discectomy, and fusion), revealing consistently significant reductions in opioid consumption across all groups.

Some of the analyses [12,17,20] suggested the presence of publication bias, whereas others [12,13,18,21,24] did not detect any.

8. Pain scores at 12 postoperative hours

Eleven SRs assessed pain scores at 12 postoperative hours, all reporting a statistically significant effect of the ESP block (Fig. 4). However, the clinical significance of this effect was limited. Only five SRs [12–14,17,23] demonstrated a reduction of at least one point on the numeric rating scale (NRS) or visual analog scale (VAS), with the highest reported reduction being 1.4 points [23].

Modified forest plot illustrating the MD in pain scores between the ESP block and control groups at 12 and 24 hours postoperatively, measured on an 11-point pain scale. Negative values indicate a reduction in pain favoring the ESP block. When both time points are reported, results for each are shown per study. MD: mean difference, ESP: erector spinae plane.

Liang et al. [13] was the only SR to report low heterogeneity for this outcome. In contrast, other SRs, despite analyzing a larger number of patients and studies, exhibited high and unexplained heterogeneity. Ma et al. [12] conducted a subgroup analysis investigating the possible different effect among ropivacaine and levobupivacaine on pain scores at 12 postoperative hours retrieving no differences.

Some analyses [12,13,23] suggested the presence of publication bias, whereas others [18,22,24] did not detect any.

9. Pain scores at 24 postoperative hours

Twelve SRs evaluated pain scores at 24 postoperative hours, all reporting a statistically significant effect (Fig. 4). However, similar to the 12-hour postoperative outcome, the clinical impact was minimal. No SR reported an overall effect greater than one point, with the highest reduction being 0.67 [12,17]. Two SRs [14,20] found low heterogeneity, while the others exhibited high, unexplained heterogeneity. Additionally, Ma et al. [12] conducted a subgroup analysis comparing the effects of ropivacaine and levobupivacaine on pain scores at 24 postoperative hours and found no significant differences.

Two of the analyses [13,23] suggested the presence of publication bias, whereas others [18,20,22,24] did not detect any.

10. Postoperative nausea and vomiting

Eleven SRs evaluated the effect of the ESP block on PONV in patients undergoing vertebral surgery. All SRs reported a statistically and clinically significant reduction in PONV, with relative risk values ranging from a minimum reduction of 0.54 [13] to a maximum of 0.29 [23]. Notably, heterogeneity for this outcome was consistently low to moderate across all analyses.

Some of the analyses [12,23] suggested the presence of publication bias, whereas others [15,18,24] did not detect any.

11. Additional analgesics

Nine SRs assessed the effect of the ESP block in reducing the number of patients requiring additional analgesics. All SRs consistently reported a statistically and clinically significant benefit, with relative risk values ranging from 0.33 [20,23] to 0.38 [15]. However, most SRs noted high statistical heterogeneity, which was not explained by the authors.

One of the analyses [23] suggested the presence of publication bias, whereas others [12,15,18,24] did not detect any.

12. Time to ambulation

Only one SR [13] evaluated the impact of the ESP block on postoperative ambulation. The study found no statistically significant effect, reporting a MD of –0.93 hours (95% CI: –1.97 to 0.07, P = 0.070) with high heterogeneity (I² = 98%).

13. Heterogeneity exploration

High heterogeneity was reported in all of the included SRs. However, the approaches to addressing this issue varied considerably. Several reviews did not attempt to explore potential sources of heterogeneity at all [13–16,18,20]. Others employed sensitivity analyses by sequentially removing individual studies [12,19,22,24], while some conducted subgroup analyses based on specific study characteristics. For example, subgroup analyses were performed according to the type of local anesthetic used [12,23], exclusion of studies published only as letters or abstracts [17], studies not prospectively registered [17], blocks performed after surgical incision [17,21,23], use of non-ultrasound-guided techniques such as freehand approaches [17], studies lacking clearly defined primary outcomes [17], and those involving more invasive procedures such as vertebral fusion surgery [17,23].

Despite these efforts, none of the SRs were able to adequately explain the observed heterogeneity. Only in the review by He et al. [22] was part of the heterogeneity attributed to specific outcomes. However, the rationale for study exclusions and the results of these sensitivity analyses were not clearly reported, limiting the interpretability of their findings. A meta regression strategy was not adopted in any of the included SRs.

DISCUSSION

The present umbrella review synthesizes evidence from SRs and meta-analyses evaluating the efficacy of the ESP block in vertebral surgeries. Despite considerable heterogeneity among the studies, the overall findings support the effectiveness of the ESP block in reducing opioid consumption, postoperative pain scores, and PONV, while also lowering the need for additional analgesics. However, significant variations between studies and inconsistencies in effect sizes necessitate careful interpretation of these results.

Regarding pain score reduction, the ESP block demonstrated a statistically significant effect at both 12 and 24 postoperative hours. However, the clinical relevance of these reductions was limited, as most SRs reported pain score reductions of less than one point. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that in clinical trials with well-structured postoperative pain management protocols, patients in both the intervention and control groups are not expected to experience significant pain. Therefore, the observed differences may be modest by design. Furthermore, it may be misleading to retrospectively apply the concept of the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) as the sole criterion for interpreting effectiveness. MCID is defined as “the smallest difference in score in the domain of interest that patients perceive as beneficial and that would justify a change in patient management in the absence of significant side effects or excessive costs” [26]. Although MCID is frequently used in research to assess clinical relevance, relying solely on this threshold can be problematic. MCID should be interpreted in the context of the interventions performed (such as postoperative analgesics and opioids), the baseline level of pain, and the overall effect size [27,28]. Therefore, even modest differences in pain scores may translate into clinically meaningful reductions in opioid consumption and additional analgesic requirements, as clearly demonstrated by the present results.

In fact, the most consistent finding across the included SRs was the significant reduction in MME at 24 postoperative hours. Eight SRs demonstrated the benefit of the ESP block, with MDs ranging from –8.70 to –18.69. However, statistical heterogeneity remained high and unexplained. This variability may result from differences in anesthetic agents (e.g., ropivacaine vs. levobupivacaine), surgical techniques, or patient populations, highlighting the need for more homogeneous study protocols in future trials and advocating for tailored subgroup analysis in future SRs.

The quality of most of the SRs was evaluated as critically low according to AMSTAR 2, raising concerns about the reliability of some conclusions. In particular, the lack of pre-registration and the failure to report funding sources in many studies introduce a potential risk of bias. Moreover, the UpSet plot revealed that some primary studies were missing from certain meta-analyses, suggesting that suboptimal search strategies or differing inclusion criteria may have led to the omission of potentially relevant data. This variation in study inclusion could have contributed to discrepancies in pooled estimates and may affect the overall consistency of the findings. In the authors’ opinion, the incomplete overlap in primary studies may partially account for the heterogeneity observed among the SRs.

While the findings are promising, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the high heterogeneity across most outcomes limits the generalizability of the results and suggests variations in study protocols, patient demographics, and surgical techniques. Second, the methodological quality of the included SRs was predominantly rated as critically low or low, according to AMSTAR 2, raising concerns about the reliability of some conclusions. Third, the absence of a standardized approach to pain assessment and outcome reporting makes comparisons challenging and reduces the robustness of meta-analytical syntheses. Fourth, this umbrella review suggests that the ESP block is effective compared to non-active controls. However, given that the present findings primarily confirm only a basic treatment effect, the more clinically meaningful question becomes whether the ESP block is superior or inferior to other commonly used regional techniques—such as other fascial plane blocks, paravertebral blocks, or local infiltration. Addressing this comparative effectiveness is crucial to guide reasoned clinical decisions, and the authors believe that future umbrella reviews should focus on this question to better inform clinical decision-making and determine the relative efficacy and utility of the ESP block in practice. Fifth, even though slight differences in PICOS criteria might explain some discrepancies, the lack of comprehensive searches remains a critical limitation.

Although the authors observed discrepancies in the inclusion of primary studies across the SRs—highlighted through the UpSet plot—they did not conduct a new meta-analysis. This decision was based on the scope and methodological aims of the present umbrella review, which were focused on synthesizing and critically appraising the findings of existing meta-analyses rather than re-analyzing primary data. Nonetheless, by mapping the overlap and gaps in study inclusion, this study provides a valuable foundation for future efforts to perform a more comprehensive and updated meta-analysis.

In conclusion, the ESP block demonstrates potential as an effective component of multimodal analgesia in vertebral surgeries, reducing opioid consumption, pain intensity, and PONV incidence. Nonetheless, the variability in study quality and high heterogeneity across outcomes call for more robust and standardized research to validate these findings and optimize clinical practice.
