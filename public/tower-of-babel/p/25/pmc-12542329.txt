Association of serum adiponectin level with glycemic control and atherogenic lipid profile in Sudanese patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Halima Babikir Eltahir, Elmahdi Mohamed Ali, Abdelrahim Osman Mohamed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (type 2 DM) involves two major abnormalities: insulin resistance(IR) and impaired insulin secretion, both of which contribute to the inability to regulate blood glucose levels. Adiponectin, a hormone secreted by adipose tissue, plays a role in glucose metabolism and possesses insulin-sensitizing properties. Additionally, adiponectin may play a central role in protecting against atherosclerosis by reducing inflammation, lipid accumulation, and oxidative stress. It has also been reported to increase nitric oxide production in endothelial cells through the activation of nitric oxide synthase. The aim of this study was to investigate the association of serum adiponectin level with glycemic control and atherogenic lipid profile in Sudanese patients with type 2 DM.Methods This was a case-control study. Patients with type 2 DM (n = 202) and healthy controls (n = 100) were consented and enrolled. Systolic and diastolic blood pressures (mm Hg), weight (kg), and height (m) were measured, and body mass index (kg/m²) was calculated. Blood samples were collected after an overnight fast. Fasting blood glucose (FBG), haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and lipid profile were measured using enzymatic methods. Adiponectin levels were determined using a sandwich ELISA. Multiple regression analysis was conducted to assess the correlation of adiponectin levels with HbA1c and lipid profile parameters.Results Adiponectin levels were significantly lower in patients with type 2 DM compared to healthy controls (p < 0.001). Adiponectin was inversely correlated with HbA1c (β = −0.200, p = 0.005), total cholesterol (TC) (β = −0.140, p = 0.044), and low- density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) (β = −0.162, p = 0.022), and positively correlated with high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (β = 0.160, p = 0.030). No correlation was observed with triglyceride (TG) levels. Patients with type 2 DM also exhibited significantly higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure, body mass index (BMI), fasting blood glucose (FBG), HbA1c, total cholesterol, and LDL-C levels, along with significantly lower HDL-C levels compared to healthy controls (p < 0.05).Conclusions Patients with type 2 DM had decreased serum adiponectin levels. The present study supports that adiponectin levels are associated with glycemic control and an atherogenic lipid profile. Adiponectin can be considered clinically relevant both as a potential dual biomarker and as a therapeutic target for glycemic control and atherogenic dyslipidemia in patients with type 2 DM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-025-02053-5.

Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycemia, which results from pancreatic beta cell dysfunction and insulin resistance [1]. The number of adults diagnosed with DM has been increasing annually, with projections showing that 578 million adults will have diabetes by 2030 and 700 million by 2045 [2]. The largest proportional increase in the number of adult diabetics among the World Health Organization regions by 2030 is expected to occur in the African Region [3]. DM is a major cause of death in many countries, accounting for 5.1 million deaths worldwide each year [4]. In Sudan, DM is emerging as a significant health problem, with an estimated prevalence exceeding 12% [5]. DM carries a high risk of disability and leads to a decreased quality of life because of serious complications, including diabetic neuropathy, retinopathy, nephropathy, and atherosclerotic vascular disease [6].

The diagnosis of DM is based on multiple measurements of blood glucose and is generally classified into type 1 DM and type 2 DM [1]. Type 2 DM is one of the most common metabolic disorders worldwide, accounting for 90%–95% of all cases. In developed countries, Type 2 DM has a 1.2% prevalence, while developing countries have a prevalence approximately four times higher [7, 8]. The pathogenesis of type 2 DM is due to two major abnormalities: defective insulin secretion by pancreatic β-cells and the inability of insulin-sensitive tissues to respond to insulin (insulin resistance), which leads to the inability to regulate blood glucose levels [9]. With β-cell dysfunction, insulin secretion is decreased, limiting the body’s capacity to maintain glucose homeostasis. In contrast, insulin resistance contributes to increased glucose production in the liver and.

decreased glucose uptake in the muscle, liver, and adipose tissue. When both β-cell dysfunction and IR are present, hyperglycemia is amplified, leading to a metabolic imbalance that activates inflammatory processes, induces oxidative stress, and gives rise to type 2 DM complications [10, 11]. Good glycemic control, initiated early, prevents these complications; however, prolonged poor control is not easily reversed [12].

Adipose tissue, besides being a fat store, secretes several hormones and proteins termed adipokines [13]. Adipokines can be classified into anti-inflammatory adipokines, which play protective and beneficial roles, and pro-inflammatory adipokines, which promote inflammation and insulin resistance [14]. Adipose tissue dysfunction is a key factor in the development and complications of type 2 DM. Several studies show that disturbances of adipokine secretion and imbalance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory adipokines may contribute to insulin resistance and/or impairment of insulin production, which can cause endothelial dysfunction and pro-atherogenic states [15]. Adiponectin is an important adipokine that influences both insulin sensitivity and inflammation, which are closely involved in the development of type 2 DM [12, 16]. Adiponectin is a 244– amino acid collagen-like protein mainly produced in adipocytes, encoded by the adipose most abundant gene transcript 1 (APM1) located on chromosome 3q27, a region that has been investigated in different populations in the search for genetic variants that are significantly related with the pathogenesis of type 2 DM and associated complications [17, 18]. Its production can sometimes occur or be up-regulated in other tissues such as the liver, skeletal muscle, and cardiac muscle, especially in the presence of inflammation; it acts as a hormone with anti-inflammatory and insulin-sensitizing properties by increasing glucose uptake in muscle cells and reducing gluconeogenesis in the liver, thereby contributing to better glycemic control [17, 19]. Serum adiponectin levels decrease in parallel with the progression of reduced insulin sensitivity; it is reported to decrease in patients with type 2 DM, obesity, and coronary heart disease [20]. Atherosclerosis is considered an inflammatory disease. The first stage of atherogenesis involves endothelial dysfunction, characterized as decreased nitric oxide (NO) production, and is a key causal link between insulin resistance and cardiovascular diseases (CVD). Adiponectin may play a central role in protection against atherosclerosis by reducing inflammation, lipid accumulation, and oxidative stress. It increases nitric oxide (NO) production in endothelial cells by the activation of NO synthase [21, 22]. Adiponectin exerts its effects by binding to two receptors, AdipoR1 and AdipoR2, both of which are expressed abundantly in human pancreatic beta cells. AdipoR1 is also abundantly expressed in the muscle, activating adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathways that inhibit gluconeogenesis. AdipoR2 is highly expressed in the liver and adipose tissues and activates peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARαα) pathways that promote energy dissipation to prevent inflammation and oxidative stress and regulate metabolic gene expression and insulin sensitivity in insulin target tissues [23, 24].

T-cadherin has also been identified as a third non-signaling receptor of adiponectin. It is abundantly expressed in injured vascular endothelial and smooth muscle cells in atherosclerotic regions. It is critical to signal transduction of the adiponectin receptor pathway; this mediates the anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects of adiponectin on endothelial cells [25, 26]. Patients with type 2 DM are at a 2- to 4-fold higher risk of cardiovascular mortality compared with non-diabetic subjects, mainly attributed to hyperglycemia, dyslipidaemia, and inflammatory mechanisms [27]. Recently, ‘metabolic dyslipidaemia’ is commonly used to reflect abnormal lipid patterns dominant in patients with type 2 DM, such as increased total cholesterol (TC), increased triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [28, 29]. Elevated LDL-C has been reported as a primary component of diabetic dyslipidaemia, contributing to the risk of CVD [30]. Patients with type 2 DM often exhibit an atherogenic lipid profile, which is defined as an increase in the serum total cholesterol, LDL-C, and TG levels and a decrease in the HDL-C level. It has been associated with an early onset of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [31]. The key therapeutic goal for the management of type 2 DM is to maintain good glycemic control to prevent micro- and macro vascular complications. It can be evaluated using glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), which reflects the average blood glucose level in patients with type 2 DM over 3 months and is considered a better predictor of coronary artery disease (CAD) [32, 33]. The identification of factors influencing glycemic control in patients with type 2 DM is crucial to institute appropriate interventions to prevent complications. The correlation of adiponectin with HbA1c and the lipid profile may enable its use as a dual marker for glycemic control and dyslipidaemia. This study aimed to investigate the association of adiponectin level with glycemic control and atherogenic lipid profile in Sudanese patients with type 2 DM.

Materials and methods

This was a case-control study conducted at the Jabir Abu Eliz Diabetic Centre in Khartoum State, Sudan. Two hundred and two patients with type 2 DM aged 40–59 years were included in this study. The inclusion criteria for the diabetic subjects involved all type 2 DM attending the Jabir Abu Eliz Diabetic Centre during the period from February 2014 to 2017. Type 2 DM was identified. A subject was considered diabetic if the fasting blood glucose (FBG) value was 7 mmol/L (126 mg/dl) or greater, or if the 2-hour glucose value during a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dl) or greater [7]. The following exclusion criteria were applied: patients with type 1 DM and women with gestational diabetes were excluded; subjects were also excluded if they were critically ill or unwilling to be included in this study. Subjects were also excluded if they had acute or chronic inflammatory illnesses, any other chronic diseases, or a history of steroid use. One hundred age-matched healthy non-diabetic volunteers from the community of Khartoum State were included as a healthy control group. All the control subjects had normal FBG (< 110 mg/dL) and HbA1c levels and were not relatives of the patients. The sample size was calculated using the single proportion formula: N = Z²pq/d² [34]. Where: N = sample size. Z = the standard normal deviation, usually set at 1.96, which corresponds to the level of the 95% confidence interval. P = the proportion in the target population estimated to have a particular characteristic. q = 1.0 – p. d = the degree of accuracy desired, set at 0.07. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Ethical Research Committee of the Board of Medical and Health Sciences, University of Khartoum, Khartoum State, Sudan (December 4th, 2013). Informed written consent was obtained from all participants. All participants were interviewed using a questionnaire to collect data, including age, sex, duration of the disease, and information related to diagnosis and follow-up (Appendix 1). A clinical examination was performed.

Blood sampling

Blood samples were collected from all subjects in the morning after a 6–8 h overnight fast using EDTA, sodium fluoride, and plain containers and were processed as follows: EDTA blood samples were used for HbA1c analysis during the same day. Then, the plasma and serum were separated by centrifugation and were immediately stored at − 80 °C for subsequent assays.

Laboratory analyses

FBG, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, and TG levels were measured using enzymatic colorimetric methods. HbA1c was measured using boronate-affinity binding enzymatic methods using Nycocard HbA1c (Axis-shieldPoC AS, Oslo, Norway) [35]. Serum levels of adiponectin were measured using a quantitative Human Adiponectin Immunoassay kit (Biorbyt, Cambridge, UK) (Catalogue Number: orb54803). The assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions [36].

Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20.0 (IBM Corp., USA, 2010). The normality of the variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution (parametric) were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non- normally distributed continuous variables (non-parametric) were presented as mean rank, as the data distributions for the two groups had different shapes. Categorical variables were described using numbers and percentages. Comparisons between two independent groups for normally distributed continuous variables (adiponectin, HDL-C) were conducted using Student’s t-test, while the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for non- normally distributed continuous variables (age, systolic blood pressure [SBP], diastolic blood pressure [DBP], body mass index [BMI], FBS, TG, TC, LDL-C). Correlations between variables were evaluated using the Pearson correlation coefficient for normally distributed variables and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient for non-normally distributed variables. Subgroup correlation analyses were performed based on age (40–49 vs. 50–59 years) and gender (male vs. female). To explore the association of adiponectin levels with glycemic control and atherogenic lipid profile, multiple linear regression analysis adjusted for sex and age was used for independent variables that showed significant correlations. For all tests, p-values < 0.05 (two-tailed) were considered statistically significant.

Results

Three hundred and two (302) subjects participated in the present study: 202 patients with type 2 DM and 100 healthy controls. The patients with type 2 DM group comprised 107 males (53%) and 95 females (47%), while the control group included 66 males (66%) and 34 females (34%). Demographic characteristics and biochemical measurements were compared using the t-test and Mann-Whitney test, as described in Table 1. There was no significant difference in age between patients with type 2 DM (mean rank = 51.91) and healthy controls (mean rank = 51.02), P > 0.05. SBP and DBP were significantly higher in patients with type 2 DM compared to healthy controls (P < 0.001). Body mass index (BMI) was also significantly increased in patients with type 2 DM compared to healthy controls (P < 0.05). Biochemical variables, including FBG, HbA1c, TC, and LDL-C, were significantly elevated in patients with type 2 DM compared to healthy controls (P < 0.001). HDL-C was significantly lower in patients (P ≤ 0.001). TG levels between the groups were not significant (P > 0.05). Patients with type 2 DM had significantly higher serum adiponectin levels compared to healthy controls (P ≤ 0.001) (Table 1). There were no significant differences in adiponectin levels between male and female patients with type 2 DM (P > 0.05) (Table 2). Correlation analysis revealed that adiponectin levels showed significant inverse correlations with HbA1c (P ≤ 0.001), TC, and LDL-C levels (P ≤ 0.05), and a direct correlation with HDL-C levels (P < 0.05) among patients with type 2 DM. In healthy controls, adiponectin levels did not show any significant correlations with biochemical parameters (P > 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the association of adiponectin levels with glycemic control, and atherogenic lipid profiles in Sudanese patients with type 2 DM. Adiponectin levels were significantly lower in patients with type 2 DM compared to the healthy controls. These findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting that adiponectin plays a role in glucose metabolism, with low adiponectin levels frequently reported in patients with type 2 DM [37–39]. The reduced adiponectin levels observed in patients with type 2 DM are believed to be associated with disorders of glucose and lipid metabolism, which may contribute to insulin resistance [40]. The anti-diabetic effects of adiponectin have been reported to promote pancreatic beta-cell function and increase insulin sensitivity by stimulating hepatic insulin signaling through enhanced expression of insulin receptor substrate 2 (IRS-2) [41]. Other potential mechanisms underlying the glucose-lowering effects of adiponectin include suppression of hepatic gluconeogenesis, stimulation of fatty acid oxidation in the liver and increased glucose uptake and fatty acid oxidation in skeletal muscle [42]. In this study, adiponectin was inversely correlated with HbA1c levels, consistent with previous research [43, 44]. This suggests that adiponectin could serve as a marker for evaluating glycemic control and disturbances in glucose metabolism in patients with type 2 DM. The reduction in serum adiponectin levels in patients with type 2 DM was independent of their body mass index (BMI). This contrasts with the previous studies that reported a negative correlation between adiponectin levels and both BMI and insulin resistance in diabetic patients [45, 46]. However, other studies have supported our findings, showing no correlation between adiponectin and BMI in diabetic patients [44, 47]. This discrepancy may be explained by the fact that adiponectin concentrations are more closely associated with insulin sensitivity rather than insulin resistance in patients with type 2 DM [48]. These findings are in agreement with previous studies [49, 50]. Serum adiponectin levels did not vary significantly between genders; however, significant differences were observed between patients and healthy controls. Adiponectin was found to be correlated with various lipid profile parameters, particularly HDL-C. The findings of the current study showed that the serum lipid profiles of patients with type 2 DM were abnormal compared to those of the healthy controls, whose lipid profiles were mostly within normal ranges. This confirms the well-established association between dyslipidaemia and type 2 DM [28, 29, 51]. Several studies have demonstrated that HDL-C levels are a strong inverse predictor of CVD events; HDL-C retards the formation of atherosclerotic lesions by removing excess cholesterol from cells and preventing endothelial dysfunction [52]. Patients with type 2 DM exhibited elevated total cholesterol and LDL-C levels and lower HDL-C levels, along with reduced HDL-C levels. These lipid parameters are integral components of the atherogenic lipid profile [31]. In this study, the positive correlation observed between serum adiponectin levels and HDL-C levels in patients with type 2 DM aligns with previous research reporting a similar association in individuals with type 2 DM [53–55]. This relationship may be explained by the potential mechanisms through which adiponectin increases HDL-C levels, including enhanced hepatic production of.

apolipoprotein AI (apo-AI), the primary apolipoprotein of HDL-C; increased expression of ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1), which facilitates HDL-C assembly via reverse cholesterol transport; activation of lipoprotein lipase (LPL), essential for HDL-C maturation; and regulation of HDL-C concentration by reducing its catabolism and inhibiting hepatic lipase activity [51, 56, 57].

Several studies have reported that elevated plasma LDL-C levels contribute to the initiation and progression of atherosclerotic plaques, thereby increasing the risk of CAD and adding to the burden of CVD [58, 59]. In the current study, inverse correlations were observed between serum adiponectin levels and both LDL-C and total cholesterol in patients with type 2 DM. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, which demonstrated an inverse relationship between adiponectin levels and LDL-C as well as total cholesterol [54, 55, 60]. This relationship is hypothesized to result from adiponectin’s ability to increase the production of LDL-C receptors through activation of PPARα in the liver. Consequently, low adiponectin levels are associated with reduced PPAR-α activation, leading to decreased expression of LDL-C receptors in body tissues and resulting in elevated LDL-C levels [43]. A previous study also reported an inverse correlation between serum adiponectin and TG level in patients with DM2 [61]. In contrast, our study found no correlation between serum adiponectin and TG levels in these patients with type 2 DM, which may be attributed to ethnic variations in PPAR-α gene expression and lipoprotein lipase activity. Adiponectin levels and their associations with type 2 DM and CVD vary across different ethnic groups due to genetic and environmental factors. Although this relationship has been studied in various populations, there is a notable lack of research focusing specifically on the Sudanese population, which possesses a unique genetic background and lifestyle. This study is novel as it is the first to investigate the association between serum adiponectin levels, glycemic control, and the atherogenic lipid profile in Sudanese patients with Type 2 DM. The findings aim to fill a significant knowledge gap and provide valuable insights for this population, potentially improving healthcare outcomes. Furthermore, this research may contribute to enhanced risk stratification, prevention strategies, targeted interventions, and a reduction in the incidence of cardiovascular disease among these individuals. Although this study was carefully designed, several unavoidable limitations exist. First, the sample size is relatively small, although the results are promising. To confirm these findings, further research with a larger participant group is necessary. Second, the study was conducted at a single center, with all cases drawn from one diabetes clinic, which may limit the generalizability of the results to the broader population. Third, there is a lack of resources and funding for extensive research in Sudan, including limited access to specialized reagents and kits for measuring adiponectin levels. These materials can be costly and difficult to obtain and maintain in the country, making large-scale studies challenging. This study explored the correlation of adiponectin levels and parameters related to HbA1c and lipid profiles. It’s important to note that correlation does not imply causation. It could be influenced by confounding factors, such as BMI, diabetes medications, or reverse causality. Therefore, our findings suggest a potential link, but further research, particularly longitudinal or interventional studies, is needed to determine the causal direction and underlying mechanisms.

Conclusions

Sudanese patients with type 2 DM had decreased levels of serum adiponectin; the present study supports that adiponectin levels are associated with glycemic control and atherogenic lipid profiles in patients with type 2 DM. Thus, adiponectin can be considered clinically relevant both as a potential dual biomarker and a therapeutic target for glycemic control and atherogenic dyslipidaemia in patients with type 2 DM.
