Response to Corcept’s retraction request on manuscript of “Relacorilant plus nab-paclitaxel for recurrent, platinum-resistant ovarian cancer: a cost-effectiveness study”
Authors: Qiaoping Xu
Published: 2025

Dear Editor and Reviewers,

We are deeply grateful for your time, care, and persistence throughout the erratum process, and we are especially thankful to the expert panel for recognizing the corrected analysis and allowing us to proceed with the erratum version. We offer our sincere and unreserved apologies for the confusion and additional work caused by our earlier inaccuracies.

Below, we provide a respectful, point-by-point reply to J Gynecol Oncol 2025;36:e139 [1], in accordance with the editorial office’s request and grounded in the revisions incorporated into the final manuscript as well as our correspondence with the editors and expert reviewers, to ensure transparency and scientific rigor.

Question 1: Invalid cost estimates

a) The cost of relacorilant is erroneously reported as $1,471.33 per 100 mg, with reference to Lynparza (www.drugs.com/price-guide/lynpraza). However, relacorilant is not (and has never been) commercially available; no published price exists. The authors fail to justify their assumptions. Lynparza does not have a labeled indication for platinum-resistant ovarian cancer and is not an appropriate reference drug.

Response: In the erratum, because relacorilant is not yet marketed, we applied a standard pharmacoeconomic approach: infer the unit price from an approved oral agent with the same mechanism/target. Both relacorilant and mifepristone are selective glucocorticoid-receptor modulators. Contemporary oncology trials use mifepristone 300 mg orally per administration; the key phase II program for relacorilant used 100 mg once daily in the continuous-dosing arm. To maintain unit comparability on an “oral-dose” basis, we mapped 300 mg mifepristone to 100 mg relacorilant to derive relacorilant’s unit cost—base-case: $867.39 per 100 mg (anchored to a 300 mg mifepristone dose). Upon resubmission, the editorial office and external reviewers judged these corrected price assumptions reasonable and acceptable. This is a cost-anchoring device only (no claim of equipotency), and we stress-tested it extensively in sensitivity/threshold analyses to assess robustness. Specifically, we tested how varying the unit price of relacorilant influences the cost-effectiveness of intermittent relacorilant + nab-paclitaxel (IN) vs. nab-paclitaxel monotherapy (N); for example, IN ceases to be cost-effective beyond $3,412 per 100 mg, whereas continuous relacorilant + nab-paclitaxel (CN) remains strictly dominated at all price levels. These price-scenario analyses support the reliability of the cost-effectiveness conclusion under wide price assumptions. All justifications and resulting cost inputs (2024 USD) are reported in Methods (Model inputs for costs) and Table S4.

b) The cost of nab-paclitaxel is misreported as $43.53 per 100 mg, citing the same source (Lynparza). The 2023 market price for nab-paclitaxel 100 mg vial ranged from $1,441.01 to $1,580.06, depending on the manufacturer [2].

Response: We corrected the nab-paclitaxel price to a verifiable U.S. market value—$1,551.30 per 100 mg—and propagated the change through the base case and sensitivity analyses; the updated unit cost and ranges are shown in Table S4, together with sources for all other drug, administration, monitoring, testing, best supportive care, and terminal care inputs.

c) The cost of adverse events does not match the original publications cited in Table 2; raising concerns about data integrity. One of the references cited also appears to be spurious (www.yaozi.com).

Response: We replaced/verified all adverse event (AE) cost sources and list grade ≥3 AE costs with distributions in Table S4; the suspect website has been removed.

Question 2: Incorrect utility estimates

The utility values used for health states and disutility values for adverse events are flawed.

a) The authors cite Elsea et al. [3] for utility weights corresponding to progression-free survival and progressive disease. However, Elsea et al. [3] analyzed a different patient population (homologous recombination deficiency-positive advanced ovarian cancer), which limits the applicability of these estimates to the current study.

b) The utility values reported in Table 1 also do not correspond to the values published in Elsea et al. [3], raising concerns about data integrity.

c) The disutility estimate for fatigue is sourced from Wallace et al. [4], a commentary that does not report disutility weights for fatigue, raising concerns about data integrity.

d) The disutility estimate for leukopenia is cited from Zhu et al. [5], but the reported value does not match the original publication; raising concerns about data integrity.

Response: For issues a) and b), we re-identified pharmacoeconomic literature specific to the same cancer type analyzed in this study and revised the progression-free survival (PFS) and progressive disease (PD) utility values to the correct figures reported therein. For issues c) and d), the discrepancies were due to our checking lapse; we have now updated the disutility values for fatigue and leukopenia to the appropriate estimates. All numerical revisions are presented in Table 1.

Question 3: Misinterpretation of cost-effectiveness results

The statement in the results section “Compared to N, IN has a certain price advantage, but IN will generate an incremental 0.84 QALYs” is inaccurate. According to Table 3, IN has a higher cost ($22,597) than N ($4,606), which contradicts the claim by the authors of a cost advantage.

Response: We revised the Abstract—Results and Results to report the findings explicitly and formally: N was the least costly strategy; IN, compared with N, generated 4.04 additional quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) at an incremental cost of US$129,880.38, yielding an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of US$32,182.70 per QALY, which is below the US$100,000 per QALY willingness-to-pay threshold; CN was dominated by IN. The pairwise CN vs. N result is reported in Table 2.

Question 4: Flawed incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) calculation

The methodology used to compute ICERs in Table 3 is inconsistent with standard economic evaluation guidelines. Cost-effectiveness analyses require treatment strategies to be rank ordered by increasing cost, with ICERs calculated sequentially. Additionally, dominated strategies that are both more expensive and less effective should be excluded. In this study, continuous relacorilant + nab-paclitaxel (CN) is a dominated strategy (higher cost, lower quality-adjusted life years [QALYs]). Therefore, an ICER should not be calculated.

Response: We conduct an incremental analysis with dominance checks: strategies are ordered by increasing total cost (N, then IN, then CN), and ICERs are computed sequentially. Because CN is dominated by IN in this ordering (higher cost with lower effectiveness), CN is excluded from the incremental ICER chain; for transparency, the pairwise CN-versus-N result is reported separately.

Question 5: Inconsistency in reporting

In Fig. 1, the footnote states that the vertical axis shows the cumulative cost of life and the horizontal axis shows the QALYs gained. However, the labels on the x-axis and y-axis of the graphs are inconsistent with the above description.

Response: We have comprehensively reviewed all figures; their axis labels and captions are now accurate and fully consistent with the text.

Question 6: Incorrect interpretation of societal perspective

The authors conclude that IN is more cost-effective than CN and N from a U.S. societal perspective. However, their analysis only considers direct medical costs, omitting essential components such as productivity loss and caregiver burden. A true societal perspective must incorporate these elements.

Response: Due to our writing error, the manuscript presented inconsistent perspectives across sections. We have corrected the perspective to the US healthcare system. Consistent with prior published studies, this perspective includes only direct medical costs borne by the health system—drug acquisition and administration, routine monitoring and disease management, management of adverse events, subsequent lines of therapy, best supportive care, and end-of-life care.

We extend our heartfelt thanks to the editors, the expert panel, and Drs. Parthan and Dobler for their valuable time, patience, and professional guidance. We again offer our sincere apologies for the inconvenience caused by our earlier errors (Data S1 and S2).
