Smoking cessation in India: A narrative review
Authors: Anubhuti Singh, Kislay Kishore
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tobacco is the leading preventable cause of death in the world, and India is the second largest consumer of tobacco. Smoking is associated with a myriad of respiratory diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, pneumonia, and lung cancer. Smoking cessation is the only modality which has shown to reduce lung function decline. Despite established benefits of smoking cessation, its services in India are limited due to time constraints, lack of awareness, and unavailability of resources. Respiratory physicians play an important role in the journey of a patient willing to quit smoking through guidance, motivation, and support. More dedication, in the form of time and effort, is required from physicians’ side to this end. This narrative review was performed to get a better understanding of smoking practices and cessation services in India to identify lacunae and help guide future interventions.

INTRODUCTION

The intoxicating properties of tobacco were first discovered by Christopher Columbus in 1492 on his journey to Tobago islands.[1] He brought it back into Europe, from where it spread to the rest of the world. In India, tobacco farming was introduced by the Portuguese in 1605.[1] In the 500 years since its discovery, tobacco has become the leading preventable cause of death, being a risk factor for six of the eight top most causes of deaths in the world.[2] India is the second largest consumer of tobacco in the world, second only to China.[2] The prevalence of tobacco smoking in India is 10.7% (19.2% among men and 2.0% among women).[3] Almost 30% of Indian adults currently use tobacco (including both smoked and smokeless tobacco).[3] Also, smoking exposure at home is a serious concern in India, with almost 40% adults being exposed to second hand smoke.[3] Tobacco smoke contains not only nicotine (which is responsible for habit formation) but also various noxious gases and carcinogens. Smoking is responsible for multiple respiratory diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, pneumonia, and lung cancer.[4] Quitting smoking recovers the years lost to smoking.[5] Smoking cessation is the only modality which has shown to reduce lung function decline.[6] Although the benefits of smoking cessation have long been established, cessation services in India are limited due to time constraints, lack of awareness, and expertise and unavailability of resources.[7] This narrative review was performed to get a better understanding of smoking practices and cessation services in India, to identify lacuna, and to help guide future interventions, especially from a pulmonary physician’s perspective to make the country as smoke-free as possible.

Why do smokers smoke?

Cigarette contains nicotine, which is a potent euphoriant (more active than cocaine, amphetamine, or morphine).[8] It improves task performance and attention time, reduces anxiety and depression, and diminishes pain. Nicotine exerts its biologic effects on α4β2 receptor (a subset of cholinergic receptors) located on the neurons in the mesolimbic system, causing release of dopamine. Dopamine makes nicotine highly addictive and is responsible for dependence.[8] The majority of smokers pick up the habit during their adolescence.[9] According to the Global Youth Tobacco Survey (GYTS-4; 2019), 7.3% of Indian students (aged 13–15) are current smokers.[10] Among this vulnerable group of population, the common reasons to start smoking are peer influence, parent’s smoking habits, enjoyment, and stress relief.[11] This is the reason tobacco companies target the young population.[12] Cigarettes are easily available at roadside shops situated near schools and colleges, placed alongside other sugary drinks and confectionaries, attracting children and adolescents alike.[12] Thus, cessation services should also be specifically directed towards this group and every step taken to prevent them from picking up the habit.

Respiratory diseases associated with smoking

Cigarette smoke has around 150 harmful substances and more than 50 carcinogens, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), volatile organic compounds (VOCs), phenols, tobacco-specific nitrosamines (TSNAs), and aromatic amines.[13] These constituents are responsible for numerous respiratory diseases. Smoking has been associated with COPD, asthma, pneumonia, and lung cancer, among others.[4] Smoking (both first and second hand) is the single most important risk factor for COPD.[1415] Cigarette smoking causes bronchial hyper-responsiveness and raised total immunoglobulin (Ig) E levels, leading to more frequent and severe exacerbations in patients with asthma.[16] Causal association between smoking and lung cancer was established by multiple seminal studies.[1718] Smoking has also been shown as a risk factor for pneumonia in adults.[19] Smoking increases the rate of progression of sub-clinical infection to active tuberculosis (TB) disease. Around half of deaths among male TB patients are attributable to smoking.[20] Smoking is also associated with increased risk of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and other interstitial lung diseases such as desquamative interstitial pneumonia (DIP) and respiratory bronchiolitis associated interstitial lung disease (RB-ILD).[21]Although a direct causal relationship has not been established, other respiratory disorders which have been associated with smoking are Langerhan’s cell histiocytosis (LCH), respiratory bronchiolitis, Goodpasture’s syndrome, sleep apnoea, and pneumothorax.[8] In addition to these, smoking has deleterious effects on other organ systems as well, including cardiovascular (coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease), oropharyngeal (precancerous lesions), and gastrointestinal (peptic ulcer disease), and has been linked to multiple cancers (bladder, cervix, oesophagus, kidney, larynx, lung, oral cavity, pharynx, pancreas, and stomach), fetal growth retardation, and pre-term delivery.[1]

Smoking in India

Smoking habits in India vary from the Western world in that bidi (7.7%) is more commonly smoked than cigarettes (4.0%).[3] Bidis are made of tobacco flakes wrapped in tendu leaves (mostly hand-rolled) and are produced at the local, cottage level industry. Bidis are easily available at small roadside vendors and are cheaper, making them more accessible than cigarettes. Due to poor processing, bidi smoke is said to have higher concentration of harmful chemicals (comparison of basic properties of bidi and cigarette is provided in Table 1).[2223242526] Recently, Datta et al.[27] analysed the nicotine content of common cigarette and bidi brands available in India and proposed the concept of bidi years wherein 20 cigarette years was found to be equivalent to 43 bidi years. Bidi smokers have lower ventilatory capacity, greater airflow obstruction, and higher prevalence of cardio respiratory symptoms.[28] The bidi industry in India is highly unregulated, flouting norms and laws and making control more difficult than cigarettes.[25]

Why is smoking cessation important?

It has been established that quitting smoking, irrespective of age, increases life expectancy.[5] In his seminal work, Fletcher (1961) conducted a prospective study of lung function of around 800 men (aged 35–59 years) for 8 years.[6] Forced expiratory volume in first second (FEV1) measurements were taken every 6 monthly and presented as slopes, famously known as the Fletcher curves since then. He concluded that FEV1 declines continuously and smoothly with age, smoking accelerates this FEV1 decline, and on quitting smoking, the rate of FEV1 loss goes back to normal, non-smoking levels.[6] Doll et al.[29] have also shown that smokers who quit by 50 years of age have reduction in mortality risk from lung cancer by half, while those who quit by 30 years of age have almost no risk of mortality from lung cancer. A South Korean (N = 1740) study found that smoking cessation after diagnosis of COPD is associated with lower all-cause (17% risk reduction) mortality.[30] Also, smoking cessation has been shown to reduce respiratory symptoms and airway hyper-responsiveness.[31]

Smoking cessation methods: pharmacotherapy

Methods to assist smoking cessation are both pharmacological and non-pharmacological. The drugs approved for smoking cessation are nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), bupropion, and varenicline. Since nicotine is the main addictive constituent of cigarette, replacing nicotine at a lower dose would alleviate withdrawal symptoms and make quitting easier for the patient. This is the principle behind NRT. As compared to placebo, NRT has been shown to increase quit rates by 7% after 6–12 months.[32] NRT is available in the form of short-acting (gum, lozenge, inhaler, and spray) as well as long-acting (transdermal patch) variants. A combination of a long-acting NRT with any short-acting NRT (for breakthrough cravings) is the recommended method for quitting.[32] Bupropion hydrochloride is a dopamine reuptake inhibitor indicated for use as a smoking cessation modality in smokers who have treatment failure or poor tolerance to NRT.[32] Bupropion has been shown to be a highly effective therapy for smoking cessation.[33] Varenicline is a partial agonist as well as antagonist to the α4β2 receptor.[34] Varenicline has been shown to be the most effective monotherapy for smoking cessation with the highest quit rates.[3536] It can be given as a combination therapy with NRT and bupropion, especially for heavy smokers.[36] Other adjunctive modalities for aiding cessation are being investigated. Recently, Singh et al.[37] have demonstrated via a randomised study that flaxseed oil (containing omega-3 fatty acids) reduced withdrawal symptoms and craving for tobacco.[38]

Non-pharmacological cessation

Other than the above-mentioned pharmacotherapy, non-pharmacological management is also an integral component of smoking cessation. The non-pharmacological modalities for smoking cessation include education, behavioural support, and motivation. The first step towards this goal is to assess whether the patient is able to identify the problem and is willing to quit or not. The five stages of readiness to change (pre-contemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, and maintenance) can be utilised to assess willingness to quit in smokers.[39] Educating the patients about ill effects of smoking should be done in every visit. Behavioural therapy is based on the principle of 5 A’s: - ask, advise, assess, assist, and arrange.[40] Behavioural support includes self-help interventions, brief advice, and counselling (individual, group, or telephonic).[32] Counselling should be done in a non-judgemental, empathetic manner. It has been found that using serial lung function parameters to demonstrate respiratory impairment as a motivational tool increases smoking quit rates.[41]

Unassisted cessation

Although guidelines recommend a combination of pharmacological and behavioural interventions to help quit smoking, the most successful method used by ex smokers is unassisted cessation.[42] Unassisted cessation implies that smokers have quit smoking on their own, either cold turkey or gradually reducing and stopping, without the assistance of medications or behavioural support.[42] Pharmacotherapy for smoking cessation is expensive, with a study conducted in Kerala reporting that cessation therapy costs up to 13 times the cost of smoking.[43] Also, setting up of specialised clinics and training of manpower is difficult in India with an over-burdened health care system. Thus, unassisted cessation holds promise for resource-limited settings such as India. But the onus of this method lies on the treating physician. On every visit, the importance of quitting should be emphasised and every attempt made by the patient should be encouraged. The option of unassisted cessation should be provided to the patient as it is equally effective as assisted methods.[42]

Smoking cessation services in India

According to the Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS 2), less than 40% smokers in India attempted to quit.[3] Until 2002, there were no formal tobacco cessation services available in India. The first clinic was set up in 2002, as a joint initiative of the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, Government of India, and the WHO. The first tobacco cessation Quitline (a toll-free number) was established by the Government of India (GOI) in Vallabhbhai Patel Chest Institute (VPCI), New Delhi.[44] In 2018, it was expanded to regional centres (with counselling available in regional languages) at Guwahati, Bangalore, and Mumbai. According to the data available from the Mumbai tobacco quitline, of the total calls received in 2020, a quit date was set for 29.2% people, out of which 27.5% quit tobacco.[44] A quarter of quitters stopped tobacco use because of telephone counselling, which are very promising and encouraging results.[44] In 2022, NRT was included for the first time in the national list of essential medicines (NLEM).[45] This inclusion is expected to improve availability and affordability of NRT in the public health sector. The first health warnings on cigarette appeared in India in 1975.[46] After another 20 years, it was felt that stronger labels should be there on all tobacco products with images. In 2003, the Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act (COTPA) was passed, which prohibited smoking in public places, stopped tobacco advertising, and mandated graphic warning labels on all tobacco products (cigarette, bidi, and smokeless tobacco). It took another 10 years, and since 2012, all tobacco products in the country have to carry four pictorial warning labels on the packaging.[46]

Physician’s perspective towards smoking cessation

According to a meta-analysis including more than 20,000 patients, even a brief physician’s advice increases smoking quit rates.[47] But, studies have found that pulmonary physicians consider smoking cessation advise to be a low priority while managing patients with chronic respiratory diseases.[32] A study conducted among Scandinavian physicians (N = 3167) reported that although physicians recognised the need for smoking cessation, only a few provided support as they found it to be too time-consuming and lacking expertise.[48] According to the Global Adult Tobacco Survey in India, less than 50% smokers were advised to quit by the health care provider in 1 year.[3] A survey conducted by Joshi et al.[49] found out that there was discordance between physicians and patient perception of discussion initiation on smoking cessation. More than half of the physicians said that they talk about smoking cessation, but less than a quarter of the patients confirmed this statement. There is scope for change in this attitude if physicians consider smoking cessation as an important aspect of patient management.

Future directions

To make young doctors aware and equipped about smoking cessation, it should be made part of both undergraduate and postgraduate medical curriculum. Respiratory physicians should have understanding and skills to manage tobacco dependence and provide cessation support. Stricter laws need to be in place for sale of tobacco products, especially for adolescents and young adults near schools and colleges, so that accessibility can be hindered.

CONCLUSION

When respiratory physicians have first contact with a smoker with respiratory disease, the predominant focus is on relief of symptoms through medications and addressing smoking becomes a low priority. It is imperative that dedicated time is given to this often neglected aspect of disease management so that the patient is encouraged, motivated, and provided all support required to quit smoking. It is also important to emphasise to the patients that willingness to quit is the major step in the path of cessation, and every quit attempt does not need to be medicated, even unassisted cessation is as effective. This change in attitude of physicians will hopefully improve quit rates and sustain abstinence.
