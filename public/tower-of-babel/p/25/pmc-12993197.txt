Patients’ expectations, willingness to use, and willingness to pay for telepharmacy in rural and urban community pharmacies
Authors: Dewi Latifatul Ilma, Vitis Vini Fera Ratna Utami, Nia Kurnia Sholihat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTelepharmacy has the potential to improve access to pharmacy services in both rural and urban settings. However, limited studies have examined differences in patients' perspectives between these populations.ObjectiveThis study assessed patients' expectations, willingness to use (WTU), and willingness to pay (WTP) for telepharmacy, and identified determinants of expectations.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among patients attending community pharmacies in rural and urban areas in Indonesia. Data were collected using a validated self-administered questionnaire assessing sociodemographic, expectations, WTU, WTP, and preferred telepharmacy services. Expectation scores were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, and determinants were examined using multiple linear regression.ResultsA total of 405 patients were included (204 rural; 201 urban). Rural patients reported significantly higher expectations toward telepharmacy than urban patients (mean rank 223.08 vs. 182.62, p < 0.001). No sociodemographic factors were associated with expectations in rural areas. In urban areas, older age (B = −0.14, p < 0.001) and lower household income (B = 3.41, p = 0.007) were associated with lower expectations. WTU was reported by 68.1% of rural and 91.5% of urban patients (p < 0.001). WTP was higher in rural areas (99.3%; most common amount IDR 10,000 [USD 0.64]) than in urban areas (92.4%; most common amount IDR 15,000 [USD 0.96]). Drug counseling was the most preferred service in both groups.ConclusionUrban patients showed greater willingness to use telepharmacy, whereas rural patients demonstrated higher expectations and willingness to pay. These findings highlight the need for patient-centered and financially sustainable telepharmacy models tailored to population differences.

Introduction

Access to pharmacy services remains a major challenge, particularly in rural settings, where poorer health outcomes and a higher burden of chronic disease are observed.1 Advances of digital technology in healthcare, including telepharmacy, have emerged as potential strategies to reduce these disparities by extending access to pharmaceutical care beyond traditional in-person settings.2 Telepharmacy, as part of telemedicine, uses telecommunication technologies to provide pharmaceutical care to patients who are not physically present at a healthcare facility.3, 4 Through this approach, pharmacists can deliver services to individuals in remote areas or those with restricted access to traditional pharmacy services.5, 6

Telepharmacy has demonstrated positive impacts on clinical outcomes by improving treatment adherence and reducing patient visits and hospitalizations among individuals with chronic diseases across low-, middle-, and high-income countries.7, 8, 9. Evidence also suggests that telepharmacy is cost-effective compared with standard care in non-cancer settings.10 These benefits apply not only to rural areas but also to urban populations. In urban context, adherence may be further strengthened through interventions such as extended hours, at-home delivery, or 90-day supplies to optimize telepharmacy services.11

The adoption of telepharmacy increased during the COVID-19 pandemic.12 Research across countries has reported favorable public acceptance of telepharmacy.13, 14, 15, 16 However, disparities remain across populations.12, 17 Some evidence suggests rural residents are less likely than urban residents to utilize telehealth services, highlighting persistent challenges in healthcare access for rural populations.18 Conversely, another study reported that rural participants were 57% less likely than their urban counterparts to reject telemedicine, indicating a greater openness to adoption in the future.12

Barriers to telepharmacy adoption include limited access to technology and low digital literacy, leading to inequitable uptake. In response, many countries have introduced regulatory framework to facilitate wider implementation.17 In Indonesia, telepharmacy is legally supported under the Law of the Republic of Indonesia Number 17 of 2023 on Health and Government Regulation Number 28 of 2024 on the Implementation of the Law, thereby allowing for further adaptation.19., 20. Sucessful implementation also depends on individuals' readiness and willingness to use such services.14 Understanding patients' perceptions and expectations, as well as the sociodemographic factors that influence them, is an important step toward effective implementation.21, 22 Beyond acceptance, willingness to pay serves as an indicator of service demand, reflecting how potential users prioritize and value proposed services, which can inform healthcare policy development and service design.23., 24

Although interest in telepharmacy is increasing, limited studies have examined differences between rural and urban populations. This study addresses this gap by exploring expectations, willingness to use (WTU), and willingness to pay (WTP) for telepharmacy, and by identifying determinants influencing expectations. Addressing this gap is crucial to inform policymakers and healthcare providers in designing telepharmacy services that are both sustainable and responsive to patient needs, while also addressing potential disparities in adoption.

Methods

Study design and settings

A cross-sectional study was conducted from May to September 2024 in Banyumas Regency, Central Java, Indonesia. Urban areas were defined as regions in which the main activities are non-agricultural and are characterised by settlements and the concentration and distribution of government, social, and economic services.25 Rural areas were defined as areas not classified as urban.

A list of registered community pharmacies was obtained from the District Health Department. All pharmacies on this list were contacted online and invited to participate in the study. Participation was voluntary, and not all contacted pharmacies agreed to take part. In total, 23 community pharmacies participated, including 12 located in rural areas and 11 in urban areas.

Participants

Participants included adult patients (aged 18 years and older) who were waiting at participating pharmacies for their medications to be dispensed, had never used telepharmacy services, and were willing to participate. All patients waiting to receive their medications during the data collection period were approached for participation. Recruitment was conducted during peak service hours as identified by each pharmacy. However, specific recruitment times varied across pharmacies and days. Convenience sampling was therefore used. Participants who completed the survey were entered into a draw for an IDR 25,000 prize.

The minimum sample size (n = 118) was determined using a two-sample comparison of independent means based on Rosner's formula,26 with 90% power, a two-sided alpha level of 0.05, and a standardized effect size of 0.5, informed by variance estimates from a previous study.27 This yielded a minimum required sample size of 59 per group (rural and urban).

Study instrument

Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire, with assistance from trained final-year undergraduate pharmacy students who served as enumerators. All enumerators received prior training on the study objectives, ethical considerations, participant recruitment, and the standardized administration of the questionnaire. The questionnaire consisted of five categories: sociodemographic; experience with telepharmacy, including prior awareness and previous use of telepharmacy services; expectations; willingness to use; and willingness to pay for telepharmacy services. The questionnaire was developed based on a literature review from Poudel & Nissen (2016),4 Alboraie et al (2021),28 Ng et al., 2021,29 Alnajrani et al (2022),30 Ilma et al., 2023,31 Wathoni et al (2023),32 and Ministry of Health Republic of Indonesia, 2019.33

Sociodemographic data collected included age, gender, level of education, occupation, personal monthly income, and monthly household income. Experience with telepharmacy was defined as participants' prior awareness of telepharmacy and any previous use of telepharmacy services provided by a pharmacist, as assessed using structured yes/no and follow-up questions. Expectations of telepharmacy services were measured using two indicators: 7 statements on pharmacists' services in telepharmacy provision and 14 statements on telepharmacy services. A four-point Likert scale was used, with options ranging from ‘strongly agree/agree/disagree/strongly disagree’. To support participants with low health literacy, questionnaires were completed with the assistance of trained enumerators. Participants could ask questions or request clarification during completion to ensure accurate understanding of each item.

For the willingness to use (WTU) questionnaire, as all participants had never used telepharmacy services, a brief explanation of telepharmacy was provided before participants were asked “If telepharmacy services were available at pharmacies in the Banyumas area, would you be willing to use these services?”. Three response options were provided: yes or no or not yet able to decide. Participants who responded “not yet able to decide” were grouped with “no” in the analysis, as both indicate a lack of immediate willingness to use telepharmacy services. Participants who responded “yes” subsequently completed the willingness to pay (WTP) assessment. The WTP questionnaire was developed using the contingent valuation method, in which participants were asked to value a hypothetical market describing the benefits of the services.34 Participants were given a hypothetical description of the concept and benefits of telepharmacy services. They were then asked how much they were willing to pay. A multi-bounded discrete choice format was presented, ranging from IDR 0 to IDR 30,000 in increments of IDR 5000. In addition, an open-ended question allowed participants to specify an amount above IDR 30,000 if their preference was not listed.

Participants were also asked to select all applicable types of telepharmacy services they were willing to pay for from a list, including prescription review and services, drug information services, therapeutic drug monitoring, medication safety monitoring, and drug counseling. These services were chosen based on regulations regarding pharmacists practice in community pharmacy in Indonesia issued by the Ministry of Health Republic of Indonesia, 2019.33 For each type of telepharmacy service selected, participants reported the amount they were willing to pay for that service individually. To determine the minimum and maximum payment, as well as the increment used in the questionnaire, a preliminary study involving 386 participants from the general public in Banyumas Regency was conducted prior to this research. This pilot assessed participants' perceived acceptable payment ranges for telepharmacy services, and the results were used to define the payment options presented in the questionnaire.

The content and face validity of the instrument was assessed by seven experts in the field of pharmacoeconomics and pharmacy practice, who evaluated and provided recommendations on the clarity, relevance, representativeness, and objectivity of the questionnaire. Ambiguous wording was revised where necessary to improve clarity and comprehensibility. Validity was estimated using the scale-level content validity index based on the average method (S-CVI/Ave). The S-CVI/Ave results for expectations, willingness to use, and willingness to pay were 0.9, 1, and 1, respectively, meeting the minimum requirement of 0.83 for seven validators.35 Prior to survey distribution, a pilot test was conducted with 30 individuals. The Cronbach's alpha value for the expectations questionnaire was 0.934, indicating acceptable internal consistency.36, 37

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participants' sociodemographic data and are presented as frequencies and percentages (%). Continuous variables are reported as means and standard deviations. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons of sociodemographic data between rural and urban participants were conducted using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables.

Expectations toward telepharmacy were measured using Likert-scale items, with responses of “strongly agree,” “agree,” “disagree,” and “strongly disagree” scored as 4, 3, 2, and 1, respectively. As individual Likert items are ordinal in nature, item-level comparisons between rural and urban participants were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Composite scores were calculated by summing relevant items to generate indicators for expectations toward pharmacists' services in telepharmacy, expectations toward telepharmacy services, and overall expectations. The normality of these composite scores was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the distributions were non-normal (p < 0.05), group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Multiple linear regression was conducted to identify predictors influencing expectations of telepharmacy services. Prior to regression analysis, multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with value less than 10 considered acceptable.38 Standard errors and 95% confidence intervals were used to estimate uncertainty, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significance. All analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel and SPSS statistical software.

Ethic approval and consent

An informed consent form was attached at the beginning of the questionnaire. All participants who agreed to participate were informed about the study's objective, methods, and the voluntary nature of participation. Data were collected from those who provided consent. Ethical approval was obtained from the Faculty of Health Sciences Ethics Committee, Jenderal Soedirman University with approval number 1417/EC/KEPK/IV/2024.

Significant differences between rural and urban participants were observed in age, education, and personal monthly income. Rural participants were older than urban participants (32.46 ± 10.03 vs 29.03 ± 10.42). In contrast, urban participants had higher educational attainment than rural participants, with a greater proportion holding a university degree (34.83% vs 5.88%). Conversely, rural participants more frequently reported only primary or secondary education (p < 0.001). Urban participants were also more likely to report personal monthly income above the regional minimum wage (37.81% vs 24.51%; p < 0.001), while no significant rural–urban difference was observed in household income (p = 0.226). Awareness of telepharmacy remained low in both groups and did not differ significantly (p = 0.074). In contrast, willingness to use telepharmacy was significantly higher among urban participants than rural participants (91.54% vs 68.14%; p < 0.001).

Participans who expressed willingness to use telepharmacy services were subsequently asked about their willingness to pay (Table 2). More than 90% of both rural and urban participants indicated willingness to pay. The majority of rural participants preferred IDR 10,000 (USD 0.64; 32.61%), while urban participants were more likely to choose a slightly higher amount, IDR 15,000 (USD 0.96; 28.23%). Regarding preferred services, drug counseling was the most frequently selected option in both rural (30.37%) and urban areas (29.26%), followed by drug information services (27.91% and 25.85% in rural and urban areas, respectively) and prescription review and services (16.87% and 22.05% in rural and urban areas, respectively). Differences were observed for less frequently chosen services: medication safety monitoring was the least selected among rural participants (12.27%), while drug use monitoring was the least selected among urban participants (10.62%).

Expectations regarding pharmacists' service indicators were consistently high among both rural and urban participants, particularly regarding the use of polite and friendly language and the discussion of possible side effects. While expectations for explanations of medications in simple terms and the provision of complete and accurate treatment information were also high in both groups, rural participants reported significantly higher expectations than urban participants for these two items (p = 0.006 and p = 0.021, respectively) (Table 3).

For telepharmacy service indicators, both groups reported high expectations for data confidentiality and service accessibility, with all participants reporting “strongly agree” or “agree” for these items (Table 4). Rural participants reported significantly higher expectations for data confidentiality (p = 0.002), improved medication adherence (p = 0.015), platform accessibility (p = 0.033), reduced time spent visiting the pharmacy (p = 0.016), lower treatment costs (p = 0.005), and a reduced incidence of infectious diseases such as tuberculosis or influenza (p < 0.001).

Discussion

Surveys assessing patient expectations, willingness to use, and willingness to pay for telepharmacy services are essential in understanding patients' perceptions of their need to design patient-centered and economically viable's telepharmacy services. Based on the available evidence, this was the first study to compare patients from rural and urban areas in a middle-income country.

Awareness of telepharmacy was low in this study (13.83%), with descriptively lower awareness among rural than urban participants (10.78% vs. 16.92%). This lack of awareness is expected, as telepharmacy is a relatively new service and has not yet been widely implemented in Indonesia.39 However, the absence of a statistically significant difference between rural and urban participants suggests that limited awareness reflects insufficient exposure to telepharmacy-specific information across settings rather than differences in place of residence or disparities in digital access. Evidence indicates that digital media and online information penetration in rural Indonesia has become increasingly widespread and broadly comparable to that in urban areas,40 highlighting potential opportunities to improve awareness of telepharmacy services in the future.

Furthermore, willingness to use (WTU) telepharmacy was 68.14% in rural areas and 91.54% in urban areas, with 79.75% of all participants expressing readiness. This statistically significant urban–rural gap provides important context for comparison with international studies reporting varying levels of telepharmacy acceptance across settings. Similar positive attitudes have been reported in high-income countries (Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates; 78.52% and 79.2%) and upper-middle-income countries (Indonesia; 89.9%), despite having no prior experience with telepharmacy.9., 13, 14, 16 In contrast, willingness was lower in a lower-middle-income country, Jordan (61.3%),9., 22 indicating variation in future adoption across income settings. Evidence on factors associated with WTU is mixed. A study in Indonesia found no association between demographic characteristics (age, gender, and education) and WTU.16 In contrast, among patients with chronic diseases (hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and chronic kidney disease), factors such as age, internet use, and daily smartphone use were significantly associated with WTU.15 In Jordan, male gender and living in rural areas were also associated with greater WTU.22 In rural communities, interest in telepharmacy reflects stronger awareness of its potential to improve access and reduce disparities in remote areas.22 By contrast, in urban areas, WTU is driven more by efficiency and flexibility,4 highlighting the different motivating factors across settings.

Among participants willing to use telepharmacy, 95.36% were also willing to pay, with a slightly higher proportion in rural (99.28%) than urban areas (92.39%). Payment preferences differed, with rural participants most frequently choosing IDR 10,000 (USD 0.64; mean ± SD: IDR 13,696 ± 6735 [USD 0.88 ± 0.43]) and urban participants choosing IDR 15,000 (USD 0.96; mean ± SD: IDR 15,676 ± 6616 [USD 1.01 ± 0.43]), reflecting higher income levels in urban populations. Previous studies have shown that individuals with higher economic status are more likely to report greater willingness to pay for telemedicine services.41 Across settings, willingness to pay (WTP) for telemedicine services varies considerably between low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) and high-income countries. In lower-middle-income countries such as Nigeria and Ghana, WTP has been reported at 39.4% (USD 2.99 ± 1.59 per month) and 73.6% (USD 6.17 per visit), respectively,9., 23., 42 consistent with more limited affordability. In contrast, studies from high-income countries, including the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia, Norway, South Korea, and Italy, report substantially higher and more variable WTP, with amounts ranging from USD 0.89 to USD 821.25.9., 24 These findings suggest that although WTP is consistently observed, both the proportion of willing participants and the affordable amounts differ markedly across regions and economic conditions.

Drug counseling was the most preferred service that participants were willing to pay for in both groups (30.4% rural; 29.3% urban). Telepharmacy serves as a key tool for pharmacists to connect with patients and provide education and counseling.43 With a greater privacy and extended duration, pharmacists can educate patients on the correct use of medications.43., 44 This is particularly important for patients prescribed high-risk medications, used medical devices such as asthma inhalers, or at risk of medication errors.43 In addition, high patient satisfaction with telepharmacy counseling has been attributed to perceived convenience, reduced travel burden, continuous access to pharmacist support, and personalised communication.45

Expectations toward telepharmacy services remained high in both rural and urban communities. However, rural participants expressed significantly higher expectations than urban participants. In rural communities, long travel distances to access healthcare services make telecommunication technology a valuable means of facilitating access.46 Telepharmacy enables pharmacists to provide pharmaceutical care remotely, bridging gaps and connecting with patients in underserved areas.47 Accordingly, higher expectations in rural areas may reflect greater reliance on telepharmacy to address limited access to pharmacy services. In urban areas, where distance and accessibility are not major barriers,48 efficiency and service flexibility appear to be the driving factors of telepharmacy utilization.4 Telepharmacy offers online services without visiting the pharmacy, thereby saving time and reducing waiting.4, 49 It also accommodates patient schedules and provides services beyond regular office hours.4

In both rural and urban communities, all participants agreed that the use of polite, friendly, and easy-to-understand language is crucial in delivering telepharmacy services. Similar findings have been reported in traditional pharmacy services, where the majority of patients were satisfied when pharmacists used respectul, polite, easy, and understandable language.50 Since both telepharmacy and traditional pharmacy services aim to provide complete and accurate medication information, it is reasonable that participants expect the same standards in both contexts. Although expectations for clear medication explanations and the provision of complete and accurate treatment information were high in both groups, rural participants reported significantly higher expectations than urban participants for these two aspects. This difference may reflect a greater reliance on pharmacists' explanations in rural settings, where pharmacy services are emphasised due to limited access to other healthcare providers and pharmacists are viewed as the most accessible and consistent source of care.1 All participants also expected pharmacists to explain medication side effects during telepharmacy consultations.51The ability to reduce the risk of adverse drug effects (ADR) is considered one of the most important benefits of telepharmacy,4, 52 providing clear explanation about the potential side effect or ADRs should be a key focus in telepharmacy delivery to improve patient awareness and medication safety.

For telepharmacy services indicators, expectations were high among both rural and urban participants regarding data confidentiality and service accessibility, with all participants indicating agreement. Notably, data confidentiality was consistently viewed as a key priority, with rural participants reporting significantly higher expectations than urban participants. Since the emergence of telepharmacy, confidentiality has consistently been a key legal and ethical concern.53, 54 Challenges in maintaining privacy and confidentiality in telehealth include environmental factors (e.g., lack of private space and the need to share sensitive health information remotely), technological factors (e.g., data security), and operational factors (e.g., regular maintenance and software updates).55 Nonetheless, one study reported that the majority of participants (78.96%) believed telepharmacy could adequately protect data confidentiality.39 Protecting confidentiality therefore requires identifying these key challenges and implementing strategies to overcome them. Another crucial telepharmacy service indicator is accessibility, with all participants expecting services to be readily available. The application of technology in remote healthcare has the potential to enhance accessibility, while also improving efficiency and quality of care.54

Interestingly, in telepharmacy services indicators, all participants in rural communities reported high expectations regarding the role of telepharmacy in reducing the incidence of infectious diseases such as tuberculosis or influenza, with significantly higher expectations than urban participants. Indonesia currently ranks second globally in TB cases after India, with an estimated incidence of 969,000 cases (354 per 100,000 population), and a mortality rate of 144,000 (52 per 100,000 population).56 Influenza also circulates year-round in Indonesia, with higher activity during the wet season.57 Given that rural communities face challenges such as limited access to medical care after infection compared to urban populations,58 it is understandable that participants viewed telepharmacy as a means to help reduce the burden of these infections. Although expectations that telepharmacy could reduce time spent visiting pharmacies were high in both rural and urban settings, rural participants reported significantly higher expectations than urban participants. This difference highlights the potential of telepharmacy to enhance access to pharmaceutical care by overcoming geographical barriers and resource limitations in rural settings.59 Telepharmacy has also been shown to support more efficient and individualised pharmaceutical care and to alleviate waiting time pressures in pharmacy services.2, 44 Additionally, rural participants reported significantly higher expectations for improved medication adherence and platform accessibility, reflecting greater emphasis on continuity of care and service access in rural settings. In rural areas, declining populations may lead to pharmacy closures, disrupting access to medicines and potentially compromising medication adherence.60 Telepharmacy offers a potential solution, with evidence indicating that adherence supported through telepharmacy is comparable to that achieved under traditional pharmacy models.61 To increase the accessibility of telepharmacy, online platforms are increasingly used in the pharmaceutical sector, supporting individualised and efficient pharmaceutical care.2

This study examined factors influencing expectations toward telepharmacy services in rural and urban communities. In rural areas, age, gender, occupation, education, personal income, and household income were not significantly associated with expectations. The low explanatory power of the model (R2 = 0.026), suggests that other unmeasured factors account for the remaining variance. Previous studies have identified digital health literacy as a key determinant of telemedicine uptake, with limited literacy potentially exacerbating healthcare access disparities among vulnerable populations such as rural residents during large-scale implementation.62 In contrast, the model for urban areas was statistically significant, explaining 19.7% of the variance in expectation scores (R2 = 0.197), with older participants reported significantly lower expectations and those with lower household income reported significantly lower expectations. The lower expectations among older adults toward telepharmacy may reflect limited perceptions of its benefits and low trust in digital services.49 Strengthening expectations in this group requires building trust in the system and providers, while ensuring older adults feel in control of their digital health use.63, 64 Additionally, lower household income was significantly associated with lower expectations, consistent with prior evidence showing that individuals with limited financial resources may face barriers such as inadequate broadband access, low digital literacy, or perceptions that digital services are less valuable than in-person care.65, 66

The presence of low awareness alongside high expectations, willingness to use, and willingness to pay suggests that acceptance of telepharmacy may not be the primary barrier in Indonesia. Instead, these findings highlight the need to translate demand into effective service delivery through context-specific implementation strategies. In rural areas, where expectations and willingness to pay were higher, telepharmacy can be prioritised to improve access to pharmacy services and support continuity of care. In urban areas, where willingness to use was higher and driven by efficiency, telepharmacy models may focus on reducing waiting times and increasing service flexibility. From a workforce planning perspective, telepharmacy enables more efficient deployment of pharmacists by extending services to underserved areas without requiring physical redistribution of personnel.4 Policymakers and health system leaders can use these findings to reduce access disparities by integrating telepharmacy into routine service delivery models.59 In practice, this involves allocating pharmacists to provide remote consultations, establishing reimbursement mechanisms and potential public financing for telepharmacy services, investing in reliable and secure digital infrastructure, and strengthening pharmacists' capacity for remote counseling, thereby enabling more equitable access to pharmacy services in underserved areas.4, 67

This study has certain limitations. First, the use of convenience sampling restricts the generalizability of the findings, as participants were recruited based on availability and willingness to participate. In addition, selection bias may have occurred at the pharmacy level, as only pharmacies that agreed to participate were included. Collectively, these non-random recruitment processes may limit the representativeness of the sample with respect to the broader population and community pharmacy settings. Additionally, this study did not collect data on participants' health conditions. Therefore, the influence of disease type on expectations and willingness to use or pay for telepharmacy services could not be assessed. As disease characteristics (e.g., chronic vs. acute or stigmatised conditions) may influence expectations, acceptance, and willingness to pay for telepharmacy, this limitation affects the interpretability of the findings. Future studies should incorporate disease classification or stratified analyses to better assess the effects of disease type on expectations, telepharmacy use and willingness to pay. Moreover, this study did not examine the influence of sociodemographic factors on willingness to use or willingness to pay for telepharmacy services, as the primary focus was on expectations toward telepharmacy services. Future research should therefore examine these relationships using analytical approaches that account for potential confounding.

Conclusion

In this study, Individuals in urban areas demonstrated higher interest in utilizing telepharmacy services than rural areas. However, slightly higher proportion in rural community was willing to pay than urban, with significantly higher expectations in rural areas toward telepharmacy services. No factors were identified to influence expectations in rural areas, whereas older participants reported significantly lower expectations and those with lower household income reported significantly lower expectations in urban areas. This result is crucial for patients' perceptions of their needs, which can inform the development of patient-centered and financially sustainable telepharmacy services.
