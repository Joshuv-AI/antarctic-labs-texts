Introduction

Mechanical complications after acute myocardial infarction are uncommon but carry very high mortality. Ventricular septal defect (VSD) is one of the most catastrophic presentations and frequently leads to cardiogenic shock, often requiring temporary mechanical circulatory support as a bridge to surgery. When acute aortic dissection (AD) develops during escalation of support, management becomes particularly challenging. In this setting, multimodality imaging is essential not only to establish the diagnosis but also to guide management and avoid misinterpretation of support-related findings.

Veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) is conventionally considered contraindicated in AD because of the theoretical risk of false lumen (FL) perfusion, dissection propagation, and coronary malperfusion. However, evidence is limited in patients with otherwise unsurvivable cardiogenic shock. This case is of particular educational value because advanced computed tomography (CT), integrated with echocardiography, identified the dissection, demonstrated inadvertent intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) positioning within the FL, and revealed VA-ECMO-related flow artefacts that could have led to diagnostic error.

Case presentation

A 56-year-old man presented to the emergency department with 6 h of chest pain. The patient had a smoking history of more than 20 cigarettes per day and no other known comorbidities. Physical examination showed bibasal crackles, blood pressure of 97/49 mmHg, heart rate of 100 b.p.m., and oxygen saturation of 94%. Electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and augmented Vector Foot. Inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction with Killip class II was diagnosed, and dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) was administered before urgent transfer to the catheterization laboratory.

Coronary angiography showed proximal occlusion of the right coronary artery. Despite multiple attempts to cross the lesion, only Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction 1 flow was achieved. The patient was then admitted to the intensive cardiac care unit. Immediately after admission, rapid clinical deterioration occurred, and a new Grade 4 holosystolic murmur appeared at the lower left sternal border. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) identified a large inferior VSD with a holosystolic left-to-right shunt in parasternal short-axis, modified apical four-chamber, and modified apical two-chamber views (Figure 1A–1C; Supplementary material online, Video S1). Left ventricular ejection fraction was 46%, and the right heart was dilated with mild right ventricular dysfunction. Immediately after VSD diagnosis, DAPT was suspended because of the anticipated need for surgery and the perceived bleeding risk, and an IABP was inserted. The procedure was technically difficult.

Transthoracic echocardiographic images showing an inferior ventricular septal defect. Colour Doppler flow shows a holosystolic left-to-right shunt in parasternal short axis view (A), modified apical four chambers view (B) and modified apical two-chamber view (C).

Approximately 2 h later, he further deteriorated to refractory cardiogenic shock, requiring orotracheal intubation, invasive mechanical ventilation, and escalating vasoactive support, consistent with Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions shock stage D. Femoro-femoral VA-ECMO was therefore implanted under transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) guidance; during this procedure, AD was documented, and urgent CT was performed.

CT proved crucial in defining the anatomy. It demonstrated type B AD according to the Society for Vascular Surgery/Society of Thoracic Surgeons classification,1 with the entry tear in Segment 2 and partial thrombosis of the FL (Figure 2A and B). Importantly, CT also showed that the IABP catheter tip had been inadvertently placed within the FL (Figure 2C and D), prompting immediate removal. CT also showed the VSD (Figure 3C, Supplementary material online, Video S2).

The entry and partial thrombosis of the false lumen is detected (A, B). IABP catheter tip placed in the FL (arrow, C, D). The outer wall calcification (usually only present in the true lumen) can help to distinguish TL and the FL (E, F).

CT interpretation was particularly challenging because peripheral femoro-femoral VA-ECMO created two competing blood streams, altering the expected contrast enhancement pattern. This made the distinction between true lumen (TL) and FL difficult on first-pass acquisition. In this context, outer wall calcification, present usually only in the TL, became a key landmark for lumen identification (Figure 2E and F). CT also demonstrated a watershed artefact caused by the meeting point of the native and extracorporeal circulations, which could have been misinterpreted as pseudolesions such as thrombus or an additional dissection plane (Figure 4B and D, green arrow). Correlation with non-contrast images and recognition of the true dissection flap were therefore essential (Figure 4A–4D, blue arrow).

Computed tomography (CT) images showing ‘watershed artefact’ and intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) catheter in the false lumen (FL). Non-contrast images (diastolic phase, swollen balloon) (A, C). Contrast images (systolic phase, deflated balloon) (B, D). The point where the two bloodstreams meet (watershed area, green arrow) can be mistaken for pseudolesions such as thrombus or dissection. The blue arrow shows the real dissection flap.

After IABP removal, the patient remained temporarily stable under extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support, and the aortic valve continued to open. Follow-up CT at 48 h showed no clear progression of the dissection, with persistent patency of both lumens. Vasoactive drugs were progressively reduced, and surgery was scheduled for Day 5.

Before surgery could be performed, the patient developed refractory ventricular fibrillation despite repeated electrical cardioversion. Emergent TEE demonstrated severe pathology involving the aortic root and proximal ascending aorta, with a largely thrombosed lumen, no aortic valve opening, and minimal residual perfusion (Figure 5A; Supplementary material online, Video S3). In the descending thoracic aorta, abundant spontaneous echocontrast was observed in both lumens (Figure 5B; Supplementary material online, Video S3), consistent with marked blood stasis. These findings raised concern for either retrograde extension of the dissection into the ascending aorta and aortic root with secondary coronary malperfusion or extensive thrombosis in the setting of severely impaired antegrade flow. Although the final mechanism cannot be established with certainty, the diffuse ST-segment elevation supported acute coronary compromise as the immediate cause of terminal deterioration. In view of the extremely poor prognosis, surgery was not pursued, and the patient died minutes later.

Discussion

From a clinical perspective, the case also underscores the dilemma of using VA-ECMO in the presence of AD. Although this is generally considered a contraindication because retrograde flow may worsen FL perfusion or promote extension of the dissection, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was instituted here in a setting of otherwise unsurvivable cardiogenic shock.2 However, the terminal event demonstrated the potential for catastrophic aortic root complications and probable coronary malperfusion.

This case illustrates the value of multimodality imaging in a patient with post-infarction VSD, refractory cardiogenic shock, and iatrogenic type B AD during escalation of mechanical circulatory support. Its main educational message lies in the imaging pathway: echocardiography established the initial mechanical complication, whereas advanced CT defined the aortic injury, identified device malposition, and revealed extracorporeal membrane oxygenation-related artefacts with major diagnostic implications. The case also adds to prior reports showing the importance of multimodality imaging in complex post-myocardial infarction mechanical complications.3

First, TTE rapidly confirmed the diagnosis of post-infarction VSD by demonstrating a large inferior defect with a holosystolic left-to-right shunt. Second, CT was decisive once an aortic complication was suspected. Beyond confirming type B AD, it identified the entry tear, documented partial FL thrombosis, and, most importantly, showed the IABP catheter within the FL. This finding directly changed management because the device was removed immediately.

Third, this case highlights the specific interpretative challenges of CT in patients on peripheral VA-ECMO. The coexistence of native cardiac output and retrograde extracorporeal flow produces two blood streams with different contrast opacification patterns. Their meeting point, the watershed area, may generate first-pass artefacts that mimic thrombus, vascular occlusion, or additional dissection planes.4 Classic echocardiographic indicators, such as a smaller area of the TL compared to the FL or systolic expansion of the TL, may be altered in this context. Likewise, discrimination between true and FL may be more difficult than in standard dissection imaging.5 In our patient, recognition of the true dissection flap and use of outer wall calcification as a marker of the TL were essential to avoid misinterpretation. In some cases, other CT signs supporting FL identification could be partial thrombosis and the cobweb sign, defined as thin residual linear structures within the FL. Careful comparison with non-contrast images, delayed-phase acquisitions when available, and multi-planar assessment of the real intimal flap can help distinguish flow-related artefact from true pathology.6

Overall, this report is particularly relevant for an advanced CT audience because it emphasizes that CT interpretation under VA-ECMO requires understanding of extracorporeal-flow haemodynamics, integration with non-contrast acquisitions, and correlation with echocardiography. Failure to recognize these features may lead to diagnostic error in critically ill patients.
