Clinical Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics of the IL-1 Receptor Antagonist Anakinra: A Systematic Review
Authors: Jia Li, Megan Clark, David K Metz, Marcel F Nold, Carl M J Kirkpatrick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveAnakinra, an interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra), has been used clinically for over 20 years, with growing interest in repurposing it across diverse indications. However, clinical dosing strategies for anakinra are variable, with limited pharmacokinetic (PK)/pharmacodynamic (PD) knowledge across patient populations and administration routes available. This systematic review aimed to summarise clinical PK and PD characteristics of anakinra and identify research gaps.MethodsA systematic search of Medline, Embase, and other databases was conducted to identify clinical studies reporting the PK, PD, efficacy, and safety of anakinra. Following the application of predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 26 articles were included in the review. Extracted data from these studies were converted into uniform units to enable cross-study comparison, and a descriptive synthesis was performed. All included studies were assessed for risk of bias.ResultsData were categorised into three groups: noncompartmental analysis (NCA) PK parameters, model-informed PK/PD parameters, and exposure–response (ER) relationships. Across all studies, anakinra demonstrated linear PK following both intravenous (IV) and subcutaneous (SC) administration, with high SC bioavailability and flip-flop kinetics. Covariate analyses from NCA and PK models consistently identified body weight and renal function as key determinants of anakinra PK variability, with clearance increasing with body weight and decreasing with worsening renal function. ER analyses identify a potential target exposure corresponding to an average steady-state concentration (Cavg,ss) of 0.4 mg/L, equivalent to a steady-state area under the concentration–time curve (AUC0–24 h,ss) of 9.6 mg·h/L. However, available anakinra paediatric clinical PK data are sparse, limiting definitive age-based comparisons. Existing PK models of anakinra lacked incorporation of endogenous IL-1Ra kinetics, and do not currently describe diverse patient populations, with only one population PK/PD model identified. Furthermore, currently available data are insufficient to validate the target exposure of anakinra across the full continuum spanning PK, PD, clinical outcomes, and safety.ConclusionsOverall, this review highlights substantial gaps in the clinical PK/PD knowledge on anakinra, and model robustness. Future studies, integrating physiologically informed covariates, improved biomarker strategies, and comprehensive ER data, are essential to support model-informed dosing for anakinra repurposing across populations and administration routes. These would facilitate the safety and efficacy of anakinra therapy across diverse populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40262-026-01667-9.

Key Points

Anakinra exhibits a linear pharmacokinetic profile with high bioavailability and flip-flop kinetics following subcutaneous administration.Body weight and renal function are key determinants of anakinra deposition, with a potential target exposure of anakinra identified.Future studies should investigate endogenous IL-1 receptor antagonist kinetics, physiologically informed covariates, and exposure–response relationships, with particular emphasis on paediatric and neonatal populations.

Introduction

Anakinra is a recombinant therapeutic protein virtually identical to intrinsic human interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra) with a molecular weight of 17.3 kilodaltons [1]. It differs from the endogenous protein by only a single amino acid. Therefore, when standard enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) are used, which represent the most common method for quantifying anakinra in biological matrices, the recombinant protein cannot be differentiated from endogenous IL-1Ra.

As anakinra competitively inhibits IL-1R binding of IL-1α and IL-1β to block proinflammatory activities [2, 3], it has been approved for use in a range of chronic inflammatory diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Clinical data for this indication support improved American College of Rheumatology responses (ACR20, ACR50, ACR70), as well as reduction in pain and inflammatory biomarkers compared with placebo [4].

Anakinra can be administered intravenously (IV) or subcutaneously (SC). However, clinically anakinra is typically administered SC due to a high bioavailability of approximately 90% [5], practicality and favourable disposition kinetics [6]. The kidneys are a major organ for anakinra clearance, accounting for approximately 80% of anakinra elimination [7], with plasma clearance reduced by 70–75% in severe renal insufficiency and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [6]. The mechanism of renal clearance is through glomerular filtration followed by proteolysis within the proximal convoluted tubule, with minimal recovery of the intact protein detectable in the urine [7].

As a small therapeutic protein, anakinra also has the potential for immunogenicity. Indeed, the FDA label reports that anti-anakinra binding antibodies have been detected in ~ 50% of patients with RA at one or more time points during treatment. However, no association between antibody development and changes in efficacy or adverse events has been observed or reported.

Despite its established and emerging clinical applications, dosing regimens for anakinra vary widely across indications, ranging from 50 to 1200 mg in adults and from 1 to 29.4 mg/kg in paediatrics [8–13]. Additionally, there is limited understanding of pharmacokinetic (PK)/pharmacodynamic (PD) differences between adults and paediatrics.

In recent years, the potential repurposing of anakinra has been actively explored in both preclinical and clinical settings, such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) in rodents [14–17], cerebral diffuse white matter injury (dWMI) in sheep [18], cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) [12]. Notably, IL-1Ra in early-life preclinical animal models can alleviate the loss of alveolar and pulmonary vascular tissue in BPD, as well as mitigate brain inflammation and preoligodendrocyte loss in dWMI, highlighting its therapeutic potential in these inflammation-driven diseases that commonly occur among preterm neonates. The evolution of chimeric antigen receptor-engineered (CAR)-T cell therapy in patients has seen the use of anakinra for treating ICANS [12].

To date, most dosing strategies in clinical settings appear to be empirical and not informed by systematic quantitative approaches, such as population PK/PD models or exposure–response (ER) analyses. There are only a small number of studies, including a phase I/IIa trial in preterm infants, Anakinra Pilot (AP; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05280340) [19], and a phase II study in adult patients with subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH) [20], that have applied population PK models and simulation to support optimal dose selection.

A comprehensive understanding of how anakinra behaves in the body, including key factors that alter the PK profiles, and, importantly, how anakinra exposure relates to PD, efficacy and safety, is essential for effective and safe dosing. Such knowledge is particularly important for repurposing anakinra in new treatment populations such as preterm neonates, patients with kidney disease, with haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis or macrophage activation syndrome [21] and individuals receiving CAR-T therapies. Therefore, this review aims to summarise and compare all available clinical data on anakinra across studies, to better characterise its PK/PD profiles and inform dosing optimisation strategies.

Methods

The systematic review was performed following the principles of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [22]. The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD42024611265. Screening of titles, abstracts, and full-text articles was conducted using Covidence systematic review software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia, available at www.covidence.org.

Search Strategy

Medline, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Central Registry of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Web of Science databases were searched from inception to October 2024 (then updated in February 2026) using the following search terms: (anakin* OR Kineret OR IL-1RA OR IL-1 receptor inhibitor* OR IL-1 receptor antagonist* OR Interleukin 1 receptor inhibitor* OR Interleukin 1 receptor antagonist*) AND (pharmacokinetic* OR PK OR concentration* OR dos*). Additional sources included ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO International Clinical Trials Registry (ICTRP), conference abstracts, and case reports when sufficient data were available for analysis. Reference lists of included studies were also screened for further relevant studies.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Studies were selected and included according to the following criteria: (i) human participants; (ii) investigation of anakinra involving PK data; (iii) published in English. Excluded studies included reviews, letters, editorials, or opinion articles, or if they lacked extractable PK-related data. Two independent reviewers assessed the full text of the articles for eligibility. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Data Extraction

Data were extracted from each included study with respect to authors’ names, publication years, dose regimen, study population, modelling information, biomarkers, clinical outcomes and safety. If graphical data were only available in figures, they were digitised using the online WebPlotDigitizer tool (V4.8) [23]. Extracted data were organised in a standardised Excel spreadsheet.

Quality Assessment

The quality of included studies was assessed using the ClinPK [24] statement for PK studies, Brendel et al.’s [25] framework for PK/PD modelling studies, the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised controlled trials, and the Risk of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool for non-randomised studies.

Details of the search strategy and quality assessment criteria are available in Appendix I and Appendix II in the electronic supplementary material (ESM).

Data Analysis

Following data extraction, descriptive analyses were undertaken with the purpose of summarising study characteristics and PK/PD findings across the included studies. To facilitate easier cross-study comparisons and interpretation, we standardised parameters to consistent units, such as clearance (CL for intravenous or CL/F for extravascular administration) to L/h, volume of distribution (V for intravenous or V/F for extravascular administration) to L, time to maximum concentration (tmax) and half-life (t1/2) to h, maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) to mg/L, and area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) to mg·h/L. When not reported, noncompartmental analysis (NCA) parameters were further calculated using PKSolver [26] using either published numerical data or digitised graphical data. Cmax and AUC were normalised to the weight-based dose, that is, mg/L to mg/L/(mg/kg) and mg·h/L to mg·h/L/(mg/kg). The weight-based dose was based on reported values, mean or median weight, or an assumed average weight of 70 kg in adults when the weight was unavailable. The average steady-state concentration (Cavg,ss) was calculated using either the daily dose (mg) divided by CL/F, or the steady-state AUC0-24h, then divided by 24 h. Qualitative synthesis was also performed to compare model structures, covariate influences, and ER relationships across studies reporting this data. All data visualisations were generated using R (V4.4.1) [27].

Results

Study Identification

A total of 2349 records were identified based on the search strategy. Following inclusion and exclusion criteria, 26 studies were included in this review (PRISMA diagram, Appendix III, ESM). The studies excluded were primarily due to insufficient clinical PK data.

Based on the included studies, data were divided into three components: NCA PK parameters, model-informed PK/PD parameters, and ER relationships.

Quality of Included Studies

The quality assessment is summarised in Appendix IV (ESM). The risk of bias for all randomised clinical studies was low. For PK studies, 87.5% were of high quality with completeness rates ranging from 71.0 to 88.9%, while 12.5% were of intermediate quality. Based on the assessment of population model evaluation [25], 87.5% of the models were evaluated as good or excellent, with one model classified as poor. Overall, the high quality and low risk of bias across the majority of included studies support the robustness of these synthesised findings.

Most studies provided sufficient information in the title, abstract and background, while prior PK data were reported in the background of only 38.5% of the studies. The most commonly unreported items in methodology sections were drug co-administration or food interactions (100%), specific body weights (90.9%), calculation formulae (64.3%), and formulation details (58.3%). Fewer than 30% of the studies reported study withdrawals or quantified missing/excluded data in the results section.

Noncompartmental Analysis PK Parameters

Parameters of NCA PK for anakinra were available from nine studies [5, 6, 8–10, 28–31]. Administration routes included IV (bolus or infusion), n = 4 [5, 6, 9, 28]; SC, n = 6 [5, 6, 10, 29–31]; and a single intraarticular (IA) study [8]. Five studies directly reported complete NCA results, while for the remaining four studies, we digitised the concentration–time and dose data and calculated NCA parameters.

The study size ranged from 2 to 25 subjects, with study populations including healthy subjects (n = 4), single studies evaluating patients with renal impairment [6], SAH [9], and evaluating the PK in target inflammatory diseases, Still’s disease [30], cryopyrin-associated periodic syndrome (CAPS) [10], RA [31], and knee osteoarthritis (OA) [8]. The age range of participants was wide (0.4–79 years), including two paediatric-specific studies [10, 30].

Given the diverse study populations, the range of administered doses was broad. For adults, the IV dose ranged from 1 to 10 mg/kg/day, with doses for extravascular routes (SC and IA) ranging from 50 to 300 mg/day. In paediatric studies, doses ranged from 1.3 to 4.1 mg/kg/day [10, 30]. Notably, in off-label clinical use, higher doses of anakinra (up to 29.4 mg/kg/day) have been reported in children with autoinflammatory diseases [13]. However, PK data at these higher doses are currently unavailable, limiting further analyses and comparisons.

Intravenous Dosing

The steady-state volume of distribution (Vss) ranged from 6.29 to 11.2 L, with higher values observed (8.47–10.38 vs 6.29–7.70 L) in heavier individuals (109.6–125.2 vs 66.3–86.9 kg) (Table 1) [5].

The CL ranged from 7.32 to 12.6 L/h in healthy volunteers, with similar values observed in SAH patients. As expected, CL decreased dramatically in ESRD patients (CL 1.09–1.15 L/h) due to decreased renal function (Fig. 1). Correspondingly, ESRD groups exhibited approximately 3-fold longer t1/2 compared with healthy subjects (7.03–7.15 vs 2.64 h).

The low percentage of dose urinary recovery in healthy individuals (3.2%) [28] and in patients with ESRD (4.1–9.1%) [6] indicates that renal clearance of anakinra is primarily driven by glomerular filtration followed by proteolysis (Table 1). Furthermore, removal of anakinra during dialysis procedures, that is, haemodialysis (HD) and continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD), accounted for only 2.3% of the dose administered, indicating that dialysis has a minimal impact on anakinra clearance [6]. This is due to the large molecular weight of anakinra (17.3 kD) relative to the pore size of the HD dialysis membranes used clinically for these dialysis modalities and likely overall low solute clearance in CAPD.

The anakinra exposure in ESRD patients showed lower dose-normalised Cmax but approximately 6-fold higher dose-normalised AUC0-∞ compared with healthy subjects (Fig. 2). The increase in dose-normalised AUC0-∞ indicated reduced CL in the ESRD group, whereas the decrease in dose-normalised Cmax was unexplained and was not consistent with the minimal change in Vss between healthy and ESRD individuals (8.86 vs 9.80 L). In contrast with adults, none of the included studies reported PK information in paediatrics.

Extravascular Dosing

Following SC administration, the tmax ranged from 3.7 to 5.98 h [5, 6, 8, 10, 29, 31] in healthy adults and in adult RA patients. A slight delay was observed in adults with renal impairment, where tmax was 6–7 h [6]. For the single IA study [8], tmax was 2 h in adults with knee OA. The late tmax across studies strongly suggests flip-flop kinetics for anakinra, where the absorption rate is slower than the elimination rate. The SC bioavailability (F) of anakinra was consistently high, ranging from 80.2 to 91.9% (Table 1) [5]. However, a marked reduction in F was reported in ESRD (49.3%), representing approximately a 1.5-fold decline compared with the normal renal function group [6].

Anakinra clearance (CL/F) in healthy adult groups ranged from 7.22 to 13.38 L/h after SC administration, with t1/2 ranging from 3.63 to 9.11 h (Table 1). Both CL/F and t1/2 patterns for inflammatory diseases were similar to those in healthy subjects (Fig. 1) [5, 6, 31]. As expected, the CL/F of anakinra decreased with decreasing renal function, resulting in a prolonged t1/2 from 5.24 to 9.71 h [6].

Administration via the IA route [8] showed a similar CL/F (range 12.59–15.15 L/h) and t1/2 (range 3.91–4.15 h) to those observed with SC administration. Despite comparable tmax and t1/2 between paediatrics with Still’s disease [30] and adults (tmax 3.06 h vs 2.0–5.98 h; t1/2 5.23 h vs 3.63–9.11 h), PK parameters were available for only two paediatric participants.

Additionally, weight effects on clearance (CL, CL/F) were observed following both IV and SC administration. Yang et al. [5] observed that individuals weighing > 100 kg had higher anakinra clearance (CL 8.28–10.80; CL/F 10.92–13.38 L/h) than those < 100 kg (CL 7.32–7.44; CL/F 8.04–8.16 L/h).

Relative to IV administration [5, 6, 9, 28], t1/2 following SC or IA [5, 6, 8] was generally longer (Fig. 1), likely due to slow absorption, further supporting flip-flop kinetics for anakinra when administered via the extravascular route (i.e. SC or IA). However, in individuals with impaired renal function [6], the flip-flop kinetics profile no longer occurs because of the reduced elimination process (t1/2 ~ 10 h), which now exceeds the absorption rate from the site of injection.

As expected, the dose-normalised Cmax for SC administration was ~ 30-fold lower than that of the IV routes, reflecting the absorption-related delay (Fig. 2). In patients with renal impairment, dose-normalised Cmax showed an opposite trend for SC compared with IV, with Cmax increasing with decreasing renal function [from 0.55 mg/L/(mg/kg) to 1.74 mg/L/(mg/kg)].

For dose-normalised AUC0–∞, the exposures were comparable between IV and SC routes [7.01–15.64 vs 7.01–12.41 mg·h/L/(mg/kg)], reflecting the observed high SC bioavailability (Fig. 2). Subcutaneous dose-normalised AUC0–∞ increased with worsening renal function, with an approximate 5-fold change from 7.34 to 31.52 mg·h/L/(mg/kg) across mild to end-stage renal impairment.

A single study reported dose-normalised Cmax and AUC0-24h in paediatrics (mean age 10.3 years) with CAPS, with anakinra dose ranges of 0.39–0.53 mg/L/(mg/kg) and 4.12–5.47 mg·h/L/(mg/kg) [10], respectively. No significant increase in exposure was observed between month 3 versus day 1 [dose-normalised Cmax at month 3 vs day 1: 0.41–0.53 vs 0.39 mg/L/(mg/kg); dose-normalised AUC0–24 h month 3 vs day 1: 5.47–5.45 vs 4.12 mg·h/L/(mg/kg)], indicating minimal accumulation of anakinra with these dosing regimens. Importantly, the presence of anti-drug antibody (ADA) did not appear to affect exposure, as dose-normalised AUCs were reported to be similar between ADA-negative and ADA-positive patients [5.45 vs 5.47 mg·h/L/(mg/kg)].

No dose-dependent trends in elimination parameters (i.e. CL, CL/F and t1/2) were observed across the studied dose ranges, regardless of administration route. These findings indicate linear PK within the evaluated range (Fig. 1), a conclusion further supported by the dose-proportional increases in both Cmax and AUC (Table 1).

A review of the available FDA anakinra drug label and collated publications identified no apparent drug–drug interactions in humans. We did identify a single toxicokinetic study in rats, which suggests no alteration in the clearance of anakinra or methotrexate when co-administered [32].

Model-Informed PK/PD Parameters

Nine studies [11, 20, 33–39] were identified which undertook some form of PK modelling of anakinra. These comprised two population pharmacokinetic (PPK) models for long-term IV infusions [20, 33], two PPK models [11, 35] describing IV and SC, two physiologically based pharmacokinetic models [34, 36] incorporating both IV and SC, and three PPK models [37–39] following SC administration only (Table 2). Notably, only one PPK/PD model following the SC administration of anakinra was identified [38]. Study populations included in the PPK models were healthy subjects, patients with SAH, acute Kawasaki disease with coronary artery aneurysms (CAA), systemic-onset juvenile idiopathic arthritis (SJIA) and RA. Seven models specifically targeted adults, while two PPK models focused on paediatrics ranging from 0.3 to 10 years [11] and 0.73–16.8 years [38].

Intravenous Dosing

For extended-duration IV infusion (4 or 24 h), a two-compartment model with first-order elimination best described the plasma PK, while a one-compartment model described anakinra cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) PK in adults with SAH in the Gueorguieva et al. study [33]. This was a sequential model which fixed the anakinra plasma PK parameters to then estimate CSF parameters, assuming no return of anakinra from CSF to plasma. An extension of this model in the Ogungbenro et al. study [20], incorporated additional data [9], improved parameter precision and reduced variability. Notably, model-predicted concentrations were subsequently combined with the individual baseline anakinra concentrations obtained from samples collected prior to dosing, which facilitate comparisons with observations.

Ogungbenro et al. [20] reported a total body CL of 7.86 L/h and the Vss was 9.3 L (Vss = V1 + V2), which is consistent with ranges reported from NCA in adults (CL 7.32–12.6 L/h; V 6.29–11.2 L). In terms of anakinra distribution into and out of the CSF, authors reported this was slow, with a plasma to CSF transfer clearance (CLin) of 0.0071 L/h and transfer clearance out of CSF (CLout) of 0.44 L/h, indicating limited central nervous system penetration of anakinra.

Interindividual variability (IIV) of plasma CL and V were relatively low at 22.8% and 19.5%, respectively. However, higher IIV was reported in CLin and CLout (~ 50%), suggesting the potential for a wide range of CSF concentrations between individuals. Renal function was not evaluated as a covariate due to the inclusion of only a single individual with impaired renal function. No additional covariates were included in the final model for either paper.

No PPK models have been built specifically for IV bolus administration, and no PPK/PD models for IV anakinra administration were identified.

Combined Intravenous and Subcutaneous Dosing

For combined IV and SC administration studies, Nakada and Mager developed a quasi-equilibrium target-mediated drug disposition (QE-TMDD) model using data from only healthy adults [35], while in the Yang et al. study, a paediatric PPK model was constructed for a dosing regimen of IV every 12 hours on the first day followed by once daily SC in children with acute Kawasaki disease (median age 1.1 years, range 0.3–10.0) [11].

The QE-TMDD model estimated an absorption rate constant (ka) of 0.163 h-1 (half-life of absorption ~ 4.3 h) with a SC bioavailability of 86.9% in healthy adults, which is consistent with NCA results (F 80.2–91.9%). The model building undertaken by Nakada and Mager [35] was a framework integrating cytokine–drug interactions across multiple biologics, including anakinra. However, most biologics incorporated in the framework were antibodies, such as canakinumab, infliximab, secukinumab and tocilizumab, for which the TMDD models are commonly applied due to their molecular structures and mechanisms of action. In contrast, it is less appropriate for anakinra, as anakinra exhibits a linear elimination profile that does not appear to support the target-mediated disposition. This final anakinra PK model was then incorporated into a fully integrated cytokine QSP model along with the aforementioned monoclonal agents. However, the model did not include the inhibitory effect of anakinra on any of the described cytokines.

The paediatric PPK model of anakinra for the treatment of Kawasaki disease estimated CL/F (7.6 L/h/70 kg) and V/F (41.8 L/70 kg), which included allometric scaling for body weight with exponents 0.75 for CL/F and 1 for V/F. In addition, an illness day covariate (defined as days of fever at diagnosis) was included as a covariate on CL/F, demonstrating that anakinra clearance increased with the illness day [11]. When scaled to the median paediatric body weight of 10.6 kg in this study, CL/F and V/F were 1.8 L/h and 6.3 L, respectively. Unfortunately, the authors did not report the IIV and residual unexplained variability (RUV) following the inclusion of body weight and disease severity.

Subcutaneous Dosing

Following SC administration in paediatric and adult populations, one-compartment models with first-order absorption and elimination were applied in two [38, 39] of three studies [37–39]. In the third study [37], the authors compared PK between anakinra and an anakinra analogue fused to an Fc fragment (i.e. HL2351), using a quasi-steady-state target-mediated drug disposition (QSS-TMDD) model.

The paediatric populations with SJIA [38] with a median age (range) of 8 years (0.73–16.8) exhibited higher ka than healthy adults [37] (0.38 h−1 vs 0.171 h−1). Due to sparse sampling in the paediatric study, the ability of the model to estimate IIV was reduced. In contrast, the intensive sampling in adults during the absorption time period allowed for a more robust ka estimate, including an IIV of 26.2%. Furthermore, given the inherent differences in disease condition, sampling times, and structural model between the paediatric and adult populations, direct comparison of ka estimates should be considered with an understanding of these potential limitations. Notably, in adults, the CL/F was comparable between the PPK model estimate (9.7 L/h) and NCA ranges (7.22–13.38 L/h).

After we scaled the CL/F to a standard 70-kg adult, the paediatric CL/F was slightly lower than that of adults (6.24 L/h vs 9.7 L/h), with the IIV of CL/F being 28% in paediatrics and 10.2% in adults. In contrast, V/F was larger in paediatrics, with estimates of 65.2 L compared with 9.9–20.2 L in adults. The reported variability in V/F was high, with IIV 41.7% in the adult model and inter-occasion variability (IOV) of 47% in the paediatric model.

Regarding the inclusion of covariates for the identified PPK models, in the adult SC model [39], creatinine clearance (CLcr) (method not specified) was incorporated as a linear covariate on CL/F, whereas body weight was included as a linear covariate on both CL/F and V/F. In contrast, in the paediatric SC model, body weight was the only identified covariate and was included on CL/F and V/F using allometric scaling with estimated rather than standard power exponents [38]. Regarding the inclusion and exclusion criteria of this study, all enrolled paediatric patients had normal renal function, which explains why renal function was not able to be identified or included in the final covariate model.

Following the described inclusion of covariates on CL/F and V/F, a PPK/PD model for paediatrics with SJIA [38] was developed using an indirect response model to describe the changes in C-reactive protein (CRP) concentrations in paediatric patients dosed chronically via SC administration. The daily dose ranged between 2 and 10 mg/kg, with a maximum absolute dose of 100 mg. The authors reported an anakinra concentration that induces a 50% decrease of CRP level (C50) of 0.03 mg/L for the indirect response model. The reported Cavg,ss of 0.4 mg/L would therefore be expected to achieve maximal CRP reduction, thus providing support for the anakinra dosing optimisation in paediatrics with SJIA [38].

The authors also were able to identify two subpopulations in this study based on differential treatment responses. Responders (RESP) were characterised by high baseline CRP and large decrease following treatment, whereas resistant patients (RESI) exhibited low baseline CRP and small decline followed by a slow increase. The proportion of RESI (62%) was higher than that of RESP, but the underlying mechanisms driving this differential response were not elucidated by the authors.

Exposure–Response Relationships

A total of 12 studies reported both exposure and efficacy or safety outcomes for anakinra (Table 4), including IV (n = 1) [40], IV bolus then SC (n = 1) [11], and SC (n = 10) administration [10, 29, 38, 41–47]. Study populations were heterogeneous, involving healthy subjects, patients with COVID-19, acute Kawasaki disease, gout flares, SAH, CAPS and RA. Across these studies, the dose ranged from 100 to 600 mg/day in adults. Four studies included paediatric patients [10, 11, 38, 46], with their doses ranging from 1 to 11 mg/kg/day (maximum 100 mg/day).

Intravenous Dosing

Only one study reported anakinra concentrations, biomarkers, and clinical outcomes in patients with COVID-19 [40], using an initial dose of 300 mg followed by 100 mg every 6 hours. However, the timing of anakinra concentration measurements was not specified, precluding confirmation of whether values represented trough (Ctrough) or other defined PK exposure metrics. No statistically significant or consistent differences in biomarkers were observed between anakinra treatment and control groups over time. Similarly, clinical outcomes, such as ICU length of stay and 28-day mortality, did not differ significantly between anakinra and standard of care groups.

Intravenous Following Subcutaneous Dosing

In one paediatric study involving acute Kawasaki disease (age range 0.3–10 years), anakinra was administered as IV bolus every 12 hours on the first day, followed by once daily SC [11]. This was a dose-escalation study with three dose levels: 2–4 mg/kg/day, 5–7 mg/kg/day and 8–11 mg/kg/day. Cavg,ss was calculated as per described in the Methods section. Across the three dose levels, Cavg,ss was 0.37–0.75 mg/L, 1.15–1.6 mg/L and 2–2.75 mg/L, respectively. However, no difference in CRP or high-sensitivity CRP (hsCRP) were observed across the dose groups, precluding the establishment of a quantitative exposure–efficacy relationship. Indeed, the lowest dose group (i.e. 2–4 mg/kg) achieved Cavg,ss values (0.37–0.75 mg/L) 12- to 25-fold above the reported C50 (0.03 mg/L) (Section 3.4.3), resulting in near-maximal inhibition (~ 92 to 96%). Therefore, the small changes in inhibition at the larger dose groups are unlikely to be clinically relevant.

Safety outcomes were reported in this study, with the incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) of anakinra of 50%, 100% and 93.3% for each dose group. A proportional increase in TEAEs with Cavg,ss was observed, which plateaued at exposures > 0.75 mg/L. However, due to the small sample and insufficient anakinra-related adverse events, a reliable exposure–safety relationship could not be established.

Subcutaneous Dosing

Defined exposure metrics of anakinra following SC administration were reported in four studies, including Cmax and AUC0–∞ in healthy volunteers, Cmax in SAH, Cmax, C24 h and AUC0–24 h in CAPS and Cavg,ss in SJIA. In the remaining studies (n = 6), we only extracted mean anakinra concentrations over time or single time-point concentrations (e.g. 4 h) through ClinicalTrials.gov [42] or digitisation of published figures, thus limiting robust ER assessment.

Only two studies [10, 38] proposed potential target efficacy exposure of anakinra in CAPS and SJIA indications. Notably, these studies included study populations from infants to adults with age ranging from 0.7 to 46.3 years, and reported comparable anakinra exposure across the population. In the study comprising paediatric and adult patients with CAPS [10], marked reductions in CRP and serum amyloid A (SAA) concentrations from baseline were observed with anakinra treatment, regardless of the presence of anti-anakinra ADAs in some patients. This study uniquely demonstrated a substantial improvement in clinical symptom severity with comparable TEAE rates by ADA status. The corresponding AUC0–24h,ss ranged from 7.08 to 9.29 mg/L·h, which translates to a Cavg,ss of approximately 0.3–0.4 mg/L. However, no relationships between a broad range of exposure metrics, such as different quartiles of Cavg,ss and biomarkers or clinical outcomes, were available in this study.

Urien et al. [38] built a PPK/PD model characterising the relationship between Cavg,ss and CRP response in paediatrics with SJIA, which identified a Cavg,ss of 0.4 mg/L as sufficient to achieve maximal CRP suppression. Neither clinical nor safety outcomes were reported in this study.

Five studies [41–45] compared biomarker responses (e.g. CRP, SAA, IL-6) between anakinra and control groups across healthy humans with inhaled endotoxin challenge or patients with gout flares, SAH, severe traumatic brain injury or recent-onset type 1 diabetes. In terms of clinical outcomes, across these indications, no significant clinical improvement was observed compared with controls.

Pardeo et al. [48] found that homozygosity for ≥ 1 high-expression single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) showed higher abundance of IL1RN mRNA (the mRNA encoding IL-1Ra) prior to anakinra treatment and was associated with a 6-fold higher risk of nonresponse to anakinra in patients with SJIA. This study suggests a potential pharmacogenomic influence on anakinra response. However, no anakinra PK data were reported in this study, limiting assessment of potential PK/PD or ER relationships. Overall, no clear PK/PD or ER relationships were identified, with the exception of a single PK/PD analysis linking anakinra to CRP.

As Moran et al. [45] reported in recent-onset type 1 diabetes and Genovese et al. [47] reported in RA indications, anakinra treatment was accompanied by a higher frequency of dermatological and injection-site reactions following SC administration. Saag et al. [42] evaluated AE incidence across different dose groups in patients with gout flares, reporting treatment-related adverse event (TRAE) rates of 26.9%, 14.5% and 3.7% for anakinra 200 mg/day, 100 mg/day and triamcinolone, respectively. However, because only mean concentrations were reported, a comprehensive quantitative exposure–safety relationship could not be built.

A potential target efficacy exposure for anakinra was identified, with Cavg,ss approximating 0.4 mg/L (i.e. AUC0–24h, ss 9.6 mg·h/L), though this is based on limited and incomplete evidence. Further validation of this target is hindered by poorly characterised and inconclusive ER relationships for both efficacy and safety. While for Cmax, there are insufficient studies currently available to evaluate an ER relationship. Furthermore, given the limited data and high heterogeneity, it is not feasible to compare IV and SC administration to identify differences in exposure–efficacy or exposure–safety relationships.

Discussion

Anakinra is an anti-inflammatory biologic with regulatory approval across inflammation-driven diseases and has increasing interest in repurposing it for additional indications and populations. This systematic review synthesised 26 publications, including nine studies providing NCA PK parameters, nine providing model-informed PK/PD and twelve for ER relationships.

Across the included studies, SC administration was the most frequent dosing route. While 21 studies involved adults, only five studies included paediatrics across an age range of 0.3–17 years [10, 11, 30, 38, 46]. Anakinra dosing varied widely across indications and populations, with fixed doses ranging from 50 to 600 mg/day or weight-based dosing from 1 to 11 mg/kg/day. Paediatric dosing strategies were predominantly weight-based, and higher doses have been reported in clinical use, reaching up to 29.4 mg/kg/day.

Immunogenicity does not appear to influence anakinra PK, clinical outcomes or safety, with comparable profiles observed between ADA-positive and ADA-negative individuals. To date, no clinically relevant drug–drug interactions or PK-related enzymes or transporters have been reported or extensively studied for anakinra.

Analyses of NCA PK demonstrated that the PK profiles of anakinra were comparable between healthy individuals and patients with inflammatory diseases, with the exception of those with impaired renal function. Anakinra exhibited high SC bioavailability in healthy individuals (> 80%), but a marked reduction in ESRD populations (~ 50%) [5, 6]. However, the biological reason for this reduced bioavailability has not been elucidated.

Following SC administration, anakinra displays flip-flop kinetics resulting in prolonged t1/2 due to slow absorption from the injection site, thereby supporting once-daily dosing described by the product drug label. This behaviour appears to be abolished in subjects with ESRD, where a markedly prolonged elimination phase determines the terminal phase of the PK profile [5, 6]. Across the available studies, data show that anakinra has linear PK across dose ranges and administration routes. Thus, compartmental modelling of anakinra data should utilise linear rather than TMDD type models.

Body weight and renal function emerge as key determinants of anakinra PK variability. Larger volume of distribution and clearance values were generally observed in heavier individuals [5], although no quantitative relationship could be established due to the high heterogeneity across studies. Declining renal function was associated with reduced clearance and increased t1/2 and exposure following both IV and SC administration, suggesting that the clearance of anakinra is dependent on glomerular filtration.

There have been only a limited number of PPK models built to describe anakinra PK, with only one PPK/PD model. One-compartment models with first order absorption and elimination appear preferred over TMDD models [35, 37] for SC administration, because empirical NCA findings and limited receptor interaction data for anakinra suggest insufficient support for a meaningful TMDD mechanism between anakinra and the IL-1 receptor. Furthermore, two-compartment models appear to better describe the rich sampling data arising from extended-duration anakinra IV infusions.

We observed that clearance (i.e. CL or CL/F) from PPK models was generally consistent across studies and aligned with NCA results, with low IIV (10.2–28%), whereas the V/F showed wide variability with high IIV (41.7%) in adults.

Despite strong evidence from the NCA data to support both body weight and renal function as key covariates, only three of seven PPK studies successfully included body weight as a covariate, with renal function incorporated into the final model in only one study. Moreover, no covariates were incorporated into the IV PPK models. Although four of seven PPK studies evaluated potential covariates, with the exception of the two TMDD models, covariates were often not retained in the final models, likely reflecting insufficient population covariate diversity within included studies; that is, the limited inclusion of paediatric and adult participants with varying degrees of renal function and/or body weight. Overall, key covariates such as body weight and renal function were largely not included in the final PPK models.

Anakinra is a recombinant protein with minimal structural differences (i.e. one different amino acid) from endogenous IL-1Ra; hence, the two cannot be distinguished using standard ELISAs. So far, no PPK models accounted for endogenous IL-1Ra, thereby limiting accurate characterisation of the endogenous PK profile and its contribution to overall anakinra disposition. Parameterisation approaches commonly applied to endogenous substrates in biologics modelling [49–51], such as baseline models and turnover models, should be considered in future anakinra PPK model development. A key input for such models would be the utilisation of a quantitative assay that has appropriate sensitivity and specificity for both endogenous and exogenous anakinra.

In summary, the existing PPK and PPK/PD models for anakinra lack sufficient robustness, as they neither account for endogenous IL-1Ra nor incorporate covariates representative of diverse populations. These limitations reduce applicability to inform dose optimisation across populations, covariates or routes of administration.

Regarding ER relationships, Cavg,ss has been proposed as a potential target exposure metric [10, 38] derived from a single PK/PD model and reported exposure data from a clinical study. This target exposure lacks prospective validation across PK/PD, clinical outcomes and safety. Furthermore, while biomarkers such as CRP, SAA and cytokines were used to assess the treatment response across the included studies, no quantitative ER relationships were identified for any biomarkers or clinical outcomes other than CRP.

Adverse effects including TEAE in acute Kawasaki disease and TRAE in gout flares showed dose-dependent increases. Nevertheless, exposure–safety relationships could not be characterised due to the narrow exposure range in acute Kawasaki disease and the absence of a clearly defined exposure metric in gout flares. These limitations suggest that robust ER relationships for anakinra will require data spanning a broader exposure range, such as exposure below and above the proposed target of 0.4 mg/L.

This review has several limitations. Due to substantial heterogeneity across the included studies in terms of populations, doses and routes, a quantitative meta-analysis could not be performed. Non-English publications were excluded due to resource limitations and to ensure accurate extraction and interpretation of PK and PD data. However, this approach may have introduced publication and language bias. Due to data sparsity, conclusions regarding age-dependent PK differences, PK/PD or ER relationships remain somewhat elusive.

Conclusions

This systematic review provides a comprehensive overview of available clinical data on anakinra. We highlight body weight and renal function as determinants of anakinra disposition and suggest a potential target Cavg,ss of 0.4 mg/L. Research gaps remain, particularly in paediatrics and neonates, where limited clinical PK data on paediatrics and none on neonates (defined as < 28 days old) constrain safe and effective repurposing of anakinra in these populations. Further clinical PK studies in paediatrics and neonatology are therefore required. Robust PPK and PPK/PD models of anakinra are lacking for both IV and SC across populations, as existing models provide limited integration of exogenous and endogenous IL-1Ra kinetics and insufficient characterisation of covariates, including body weight and renal function. Furthermore, consistent and clinically informative biomarkers, as well as well-defined ER relationships for anakinra, remain largely unestablished, precluding therapeutic target validation and constraining model-informed dose optimisation. Further research is therefore warranted to address these research gaps and advance the understanding of anakinra clinical pharmacology across neonates, infants, children and adults, thereby improving the safety and effectiveness of anakinra therapy for patients worldwide.
