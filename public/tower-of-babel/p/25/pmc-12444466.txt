Re: ‘Enhanced Bone Regeneration Using Demineralized Dentin Matrix: A Comparative Study in Alveolar Bone Repair’
Authors: Parth Aphale, Shashank Dokania, Himanshu Shekhar
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

The article by Sultan et al presents a rigorous comparative study on the osteoinductive potential of dentin matrices with varying degrees of demineralisation, highlighting partially demineralised dentin matrix (PDDM) as superior for alveolar bone regeneration in rabbits.1 While this study adds meaningful insight to regenerative dentistry, several pivotal aspects remain unaddressed, which may limit its reproducibility and translational potential.

The core hypothesis attributes PDDM’s superiority to preserved growth factors such as Bone Morphogenetic Proteins and osteopontin. However, the study lacks direct quantitative comparison of these proteins across Undemineralised Dentin Matrix, PDDM, and Completely Demineralised Dentin Matrix preparations. This omission weakens the causal link between bioactivity and regenerative outcomes. Quantitative Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay or mass spectrometry-based profiling of retained BMP-2, Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor, and collagen I/III would enhance material standardisation and clinical reproducibility. As noted by Khurshid et al, demineralisation methods significantly impact protein integrity, influencing osteoinduction.2

Rabbit models, while efficient for early-stage screening, differ substantially from human alveolar bone in trabecular density, healing time, and loading mechanics. The socket size and metabolic rate in rabbits may overestimate regenerative outcomes. As supported by Turonis et al and Elfana et al, findings in rodent or small-animal calvarial models often fail to replicate in human trials.3,4 Hence, further evaluation in canine or porcine models or Phase I human studies is essential before clinical extrapolation The study could have gained stronger clinical relevance by including a positive control such as deproteinised bovine bone mineral (eg, Bio-Oss), which is widely used in alveolar preservation. Previous studies also have demonstrated favourable outcomes of autogenous Demineralised Dentin Matrix compared to Bio-Oss, reinforcing the value of such comparisons.5

In conclusion, while the study by Sultan et al offers a commendable contribution to the field of dentin-based regenerative materials, its translational trajectory would be significantly strengthened by integrating quantitative biomolecular profiling, benchmarking against established graft materials, and validation in clinically relevant large-animal or human models. These additions would not only substantiate the biological rationale behind PDDM’s superior performance but also ensure its reproducibility, regulatory readiness, and clinical applicability in modern implant dentistry.
