Increasing rail transit ridership by improving passenger ride comfort
Authors: Javad Sadeghi, Hamidreza Heydari, Hiva Rabiee, Ayda Azimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In urban rail transit systems, passenger ride comfort (PRC) significantly influences ridership levels. Various factors affect PRC, with track geometry playing a crucial role. Among geometry parameters, the minimum required tangent length between consecutive railway curves is particularly significant. While this parameter affects passenger comfort considerably, it has received less attention in current design practices. This study addresses this gap by optimizing tangent length requirements to improve passenger comfort. To this end, a vehicle-track interaction model was established and subsequently validated through comprehensive field measurements. The influence of curve radius, tangent length, and speed on the PRC was investigated through a parametric analysis, considering various curve configurations. As a result, a large data bank was created, and a model was developed using data mining techniques to predict minimum tangent lengths. The accuracy and computational performance of the model were discussed. The results obtained indicate that reverse curves require 5% longer tangent lengths and have a 9% higher ride comfort index compared to those of compound curves. The model optimizes tangent lengths for speeds above 80 km/h, leading to improved ride comfort. This improvement contributes to increased rail transit ridership.

Introduction

Rail transit systems play a vital role in urban mobility by offering numerous advantages including environmental sustainability, high capacity, comfort, and reliable transportation1–3. However, reported instances of deficiencies in passenger comfort have necessitated increasing ridership by addressing insufficient passenger ride quality in some parts of the urban rail system4. Passenger comfort stands as a crucial factor in attracting and retaining users5. While various factors contribute to passenger satisfaction in metro systems, comfort significantly influences passengers’ choice to use rail transit as their preferred mode of transportation6,7.

Passenger ride comfort (PRC), primarily determined by vehicle vibrations and oscillations affecting passengers, alongside factors such as thermal conditions and acoustics, requires precise measurement8–10. This parameter represents a primary consideration in contemporary railway transportation11–13. The quantification of PRC typically employs standardized measures such as the Sperling ride index, ISO 2631, and UIC 51314,15. The Sperling ride index has demonstrated significant effectiveness in assessing vibration-induced comfort effects through the analysis of filtered RMS accelerations16–18. In urban transit systems, longitudinal acceleration serves as a key indicator for assessing PRC, as validated through passenger feedback19.

Several studies have highlighted the relationship between PRC and rail transit ridership. Research by20 emphasized that PRC significantly influences urban residents’ choice in selecting subway trains, suggesting a positive correlation between improved comfort and increased ridership. Similarly, Munawir et al.21 identified PRC as a crucial factor in passenger satisfaction and retention in light rail transit systems.

The importance of track geometry in passenger comfort has been emphasized through various aspects of railway operations. Track geometric elements significantly influence wheel-rail contact forces and vehicle dynamics, particularly on curved sections, which directly impacts passenger comfort and safety22,23. Notably, passengers consistently report increased discomfort during transitions from tangent tracks to curved Sect.24. The optimization of transition curve lengths and their dynamic response characteristics play a crucial role in ensuring smooth passenger experience25. Other critical parameters affecting ride quality include horizontal curve radius, cant, vertical curve radius, profile, twist, cross-level, alignment, and gauge12,26–28. Additionally, the interaction of horizontal and vertical curves influences ride comfort, necessitating optimized alignment solutions29. Furthermore, track geometry quality, especially track irregularity patterns and long-wavelength irregularities, has been identified as a primary factor in determining comfort levels across different rail transit systems30,31. Regular monitoring of these geometric parameters through ride comfort measurements helps identify localized infrastructure issues requiring maintenance intervention32. Despite extensive research on track geometry effects, quantitative assessment of passenger responses to specific track elements remains limited.

This study focuses on Tehran Metro Line 1, where various track geometry elements affecting passenger comfort were investigated. Tehran Metro Line 1 is the longest and oldest operational metro line in Iran’s railway transit system. The line extends 37.5 km along the north-south corridor of Tehran, serving 29 stations. It connects the northern terminal at Tajrish Station, located at the beginning of Shariati Street, to the southern terminal at Kahrizak Station (Fig. 1). The line’s infrastructure incorporates diverse geometric elements, including horizontal and vertical curves and track switches, making it an ideal case study for passenger comfort analysis. Through comprehensive passenger surveys, seven key parameters were identified as significant factors in ride comfort: curve transitions, station operations, acceleration and deceleration phases, track switches, vertical alignment transitions, track irregularities, and platform interface. To understand the most important factor among these parameters, a detailed survey was conducted with 1,135 passengers representing diverse demographic groups. The survey was conducted over a one-month period by a five-member team, collecting approximately 35 responses per day. Prior to the survey, passengers were provided with a descriptive information sheet including illustrative figures of key track geometry elements, along with a brief verbal explanation by team members at the boarding stations. Critically, passengers were not required to identify or judge any technical track geometry concepts themselves. Instead, the survey was administered in real time during the journey, and as the train passed through each specific section, passengers were asked to indicate their comfort level at that moment. The association between reported discomfort locations and specific track geometry parameters was subsequently performed by the research team using official railway design records. The survey population included both male (63%) and female (37%) passengers. Respondents’ ages ranged from 18 to over 60 years. Their educational backgrounds varied, comprising university graduates (27%), high school graduates (45%), and others (28%).

In the survey, passengers were explicitly asked to select the single most important track geometry factor affecting their ride comfort. Analysis of the survey data revealed that transitions between consecutive curves were identified by 30% of respondents as the most significant factor affecting their comfort during travel. This was followed by acceleration and deceleration phases (24%), station arrival and departure (22%), and track switches (12%). Curve super-elevation and other factors accounted for 8% and 4% respectively (Fig. 2). To improve passenger comfort during curve transitions, the intermediate tangent length serves as a key design parameter. This parameter becomes especially significant when considering the diverse combinations of curve radii and the operational speeds in metro systems. The optimization of tangent lengths between consecutive curves has not been thoroughly investigated in previous studies. Therefore, this study aims to develop a model for determining minimum required tangent length between consecutive railway curves based on passenger ride comfort using data mining methods.

To achieve these objectives, this study employed the Sperling ride index to evaluate required tangent length between consecutive curves. A vehicle-track interaction model was established and subsequently validated through comprehensive field measurements in Tehran metro line 1. Vehicle acceleration data were recorded through tangent and curved track sections. The influence of curve radius, tangent length, and speed on the PRC was investigated through a parametric analysis, considering various curve configurations. As a result, a large data bank was created, and a model was developed using data mining techniques to predict minimum tangent lengths. The accuracy and computational performance of the model were discussed. The results obtained indicate that reverse curves require 5% longer tangent lengths and have a 9% higher ride comfort index compared to those of compound curves. The results also demonstrate that the proposed methodology provides a more precise and optimized approach for determining minimum intermediate tangent lengths. This is particularly evident for train speeds greater than 80 km/h. This optimization improves passenger ride comfort and potentially increases railway ridership. Description of vehicle-track dynamic model

In this study, the vehicle-track interaction model was developed using the Universal Mechanism (UM) software33. This software is an advanced simulation platform for analyzing the dynamic behavior of multi-body systems and rail vehicles. The computational framework comprises two primary components: the railway track and the rail vehicle, as detailed in the following sections.

Model of railway track

The railway track model was developed based on Winkler’s foundation theory34. This theory represents the track as a beam element supported by an elastic foundation. The foundation uses spring-dashpot systems to simulate dynamic load response. The model parameters were established according to Tehran Metro Line 1 specifications. The track geometry incorporates a UIC 54-type rail with 1:20 rail inclination, 60 m transition curve length, and a circular curve section of 589.924 m, featuring a 290 m radius, and 120 mm super-elevation. Track irregularities significantly influence vehicle dynamic behavior, particularly in curved sections where their effects on ride comfort and safety parameters are amplified. Vertical and lateral track irregularities can generate substantial forces on vehicle wheels, potentially affecting operational stability. Model of rail vehicle

The railway vehicle was modeled using the Multi-Body Systems (MBS) dynamics principles. The numerical model integrates the car body, bogies, wheelsets, primary and secondary suspension systems, and wheel-rail contact interfaces. Each wheelset and the bogie frame have six degrees of freedom (three translational and three rotational). The car body is also modeled with six degrees of freedom.

Field test and model validation

Model validation was conducted through comprehensive field testing on Tehran Metro Line 1. The testing focused on curved sections between Mirdamad and Shariati stations, as shown in Fig. 1. Figure 6a–d presents the view of curve track location and experimental measurement setups, including the rail vehicle, accelerometer, data logger and laptop. Acceleration data was recorded during train passages at 60 km/h.

Fig. 6Experimental setup: (a) curve track location, (b) rail vehicle, (c) accelerometer, (d) data logger.

Experimental setup: (a) curve track location, (b) rail vehicle, (c) accelerometer, (d) data logger.

Acceleration data was recorded as the train passed through the specified route at a speed of 60 km/h. The accelerometers used in this study were manufactured by Tokyo Sokki Kenkyujo (Japan). The ARF-100 A-T accelerometer has a mass of 85 g and a frequency range up to 130 Hz. The system has a throughput of 100, and the minimum measurable acceleration for each accelerometer is 0.001 of its capacity.

Data processing techniques were employed to ensure accurate analysis of the collected data. Raw data was initially recorded and filtered to eliminate noise using a 100 Hz low-pass filter. A 4th-order Bessel filter38, as illustrated in Fig. 7, was applied to the measured data within this frequency range. Figure 8 shows vertical carbody acceleration measurements on a tangent track and on a curved track with a 300 m radius. The filtered data enhances the clarity of acceleration profiles by removing measurement noise. The resulting data allows precise analysis of the vehicle-track system’s dynamic response through consecutive curved sections.

Comparative analyses between numerical simulations and field measurements demonstrate satisfactory agreement for Sperling ride comfort indices. Specifically, in the curved track section, the numerical predictions exhibited a 7% deviation from experimental measurements for the Sperling index, while maximum acceleration values maintained higher accuracy with deviations not exceeding 5%. On the tangent track section, the Sperling index deviation was 5% and the maximum acceleration deviation was 8%. Based on these results, the proposed numerical model can predict the vehicle-track system’s dynamic behavior and ride comfort. Overall, the predictions show acceptable differences when comparing both the Sperling ride comfort index and maximum acceleration values. Furthermore, the close agreement between the model predictions and field measurements proves the reliability and accuracy of the modeling approach. Consequently, the results reinforce its effectiveness in evaluating track behavior in different track geometry conditions.

Parametric analysis

A comprehensive parametric study was conducted utilizing a validated numerical model to investigate the correlation between consecutive curve parameters and the intermediate tangent length. The investigation encompassed critical geometry and operational variables, specifically curve radii and vehicle speeds. The methodological framework incorporated systematic variations of these parameters to evaluate their influence on passenger ride comfort indices and track dynamics.

To evaluate the effects of track parameters on ride comfort, the Sperling ride comfort index was used as the comfort evaluation criterion21. Discussion and derivation of the model

In order to make the results applicable in the real world of practice, mathematical expressions were formulated through integration of operational speed and equivalent curve radius parameters. To this end, data mining approaches were employed to develop predictive models for both ride comfort and required tangent length requirements. These models automatically identify critical thresholds where the relationships between design variables significantly change, enabling more accurate predictions across diverse operating conditions.

Derivation of RCI and required tangent Length model

In order to develop expressions for RCI and required tangent length, a combination of power-law modeling and data mining algorithms was employed. These expressions are based on vehicle speed and equivalent curve radius. Based on parametric analysis, power-law functions were used to model the non-linear relationship between parameters, RCI, and the tangent length. This mathematical structure provides both flexibility and physical interpretability, allowing the model to capture complex interactions while maintaining clear engineering significance. A Decision Tree Regressor was used to identify the critical threshold by considering variations in lower and higher speeds for RCI nomograph. The model also examined smaller and larger radii for the required tangent length expression. The differential evolution algorithm, a sophisticated global optimization technique based on principles of natural selection, was implemented to optimize the coefficients of the models. This stochastic, population-based optimization method effectively navigated the complex parameter space to find optimal values for the power-law mathematical functions. Comparison of derived expression with existing guidelines

Although international standards provide various guidelines for determining the required tangent length, reports from multiple metro systems indicate that passenger ride comfort remains insufficient4. This shortfall is largely attributed to metro line geometry. To address this issue, the recommended tangent lengths were compared with the expression derived in this study. For instance the BSEN13803 regulation40 recommends some equations to determine the required tangent length based on the vehicle speed. The Chinese standard41 suggests the required tangent length based on geological condition. The standard distinguishes between general conditions applicable to normal terrain and difficult conditions applicable to challenging geological environments. The AREMA standard42 recommends that the tangent length be at least as long as the longest car length navigating the curve. The Australian standard AS763543 suggests railway tracks with standard gauges covering a range of passenger railways, including high-speed, moderate-speed, and low-speed. Notably, this standard specifies a minimum tangent length of 20 m as the standard value for these types. The Iranian railway standard code 28844, requires consecutive curves to be separated by a tangent track length calculated using the equation presented in Table 3. The Indian standard45 recommends minimum tangent lengths based on operating speeds. For urban rail lines, it specifies 30 m for speeds below 130 km/h and 50 m for higher operating speeds. The TCRP46 standard provides some mathematical expressions for the required tangent length between consecutive curves. The formula, derived and proposed in this research, demonstrates an improvement in accuracy over existing guidelines. This improvement is achieved by incorporating both curve radii and vehicle speed. A key strength of this approach is its ability to account for consecutive curves through the equivalent radius calculation. This method considers both the sequence and radii of consecutive curves, where the combination of different radii significantly influences the results. The model employs power functions rather than linear speed-based formulas. This approach reflects the exponential relationship between speed and required tangent length, which becomes particularly pronounced at speeds exceeding 80 km/h. Additionally, the formula incorporates ride comfort parameters, ensuring passenger comfort requirements are met throughout the tangent track. Comparative analysis reveals noticeable differences between the proposed model and established standards across different railway types. For urban transit systems, the formula shows deviations ranging from − 71.8% (BS EN 13803) to + 12.8% (Chinese Railway Normal conditions). The discrepancy becomes more pronounced for conventional rail. For these systems, the model predicts tangent lengths that are 72.0% longer than AS7635 and 57.9% longer than several other standards, including BS EN 13,803, Indian Railway, and AREMA. For high-speed rail applications, the divergence becomes even more critical. The model predicts tangent lengths up to 89.6% longer than AS7635 guidelines and 84.4% longer than AREMA standards. Among all standards, the Chinese Railway guidelines for general conditions show the closest alignment with the proposed model. The deviations are 12.8% for urban rail, 34.6% for conventional rail, and 4.0% for high-speed rail. Moreover, the model maintains the practical minimum tangent length of 30 m while considering the geometric effects of equivalent radius. Conclusion

It has been shown that there is a need to increase rail transit ridership by improving passenger ride comfort. In this research, the tangent length between two consecutive curves is identified as a key parameter with the greatest impact on passenger ride comfort and rail ridership. To address this issue, a comprehensive numerical model of track and rolling stock was developed and validated through extensive field testing. Parametric analyses examined the influence of vehicle speed and curve radii on required tangent length requirements. The obtained results were used to develop predictive model through systematic power-law modeling and data mining algorithms. The model was subsequently optimized using differential evolution algorithms to ensure maximum accuracy while maintaining physical interpretability across diverse operational conditions.
