Tandem CAR-T cells targeting mesothelin and MUC16 overcome tumor heterogeneity by targeting one antigen at a time
Authors: Diego Salas-Benito, Filippo Birocchi, Sangwoo Park, Cassidy E Ho, Alexander Armstrong, Aiyana L Parker, Amanda A Bouffard, Jessica A Frank, Eugene Kim, Tamina Kienka, Kiana Graham, Christopher Kelly, Sadie Goncalves, Mark B Leick, Giulia Escobar, Bo Rueda, Trisha R Berger, Marcela V Maus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTumor heterogeneity and antigen escape are mechanisms of resistance to chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy, especially in solid tumors. Targeting multiple antigens with a unique CAR construct could be a strategy for a better tumor control than monospecific CAR-T cells on heterogeneous models. To overcome tumor heterogeneity, we targeted mesothelin (meso) and Mucin 16 (MUC16), two antigens commonly expressed in solid tumors, using a tandem CAR design.MethodsWe designed a series of tandem CAR constructs based on various anti-meso (SS1) and anti-MUC16 ectodomain (MUC16ecto) (4H11) single-chain variable fragment (scFv) arrangements and G4S linker lengths. Then we determined the best tandem CAR design based on binding of soluble antigens, steric hindrance, avidity and functionality against cell lines expressing one or both antigens in vitro. Finally, we compared the tandem CAR to monospecific CAR-T cells in mixed tumor models in vitro (two-dimensional and three-dimensional models) and in vivo.ResultsWe show that the scFv arrangement and linker length impacted antigen binding and CAR expression in T cells. Tandem CAR configuration (TanCAR1) (with SS1 scFv located distally and one G4S repeat as the linker between scFvs) had the best binding and activation profile in vitro and outperformed SS1 and 4H11 monospecific CAR-T cells in mixed tumor models in vitro and in vivo, showing an antigen-driven killing of tumor cells based on antigen density. Moreover, acoustic force microscopy, using tumor cells with different levels of antigen expression, revealed that TanCAR1-T cells likely bind to one antigen at a time rather than simultaneously.ConclusionsThis is the first time using a tandem CAR design targeting meso and MUC16, and demonstrating a benefit on tumor control over monospecific CAR-T cells. Tandem CAR-T cells targeting meso and MUC16ecto could be employed as a strategy to overcome tumor cell heterogeneity in ovarian and pancreatic tumors, and may help to design therapeutic approaches relying on its one-antigen-at-a-time binding properties and on its antigen-driven killing of tumor cells based on antigen density.Keywords: Chimeric antigen receptor - CAR, Ovarian Cancer, Gastrointestinal Cancer

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

In this study, we have discovered a tandem CAR configuration (TanCAR1) that can bind meso and MUC16 ectodomain (MUC16ecto) without steric hindrance. Moreover, TanCAR1 demonstrates a better control of mixed tumor models in vitro (two-dimensional and three-dimensional) and in vivo compared with monospecific CAR-T cells. We find that, on double-positive tumor cells with different antigen expression levels, tandem CAR-T cells bind one antigen at a time and show an antigen-driven killing of tumor cells based on antigen density.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Our findings raise the idea that tandem CAR design might be an iterative process where each new version requires individualized testing and validation. In this regard, antigen heterogeneity and density may affect tandem CAR killing. Tandem CAR-T cells targeting meso and MUC16ecto can be an impactful therapeutic strategy for heterogeneous tumors such as ovarian and pancreatic cancers.

Introduction

Adoptive cell therapies using T cells engineered to express a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) have shown outstanding results in B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, B-cell lymphomas, and multiple myeloma, with corresponding Food and Drug Administration approvals.1,3 Even with these achievements, relapsing disease due to loss of targeted antigen expression is a common mechanism of resistance to CAR-T cell therapy.4,6 In some cases, tumor relapse is due to heterogeneous antigen expression in the tumor.6 7 One strategy to overcome tumor antigen heterogeneity and prevent antigen escape is to design CARs that target more than one antigen, such as by linking two single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) for the antigen-binding domain. This tandem configuration has been tested in hematological malignancies and solid tumor models,8,13 in some cases resulting in better tumor control compared with the coadministration of monospecific CAR-T cells13 or the monospecific CAR-T cells alone.810,12

Mesothelin (meso) and Mucin 16 (MUC16) are two tumor-associated antigens commonly expressed in various solid malignancies including gynecological tumors (ovarian, uterine, and cervical), pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, and lung adenocarcinoma.14,17 CAR-T cells targeting meso or MUC16 demonstrated promising results in preclinical studies18,20 but were less effective in clinical trials.21,24 The best response to meso-CAR-T cells in patients with ovarian cancer, pancreatic cancer or mesothelioma was stable disease.21,23 There have been clinical improvements using meso-CAR-T cells combined with pembrolizumab or using a fusion construct of an anti-meso scFv integrated into the endogenous T-cell receptor.25 26 Similarly, MUC16-CAR-T cells have only achieved stable disease for patients with ovarian cancer, even when combined with interleukin (IL)-12 secretion to enhance antitumor activity.24 In some of these trials, antigen expression levels and patterns of expression varied across patients,22 23 which could explain these unsatisfactory clinical outcomes. This heterogeneous antigen expression has been observed in ovarian, pancreatic and adenocarcinoma lung cancer patient tumors, resulting in a mixture of single and double positive cells.27,30 These data indicate that targeting both antigens could address tumor heterogeneity in solid tumors.

Tandem CAR-T cells are designed to bind two target antigens and confer better control of tumors that express antigens heterogeneously compared with monospecific CAR-T cells. Studies using heterogeneous tumor models have shown that tandem CAR-T cells prevent tumor antigen escape and increase tumor clearance compared with monospecific CAR-T cells.8 9 12 However, when only one target antigen is expressed in the tumor, the antitumor activity and cytokine production of tandem CAR-T cells may be lower than monospecific CAR-T cells.8,11 Conversely, in heterogeneous or mixed populations of tumors, or when both antigens are expressed, most published variations of tandem CAR-T cells have antitumor effect that is generally better than or similar to monospecific CAR-T cells.9 11 31 These data raise the question as to whether tandem CAR-T cells bind to one antigen at a time or if they are capable of binding both target antigens simultaneously. Likewise, it remains to be determined whether tandem CAR-T cells preferentially kill tumor cells within heterogeneous tumors based on their antigen density and/or the affinity of the scFvs composing the tandem CAR, as monospecific CAR-T cells often do.32 33 We hypothesize that tandem CAR-T cells can bind one antigen at a time on tumor cells that express two antigens, and this may lead to a preferential killing of tumor cells displaying the highest antigen density in heterogeneous tumor models.

To answer these questions, we designed tandem CAR T cells targeting meso and the MUC16 ectodomain (MUC16ecto) and examined their functionality in heterogeneous tumor models. After determining the optimal design in terms of scFv order and the linker length connecting the scFvs, we tested the one-antigen-at-a-time binding hypothesis using a ramp of acoustic force to measure the avidity of tandem CAR-T cells to tumor cells. To mimic tumor antigen heterogeneity, we prepared mixed tumor models in vitro and in vivo and compared the antitumor efficacy of tandem CAR-T cells to monospecific CAR-T cells. We show that, on double-positive tumor cells, tandem CAR-T cells bind one antigen at a time and overcome tumor antigen heterogeneity more efficiently than monospecific CAR-T cells in mixed tumor models in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, tandem CAR-T cells show a preferential killing of tumor cells based on their antigen density.

Material and methods

Cell lines

ASPC-1 (cat. No. CRL-1682), Capan-2 (cat. No. HTB-80), BxPC-3 (cat. No. CRL-1687) pancreatic adenocarcinoma cell lines, SKOV-3 (Cat. No. HTB-77) and OVCAR3 (cat. No. HTB-161) ovarian cancer cell lines, and Jurkat cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured as recommended. OVCAR4 was a gift from Dr. O O Yeku at our institution. All cell lines were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) (Gibco) media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (R10 media). OVCAR3 cell line was cultured in RPMI media (Gibco) and supplemented with 20% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (R20 media). TrypLE Express (Gibco) was used to disassociate all tumor cell lines between routine passaging to preserve expression of meso. ASPC-1 and OVCAR3 meso knockout, OVCAR3 MUC16 knockout and OVCAR3 double knockout, MUC16ecto transduced ASPC-1, and color-coded and antigen-coded cell lines were generated as indicated in online supplemental Material and Methods. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination. Cell lines ordered from the ATCC were used within 6 months of ordering or authenticated via STR (short tandem repeat) analysis.

Human samples

High-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) samples were obtained following informed written consent under DFHCC (Dana-Farber/Harvard Cancer Center) protocol 07–049. The histology of the tumor was confirmed by MGB pathology. The tumor/cells were banked and/or used to generate organoid or xenograft establishment. Independently, ovarian and pancreatic patient-derived xenograft (PDX) samples and their correlate information were obtained from the Center for Patient Derived Models database at Dana-Farber (cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics). Tissue microarrays of ovarian (OV242b) and pancreatic (PA242f) tumors from patients were obtained from US Biomax for immunohistochemistry (IHC). Each microarray contains 20 cores of different types of either ovarian or pancreatic cancer with adjacent normal tissue.

Generation of CAR constructs

Transgenes for anti-meso, anti-MUC16ecto and tandem CAR were designed in Geneious Prime (V.2023.1.1). After synthesizing, the constructs were cloned into a lentiviral (LV) plasmid backbone regulated by a human EF-1a promoter (Genscript). Tandem CARs, anti-meso and anti-MUC16ecto CAR constructs contain a CD8 signal sequence, a CD8 hinge and transmembrane domains, a 4–1BB costimulatory domain, and an intracellular CD3z signaling domain. Each construct also contains mCherry as a fluorescent marker for the evaluation of transduction efficiency. The scFvs against meso and MUC16ecto were derived from sequences of SS134 and 4H1119 (available to the public: PCT/US13/28980 and PCT/US2011/030025). Six tandem iterations were designed based on scFv positioning and linker length between scFvs. The scFv of SS1 and 4H11 was linked using a G4S linker (from one to three repetitions). The order of the scFv was swapped. The tandem scFvs iterations are as follows: SS1-(G4S)x1-4H11 (tandem1), SS1-(G4S)x2-4H11 (tandem2), SS1-(G4S)x3-4H11 (tandem3), 4H11-(G4S)x1-SS1 (tandem4), 4H11-(G4S)x2-SS1 (tandem5) and 4H11-(G4S)x3-SS1 (tandem6). All sequences were codon optimized using IDT Codon Optimization Tool and GenSmart Codon Optimization tool from Genescript.

CAR T-cell production

Leukapheresis product from anonymous healthy human donors was purchased from the MGH blood bank, under an institutional review board–exempt protocol. T-cell isolation kits (Stem Cell Technologies) were used according to the manufacturer’s protocols and T cells were cryopreserved. For CAR T-cell production, cells were thawed in R10 media and activated with human CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (Life Technologies), which were added at a 3:1 bead to T-cell ratio, along with 20 IU/mL media of recombinant human IL2 (PeproTech). The next day, a corresponding amount of lentivirus was added to each well to have a MOI (multiplicity of infection) of 5 per construct. Untransduced (UTD) T cells from matching donors were cultured simultaneously under the same conditions to serve as controls. Every 2–3 days, cells were counted and R10 media was added to have a 5×105 cells/mL, and IL2 was added to a final concentration of 20 IU/mL. After 7 days, magnetic separation was used to remove Dynabeads from T-cell culture. Transduction efficiency was evaluated on day 12 or 13 through mCherry expression, on a BD LSRFortessa 20x or NovoCyte Penteon (Agilent). On day 14, bulk CAR T cells were cryopreserved for further assays in vitro and in vivo.

Flow cytometry

The following antibody clones were used for flow cytometry: Human anti-Mesothelin (Maus-Laboratory antibody, A2A11), Mouse Fab2 (Cell Signaling Technology, 4410), Human anti-Mesothelin (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-118-096), Human IgG1 (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-113-434), Human anti-CA125 (R&D Systems, MAB5609), Mouse IgG1 (R&D Systems, FAB9627P), Human anti-MUC16ecto (synthesized by GenScript from 4H11 scFv), Mouse Anti-IgG (BioLegend, 405308), Human anti-CD19 (BioLegend, 302240), Human anti-G4S (Cell Signaling Technology, 38907, and 69782). For direct staining of anti-meso scFv (SS1) expression, we used His-tag-FITC-Labeled Human Mesothelin (ACROBiosystems, MSN-HF223) and FITC-Labeled Human Mesothelin (Kactus, MSL-HM280F). For direct staining of anti-MUC16ecto scFv (4H11) expression, we used His-tag Labeled Human CA125 (ACROBiosystems CA5-H52H6) and anti-His-tag antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 14931). Appropriate isotype control antibodies were used as necessary. In general, cells were washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) 2% FBS before staining with respective antibodies/labeled proteins at ambient temperature or 4°C for 15 min in the dark, following manufacturer’s recommendations. Cells were washed again and stained with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole), 7AAD (BioLegend, 420404) or Live/Dead Fixable Far Red Dead Cell Stain Kit (Thermo Fisher, L34974) to assess cell viability before analyzing on a BDFortessa X-20 or a NovoCyte Penteon (Agilent). To assess meso and MUC16 expression in HGSOC patient-derived organoids by flow cytometry, the organoids were transferred from the plate to a conical tube by aspirating Matrigel domes from each well. After centrifuging, the pellet was resuspended in TrypLE (ref# 12604–021) at 37°C for dissociation, and cells were spun down and washed with PBS twice. The antibody staining protocol was the same as for tumor cell lines. Analysis was done in FlowJo.

Real-time cytotoxic assays

Different types of ASPC-1 tumor cells regarding meso and MUC16ecto expression were cultured overnight in a 48-well plate (Corning, 3548). CAR-T and UTD-T cells were added to the 48-well plates in an effector to target ratio of 1:1. CAR-T/UTD cells were co-cultured with tumor cells for 96 hours. Cytotoxicity was analyzed using an IncuCyte Live-Cell Analysis system. The Green Area (tumor cells) per well for 96 hours normalized to time point 0 (beginning of the assay) was the parameter to establish the antitumor activity of CAR/UTD-T cells. The Red Area (mCherry+cells) per well for 96 hours normalized to time point 0 was the parameter to quantify the proliferation of CAR-T cells. Tumor cells used for these assays expressed GFP or iRFP proteins.

Spheroids killing assays

Mixed spheroids of MesoendoMUC16neg ASPC-1 cells and MesoKOMUC16ectoTR ASPC-1 were made co-culturing these tumor cells, in a 1:1 ratio, 20×103 total cells/well, with R10 media with a 0.1% of methyl cellulose (Sigma-Aldrich). Tumor cells were seeded in a 96-well plate, centrifuged for 3 min at 1,630 rpm, and incubated for 3 days at 37°C CO2 5%. After 3 days, 20×103 total CAR and UTD-T cells were added to each well. Cytotoxicity was monitored for 106 hours using an IncuCyte Live-Cell Analysis system (see previous paragraph Real-time cytotoxic assays). CAR-T cells were recovered and counted by flow cytometry using CountBright Plus Absolute Counting Beads (Thermo Fisher), in a Fortessa 20x.

Avidity assessment with acoustic force cell microscopy

Poly-L Lysine–coated flow cell z-Movi chips (Lumicks) were seeded with different types of ASPC-1 cells based on meso and MUC16ecto expression, at a 40×106 cells/mL, and incubated for 1.5 hours. CAR/UTD-T cells were sorted in a Sony MAB900 (Sony Biotechnology) 48 hours before the avidity assessment, using 7AAD−, mCherry+ gates to harvest only the CAR-T cells. Sorted CAR-T cells and UTD-T cells were stained with a CellTrace Far red Proliferation kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each chip was run first with UTD-T cells, to prevent unspecific confounding binding of CAR-T cells, then run with monospecific and tandem CAR-T cells, and finally run with UTD-T cells. To prevent any potential benefit of binding in the earlier runs, we alternated the order of monospecific and tandem CAR-T cells to be tested. T cells were incubated for a 5 min binding period and visualized on the z-Movi cell avidity analyzer (Lumicks), meanwhile a 1,000 pN acoustic force ramp was applied. The percentage of cells bound as a function of acoustic force applied was then analyzed through Oceon software (V.1.5.5 Lumicks). Each chip was run at least five times.

In vivo models

NOD-SCID-y chain−/− (NSG) mice (The Jackson Laboratory) were housed and bred at the Center for Comparative Medicine at Massachusetts General Hospital under pathogen-free conditions. Mice were maintained and used for conducting experiments in adherence to an MGH Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC)-approved protocol (No.2020N000114). All mouse handling, including injections and monitoring, was performed by three animal technicians independent (blinded) from the scientific hypotheses. Mice included were from both sexes and were normalized before treatment to have similar tumor engraftment (as measured by mean caliper measurement or bioluminescence) across all groups. Only female mice were used for ovarian cancer in vivo experiments. Mixed models of ASPC-1 made of MesoendoMUC16neg, MesoKO MUC16ectoTR and MesoendoMUC16ectoTR cells, in a 1:1:1 ratio, were resuspended to 2×106 cells/150 µL of a 1:1 mixture of PBS and Matrigel (Corning) and injected into each mouse subcutaneously. Caliper measurements were taken biweekly. Fourteen days after tumor injection, CAR or UTD-T cells were thawed, counted and resuspended to 3×106 CAR/UTD-T cells/100 µL of PBS, and finally administered intravenously through the tail vein. For T cell infiltration into tumor, the same mixed model of ASPC-1 was used as described above, and the same CAR-T/UTD-T cell number and route of administration were used. Mice were euthanized after 21 days of CAR-T/UTD-T cells injection to analyze the abundance of T cell infiltration by IHC. OVCAR3 CBG-GFP tumor cells were resuspended to 3×106 cells/150 µL of PBS, and injected intraperitoneally. Mice were monitored weekly for bioluminescent emission using an Ami HT optical imaging system (Spectral Instruments) after 5 min post intraperitoneal substrate injection of D-Luciferin (30 mg/mL). Images were analyzed with Aura software (V.4.5.0). 14 days after tumor injection, CAR-T or UTD-T cells were thawed, counted and resuspended to 1×106 CAR T cells/100 µL of PBS, and finally, administered intraperitoneally. Mouse experiments continued until IACUC guidelines recommended euthanasia, or until 90 days post CAR-T/UTD-T cell injection, whatever happened first.

Statistical analysis

Data are generally depicted as mean±SEM, unless stated otherwise within figure legends. Statistically significant differences were determined by tests as indicated within figure legends. A p value of<0.05 was considered as significant. P values are indicated with asterisks as indicated in each figure legend and are listed as follows: *p<0.05; **p<0.01; ***p<0.001; ****p<0.0001; and not significant, p>0.05. The number of repeated biological and technical replicates is indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Statistical analysis was conducted in GraphPad Prism (V.10.2.2).

Results

Mesothelin and MUC16 are heterogeneously expressed across patients with ovarian and pancreatic cancer tumors and cell lines

We confirmed the expression of meso and MUC16 across ovarian and pancreatic tumors using various methods. First, we evaluated samples from patients diagnosed with high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma by flow cytometry. The samples were collected at different time points of the disease, with patient 1 collected from a debulking surgery in a platinum-sensitive disease, patient 2 from ascites with platinum-resistant disease, and patient 3 from pleural effusion with platinum-resistant disease. In these samples, the expression of meso and MUC16 was highly heterogeneous. Most of Patient 1’s cells expressed both antigens, with 66% being double-positive, and 4.57% and 26.3% single positive for meso or MUC16, respectively. Patient 2 had mostly meso single-positive cells (72.7%) and 15% double-positive, with <2% single-positive for MUC16. Patient 3 had mostly double-positive cells (78.5%), followed by MUC16 single-positive (14.1%) and 3.4% meso-single-positive (figure 1A).

We then used the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics, to analyze meso and MUC16 mRNA expression across 39 ovarian and 45 pancreatic cancer PDX samples. Pancreatic cancer PDXs had overall higher expression of meso but lower MUC16 expression compared with ovarian cancer PDXs (figure 1B and online supplemental figure S1A). We noted that ovarian PDXs with the lowest meso RNA expression generally corresponded with less common histologies such as Müllerian mixed tumor, carcinosarcoma, yolk sac tumor and non-specified. These tumors also had low expression of MUC16 mRNA. We also analyzed a variety of ovarian and pancreatic patients’ tumor tissue by IHC and observed heterogeneous antigen expression (figure 1C). HGSOC and low-grade serous ovarian cancer abundantly expressed meso and MUC16, whereas the expression was more variable in ovarian clear cell carcinoma, mucinous pancreatic carcinoma and ductal adenocarcinoma. Ovarian endometrioid carcinoma did not show expression of either antigen, and pancreatic papillary adenocarcinoma showed expression of meso but scarcely expressed MUC16.

Similar to primary human tumors, meso and MUC16 antigen expression was also heterogeneous across established tumor cell lines. All ovarian cancer cell lines expressed both antigens to different extents. OVCAR3 was mostly double-positive and had the highest expression of either antigen by MFI (mean fluorescence intensity) (online supplemental figure S1B). OVCAR4 had roughly equal percentages of double-positive, MUC16 single-positive, and double-negative cells; and SKOV3 cells were mostly double-negative. Among the pancreatic cancer cell lines, only CAPAN2 expressed MUC16, while all the pancreatic cell lines evaluated expressed meso, with ASPC-1 having the highest meso expression by MFI (online supplemental figure S1C and D).

These data confirm the heterogeneity of expression of Meso and MUC16 and therefore the potential benefit of targeting meso and MUC16 with CAR-T cell therapy in some histological types of ovarian and pancreatic cancer.

CAR T cells with tandem scFvs targeting mesothelin and MUC16 bind to both antigens

To target both antigens, we designed a tandem CAR using scFvs specific for meso and MUC16. We and others have demonstrated that the distal versus proximal position of the scFvs relative to the cell membrane can affect the antigen-binding capacity of the CAR.8,1235 Likewise, the length and composition of the linker between the scFvs determine its flexibility and antigen binding, which impact CAR efficacy.10 37 39 For this reason, we investigated which scFv rearrangement and linker length conferred the highest meso and MUC16 binding in a tandem design. We designed a monocistronic LV vector encoding a 4-1BB-costimulated CAR containing anti-meso (SS1) and anti-MUC16ecto (4H11) scFvs connected by a G4S linker. We designed six constructs with varying G4S linker lengths and scFv arrangements (figure 2A; online supplemental figure S2A) and generated SS1 and 4H11 monospecific CAR constructs as controls. The antitumor activity of CAR-T cells containing SS1 scFv and 4H11 scFv has been reported elsewhere.19 22 All constructs were based on an EF1-alpha-driven, second-generation CAR backbone containing the intracellular domains of 4-1BB and CD3z. An mCherry reporter transgene was added to each construct as a marker of transduction efficiency (online supplemental figure S2A).

We transduced an immortalized human T cell line, Jurkat cells, with LV vectors containing each of the tandem and monospecific constructs and assessed transduction efficiency (by mCherry expression; online supplemental figure S2B and C) and cell surface CAR expression (using an anti-G4S antibody; online supplemental figure S2D) by flow cytometry. Transduction efficiency was similar among the constructs, except for tandem CAR configurations (TanCARs) 1–3, which showed overall lower transduction efficiency. Additionally, transduction of TanCARs 1–3 was proportionally lower with increasing length of the G4S linker. As expected, the MFI of the G4S+ population increased with the number of G4S repetitions (online supplemental figure S2D). The 4H11 monospecific CAR had a high MFI value for mCherry, but the lowest G4S expression, suggesting that this CAR is not efficiently expressed on the cell surface (online supplemental figure S2C and D). We then measured the antigen-binding capacity of the tandem and monospecific CARs to soluble meso and MUC16 (CA125) by flow cytometry (figure 2B, online supplemental Material and Methods). The monospecific SS1 CAR had the highest binding capacity to meso, followed by the tandem configurations with the SS1 scFv in the distal position (TanCARs 1–3), and then the tandem constructs with distal 4H11 (TanCARs 4–6). TanCAR1, with one G4S repeat as linker, had the highest binding for meso, followed by TanCAR2 ((G4S)×2) and TanCAR3 ((G4S)×3). TanCAR1 also had the highest binding capacity for MUC16, which was even higher than the monospecific 4H11 CAR, likely due to the higher CAR expression. We also investigated whether there was any steric hindrance that may impair the binding of both soluble antigens to tandem CARs. We observed that TanCAR3-6 had lower MFI value for bound meso and/or MUC16 when we used a (sequential) double staining compared with single staining, suggesting a steric hindrance for one of the antigens when other antigen is bound (figure 2C and D). TanCAR1 and TanCAR2 did not show any steric hindrance for meso or MUC16 binding (figure 2C and D). These data suggest that the scFV order and the linker length impact the binding of soluble antigens by the tandem CAR, with linker length being inversely related to soluble antigen binding.

We next evaluated the activation of CAR-Jurkat cells on exposure to tumor cells. We generated ASPC-1 pancreatic cancer and OVCAR3 ovarian cancer cell lines that expressed different combinations of meso and MUC16 (online supplemental Material and Methods). ASPC-1 cells express endogenous meso and are negative for MUC16 at baseline (MesoendoMUC16neg), serving as single meso antigen-expressing cells. To better recapitulate patient samples where both antigens are expressed,28 29 we generated double and single MUC16 antigen-expressing cells using the MUC16ecto. We chose to only express the ectodomain (the domain that the 4H11 scFv binds to) due to the large size of MUC16,40 which makes it difficult to transduce a transgene coding for the whole protein. The MUC16ecto was transduced into wild-type ASPC-1 (MesoendoMUC16ectoTR) or ASPC-1 with meso knocked out (MesoKOMUC16ectoTR), whereas wild-type ASPC-1 with meso KO served as a double-negative control (MesoKOMUC16neg; figure 2E). OVCAR3 cells express endogenous meso and MUC16 at baseline (MesoendoMUC16endo) and were knocked out for meso and/or MUC16 to generate single antigen-expressing (MesoKOMUC16endo; MesoendoMUC16KO) and double-negative cells (MesoKOMUC16KO; figure 2E, online supplemental figure S1E). We observed that the level of expression of the MUC16ecto in OVCAR3 (MesoendoMUC16endo) and (MesoKOMUC16endo), was lower than in artificially transduced ASPC-1 with the MUC16ecto (figure 2E).

We then transduced monospecific or tandem CARs into Jurkat NFAT-eGFP reporter cells, which express GFP on activation. When co-cultured with tumor cells, Jurkat NFAT-eGFP cells transduced with the tandem CARs expressed GFP in the presence of tumor cells that had either or both antigens but did not express GFP when the tumor cells did not express the antigens. As expected, Jurkat NFAT-eGFP cells transduced with either monospecific CAR only expressed GFP when co-cultured with tumor cells expressing the corresponding antigen. None of the CAR-Jurkat NFAT-eGFP reporter cells showed relevant GFP expression in the absence of tumor, demonstrating a lack of tonic signaling. These data indicate that tandem CARs recognize meso and MUC16ecto (either as in the endogenous MUC16ecto or in the transduced one) on tumor cells, whether one or both antigens are present, and drive T cell activation in an antigen-specific manner (figure 2F). Collectively, the data from the soluble antigen binding and activation on tumor cell exposure demonstrated that TanCAR1 had the highest binding capacity for meso and MUC16, without steric hindrance when binding both antigens, and the highest activation in response to both tumor cell lines compared with the other tandem constructs (figure 2G and H). TanCAR2 and 3 had the next highest antigen binding, but TanCAR3 showed slightly stronger activation. For this reason, we chose TanCAR1 and TanCAR3 for further studies in primary human T cells.

TanCAR1 T cells have superior in vitro cytotoxicity

To examine TanCAR1 and TanCAR3 function in primary human T cells, we transduced primary human T cells isolated from normal donors with LV encoding the SS1, 4H11, TanCAR1, or TanCAR3 construct. The transduction efficiency of these four constructs varied, as demonstrated by differences in mCherry+ cells measured by flow cytometry (online supplemental figure S3A). SS1 CAR-T cells showed the highest transduction efficiency (56.7%), and TanCAR1-T cells the lowest (32.3%). Similar to the CAR-Jurkat cells, the 4H11 construct had the lowest G4S expression by flow (online supplemental figure S3B), suggesting decreased CAR expression despite similar transduction efficiency. Likewise, TanCAR3 had slightly higher G4S expression compared with TanCAR1, likely due to the increased linker length. To normalize the number of CAR T cells used for functional analyses across constructs, we used UTD-T cells to dilute the percentage of mCherry+ cells to the lowest transduction efficiency. We then performed in vitro tumor lysis assays measuring luciferase activity after 24 hours of co-incubating tumor cells and CAR-T cells (online supplemental Material and Methods). We used ASPC-1 and OVCAR3 with different antigen expression patterns that also express luciferase and GFP to allow for tracking in vitro and in vivo. We observed that TanCAR1 and TanCAR3 lysed ASPC-1 and OVCAR3 tumor cells in all conditions (single-positive and double-positive tumor cells). As expected, the monospecific CAR-T cells only lysed the tumor cells when the target antigen was expressed (figure 3A and B; online supplemental figure S3C–J). This cytotoxic activity was antigen-specific since UTD-T cells did not kill tumor cells, and none of the CAR T cells killed the double-negative cell lines (figure 3A and B). TanCAR1 and TanCAR3 killed ASPC-1 equally well, regardless of the different antigen-expression patterns, but TanCAR1 had superior killing activity than TanCAR3 against MesoKOMUC16endo and MesoendoMUC16endo OVCAR3 cells.

We also measured Th1 and Th2 cytokine production by the CAR-T cells 24 hours after exposure to tumor cells. We collected supernatant from the killing assay cultures and measured Th1 and Th2 cytokines by Luminex assay (online supplemental figure S3K and L) (see online supplemental Material and Methods). As expected, the monospecific CARs (SS1 and 4H11) produced Th1 and Th2 cytokines when their target antigen was expressed by the cell line but not when the antigen was not expressed (online supplemental figure S3K and L). For ASPC-1 cells, Th1 (IL-2, interferon-gamma, TNFα and GM-CSF) cytokine production was similar when both antigens were expressed; as well as for OVCAR3 cells (figure 3C and D). SS1 CAR-T cells expressed less cytokines in response to double+ versus meso-single+ (figure 3D). Likewise, the level and patterns of cytokine expression correlated with the level of meso and MUC16ectoTR or MUC16 antigen expressed in the cell line. For example, ASPC-1 has higher levels of MUC16ecto since it was artificially introduced into the cells versus what is naturally expressed by OVCAR3. Therefore, we were not surprised to note that 4H11 and the TanCARs had higher cytokine expression under the stimulation of MUC16ectoTR ASPC-1 cells than MUC16endo OVCAR3 (figure 3C and D, online supplemental figure S3K and L).

To distinguish functional differences between TanCAR1 and TanCAR3, we compared cytokine production in response to each cell line. In ASPC-1, the TanCAR cytokine production was mainly driven by MUC16 recognition, whereas in OVCAR3 co-cultures we observed more cytokine secretion in response to meso (figure 3C and D). These data correlated with the soluble protein binding capacity of each CAR construct (figure 2B), and antigen abundance (figure 2E, online supplemental figure S2E). The level of cytokine production was very similar between TanCAR1 and 3 in ASPC-1 and OVCAR3 co-cultures (figure 3C and D). To elucidate any structural difference(s), we also looked at the in-silico structure prediction of TanCAR1 and TanCAR3 using AlphaFold341 (online supplemental Material and Methods). In general, each scFv structure was predicted with high confidence while there was lower confidence in the relative position of the SS1 and 4H11 scFvs to each other (online supplemental figure S3K–N). As expected, areas with greater flexibility are predicted with lower confidence, that is, the G4S region (online supplemental figure S3K and L). Moreover, the expected position error, which estimates the error in the relative position between two residues of the structure, was higher for TanCAR3 between the VH (variable heavy) and VL (variable light) of each scFv, than for TanCAR1, which might be due to a higher flexibility of the structure that may interfere with the antigen binding (online supplemental figure S3M and N). Therefore, the only difference between the two designs was the increased killing of OVCAR3 cells in vitro by TanCAR1 versus TanCAR3. For this reason, we chose to further analyze TanCAR1 to understand better its avidity and cytotoxicity against heterogeneous tumor models in vitro and in vivo.

Tandem CAR-T cells bind one antigen at a time

Tandem CARs are designed to target two antigens on tumor cells; however, it is not clear whether each tandem CAR molecule binds to both antigens at once or only one antigen at a time. As we have mentioned above, TanCAR1 did not have any steric hindrance to bind both soluble antigens, but we wanted to investigate if TanCAR1 was able to bind both antigens when they are anchored to tumor cells. Hedge et al,13 showed that tandem CAR-T cells cluster the targeted antigens in the same immune synapse but did not determine if each CAR was binding one or both antigens. To answer this question, we analyzed the binding avidity between the tandem CAR-T cells and tumor cells that expressed one or both target antigens using acoustic force and the z-movi analyzer, as described above. We first measured the avidity of TanCAR1 against ASPC-1 cancer cells that expressed the single antigens (MesoendoMUC16neg or MesoKOMUC16ectoTR) and compared its avidity with the monospecific CAR-T cells. TanCAR1 showed a similar avidity to the monospecific CAR-T cell with specificity to the antigen that was expressed (online supplemental figure S4A and B), suggesting that scFv avidity is not affected by tandem design and the avidity of the individual scFv is maintained.

Next, we analyzed the avidity of TanCAR1 for tumor cells that express both antigens. We hypothesized that if TanCAR1 binds one antigen at a time, its maximum avidity would be dictated by the scFv with the highest avidity (in this case, the SS1 scFv which has a KD(equilibrium disociation constant)=0.1 nM42; 4H11 scFv KD=2±1 nM43). Instead, if TanCAR1 binds two antigens at a time, there would be a synergistic effect of the two scFvs, rendering a higher avidity value compared with the monospecific CARs (figure 4A). For this purpose, we prepared ASPC-1 tumor cells with different levels of expression of meso and MUC16ecto (figure 4B and online supplemental figure S4C) and tested the avidity of single CAR and TanCAR1 for each one. When using ASPC-1H/H (high/high), which had high levels of expression of both antigens, we observed that the avidity of TanCAR1 was the same as the SS1 CAR (figure 4B). Moreover, we did not observe any difference between TanCAR1 and SS1 avidity for ASPC-1H/L (high/low). Similarly, there was no difference between TanCAR1 and 4H11 for ASPC-1L/H (low/high), which had high MUC16 expression (figure 4C–G). When the level of antigen expression was in between (medium), TanCAR1 showed a lower avidity compared with its avidity for cells with high antigen expression, and again, we did not observe any difference between TanCAR1 and SS1. These data suggest that TanCAR1 may bind one antigen at a time since its avidity is not better than the single CAR when the correlative antigen is highly expressed.

We next wanted to explore if the avidity is mainly driven by one antigen or both (figure 4H). We showed that avidity was driven by the cognate antigen level for single CAR (online supplemental figure S4D and E), but TanCAR1 showed the highest avidity when either antigen was overexpressed (H/H, L/H and H/L; figure 4I). In fact, there were statistical differences in TanCAR avidity based on antigen expression level. When either or both antigens were highly expressed, the avidity of TanCAR1 was higher than if both were expressed at medium (M/M) or low (L/L) levels (figure 4J and K). Therefore, TanCAR1 avidity is driven by the level of expression of both antigens, but it can only bind one antigen at a time.

TanCAR1 T cells overcome tumor antigen heterogeneity in mixed tumor models in vitro

We next sought to determine the efficacy of TanCAR1 against heterogeneous tumors using a mixed tumor model. We generated in vitro mixed cultures of ASPC-1 tumor cells expressing the single antigens (MesoendoMUC16neg, MesoKOMUC16ecto) and both antigens (MesoendoMUC16ectoTR; figure 5A) or neither antigen (MesoKOMUC16neg), what we called a stressed heterogeneous tumor model (online supplemental figure S5A). We then added SS1, 4H11, TanCAR1, or UTD-T cells to the mixed cultures and measured tumor cell killing by the change in the tumor area over time using an IncuCyte Live-Cell analysis system. TanCAR1 eliminated significantly more mixed ASPC-1 tumor cells compared with SS1CAR-T cells but demonstrated similar cytotoxicity to 4H11CAR-T cells (figure 5B, online supplemental figure S5B). To determine the cell composition at the end of the co-culture (96 hours), we collected the tumor cells and quantified each population by flow cytometry (online supplemental Material and Methods). The absolute number of tumor cells was lowest in the TanCAR1 coculture, followed by the 4H11 and then by the SS1 CAR T cells (figure 5C, online supplemental figure S5C). TanCAR1 targeted all tumor cell populations, except the double-negative cells (MesoKOMUC16neg). Meanwhile, the monospecific CAR-T cells only targeted tumor cells expressing their target antigen (figure 5D, online supplemental figure S5D). There were no differences in the tumor cell populations between the UTD-T cell-treated and tumor-only groups (online supplemental figure S5E–H).

Since we showed that TanCAR1 avidity depends on the presence of either antigen, and does not rely on one specific antigen, we next investigated whether TanCAR1 killing activity was antigen-driven based on antigen density in heterogeneous tumor models. To test this, we prepared mixed tumor cell cultures of ASPC-1 MesoendoMUC16neg and MesoKOMUC16ectoTR (figure 5E) in which ASPC-1 MesoKOMUC16ectoTR expressed more antigen than ASPC-1 MesoendoMUC16neg (figure 5F). To distinguish these two populations, the MesoendoMUC16neg cells were transduced with iRFP, and the MesoKOMUC16ectoTR cells were transduced with GFP. We then co-cultured the mixed tumor cells with CAR-T cells or UTD-T cells and measured tumor cell killing using the IncuCyte Live-Cell Analysis system. Again, TanCAR1 controlled tumor growth better than monospecific CAR-T cells when looking at the tumor cell population as a whole (figure 5G). When looking at cellular composition, TanCAR1 decreased the area of iRFP+ (MesoendoMUC16neg) cells better than the 4H11 CAR, but worse than the SS1 CAR-T (figure 5H). TanCAR1 also killed GFP+ (MesoKOMUC16ectoTR) cells better than the SS1 CAR but similar to the 4H11 CAR (figure 5I). When comparing which cells were preferentially targeted by TanCAR1, the MesoKOMUC16ectoTR cells were killed significantly more than the MesoendoMUC16neg (figure 5J).

Finally, we also used a three-dimensional in vitro spheroid model to measure CAR T cell killing of heterogeneous tumors. We generated spheroids from ASPC-1 MesoendoMUC16neg iRFP+cells and MesoKOMUC16ectoTR GFP+cells (online supplemental figure S5I). Spontaneously, the MesoendoMUC16neg cells organized in the core of the spheroids and the MesoKOMUC16ectoTR cells in the periphery (online supplemental figure S5J). Three days postseeding, we added CAR-T cells or UTD-T cells to the spheroids and monitored the area of the spheroids in each tumor cell line and CAR-T cells using an IncuCyte Live-Cell Analysis system. TanCAR1 controlled tumor spheroids better than monospecific CAR-T cells (figure 5K; online supplemental figure S5K), and killed both ASPC-1 cells more effectively than monospecific CAR-T cells (figure 5L and M). We also evaluated CAR-T cell infiltration into the spheroid structure and their expansion by mCherry expression. TanCAR1 better infiltrated the spheroid compared with the SS1 and 4H11 CARs (figure 5K). Intriguingly, the SS1 CAR-T cells surrounded the spheroid but were not able to infiltrate as well as the 4H11 CAR and TanCAR1 (figure 5K). This pattern was likely due to the preferential location of the MesoKOMUC16ectoTR in the periphery, which is not targeted by the SS1 CAR-T cells and possibly blocked access to the MesoendoMUC16neg cells. Similarly, the 4H11 CAR-T cells tended to stay in the periphery of the spheroid, colocalizing with the MUC16ectoTR cells but not infiltrating further (figure 5K). TanCAR1 showed a higher expansion across time via the IncuCyte quantification compared with the SS1 CAR-T cells, but we did not see statistical differences between TanCAR1 and 4H11 (online supplemental figure S5L). We confirmed these results by flow cytometry for mCherry+cells following dissociation of the spheroids (online supplemental figure S5M).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that TanCAR1 effectively targets heterogeneous tumors in pancreatic cell line mixed tumor models showing increased killing compared with monospecific CAR-T cells. It also demonstrates an antigen-driven killing of tumor cells based on antigen density.

TanCAR1 T cells expand more and control heterogeneous tumor growth better than monospecific CAR-T cells in vivo

We next examined TanCAR1-T cells in vivo, first using the ovarian cancer cell line OVCAR3, which naturally expresses both antigens. NSG mice were engrafted intraperitoneally with OVCAR3 tumor cells, treated intravenously 14 days later with CAR-T cells, and monitored for tumor growth via bioluminescent imaging (figure 6A; online supplemental figure S6A). All mice treated with CAR-T cells experienced tumor reduction compared with the tumor only and UTD-treated groups, without differences between TanCAR1-T cells and monospecific CAR-T cells (figure 6B). To better model the tumor heterogeneity observed in patients with cancer, we next prepared a mixed tumor model of ASPC-1 MesoendoMUC16neg, MesoKOMUC16ectoTR and MesoendoMUC16ectoTR in a 1:1:1 ratio (online supplemental figure S6B), and engrafted this mixture subcutaneously in NSG mice. After 14 days, we administered CAR or UTD-T cells intravenously and monitored tumor growth via caliper measurements (figure 6C). Again, all mice treated with CAR-T cells had controlled tumor growth compared with tumor-only and UTD-treated mice; however, TanCAR1 further decreased tumor growth compared with the monospecific CAR-T cells (figure 6D) and, in some cases, completely eradicated the tumor (online supplemental figure S6C). Mice treated with monospecific CAR-T cells also showed an earlier relapse of the tumor compared with TanCAR1-treated mice (figure 6D).

To determine which cells were targeted most in vivo, when possible, we collected the tumors at the time of endpoint disposition and determined the tumor cell populations by flow cytometry (figure 6E). From one mouse treated with SS1 CAR-T cells, the remaining tumor had almost no MesoendoMUC16neg cells, but only MesoKOMUC16ectoTR and MesoendoMUC16ectoTR tumor cells. Tumors from mice treated with 4H11 CAR-T cells showed a higher proportion of MesoendoMUC16neg cells than MesoKOMUC16ectoTR and MesoendoMUC16ectoTR tumor cells. Interestingly, in tumors from mice treated with TanCAR1-T cells, the most abundant tumor population was MesoendoMUC16neg. This suggests that TanCAR1-T cells preferentially kill cells with MUC16ectoTR in vivo (figure 6F). This data aligns with the in vitro mixed tumor killing assays, where TanCAR1-T cells had better antitumor activity over the monospecific CAR-T cells and a skewed killing towards MUC16ectoTR tumor cells, which express more antigen than MesoendoMUC16neg.

Finally, we studied the infiltration of CAR-T cells into tumors, using an in vivo tumor model of mixed ASPC-1 (MesoendoMUC16neg, MesoKOMUC16ectoTR, and MesoendoMUC16ectoTR). Briefly, after 14 days of subcutaneous tumor engraftment, we administered CAR or UTD-T cells and collected the tumors 21 days later to analyze T cell infiltration by IHC for CD3+ cells (figure 6G; online supplemental figure S6D and E) and distribution of tumor cells in the tumors (online supplemental figure S6F and G) (online supplemental Material and Methods). We observed that meso single-positive tumor cells expanded less than MUC16ecto (either single or double positive) tumor cells, by flow cytometry (online supplemental figure S6F). Moreover, positive cells for meso staining, by IHC, were mainly organized in the inner part of the tumor, surrounded by meso negative or light positive tumor cells (online supplemental figure S6G). Mice treated with UTD-T cells had a scarce number of T cells in tumors, while TanCAR1-treated mice showed the highest CD3+ cell infiltration, which was statistically significant compared with SS1 and 4H11 tumor-treated mice (figure 6H–I). These data suggest that TanCAR1 has a better antitumor effect and higher intratumoral expansion than monospecific CAR-T cells.

Discussion

Strategies that target multiple antigens could avoid or reduce tumor relapse. CAR-T cell therapy has not yielded results as promising in solid tumors as in hematologic malignancies. One explanation for this discrepancy is that solid tumors tend to have more antigen heterogeneity, which makes monospecific CAR-T cells a suboptimal strategy for these tumors.22 44 45 In this study, we designed dual-targeting CAR-T cells to bind two antigens highly expressed in ovarian and pancreatic cancer, in an attempt to improve their efficacy against these hard-to-treat tumors. We built a series of tandem scFvs targeting meso (SS1) and the MUC16ecto (4H11) and found that TanCAR1 (with SS1 as the distal scFv and only one G4S linker between it and the 4H11 scFv) had the best antitumor activity in vitro. TanCAR1 also controlled tumor growth and increased T cell infiltration in a mixed tumor model in vitro and in vivo compared with the monospecific CAR-T cells. Moreover, we demonstrated that TanCAR1-T cells likely bind to one antigen at a time, which skews their killing function toward the antigen that is more highly expressed in the tumor.

To our knowledge, this is the first time targeting meso and MUC16 using a tandem CAR design. While meso and MUC16 monospecific CAR T cells have been used in clinical trials, neither has achieved results beyond stable disease.21,24 Nevertheless, the safety profile from clinical trials with SS1 and 4H11 as anti-meso and anti-MUC16ecto scFv, respectively, did not show any relevant on-target off-tumor toxicity.22 24 As we report here and others have reported previously,27,29 meso and MUC16 expression in tumors is heterogeneous. We noted that MUC16 expression in human pancreatic cell lines and PDX is lower than reported by Einama et al and Shuzimi et al.28 29 For that reason, we overexpressed the MUC16ecto in a pancreatic cancer cell line to better represent antigen expression in patients. We found that the tandem CAR-T cells outperformed the monospecific CAR-T cells in settings where there was a mix of single and double antigen-expressing cells. Mixed populations of tumor cells have been observed in patient tumors.27,29 However, the tandem CAR-T cells did not show superiority compared with monospecific CAR-T cells against double-positive tumor cells alone in vitro and in vivo. These results are similar to those reported by us and others, where tandem CAR-T cells targeting various antigens did not show superiority to their monospecific CAR-T cell counterparts when both antigens are expressed by the tumor cells.8 11 39 Therefore, the benefit of tandem over monospecific CARs is especially relevant in heterogeneous models in terms of antitumor activity and T-cell expansion.8 9 31 46

Activation and killing activity of the TanCAR1 is driven by the level of antigen expression and antigen accessibility. In response to ASPC-1 with MUC16ectoTR in vitro, TanCAR1 had higher cytokine secretion and greater activation than cells without MUC16ectoTR. However, cytokine production by TanCAR1 on OVCAR3 cell line exposure, was higher when meso was expressed. In our mixed tumor models, TanCAR1 preferentially killed tumor cells that express MUC16ectoTR in vitro and in vivo. In heterogeneous tumors in vivo, tumor relapse after TanCAR1 treatment was mainly driven by Mesoendo MUC16neg ASPC-1 cells, demonstrating an increased killing of tumor cells with higher antigen expression. This was in line with what we observed in mixed tumor models in vitro, where TanCAR1-T cells preferentially killed MesoKOMUC16ectoTR cells over MesoendoMUC16neg when they were tracked with cell-specific fluorescent proteins. This increased killing is likely due to a higher density of MUC16ectoTR from being transduced into the cells versus the endogenously expressed meso. This is in line with data from monospecific CAR-T cells, where increased antigen expression results in increased killing.32 33

The increased killing of MUC16ectoTR cells by TanCAR1-T cells could also be due to increased accessibility of the MUC16ecto when lacking expression of the whole protein. The ectodomain, where the 4H11 scFv binds, is the portion of the protein that is proximal to the cell membrane and the portion that remains when MUC16 is cleaved.47 In OVCAR3 cells, where the full-length MUC16 is naturally expressed, the ectodomain is detected in a lower level than in artificially transduced ASPC1, explaining why the 4H11 monospecific CAR-T cells had a low activation and cytokine secretion in response to MesoKOMUC16endo cells in vitro, whereas ASPC-1 cells with MUC16ectoTR readily activated and induced cytokine production by 4H11 CAR-T cells. This suggests that, in vitro, 4H11 scFv cannot properly reach its binding epitope in MUC16. In contrast, 4H11 monospecific CAR-T cells controlled OVCAR3 tumor growth equally as well as the SS1 and TanCAR1 CAR-T cells in vivo. This paradoxical phenomenon could be related to differences between in vitro and in vivo shedding conditions of MUC16, which leads to the exposure of the epitope to 4H11 CAR-T cells. Mechanisms related to MUC16 cleavage are not well known, but it has been associated with the presence of proinflammatory cytokines48 and an acidic media in the Golgi apparatus.40 Also, when full-length MUC16 is present in OVCAR3 cells, activation and cytokine secretion by SS1 monospecific CAR-T cells are lower than when it is knocked out. This difference could be due to the competitive binding of MUC16 and SS1 scFv to meso.49 50

We further observed that the TanCAR1-T cells behave like the monospecific CAR-T cells of the antigen that is more accessible (MUC16ecto in ASPC-1 and meso in OVCAR3). This similarity suggested that tandem CAR-T cells target one antigen at a time, even though there was not steric hindrance to bind both soluble antigens. We decided to test this hypothesis using acoustic force microscopy to measure the avidity between the CAR-T and tumor cells. This was the first time this technique was used to characterize the avidity of tandem CAR-T cells. We found that TanCAR1 had the same avidity as the monospecific CAR-T cells for the single positive tumor cells, but the affinity to the soluble antigen was different between monospecific CAR and tandem CAR-T cells. Discrepancies between affinity and avidity have been recently published comparing different CAR constructs, in which even though having similar affinity values, avidity made the difference in tumor control.51 52 Moreover, based on avidity data, we observed that TanCAR1 bound one antigen at a time since we did not see any significant increase in avidity over monospecific CAR-T cells after exposure to cell lines that expressed high levels of both or one of the antigens, and we observed a reduction in the avidity when both antigens were expressed in a medium level. These results align with the similar killing activity of double-positive tumor cells by tandem and monospecific CAR-T cells.

The finding that our tandem CAR T cells bind to one antigen at a time is different from the tandem CAR to Her2/IL13Ra2 that was reported by Hedge et al.13 They found that anti-HER2/IL13Ra2 tandem CAR had better cytotoxicity than monospecific CAR-T cells for double+tumor cells. However, the tandem CAR-T cells seemed to have superior cytokine secretion on exposure to single positive tumor cells than monospecific CAR-T cells, which apparently make the tandem CAR superior to the monospecific, even in a single positive tumor cell setting. They also used confocal microscopy, stimulated emission depletion microscopy, and proximity ligation assay to show that both antigens colocalized with their tandem CAR design.13 This suggested the tandem CAR they designed may bind both antigens at the same time. Tong et al,10 describe a tandem CAR against CD19 and CD20. In that study, tandem CAR outperformed monospecific CAR-T cells to control double-positive tumor cells in vivo and in vitro. However, cytokine production was not superior to monospecific CAR-T cells. They showed that tandem CAR-T cells could establish a more stable immune synapse and faster degranulation than monospecific CAR-T cells. The authors do not state whether the tandem CAR-T cells bind one or two antigens at the same time, and the reasons why the tandem is more efficacious than monospecific CAR-T cells beyond a stronger immune synapse and faster degranulation are not detailed. It is likely that these differences among tandem CAR designs and how they bind each antigen reflect differing epitope interactions (ie, distance to the membrane of each antigen), each antigen density and the natural proximity versus exclusion of the individual antigens targeted by the tandem CARs, since we observed that there was not steric hindrance to bind both soluble antigens, but an absence of increased avidity regarding single CARs when both antigens were expressed on the tumor cell surface. Furthermore, differences in tandem CAR design may also impact how CAR/antigen interactions are sustained. Together these findings imply that just as there are few “rules” for CAR design that can be applied across CAR constructs, there may be idiosyncrasies of tandem CAR designs as well, which will make their design an iterative process where each new version requires individualized testing and validation.

In conclusion, TanCAR1 is the first tandem CAR design against meso and MUC16ecto that shows benefit over monospecific CAR-T cells, especially in heterogeneous tumor models. The one-antigen-at-a-time binding properties of TanCAR1 and its apparently preferential killing of tumor cells with high antigen density, may help to design therapeutic strategies to overcome tumor heterogeneity in ovarian and pancreatic tumors.
