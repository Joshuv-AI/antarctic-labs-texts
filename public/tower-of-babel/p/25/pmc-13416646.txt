Analysis of scapular morphology as a key factor in unstable painful shoulder: an magnetic resonance imaging-based study
Authors: Yasutaka Yoshimura, Daichi Morikawa, Yoshiaki Itoigawa, Hirohisa Uehara, Kenta Shibuya, Yoshimasa Saigo, Shu Nishimura, Hironori Tsurukami, Fumitoshi Hatae, Kazuki Yoshida, Yusuke Tatebayashi, Takayuki Kawasaki, Takefumi Kaketa, Muneaki Ishijima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnstable painful shoulder (UPS) is a recently recognized condition characterized by pain as the chief symptom, with no awareness of dislocation or subluxation but presenting imaging or arthroscopic findings indicative of anterior shoulder instability (ASI). However, the factors contributing to symptoms differentiating UPS from ASI remain unclear. The purpose of this study was to investigate and compare morphological factors on magnetic resonance imaging between pain-predominant (UPS) and apprehension-dominant (ASI) instability presentations.MethodsFrom our institutional database of 5,400 patients presenting with shoulder complaints between January 2013 and December 2023, we extracted 2,278 patients who underwent shoulder magnetic resonance imaging and identified active athletes matching strict eligibility criteria. The study included 74 patients diagnosed with UPS, 81 with only one dislocation episode because of ASI, and 36 controls with no shoulder pathology. The UPS and ASI groups were limited to collision sports, and each contained 57 patients matched for sex, height, and age. Fourteen parameters related to glenoid, acromial, and coracoid morphology were compared among the 3 groups. Logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic curve analyses were performed to identify predictors of UPS.ResultsThe UPS group had a significantly greater glenoid width and depth than the ASI and control groups (P < .001). The UPS group also had a significantly higher posterior acromion height and significantly less anterior and posterior acromial coverage (P < .05). Logistic regression analysis identified glenoid depth as the strongest independent predictor of UPS (odds ratio, 9.73; 95% confidence interval, 3.11–30.47). A cutoff value of 1.85 mm for glenoid depth distinguished UPS from ASI with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.790.Discussion and ConclusionGlenoid depth was identified as the strongest independent parameter distinguishing pain-predominant (UPS) from apprehension-dominant (ASI) instability. UPS was characterized by a stable scapular morphology, featuring a deeper and wider glenoid and a more anteriorly covering acromion. These subtle native structural variations characterize distinct phenotypes within the shoulder instability spectrum. While pain-predominant instability is a recognized clinical phenotype, understanding this characteristic scapular morphology may help clinicians to anticipate the presence of occult instability lesions, prevent delays in clinical diagnosis, and avoid misdiagnosis as alternative shoulder pathologies due to the lack of subjective dislocation history.Keywords: Unstable painful shoulder, Anterior shoulder instability, Magnetic resonance imaging, Scapular morphology, Glenoid depth, Collision sport

Shoulder injuries are common in collision sports.35 In particular, anterior shoulder dislocation is one of the most frequent traumatic shoulder injuries in collision sports.2,15,21 However, young athletes in collision sports often feel only pain without recognizing instability and continue to participate in sporting activities. It was recently proposed that this condition should be referred to as unstable painful shoulder (UPS).5 UPS is characterized by pain as the chief complaint, without awareness of dislocation or subluxation, but with imaging or arthroscopic findings indicative of anterior shoulder instability (ASI), as soft tissue or bony lesions, such as a Bankart lesion or humeral avulsion of the glenohumeral ligament lesion, confirmed by arthroscopy or imaging.5,14 This definition was later refined to include the necessity of a traumatic onset.14 Although UPS and ASI have similar soft tissue lesions on imaging or arthroscopic examination, the main complaints of patients with UPS and ASI are different, and the reasons for the different clinical presentations are unknown.

ASI is associated with various risk factors, including age and activity level.12,17,24 Other nonmodifiable risk factors include physical examination findings, anterior apprehension test results, and relocation test results.29,36 Additionally, the presence and size of glenoid bone loss are widely recognized as critical factors that dictate the risk of recurrent anterior shoulder dislocation.4,6,8,34 Consequently, the quantification of bone defects of the glenoid and humerus has become a key factor in modern treatment algorithms, often guiding the choice between arthroscopic soft tissue lesion repair and more invasive bony augmentation procedures.7,16,31 Furthermore, recent studies report that the morphology of scapular features other than the glenoid, such as the acromion and coracoid, also plays an important role in shoulder stability.3,20,26,30,38

While the risk factors for and clinical management of ASI have been established, the risk factors for UPS have not been identified, and the condition remains unclear. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate and compare morphological factors on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) between pain-predominant (UPS) and apprehension-dominant (ASI) instability presentations. We hypothesized that individuals with UPS would have fundamentally different and more statically stable bony morphology compared with individuals with ASI and healthy controls and that the different clinical presentations of UPS versus ASI are attributable to the scapular bone morphology.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical considerations

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at a single academic institution. Our institution’s institutional review board approved the study protocol (protocol number: E24-0082) and waived the requirement for written informed consent owing to the retrospective nature of the data analysis. The study was performed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Participant demographics

From our institutional database of 5,400 patients presenting with shoulder complaints between January 2013 and December 2023, we extracted 2,278 patients who underwent shoulder MRI and identified active athletes matching strict eligibility criteria. From this cohort, we identified 3 distinct groups. The inclusion criteria for all groups were an age between 15 and 40 years and the availability of 1.5 or 3.0-T MRI scans suitable for morphological analysis. The exclusion criteria were (1) history of previous shoulder surgery, (2) clinical diagnosis of habitual or multidirectional instability, (3) shoulder-related fractures, (4) glenoid version >15°, (5) glenoid bone loss >20%, (6) rotator cuff tear, (7) glenohumeral osteoarthritis, or (8) connective tissue disease. Among the patients who met the eligibility criteria, there were 74 in the UPS group, 81 in the ASI group, and 36 in the control group. A propensity score matching analysis was then performed for the UPS and ASI groups. These groups were limited to patients who participated in collision sports and were matched in a 1:1 ratio for sex, age, and height. Propensity score matching was performed using a nearest-neighbor matching algorithm with a caliper of 0.2 times the standard deviation. There were 57 patients in each of the UPS and ASI groups. A total of 150 participants were included in the final analysis: 57 in the UPS group, 57 in the ASI group, and 36 in the control group (Fig. 1).

The absence of subjective instability was strictly defined during the medical record review, and patients were included only if the examining shoulder surgeon had explicitly documented “no history of subjective subluxation, dislocation, or giving way,” combined with imaging or arthroscopic findings indicative of ASI.

Magnetic resonance imaging acquisition and measurements

All patients underwent MRI of the affected shoulder using a 1.5 or 3.0-T scanner. The MRI protocol included axial, sagittal, and coronal sequences. MRI scans were then manually reformatted using imaging analysis software (SYNAPSE VINCENT; Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan). The sagittal view was perpendicular to the long axis of the scapula, with the glenoid en face. Fourteen parameters related to glenoid, acromion, and coracoid were measured on MRI using SYNAPSE VINCENT software. The definitions of the 14 morphological parameters were based on the literature.3,9,20,22,23,25, 26, 27,40

Glenoid parameters

The glenoid width and version were measured on axial magnetic resonance (MR) images based on the methods reported by Mauro et al22 (Fig. 2, A and C). The glenoid depth was measured on axial MR images as described by Moroder et al26 (Fig. 2B). The glenoid index was measured on sagittal MR images as the ratio of the glenoid height to width (ab/cd) based on the methods reported by Yellin et al40 (Fig. 2D). The glenoid bone loss was calculated using the best-fit circle technique on axial MR images based on the methods reported by Friedman et al9 and Nofsinger et al27 (Fig. 2E).

These 3 measurements were made on the same axial image at the inferior one-third level of the glenoid. (B) The glenoid depth is defined as the distance from the deepest point of the concavity to the diameter line. The glenoid version is measured with line 2 and a line connecting the apex of the subchondral bone anteriorly and posteriorly. Sagittal magnetic resonance imaging shows the measurements of the (D) glenoid index (GI) and (E) glenoid bone loss. (D) The glenoid height (ab) is measured from the superior glenoid tubercle to the inferior glenoid, while the glenoid width (cd) is measured as the widest part of the glenoid, perpendicular to the height axis. The GI is calculated as ab/cd. (E) Glenoid bone loss is measured by the circle method, where the area of bone loss is divided by the area of the full circle to determine the percentage of glenoid bone loss.

Acromial and coracoacromial arch parameters

The critical shoulder angle was measured on true coronal MR images based on the methods reported by Moor et al.25 The scapular axis line was drawn on sagittal MR images from the inferior angle of the scapula through the center of the glenoid with extension superiorly (Fig. 3). The scapular axis line was drawn on sagittal MR images from the inferior angle of the scapula through the center of the glenoid with extension superiorly.3 The posterior acromial tilt, posterior acromial coverage, anterior acromial coverage (AAC), and posterior acromial height were measured on sagittal MR images based on the methods reported by Arner et al3 (Fig. 4, A-D). The shoulder arch angle, anterior coracoid tilt, coracoid height, and scapular Y angle were measured on sagittal MR images based on the methods reported by Lopez et al20 (Fig. 5, A-D).

The distance from this line to the center of the glenoid is then measured in mm. (D) The scapular Y angle is measured in degrees as the angle formed by the columns of bone of the scapular spine and the coracoid.

Bias

To minimize measurement bias, all MRI measurements were conducted by 2 independent orthopedic surgeons (Y.Y. and K.S.) who were blinded to the group allocations. The intraclass correlation coefficients were calculated for rounds 1 and 2 of the primary rater to assess intrarater reliability and for the first round of raters 1 and 2 (rater 1 round 1 vs. rater 2 round 1) to assess interrater reliability. The first round of measurements of each rater was used for analysis.

Study size

Based on a prior power analysis, it was demonstrated that with a significance level of 0.05 (α error, type I error), a sample size of 35 patients in each group would ensure 80% power (1-β) to detect significant differences in each parameter.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Differences in morphological parameters among the 3 groups (UPS, ASI, and control) were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by post hoc tests with Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons. Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with UPS. The goodness-of-fit of the model was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal cutoff value for the most significant factor differentiating UPS from ASI, and the area under the curve was calculated. P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Scapular morphology

The intrarater reliability and the interrater reliability were acceptable for the measurements of all scapular morphology variables, with intraclass correlation coefficients ranging from 0.84 to 0.97 and 0.78 to 0.98, respectively. There were no significant differences among the 3 groups in age, sex, or height. The demographic characteristics of the 3 groups are summarized in Table I. The comparison of the 14 scapular morphological parameters among the 3 groups revealed several significant differences (Table II). The UPS group had a significantly greater glenoid width and glenoid depth than the ASI and control groups (all P < .001). The UPS group had a significantly lower posterior acromial coverage and AAC than the ASI and control groups (all P < .05). Conversely, the posterior acromial height was significantly greater in the UPS group than in the ASI and control groups (P = .003). There were no significant differences between the UPS group and the other 2 groups in the glenoid index, glenoid version, glenoid bone loss, critical shoulder angle, posterior acromial tilt, shoulder arch angle, anterior coracoid tilt, coracoid height, and scapular Y angle.

Predictive factors for unstable painful shoulder

The results of binary logistic regression analysis performed to identify the most significant morphological factor associated with UPS are detailed in Table III. The analysis identified glenoid depth as the sole independent predictor of UPS, with an odds ratio of 9.73 (95% confidence interval, 3.11 to 30.47; P < .001). Furthermore, ROC curve analysis conducted to evaluate the ability of glenoid depth to discriminate between UPS and ASI had an area under the curve of 0.790, indicating good discriminative ability (Fig. 6). A glenoid depth of 1.85 mm was identified as the optimal cutoff value, yielding a sensitivity of 57.9% and a specificity of 93%.

Discussion

The present study is the first to focus on the associations between intra-/extra-articular morphology and UPS. The principal finding of this study was that patients with UPS had a deeper and wider glenoid than in patients with ASI and controls. Additionally, UPS had characterized higher and more AAC. Logistic regression identified glenoid depth as the strongest independent predictor of UPS. A cutoff value of 1.85 mm for glenoid depth distinguished UPS from ASI with an area under the ROC curve of 0.790.

The glenoid depth is known to play a fundamental biomechanical role and provide static stability through the “concavity-compression” mechanism.13,19,26 Previous research has reported a biomechanical correlation between the glenoid depth and shoulder stability.19 Recently, Oenning et al28 showed that high native glenoid concavity requires approximately 1.5 times the force (44.24 N) to achieve a 5 mm anterior translation compared to low native glenoid concavity (28.95 N). Moreover, even with 20% bone loss, high-concavity glenoid had higher absolute stability values (35.71 N) than native low-concavity glenoid without glenoid bone loss (28.95 N). Similarly, another study showed that the stability ratio is approximately 1.8 times greater in the high-concavity glenoid (53.1%) than in the low-concavity glenoid (29.7%).39 Our findings suggested that a deeper glenoid in UPS provided horizontal stability against translational force. Additionally, previous research has also shown that the glenoid width contributes to stability. A biomechanical analysis showed that glenoid bone loss of as little as 2 mm of the width of the anterior margin of the glenoid or an average glenoid bone loss of 7.5% results in a significant decrease in force prior to dislocation.32 Our findings suggested that a wider glenoid in UPS may be more resistant to the creation of a critical bone defect that would lead to recurrent instability. This glenoid morphology in UPS may play a two-fold protective role and may not lead to awareness of dislocation.

The acromial morphology in UPS, featuring greater anterior coverage, also appears to contribute to stability. A previous MRI-based study reported that patients with posterior shoulder instability have an acromion that is significantly higher and has less posterior coverage compared with those with anterior instability and controls.3 Meyer et al23 postulated that this pathophysiology may result from the absence of a lower and more vertical bony acromion backstop. Additionally, morphological variations of the acromion may be associated with a more horizontal orientation of the coracoacromial ligament, which is a structure known to contribute to passive anterior stability.10,18,33 Although no biomechanical studies have reported a direct correlation between the acromion morphology and shoulder stability, the findings of our study and previous studies suggested that an acromion with more anterior coverage and less posterior coverage in UPS may provide a more constrained anterosuperior osseous buttress and coracoacromial ligament and decrease the risk of anterior instability.23

Although UPS and ASI have similar soft tissue lesions on imaging and arthroscopic examination, the reasons for the different clinical presentations remain unclear. We propose that the more stable morphology in UPS augments the concavity-compression mechanism, potentially resulting in a distinct injury pattern. In contrast to the relatively flat glenoid seen in ASI, which is often associated with a single catastrophic anterior dislocation, stable scapula morphology in UPS may limit complete dislocation and lead to recurrent micro-subluxations. This mechanism may concentrate mechanical stress onto the glenoid rim instead of causing obvious dislocation. The glenoid rim is lined with soft tissues, such as the labrum, glenohumeral ligament, and capsule, and soft tissues are generally rich in nociceptive and proprioceptive nerve endings and free sensory nerve fibers.1,11,37 This suggests that the presence of sensory nerve fibers may explain why tears of the glenoid labrum induce pain and play a role in glenohumeral joint proprioception. Therefore, patients with UPS may feel more significant pain than those with ASI because the mechanical stress is concentrated on the glenoid rim, which is populated by soft tissues rich in neural receptors.

These subtle native structural variations characterize distinct phenotypes within the shoulder instability spectrum. While pain-predominant instability is a recognized clinical phenotype, recognition of this characteristic bony morphology helps clinicians to anticipate the presence of occult instability lesions, such as Bankart lesions, even in the absence of a clear dislocation event. This understanding may prompt earlier use of advanced imaging, such as MRI, help reduce diagnostic delay, and avoid misdiagnosis as alternative shoulder pathologies.

The present study has several limitations. First, the retrospective, single-center design may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, although we used a propensity score-matched cohort limited to individuals participating in contact sports, the sample size was small, particularly for the control group. Future prospective, multicenter studies are needed to validate these morphological findings and correlate them directly with specific intra-articular pathologies observed during arthroscopy. Another limitation is that the absolute differences in glenoid depth (1.26 mm) and acromial parameters between the UPS and ASI groups were relatively small and fell within the range of the standard deviations. We acknowledge that a 1 mm variance has limited utility for immediate manual diagnosis or modifying surgical indications in a routine clinical practice. These morphological variations should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as factors explaining distinct symptom expressions within the instability spectrum, rather than as definitive tools for clinical management. Furthermore, biomechanical studies are necessary to investigate how the combination of a deep glenoid and a higher and more anteriorly covering and less posteriorly covering acromion affects capsulolabral strain patterns. Despite these limitations, this study is the first to suggest the key morphological differences between UPS and ASI.

Conclusion

Patients with UPS have a deeper and wider glenoid and a more anteriorly covering and less posteriorly covering acromion on MRI than those with ASI and healthy controls. Glenoid depth (cutoff 1.85 mm) was identified as the strongest independent predictor distinguishing UPS from ASI and was a key anatomical parameter explaining the distinct pathomechanisms within the shoulder instability spectrum. This study is the first to identify distinct scapular morphological features that differentiate UPS from ASI. While pain-predominant instability is a recognized clinical phenotype, understanding this characteristic scapular morphology in UPS may help clinicians to anticipate the presence of occult instability lesions, prevent delays in clinical diagnosis, and avoid misdiagnosis as alternative shoulder pathologies due to the lack of subjective dislocation history.
