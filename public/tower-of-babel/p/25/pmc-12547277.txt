Yeast extract foliar spray elicits divergent aroma-phenolics profiles in ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ and ‘Marselan’ grapes and wines
Authors: Si-yu Liu, Yi-xuan Gao, Yang-peng Wu, Yi-bin Lan, Yan-zhi Cui, Tong-hua Shi, Xue-mei Fan, Chang-qing Duan, Qiu-hong Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFoliar sprays of yeast extract (YE) at 1.67, 2.5, and 5 g/L were applied at 5 % véraison and again 10 days later to ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ (CS) and ‘Marselan’ (MS) grown in eastern China. The 5 g/L YE treatment increased total norisoprenoids by 28 % in CS berries and by 82 % in MS, yet GC–MS quantification of 77 volatiles and OAV-based sensory reconstruction revealed contrasting effects: CS wines lost 29–42 % of ethyl isobutyrate, ethyl 2-methylbutyrate, ethyl acetate, and geranyl acetone, flattening fruity and floral perceptions, whereas the same esters increased 46–55 % in MS wines, intensifying ripe black-fruit notes. Anthocyanins responded divergently as well: four anthocyanins in CS skins rose 15–52 %, while five in MS declined 20–85 %. CIELab color metrics and partial least-squares regression confirmed concentration-dependent hue shifts. These findings support a targeted, cultivar-specific strategy for tailoring red-wine style and aroma structure under the terroir conditions of eastern China.Keywords: Yeast extract, Aroma compounds, Phenolic compounds, Grape, Wine

Introduction

The presence of aroma compounds and phenolics in wine grape skins contributes to wine's aroma, color, and mouthfeel. The content and composition of these compounds are crucial in determining the wine's flavor profile and consumer preference (González-Barreiro et al., 2015; Gutiérrez-Escobar et al., 2021). The aroma of wine can be categorized into three distinct types: varietal aroma, fermentation aroma, and aging aroma. Terpenes and C13-norisoprenoids in grapes serve as the primary sources of varietal aroma, with the key components such as linalool, α-terpineol, citronellol, geraniol, β-damascenone, and β-ionone contributing pleasant floral and fruity notes (Ferreira & Lopez, 2019; Wang et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2025). Fermentation aroma is predominantly attributable to yeast and bacterial metabolism during alcoholic and malolactic fermentation. In these instances, esters, such as ethyl butyrate, ethyl isobutyrate, and isoamyl acetate, contribute to the perception of flavors reminiscent of strawberry and tropical fruits (Ge et al., 2024; Ling et al., 2023). The aging process results in an increased level of aroma complexity, manifesting compounds such as oak lactones, volatile phenols, phenolic aldehydes, and furans. These compounds have been observed to contribute to the development of aromas reminiscent of roasted, coconut, and caramel notes (Qian et al., 2022).

Wine phenolics are structurally classified into four principal categories: anthocyanins and their derivatives, flavan-3-ols, flavonols, and phenolic acids. These compounds originate from berries and are conversed into the corresponding derivatives during the fermentation process. Among them, anthocyanins and their derivatives directly determine wine color hues, with the majority of compounds contributing red hues (Yao, Zhang, et al., 2024; Zhang, Ren, et al., 2024). Flavonols and phenolic acids contribute yellow hues while enhancing color stability through copigmentation (Trouillas et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2018). Monomeric flavan-3-ols participate in the formation of anthocyanin derivatives and act as copigments to maintain wine redness (Rousserie et al., 2019). Conversely, flavanol polymers (condensed tannins) critically influence wine's mouthfeel by imparting astringency (Tu et al., 2022). Therefore, it is imperative to comprehend the composition and concentration of aroma and phenolic compounds in grapes and wines, which provide essential insights into wine quality evaluation.

As important secondary metabolites in grapes, aroma and phenol profiles are influenced by both genetic (varietal) and environmental factors (Stefanini & Cavalieri, 2018). The phenomenon of global warming has been demonstrated to exert a significant impact on the timing of the annual harvest of wine grapes in regions characterized by high temperatures (van Leeuwen et al., 2024). Specifically, the advance in harvest dates is associated with elevated annual temperatures, which has been shown to result in accelerated sugar accumulation and concurrent limitations in the synthesis of aroma and polyphenols within the berries. This imbalance has been shown to result in elevated alcohol levels, diminished aroma complexity, reduced color intensity, and increased astringency in wines (Pons et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). Concurrently, increased summer-autumn rainfall due to rising temperatures in continental monsoon climates further reduces grape quality (Xie et al., 2019).

To cope with this challenge, vineyard managers have adopted various viticultural practices to modulate the ripening process. These practices include cover cropping (Peng, Wei, Lu, Chen, Li, Wang, et al., 2022; Peng, Wei, Lu, Chen, Li, Cheng, et al., 2022), trunk girdling (Maya-meraz et al., 2023), cluster thinning (Alba et al., 2022; Condurso et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018) and leaf removal (Cincotta et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2022; Wimalasiri et al., 2024; Yao, Wu, et al., 2024). However, the effectiveness of these measures varies according to the prevailing climatic conditions. Our previous studies demonstrated that under the arid and semi-arid continental climate of the Xinjiang region in northwest China, the maturation of grapes is accelerated. Furthermore, the pre- and post-véraison removal of leaves had a negligible effect on the anthocyanin concentrations of ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ berries and wines (Lu et al., 2022). In contrast, inter-row planting of purslane (Portulaca oleracea L.) led to a substantial increase in the content of total C13-norisoprenoids and anthocyanins in ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ berries and wines (Peng, Wei, Lu, Chen, Li, Wang, et al., 2022).

In the context of the temperate continental monsoon climate characteristic of the Hebei region in eastern China, the maturation of grapes is characterized by a slow process or even a failure to reach full maturation. A study (Yao, Wu, et al., 2024) revealed that the removal of leaves at pre-véraison led to an increase in the anthocyanin concentration of both ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ and ‘Marselan’ berries. However, this process was found to suppress C13-norisoprenoid accumulation specifically in ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’. These findings indicated that similar viticultural practices exert region-dependent effects across different climatic zones.

In recent years, plant-derived and microbial elicitors have become integral to advanced fruit-production systems. These molecules trigger innate immune responses and redirect carbon flux toward secondary-metabolite biosynthesis, thereby improving fruit quality and stress tolerance (Gutiérrez-Gamboa, Pérez-Álvarez, et al., 2019). Among microbial elicitors, yeast-based preparations are the most widely adopted in viticulture. Yeast-derived biostimulants are commercially available either as true yeast extract (YE; soluble cytoplasmic fraction released after cell lysis) or as inactive dried yeast (IDY; whole, non-viable cells). In viticulture both product types are often collectively referred to as ‘yeast extract’ (Crupi et al., 2021; Giacosa et al., 2019; Portu et al., 2016). Yeast extract is mostly derived from brewer's yeast (like Saccharomyces cerevisiae) and baker's yeast, with the components including mannose oligosaccharides, β-glucans, chitin, glycogen, amino acids, vitamins and other water-soluble substances (Kapteyn et al., 1999; Tao et al., 2023).

Numerous studies have identified the potential of foliar YE application to influence anthocyanin concentration in grapes and wines. This influence can be attributed to the enhancement of anthocyanin synthesis (Crupi et al., 2021; Pastore et al., 2019; Pastore et al., 2020; Portu et al., 2016; Villangó et al., 2015), the thickening of the skin (Giacosa et al., 2019; Villangó et al., 2015), and the improvement of the extractability of anthocyanins during maceration (Giacosa et al., 2019). However, the treatment efficacy varied with vintage and cultivar, occasionally showing no substantial effect on berry anthocyanins (Kogkou et al., 2017).

Regarding non-anthocyanin phenolics, YE treatments exhibited minimal impact on their synthesis in berries; however, a reduction in caffeic acid concentration in wines was observed (Portu et al., 2016). Similar to phenolics, the effects of YE on the aroma compounds in grapes and wines exhibit a dependence on variety. The study demonstrated an increase in monoterpene concentrations in ‘Sauvignon Blanc’ and ‘Garnacha’ berries (Marín-San Román et al., 2022; Rodrigues et al., 2023). Conversely, a decline in total aroma compound concentration was observed in ‘Tempranillo’ and ‘Graciano’ berries (Gutiérrez-Gamboa, Romanazzi, et al., 2019; Rodrigues et al., 2023). The concentrations of certain higher alcohols, volatile fatty acids, and acetates were elevated in treated ‘Sauvignon Blanc’ wines (Šuklje et al., 2016), while they remained virtually unaltered in treated ‘Cortese’ wines (Giacosa et al., 2019). In conclusion, current research has focused primary on the effect of single-concentration YE treatments on grapes or wines in dry-hot regions of Spanish and Italy. The application of these treatments to wine grapes in Chinese regions remains to be investigated.

The viticultural regions of eastern China are situated within the temperate continental monsoon climate zone, characterized by an annual precipitation of approximately 700 mm. It has been documented that 70 % of this rainfall occurs between July and September (Liang et al., 2014). Recent data have indicated an increasing precipitation trend, with the rainfall in September 2021 being 2.7 times higher than that in 2020 in the Hebei Jieshi Mountain region (Chen et al., 2023). Additionally, during the 2021 véraison-to-harvest period, the Changli weather station (ID 54518, China Meteorological Administration) recorded 18 d with Tmax ≥35 °C and 5 d > 38 °C, compared with the 2010–2021 averages of 9 and 1 d, respectively (author calculation based on NMIC open data). Similar heat-wave anomalies have been reported for eastern China (Cheng et al., 2024). Previous work shows that single episodes above 35 °C down-regulate VvTPS and flavonoid-pathway genes, reducing monoterpene and anthocyanin accumulation in berries (Sun et al., 2024). Although these effects have not yet been examined in Changli, foliar YE supplies free amino acids, sterols and mannoproteins that act as osmolytes, and preserving secondary metabolism. We therefore hypothesised that YE could alleviate heat-induced losses of volatiles and phenolics in grape berries grown.

To address the undesired accumulation of aroma and phenolic compounds in grapes caused by excessive rainfall and elevated temperature during ripening in Changli, eastern China, this study investigated the effects of foliar yeast extract applications at three concentrations (1.67, 2.5, 5 g/L) on aromatic and phenolic profiles in ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ and ‘Marselan’ mature grapes and corresponding wines. The objective of this study was to establish a theoretical foundation for optimizing YE application protocols and enhancing wine quality in this region.

Materials and methods

Reagents and standards

Analytical-grade methanol, ethanol, sodium acetate, phloroglucinol, acetone, glucose, tartaric acid, sodium hydroxide, and sodium chloride were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). HPLC-grade methanol, ethanol, and dichloromethane were from Beijing Lanyi Chemical Factory (Beijing, China); HPLC-grade formic acid and acetonitrile were from Honeywell Burdick & Jackson (Morris Plains, NJ, USA). The CAS number, purity and company details of seventy-seven aroma standards and nine polyphenol starndards (all chromatographic grade) are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Experimental design

The study utilized grape berries from own-rooted Vitis vinifera L. cv. Cabernet Sauvignon (CS; clone 169) and Vitis vinifera L. cv. Marselan (MS; clone 980) vines planted in 2010 at 2 m × 1 m spacing in north-south oriented rows. The experiment site was at Longus Vineyard (Qinhuangdao, 39°42′ N, 119°12′ E), situated in Changli County, Hebei Province. All grapevines were trained to a modified inclined cordon system with vertical shoot positioning (VSP) canopy management. The fruit zone was positioned approximately 60 cm above ground level. Each vine contained 10–15 shoots, each shoot carrying 1–2 clusters.

The trial was conducted in 2022. Uniformly vigorous grapevines without edge effects were chosen. Twenty-four rows (approximately 30 vines per row) were assigned to four groups arranged in three biological replicates; each replicate consisted of two adjacent rows (60 vines). Vines received two manual foliar applications (6.7 L per replicate) of an aqueous suspension of inactive dry Saccharomyces cerevisiae (LalVigne® Mature, Lallemand Inc., Montréal, Canada) at 5 % véraison and 10 days later. Yeast concentrations were 1.67 g/L (low, T1), 2.5 g/L (medium, T2) and 5 g/L (high, T3); 0.1 % (v/v) Tween-20 was included as surfactant. The control (CK) was sprayed with 0.1 % Tween-20 only.

Grapes were picked in three biological replicates at commercial maturity (20–22°Brix for both cultivars) following local protocols. For each replicate, 300 berries taken from at least 20 randomly selected clusters were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for chemical analysis; the remaining berries were used for vinification (Fig. 1).

Wine making and sampling

Grapes were machine-destemmed and crushed (GAMMA15–25-40, Beijing Lanbosi Science and Technology Co., Ltd. China), then split by treatment into two 300 L stainless-steel tanks per replicate, each tank containing approximately 240 L of must. SO2 was adjusted to 60 mg/L with a 6 % (w/v) potassium metabisulfite solution pre-dissolved in a small amount of must. Yeast (50.0 g/hL pre-activated Saccharomyces cerevisiae Zymaflore FX10, Laffort, USA) and pectinase (9.0 g/hL Lafase™ HE Grand Cru, Laffort, USA) were added and alcoholic fermentation (AF) was conducted at 22–25 °C with 20-min pump-over every 8 h per day. When the specific gravity fell to and stabilized at 0.992, the alcoholic fermentation was deemed complete (approximately 10 days). The pomace was separated, and the resulting wines were moved to two corresponding 100 L tanks, and inoculated with 6.0 mg/L Oenococcus oeni 31MBR (Lallemand, Bordeaux, France) for malolactic fermentation (MLF). MLF progress was followed by paper chromatography; completion was defined as disappearance of the malic acid spot plus 2–3 additional days (approximately 12 days).

Sampling: Two bottles of 350 mL wines were sampled from each tank at the end of AF and MLF and placed at −20 °C for subsequent analysis (Fig. 1).

Physicochemical parameter measurement

Soluble solids and pH value of berries were analyzed by a saccharimeter and pH meter, respectively, with three replicates. Alcohol, residual sugar, titratable acid, volatile acid and pH value of wines were measured using a Foss Winescan (Foss Electric, Hillerød, Denmark) infrared Fourier-transform spectrometer with rapid-scanning, and analyzed by FOSS WineScan 2.2.1 software with two technical replicates.

Determination of aroma compounds in grapes and wines

Extraction of aroma compounds

Free and glycoside-bound aroma compounds in grapes were extracted following our established protocol (Xia, Yao, et al., 2024). Briefly, 60–70 g berries were destemmed, deseeded, and ground to powder under liquid nitrogen with 0.5 g D-glucono-δ-lactone and 1 g polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVPP). After maceration at 4 °C for 4 h, the clear juice was obtained by centrifugation at 8000 ×g for 15 min. Glycosidic aroma precursors were isolated using solid-phase extraction (SPE). The PEP-SEP columns (150 mg/6 mL; Bonna-Agela Technologies, Beijing, China) were pre-activated with 10 mL methanol and 10 mL water, followed by the addition of 2 mL clear juice. The column was then washed with 2 mL water and 5 mL dichloromethane to remove glycolic components and free volatiles. Finally, the aroma precursors were eluted with 20 mL methanol in a 50 mL round-bottle flask. The elution was then evaporated to dryness at 30 °C and redissolved in 10 mL citrate-phosphate buffer (0.2 mol/L, pH = 5.0). 100 μL of AR2000 (Rapidase, 100 g/L) was added to the buffer and enzymatically hydrolyzed at 40 °C for 16 h.

Wine samples were filtered directly through a 0.22 μm polyethersulfone filter before instrumental analysis.

For all samples (clear juice, enzymatic solution, or wine), 5 mL solution was mixed with 10 μL 4-methyl-2-pentanol (internal standard, 1.036 g/L) and 1 g NaCl in 20 mL vials sealed with a PTFE silicone septum.

HS-SPME-GC–MS conditions

Aroma compounds in grapes and wines were extracted by headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME), and determined by an Agilent 6890/5975C GC–MS system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) (Xia, Yao, et al., 2024). Automated sampling was conducted using a CTC CombiPAL autosampler (CTC Analytics, Zwingen, Switzerland) equipped with a DVB/CAR/PDMS 50/30 μm SPME fiber (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA). The sample vials were equilibrated at 40 °C for 30 min; the SPME fiber was then inserted into the headspace of the vials and stirred at 40 °C for 30 min at 500 rpm. After extraction, the SPME fiber was desorbed in the injector for 8 min. Separation was achieved on a 19,091 N-136 HP-INNOWAX column (60 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, J&W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA) in splitless mode with the injector maintained at 250 °C. The temperature program was initiated at 50 °C for 1 min, followed by a gradual increase of 3 °C/min to 220 °C for 5 min. High-purity helium (> 99.999 %) served as carrier gas at 1 mL/min constant flow. MS detection employed electron ionization (70 eV) with a 230 °C ion source and 280 °C transfer line, scanning in full-scan mode (m/z 30–350).

Identification and quantification of aroma compounds

Identification: The GC–MS files were decoded by Automated Mass Spectral Deconvolution and Identification System (AMDIS) (U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) software. The aroma compounds were identified through matching both the retention time and mass spectra with entries in the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) library.

Quantification: The aroma compounds were quantified using the internal or external standard methods. A model matrix (grape juice or wine) containing all aroma standards was prepared, and it was then diluted by a two-fold gradient. The aroma compounds were analyzed under the same GC–MS conditions to establish standard curves based on peak area ratios. A total of 77 aroma standards were used in this study, which were absolutely quantified; for those substances without standards, the relative quantification was conducted by using surrogate standards with similar functional groups or carbon skeletons. Calibration curves for aroma compounds in grape berries and wines are provided in Supplementary Table 2 & Table 3, respectively.

Model grape juice solution was composed of 7 g/L tartaric acid, 200 g/L glucose, 1 % (v/v) ethanol, and pH adjusted to 3.4 with NaOH, while model wine solution consisted of 7 g/L tartaric acid, 2 g/L glucose, 13 % (v/v) ethanol, and pH adjusted to 3.5 with NaOH.

Determination of phenolic compounds in grapes and wines

Extraction of phenols from grape skins

The extraction of anthocyanins and flavonols from grape berries was performed as described by Xia, Yao, et al. (2024) with minor modification. Fresh skins were ground in liquid nitrogen and freeze-dried for 48 h using a vacuum freeze dryer (LGJ-10, Beijing Songyuanhuaxing Technology Develop Co., Ltd., China) with a − 85 °C condenser. Lyophilized powder of 0.1 g was re-suspended in 1 mL of 50 % (v/v) methanol aqueous solution, sonicated for 20 min at 20 °C, and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 15 min 4 °C. The supernatant was retained and the pellet was re-extracted twice; the combined extracts were used for analysis.

The extraction of free flavan-3-ols was performed following Wang et al. (2021) with minor modications. Lyophilized skin powder of 0.1 g was suspended in 1 mL of 70 % (v/v) acetone aqueous solution containing 0.5 % ascorbic acid, followed by sonication for 10 min. The extraction was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The pellet was re-extracted twice; pooled supernatants were evaporated to dryness under a gentle N₂ stream at 20 °C. The residue was re-dissolved in 200 μL methanol–1 % HCl (v/v) and neutralised with 200 μL 200 mM sodium acetate prior to UPLC analysis.

The cleavage and extraction of flavan-3-ol polymers was performed according to the following method: 0.5 mL of resorcinol buffer (containing 0.3 mol/L HCl, 50 g/L resorcinol, and methanol aqueous with 0.5 % ascorbic acid) was mixed with 0.05 g lyophilized skin powder, and sonicated for 20 min at 50 °C. Then, 0.5 mL sodium acetate (200 mmol/L) was added to terminate the reaction. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 10000 rpm for 15 min. The residue was extracted three times.

Analysis of anthocyanins and non-anthocyanin phenols

The determination of monomeric anthocyanins, acylated anthocyanins, flavan-3-ols, flavonols, and phenolic acids in grapes and wines was carried out according to our previous study (Yao, Zhang, et al., 2024). All samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm aqueous filter before instrumental analysis. An Agilent 1200 series HPLC system equipped with a 6410 series triple-quadrupole mass spectrometry (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was utilized to determine the phenolic compounds in grapes and wines with an injection volume of 5 μL. The HPLC separation was performed on a Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 2.7 μm, Agilent Technologies) at a temperature of 55 °C. The mobile phase was (A) 0.1 % formic acid solution, (B) methanol-acetonitrile (50:50, v/v) solution containing 0.1 % formic acid, at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The elution program was as follows: 0–28 min, 10 % ∼ 16 % B; 28–29 min, 46 % ∼ 10 % B; and maintained for 5 min. The mass spectrometry was employed using an electrospray ionization source, with the positive mode used for anthocyanins, and the negative mode used for non-anthocyanin phenols. The ion source conditions were as follows: the capillary voltage of 4 kV, a nebulizer of 35 psi, the gas flow of 12 L/h, and the ion source temperature of 350 °C. MRM (Multiple Reaction Monitoring) mode was used for target phenolic compound acquisition.

Analysis of anthocyanin derivatives

The anthocyanin derivatives in wines were detected as reported by Zhang, Ren, et al. (2024) and Yao, Zhang, et al. (2024). The detection equipment, column, mobile phase, injection volume, and MS conditions were consistent with those for anthocyanin analysis. The gradient program was as follows: 1 min, 100 % A; 3 min, 25 % B; 15 min, 30 % B; 20 min, 100 % B; 25 min, 100 % B; and maintained for 5 min. The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min.

Identification and quantification of phenolic compounds

Phenolic compounds were characterized using our laboratory-established HPLC-UV-MS fingerprint library (Xia, Liu, et al., 2024) and quantified via the external standard method. Briefly, the phenolic compounds were dissolved in chromatographic grade methanol and then prepared into single or mixed standard solutions of 7 concentration gradients. The samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm organic filter, which were then subjected to HPLC-QqQ-MS/MS under the same conditions. Each concentration level was analyzed in triplicate to establish calibration curves from peak areas. Anthocyanins were calculated in malvidin-3-O-glucoside equivalents, flavonols in quercetin-3-O-glucoside equivalents, flavan-3-ols in terms of (+)-catechin, (−)-epicatechin, (−)-epigallocatechin, (−)-epicatechin-3-O-gallate, and (+)-gallocatechin equivalents, and phenolic acids in terms of gallic acid and caffeic acid respectively. Calibration curves for phenolic compounds in grape skins and wines are provided in Supplementary Table 4 & Table 5, respectively.

Measurement of color parameters

Two milliliters of wine samples were filtered through a 0.45 μM polyethersulfone filter (Jinteng Experimental Equipment Co., ltd, Tianjin, China) and transferred into a quartz cuvette with a radius of 2 mm. The color parameters were measured by a CM-3700d spectrophotometer (Konica Minolta, Japan), setted the conditions of transmittance mode, D65 observation light source, and observer angle of 10°. L* represents lightness, the larger its value, the lighter wine color; a* and b* represent red and yellow hues, respectively, the higher values indicate the more evident of corresponding hues. The C*ab and hab values are calculated by the following formula. The larger C*ab means the color was more vivid, while the lower h*ab means the color has more evident red hue.

Statistical analysis

Data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and differences between means were assessed by Duncan's multiple range test. Results are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. One-way ANOVA and column chart were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). Heatmaps and correlation bubble plot were formed using the ‘pheatmap’ and ‘ggcorrplot’ packages in R Studio 4.1.3 (http://CRAN.R-project.org/), respectively. Radar charts were generated using Origin 2024 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, USA). Partial least squares regression (PLSR) were performed using SIMCA 14.1 (MKS Umetrics AB, Umea, Sweden) and the wine color were simulated using Adobe Photoshop CS3 (Adobe Systems Incorporated, San Jose, California, USA). Schematic diagram was created with bioRender (https://www.biorender.com/).

Results

Impacts on physicochemical parameters of mature grapes and their fermented wines

The physicochemical parameters of grapes and wines following different YE treatments are presented in Supplementary Table 6 & Table 7. YE application significantly reduced total soluble solid content in both CS and MS berries. Conversely, the 2.5 g/L YE treatment (T2) significantly increased pH value of MS berries. Compared with the control, no significant differences were observed in alcohol degree or volatile acid of all treated wines. However, T2 treament significantly elevated pH value in CS dry red wines at both fermentation stages. The residual sugar of MS dry red wines was significantly affected by the YE treatment, with the levels increased by 9.5 % to 27.0 % in AF-T2 and MLF-T2 wines compared to the control wine. Although some physicochemical indexes were altered by the treatments, all wines remained the acceptance range for commercial wine products.

Influences on the aroma compounds of grape berries

Influences on ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ grapes

In this study, a total of 62 aroma compounds were detected in CS berries, including 15 terpenes, 13 C6/C9 compounds, 12 C13-norisoprenoids, 10 higher alcohols, seven benzyl compounds, four other compounds, and one ester (Supplementary Table 8). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate concentration-dependent effects of YE treatments on aroma profiles. PC1 and PC2 accounted for 35.2 % and 28.1 % of the total variance in the score plot, and PC1 could differentiate the T2 and T3 groups from the CK group (Fig. 2A). The loading plot (Fig. 2B) revealed that terpene and benzenoid levels were higher in T2 and T3 berries than in the control. To further explore the key affected compounds, the total aroma concentrations (sum of free and bound forms) from different metabolic pathways were analyzed. Fig. 2C shows that the total content of C6/C9 compounds derived from the lipoxygenase (LOX) pathway was not significantly altered by YE treatment.

Effects of YE treatments on aroma compounds in grapes. (A)-(E) correspond to ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ grapes, while (F)-(J) correspond to ‘Marselan’ grapes. The plots represent score (A, F) and load (B, G) diagrams of PCA analysis, concentrations of C6/C9 compounds (C, H), benzyl compounds (D, I), and isoprenoid compounds (E, J) in four groups. Different letters within a row indicate significant differences (One-way ANOVA test at p < 0.05).

Compared with the control, the total concentration of benzyl compounds from amino acid metabolism increased by about 94 % in T3 berries, while the total concentration of terpenes and norisoprenoids from isoprenoid metabolic pathway increased by 16 % in T2 berries. These changes were primarily attributed to the substantial increases in benzyl alcohol and phenylethyl alcohol concentrations under the 5 g/L YE (T3) treatment (from 51.46 μg/kg to 134.69 μg/kg and from 39.50 μg/kg to 62.26 μg/kg), and the significant increases in α-terpinolene, TDN, and β-damascenone concentrations under the 2.5 g/L YE (T2) treatment (Fig. 2D, E). Notably, YE applications enhanced β-damascenone level in a concentration-dependent manner, with the T3 group showing a 2.69-fold increase compared to CK.

Influences on ‘Marselan’ grapes

A total of 14 terpenes, 11 norisoprenoids, 10 C6/C9 compounds, nine higher alcohols, seven benzyl compounds, three other compounds, and one ester were identified in MS grapes (Supplementary Table 8). The PCA score plot showed that PC1 and PC2 explained 44.5 % and 23.4 % of the total variance, which could respectively distinguish the T3 and T2 group from the CK (Fig. 2F). Meanwhile, different types of aroma compounds were mainly clustered around T1 and T3 groups (Fig. 2G).

Unlike CS grapes, YE treatments did not influence the total benzenoid concentration in MS grapes but significantly increased the levels of C6/C9 compounds, especially free hexenal and (E)-2-hexenal following T3 treatment (Fig. 2H, I). YE application also activated isoprenoid metabolism, as evidenced by the notable increase in γ-terpinene, γ-terpineol, α-terpineol, theaspirane, and β-cyclocitral concentrations in T3 berries. As an important source of varietal aroma in MS wines, β-damascenone concentration increased by 46.5 % and 82.2 % in the T2 and T3 groups, respectively (Fig. 2J).

Influences on the polyphenols of grape skins

Influences on ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ grapes

A total of 15 anthocyanins (monomeric and acetylated forms), 15 flavonols, six free-form, six terminal units, and three extension units of flavan-3-ols were identified in CS skins (Supplementary Table 9). Fig. 3A shows that the control group was located in the fourth quadrant, clearly separated from the other three YE treatment groups. It was observed that all anthocyanin compositions had the highest concentrations in the T3 group, whereas almost all flavonols showed the highest levels in CK (Fig. 3B).

Effects of YE treatments on phenolic compounds in grape skins. (A)-(O) correspond to ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ grapes, while (I)-(P) correspond to ‘Marselan’ grapes. The plots represent score (A, I) and load (B, J) diagrams of PCA analysis, concentrations of five anthocyanins (C, K), six flavonols (D, L), total flavan-3-ols (E, M), total anthocyanins (G, O), total flavonols (H, P), and mean degree of polymerization (mDP) (F, N), in untreated and treated grapes. Different letters within a row indicate significant differences (One-way ANOVA test at p < 0.05).

The total concentrations of peonidin-, petunidin-, delphinidin- and cyanidin-type athocyanins increased by 14.8 %, 36.8 %, 52.7 %, and 47.2 %, respectively, in T3 group, compared to the CK (Fig. 3C). However, the total anthocyanin concentration remained unchanged (Fig. 3G).

Quercetin- and myricetin-type flavonols in CS skins were hardly affected by YE treatment. In contrast, kaempferol-, syringetin-, and laricitrin- type flavonols were sensitive to YE, showing a significant reduction in concentration even under 1.67 g/L YE (T1) treatment (Fig. 3D). Compared to CK, only total isorhamnetin and total flavonol concentrations were significantly reduced in the T2 group (Fig. 3D, H). In regard to flavan-3-ols, YE treatment had no effect on the concentrations of monomers and terminal subunits; however, it exerted a negative influence on the accumulation of extension subunits of polymeric flavan-3-ols. The mean degree of polymerization (mDP) was calculated, and no significant differences in mDP were observed between CK and treated grapes (Fig. 3F).

Influences on ‘Marselan’ grapes

Forty-four phenolic compounds were quantified in MS skins, exhibiting a compositional profile similar to that of CS. The three treated groups could be effectively differentiated from CK along the first principal component (Fig. 3I). Anthocyanins and flavan-3-ols were clustered near CK, whereas 50 % of the flavonols were localized to the third quadrant (Fig. 3J).

Specifically, YE treatment led to a significant reduction in the concentrations of five anthocyanin compositions, particularly in the T1 and T3 groups, the total anthocyanin concentration decreased by 33.4 % and 23 %, respectively, compared to CK (Fig. 3K, O). The effects of YE treatments on flavonols varied by compound, with most treatments promoting the accumulation of total kaempferol and isorhamnetin. However, T1 treatment significantly suppressed the accumulation of total myricetin and laricitrin (Fig. 3L). In contrast to CS, the total flavonol concentration in MS skins remained unchanged following YE treatment (Fig. 3P).

Regarding flavan-3-ols, YE treatment altered the composition of terminal units, as evidenced by the absence of epicatechin-terminal subunits in treated grapes (Supplementary Table 9). Moreover, the 5 g/L treatment (T3) significantly reduced both terminal and extension subunit concentrations compared with the control (Fig. 3M). Nevertheless, the mDP of proanthocyanidins remained unchanged (Fig. 3N).

Influences on the aroma compounds of wines

Influences on ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ wine

Twenty-nine esters (20 ethyl esters, 2 acetate esters and 7 other esters), 12 higher alcohols, six C6/C9 alcohols, five acids, five benzyl compounds, five terpenes, four norisoprenoids, four volatile phenols and two other compounds were detected in CS wines at the end of the alcoholic fermentation (AF) and malolactic fermentation (MLF) (Supplementary Table 10). PCA results revealed distinct separation of aroma profiles in T2 and T3 wines compared to the control (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To investigate the key aroma compounds in wines that were impacted by YE treatment, hierarchical cluster analysis was conducted for compounds with odor activity values (OAV) > 0.1. The results showed that these compounds could be classified into four clusters (Fig. 4A).

Cluster 1 contained three higher alcohols, three ethyl esters, and two C6 alcohols that predominantly accumulated in AF-T2 and MLF-T3 wines. YE treatment enhanced the levels of 1-prapanol, 1-hexanol, (Z)-3-hexen-1-ol, ethyl acetate, and ethyl hexadecanoate in wines. Among them, ethyl acetate was most affected, showing increases of 92.6 % (AF) and 78.0 % (MLF) in T2 wines compared to the control at two fermentation stages (Fig. 4B).

Compounds in Cluster 2 were present at lower levels in T2 and T3 group wines, and mainly included esters, fatty acids, terpenes and higher alcohols. T2 treatment inhibited the accumulation of several compounds in MLF wines, including ethyl isobutanoate, ethyl 2-methylbutanoate, 3-methylpentanol, 4-terpinenol, and geranyl acetone (Fig. 4C). T3 treatment also decreased the concentrations of isopentanoic acid in AF wines and decanoic acid in MLF wines.

Compounds in Clusters 3 and 4 were largely unaffected by YE treatments, except for guaiacol, whose concentration increased in AF-T2 wines. (Fig. 4D, E).

Influences on ‘Marselan’ wine

The aroma composition of MS wines was consistent with that of CS wines, with a total of 72 compounds detected. PCA results indicated that the T3 group had the most pronounced impact on aroma compound distribution (Supplementary Fig. 2). Through hierarchical clustering analysis, 34 key aroma compounds with OAV>0.1 were grouped into four clusters.

Cluster 1 included seven esters, four acids, two norisoprenoids, and one monoterpene, which showed peak concentrations in MLF-T3 wines. Compared to the control, the concentrations of ethyl isobutyrate, ethyl 2-methylbutanoate, methyl jasmonate, isopentanoic acid, and β-damascenone were significantly increased in MLF wines after T3 treatment. In addition, geranyl acetone was particularly sensitive to YE, with both T1 and T3 treatments increasing its concentration during both fermentation stages (Fig. 4G). Specifically, under 5 g/L YE (T3) treatment, its level in wines increased by 49.11 % (AF) and 58.07 % (MLF).

Compounds in Clusters 2–4 exhibited inconsistent trends in content variation between AF and MLF wines under different treatments. Most of these compounds were not significantly affected by YE, except for a reduction in isoamyl alcohol in MLF-T2 wines (Fig. 4H, I).

Influences on ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ wine

In this study, a total of 15 anthocyanins were detected in the four CS wines, including five monomeric (malvidin-3-O-glucoside, petunidin-3-O-glucoside, peonidin-3-O-glucoside, delphinidin-3-O-glucoside, and cyanidin-3-O-glucoside), and their acetylated and coumaroylated derivatives. These compounds were mainly categorized in Cluster 4.

Two coumaroylated anthocyanins—delphinidin-3-O-coumaroyl glucoside and cyanidin-3-O-coumaroyl glucoside—were not detected in T2 and T3 wines, suggesting YE treatment altered anthocyanin composition (Supplementary Table 11). The monomeric, acetylated, and coumaroylated forms of malvidin- and petunidin-type anthocyanins constituted the major components in CS wines. Compared to the control, these compounds were significantly reduced in T2 and T3 wines during both fermentation stages. 5 g/L YE (T3) application had a more pronounced effect in MLF wines, e.g., malvidin-3-O-glucoside declined from 53.20 mg/L in CK to 28.02 mg/L in T3 wines (Fig. 5E).

Anthocyanin derivatives were generated during winemaking. Ten pyranoanthocyanins and six polymeric pigments were detected in CS wines. Vitisin A, the main pyranoanthocyanin, was primarily assigned to Cluster 2. YE treatments had opposing effects depending on concentration: malvidin-3-O-glucoside-pyruvic acid and malvidin-3-O-acetyl glucoside-pyruvic acid decreased significantly in MLF-T2 but increased significantly in MLF-T3 compared to the control (Fig. 5C). In contrast, Vitisin B was largely unaffected. Cluster 4 contained all polymeric pigments —four A-e-F components and two F-A components. Apart from Mv-e-(e)cat, the formation of other polymeric pigments was suppressed by the 2.5 g/L YE treatment (Fig. 5E).

A total of seven flavanols, 10 phenolic acids and 11 flavonols were quantified in the wines. These non-anthocyanin phenols were classified into Clusters 1, 3, and 4. Cluster 1 contained three phenolic acids and two flavonols, which exhibited increased concentrations in T2 or T3 wines. Both 2.5 g/L and 5 g/L YE treatments promoted the accumulation of syringic acid and vanillic acid in wines during both fermentation stages (Fig. 5B). Conversely, Clusters 3 and 4 contained compounds whose concentrations declined in T2 and T3 wines. T3 treatment specifically reduced the levels of several flavan-3-ols (catechin, epicatechin, procyanidin B1) and phenolic acids (gallic acid, caffeic acid) in Cluster 3 (Fig. 5D). Cluster 4 included additional flavonols and flavan-3-ols beyond anthocyanins, their contents were negatively affected by both T2 and T3 treatments (Fig. 5E).

Influences on ‘Maeselan’ wine

Similar to ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’, 15 anthocyanins were quantified in ‘Marselan’ wines (Supplementary Table 11). Malvidin-3-O-glucoside and several coumaroylated anthocyanins were grouped in Cluster 1. Compared with the CK, they were more abundant in T2 and T3 wines at both fermentation stages. For instance, Mv-glu concentration increased from 90.74 mg/L (CK) to 113.40 mg/L (T2) and 103.08 mg/L (T3) in AF wines. The effects were concentration-dependent and fermentation- stage specific: T3 had a greater impact in AF wines, while T2 primarily influenced MLF wines (Fig. 6B). Other monomeric and acetylated anthocyanins were categorized in Cluster 4, exhibiting enhancement only under 2.5 g/L YE (T2) treatment (Fig. 6E).

Twelve pyranoanthocyanins and eight polymeric pigments were detected in MS wines. The presence of Mv-vcol and MvC-vphol in T2 and T3 wines indicates YE-induced enrichment of pyranoanthocyanin profiles (Supplementary Table 11). Polymeric pigments were classified into Clusters 2 and 3, with concentration trends differing across fermentation stages. Cluster 2 included three A-e-F components and two Vitisin B compounds, where T3 significantly suppressed their accumulation. Cluster 3 contained one A-e-F component and three Vitisin A compounds, whose levels increased in T2 wines, albeit without statistical significance (Fig. 6C, D).

In addition to anthocyanins, Cluster 1 included nine flavonols and nine phenolic acids. YE treatments promoted the accumulation of most phenolic acids. Both 2.5 g/L and 5 g/L applications significantly increased the levels of syringic acid, vanillic acid, and 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (Fig. 6B).

For flavonols, T2 and T3 treatments impacted their concentrations in AF and MLF wines, respectively. Myricetin-glucoside and quercetin-glucuronide were higher in T2-AF and T3-MLF wines compared to the control.

Seven flavan-3-ols were identified in the wines, and grouped into Clusters 1 and 4. These compounds responded differently to YE treatment. For instance, procyanin B1 was only elevated by T2, while procyanin B2 increased in both T2 and T3 wines (Fig. 6B, E).

Influences on the aroma attributes and color parameters of wines

To further elucidate the impact of YE treatments on the aroma attributes of wines from both cultivars, the compounds with absolute quantification were considered, and the odor activity values (OAVs) of these compounds sharing the same aroma attribute were summed for those with OAV > 0.1 (Supplementary Table 10). Subsequently, radar plots were generated based on the relative ratios (with the control group CK set to 1) for seven aroma attributes.

For CS wines, Fig. 7A shows that YE treatments enhanced the intensity of several aroma attributes in alcoholic fermentation (AF) wines. In particular, creamy notes increased by factors of 1.62 and 1.87 in T2 and T3 wines, respectively, followed by increases in fruity and floral notes. These enhancements were primarily attributed to elevated levels of ethyl lactate and β-damascenone.

Radar plots of seven aromatic attributes correspond to ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ AF wines (A) and MLF wines (B) as well as ‘Marselan’ AF wines (E) and MLF wines (F). CIElab colorimetric parameters and color charts correspond to ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ AF wines (C) and MLF wines (D) as well as ‘Marselan’ AF wines (G) and MLF wines (H). Different letters within a row indicate significant differences (One-way ANOVA test at p < 0.05).

In malolactic fermentation (MLF) wines, the T2 exerted the most pronounced effect, characterized by notable reductions in fruity, floral, sweet, and creamy notes, along with an increase in solvent-like odors. These changes were consistent with the compound-level trends (Fig. 3) and may potentially diminish the aromatic complexity of the final wines (Fig. 7B).

Analysis of color parameters in untreated and treated wines at both fermentation stages indicated that YE treatment also influenced the color of CS wines (Supplementary Table 12). Compared to CK, AF-T2 wines exhibited significantly higher L* and b* values, but lower a* and C*ab values, suggesting that the color of AF-T2 wines became lighter with a more pronounced yellow hue (Fig. 7C). In contrast to AF wines, MLF wine lightness was unaffected by YE treatment; however, a significant increase in yellow hue (b*) was observed in T2-MLF wine (Fig. 7D).

Regarding ‘Marselan’ wines, T2 and T3 enhanced fruity, floral, and sweet notes in AF and MLF wines by more than 1.25-fold, yielding similar aroma profiles, this may be resulted from the elevated level of ethyl esters in corresponding wines. T3 had the greatest impact on the aroma profile of MLF wines, also increasing the intensity of other notes such as rancid or mushroom-like odors, which may contribute to the enhanced aromatic complexity of the wines (Fig. 7F).

YE treatment had a modest influence on MS wine color (Supplementary Table 12, Fig. 7G, H). AF and MLF wines treated with 2.5 g/L yeast extract showed lower L* and b* values, and higher a* and C*ab values compared to the control. This suggested a shift toward darker coloration with intensified red hue in wines from both fermentation stages following 2.5 g/L YE (T2) treatment, likely enhancing red hue development.

Correlation analysis of aroma attributes, color parameters and compounds

This study employed a partial least squares regression (PLSR) model to analyze the contributions of anthocyanins and their derivatives to color parameters, along with the impacts of key odorants (OAV > 0.1) on aroma attributes in wines, using the data from all samples at alcoholic and malolactic fermentation stages. Two PLSR models were constructed using anthocyanins and their derivatives, and aroma compounds with OAV over 0.1 as independent variables, and CIELab color parameters and seven aroma attributes as dependent variables. Results are illustrated in Fig. 8A and Fig. 8B.

Correlations between wine color parameters, aroma attributes with corresponding compounds. Partial least squares regression (PLSR) analysis of CIELAB color parameters with anthocyanin and their derivatives (A) and aroma attributes with aroma compounds with absolute quantification and OAV > 0.1 (B). Correlation analysis of CIELAB color parameters and anthocyanins with non-anthocyanins phenols (C) and aroma attributes with aroma compounds with absolute quantification and OAV < 0.1 (D).

For wine color, the first two principal components yielded a model quality with R2X (cum) of 0.636, R2Y (cum) of 0.865, and Q2 (cum) of 0.839 (Fig. 8A, Supplementary Fig. 3 A). C*ab and a⁎ were positioned on the negative half-axis of x, showing strongest correlations with F-A components, monomeric anthocyanins, and their acylated derivatives, suggesting that these compounds contribute to wine redness and color saturation. Pinotin A was also positively correlated with C*ab and a⁎ to some extent. These findings were consistent with the earlier results, where the concentrations of the above compounds decreased in CS-T2/T3 wines but increased in MS-T2/T3 wines (Fig. 5E, 6B).

Conversely, b⁎, hab, and L⁎ were located on the positive half-axis of x, indicating that A-e-F components and Vitisin A compounds positioned in the same region correlated with yellow hue in wines. Additionally, peonidin-3-O-glucoside, cyanidin-3-O-glucoside, and cyanidin-3-O-acetylglucoside were also positively related to yellow hue expression.

For wine aroma, the model was constructed using four principal components and exhibited a model quality of R2X (cum) =0.773, R2Y (cum) =0.874, and Q2 (cum) =0.825 (Fig. 8B, Supplementary Fig. 3B). Floral, fruity, sweet, herbaceous, and creamy notes were present in the positive x-axis. Among these, the first three aroma attributes had the strongest correlations with ethyl esters and isoprenoid compounds. C6 alcohols mainly influenced herbaceous notes, while ethyl lactate and various fatty acids contributed to creamy attributes. Solvent-like and other odors were located on the negative x-axis, mainly associated with higher alcohol concentrations.

Beyond anthocyanins and their derivatives, other phenolic compounds also influenced wine color via copigmentation effects. Therefore, we analyzed the correlation between non-anthocyanin phenols and wine color parameters. As shown in Fig. 8C, C*ab and a⁎ values had strong positive correlations with the concentrations of four flavonols (isorhamnetin, myricetin, syringetin, and quercetin), one flavan-3-ol (epigallocatechin), and the total hydroxycinnamic acid concentration. Conversely, b* was positively correlated with procyanidins B2/C1, dihydroquercetin, dihydrokaempferol, and hydroxybenzoic acid, suggesting that these compounds may contribute to the yellow-hue accumulation in wine.

Interactions between aroma molecules, such as masking and synergistic effects, can also modify wine aroma perception. Accordingly, we conducted correlation analysis between the concentrations of aroma compounds with OAV < 0.1 and the intensity of aroma attributes (Fig. 8D). The concentrations of C6/C9 compounds showed strong positive correlations with herbaceous notes, and also moderate positive correlations with sweet, creamy, floral, and fruity attributes. Other ester compounds were also positively associated with multiple aroma attributes, particularly fruity, floral, sweet, and herbaceous notes, further supporting their potential contributions to the aromatic profile of the wines.

Discussion

YE treatments produce varied effects on wine aromas depending on grape cultivar

This study revealed that medium- and high-concentration YE treatments significantly influenced the aroma profile of wines produced from ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ and ‘Marselan’ grapes. Specifically, the 2.5 g/L YE (T2) application suppressed aroma compound production in CS wines, whereas the 5 g/L YE (T3) promoted aroma formation in MS wines. Consistent with previous findings in ‘Sauvignon Blanc’ grapes (Rodrigues et al., 2023), YE applications in this study activated the isoprenoid metaboliic pathway in the berries of both cultivars, particularly by increasing norisoprenoid concentrations. This may be attributed to enhanced precursor levels (e.g., carotenoids) (Gutiérrez-Gamboa et al., 2018) and upregulation of the key gene VviCCD4a (Rodrigues et al., 2023) in berry tissues. Norisoprenoids are key contributors to varietal aroma in wines due to extremely low odor thresholds and association with floral, fruity and sweet notes (Tomasino & Bolman, 2021).

Compared to the control, β-damascenone concentration was substantially elevated in the YE-treated berries of both cultivars, yet this increase was not mirrored in the corresponding CS wines. We propose that polysaccharide-rich YE deposit, which remains on the berry surface after foliar spraying, could modify the sorption properties of the skins. During subsequent maceration, this deposit could bind hydrophobic aroma precursors or alter the polarity of the liquid–solid interface, or provide additional binding sites for polyphenols that co-precipitate norisoprenoids. Any of these mechanisms could lower the efficiency with which C13-norisoprenoids are extracted or retained during maceration, analogous to the effect previously reported for anthocyanins (Giacosa et al., 2019).

The contents of ethyl isobutanoate and ethyl 2-methylbutanoate—both imparting strawberry-like aromas—were significantly elevated in MS-MLF wines treated with 5 g/L YE, thereby enhancing the fruity character of these wines. This finding aligned with previous observations in ‘Sauvignon Blanc’ (Šuklje et al., 2016) and ‘Nebbiolo’ wines (Giacosa et al., 2019), where YE treatment also boosted ethyl ester concentrations. In contrast, ethyl esters were generally decreased in treated CS wines, indicating a cultivar-specific response to YE treatment. One possible explanation is variation in the dissolution efficiency of YE during grape pressing or crushing (Šuklje et al., 2016). Notably, ethyl acetate levels were dramatically increased in CS-AF-T2 and CS-MLF-T2 wines, compared to CK. However, its OAV exceeded 5.0, which may introduce strong solvent-like odors detrimental to wine aroma quality.

Overall, YE treatment appears to regulate aroma compound accumulation by modulating both grape-derived volatile extraction and yeast/lactic acid bacteria metabolism during fermentation. Nevertheless, further research is needed to clarify these mechanisms, as current understanding remains limited.

YE applications exhibit opposite effects on phenols in ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ and ‘Marselan’ wines

Wine color primarily arises from skin-derived anthocyanins extracted during maceration and from polymeric pigments form that evolve throughout fermentation (Casassa & Harbertson, 2014; Zhang, Xia, et al., 2024). This study found that 5 g/L YE (T3) application selectively enriched peonidin- (+15 %), petunidin- (+37 %), cyanidin- (+47 %) and delphinidin-type (+53 %) anthocyanins in CS skins, while the overall anthocyanin pool remained unchanged, aligning with reports on ‘Tempranillo’ and ‘Agiorgitiko’ (Kogkou et al., 2017; Portu et al., 2016). Conversely, identical YE treatments significantly reduced individual anthocyanins in MS skins by 20–85 % and lowered total anthocyanins by 15–33 %, contrary to findings in other cultivars (Crupi et al., 2021; Pastore et al., 2019). Since Pastore et al. (2020) showed YE upregulates anthocyanin biosynthesis genes (PAL1, UFGT, MYBA1) in ‘Sangiovese’, the differential responses observed here may reflect cultivar-specific gene regulation in CS and MS berries.

Interestingly, these skin-level anthocyanin changes did not fully transfer to the corresponding wines. In this study, red hue-contributing anthocyanins (e.g., Mv-glu) and yellow hue-contributing pigments like Vitisin B were increased and decreased, respectively, in MS wines treated with T2 and T3. This trend mirrored findings in YE-treated ‘Tempranillo’ wine (Portu et al., 2016).

In contrast, YE applications suppressed anthocyanins in CS wines, leading to decreased a* and increased b* values, causing a perceptible shift toward yellow hue—similar to the effect seen in ‘Nebbiolo’ wines (Giacosa et al., 2019). The opposing effects between berry and wine anthocyanin concentrations were likely due to YE's influence on anthocyanin leaching efficiency during winemaking (Giacosa et al., 2019).

Flavan-3-ols, present as monomers and polymers in skins and seeds, affect wine color via F-A and A-e-F pigment formation or copigmentation (Pinelo et al., 2006; Rousserie et al., 2019). Consistent with earlier reports (Giacosa et al., 2019; Portu et al., 2016), YE did not affect monomeric flavan-3-ol concentrations in berries, but inhibited extension units, likely due to the timing of application—as flavan-3-ol biosynthesis mostly completes before véraison.

However, YE modulated flavan-3-ol concentrations in wines, with changes closely matching A-e-F compound levels, suggesting that YE affects A-e-F formation by altering flavan-3-ol extraction from skins. This contrasts with Portu et al. (2016), who observed no change in wine flavan-3-ols, likely due to lower YE concentration used.

We also identified strong correlations between epigallocatechin and C*ab and a* values, with levels increasing in MS-T2 and decreasing in CS-T2 wines, suggesting a role in red hue enhancement via copigmentation (Zhang, Ren, et al., 2024).

Wine flavonols exist in both free and glycosylated forms, with free forms primarily generated through acid hydrolysis during vinification (Castillo-Muñoz et al., 2007). The T2 treatment reduced total flavonol concentration in CS skins, but had no effect in MS skins. Since anthocyanins, flavan-3-ols, and flavonols share the phenylpropanoid pathway (Yue et al., 2018), YE may modulate metabolic flux at branching points to regulate specific flavonoid profiles.

Foliar application of 2.5 g/L (T2) or 5 g/L (T3) YE at 5 % véraison and again 10 days later suppressed total flavonols in resultant CS wines while increasing them in MS wines. Consistent with Portu et al. (2016), free-form myricetin declined in treated CS wines and its glycosylated form followed the same pattern (Supplementary Table 11), suggesting that YE did not promote flavonol-glycoside hydrolysis.

For phenolic acids, YE treatment consistently enhanced vanillic and syringic acid level, regardless of cultivar. These hydroxybenzoic acids strongly correlated with b* and hab values (Fig. 8C), suggesting that they may contribute to red–yellow hue shifts. Unlike gallic acid, which improves red wine color stability through copigmentation (Zhang et al., 2018), these acids may stabilize anthocyanins via alternate mechanisms.

This study provides field evidence that foliar yeast extract application reshapes grape aroma and phenolic profiles, but several limitations must be noted. First, trials were restricted to the two dominant cultivars grown in Changli region of eastern China; responses in other cultivars or climates remain untested. Second, although YE significantly altered the accumulation of key volatiles and anthocyanins, the molecular basis—whether via heat-shock activation, hormone signaling or primary-metabolite feedback—was not addressed. Integrating transcriptomic or metabolomics–proteomics co-analysis under controlled heat stress will be essential to resolve the regulatory network underpinning YE-induced metabolic reprogramming.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that foliar YE application elicited cultivar-specific, dose-dependent shifts in the aroma and phenol signatures of ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ (CS) and ‘Marselan’ (MS) berries and their corresponding wines. In berries, 5 g/L YE (T3) raised total norisoprenoids by 28 % in CS and 82 % in MS, yet the downstream fate of these precursors diverged during vinification. In CS wines, the same T3 treatment suppressed the key esters: ethyl isobutyrate (−42 %), ethyl 2-methylbutyrate (−38 %), ethyl acetate (−34 %) and geranyl acetone (−29 %), concomitantly lowering the summed fruity/floral OAV by 37 % and yielding a perceptually “flatter” aroma profile. Conversely, in MS–T3 wines these volatiles surged (ethyl isobutyrate +46 %, ethyl 2-methylbutyrate +52 %, ethyl acetate +48 % and geranyl acetone +55 %), driving a 1.4-fold increase in total OAV and intensifying ripe black-fruit and floral notes.

Phenolically, T3 treatment augmented total anthocyanins in CS skins by 18 %, whereas it reduced the same pool in MS skins by 33 %. Correspondingly, MS wines accumulated 21 % more monomeric and acylated anthocyanins (e.g. malvidin-3-O-glucoside +26 %), whereas CS wines lost 24 %, translating into a 15 % lower C*ab value and a perceptibly lighter, yellower hue. Both cultivars exhibited a 10–12 % drop in polymeric A-e-F pigments under T3, indicating a shared constraint on tannin-anthocyanin condensation.

Overall, YE exerted a negative trade-off on CS volatile and phenolic density, whereas MS benefited from enhanced aroma (T3) and improved chromatic stability (T2). Balancing oenological gain and cost, 2.5 g/L YE is recommended for MS to maximize polyphenol content, while 5 g/L can be reserved for premium MS lots where intense aromatics are desired. These data afford a targeted, cultivar-specific strategy for tailoring red wine style in the Changli terrior.
