Prophylactic biological mesh reinforcement during ileostomy closure surgery evaluated by the image-based deep learning model for the prevention of stoma-site incisional hernia: phase II study protocol for a single-centre, prospective, randomised controlled clinical trial
Authors: Zihang Liu, Zhongyi Dong, Yeqian Zhang, Yujing Guan, Long Bai, Jiayi Gu, Bo Ni, Hao-yu Zhang, Muerzhate Aimaiti, Shuchang Wang, Ben Yue, Xiang Xia, Zizhen Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProphylactic ileostomy plays a critical role in the radical resection of low rectal cancer, but the incidence of stoma site incisional hernia (SSIH) after stoma closure remains high. No study has been reported in which radiomics has been used to predict SSIH. The primary aim of this study is to evaluate the safety and efficacy of biological mesh in preventing incisional hernia in patients with high-risk incisional hernia factors, as identified by image-based deep learning model, undergoing ileostomy closure surgery.Methods and analysis40 patients who need to undergo ileostomy closure and have been identified with high risk factors for SSIH by image-based deep learning model will be selected for this study. Patients will be randomly assigned equally to the prophylactic biological mesh placement group and the control group, and outcomes will be tracked via clinic review at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months and 12 months postoperatively. The outcome measures are the rate of postoperative incisional hernia, local pain, incisional infection, seroma and so on. This study demonstrates that prophylactic placement of biological mesh with ileostomy closure reduces the incidence of SSIH. Furthermore, it validates the feasibility of image-based deep learning models in predicting postoperative complications and identifying high-risk SSIH patients.Ethics and disseminationInformed consent has been obtained from all subjects. This protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (KY2022-087-B). The findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed manuscripts, reports and presentations.Trial registration numberChiCTR2200064995. Registration date: October 2022. Introduction

The latest data on the global cancer burden in 2020, published by the WHO’s International Agency for Research on Cancer, shows that more than 1.93 million new cases of colorectal cancer will be diagnosed worldwide, accounting for 9.7% of all new cancers diagnosed worldwide and more than 940 000 deaths.1 In recent years, the incidence and mortality rates of colorectal cancer in China have been increasing, and the cancer incidence and mortality in China (2022) showed that the incidence and mortality rates of colorectal cancer in China ranked second and fourth among all malignant tumours.2

According to the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology: clinical guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of colorectal cancer (2024 edition),3 locally advanced low rectal cancer is usually treated with low anterior resection with total mesorectal excision with ileostomy. In contrast, proximal colonic tumours may undergo segmental colectomy (right/left hemicolectomy) with stoma creation reserved for high-risk cases, such as obstructing lesions or compromised anastomotic integrity. Stoma site incisional hernia (SSIH) is one of the common long-term complications after prophylactic ileostomy closure surgery and one of the main reasons for patients to undergo secondary surgery, which not only brings patients the pain of secondary surgery, but also has a certain recurrence rate after repair. However, domestic clinicians do not pay attention to the high incidence of postoperative SSIH in ileostomy closure surgery and fail to take effective precautions against the complications of prophylactic ileostomy closure surgery. Also, according to the guidelines for diagnosis and treatment of abdominal wall incision hernia (2024 edition),4 an incisional hernia is an abdominal external hernia formed under intra-abdominal pressure due to the failure of the original surgical incision of the abdominal wall to heal completely in the fascia and/or muscularis propria, and the hernia sac may have intact or incomplete peritoneal epithelium, which is usually seen in the anterior abdominal wall incision.

With the progress of laparoscopic technology, the surgical treatment of rectal cancer, especially low rectal cancer, has made great progress. Ileostomy has important application value in laparoscopic radical surgery for low rectal cancer, which can effectively reduce perioperative stress and trauma, lower the incidence of postoperative anastomotic leakage, accelerate the recovery speed of patients and promote the early recovery of anal function. Prophylactic ileostomy closure surgery is usually performed within 3–6 months after primary surgery. Due to the special characteristics of the surgical site and incision, the incidence of incisional hernia and incisional infection at the stoma site remains high.4 SSIH occurs at the original stoma site after ileostomy closure surgery, with an incidence rate as high as 30–35%, which is much higher than the incidence of general incisional hernia.5 6 More than 50% of these SSIH patients require surgical treatment due to abdominal pain, embedded hernial contents, bowel obstruction and decreased quality of life.7 Therefore, it has been suggested that SSIH is a grossly underestimated clinical problem. Some researchers conducted a meta-analysis of 33 clinical trials of SSIH after ileostomy closure surgery, which included a total of 4679 patients, and individually analysed 11 of them that had SSIH as the primary endpoint of the study, which had an incidence rate of 17.7%.5 A meta-analysis that included 14 studies with SSIH as the primary study outcome showed that the incidence of SSIH was 36.1%.8 Japanese researchers Kaneko et al conducted an observational study of 134 patients with gastrointestinal tumours who underwent temporary ileostomy and ileostomy closure surgery. The incidence of SSIH diagnosed by CT imaging was 23.9%, and the median time to diagnosis was 8 months (2–39 months), while 22% of SSIH occurred more than 1 year after ileostomy closure surgery.9 It is evident that as the efficacy of colorectal cancer continues to improve, the incidence of SSIH will increase with longer follow-up after prophylactic ileostomy closure surgery. Therefore, colorectal cancer-associated SSIH deserves the common concern of gastrointestinal and hernia surgeons.

Recent evidence supports the prophylactic mesh placement during ileostomy closure to reduce SSIH risk. A pilot study highlighted the technical feasibility and short-term safety of biological mesh reinforcement,10 while a meta-analysis confirmed its efficacy in lowering hernia rates (7.8% vs 18.1%) and reoperation needs without increasing major complications.11 These findings underscore the potential of targeted mesh application, though long-term validation remains critical. In addition, ongoing investigation (Reinforcement of Closure of Stoma Site (ROCSS) Trial) by other research groups is also exploring the role of biological mesh in preventing SSIH.12 13 Their forthcoming results will provide a comprehensive evidence base to refine best practices for abdominal closure.

Radiomics is an emerging technology that employs high-throughput imaging genomics features to quantify the characteristics of the region of interest (). This region is currently divided into two main categories: manual imaging radiomics and deep learning-based imaging radiomics.14 It has been extensively used in the context of personalised diagnosis and treatment of tumour patients, with notable outcomes. Presently, the integration of imaging histology labels with clinical features in hybrid models has markedly enhanced the predictive capacity.15 Nevertheless, its application in the domain of postoperative complications remains limited, and studies employing deep learning imaging histology to predict SSIH have yet to emerge.

This study aims to identify patients at an elevated risk of developing SSIH by employing an image-based deep learning model in conjunction with an imaging histology approach, and high-risk patients were included in the randomised controlled study to explore whether prophylactic implantation of biological mesh could reduce the incidence of SSIH. At present, there is a lack of high-quality evidence to support the effectiveness of prophylactic use of biological mesh, and its effect urgently needs to be verified through strict randomised controlled trials (RCTs).10 13 Through rigorous RCT design, innovative stratification methods for high-risk patients and standardised intervention procedures, this study scientifically verified the value of biological mesh in preventing SSIH after ileostomy closure surgery, which has significant clinical significance and methodological rationality. Our deep learning model integrates preoperative CT-derived radiomic features and clinical parameters to generate a dynamic, patient-specific risk profile. This approach addresses the multifactorial pathogenesis of SSIH, including subclinical tissue remodelling undetectable by conventional metrics. For example, the model identified stoma size, age, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and as independent predictors of SSIH, which are not captured by traditional risk scores. The mixed model showed good discrimination of the SSIH patients on all cohorts, which outperformed deep learning, radiomics and clinical models alone (overall AUC: 0.947 in the primary cohort; 0.876 in the external validation cohort 1 and 0.776 in the external validation cohort 2).16 Moreover, the sensitivity, specificity and precision for predicting SSIH were improved in the mixed model. Thus, the mixed model can provide more information for SSIH precaution and clinical decision-making. Further details are provided in our prior publication.16

In conclusion, the RCT evaluated the safety and efficacy of biological mesh for the prevention of SSIH in patients who underwent ileostomy closure surgery and had high-risk factors for incisional hernia, as identified through an image-based deep learning model. The findings provide a scientific rationale for the broader implementation of the use of biological mesh in the prevention of incisional hernia in patients undergoing ileostomy closure surgery during radical laparoscopic rectal cancer surgery. Furthermore, the results of this study will provide new ideas for the application of artificial intelligence (AI) models and deep learning to guide surgical decision-making.

Methods and analysis

Study design

The study is a single-centre, prospective, randomised controlled study that will be conducted from November 2024 to November 2026 at Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. This timeframe includes a 12-month recruitment period (November 2024–November 2025) followed by a 12-month follow-up period to ensure complete postoperative monitoring for all participants. Perioperative book data will be collected and analysed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of the biological mesh for the prevention of SSIH in patients who underwent ileostomy closure surgery and have high risk factors for incisional hernia as assessed by an image-based deep learning model. Confirmed rectal cancer patients who underwent rectal resection with prophylactic ileostomy and required postoperative closure surgery (3–6 months postoperatively) were enrolled by surgeons and imaging physicians according to the inclusion criteria and after completing the preoperative evaluation. The data and follow-up results were independently counted. The set primary efficacy evaluation indexes, secondary efficacy evaluation indexes and safety evaluation indexes were assessed and recorded as required, and the intraoperative situation, postoperative hospitalisation time, presurgical and postsurgical laboratory tests (blood counts, coagulation function and blood biochemistry) and postoperative incisional hernia, incisional infection incidence, etc, were observed, so as to scientifically assess the same period of time for performing the ileostomy closure surgery in the present study. To scientifically evaluate the safety, effectiveness and feasibility of using biological mesh to reduce incisional hernia and incisional infection during the same period of ileostomy closure surgery in this study and to provide an evidence-based scientific basis for further popularising the application of biological mesh in reducing incisional hernia and incisional infection.

In this study, we used SPIRIT reporting guidelines to monitor and refine the structure of our study protocol.17

This study will be conducted in accordance with the International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects (CIOMS), the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice (GCP) ethical principles. In addition, this protocol has been approved by the ethics committee of Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (KY2022-087-B) and registered in the China Clinical Trials Registry (ChiCTR2200064995).

Image-based deep learning model to predict SSIH and the risk score

Recently, our research team published ‘Image-based deep learning model to predict stoma-site incisional hernia in patients with temporary ileostomy: a retrospective study’.16 Our findings indicate that an AI machine learning radiomics-based approach is an effective method for identifying individuals at high risk for incisional hernia following ileostomy closure surgery. Given the high cost of biological mesh and the time-consuming nature of the surgical procedure, there is a potential for an increase in the social healthcare burden. Accordingly, this study proposes the reinforcement of biological mesh placement surgery in the population at high risk for incisional hernia. This approach will help to reduce the cost of hospitalisation for patients, reduce the time of surgery for doctors and reduce the healthcare burden for society. The safety and efficacy of biological mesh for the prevention of incisional hernia in patients with high-risk factors for incisional hernia, as assessed by an image-based deep learning model who underwent ileostomy closure surgery, will be evaluated in a randomised controlled study, as predetermined in this study.

The model can predict the risk of complications in patients undergoing temporary ileostomy, including the formation of SSIH after stoma closure. In this model, we conducted a retrospective analysis of 220 patients with incisional hernia after previous stoma retraction at Renji Hospital. Based on these patients who developed SSIH, we constructed a tool, an image-based deep learning model to predict SSIH in patients with temporary ileostomy, which serves as an easy-to-use preoperative individualised diagnostic tool for SSIH for calculating the score of incisional hernia development in patients who underwent ileostomy closure surgery and for predicting the proportion of patients who will develop SSIH within 1 year. The score for SSIH based on the integration of three types of parameters: clinical parameters, such as age, stoma plane size, COPD and body mass index (BMI); radiomics features, such as shape features, texture features and LASSO regression screening and deep learning features, such as extraction of CT images through migration learning using the MobileNetV2 model of deep image features, as well as focusing on grey-scale intensity differences in soft tissue and muscle regions (indirect risk factors such as infection, oedema, etc).16 Based on this, we developed and validated a hybrid model combining clinical characterisation, radiomics and deep learning to accurately predict SSIH with ROI of preoperative CT images. To screen for potential high risk of SSIH within 1 year, we defined patients with a tool score above the median as having high risk factors for incisional hernia and enrolled them in an exploratory randomised controlled study.

To explicitly compare the predictive performance of clinical-only models vs hybrid (clinical+AI) models, we conducted retrospective validation using the same cohorts. The clinical model, constructed from five key variables (stoma size, age, parasternal hernia, prechemotherapy and COPD), achieved an AUC of 0.776 (95% CI: 0.682 to 0.854) in the primary cohort. In contrast, the hybrid model, integrating clinical features with radiomics and deep learning outputs, demonstrated significantly superior performance, with an AUC of 0.947 (95% CI: 0.850 to 0.997) in the same cohort (p<0.001). Similar trends were observed in external validation cohorts, where the hybrid model maintained higher sensitivity (0.867 vs 0.611), specificity (0.918 vs 0.699) and precision (0.684 vs 0.402) compared with the clinical-only model.16 These findings underscore the added value of AI integration in enhancing SSIH risk stratification and clinical decision-making.

Study participants

In this study, a total of 40 subjects who required rectal cancer resection with prophylactic ileostomy closure surgery and were assessed as having high risk factors for incisional hernia based on the image-based deep learning model were selected from November 2024 to November 2026, and these 40 patients were randomised into intervention group (group A) and control group (group B). In the intervention group, ileostomy closure surgery and biological mesh placement were performed, and in the control group, ileostomy closure surgery was performed. The study subjects were followed up by telephone or outpatient clinic at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months and 12 months after the operation, and the corresponding outcome indicators, baseline data, other laboratory tests and other data related to the study were recorded. Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined through a priori power analysis using PASS V.2021 software and was calculated based on ileostomy-specific SSIH incidence rates derived from high-risk populations. Based on previous studies reporting, we assumed a 30% SSIH rate in high-risk rectal cancer patients after ileostomy5 6 and expert consensus predicting a clinically meaningful reduction to 15% with the novel intervention, we calculated the required sample size with α=0.05 (two-sided) and 80% power. The χ2 test calculation yielded 19 patients per group (total n=38). To account for 5% potential attrition and protocol deviations, we increased the sample size to 20 per group (total n=40). This aligns with phase II trial standards and ensures feasibility while maintaining statistical rigour (78% power). This sample size maintains adequate sensitivity through our standardised image-based deep learning assessment protocol. It is worth noting that the small sample size (n=40) poses a significant statistical limitation, particularly for detecting differences in secondary endpoints such as infection or pain.

Randomisation

The study employed a block randomisation method with block size of four, generated through R software (V.4.2.1) by a researcher. To ensure allocation concealment, group assignments (group A: intervention; group B: control) were sealed in sequentially numbered opaque envelopes with tamper-evident security labels, which were validated weekly by the research coordinator. A 1:1 allocation ratio was strictly maintained, resulting in 20 cases per group (total n=40). Blinding protocols were implemented through double-masking: (1) participants received standardised preoperative instructions without disclosure of group allocation; (2) surgical teams were only informed of specific closure techniques after anaesthetic induction. The complete randomisation log, including timestamped envelope openings and dual verification signatures, was archived for audit purposes. Randomisation integrity was monitored through quarterly audits by the institutional review board, with all procedures compliant with Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for surgical trials.

Biological mesh selection

The biological mesh (VIDASIS) is a regenerative repair material with the following physical (framework) microenvironment for cell growth. The utilisation of non-crosslinked treatment for the biological mesh employed in this study markedly enhances histocompatibility and circumvents the risk of erosion or infection associated with synthetic patches in comparison to traditional biomaterials. A comparative study of two types of artificial blood vessels, one composed of small intestinal submucosa (SIS) and the other of polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE), was conducted by Daniel H. Shell et al in the presence of gram-positive bacterial contamination. Compared with ePTFE vascular grafts, SIS showed no bacterial growth in the environment contaminated with Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis (1.7×and 6×CFU/mL, respectively, in the ePTFE group, p<0.001).18 Bhanot et al’s study highlighted the results of several commonly used biologic grid products, demonstrating that the postoperative hernia recurrence rate of SIS biological mesh was significantly lower than other biomaterials.19 Jiang et al conducted a retrospective analysis of clinical data. SSIH incidence was only 4.5% (30–35% in the control group) in 22 ileostomy patients using SIS mesh with no increased complications such as infection, seroma and mild postoperative pain.20 The biological mesh is a novel repair material that offers the advantages of anti-infection, good histocompatibility, less scarring, no long-term inflammation and low adhesion. Its characteristic low adhesion properties make it safe for use in adhesion-prone areas, making it an effective choice for prophylactic applications to prevent incisional infections and incisional hernias after stoma closure.

The biological mesh used in this study is a novel biologic tissue regeneration material, developed by Beijing BIOSIS HEALING Co. The product is a heterogeneous decellularised tissue matrix material prepared from the submucosa of porcine small intestine as the raw material. It effectively removes immunogenicity, exhibits good biocompatibility, mechanical strength, suitable porosity and degradability. Some studies have demonstrated that biological mesh is a safe and effective method for reducing the incidence of incisional hernia.21,23 To date, no relevant literature has been identified that reports on the use of biological mesh produced by Beijing BIOSIS HEALING Co.

Standard operating procedure and postoperative care

Standard operating procedures are detailed in online supplemental table 3. The decision to standardise prophylactic intraperitoneal onlay mesh (IPOM) and linear skin closure was driven by the need for procedural consistency and reduced heterogeneity in surgical outcomes. Linear closure was selected for its operational simplicity and reduced surgical time, which are critical in ensuring procedural consistency across all participants. While meta-analyses indicate that purse-string closure may lower infection rates by facilitating passive drainage,24,26 our protocol incorporated prophylactic closed suction drains to address fluid accumulation and infection risks. This approach aligns with some studies and research showing no statistically significant differences between linear and purse-string closure in hospitalisation duration, incisional hernia incidence or wound healing time when drains are used.24 Intraoperatively, the surgeon needs to observe the principle of asepsis and pay attention to wound protection. Postoperatively, researchers will increase the frequency of dressing changes. If infection occurs, intensifying dressing changes and symptomatic supportive treatment are essential. If conservative treatment is ineffective, researchers will perform debridement or even secondary surgery to remove the source of contamination.

To minimise complications, postoperative care will include: (1) dietary management: clear liquids within 24 hours, advancing to low-residue foods by 48 hours, followed by a protein-rich diet avoiding heavy meals to reduce intra-abdominal pressure; (2) Activity Restrictions: Early ambulation (3–4 walks/day), strict avoidance of lifting >5 kg for 6 weeks and high-impact activities for 8 weeks, with abdominal binders provided for high-risk patients (BMI >30 or COPD) and education on controlled coughing/sneezing; (3) drain management: daily output monitoring (<30 mL/day for removal) and sterile dressing changes; (4) infection prevention: daily wound inspection, antimicrobial dressings for high-risk cases and protocol-driven antibiotics. This protocol standardises interventions targeting abdominal strain, infection and healing to ensure comparability across study groups.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis plan will adhere to CONSORT guidelines, using SPSS V.22.0 and R V.4.2.1. Continuous variables (eg, operative time, hospital stay, blood loss) will be expressed as mean±SD or median (IQR) based on normality (Shapiro-Wilk test), while categorical variables (eg, SSIH incidence, infection rates) will be reported as frequencies (%). The primary outcome—SSIH incidence at 12 months—will be compared via χ2/Fisher’s exact test, with relative risk and 95% CI. Secondary outcomes include incisional infection, seroma, local pain (analysed similarly) and pain severity (WHO scale, ordinal logistic regression adjusted for BMI/preoperative pain). Additional analyses will assess demographics (age, sex, BMI, diabetes, ASA class, tumour, node, metastases stage), laboratory parameters (haemoglobin, leucocytes, INR, APTT, albumin via mixed-effects models), quality of life (EuroQol-5D, EQ-5D/Short form health survey, SF-36 scores via ANCOVA), imaging outcomes (hernia size/fascial defect width via linear regression) and adverse events (AEs) (grade ≥3 AEs per NCI Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) V.5.0, Fisher’s test). Safety and AE monitoring

The evaluation of AEs was conducted using a combination of the Accordion Severity Grading System and the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, V.4.0. In classifying AEs, the most closely aligned definitional elements of grades 0–4 were used. In the event of a treatment-related death, the AE should be classified as grade five in accordance with the original CTCAE. For items pertaining to toxicity that are outlined in the section on procedure-related AEs, the date of discovery of the grade and the grade itself should be recorded in the relevant section of the treatment procedure report. In the event that toxicities are observed that are not specified in procedure-related AEs, the toxicity item, grade and date of discovery of the grade must be recorded in the free entry field of the procedure report. This is only necessary if a grade of 3 or higher is observed. Furthermore, the grade recorded on the aforementioned recording paper must also be recorded in the case. In the fourth edition of the CTCAE, an AE is defined as ‘all undesired signs (including abnormalities in clinical examination values), symptoms or conditions observed at the time of treatment or disposition that are not expected to occur, whether or not causally related to the treatment or disposition’. In other words, there are two types of cases: those judged to be causally related and those judged not to be causally related. Therefore, an ‘AE’ is considered to be an ‘AE’ even if it is ‘clearly caused by the primary disease (cancer)’ and is not caused by the treatment of the study protocol (protocol treatment) itself, but by the supportive therapy or combination therapy.

It is imperative that any AE, regardless of whether it is related to surgery, be recorded in detail in the case report form (CRF). This includes the name of the AE, the time of occurrence of the AE, the symptoms, signs, duration, examination indices, treatments, process, results, follow-up time and any other pertinent information. The recording of this information is essential for analysing the correlation between the AE and surgery. Once recorded, the form must be signed and dated. In the event of a serious AE or an unintended AE, the relevant supervisory and regulatory authorities and ethics committees must be informed in a timely manner, in accordance with the requirements of the applicable regulations.

Discussion

Currently, colorectal cancer represents the fourth most lethal form of cancer globally, with an estimated 900 000 fatalities annually. It is the second most prevalent form of cancer diagnosed in women and the third most prevalent in men.27 Thereinto, rectal cancer remains a significant global health concern, with a particularly devastating impact on patients. In order to safeguard a distal colonic anastomosis and avert pelvic sepsis, an ileostomy is created in low anterior resection.28 The long-term adverse effects of ileostomy closure include the development of an incisional hernia. The incidence of incisional hernia following ileostomy closure has been quantified in more recent articles at a rate between 5% and 23.9%.9 29

In light of the resurgence in the prevalence of SSIH, the prevention of this specific type of incisional hernia has emerged as a prominent area of investigation in recent years. The majority of pertinent clinical studies have focused on the efficacy and safety of prophylactic patch placement during the same period of stoma reduction. The results of the large-sample, multicentre, RCT ROCSS demonstrated that prophylactic placement of a biological mesh at the time of enterostomy closure was an effective intervention for reducing the incidence of SSIH over a 2-year period (9% vs 21%).30 Furthermore, concomitant prophylactic placement of material patches during reduction surgery was found to be an effective method for reducing the incidence of SSIH associated with both ileostomy (7.0% vs 17.7%) and colostomy (10.8% vs 19.2%). A study demonstrates that biological mesh not only reduces the incidence of incisional hernia but also significantly reduces the rate of superficial tissue infections in individuals with colostomies and ileostomies.31 The aforementioned clinical findings indicate that stoma reduction with patch placement is a safe and effective preventive measure for SSIH.11 Nevertheless, some scholars have proposed that prophylactic patch placement in all patients with ileostomy may be perceived as an excessive form of treatment. Accordingly, in consideration of the aforementioned risk factors associated with SSIH, it may be more aligned with the principles of health economics to identify the population with a high incidence of SSIH and perform prophylactic patch placement.

It is therefore imperative to accurately determine the indication for biological mesh placement based on whether a patient has high-risk factors for SSIH before ileostomy closure surgery. This study’s distinctive approach lies in its integration of cutting-edge imaging histology, image-based deep learning modelling and other advanced technologies, complementing traditional risk assessment methods. This integration aims to enhance the scientific accuracy and precision of SSIH risk stratification, paving the way for the development of more effective and targeted preventive interventions. A critical consideration in this study is differentiating whether SSIH occurrence stems from biological mesh inefficacy or inaccuracies in the deep learning model’s risk stratification. To address this, we have implemented multiple measures. First, the model’s predictive accuracy will undergo rigorous validation post hoc via receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, comparing preoperative risk scores with actual SSIH incidence in both groups. Second, in the intervention group (biological mesh), SSIH cases will be cross-referenced with the model’s preoperative risk classification to identify potential model misclassifications. Conversely, in the control group (no mesh), SSIH incidence in high-risk patients will isolate the mesh’s independent effect. Third, intraoperative documentation and postoperative CT imaging will evaluate mesh integrity (displacement, degradation), while multivariate regression will adjust for confounders (BMI, surgical technique) to disentangle model performance from mesh-related factors. Finally, extended follow-up beyond 12 months (planned for phase III) will monitor late-onset hernias, which may reflect mesh degradation rather than model limitations. While the current sample size may constrain granular causality attribution, these strategies collectively enhance the interpretability of SSIH aetiology.

Another noteworthy aspect of this study is the use of a biological mesh, which has superior compatibility and improves the success rate of the surgery and the quality of the patient’s postoperative recovery to a certain extent. The advent of advanced materials has enabled the development of a biological mesh composed of collagen, which can be safely integrated into the surrounding tissue after surgical incision. The biological mesh reduces the risk of infection and maintains the strength of the incision in comparison to a typical patch. A large multicentre RCT in Europe, which included 37 hospitals, reported that prophylactic placement of a biological mesh during stoma reduction significantly reduced the incidence of postoperative incisional hernia and did not affect the incidence of AEs such as wound infection or haematoma.30 Based on the current evidence-based medical evidence, the prophylactic use of a biological mesh against incisional hernia in ileostomy reduction is a feasible approach.

This study protocol is groundbreaking in that it is the first to use an AI image deep learning model and preoperative ileostomy planar CT images of patients to successfully screen those about to undergo ileostomy closure for high-risk factors for SSIH. The patients identified as high-risk were included in the study group and underwent either the biological mesh interventional or non-interventional surgical measures, respectively. This study was conducted as a prospective, exploratory, RCT and the sample size calculation met the requirements to detect clinically relevant differences in the primary endpoint. Although the sample size is small compared with some validation studies, the internal validity of the study conclusions is ensured by precise patient stratification, standardised intervention protocols and an objective deep learning scoring system, which can provide reliable effect size estimates for subsequent phase III studies. In the future, phase III trials will prioritise broader inclusion criteria and external validation of the AI model. It is notable that several authoritative studies have demonstrated a strong association between stoma orifice size and the incidence of SSIH.32,34 Accordingly, the enrolment criteria of this study were developed in accordance with the findings of relevant studies and were also strongly supported by evidence-based medicine, thus ensuring a highly scientific and rational study protocol. The ultimate outcome of this study will not only substantiate the viability and efficacy of AI image deep learning models in clinical decision-making but also significantly advance the integration of AI models into the medical domain, offering novel insights and avenues for future medical research and clinical practice.
