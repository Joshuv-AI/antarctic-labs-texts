Value‐Based Medicine in the Management of Abdominal Aortic Aneurysms
Authors: Daniel Raskin, Sasan Partovi, Francis Caputo, Sean P Lyden, Enrico Prajiante Bertolino, Gregory Piazza, Matthew J Eagleton, Lee Kirksey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe implementation of value‐based medicine reflects a transformation from volume incentivized care to a quality‐of‐care directed reimbursement model. Value‐based medicine aims to concurrently improve health care quality and control growing health care expenditures by aligning evidence‐based and patient‐centered interventions with sustainable resource use. We apply a value‐based medicine framework to abdominal aortic aneurysm care delivery, highlighting opportunities to improve patient outcomes, optimize resource allocation, and reduce health care expenditures across the continuum of disease management. A comprehensive literature review was conducted, evaluating recent clinical guidelines, economic analyses, and real‐world practice data on abdominal aortic aneurysm management, including screening, surveillance, timing of intervention, modality selection (endovascular aneurysm repair versus open surgical repair), and follow‐up strategies. Contemporary real‐world published practice patterns reveal wide variation in screening uptake, surveillance intensity, repair criteria thresholds, procedure and device selection, and post‐endovascular aneurysm repair imaging, producing inconsistent outcomes and inefficient spending. Endovascular aneurysm repair predominates in elective abdominal aortic aneurysm repair because of lower perioperative morbidity and shorter length of stay; however, downstream costs from surveillance, endoleak management, and reinterventions may erode its initial economic advantage over open surgical repair. Key value drivers include risk‐aligned screening, standardized surveillance anchored to anatomy and device risk, judicious use of advanced imaging, optimized perioperative pathways, and careful device selection. Savings opportunities include predictive analytics to individualize surveillance, deescalation of routine computed tomography after documented sac shrinkage, smoking cessation, medical optimization, and structured shared decision‐making aligned with life expectancy and patient preferences. Integrating value‐based medicine principles into abdominal aortic aneurysm care through regional centers of excellence, bundled reimbursement models, transparent cost structures, and surveillance strategies may offer a pragmatic roadmap to achieve safer, economically sustainable, and patient‐centered management, ultimately advancing both clinical outcomes and cost efficiency.Keywords: abdominal aortic aneurysm, health care costs, patient outcomes, value‐based medicine, vascular surgerySubject Categories: Health Services, Quality and Outcomes, Aneurysm, Vascular Disease

Value‐based medicine (VBM) provides a roadmap for delivering high‐quality, safe, evidence‐based care in the most cost‐efficient manner. In practice, it aligns patient‐centered outcomes with sustainable resource use through the Triple Aim: (1) enhancing patient experience, (2) improving clinical outcomes, and (3) reducing population‐level expenditure. 1 Contemporary iterations incorporate clinician and caregiver well‐being, adding a Quadruple Aim. 2

This review translates VBM from concept to practice for abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) management. We synthesize guideline recommendations, practice variation, and economic evidence across screening, surveillance, timing and modality of repair, and lifelong follow‐up. Building on these data, we outline a 5‐point implementation roadmap: regionalization, episode‐based payments, cost transparency, predictive analytics (including artificial intelligence [AI]‐enabled surveillance), and collaborative savings models to support consistent, safer, and economically sustainable AAA care.

METHODS

This narrative review was conducted to summarize contemporary evidence on AAA care delivery across the continuum (imaging, repair selection, follow‐up, and payment/implementation). We searched PubMed/MEDLINE and Google Scholar for English‐language publications from January 1, 2014, to August 2025. Seminal pre‐2014 trials/guidelines were included when foundational to AAA care. We also reviewed reference lists of key guidelines and reviews to identify additional studies. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, followed by full‐text assessment. Inclusion criteria prioritized clinical guidelines, consensus documents, comparative effectiveness studies, cost‐effectiveness analyses, cohort studies, and pragmatic trials relevant to AAA imaging, surveillance pathways, periprocedural planning, outcomes, resource use, or cost. Exclusion criteria included case reports, nonabdominal aneurysmatic aortic disease (eg, isolated thoracic aorta), nonhuman studies, and purely technical imaging physics without clinical applicability. This review is narrative and does not claim Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses‐level systematic completeness; methods are reported to improve transparency and reproducibility of study selection. All monetary values are reported in US dollars and, when derived from prior‐year publications, are presented in the price year reported by the source. We distinguish hospital costs (resource‐use accounting), charges (billed amounts), and reimbursement/payments (payer allowed amounts); the term used in‐text matches the source. Dollar amounts are reported in the price year stated in the source publication (or the data‐collection year when explicitly specified). When payer perspective is relevant, we specify whether figures reflect Medicare allowed amounts, commercial payments, or hospital accounting costs; we do not convert across perspectives.

BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE

AAA exemplifies a condition in which a focus on value‐based management principles across the time continuum can reduce the overall health care expenditures associated with this disease state, which is disproportionately composed of Medicare reimbursed care. Large population‐based observational studies have documented an increasing prevalence of nonruptured AAA pathology over recent decades. This observation is theorized to be a consequence of increased use of diagnostic radiographic imaging modalities that is, incidental findings. 3 , 4 , 5 A concurrent increase in the performance of aortic aneurysm interventions‐specifically endovascular therapies—has been observed during this period. 5 , 6 , 7

Unlike many chronic vascular conditions, early identification of nonruptured AAAs offers a clear, evidence‐supported opportunity to prevent catastrophic events through a single, low‐cost test. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) cover‐ a once‐in‐a‐lifetime abdominal ultrasound for beneficiaries identified as high risk. 8 Population modeling and long‐term follow‐up of randomized trials show that such screening prevents 0.5 to 1.5 rupture deaths per 1000 people screened and yields incremental cost‐effectiveness ratios. 9 , 10 Despite these favorable economics, uptake remains suboptimal, with fewer than half of eligible Medicare beneficiaries receiving such a scan. 11 In integrated systems that actively operationalize the CMS benefit, screening has reduced rupture‐related mortality by ∼40% and shifted care from emergency to elective repair, underlining its value proposition for VBM. 12 , 13

OPPORTUNITIES FOR VBM ACROSS THE CONTINUUM OF AAA CARE

Elective AAA repair is a prophylactic intervention that by design aims to prevent catastrophic rupture. However, procedural intervention for the index pathology introduces a series of potential negative consequences including procedure‐associated complications, postprocedural surveillance and re‐intervention each associated with patient harm and additional expenditure. With the identification of AAA pathology, a surveillance strategy initiates and informs decisional steps around (1) medical optimization, (2) timing of repair, (3) device selection, and (4) the specific modality and cadence of postprocedural surveillance imaging. Although clinical guidelines exist for the management of AAA, the application of a rubric for these decision‐making points is poorly understood and inconsistent with broad variability across geographic regions of the country and clinical specialties. 14 , 15 , 16

The comparative clinical outcomes and costs associated with endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) versus open surgical repair (OSR) has been debated since the release of the United Kingdom's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence guidelines released in 2020. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence recommends EVAR only when open surgery is relatively contraindicated (eg, hostile abdomen) or when significant medical or anesthetic risk precludes OSR. 17 , 18

The value‐based framework emphasizes comparing treatments based on patient outcomes relative to total cost of care. 19 From this perspective, EVAR's early perioperative advantages may be offset by higher downstream expenses for surveillance imaging and reinterventions, yielding long‐term costs that are similar to open surgery. 20 The value‐based lens underscores the importance of weighing EVAR's short‐term benefits against its total cost of care over time.

Clinical guidelines for AAA management are available and published across multiple societies and specialty groups including vascular surgery, interventional radiology, and cardiovascular medicine. In general, these recommendations emphasize the importance of risk‐targeted screening 9 and recommend stratified imaging follow‐up, 21 yet real‐world data reveal inconsistent adherence to these recommendations, with attendant waste and potential patient harm. 11 , 22 , 23 At the treatment phase, EVAR now dominates elective practice, largely because of patient preference, physician training and specialty, minimally invasive nature, shorter length of hospitalization, and reduced recovery. 22 , 23 Key quantitative anchors used throughout this review are summarized in Table 1.

VBM frames decisions from a population perspective across the care continuum, from diagnosis through lifelong surveillance. Contemporary US analyses indicate that EVAR's device, imaging, and reintervention costs can offset perioperative savings relative to OSR, creating a potential value gap for payers. 24 Importantly, this potential “value gap” is sensitive to the intensity and modality of post‐EVAR surveillance. Contemporary society guidelines recommend early cross‐sectional imaging to establish a postimplant baseline (eg, computed tomography angiography [CTA] within ~30 days), followed by reassessment at 6 to 12 months and then long‐term annual surveillance. However, these pathways increasingly allow risk‐adapted substitution of duplex ultrasound for routine CTA in low‐risk phenotypes, with escalation to cross‐sectional imaging reserved for clinical or imaging triggers. SCREENING AND SURVEILLANCE CHALLENGES

The contemporary published literature reveals a broad consensus agreement regarding the use of clinical risk profile‐targeted ultrasound for the diagnosis of AAA, “Welcome to Medicare” program and Viborg screening. 10 Despite this evidence, wide gaps in implementation and use are present that lead to missed or delayed diagnoses. Most screening guidelines recommend 1‐time abdominal ultrasound for men aged 65 to 75 years who have ever smoked. The 2024 American College of Radiology Appropriateness Criteria rank grayscale ultrasound “usually appropriate” and CT or magnetic resonance imaging only when ultrasound is indeterminate. 15 , 25

The primary challenges in applying evidence‐based screening guidelines is increasing their adoption across the broader healthcare community, particularly within the fragmented landscape of Medicare and private payers. 11 The Veterans Administration likely represents the most successful model for broad scale and consistent implementation of screening programs. 12 , 13 Within integrated systems, best‐practice advisory and AI‐based tools that identify at‐risk patients and prompt providers can help achieve high‐value screening. Economic incentives for accountable care organizations should align with appropriate use.

SURVEILLANCE AND SECONDARY PREVENTION

Once aortic pathology is identified, management aims at (1) appropriate, cost‐effective surveillance for AAA growth and (2) secondary prevention to slow aneurysm growth. Adherence to surveillance protocols is inconsistent due to provider awareness, fragmented care, patient noncompliance, and resource limitations. Beyond clinical end points, AAA surveillance carries a measurable patient burden. Repeated imaging and prolonged “watchful waiting” can increase anxiety, alter perceived health status, and affect health‐related quality of life, particularly when communication is inconsistent or follow‐up is fragmented. From a VBM perspective, surveillance pathways should therefore be designed to be both guideline concordant and patient centered, minimizing unnecessary visits and tests while maintaining safety and emphasizing clear counseling on growth expectations, rupture risk, and the specific findings that would prompt intervention. 26

Implementation typically lags behind policy: private‐insurance claims show fewer than half of eligible US men receive recommended ultrasound surveillance, with marked site‐to‐site variation. 27 A value‐based approach prioritizes guideline‐concordant, risk‐stratified surveillance with ultrasound as the default modality. Although improved adherence may increase near‐term ultrasound use, this strategy can still improve overall value by supporting timely elective management and reducing rupture presentations, which are associated with markedly higher morbidity, mortality, and total episode cost. In parallel, limiting routine cross‐sectional imaging when ultrasound is adequate represents a complementary opportunity to reduce low‐value care. Countries with national screening show reduced AAA mortality and increased detection of nonruptured aneurysms. 28 , 29 Emergency AAA intervention carries a significantly higher risk of perioperative morbidity and mortality far exceeding elective repair rates. 30 , 31 Once diagnosed, secondary preventive measures which reduce AAA progression should be implemented. The role of smoking cessation in the mitigation of AAA growth is widely recognized and is a level 1 recommendation from the Society for Vascular Surgery, European Society for Vascular Surgery (ESVS), American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology. 15 , 23 , 32 , 33 Observational meta‐analyses suggest statin therapy is associated with slower AAA growth (~1 mm/year reduction), lower rupture risk, and improved peri−/long‐term survival; however, heterogeneity and residual confounding limit causal inference, and large growth‐modification randomized controlled trials are lacking (small perioperative randomized controlled trials show favorable tissue biomarker shifts without clinical end points). 34 , 35 , 36 Uncertainty notwithstanding, given the high prevalence of cardiovascular disease within this cohort, statin therapy is frequently indicated for that indication alone.

The concept of “low‐value imaging” refers to surveillance imaging that is more intensive than guideline‐concordant pathways and is unlikely to change clinical management or improve AAA related outcomes. Non–evidence‐based overuse of advanced imaging (eg, repeated CT instead of ultrasound) adds unnecessary costs and patient harm (radiation, contrast, anxiety). 26 , 37 Low‐value imaging occurs in both the preprocedural monitoring phase as well as the postprocedural surveillance period. 38 Guideline‐consistent surveillance calls for duplex ultrasound as first‐line follow‐up, reserving CT for equivocal findings; however, registries show that contrast‐enhanced CT is still used in up to 60% of routine visits.

Operationally, CTA (or alternative cross‐sectional imaging) is most appropriate when duplex is indeterminate/limited, when there is interval sac enlargement or rapid growth, when EVAR complications are suspected (eg, endoleak, migration, kinking, limb occlusion), when duplex findings are equivocal and would change management, or when planning reintervention/complex anatomy. When iodinated contrast is undesirable, noncontrast CT, magnetic resonance angiography, or contrast‐enhanced ultrasound may be considered depending on the clinical question. 38 Each abdominal CT commonly delivers ~5 to 10 mSv of radiation; payer‐level costs and patient cost‐sharing vary materially by contract and site. 39 , 40 Repeated CT has not been shown to improve rupture‐free survival compared with risk‐stratified ultrasound protocols, yet it doubles imaging expenditures and increases patient‐reported anxiety and nephrotoxicity risk, particularly in chronic kidney‐disease cohorts. 39 , 41

Much investigation has focused on the best clinical indicators to determine the threshold for aneurysm procedural intervention. To date, maximal transverse diameter is the most widely accepted surrogate for AAA rupture risk. Ultrasonographic measurement of aneurysm wall strain/stress and CT measured aneurysm sac volumetric measurement are 2 such efforts to identify surrogate modalities to assess aneurysm rupture risk. 15 , 25 , 42 , 43 ESVS and American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines encourage the use of ultrasound for prerepair surveillance performed at various time intervals: 3 to 3.9 cm Q3 years, 4 to 4.9 cm Q1 year, 5 to 5.5 cm Q6 months. 23 , 32 Evidence suggests that once diagnosed with AAA pathology, CT scan imaging may be excessively used for patients out of “an abundance of caution” leading to excessive cost to payors/patients/health care system and unnecessary patient anxiety. 29 , 39 Beyond maximal diameter, emerging 4‐dimensional ultrasound strain metrics correlate with CTA‐derived wall stress and may refine biomechanical risk profiling, whereas CT‐based volumetric measurements (alone or combined with flow metrics) better predict aneurysmal sac growth and rising wall stress than diameter alone in several cohorts and systematic reviews; these tools remain adjunctive and are not yet guideline standard. 43 , 44 , 45

TIMING OF INTERVENTION

Contemporary guidelines converge on diameter‐ and growth‐based triggers for elective AAA repair, with nuanced adjustments for sex. The 2022 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association aortic‐disease guideline recommends repair in asymptomatic patients once maximal sac diameter reaches ≥5.5 cm in men or ≥5.0 cm in women or sooner if growth is ≥0.5 cm within 6 months or symptoms develop. 32 The 2024 ESVS guideline endorses the same 55 mm threshold for men but advises considering repair at ≥50 mm in women because their rupture risk is proportionally higher; rapid expansion of ≥10 mm per year should also prompt reassessment and possible earlier intervention. Once criteria are met, ESVS advocates a “fast‐track” pathway completing repair within 8 weeks, with an even shorter window for sacs >70 mm to minimize interval rupture risk. Repair can also be considered sooner when expansion is rapid (≥ 10 mm per year or ≥5 mm in 6 months) because accelerated growth signals heightened rupture risk. 23 , 46 , 47 VQI (Vascular Quality Initiative) data from Michigan and small aneurysm trials demonstrated the absence of survival advantage for those aneurysms treated earlier than the accepted contemporary threshold of 4.0 to 5.5 cm. 48 , 49

Although early randomized trials (EVAR‐1 [Endovascular Aneurysm Repair 1] and DREAM [Dutch Randomized Endovascular Aneurysm Management]) demonstrated a perioperative survival advantage with EVAR compared with OSR, longer follow‐up showed attenuation of this early benefit, with convergence in overall survival and higher reintervention rates after EVAR. 50 , 51 , 52 Subsequent evidence, including the OVER [Standard Open Surgery Versus Endovascular Repair of Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm] trial, similarly showed no sustained long‐term survival advantage for EVAR over OSR, underscoring that late outcomes are influenced by endograft durability and the need for ongoing surveillance and reinterventions. 53 In real‐world Medicare experience, Schanzer and colleagues reported aneurysm sac enlargement in 41% of patients, particularly when EVAR was performed outside device guidelines, highlighting durability as a key determinant of long‐term value. 54 , 55

These clinical departures from the accepted, evidence‐based practices increase the overall cost of care without improvement in quality of care and potentially introducing patient harm. Viewed through a value‐based medicine lens, non–guideline‐concordant early repair and off‐label EVAR lower value by raising device and surveillance costs and exposing patients to avoidable complications without measurable gains in survival or patient‐centered outcomes.

ACHIEVING REPAIR: EVAR VERSUS OSR

Consensus exists that the goal of management is the complete and permanent exclusion of the aneurysm sac. However, the overarching goal in management of the patient with AAA must be not just the exclusion of aneurysm pathology but also to limit patient morbidity. OSR provides durable exclusion without lifelong requirement for radiologic surveillance and the attendant cumulative effects of radiation. National Medicare registries suggest that 80% of AAA are treated with EVAR. A few factors are responsible for this clinical pattern including (1) the specialty of the treating physician and (2) declining levels of clinical exposure to OSR of AAA for surgical trainees and practicing vascular surgeons. 56

Currently, OSR may be preferred for (1) anatomically hostile necks (length, thrombus, angulation, calcium), (2) younger age in combination with favorable comorbidity risk profile, (3) patient preference, and (4) when endovascular resources are lacking. It carries higher perioperative morbidity and longer recovery, yet long‐term aneurysm associated reintervention rates are lower than EVAR and graft durability is proven beyond 15 years. A decreased risk of long‐term aneurysm‐related mortality is observed with OSR. 23 , 57 EVAR, now performed in >80% of elective US cases, 22 , 58 offers <1% perioperative mortality and median 2‐day length of stay but demands strict imaging follow‐up to detect endoleaks, migration, or aneurysmal sac enlargement. 22 Late complications erode the early survival advantage by 8 years. 24 , 50 , 51 Contemporary evolution of fenestrated and branched EVAR devices will continue to broaden the application of EVAR technology.

Index‐admission analyses show that EVAR's higher supply/device expenditures are often offset by shorter hospitalization and lower fixed (overhead) costs. In Gupta et al.'s study, EVAR's mean admission cost was $4039 (2015 US dollars) lower than OSR ($32 052 versus $36091), despite higher supply and variable costs; over the study period, EVAR costs increased by 18.5% whereas OSR costs remained stable. 22 Although the index‐admission cost differential could plausibly cover a limited amount of follow‐up imaging, longer‐term costs are driven by the broader downstream pathway, including triggered imaging and reinterventions. Systematic reviews echo this mixed picture: Nargesi et al. found EVAR more cost effective only in high‐risk patients because reintervention and surveillance narrow its advantage over time, 59 whereas a high‐volume‐center analysis by Canning et al. reported favorable cost‐per‐quality‐adjusted life year for EVAR when device prices were negotiated aggressively. 60 Ambulatory EVAR programs may further cut 1‐year costs versus inpatient care, highlighting the influence of institutional logistics. 61 In resource‐constrained settings, however, OSR remains less costly overall since EVAR's device cost outweighed its peri‐operative savings. 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 , 66

In contemporary complex EVAR (branched EVAR/fenestrated EVAR/chimney EVAR) across 5 high‐volume centers, median implantable device (hospital supply) cost was $28 470 (2024 US dollars), representing ~80% of weighted Medicare reimbursement ($35 755) and ~90% for chimney EVAR, highlighting reimbursement constraints for high‐complexity endovascular repair. 63 However, value assessment should be interpreted against the appropriate open comparator, open juxtarenal repair with suprarenal clamping and reconstruction, which may carry longer operative time, greater physiologic stress, and higher risk of perioperative renal injury with associated downstream costs. Pragmatic, episode‐based interventions have demonstrated 30.8% reductions in device cost, 92.9% lower imaging costs (including 99% less postprocessing), and a 50% drop in preprocedure admissions without length‐of‐stay penalties, offering an actionable path to improve value. 67

Clinical practice guidelines urge centers to match modality to surgeon and facility competencies: hospitals with high EVAR volumes, hybrid suites, cone‐beam CT, and access to branched or fenestrated grafts can safely extend minimally invasive options to complex anatomies, whereas high‐volume open programs achieve superior outcomes even in challenging cases. 23 This service‐standards section cites multiple population studies demonstrating lower mortality, shorter length of stay, and fewer complications when AAA repair is performed in high‐volume hospitals (≥60–100 repairs/year) and by teams with dedicated training. 68 , 69 Practically, this argues for regional centers of aortic disease excellence that provide the full spectrum of AAA care (EVAR and OSR), track volumes and outcomes by modality (EVAR versus OSR, in addition to total AAA volume), and standardize transfer pathways so that patients presenting at lower‐volume or rural hospitals can be moved efficiently when advanced resources are needed. However, regionalization can shift nontrivial travel and time costs onto patients and caregivers, especially for rural populations, those with limited transportation, and patients requiring lifelong surveillance. Value‐based regionalization should therefore include explicit access safeguards, standardized transfer pathways for urgent presentations, comanagement models that allow routine surveillance and medical optimization to occur locally when appropriate, and transparent referral criteria so that “right‐site” care does not become a barrier to timely care. 69

The VQI registry can provide the data that identifies such regional centers. In many instances, informal referral patterns regionalized centers have organically developed by clinical natural selection. These institutions should function with the VBM rubric to deliver high‐quality long‐term clinical outcomes through multimodality treatment, while exercising cost stewardship. Linking VQI procedure records to Medicare claims has already boosted reintervention detection to 92%, giving regional centers a reliable benchmark for quality reporting (6% at 1 year, 16% at 3 years) and a concrete trigger for value‐improvement cycles. 70

PROCEDURAL CODING AND PAYER REIMBURSEMENT

We have purposely limited the discussion of VBM in this article to the general category of AAA as the most commonly and broadly treated aortic pathology. Endovascular treatment of thoracoabdominal and aortic arch aneurysmal pathology introduces an additional layer of complexity to this discussion. Greenberg et al. described the early experience with fenestrated and branched aortic repair, much of which was done within the confines of physician sponsored investigational device exemption trials. 71 Contemporary published data confirm the relative clinical outcomes benefit of endovascular therapy for descending thoracic aneurysms. 72 , 73 The clinical availability of arch and thoracoabdominal branched and fenestrated devices have ushered in another wave of device iteration that has yet to be definitively analyzed from a comparative device cost‐clinical outcomes perspective. Early efforts by Brinster et al. to subject complex endovascular aortic aneurysm repair to fiscal scrutiny suggests that current Medicare reimbursement levels are inadequate to offset procedural costs. 63 , 74

In 2015, the US CMS reclassified EVAR within the Medicare Severity‐Diagnosis Related Group (MS‐DRG) framework, retiring DRGs 237/238 and creating more specific aortic/assist DRGs. 75 CMS projected increased hospital reimbursement following this reclassification, ∼15% for noncomplex EVAR and 24% for complex EVAR. 74 , 75 The transition to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10; October 2015) and subsequent Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) updates differentiated OSR from EVAR with much greater precision. The transition to ICD‐10 and subsequent CPT updates also differentiated open repair from EVAR with greater precision. Notably, the CPT code set for endovascular infrarenal AAA repair was overhauled in 2018, introducing anatomy‐based EVAR repair CPT codes 34701–34708 and adjunct/extension endograft CPT codes 34709–34716. 76 These codes distinguish, for example, aorto‐bi‐iliac grafts from aorto‐unilateral grafts and even flag ruptured versus nonruptured aneurysm repairs. Such enhancements ensure that resource‐intensive cases (eg, fenestrated or branched EVARs and emergency rupture repairs) are assigned appropriately higher reimbursement rates, aligning payment with the procedure's complexity and resource use—reflecting the true costs and risks involved, rather than being constrained by earlier one‐size‐fits‐all codes.

Under Medicare's MS‐DRG system, documentation of major comorbidities or complications can shift AAA repair hospitalizations into a higher‐weighted DRG with higher payment (eg, post‐2015 EVAR MS‐DRG 268 with major comorbidities or complications versus 269 without). A single‐center review of elective infrarenal EVARs performed from 2010 to 2015 found a 9.6% miscoding rate, with all miscoded cases reflecting down‐coding. Patients who met criteria for MS‐DRG 237 but were assigned MS‐DRG 238 accounted for a meaningful “lost billing opportunity,” and the cumulative lost billing opportunity across all miscoded encounters was $587 799 (US dollars 2015). 64 Undercoding is likely underrecognized across institutions and represents a practical target for audit‐feedback and documentation/coding process improvement. 77

Commercial insurers have likewise embraced more granular coding in their payment contracts, often mirroring CMS's code definitions and reimbursement logic. Hospitals that fully document the complexity of an AAA repair can negotiate higher case rates or invoke outlier payments for extraordinarily costly cases, improving cost recovery. In addition, the integration of value‐based purchasing metrics into reimbursement frameworks has made meticulous coding even more critical. 78 Accurate procedural and diagnostic coding feed these programs by enabling proper risk adjustment: ensuring hospitals are judged (and paid) in light of their patients' acuity rather than raw outcomes alone.

SURVEILLANCE AFTER REPAIR AND TERTIARY PREVENTION

Surveillance after AAA repair must detect occult complications including endoleak, graft migration, aneurysm sac expansion, while minimizing costs, radiation exposure, and patient burden. 15 , 33 , 40 , 79 Specialty society guidelines recommend CTA within 30 days of EVAR to establish a postimplant baseline, followed by contrast‐enhanced CT or duplex ultrasound at 12 months and then ongoing surveillance thereafter. 33 , 40 , 80 In contemporary guidance, long‐term follow‐up can be risk‐adapted, with duplex‐first surveillance in selected low‐risk phenotypes and periodic cross‐sectional imaging (eg, a late 5‐year CTA in some pathways) to reassess for delayed complications. 81 , 82 In contrast, OSR carries a lower late‐failure risk, and the 2024 ESVS guidelines endorse deferred imaging until 5 years post repair for asymptomatic patients, reflecting the durability of open grafts. 15 , 23 , 40

Cumulative radiation from annual CTA can exceed 50 mSv over a decade, and repeated contrast studies drive substantial downstream costs. 41 We consider duplex‐only surveillance appropriate only after ≥5 mm sac regression at ~12 months and no evidence of endoleak with an adequate‐quality duplex examination with stable or improving sac diameter. Exceptions where routine CTA (or magnetic resonance angiography/contrast‐enhanced ultrasound when contrast is undesirable) should be maintained include inability to obtain high‐quality and reliable ultrasound, off‐instructions for use implantation/hostile neck anatomy, history of endoleak, complex EVAR constructs (eg, fenestrated/branched/chimney), or prior reintervention. Alternating use of noncontrast CT to validated AAA size every 2 to 3 years mitigates exposure radiation and contrast dye. 83

Escalation triggers that warrant prompt cross‐sectional imaging include any sac enlargement (eg, ≥5 mm increase), new/suspected endoleak, equivocal/limited duplex windows, new symptoms, or findings suggesting graft/limb complication (migration, kinking, stenosis/occlusion) duplex examination. 40 , 52 , 75 , 76 , 77 Early aneurysm sac shrinkage, predicts a low risk of late complications and identifies patients in whom duplex‐only surveillance may safely replace routine CTA. 84 , 85 The overuse of imaging within this time frame or enhanced frequency of imaging add significantly to the overall expense of care. 38

Endoleak rates (all types) are reported to occur at a rate of up to 50% post EVAR placement. 86 Interventions are reserved for those patients experiencing aneurysm sac progression. As mentioned, increased rates of aneurysmal sac progression are seen in patients undergoing off‐instructions for use application of EVAR. 54 Various approaches to endoleak management are customized to the endoleak type and have been described elsewhere. 87 The need for additional imaging and associated interventions for endoleaks adds significantly to overall costs and diminishes patient experience. 26 , 59 , 86 , 87

Recent efforts to improve long‐term outcome tracking have demonstrated the value of integrating registry and administrative data. 70 Despite this, the detection of EVAR failure is hindered by high rates of patients lost to follow‐up postprocedurally over time. 88 Strategies to better educate patients on the necessity of lifelong surveillance have had limited success 89 and strategies aimed at using the electronic medical record have not had uniform success. 21 , 88

In keeping with a VBM approach, emerging AI‐enabled surveillance tools can automate aneurysm sac segmentation and trend diameter/volume on serial CT to identify outlier growth trajectories and support risk‐stratified follow‐up, while preserving clinician oversight. 90 As currently studied, these systems are best positioned as human‐in‐the‐loop triage: they can prepopulate measurements, highlight interval change beyond prespecified thresholds, and route “high‐risk” studies (eg, suspected endoleak features or rapid sac expansion) for expedited review rather than replacing radiologist interpretation. Practical performance expectations should be reported explicitly (eg, segmentation agreement/error, reliability of interval‐change detection, and, when endoleak detection is targeted, sensitivity with a conservative false‐negative profile), and implementation should include feedback to monitor drift across scanners, protocols, artifacts/metal, and uncommon anatomies. Limitations include retrospective training/validation, imperfect generalizability, and potential bias if training data underrepresent key subgroups; therefore, prospective and multicenter validation remains important before widespread deescalation decisions are automated. 82 , 84 , 89 , 90 , 91

For those patients who have continued progression in spite of appropriate attempts to address endoleaks, OSR with partial or complete EVAR explant may be necessary. We and others have described the technical approach and outcomes associated with EVAR explant. 92 The morbidity and mortality associated with explants exceed that of OSR performed for index pathology representing the importance of selecting appropriate therapy up front, that which is most likely to result in the definitive exclusion of the aneurysm sac. 93

IMPLICATIONS FOR VALUE‐BASED MEDICINE IN AAA MANAGEMENT

Value‐based analysis is focused on the overall quality to expenditure comparative analysis through the time continuum of the patients care from diagnosis to death. Devise‐based procedural intervention and imaging use represents a significant contribution to both variables. Thoughtful therapeutic selection (EVAR versus OSR) that definitively manages the index aortic pathology and minimizes reintervention ultimately determines a favorable value‐based index. Multiple peer‐reviewed publications have demonstrated the direct relationship of hospital and surgeon volume with improved perioperative survival following OSR. 94

As we previously published elsewhere, 19 complete adoption of a value‐based approach to AAA management is incented by health care system participation in shared savings or risk‐based payor contracts. These agreements align payment incentives for individual health care providers/groups along with those of the integrated health care systems within which those providers work. To date, the employment status of the individual provider (employed or independent) has not been shown to correlate with reduced procedural expenditure.

In such models, those savings created by the efficient delivery of care (eg, adherence to minimal/appropriate imaging for surveillance) paired with the long‐term savings of the appropriately timed and selected aortic intervention modality that leads to the lowest postprocedural expenditure will lead to the highest value‐based index outcome. 19 Here, we use “value‐based index outcome” to denote an operational composite end point that integrates (1) patient‐centered clinical outcomes (eg, survival, rupture prevention, complications), (2) guideline‐concordant process measures (eg, risk‐stratified surveillance with appropriate imaging modality), and (3) downstream use and spending over a prespecified episode‐of‐care horizon. The novelty is not the concept of value itself, but the explicit translation of AAA care into measurable, episode‐based targets that can be used for benchmarking and payment alignment across stakeholders.

Contemporary AAA management possesses nonunified components of VBM: regionalization, quality accountable payment models, cost transparency, and data‐driven precision. A practical summary of these value‐based levers in AAA care is shown in Figure 3. However, these elements are poorly defined and writ large unevenly adopted. Shifting repair of complex aortic pathology (aortic pathology‐specific, ie, OSR, branched EVAR/fenestrated EVAR/chimney EVAR) to high‐volume hospitals has probably reduced perioperative mortality by roughly one quarter, largely through greater multidisciplinary expertise, standardized clinical pathways as well as selective and patient‐tailored offering of the array of repair options. Many centers now participate in CMS episode‐based reimbursement programs, reflecting a broader shift toward accountability for total episode spending and quality outcomes rather than isolated services. 95 , 96

Although not AAA specific, CMS's Transforming Episode Accountability Model illustrates how bundled accountability can be operationalized: the episode is initiated by an anchor hospitalization or outpatient procedure and extends through 30 days after discharge/procedure, encompassing most Medicare Part A and B items and services. Episodes are attributed to the hospital where the anchor event occurs, requiring coordination across specialties involved in periprocedural and postacute care. Target prices are risk‐adjusted at the beneficiary level (age, comorbidity burden using hierarchical condition category counts, and social‐risk measures such as Area Deprivation Index and dual‐eligibility/low‐income subsidy status) and at the hospital level (bed size and safety‐net status), with reconciliation adjusted for quality performance. 24 , 97 , 98 , 99

Implant expense is the single largest modifiable cost driver in EVAR. A multicenter study showed the mean stent‐graft price ranged from $8000 to $28 000 (2014 US dollar) for standard cases, despite identical clinical indications. 65 Centers that disclose vendor pricing and link device choice to episode budgets have reported measurable savings without outcome penalties. 67 To avoid perverse incentives (eg, discouraging appropriate treatment of complex anatomy), pricing transparency should be paired with safeguards: cost dashboards should be reported with anatomic and procedural complexity adjustment (standard EVAR versus complex EVAR constructs) and reviewed at the program level rather than as punitive, individual‐surgeon scorecards. 65 , 74 An explicit exception pathway should allow higher‐cost devices when justified by anatomy (eg, hostile neck/off‐instructions for use features, inadequate access, need for fenestrated/branched/chimney configurations), documented using published standardized anatomic criteria and adjudicated through a multidisciplinary aortic conference or value‐analysis process. 23 , 63 Finally, value initiatives should continuously monitor prespecified quality metrics (30‐day mortality, complications, readmissions, endoleak/sac expansion, and reintervention) to confirm that savings are not achieved at the expense of outcomes or equitable access. 67 , 70

To translate VBM into practice, we operationalize a 5‐point implementation roadmap of regionalization (pathology specific, volume‐outcome standards), episode‐based payments (align incentives to total cost and quality), cost transparency (curb unwarranted price variation), predictive analytics including AI‐enabled post EVAR surveillance (risk‐stratified follow‐up), and collaborative device purchasing models (gainsharing/value analysis) such as group purchasing organizations. Applied together, these levers can shift AAA care toward a more consistent, equitable, and sustainable model.

CONCLUSIONS

AAA care is at an inflection point: elective repair averts rupture and prolongs life, yet climbing device costs, variable imaging habits, and fragmented care sap system value. A value‐based approach—regional centers of excellence, transparent bundled pricing, AI‐guided surveillance, and shared‐savings contracts—can realign incentives so that every scan, stent‐graft, and clinical interaction serves the ultimate goals of rupture prevention, long‐term survival, and cost‐efficient lifelong follow‐up. By integrating these implementation components, vascular teams can deliver safer, more equitable, and economically sustainable AAA management.
