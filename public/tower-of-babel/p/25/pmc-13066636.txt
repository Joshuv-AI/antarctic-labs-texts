Integration of circulating tumor DNA data enhances lung cancer prediction in patients with COPD
Authors: Soojin Cha, Sun Hye Shin, Seung-Ho Shin, Ho Yun Lee, Yeon Jeong Kim, Donghyun Park, Kyung Yeon Han, You Jin Oh, Woong-Yang Park, Myung-Ju Ahn, Hojoong Kim, Hong-Hee Won, Hye Yun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are at increased risk of lung cancer, and the identification of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) mutations may help detect lung cancer in them. However, whether the addition of ctDNA information improves lung cancer prediction remains to be elucidated. Blood samples from 236 patients with COPD (119 with lung cancer and 117 without lung cancer) were genotyped using targeted deep sequencing. With 40 clinical and genomic/molecular variables, nine machine learning (ML) models to predict lung cancer were constructed. Prediction performances were compared between models with clinical variables only and those with additional genomic/molecular variables. Three clinical variables (smoking amount, C-reactive protein [CRP], COPD symptom burden) and four genomic/molecular variables (ctDNA mutation detected, lung cancer driver gene, max variant allele frequency, and median duplex) were significantly associated with lung cancer (P < 0.05). Notably, four of the nine ML models, incorporating clinical and genomic/molecular variables, outperformed models using clinical variables alone (AUC of the best models = 0.729 vs. 0.620, P < 0.05). Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a major global health burden, contributing to 3.2 million deaths worldwide each year1. With aging, patients with COPD frequently present with various comorbidities, of which lung cancer being the major cause of death, particularly in patients with mild to moderate COPD2,3. Given the well-established association between COPD and lung cancer development, independent of smoking exposure4–6, the importance of implementing lung cancer screening in patients with COPD has been increasingly emphasized7,8. Indeed, patients with mild to moderate COPD who underwent lung cancer screening showed a significantly decreased lung cancer mortality9. However, confirming a diagnosis of lung cancer in certain COPD patients remains challenging, particularly in those with advanced COPD with multiple comorbidities and emphysema surrounding a nodule or mass. These factors often elevate the risk of complications from invasive diagnostic procedures10.

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) is a non-encapsulating DNA in the peripheral blood, which provide an opportunity for cancer detection, including lung cancer11. However, meta-analyses have demonstrated pooled specificity and sensitivity of approximately 80% for the diagnostic performance of cfDNA in lung cancer screening, suggesting that cfDNA alone is not recommended as a screening tool due to its relatively limited discriminative power12–14. In addition, cfDNA level increases proportionally with inflammation and cellular damage. Subsequently, they are often elevated in COPD patients, particularly during exacerbations15. While, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis is a potential approach for the detection of lung cancer as a less invasive method from blood sampling, which is widely known that the genomic alterations of ctDNA are highly specific to those of the original tumor, including lung cancer. However, ctDNA analysis still had the relatively low sensitivity for the diagnosis of lung cancer16 and there was no available study to integrate ctDNA data and clinical factors to predict lung cancer diagnosis in COPD patients.

Thus, we aimed to examine the specific characteristics of COPD patients with and without lung cancer by comparing clinical and genomic/molecular features. Additionally, we developed nine machine learning (ML) models to predict lung cancer among COPD patients and identified the variables that contribute to predicting lung cancer in COPD patients.

Methods

Sample collection and library preparation

We analyzed samples from patients with COPD prospectively enrolled at a single referral hospital between October 2017 and September 2020 (Fig. 1)16. Inclusion criteria were spirometry-defined COPD (post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 s [FEV1]/forced vital capacity [FVC] < 0.7) and age ≥ 40 years. Patients with a history of other malignancy were excluded. For this study, we included 255 patients with moderate COPD (50% pred ≤ FEV1 < 80% pred), who had coexisting lung cancer (n = 123) or not (n = 132). According to the result of the previous study that showed an inverse relationship between emphysema and the rate of ctDNA detection in this cohort16, we excluded 16 patients with extremely severe emphysema (emphysema index ≥ 30%). After excluding three patients with missing clinical variables, final analysis was performed in 236 patients (119 with lung cancer and 117 without lung cancer). In the COPD patients with lung cancer group, the presence of other malignancies was systematically excluded using chest computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography–computed tomography, and brain magnetic resonance imaging. In the COPD patients without lung cancer, the absence of lung cancer or premalignant lung lesions was confirmed by using chest CT. This study was approved from the IRB SMC 2017–08-128. All participants of our study provided informed consent for blood collection and publication of research results. For library preparation, whole blood was collected in BCT tubes, and plasma was separated by sequential centrifugation and stored at − 80 °C. cfDNA was extracted from plasma using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen, Santa Clarita, CA, USA), while genomic DNA (gDNA) was isolated from blood cells using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Santa Clarita, CA, USA). gDNA was fragmented to 150–200 bp using sonication (Covaris S2; Covaris Inc., Woburn, MA, USA). Library preparation included end-repair and A-tailing steps, followed by overnight adapter ligation at 4 °C using the KAPA Hyper Prep Kit (Kapa Biosystems, Woburn, MA, USA). Hybrid capture enrichment was subsequently performed using three customized gene panels (LungCancer v1, LiquidSCAN v2-PanCancer, and IVD v1.0; GENINUS, Korea). Detailed protocols are described in our previous study16.

Overview of the workflow. Moderate COPD patients with (n = 119) and without lung cancer (n = 117) were analyzed using nine machine learning models within a five-fold cross-validation framework. Model performances based on clinical variables alone versus combined clinical and genomic/molecular variables were compared using permutation tests (F1, precision, sensitivity, and specificity) and DeLong’s test with bootstrapping (AUC). The best model was identified based on the highest F1 score among models with sensitivity > 0.6.

NGS sequence data processing

Sequencing data processing followed the pipeline described in our previous studies16,17. Briefly, sequencing reads were aligned to the hg19 reference genome using BWA-mem, processed with GATK and SAMtools for base quality recalibration and sorting, grouped into UMI families using Picard, and collapsed with UMI-based error suppression. Somatic SNVs identified from the union of 24 regions covered by the three sequencing panels were annotated using VEP and visualized by maftools18,19. During variant calling, we applied filters based on base quality (Phred score ≥ Q30) and sequencing depth (≥ 500 ×) to reduce false-positive calls at low-quality positions. In addition, germline variants and background noise were removed by leveraging matched WBC gDNA16.

Lung cancer prediction using nine machine learning algorithms

Features

Four categories of variables—demographic, clinical, genomic, and molecular—were considered as candidate features for model construction. Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, dyspnea measured with modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) grade, symptom burden measured with COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score, post-bronchodilator FVC (% pred), post-bronchodilator FEV1 (% pred), white blood cell (WBC) count, eosinophil percentage, and C-reactive protein (CRP). For genomic variables, we included the ctDNA mutation status (yes or no), mutation status of 24 ctDNA genes, and the presence of lung cancer driver genes (yes or no) based on the TCGA driver gene list for lung squamous cell carcinoma and lung adenocarcinoma20. Among 35 unique driver genes, 13 overlapping genes with our gene list were used in our analysis. Molecular variables comprised cfDNA amount, maximum variant allele frequency (VAF), and median duplex depth.

Model scheme

To predict the presence of lung cancer among COPD patients, nine ML models were constructed using 40 feature candidates and 236 samples (119 patients with lung cancer and 117 patients without lung cancer, Fig. 1). The nine ML models included logistic regression, regularized regression (ridge, LASSO, and elastic net), linear support vector machine (SVM [L]), radial support vector machine (SVM [R]), random forest (RF), gradient boosting model (GBM), and extreme gradient boosting model (XGBoost), comprising five linear models and four non-linear models. The samples were split into a training set and a test set in a 4:1 ratio and analyzed within a fivefold cross-validation framework. For each training set, variables with non-zero variance were used as features of the ML models. In the preprocessing step, the features were centered and scaled, and hyperparameters were optimized in the training set within a fivefold cross-validation framework using the caret R package. Detailed parameters are described in the Supplementary Methods. Spearman’s rho was used to evaluate correlations among features included in the model.

Evaluating and selecting the best predictive model

We evaluated the models using various performance metrics, including the area under the curve (AUC), F1 score, precision, sensitivity, and specificity in the test set. To compare model performance between models incorporating both clinical and molecular/genomic variables and those using only clinical variables, we compared average performance across five folds using permutation tests (n = 100,000) for each metric. For a more precise comparison of AUCs, we conducted DeLong’s test separately for each fold and combined the resulting p-values using the Fisher’s method. In addition, 95% confidence intervals for AUC differences were estimated via bootstrapping with 1000 resamples. This fold-wise approach accounts for cross-validation variability and provides robust statistical inference for AUC differences. Statistical significance was defined as p-value < 0.05. Because sensitivity is a critical factor in clinical prediction models, we prioritized models that showed a statistically significant improvement in sensitivity and F1 score among those with sensitivity above 0.6. SVM (R) was selected as the best prediction model and used for subsequent analyses.

Variable importance

To identify important features for predicting lung cancer in COPD patients, variable importance was computed for each cross-validation fold using the varImp function from the caret package. The mean and standard deviation of importance scores across folds were then used to prioritize features.

Sensitivity analyses

To address potential confounding from correlated variables (Spearman’s ρ > 0.8), we performed sensitivity analyses by building models with only one variable from each correlated group, including median duplex, maximum VAF, ctDNA mutation status, and lung cancer driver genes. To validate that the performance improvement was not caused by the large number of features, we developed a LASSO-based reduced model. After selecting key features via LASSO, we evaluated the model’s performance using nine different ML classifiers to confirm the added value of genomic data over clinical variables alone. Comparison of model performances was conducted using DeLong’s test for AUC and permutation test for other metrics consistent with the original model analysis.

Results

Characteristics of COPD patients with and without lung cancer

A total of 236 COPD patients were analyzed, comprising 119 with lung cancer and 117 without lung cancer. Most of the patients were male (> 90%) in both groups (Table 1). Among COPD patients with lung cancer, 40.3% had stage I lung cancer, and 53.8% and 32.8% had squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma histology, respectively. Compared with COPD patients without lung cancer, those with lung cancer were more likely to have a smoking history of ≥ 30 pack-years and elevated CRP levels, but had less symptom burden of COPD, with a lower proportion having a total CAT score ≥10.

Although the cfDNA amount was similar in both groups, ctDNA mutations (26.1% vs. 3.4%, P < 0.0001) and lung cancer driver genes (23.5% vs. 2.6%, P < 0.0001) were detected at a significantly higher frequency in COPD patients with lung cancer than those without lung cancer, indicating that our ctDNA mutation detection pipeline has high specificity for detecting lung cancer mutations. In terms of molecular characteristics, COPD patients with lung cancer were more likely to have higher maximum VAF, and duplex support compared to those without lung cancer (Table 1). For ctDNA mutations, we used 24 genes (SNVs only) as feature candidates that were present in at least one of our samples (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S1). Among these genes, 13 genes were reported as lung cancer driver genes in TCGA data20. Most of the SNVs were C > T transversions, and TP53 was the most frequently mutated gene, consistent with previous studies on the lung cancer genome20,21.

Improvement in model performance by adding genomic/molecular features to clinical features

We developed nine ML models to predict lung cancer among COPD patients using 39 feature candidates within a five-fold cross-validation framework (Fig. 1). Among the nine ML models, SVM (R) model was selected as the best performing model, based on its high sensitivity (0.639) and F1 score (0.65, Fig. 3A, Table 2 and Supplementary Table S2). We compared the performance of the SVM (R) model using only clinical variables and that of the model incorporating additional genomic/molecular features. When genomic/molecular features were added to the clinical variables, model performance was significantly improved in sensitivity (from 0.521 to 0.639), F1 score (from 0.552 to 0.651), and AUC (from 0.620 to 0.729, all P < 0.05, Table 2, Fig. 3A,B). Two other non-linear models (XGBoost and GBM) also demonstrated significant improvements in AUC when genomic/molecular variables were added (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S2).

Given the strong correlations observed among four variables (ctDNA mutation status, median duplex, max VAF, and lung cancer driver) with Spearman’s rho > 0.8 (Supplementary Fig. S1), we conducted a sensitivity analysis by constructing models that included only one of these variables at a time and compared their performances with that of the original model. The models showed comparable AUCs, indicating that these correlated variables did not significantly affect the overall model performance (Supplementary Table S3). Analysis of the LASSO-based reduced model showed that integrating genomic/molecular variables consistently enhanced the predictive trend across all nine algorithms (paired Wilcoxon P = 0.0039). Although individual statistical significance was reached only in the Random Forest model, this universal upward trend suggests that such data provides complementary predictive value, even within a minimal feature set (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Figure S2).

Feature importance of clinical and genomic/molecular variables in predicting lung cancer in patients with COPD

Subsequently, we examined which features contributed to the performance of models with clinical variables alone and those with both clinical and genomic/molecular variables. In models with clinical variables only, CRP, CAT score, WBC count, eosinophil (%), and smoking amount were selected as important predictors in the SVM model. In contrast, in the models including both clinical and genomic/molecular variables, duplex-related features (median duplex, max VAF, ctDNA mutation status, and lung cancer driver), and mutations in TP53 were important variables (Fig. 3C).

Discussion

In this study, we compared 39 clinical and genomic/molecular variables in 236 COPD patients with and without lung cancer and constructed nine ML models to predict lung cancer. Among the nine models, SVM (R) was selected as the best performing model based on sensitivity and F1 score. The addition of genomic/molecular variables to clinical variables significantly improved the model performance for lung cancer detection. Our data demonstrated the value of integrating genomic and molecular biomarkers from ctDNA into prediction models to enhance diagnostic accuracy for lung cancer.

Our study showed that cfDNA levels were similar between COPD patients with and without lung cancer but ctDNA analysis revealed significantly higher mutation detection rates in the lung cancer group, particularly involving known lung cancer driver genes. Additional molecular features including maximum VAF, median duplex support, and the presence of ctDNA mutations were markedly enriched in the lung cancer group, suggesting a robust performance of ctDNA mutation detection pipeline. Genomic profiling further supported this distinction. We examined ctDNA mutations of 24 genes and observed that TP53 emerged as the most frequently mutated gene in our study population, comprising lung squamous cell carcinoma (53.8%) and lung adenocarcinoma (32.8%). These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that lung cancer patients with coexisting COPD exhibit a significantly higher mutation frequency of tumor suppressor genes, including TP53, in tumor tissue compared to those without COPD22. Our study extended these findings by demonstrating similar patterns using ctDNA derived from blood, underscoring the clinical utility and specificity of ctDNA as a non-invasive biomarker for malignancy detection in COPD populations.

We next explored the clinical characteristics distinguishing COPD patients with and without lung cancer. Comparative analysis showed that COPD patients with lung cancer were more likely to have a heavy smoking history (≥ 30 years), elevated CRP levels, and a lower symptom burden, as indicated by a reduced proportion with CAT scores ≥ 10. A history of long-term smoking is a well-established risk factor for the development of lung cancer in patients with COPD6,23. Notably, CRP levels showed the highest feature importance among clinical variables. CRP elevation reflects the chronic systemic inflammation that promotes carcinogenesis in susceptible COPD patients24, and is linked to higher lung cancer risk, particularly for lung squamous cell carcinoma25. Given that our study population with underlying COPD is enriched with squamous cell carcinoma than lung cancer patients in general26, this may also account for the observed association. Interestingly, coexisting lung cancer was associated with a lower symptom burden of COPD in our dataset. This finding may be explained by the composition of the study population: patients with newly diagnosed lung cancer predominantly had mild to moderate COPD27, whereas the control group without lung cancer comprised individuals who were already under regular follow-up for COPD at a tertiary referral hospital. Accordingly, the greater symptom burden observed in the non-cancer group likely reflects the underlying severity of COPD itself.

In addition to these clinical features, the integration of genomic data derived from the blood into predictive models shows strong potential to further improve diagnostic accuracy for identifying lung cancer among COPD patients. Feature importance analysis further revealed a shift in dominant predictors following the incorporation of molecular data. While smoking history, CRP, and CAT score were key contributors in models based solely on clinical variables, TP53 mutation status and duplex-related features emerged as the most influential variables in the integrated models. This shift underscores a potential paradigm change in lung cancer risk assessment in COPD, from reliance on conventional clinical biomarkers to the adoption of precision molecular diagnostics.

To evaluate whether adding genomic/molecular information improves lung cancer prediction, we compared the predictive performance of nine ML models using only clinical variables with that of nine models incorporating both clinical and genomic/molecular variables. Among the models with sensitivity greater than 0.6, the SVM (R) model showed the highest sensitivity and F1 score and was selected as the best model (AUC = 0.729), significantly outperforming the clinical-only model. Moreover, across all the nine models, three of the four non-linear models (SVM (R), XGBoost, and GBM) demonstrated significant improvements when genomic/molecular variables were added, whereas linear models did not, suggesting that genomic/molecular variables contribute to prediction mainly through non-linear relationships or interactions with clinical variables rather than simple additive effects.

Regarding the clinical positioning of our model, the AUC of 0.729 suggests a distinct utility in adjudicating indeterminate pulmonary nodules rather than as a stand-alone primary screening tool. Although the sensitivity of our best model (63.9%) is lower than that of standard LDCT screening, which generally exceeds 90%, LDCT is inherently limited by a high false-positive rate. This is particularly prevalent in patients with COPD or emphysema, where inflammatory nodules often lead to diagnostic uncertainty28. Furthermore, COPD patients often have limited pulmonary reserve and face a higher risk of potentially fatal complications from invasive procedures, such as transthoracic needle aspiration or bronchoscopic biopsy10. By integrating genomic and molecular biomarkers, our model may allow clinicians to bypass high-risk diagnostic procedures for certain patients. In addition, from a cost–benefit perspective, despite the cost of NGS testing, these expenses could be potentially offset by reducing medical costs associated with frequent follow-up imaging and the management of biopsy-related complications such as pneumothorax or hemorrhage.

There are several limitations in this study. First, although we split the dataset into training and test sets and applied five-folds cross-validation, we did not perform external validation using an independent cohort. Therefore, our findings should be replicated in future research to draw more robust conclusion. Second, this study was conducted in a Korean population from a single tertiary referral center, which limits the generalizability of the results to other populations and settings. However, patient demographics and COPD-related characteristics mirror those reported in nationwide multicenter observational studies and are therefore representative of clinically diagnosed COPD patients in Korea29,30. Third, the absence of a non-COPD control group limits our ability to determine whether the observed cfDNA and ctDNA features are specific to COPD status or reflect lung cancer–related processes alone. Fourth, systemic search for other malignancy was not done in COPD patients without lung cancer, therefore, we might have missed occult cancer at the time of ctDNA sampling. During the follow-up, five patients were diagnosed with lung cancer at median 19.6 months and one patient with prostate cancer at 11 months and none of these patients had ctDNA mutation at baseline. On the other hand, there were four (3.4%) patients who had positive ctDNA mutation in COPD without lung cancer group, albeit with significantly lower VAF and duplex support than COPD with cancer group, indicating high specificity of our analysis. These patients did not develop any cancer during the median follow-up period of 7.8 months (range, 5.5–27.5). Finally, we evaluated SNVs of 24 genes using three targeted sequencing panels. ctDNA-based targeted sequencing platforms covering a larger number of genes (e.g., > 500) and capable of detecting diverse types of genomic alterations (such as SNVs, insertions/deletions, copy number variants, gene fusions, microsatellite instability, and tumor mutational burden) may offer more comprehensive predictive features. Therefore, the use of such advanced assays could potentially improve or refine the findings.

In this study, we developed nine ML models and demonstrated that the integrating ctDNA data with clinical features improved the prediction of lung cancer in patients with COPD. Given the higher incidence of lung cancer and the greater risk of complication from invasive diagnostic procedure, our findings underscore the value of incorporating ctDNA-based information into risk stratification strategies for COPD patients at high risk of lung cancer. Future studies with more diverse populations, as well as the application of comprehensive genomic platforms, are warranted to validate and extend our results.
