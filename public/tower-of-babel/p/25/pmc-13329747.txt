Evaluating the Suitability of HepaRG Cells for Regulatory Micronucleus Testing: Comparing Results for a Diverse Set of 28 Chemicals to Regulatory Accepted TK6 Cells
Authors: Ashley Allemang, Stefan Pfuhler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThere is growing interest in in vitro‐based new approach methodologies (NAMs) for assessing genotoxicity. These approaches are desirable for industries like cosmetics which are prohibited from animal testing due to regulatory restrictions, as well as “3R” considerations to avoid in vivo testing. One NAM is the adaptation of the in vitro micronucleus assay for metabolically competent human hepatic HepaRG cells. However, per OECD Test Guideline 487, the use of non‐validated cell types such as these needs to be justified based on their proficiency in the assay. To this end, we have tested 28 chemicals in HepaRG and TK6 human lymphoblast cells, a standard cell line considered validated for use in the in vitro micronucleus assay. HepaRG cells showed a high overall accuracy of 86%, compared to 75% for TK6 cells across a balanced set of expected in vivo positives and negatives across various genotoxic modes of action. The improved overall performance of HepaRG cells was predominantly based on their lower susceptibility towards compounds known to cause “false positive” responses (positive results in vitro, but are known to be negative in vivo), particularly those related to oxidative stress as demonstrated by the ToxTracker assay. The study indicates a high predictive capacity of HepaRG cells, suggesting that with further examination of chemicals requiring metabolic activation, they may be suitable for regulatory testing. These findings advocate for the incorporation of HepaRG cells into genotoxicity assessment frameworks, providing a more human‐relevant approach that has the potential to improve risk assessment outcomes.

Introduction

There is a growing interest in the application of in vitro‐based new approach methodologies (NAMs) in the assessment of genotoxicity potential. Benefits of in vitro NAMs include providing mode of action (MoA) based information not available in standard assays without additional modification and they generally have higher throughput compared to standard assays (Fortin et al. 2023). NAMs are fit‐for‐purpose and, regardless of the sector (regulatory, industry or academic research), they can be beneficial at multiple stages of risk assessment. For example, NAMs can be a cost‐effective way to screen and prioritize new ingredients before investing in more costly and time‐consuming regulatory tests. NAMs can also be used in tiered testing strategies by serving as a follow‐up step to determine the in vivo relevance of any in vitro positive genotoxicity responses encountered using the standard in vitro 2‐test battery approach assessing mutagenicity, clastogenicity and aneugenicity (Pfuhler et al. 2010). Depending on the NAM used, the MoA information provided can help determine whether an observed positive response is likely to be the result of a direct or indirect genotoxicity mode of action, a critical consideration in risk assessment (Dearfield and Moore 2005; Kirkland, et al. 2007a; Kirkland, et al. 2007b). For the purposes of this paper, a direct acting genotoxicant is considered one that has the capacity to enter the nucleus of a cell and directly interact with DNA molecules of an organism, which may include reactive metabolites of the chemical in question. An indirect genotoxicant does not directly interact with a DNA molecule; rather it induces genotoxicity via other mechanisms, such as inhibition of topoisomerase II, production of cellular reactive oxygen species, and inhibition of DNA synthesis (Sasaki et al. 2020). For industries such as cosmetics that are prohibited from in vivo follow‐up testing due to regulatory requirements like the 7th amendment to the Cosmetics Directive (EU 2003), NAMs are often the only option to de‐risk an ingredient of interest by demonstrating that the observed positive response in an in vitro assay is relevant to in vivo scenarios, or may be due to an indirect genotoxicity MoA rather than the result of direct interaction with the DNA.

We have previously demonstrated that a fit‐for‐purpose approach combining different NAMS, including the in vitro MicroFlow assay to assess micronuclei (MN) in human TK6 lymphoblast cells and the ToxTracker assay for mechanistic insight can improve predictive capacity to detect genotoxins (Allemang et al. 2021). We now aimed to explore whether the use of other human‐relevant cell types can further improve these results. While TK6 cells are considered an improvement over p53 deficient rodent cell lines like Chinese hamster ovary and lung cells (CHO and V79), they still require exogenous sources of metabolism such as rat liver S9 and can be prone to false positive responses due to the induction of indirect genotoxicity that may not be relevant to human exposure situations, including those relating to an oxidative stress MoA.

The metabolically competent human hepatic cell line HepaRG is increasingly being used in a variety of in vitro genotoxicity endpoints, as described in the recent review by Guo and colleagues (Guo et al. 2024). The use of inherently metabolically active cells removes the need for exogenous rat or mouse liver S9, which is less reflective of human metabolism as it underestimates phase II metabolism (Guengerich et al. 1982; Zeller and Pfuhler 2014). There are also practical considerations such as avoidance of S9‐related increases in cytotoxicity during treatment and the societal and regulatory pressure for reduction in animal use. HepaRG cells also compare more favorably to primary human hepatocytes than HepG2 cells, another common metabolically competent human liver cell line used in genotoxicity testing, as demonstrated by a number of studies either measuring CYP450 activity or gene expression profiles (Hart et al. 2010; Jennen et al. 2010; Jetten et al. 2013; Seo et al. 2019). However, the use of HepaRG cells for regulatory purposes is currently limited to weight of evidence (WOE) based assessments, as the OECD Test Guideline 487 for the In vitro Mammalian Cell Micronucleus Test specifies that the use of non‐validated cell types (such as HepaRG) needs to be justified based on their demonstrated performance in the assay (OECD 2016). While most genotoxicity research employing HepaRG cells to date has unsurprisingly focused on compound classes requiring metabolic activation such as pyrrolizidine alkaloids (Allemang et al. 2018; Louisse et al. 2019) and nitrosamines (Seo et al. 2025, 2023), it is also of interest to demonstrate the general suitability of these cells for detecting a range of MoA's. Current OECD guideline recommendations require testing both with and without metabolic activation and the use of HepaRG cells would eliminate the need for the metabolic activation exposure condition, resulting in both time and cost savings, in addition to its above‐mentioned increased human relevance compared to rodent liver S9. Compounds requiring metabolic activation were not selected for this study because the intention was to focus on the question of whether such an approach would retain a high sensitivity for direct‐acting genotoxins. This is of particular importance as the lack of an ‘without S9’ condition may trigger concerns of missing so‐called ‘site‐of‐first‐contact’ genotoxins. HepaRG cells have successfully been adapted to a number of genotoxicity and DNA damage assays such as the micronucleus (MN) and comet assays (Allemang et al. 2018; Hégarat et al. 2014; Recio et al. 2023, 2025), gamma H2AX (Quesnot et al. 2016), MultiFlow DNA damage assay (Guo et al. 2020; Seo et al. 2023) and TGx‐DDI DNA damage and GENOMARK biomarkers (Buick et al. 2020, 2021; Thienpont et al. 2024), as well as to more recently developed technologies such as error corrected sequencing (Barutcu et al. 2025; Seo et al. 2024).

In the work presented here, we employ the high throughput flow cytometric micronucleus assay to compare in vitro MN results in HepaRG cells to results in regulatory accepted TK6 cells. A total of 28 compounds were examined including non‐genotoxicants, direct genotoxicants and indirect genotoxicants representing a variety of MoAs. A majority of the genotoxicants evaluated represent oxidative stress‐mediated genetox MoAs, a common indirect genotoxicity MoA which we have also evaluated using a pathway analysis, Connectivity Mapping, and transcriptional benchmark dosing‐based framework (De Abrew et al. 2025). Compounds were also examined using the ToxTracker assay, which provides mechanistic information and was included to aid our understanding of possible differences observed between the two cell types. Specifically, this assay uses a panel of GFP‐tagged mouse embryonic stem cell lines to evaluate a number of parameters such as DNA damage, oxidative stress, protein damage, and p53 that are useful not only for identifying genotoxicants versus non‐genotoxicants, but also establishing whether observed responses are likely the result of direct or indirect modes of action (Hendriks et al. 2012, 2016). Its recent interlaboratory validation further demonstrated that the Toxtracker assay has high sensitivity and specificity to predict in vivo genotoxicants (Hendriks et al. 2024), and it is increasingly being used in risk assessment contexts (Allemang et al. 2022a; Allemang et al. 2022b; Boisvert et al. 2023; Brown et al. 2019; Derr et al. 2022; Thakkar et al. 2023). TK6 in vitro MN and ToxTracker assay (‐S9 only) results for 18 of the 28 compounds tested here have previously been reported in (Allemang et al. 2021), however, these results are presented again here to enable direct comparison with the newly generated results in HepaRG cells.

A variety of exposure durations have been reported for HepaRG cells in different endpoints including single and repeated exposures for MN, comet, and gamma H2AX (Allemang et al. 2018; Jossé et al. 2011; Quesnot et al. 2016; Seo et al. 2019). We therefore chose to also compare two different exposure protocols (single exposure versus three repeated exposures spaced 24 h apart) to evaluate the impact of extended exposures on the predictive capacity of the HepaRG MN assay. Ultimately, the goal of the work presented below is to go beyond WOE approaches and explore the usefulness of HepaRG cells for regulatory assays such as the in vitro micronucleus assay.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

All chemicals were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO) except for sodium arsenite, which was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc. (Dallas, TX) and all were tested within their specified re‐test/expiration dates. Stock solutions of each chemical were prepared immediately prior to use in either DMSO or water. Each compound was tested in triplicate in a range of 5–7 evenly spaced concentrations. Per OECD TG487, the top concentration tested in the in vitro MN experiments was 10 mM, unless limited by solubility or cytotoxicity. The top concentration tested in the ToxTracker assay was 1 mM, or the limit of cytotoxicity, per standard protocols for that assay. Cell Culture & Micronucleus Assessment

TK6 cells (ATCC CRL‐8015) were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2 and 95% humidity in RPMI 1640 with 10% heat‐inactivated FBS and 1% pen‐strep. Cells were maintained at or below 1 × 106 cells/ml per ATCC specifications. Cells were treated in triplicate wells in 96‐well plates using a 4‐h exposure followed by a 20‐h recovery period as described in Allemang et al. (2021). The final solvent concentration in TK6 cell experiments was limited to 0.5% (v/v) DMSO or water, although in a few isolated cases 1% DMSO or 10% water was used at a top concentration (in accordance with OECD TG487). As indicated in Table 1, TK6 MN results for a subset of these compounds were previously reported in Allemang et al. (2021). Fully differentiated No‐Spin HepaRG cells (Lonza Walkersville Inc., Walkersville, MD) were cultured as described in Allemang et al. (2018). Briefly, cells were thawed and plated in 96‐well collagen‐I coated plates at the manufacturers recommended cell density and cultured for 6 days in pre‐induction toxicity medium before being treated for 24 or 72 h (fresh treatment medium every 24 h). The final DMSO concentration in HepaRG experiments was limited to 1.8%, corresponding to the amount of DMSO present in the maintenance and metabolism cell culture medium used during treatment. After treatment, cells were cultured for an additional 72 h in pre‐induction toxicity medium supplemented with 200 ng/mL human epidermal growth factor (hEGF) (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, Catalog # 72528S) to stimulate sufficient cell division during which micronuclei could form.

MN induction was measured flow cytometrically in both cell types using the Litron (Rochester, NY) in vitro Microflow method, which has been well described previously (Avlasevich et al. 2006). TK6 cells were processed using the suspension cell protocol while HepaRG cells were analyzed using the adherent cell protocol. Latex counting beads were included for both cell types to determine relative viability and > 5000 healthy nuclei were collected per sample. Samples were analyzed using high‐throughput samplers on either a BD Biosciences (San Jose, CA) FACSCanto II instrument (TK6 cells) or a Miltenyi Biotec (Gaithersburg, MD) MACSQuant 10 analyzer (HepaRG cells). All data represent the average of 3 or more replicates, ± standard deviation.

Statistical Analysis and Benchmark Dose Analysis

Data was compiled in Microsoft Excel so that ‘percent micronuclei’ and ‘relative viability’ could be calculated, and subsequent statistical analysis were performed in Graphpad Prism v8.4.3 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA). Individual doses were compared to vehicle controls using the Dunnett's Multiple Comparison test and a p value < 0.05 was considered a significant difference. In addition, benchmark dose (BMD) analysis was performed to compare responses between TK6 and HepaRG cells and the two HepaRG exposure conditions. Only the in vitro positive compounds were examined, as accurate benchmark doses cannot be calculated when no increase in micronuclei has occurred. Data at overly cytotoxic concentrations (< 40% relative cell viability) was not included in BMD analysis. MN data was converted to text files and analyzed using the PROAST Web application (RIVM, The Netherlands, version 71.1, https://proastweb.rivm.nl/) and a benchmark response (BMR) of 1 was used, representing a 100% increase over the background level (doubling of the micronucleus response over the corresponding control). A doubling of the background rates is also often used as a signal for the biological relevance of an effect when judging genotoxicity data. The lower and upper confidence interval (CI) limits of the BMD (BMDL, BMDU) resulting from the exponential and Hill models were compiled for all compounds. Results from the exponential and Hill models were similar, therefore the lowest BMDL and the highest BMDU values from each model were used for visual comparison.

ToxTracker Assay

The ToxTracker assay is a mouse embryonic stem cell‐based reporter assay which uses six different biomarker genes to identify the induction of DNA damage (Bscl2, Rtkn), oxidative stress (Srxn1, Blvrb), protein damage (Ddit3), and p53 activation (Btg2). The assay has undergone extensive validation and been demonstrated to detect in vivo genotoxicants with high sensitivity and specificity (Hendriks et al. 2024, 2012, 2016). All 28 chemicals were blinded and analyzed with and without metabolic activation (rat liver S9) by Toxys (Leiden, The Netherlands) using their standard procedures. As indicated in Table 1, ToxTracker results (‐S9) for a subset of these compounds were previously reported in Allemang et al. (2021).

Results

The purpose of this study was to compare the performance of HepaRG cells in the in vitro MN assay to TK6 cells, which are a well‐established cell line considered validated for use in regulatory guideline compliant studies. A total of 28 chemicals were examined including 5 non‐genotoxicants with known negative outcomes in both in vitro and in vivo genotoxicity assays, 9 chemicals with reports of positive outcomes in in vitro assays but negative outcomes in in vivo assays, and lastly, 14 genotoxic compounds with positive outcomes in both in vitro and in vivo assays. All compounds were also tested in the ToxTracker assay to provide MOA information to aid in interpretation of results. Detailed micronucleus and ToxTracker results are available as Supplementary Figures (Figures S1a–c, S2a–c) and summarized micronucleus data are presented in Table S1. In Vitro and in Vivo Negative Results

HepaRG cells correctly identified all 5 in vitro and in vivo negative compounds in the in vitro MN assay when tested to the limit of solubility or cytotoxicity in either exposure condition (Table 2). TK6 cells, however, misidentified sodium diclofenac, which induced a statistically significant increase in MN in TK6 cells at the highest concentration tested which did not exceed the OECD guideline recommended cytotoxicity threshold (Figure S1). Sodium diclofenac was also considered genotoxic in the ToxTracker assay, as it induced the Rtkn DNA damage marker at doses also exhibiting ~40% cell survival, but not exceeding the < 25% cell survival threshold for that assay (Figure S2a). 2DG was the only other expected negative compound to also induce positive responses in the DNA damage reporters of the ToxTracker assay (Figure S2a).

In Vitro Positive but in Vivo Negative Results

Mixed results were observed for the in vitro positive but in vivo negative compounds in both cell types. HepaRG cells correctly identified the large majority of these compounds (6/9 (1X exposure) or 7/9 (3X exposure)), while the TK6 cells only correctly detected one third (3/9) (Table 2). Both TK6 cells and HepaRG cells (in either exposure condition) incorrectly identified TBHQ and HP as positive in the in vitro MN assay when tested to the limit of cytotoxicity. However, TBHQ was only identified as weakly genotoxic (1.5‐fold induction in the Rtkn reporter) in the ToxTracker assay at concentrations that also exhibited cytotoxicity and significant induction (> 2‐fold threshold) of both oxidative stress reporters (Figure S2b). In addition, ToxTracker results were similar with and without metabolic activation; however, the overall induction levels of Srxn1 were substantially lower when metabolic activation was included (26‐fold vs. 11‐fold). Both cell types also detected QUE as positive; however, the positive HepaRG response was observed only in the 1X exposure condition. The HepaRG MN response using the 3X exposure was negative even at concentrations exhibiting similar levels of cytotoxicity observed in the 1X exposure condition (Figure S1b). The ToxTracker assay classified QUE as positive for the DNA damage and oxidative stress reporters. Notably, strong induction of the oxidative stress reporter Srxn1 occurred at low concentrations with minimal cytotoxicity that had not yet resulted in significant increases in the DNA damage reporters (Figure S2b). The inclusion of metabolic activation did not impact the overall calls for the oxidative stress response; however, the induction of the DNA damage markers was stronger in the presence of S9.

TK6 cells incorrectly identified AA, EUG, and IBA as positive in the MN assay whereas they were negative when tested in the HepaRG cells (Table 2). Clear, dose‐dependent increases in MN were observed for AA and IBA, while the EUG response was smaller, but statistically significant at the highest concentration (Figure S1b). Minimal cytotoxicity was observed in the HepaRG cells, whereas the top concentration tested was limited by strong cytotoxic effects in TK6 cells. AA and IBA were also considered to be negative in the ToxTracker assay as no induction of any of the reporters was observed. EUG was considered genotoxic in the ToxTracker assay as the Rtkn DNA damage reporter exceeded the 2‐fold threshold only in the absence of metabolic activation. Srxn1 was also significantly activated in the absence of metabolic activation, whereas both oxidative stress markers exhibited a weak (> 1.5 but < 2‐fold induction) in the presence of S9.

ETHIN and SACC were considered negative in both cell types as well as the ToxTracker assay (Figures S1b and S2b). CUR was considered negative in both cell types in the MN assay, although MN induction was observed at overly cytotoxic concentrations in TK6 cells (Figure S1b). CUR was weakly genotoxic in the ToxTracker assay in the Rtkn DNA damage reporter, and significant induction was observed in both oxidative stress reporters (Figure S2b). There was minimal impact of metabolic activation on the ToxTracker response; however, Ddit3 induction was observed only with S9.

In Vitro and in Vivo Positive Results

TK6 cells correctly identified all 14 in vitro and in vivo positive compounds, whereas depending on the exposure condition, HepaRG cells correctly detected 12/14 (1X exposure) or 11/14 (3X exposure) compounds. Similarly, the ToxTracker assay correctly identified 11/14 compounds. The two compounds not correctly detected by HepaRG cells in the MN assay in either exposure condition were ENU and o‐TOL (Table 2). ENU was tested to the limit of cytotoxicity and no increases in MN were observed, although it was correctly detected by the ToxTracker assay (Figure S2c). O‐TOL was incorrectly detected by both the MN assay when tested to the maximum recommended concentration of 10 mM (Figure S1c) as well as the ToxTracker assay, although the Srxn1 oxidative stress and Ddit3 reporters were activated in the absence of metabolic activation (Figure S2c). CAD induced statistically significant increases in MN in the top three concentrations when tested using the 1X exposure condition, however this compound was negative when tested using the 3X exposure (Figure S1c). The ToxTracker assay was also negative, although CAD resulted in significant induction in both oxidative stress reporters and Ddit3 in the presence and absence of metabolic activation (Figure S2c). Lastly, OTA was correctly identified as positive in the MN assay by both TK6 and HepaRG cells, but it did not result in induction of any of the reporters in the ToxTracker assay and was therefore considered negative in that assay (Figure S2c).

To visually compare responses between TK6 cells and HepaRG cells in both exposure conditions, the BMD confidence intervals representing a 100%, or 2‐fold increase, in MN induction were plotted (Figure 1). In the HepaRG 1X exposure condition, four compounds (5FU, CAD, OTA, AZT) could not be analyzed due to relatively flat dose responses, despite statistically significant increases in MN (Figure S1c). In general, BMDs were lower in TK6 cells compared to HepaRG cells (Figure 1). Exceptions include 5FU, ARE and PQ, in which TK6 and HepaRG 3X exposure BMD confidence intervals were either overlapping or closely spaced, and VB, in which the HepaRG 1X exposure BMD was lower than in TK6 cells or the 3x HepaRG exposure condition. When the different HepaRG exposure conditions are compared, BMDs are generally 0.5–1 order of magnitude lower in the 3X exposures compared to the single exposures. Exceptions include VB as previously mentioned, as well as ETOP, and MMS, for which the confidence intervals overlap, or are very closely spaced.

Discussion

The intent of the work presented here is to evaluate the performance of the HepaRG cell line in the in vitro micronucleus assay compared to TK6 cells, which are considered a standard cell line in OECD TG 487 and therefore appropriate for use in regulatory contexts. A total of 28 compounds were evaluated, including non‐genotoxins and genotoxins which act via both direct and indirect mechanisms of action. The ToxTracker assay was also performed for all compounds since it was thought to provide valuable information where discrepancies were observed between cell types to better understand possible mechanisms responsible for these differences.

HepaRG cells correctly detected all in vitro and in vivo negative expected compounds as negative in the MN assay using either exposure condition, while the TK6 cells incorrectly classified one compound, sodium diclofenac, as positive. As discussed in Allemang et al. 2021, sodium diclofenac has been reported as positive in other in vitro assays such as the MultiFlow assay performed in TK6 cells (Smart et al. 2020) and the reconstructed human skin MN (RSMN) assay (Pfuhler et al. 2021). It is likely that these results are driven by an indirect oxidative stress MOA, as supported by the induction of the oxidative stress reporter, Srxn1, in the ToxTracker assay reported here. The negative HepaRG results are in line with the recent review of historical sodium diclofenac data (Hartmann et al. 2021), suggesting that the negative result in HepaRG cells better reflects its lack of genotoxic activity in vivo for non‐dermal route of exposures.

In the context of in vitro positive but in vivo negative compounds, both cell types incorrectly classified HP and TBHQ as positive. This effect is likely linked to the presence of reactive oxygen species (ROS) at concentrations that the cells cannot neutralize, leading to oxidative DNA lesions that will lead to clastogenicity/gene mutation if unrepaired (Cho et al. 2022). HP itself is considered a ROS, while THBQ generates ROS intracellularly via redox cycling (Wang et al. 2010), and it may therefore not be surprising to see a similar response pattern. HepaRG cells correctly detected AA, EUG and IBA as negative in the MN assay, whereas TK6 cells did not. Both AA and EUG are considered antioxidants and it is known that antioxidants, like the above ROS producers, often act as pro‐oxidants at high concentrations capable of producing genotoxic effects if oxidative stress is not buffered (Park 2024). Similarly, although less well established, an oxidative mechanism has been proposed as a possible mechanism for positive in vitro genotoxicity results when treated with IBA (Kirkland et al. 2016). Surprisingly, AA and IBA were negative in the ToxTracker assay as no reporters, not even those for oxidative stress, were induced. When EUG was tested in the ToxTracker assay the Rtkn (DNA damage) and Srxn (oxidative stress) markers were activated and it was therefore considered genotoxic in the absence of metabolic activation. In contrast, this response was mitigated in the presence of metabolic activation, consistent with the negative response observed in HepaRG cells.

TK6 cells correctly identified all in vitro and in vivo positive compounds while HepaRG cells correctly identified 12/14 (86%) compounds using the 1X exposure or 11/14 (79%) compounds using the 3X exposure. The three compounds that were either not detected or detected in only one exposure condition using HepaRG cells in this study were ENU, o‐TOL, and CAD. ENU has, however, been correctly detected in the HepaRG MN assay by others (Guo et al. 2020; Hégarat et al. 2014). The lowest effective concentration (LEC) in these studies is variable; Guo et al. reported a LEC of 1024 μM, while the LEC reported in Hégarat et al. was about half of this concentration, 625 μM. One potential explanation for the difficulty detecting ENU in HepaRG cells is the difference in MGMT activity between cell types. The DNA repair protein MGMT plays an important role in the repair of alkylation damage like that caused by ENU, but TK6 cells are MGMT deficient, making them particularly sensitive to this type of damage (Kaina et al. 1991; Nagel et al. 2019). In contrast, MGMT activity is known to be high in human liver cells (Gerson et al. 1986), and alkylation damage will likely be efficiently repaired in HepaRG cells, although this has not been directly measured in this cell type yet. ENU has also been detected as positive in HepaRG cells in the CometChip assay (Seo et al. 2019), which may be a more sensitive assay for detecting ENU given that it is primarily a strong alkylating agent. Alkylating agents are efficient inducers of alkali‐labile sites, which will show as strand breaks in the alkaline Comet assay (Muruzabal et al. 2021). o‐TOL was incorrectly identified when tested in HepaRG cells as well as in the ToxTracker assay. As discussed in more detail in Allemang et al. 2021, its weak genotoxic response is likely driven by oxidative stress when testing at high, cytotoxic concentrations. The third compound CAD, a known genotoxic carcinogen, was only detected using the 1X HepaRG exposure condition. This compound has also been incorrectly identified as a non‐genotoxicant in other HepaRG MN and/or comet studies (Guo et al. 2020; Hégarat et al. 2014) as well as in the ToxTracker assay, since the only reporters activated were for oxidative stress and protein damage (Table 2). As noted in the recent ToxTracker interlaboratory validation, despite CAD being a known genotoxicant in vivo, it does not appear to be directly DNA reactive in vitro (Hendriks et al. 2024). It is important to note that CAD and ENU are both detected as positive in the Ames assay and would therefore be correctly identified as genotoxic when using an in vitro two‐test battery approach that captures all genotoxicity endpoints (Pfuhler et al. 2007). O‐toluidine, however, is an Ames negative rodent carcinogen that would still not be identified using this approach. OTA was correctly detected by both cell types in the in vitro MN assay, but it did not activate any gene reporters in the ToxTracker assay. Similarly, minimal pathway enrichment was observed when analysis was performed on the same TK6 cell samples for genes involved in oxidative stress and genotoxicity relevant pathways (De Abrew et al. 2025), despite the strong increase in MN observed here (Figure S1c).

Some variation in the HepaRG results between different laboratories such as those discussed above for ENU is perhaps unsurprising, given that a number of different protocols have been reported throughout the literature. HepaRG cells were originally envisioned as a tool for drug discovery and the standard recommended protocols provided by suppliers of this cell line are therefore focused on drug metabolism and liver toxicity studies. The application of these cells to other research areas, such as genotoxicity evaluation, is less well established. However, interest is increasing, and experience in this field has been growing in recent years. One key difference between reported protocols is the use of a variety of media formulations such as “thawing and plating,” “pre‐induction and toxicity” and “metabolism and maintenance” media, which differ primarily in DMSO content, a critical component in boosting and maintaining metabolic activity in HepaRG cells. One additional source of variation is whether cells are differentiated in‐house versus the use of terminally differentiated cells produced by a supplier which may be less susceptible to variation. Further optimization of media conditions and the development of a standardized HepaRG protocol specifically for genotoxicity testing would be beneficial and is currently ongoing under the auspices of the Genetic Toxicology Technical Committee (GTTC) of the Health and Environmental Sciences Institute (HESI Global). Standardization is especially important as the use of HepaRG cells is expanding quickly, beyond standard genotoxicity endpoints such as micronucleus and comet assay to include newer assays such as biomarkers (TGx‐DDI, MultiFlow and GENOMARK) (Buick et al. 2020, 2021; Thienpont et al. 2024), and error‐corrected sequencing technologies (Ates et al. 2018; Barutcu et al. 2025; Buick et al. 2021; Seo et al. 2024).

The results presented here demonstrate a high accuracy of the HepaRG MN assay for the single and 3X exposure protocols with 24/28 (86%) correct predictions. There were only two compounds where a conflicting response was observed between the single or repeated exposure protocols in HepaRG cells. Specifically, QUE was positive after a single exposure at a concentration of 1000 μM, but it was negative after exposure to three repeated lower doses of 550 μM (the limit of cytotoxicity). In the case presented here the repeated dose protocol would have led to the correct negative in vivo prediction. In the second case, CAD induced weak, but statistically significant, increases in MN at multiple concentrations, but was negative when tested using the repeated exposure. For most compounds that displayed cytotoxicity, similar micronucleus outcomes were obtained, but in general had lower BMDs in the repeated exposures. Two notable exceptions are the aneugens COL and VB, which had lower BMDs in the single exposure condition (Figure 1). Our data involving several chemical classes support the use of either dosing regimen, however, for nitrosamines in particular, there is indication in the literature that points towards a high sensitivity of the 3X dosing (Seo et al. 2024). An additional benefit of a repeated exposure protocol is that, in cases where solubility is limited, higher cumulative doses may be achieved which is more reflective of in vivo exposures.

Both HepaRG protocols showed a higher accuracy for predicting in vivo responses compared to TK6 cells (21/28 (75%) correct calls). An OECD 487 compliant protocol that includes treatment in the presence and absence of S9 was not used here and its inclusion cannot be expected to improve assay performance for this set of 28 chemicals since sensitivity is at 100% and specificity could only decline further. The overall lower accuracy stems from a much lower specificity of TK6 cells which were only correctly predicting one third of the compounds that have been reported to give so‐called “false” or “misleading” positive results in other in vitro genotoxicity assays. One possible explanation for this relates to clear differences in the observed cytotoxicity profiles between cell types. High levels of cytotoxicity is a known cause of false‐positive responses in the MN assay (Kirkland, Pfuhler, et al. 2007). In general, TK6 cells were more susceptible to cytotoxicity, and when cytotoxicity was observed in both cell types, it typically occurred at lower concentrations in TK6 cells than for HepaRG cells. This is likely due to fundamental differences in key cellular processes between the two cell types. For example, in a comparative analysis of TK6 and HepaRG cells treated with genotoxic compounds, transcriptional responses were significantly different between the two cell types, with HepaRG cells displaying a broader and stronger response in transcription factors associated with DNA repair and cell proliferation/survival and stress responses (Kreuzer et al. 2022). Conversely, TK6 cells detect all in vivo positive compounds which were selected with a focus on direct‐acting compounds that do not need bioactivation. Importantly, HepaRG cells have already been demonstrated to be highly sensitive for genotoxins that require metabolic activation such as pyrrolizidine alkaloids and nitrosamines (Allemang et al. 2018; Hégarat et al. 2014; Quesnot et al. 2016; Seo et al. 2019, 2023). The data presented here, therefore, extend the understanding of the predictive capacity of the HepaRG MN. Given that the compound selection for this study did not favor HepaRG cells, the observed sensitivity of 86% (1X) and 79% (3X) is considered informative and suggests that HepaRG cells are well suited as an “all‐purpose” cell line useful in screening and regulatory testing approaches. Aside from its high predictivity there are also practical benefits for their use. Eliminating the need for separate exposures with and without metabolic activation would streamline an experiment to a single condition, leading to significant cost and time savings. Avoiding the external metabolism condition also addresses 3R considerations in that animals would not be needed for the generation of rodent liver S9 preparations. The use of HepaRG cells not only aligns with ethical considerations but also enhances human risk assessment due to their closer resemblance to human liver metabolism (Guo et al. 2024). Our data also shows that HepaRG cells more accurately identify in vivo negative compounds with an indirect oxidative stress related MOA as non‐clastogenic, while these compounds led to 66% false‐positive calls in TK6 cells. This may be explained by a more in vivo‐like balance of phase I and II metabolism activity in HepaRG cells, leading to more efficient detoxification of these compounds. Recent examples of HepaRG cells being used in risk assessment contexts include potency ranking and mixture assessment of DNA reactive classes of compounds such as nitrosamines, aromatic amines and pyrrolizidine alkaloids (Allemang et al. 2018; Allemang, Mahony, and Pfuhler 2022; Dumont et al. 2010; Kopp et al. 2020; Louisse et al. 2019; Seo et al. 2025), as well as assessing genotoxicity risks of individual compounds such as naphthalene (Recio et al. 2023) and 2‐chloroethanol, a food‐relevant degradant of ethylene oxide (Allemang, Lester, et al. 2022). Taken together, HepaRG cells appear to be suited for inclusion into regulatory testing regimens.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the results presented here for 28 compounds representing a balanced set of in vivo negative and positive compounds with a range of genotoxicity modes of action demonstrate a high overall accuracy of the HepaRG in vitro MN assay for predicting in vivo results that is better than the accuracy obtained with TK6 cells, a cell line often used for OECD guideline testing. These promising results mark an important step towards demonstrating the value of these cells for regulatory genotoxicity testing. Further optimization and testing of additional compounds, especially those requiring metabolic activation, will be essential.
