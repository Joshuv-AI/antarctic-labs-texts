Assessing loneliness in later life: validation of the De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Scale for Portuguese older adults
Authors: João Tavares, Oscar Ribeiro, Javier Yanguas Lezaun, Sofia Silva, Carolina Soares, Matilde Jóia-Gomes, Sara Guerra, Liliana Sousa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Assessing loneliness is essential to better understand older adults’ mental health needs and to inform timely and appropriate interventions. The De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Scale (DJGLS) is one of the most widely used measures of loneliness worldwide. However, there is no evidence regarding its psychometric adequacy for older adults in Portugal. This study analysed the psychometric properties (validity and reliability) of the Portuguese version of the DJGLS (both the 11-item and 6-item versions) for community-dwelling older adults. A cross-sectional study was conducted with 466 participants. Construct, convergent and divergent validity were evaluated, and test–retest reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) and Bland–Altman plots. The 11-item DJGLS showed a bifactor structure with a general loneliness factor and two specific factors (social and emotional loneliness), with good model fi, whereas the 6-item DJGLS demonstrated excellent fit. Both versions showed a significant correlation with ULCA-16 (r ≥ 0.6), adequate internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.8 (11 item) and 0.67 (6 item)), excellent test–retest (two-weeks) reliability (ICC ≥ 0.9) and acceptable agreement on the Bland–Altman plots, without evidence of proportional biases. The Portuguese versions of the DJGLS-11 and DJGLS-6 are valid and reliable instruments for assessing loneliness in Portuguese community-dwelling older adults, supporting their use in research and screening contexts. The validated version of the DJGLS enable the assessment of social and emotional loneliness, expanding the range of loneliness measures currently available in Portugal.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07875-z.

Introduction

Loneliness is increasingly recognized as a significant public health issue, affecting approximately one in four adults aged 60 years and older [11]. Older adults are particularly vulnerable to loneliness due to changes in physical health (e.g., chronic illnesses; loss of mobility) and in social connections arising from life transitions, such as retirement and widowhood [24].

Loneliness is subjective and lived as a deeply negative experience [15, 24]. Perlman and Peplau [24] conceptualized loneliness as an individual's experience of an unpleasant or lack of (quality of) certain relationships. Therefore, if the social network is too small or the relationships are perceived as lacking quality, the person often feels lonely [15] Perlman & Peplau 24.

Loneliness has been extensively studied and has demonstrated robust associations with worsened perceived health, depression, anxiety, cardiovascular disease and dementia and cognitive decline [7, 13, 24]. Loneliness is a risk factor for mortality, with earlier research reporting a 26% increased likelihood of death [24]. More recently, a meta-analysis of over two million adults aged 18 or older confirmed this association, linking loneliness to a 14% higher risk of all-cause mortality [24]. Among older adults (≥ 60 years), a meta-analysis of 36 studies found that loneliness was associated with increased mortality risk (HR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.06–1.14; [24].

In Portugal, factors such as living alone, low income and fragile informal support networks contribute to the increased risk of loneliness [24]. Rocha-Vieira et al. [24] found that nine out of 10 older Portuguese adults experience some degree of loneliness, with over a third facing moderate to severe loneliness, particularly those over 80 years old. According to the National Institute of Statistics, nearly 40% of Portuguese individuals aged 80 and above were living alone in 2021, with a growing proportion of people aged 65 and over residing in single-person households (Instituto Nacional de Estatística [INE], [24]). Although living alone is not synonymous with loneliness, it is recognized as a significant risk factor for loneliness, particularly in older age groups [24].

Given the profound impact that loneliness can have on health, well-being and social functioning [7, 24], the ability to accurately assess it is crucial for designing and implementing effective interventions [24, 24]. Some of the most widely used instruments include the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Loneliness Scale and the De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Scale (DJGLS) [9, 24, 24]. The UCLA measures subjective feelings of loneliness, with the 20-item version being the most widely used form across research and clinical settings [24]. The DJGLS measures overall loneliness, as well as emotional loneliness (EL; feeling of lacking an intimate relationship) and social loneliness (SL; feeling of lacking a social network), and uses fewer items (6 or 11 items) [24].

A recent systematic review examined and critically appraised the measurement properties and the cross-cultural adaptation of the DJGLS for older adults. It reported that both the 11-item and 6-item versions were cross-culturally adapted and translated into 18 languages (Polish, Hebrew, Spanish, Dutch, Arabic, Turkish, Persian, Chinese, Brazilian Portuguese, Bahasa, Punjabi, Urdu, Gujarati, Hindi, Mandarin, Bengali, Somali and Yoruba) across 12 countries [1]. The authors argue that a more comprehensive assessment of the scale’s measurement properties is required. In this regard, this study sought to provide a more thorough evaluation of the DJGLS by incorporating multiple forms of validity and reliability testing.

The DJGLS is considered a good choice in international comparative studies due to its availability in different languages. Yet, the DJGLS is not available in the European Portuguese language. According to [9], it would be relevant to have the DJGLS validated for the older Portuguese population to increase the limited number of instruments currently available for research and practice. Furthermore, in being one of the most used instruments internationally, having a Portuguese version available would benefit cross-cultural comparisons in the field of loneliness.

This study analyzed the psychometric properties (validity and reliability) of the Portuguese version of the DJGLS (both the 11-item and 6-item versions) for community-dwelling adults aged 65 + years old.

Methods

Design

This cross-sectional study adhered to the Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) checklist [24] and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Nursing School of Coimbra, Portugal: Reference: P1038_04_2024).

Study settings and participants

A non-probabilistic convenience sampling strategy was employed. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 65 years, residence in Portugal; fluency in Portuguese; living in a community setting; and sufficient cognitive capacity to comprehend and respond to the questionnaire. Exclusion criteria included residence in a residential care unit and the presence of cognitive impairment, defined as a positive screening for cognitive deficit on the Mini-Cog (score ≤ 2). A participant-to-item ratio of 10:1 is recommended to determine sample size [24], requiring at least 110 participants for the 11-item DJGLS; however, to perform both Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), a minimum of 200 participants is needed to ensure model adequacy. Thus, the target sample size was set at n ≥ 200.

Instruments

Sociodemographic questionnaire

A structured sociodemographic questionnaire was administered to collect information on sex, age, years of formal education, marital status, living arrangements (if living alone: duration), area of residence, housing situation, household income, number of children, grandchildren and great-grandchildren, and current professional status.

Mini-Cog

This is a brief cognitive screening instrument developed by [2] for the rapid detection of cognitive impairment and is available in multiple languages, including European Portuguese (Mini-Cog© Quick Screening for Early Dementia Detection, 2022). This test combines a three-word recall task and a clock drawing task depicting a specified time. Total scores range from 0 to 5 and are interpreted as follows: a recall of three words indicates no cognitive impairment; a recall of 1–2 words with a normal clock indicates no cognitive impairment; a recall of 1–2 words with an abnormal clock indicates a positive result for cognitive impairment; a recall of 0 words indicates a positive result for cognitive impairment.

De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Scale (DJGLS)

The DJGLS was developed by [16] to assess loneliness as a multidimensional construct comprising emotional loneliness (EL; lack of close, intimate relationships) and social loneliness (SL; lack of a broader social network) (de Jong-Gierveld & [17]).

The original 11-item DJGLS includes six negatively worded items (EL: Items 2, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10) and five positively worded items (SL: Items 1, 4, 7, 8, 11). Responses are rated on a three-point scale (‘Yes’, ‘More or less’, ‘No’) and dichotomized for scoring: (i) Negatively worded items: ‘Yes’ and ‘More or less’ = 1; ‘No’ = 0; (ii) Positively worded items: ‘More or less’ and ‘No’ = 1; ‘Yes’ = 0. Total scores range from 0 to 11 and are categorized as not lonely (0–2), moderately lonely (3–8), and strongly lonely (9–11). Reported internal consistency values range from α = 0.71 to 0.92 [1].

The shortened six-item version (DJGLS-6) [18] retains the two-factor structure comprising three negatively worded EL items (items 1–3) and three positively worded SL items (items 4–6). Scoring follows the same principles, yielding total scores from 0 to 6 (0–1: not lonely; 2–4: moderately lonely; 5–6: strongly lonely). Reported reliability coefficients for the DJGLS-6 range from α = 0.44 to.95 [24]. The translation and cultural adaptation followed the ISPOR guidelines [24], and content and face validity were assessed in accordance with COSMIN guidelines [39]; details are published elsewhere [63].

UCLA loneliness scale (UCLA-16)

The UCLA-20, developed by Russel in 1978 and subsequently refined, was validated for the Portuguese population resulting in a 16-item version [24]. This version assesses two dimensions: social isolation (12 items) and affinities (4 items). All items are negatively worded. Total scores range from 16 to 64, with a score above 32 indicating a negative feeling of loneliness. In the Portuguese validation, the internal consistency was high, with a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.905 for the total scale, 0.920 for social isolation, and of 0.824 for affinities [24].

Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS)

The SWLS, developed by [22], assesses the degree of satisfaction a person has with his/her living conditions The present study used the Portuguese version by [24], which consists of five items with a five-point Likert scale (1—strongly disagree to 5—strongly agree). The total scores range from 5–25, with higher values indicating greater life satisfaction. The Portuguese version demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.77) and convergent validity [24].

Geriatric Depression Scale-4 (GDS-4)

The GDS was developed by [66] to assess the presence of depressive symptoms in older adults. This study employed the 4-item version (GDS-4) validated for the Portuguese population by [24], which showed acceptable model fit and good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.71). Items are dichotomous referring symptoms experienced during the previous week (yes = 0 points; no = 1), yielding total scores from 0 to 4. Although there is no universal consensus regarding the optimal cut-off score for the GDS-4, a threshold of ≥ 1 was adopted in accordance with previous recommendations [4].

Brief Resilient Coping Scale (BRCS)

The BRCS was developed by [24] and translated and culturally adapted for the Portuguese population by [24]. It is a unidimensional measure consisting of four items designed to assess adaptive coping in response to stress. Responses are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). Total scores range from 4 to 20 and are classified as low resilience (4–13), moderate resilience (14–16) and high resilience (17–20).

Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS)

The MSPSS was developed by [24] and translated and validated for the Portuguese population by [8]. The scale has been widely used across diverse populations, including older adults [24] Stanly et al., [24]). It consists of 12 items assessing perceived social support across three sources: family, friends and significant others. Each subscale comprises four items evaluating practical assistance, emotional support and perceived availability of others to discuss problems and assist with decision-making [14]. Items are rated on a seven-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree), yielding total scores from 12 to 84. Scores are categorized as low (12–48), moderate (49–68), or high perceived social support (69–84). The Portuguese version confirmed the original three-factor structure, with Cronbach’s α coefficients ranging from 0.85 to 0.95 and test–retest reliability coefficients ranging from 0.40 to 0.91 [8].

Data collection

Data were collected between August 2024 and January 2025. Participants were recruited via three channels: researchers’ personal networks; nationwide organizations with formal agreements with the University of Aveiro or the RISE-Associated Laboratory (e.g., municipal services, cultural associations); and newly contacted community organizations. For the second and third channels, formal invitation emails explained the study’s aims, ethical approval, inclusion criteria, and collaboration options. Potential participants were identified, and meetings were arranged to provide detailed information. Informed consent to participate in the study was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment, i.e., before assessing cognitive eligibility using the Portuguese Mini-Cog. Screening was conducted individually by trained researchers or professionals. Participants without cognitive impairment proceeded with data collection, while those who screened positive concluded participation with thanks for their willingness to collaborate.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to assess the demographic and scores of the scales of the study participants.

To explore the factor structure of the DJGLS (11 and 6 items), an Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was first conducted, followed by a Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) to test the fit of the identified structure. For the EFA with dichotomous data, a tetrachoric correlation matrix was used, followed by principal component analysis (PCA) with oblique rotation. Sampling adequacy was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO ≥ 0.70) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.01) [24]. The number of retained factors was determined using the scree plot and ‘elbow rule’ and parallel analysis (observed eigenvalues with those obtained from simulated data). The number of items per factor was determined using the latent root criterion, considering items with communalities (h2) > 0.40, eigenvalues > 1 and a cumulative variance ≥ 60% [24].

To conduct the CFA with non-normal data structured in a tetrachoric correlation matrix, robust estimation methods were used: weighted least squares with mean and variance for the 11-item scale and weighted least squares for the 6-item scale. Model fit was evaluated using standard fit indices, including the chi-square statistic, the p-value, the ratio with degrees of freedom (χ2/df—p > 0.05), the comparative fit index (CFI ≥ 0.90), the Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI ≥ 0.90), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA < 0.05, with a 90% confidence interval upper limit below 0.10) and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR < 0.08) [24].

Convergent and divergent validity were examined by calculation of Pearson’s correlation coefficient (no violation of normality test, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test) between the DJGLS (total and scales) and the following scales: UCLA (convergent validity), SWLS, GDS, BRCS and MSPSS (divergent validity). Correlation coefficient was interpreted as: strong (≥ 0.50), when comparing the DJGLS with instruments measuring similar constructs; moderate (0.30 ≤ r < 0.50) when correlating with related constructs; and weak or negligible (r < 0.30) when assessing associations with unrelated constructs [24]. Additionally, convergent validity was assessed based on the results of the CFA using the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR). According to [12], convergent validity is considered satisfactory when AVE > 0.50 and CR > 0.70 and when CR exceeds AVE (CR > AVE). Internal consistency was evaluated using two different indices: Cronbach’s alpha [α] and CR. Furthermore, the attenuation index was calculated.

The assessment of test–retest reliability (two-weeks of interval) was performing using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), Bland–Altman plot analysis and paired sample correlation, in a subsample of 27 participants. The ICC cut-off values used were: < 0.50 (poor), 0.50–0.75 (moderate), 0.75–0.90 (good) and > 0.90 (excellent) [33, 35]. The standard error of measurement (SEM) was calculated using the formula: SEM_consistency = (SD_T1 + SD_T2)/2) × √(1 – ICC_consistency). The smallest detectable change (SDC) was derived from the SEM: SDC_ind = 1.96 × √2 × SEM (de 011) [21].

These analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26, IBM SPSS AMOS) and R statistical software (version 4.2.2) with the following packages: psych, rela, MASS, parallel, corpcor, GPArotation, lavaan, MVN, tidySEM, dplyr and polycor. Results with p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

The construct validity of the DJGLS-11 and of the DJGLS-6

The construct validity was examined using both EFA and CFA. For EFA and CFA, it is generally recommended to use different samples [24]. Therefore, for the EFA, a separate subsample comprising 255 older community-dwelling Portuguese adults was collected. The CFA was performed using the sample in this study (n = 466).

DJGLS-11

Exploratory factor analysis

The KMO = 0.82 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant (χ2 = 824.03, degrees of freedom (df) = 15, p < 0.05). Parallel analysis and inspection of the scree plot supported a two-factor structure. Factor 1 (EL) comprised items 2, 3, 5, 6, 9 and 10, with factor loadings ranging from 0.61 to 0.90 and accounted for 37% of the total variance. Factor 2 (SL) included items 1, 4, 7, 8 and 11, with loadings ranging from 0.47 to 0.97 and explained 27% of the total variance (Appendix A, Table 1,).

Reliability

The DJGLS-11 presented good internal consistency and showed a α = 0.804 and CR = 0.85. The EL and SL subscales showed α values of 0.764 and 0.714, respectively. The attenuation index was 5%, indicating that Cronbach’s α was not significantly distorted. The overall mean inter-item correlation for the full scale was 0.464, with individual correlations ranging from 0.355–0.540 (for detailed results, see Appendix A, Table 2).

Test–retest reliability

The total score of DJGLS-11 showed excellent reliability (ICC = 0.933; 95% CI 0.851–0.969). The ICCs for agreement and consistency were calculated and showed moderate reliability in items 4, 5 and 8 (0.6–0.75), good reliability in items 1, 2, 6, 7, 9, 10 and 11 (0.75–0.9) and excellent in item 3 (> 0.90) (Appendix A, Table 3,). The Bland–Altman plot showed good agreement between test and retest scores, with most values within the limits of agreement (4 out of 27 observations; 14.81% fell outside the 95% limits of agreement (CI: [−3.81, 3.59]) (Appendix A, Fig. 1). Regression analysis revealed no evidence of proportional bias (β = 0.142, p = 0.264). The SEM for the total scale was 0.72, resulting in the SDC for an individual (SDC_ind of 1.99).

DJGLS-6

Exploratory factor analysis

The KMO = 0.75 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant (χ2 = 1735.9, df = 55, p < 0.05). Parallel analysis and inspection of the scree plot supported a two-factor structure. Factor 1, EL comprised items 3, 9 and 10, with factor loadings ranging from 0.81 to 0.93, and accounted for 40% of the total variance. Factor 2, SL included items 4, 7 and 8, with loadings ranging from 0.70 and 0.94, and explained 38% of the total variance (Appendix A, Table 4).

Confirmatory factor analysis (Table 3). Two models were tested using CFA: 1) a unidimensional model with a single substantive factor of loneliness and 2) a two-factor model distinguishing EL and SL. Model 1 demonstrated a poor fit to the data, failing to meet the established thresholds for acceptable model fit. Model 2 provided the best fit indices indicated a good model: χ2(8) = 19.66, χ2/df = 2.5, CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.97, RMSEA [90% CI] = 0.056 [0.025, 0.088] and SRMR = 0.06 (Table 4). Convergent and divergent validity

The DJGLS-6 demonstrated strong correlations with UCLA-16 (r > 0.60) (Table 2). The correlations between the DJGLS-6 subscales and the UCLA-16 were moderate to strong correlation (r > 0.40). The EL and SL subscales yielded AVE values of 0.67 and 0.67, respectively. The CR was 0.725 for both subscales. exceeding the corresponding AVE values. Negative correlations were observed between the total DJGLS-6 and SWLS, BRCS and MSPSS, with correlation coefficients of r = −0.438, r = −0.307 and r = −0.460, respectively. Similar correlation patterns were found for the EL and SL subscales in relation to those three constructs. The total DJGLS-6 score showed weak correlations with GDS-4 (r = 0.366) (Table 2).

Reliability

Internal consistency of DJGLS-6 presents a α = 0.669 and CR = 0.75. The EL and SL subscales showed Cronbach’s α values of 0.639 and 0.639, respectively. The attenuation index of 10.8% suggested that Cronbach’s α slightly underestimated the true reliability, without substantial distortion. The overall mean inter-item correlation for the full scale was 0.456, with individual correlations ranging from 0.416–0.489 (For detailed results, see Appendix A, Table 5).

Test–retest reliability

The total score of DJGLS 6, showed excellent reliability (r = 0.889, ICC = 0.936; 95% CI 0.867–0.972) (Appendix A, Table 3). The Bland–Altman plot showed good agreement between test and retest scores, with most of values within the limits of agreement (2 out of 27 observations (7.01%) fell outside the 95% limits of agreement (CI: [−3.91, 3.73]) (Appendix, Fig. 2). Regression analysis revealed no evidence of proportional bias (β = 0.298, p = 0.097). The SEM for the total scale was 0.37, resulting in the SDC for an individual (SDC_ind of 1.01).

Discussion

This study analysed the psychometric properties of the 11- and 6-item versions of the DJGLS for Portuguese community-dwelling older adults. In line with recommendations from a recent systematic review [1], this study followed gold-standard psychometric guidelines (COSMIN) and employed multiple complementary approaches to assess construct validity, convergent and discriminant validity, reliability and measurement error.

For the DJGLS-11, the better fit model was the bifactor model [10, 24, 34]. The bifactor model suggests that all items reflect a general experience of loneliness while confirming the presence of two dimensions: SL and EL. This model aligns with the classical theoretical distinction between emotional and social loneliness. A recent systematic review synthesizing evidence from multiple studies supports the bifactorial structure of the DJGLS-11 as higher-quality evidence [1]. Our findings reinforce the adequacy of the scale’s original theoretical structure, demonstrating its ability to capture both the global and multidimensional nature of loneliness. Nonetheless, future research should explore bifactor models that account for method effects, particularly the potential influence of negatively worded items [10].

The dimensionality of the DJGLS-6 has been less frequently examined in validation studies [1] with two primary models proposed: unidimensional and two-factor. Our study assessed both models, with fit indices supporting the two-factor solution, consistent with findings from prior studies [6, 12, 24]. In contrast to other studies [12, 24], the Portuguese version did not show any items with cross-loadings that might have contributed to lower goodness-of-fit index values.

Two complementary approaches were employed to assess convergent validity. First, strong correlations were found between both the DJGLS-11 and DJGLS-6 and the UCLA-16, including its subscales [24, 24, 24]. These findings further support the convergent validity of the DJGLS-6. According to COSMIN guidelines, correlation coefficients ≥ 0.50 are indicative of acceptable convergent validity when comparing instruments measuring similar constructs. Second, the AVE was > 0.50 in 11 and 6 items versions exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.50. Additionally, CR was > 0.7 for both scales, exceeding the AVE and indicating that the variance explained by the latent constructs outweighs measurement error. These indicators confirm that the items meaningfully contribute to the constructs they are intended to measure.

Correlations were also examined between the DJGLS and theoretically related but conceptually distinct constructs, including depression, life satisfaction, resilience and perceived social support [16]. As expected, total scores on both the 11- and 6-item versions of DJGLS showed moderate positive correlations with depression, supporting the theoretical association between loneliness and depressive symptomatology. The EL subscale demonstrated a stronger association with depression than the SL subscale, suggesting that the absence of meaningful emotional bonds may be more closely linked to depression than the lack of social connections, a pattern consistent with previous research [23]. Moderate negative correlations were also observed between the DJGLS and indicators of well-being, with the DJGLS-6 showing a negative association with perceived social support. Overall, higher levels of loneliness were associated with lower resilience, reduced life satisfaction and diminished perceived social support, in line with theoretical expectations and empirical findings [24, 24]. Future research could further explore criterion validity by examining variables known to influence loneliness, such as marital status and self-perceived health status [1].

Our results support the good internal consistency of DJGLS-11, with Cronbach’s α and CR values exceeding 0.80 for the total scale and 0.70 for the subscales, meting commonly accepted criteria (> 0.7) [5, 24]. These findings were consistent with previous findings reporting α coefficients ranging from 0.71 to 0.92 [1]. The DJGLS-6 exhibited slightly lower internal consistency (α = 0.67; CR = 0.75). However, for brief scales, α values between 0.60 and 0.70 are generally considered acceptable [24], particularly given that reliability coefficients are sensitive to the number of items: as the number of items decreases, internal consistency estimates tend to be attenuated [24]. This pattern is commonly observed in short scales (fewer than 10 items) and is reflected in the difference between the internal consistency values of the 11- and 6-item versions in our study. Our findings align with prior research [3, 24], which also reported satisfactory reliability for the DJGLS-6 and its subscales. Given that Cronbach’s α may underestimate reliability in short version, mean inter-item correlations were also examined. For the DJGLS-6, these ranged from 0.456 to 0.489, falling within the ideal range for brief scales (0.20–0.50; [5]. The DJGLS-11 showed slightly higher values (0.464–0.508), further supporting the internal coherence of both versions and suggesting that the items are sufficiently interrelated to reflect global, emotional and social loneliness. Nevertheless, as noted by de Jong Gierveld and van Tilburg [19], cultural and geographic differences may influence inter-item correlations and should be considered when interpreting these results.

Finally, our study also showed excellent two-week test–retest reliability for total scores, as recommended in the literature [24]. As no prior test–retest data exist for the DJGLS-11 and only limited evidence for the DJGLS-6 [24], this study provides novel ICC data. Following COSMIN guidelines [24], high reliability and temporal stability were confirmed for both versions. This stability was corroborated by Bland–Altman and regression analyses, which showed no significant systematic or proportional bias. Additionally, the SEM and the SDC were calculated in line with recommendations by [20]. The SEM values were low (DJGLS-11 = 0.72; DJGLS-6 = 0.37), with corresponding SDCs of 1.99 and 1.01, respectively. These metrics indicate high measurement precision and the ability of both versions to detect meaningful individual-level changes, enhancing the clinical utility of the scale for monitoring loneliness over time.

Beyond their psychometric robustness, these findings have important implications for research and primary healthcare contexts in Portugal. The validated DJGLS-11 and DJGLS-6 provide reliable instruments for assessing loneliness in community-dwelling older adults, a condition increasingly recognized as a relevant public health concern in ageing populations. In particular, the DJGLS-6 may be suitable for use in primary care settings, such as general practice and family medicine consultations, where time constraints often limit the use of longer assessment tools.

Incorporating brief and validated measures of loneliness into routine assessment procedures in primary care and community-based research may facilitate the early identification of socially and emotionally vulnerable older adults. This is particularly relevant in the context of Portuguese primary healthcare services, where there is a growing emphasis on preventive care and community-based approaches to healthy ageing. By enabling efficient assessment within routine consultations or research settings, the DJGLS instruments may contribute to a better understanding of loneliness and its correlates in older adults, supporting more person-centred approaches in future applied research and practice development.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is its rigorous and comprehensive methodological design, which integrated multiple complementary approaches to assess validity and reliability, thereby enhancing the robustness. In addition, the validation of both the 11-item and the 6-item versions increases the study’s value by providing flexible instruments suitable for diverse research and applied contexts, including large-scale surveys and time-constrained clinical assessments. Future studies could further strengthen the validation of the DJGLS in the Portuguese context by incorporating additional psychometric approaches, such as Rasch modelling.

Some limitations should be considered. First, the use of a non-probabilistic sampling may limit generalisability of the findings, as reflected in the sex distribution, where the sample was predominantly female (72.7%). This imbalance may have influenced the results, given that men and women tend to experience and express loneliness differently: men typically report higher levels of social loneliness (i.e., the absence of an engaging social network), whereas women tend to report higher levels of emotional loneliness (i.e., the absence of a close emotional attachment) [24]. Although participants were recruited from multiple regions of Portugal and exhibited some sociodemographic heterogeneity, future studies should include older adults from more diverse socioeconomic and geographic backgrounds, particularly those living in rural areas.

Second, measurement invariance across key sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, education level) was not examined: addressing this in future research is necessary to ensure comparability across groups.

Third, it is important to acknowledge that the test–retest reliability analysis was conducted in a relatively small subsample (n = 27). According to COSMIN guidelines, this represents a limitation of the study, and further research with larger samples is required to confirm the temporal stability of the scales.

As a self-report measure, the DJGLS may be subject to social desirability bias. While anonymity and self-administration were used to minimize this risk, alternative data collections methods (e.g., online platforms, mobile applications or computer-assisted interviewing) should be explored. Finally, test–retest reliability was assessed in a relatively small subsample (n = 27), which may have limited the precision of the estimates, and inter-rater reliability was not examined, which is particularly relevant for interviewer-administered formats.

Conclusion

This study provided robust evidence supporting the construct, convergent and divergent validity, as well as the reliability of the Portuguese versions of the DJGLS-11 and DJGLS-6. Both versions exhibited a two-factor structure (EL and SL) consistent with the original theoretical model. Internal consistency was good for the DJGLS-11 and adequate for the DJGLS-6, and both instruments exhibited excellent temporal reliability for total scores. Overall, these findings support the DJGLS-11 and DJGLS-6 as valid and reliable instruments for assessing loneliness among older community-dwelling Portuguese adults. This study expands the range of psychometrically sound tools available in Portugal to evaluate loneliness, enabling meaningful comparisons with other populations and subgroups, both nationally and internationally. The Portuguese versions of the DJGLS-11 and DJGLS-6 are valid and reliable instruments for assessing loneliness in Portuguese community-dwelling older adults, supporting their use in research and screening contexts. While the findings suggest potential applicability in clinical and primary healthcare settings, this should be interpreted cautiously; further studies with larger samples are needed before definitive conclusions regarding clinical use can be drawn.
