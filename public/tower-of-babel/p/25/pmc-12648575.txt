Low-energy vertebral fractures requiring a fusion in the elderly associate higher mortality than high-energy injuries
Authors: Raquel Gutiérrez-González, Andrea Barbero, Alvaro Zamarron, Alberto Vallejo-Plaza, Ana Royuela
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOsteoporotic vertebral fractures are associated with excess mortality in the first years after diagnosis. Vertebral augmentation appears to reduce this excess. However, there is no evidence on survival in elderly patients who suffer high-energy impacts and those who require vertebral fusion for treatment.Research questionis mortality in elderly patients with thoracolumbar fractures requiring spinal fusion different according to the mechanism (high vs. low energy trauma)? Which factors predict survival in this group of patients?Material and methodsPatients aged ≥50 years with thoracolumbar fractures who required vertebral fusion for this reason between 2008 and 2021 were retrospectively selected. Survival was determined with the Kaplan-Meier method, and Cox regression analyses were used to determine mortality predictors.ResultsThe analysis encompassed a total of 141 patients, with a mean age of 66.3 years (SD 10,23) and predominance of female subjects (56 %). The survival probability was found to be significantly higher in patients with high-energy trauma, both in the short and long term, according to the injury mechanism (HR 0.18 [95 % CI 0.08–0.38]; p < 0.001). Multivariable analysis identified low-energy mechanism, Charlson comorbidity index score, and emergent procedures as independent risk factors for mortality.Discussion and conclusionThe findings of this study demonstrate an elevated risk of mortality in elderly patients when the fracture treated with fusion is produced by a low-energy mechanism. This observation may be attributable to the increased fragility of these patients. Individualized patient evaluation is necessary, with a focus on preoperative optimization, particularly in those with higher-risk profiles.Keywords: Aged, Comorbidity, Risk factors, Survival, Vertebral fracture

Introduction

As life expectancy has risen, so has the prevalence of comorbidities among older adults, affecting their perceived health and quality of life (National Statistics Institute). Elderly patients are more susceptible to injury from minor trauma than younger individuals, and they now account for over 25 % of all major injuries in the United Kingdom (Atinga et al., 2018). Fractures are the most common traumatic injuries in this group, particularly those involving the ribs, distal radius, and pelvic ring, while cervical spine fractures with spinal cord injury show the highest mortality and complication rates (21 % for thoracic and 15 % for lumbar fractures) (Keller et al., 2012). The annual incidence of thoracolumbar fractures, including osteoporotic ones, is estimated at 30 per 100,000 inhabitants, with a progressive increase observed among older adults (Eckert and Martin, 2017; Zileli et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2023). In a study of 13,496 patients with thoracolumbar fractures, 66 % were over 60 years old; falls from standing height caused 59 % of fractures, while high-energy trauma accounted for 12 % (7.9 % due to falls from height and 4.2 % to traffic accidents) (Jansson et al., 2010).

Mortality related to fractures is comparable to that of other major causes of death (Gutiérrez-González et al., 2023), and these injuries also lead to significant reductions in quality of life (Viola et al., 2025). The increased risk of death following vertebral compression fractures can persist for up to five years, being highest immediately after the event (Gutiérrez-González et al., 2023). Older adults are more likely to sustain severe injuries, with mortality rates up to three times higher, longer hospital stays, and greater use of healthcare resources (Keller et al., 2012). In a mortality analysis by primary diagnosis, fractures accounted for 5.7 deaths per 1000 cases, with thoracic injuries showing the highest risk (Smith et al., 2012). A population-based study in South Korea further found that patients over 65 years with vertebral compression fractures due to high-energy trauma had a higher mortality risk (HR 1.42, 95 % CI 1.04–1.94) compared to those with osteoporotic fractures (HR 0.95, 95 % CI 0.94–0.96) (Kim et al., 2023).

The choice of treatment for vertebral fractures depends on both the characteristics of the injury—such as instability, deformity, or neurological deficit—and patient-specific factors including comorbidities and frailty (Genev et al., 2017; Winkler et al., 2015). The likelihood of requiring spinal fusion is higher in fractures caused by high-energy trauma. Jansson et al. (2010) reported that in patients over 60 years old, the probability of needing spinal fusion was 3.32 times higher after falls from height and 2.92 times higher after traffic accidents, compared with same-level falls. This procedure has become increasingly common in the aging population, even for low-energy fractures (Jansson et al., 2010). Similarly, Zaw et al. (2025) found that elderly patients (≥80 years) who underwent vertebral fusion after low-energy thoracolumbar fractures achieved postoperative improvement comparable to younger patients, despite a higher prevalence of osteoporosis and no significant differences in implant failure or revision rates. Moreover, minimally invasive surgery (MIS) has been shown to increase the likelihood of surgical intervention in this group (Viola et al., 2025).

Spinal surgery has traditionally been associated with an elevated mortality rate. However, this may reflect the baseline risk of patients requiring surgery rather than the procedure itself (Smith et al., 2012; Schoenfeld et al., 2011). In an analysis of 10,849 spinal operations, mortality after spinal fusion (0.24 %) exceeded that of other procedures (0.06 %), but rates varied by indication—from 0.1 % in degenerative pathology to 2.2 % in metastatic disease (Smith et al., 2012). Age and wound contamination were identified as key predictors of early postoperative mortality (Schoenfeld et al., 2011). Similarly, a large study from the University of California involving nearly 23,000 patients over 55 years found that spinal fusion, despite being associated with longer hospital stays and more complications, was linked to lower mortality than conservative management (Winkler et al., 2015).

Despite the evidence of studies that have delineated the correlation between vertebral surgery and mortality risk, a paucity of research has been conducted that has sub-analyzed the findings according to the mechanism of fracture production. Furthermore, the current literature does not provide a definitive answer to the question of whether fractures resulting from high-energy trauma pose a higher risk of postoperative mortality in older patients treated with vertebral fusion compared to those resulting from low-energy trauma. In a clinical context, this distinction is of pertinence, especially considering potential confounding and collinear factors in the analysis, given that the indication for surgery and the chosen technique can exert a substantial influence on patient health outcomes.

This study aims to analyze whether all-cause mortality differs between high- and low-energy thoracolumbar fractures requiring surgical fusion in patients over 50 years, after adjusting for potential confounding factors. The secondary purpose is to identify which factors increase the risk of mortality in this specific population.

Material and methods

The study used a unicentric retrospective observational design based on hospital registry data. It was conducted at Puerta de Hierro University Hospital, which treats around 150 patients with thoracolumbar compression fractures and 50 patients with high-energy thoracolumbar fractures annually. Most osteoporotic fractures undergo non-surgical management (80 %), followed in frequency by vertebral augmentation procedures (14 %) and spinal instrumentation (6 %). The study was approved by the local Ethics Committee (reference PI 94/24) and conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and subsequent amendments or equivalent ethical standards.

For the mortality analysis, the date of end of the follow-up or death was obtained from the public health information system or by contacting the patient or family members by telephone if the follow-up could not be performed due to changes in the residence or country. The cause of death was also recorded when applicable. For surviving patients, the end of follow-up was February 13th and 14th, 2025.

Statistical analysis

The information was stored in the database and processed and analyzed using Stata version 18 (StataCorp. 2023. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).

Numerical variables were represented by either the mean and standard deviation (SD) or the median and the 25th and 75th percentiles (P25; P75) and were contrasted with the student-T test or the Mann Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests. Absolute and relative frequencies were used to describe categorical variables, which were contrasted with the Chi-square test.

Overall survival was assessed by defining the event as death from any cause and measuring the time from the date of surgery until the date of death or the last follow-up date for patients without an event. Patients who were still alive at the last follow-up date were censored on this date. Median follow-up time was estimated by the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. Survival was determined using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences according to the mechanism of fracture production were compared using the log-rank test.

Several univariable Cox regression analyses were performed to evaluate risk factors associated with overall survival. Next, a multivariable Cox regression analysis was constructed by including variables with p < 0.05 in the univariable analysis. Collinearity between ASA classification and Charlson comorbidity index was assessed using pairwise correlation and variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis. Due to a strong correlation between ASA and Charlson index, the ASA variable was excluded from the final model to avoid multicollinearity. HRs were estimated for all analyses along with their corresponding 95 % confidence intervals (95 % CIs). The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. If this assumption was not met, the effects of time-varying covariates were modeled using interaction with log(time). Each statistical hypothesis was tested with two tails. A significance value of 5 % was accepted for all tests.

This study was made following the recommendations of the STROBE statement.

Results

Sample description

Of the 151 patients that initially met inclusion criteria, four were excluded due to a concomitant cervical fracture which actually needed cervical fusion, one due to a second fracture process occurring at a later date, three due to pathological fractures with an underlying oncological process, and two due to loss to follow-up following transfer to a mutual insurance company or their country of origin. Thus, the final analysis included 141 patients (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram showing the process of patient selection.

The mean age was 66.3 years (SD 10.23), and most participants were women (56.0 %). The mean Charlson comorbidity index score was 3.4 (SD 2.29), and the median was 3 (interquartile range 2–5), corresponding to an estimated 10-year survival rate of 77.5 %. Most patients (88.5 %) had an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification of ASA II or III. Of these patients, 80.9 % had fractures in a single vertebra, compared to 19.1 % in two, three or four vertebrae. The number of vertebrae fused was also examined, and the majority required fusion of two to four segments (46.8 % and 32.6 %, respectively), with a mean of 3.0 (SD 1.17). Of the patients, 61.7 % underwent spinal fusion only, while 33.3 % also required cementation and 5.0 % decompression. Additionally, most interventions were open (78.7 %). Only 4.3 % of patients required emergent surgery; the remainder of the procedures were performed on a scheduled basis. The median hospital stay from admission to discharge was 18 days (interquartile range 13–24). Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the sample.

Survival analysis

The median follow-up period was 7.9 years (95 % confidence interval: 7.17 to 8.59). The overall survival rates at 1, 3, 5, 10, and 15 years for patients over 50 with a thoracolumbar vertebral fracture requiring spinal fusion were 95.7 %, 85.0 %, 79.4 %, 71.9 %, and 59.5 %, respectively. Comparing mortality according to fracture mechanism, significantly higher mortality was observed when the fracture was secondary to minor trauma (p < 0.001), with differences observed in both the short and long term (Fig. 2; Table 2).

Risk factors for mortality

Cox univariable analysis revealed a statistically significant association between the hazard of mortality and the following factors: a low-energy mechanism, a higher Charlson comorbidity index score, urgent surgical intervention, and patient age (Table 4). However, no such association was observed concerning sex, the number of fractured vertebrae, the type of procedure performed, the technique used, or the number of segments fixed.

For the multivariable analysis, the following variables were selected based on a p-value under 0.05 in the previous analysis: age, the Charlson comorbidity index, fracture mechanism, and time of surgery. The ASA classification was discarded because of the strong collinearity with the Charlson comorbidity index. As the proportional hazards assumption was not met for age, an interaction term was added to the model to account for the varying effects of age over time. The adjusted Cox regression model confirmed that, even after accounting for age and comorbidity, a low-energy mechanism remained independently associated with higher mortality (HR 2.82, 95 % CI 1.28–6.17), together with higher Charlson index (HR 1.42, 95 % CI 1.25–1.63), and emergent surgery (HR 4.27, 95 % CI 1.43–12.77) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that older patients undergoing spinal fusion after thoracolumbar fracture have a higher risk of short- and long-term mortality when the fracture is the result of a low-energy mechanism compared to high-energy mechanisms, even after adjusting for age and comorbidity. In addition, a high Charlson comorbidity index and emergent surgery were also identified as independent risk factors for mortality. However, the influence of potential confounding factors remains substantial, suggesting that the apparent effect of trauma mechanism partly reflects the patients’ overall frailty and health status.

These findings contrast with those obtained in the only comparable study analyzing this relationship (Kim et al., 2023). This is a population-based study conducted in South Korea between 2005 and 2018, in which fractures resulting from high-energy mechanisms in patients over 65 years old were associated with an elevated mortality risk (HR 1.42) relative to a control group. The effect was more pronounced in the age group younger than 65 (HR 2.06). Conversely, osteoporosis manifested as a protective factor in older patients, although the Korean study exclusively encompassed compression fractures and did not stratify patients based on treatment (Kim et al., 2023). The authors posit that the observed outcomes are attributable to a heightened prevalence of traumatic injuries among younger patients and an elevated incidence of osteoporosis among older patients.

Nonetheless, Despite the heterogeneity between cohorts regarding age, sex, and Charlson comorbidity index, as well as the interaction among age, comorbidity burden, and trauma mechanism, the multivariable analysis in the present study revealed a higher mortality risk when spinal fusion was performed for fractures caused by low-energy trauma. This association may reflect the increased frailty of elderly patients and their reduced physiological reserve to recover after fracture and surgery. Previous studies have linked frailty to a higher incidence of postoperative complications and mortality (Wahl et al., 2017). A recent study analyzed one-year mortality in patients of any age suffering thoracolumbar fractures, reporting a 50 % reduction in the rate when the injury was produced by a high-energy mechanism, observing a negative impact (although not significant) in those patients who underwent surgery versus those who did not (Striano et al., 2024). In this instance, however, the study did not differentiate according to age group, thus precluding meaningful comparisons.

The relationship between age, treatment choice, and mortality after vertebral fractures has been previously explored. Winkler et al. (2015) found that spinal fusion significantly reduced mortality both in patients aged 55–69 years (odds ratio [OR] 0.20, 95 % CI 0.12–0.34) and in those over 70 years (OR 0.64, 95 % CI 0.43–0.94). However, age above 70 remained an independent predictor of mortality. Although their study included similar proportions of low-energy (40.4 %) and high-energy (42.5 %) fractures, the authors did not analyze whether the trauma mechanism itself influenced postoperative mortality (Winkler et al., 2015).

Regarding the factors associated with increased mortality, our results identified both a high Charlson comorbidity index and the need for urgent surgical intervention as independent predictors of death. Previous studies have also shown that urgent procedures are linked to higher postoperative mortality. In general surgery, 30-day mortality is significantly greater for urgent (OR 2.92) and emergent (OR 2.32) interventions compared with elective ones (Mullen et al., 2017). Similarly, in neurosurgery, urgent operations in older patients have been associated with higher 30-day and overall mortality, as well as a greater rate of complications (Bligh et al., 2020).

The influence of comorbidities on postoperative outcomes has been extensively documented. Patients with a high Charlson comorbidity index experience a markedly higher mortality following spinal trauma surgery (13.8 %) than those with a low index (1.7 %) (McGee et al., 2022). Moreover, a large proportion of patients with proximal or distal fractures present with at least two comorbidities at the time of injury—more frequently in men (50.7 %) than in women (39.4 %)—and this disease burden negatively affects recovery, particularly in vertebral fractures. Men with few comorbidities have shown an excess mortality up to 7.3 times higher than the general population, while women show a 4.9-fold increase, though still lower than that observed in men (Tran et al., 2022). This difference may be partly explained by underdiagnosed chronic conditions in males, which could contribute to higher postoperative complication rates (Kannegaard et al., 2010). These observations align with the findings of Striano et al. (2024), who suggested that the higher mortality observed in injured men results from the combined effect of a greater comorbidity burden and a higher incidence of high-energy trauma. Altogether, these data highlight the interaction among age, fracture pattern, injury mechanism, trauma severity, and comorbidities as key determinants of survival (Striano et al., 2024).

In addition to the Charlson comorbidity index, there are alternative scales to analyze patient frailty and its impact on surgical interventions. Taghlabi et al. (2024) applied the 5-item Modified Frailty Index to explore the effect of comorbidities on mortality after thoracolumbar spinal fusion. The authors observed that patients with higher frailty scores had a 1.38-fold greater risk of 30-day mortality (95 % CI 1.24–1.54) (Taghlabi et al., 2024). Similarly, Fields et al. (2024) reported that higher scores on the Risk Analysis Index—a tool for identifying frail patients who may require additional evaluation—were associated with an increased probability of death within one year after surgery (Fields et al., 2024). The data hereby detailed emphasize the significance of preoperative patient selection and, in the case of intervention, the selection of those patients who can wait for scheduled intervention.

The extant literature also describes other factors possibly related to mortality in thoracolumbar fractures, such as multiple fractures or the choice of an open technique versus MIS. The latter has been associated with reduced immediate postoperative pain, minimized blood loss and a shorter mean length of stay. Nevertheless, the impact of this phenomenon on mortality remains uncertain (Schoenfeld et al., 2011; Daher et al., 2025). Finally, Tezeren and Kuru (2005) conclude that long posterior fusion does not demonstrate significant clinical results compared with short posterior fusion, despite radiographic parameters demonstrating that short fixation is more effective in a recent study of limited sample size (Tezeren and Kuru, 2005). However, it does not analyze the impact on mortality. None of the aforementioned factors in the present cohort were significantly associated with an elevated mortality risk. However, the necessity to incorporate a cementation procedure into the fusion was associated with an elevated risk, yielding a result that did not attain statistical significance.

Several limitations must be mentioned regarding this study. Firstly, the retrospective design of the study makes it difficult to access all information needed. Furthermore, this restricted access to information may influence the outcomes observed. However, only two patients were excluded from the study due to the absence of follow-up (both were younger than 75 years, belonged to the high-energy cohort, and traveled to their respective home countries after hospital discharge). Therefore, it appears improbable that their exclusion would have had a significant impact on the study's results. Secondly, potential residual confounding factors are not included, such as antiosteoporosis drug intake, bone density T-score, or modified 5-item frailty index. Additionally, the study was conducted in a single hospital center, which limits the generalizability of the results. Finally, it is possible that the combination of the sample size and the low number of events overestimated the differences observed. The design of a prospective multicenter study that includes information on patient frailty and osteoporosis diagnosis could provide more solid information. However, a significant strength of this study is the use of a multivariable analysis, enabling the identification of independent associations (and quantifying them) and minimizing the effect of confounding factors.

Conclusion

After adjustment for age, comorbidity, and surgical urgency, elderly patients who sustained thoracolumbar fractures and underwent spinal fusion surgery exhibited a substantially diminished survival probability when the fracture was caused by minor or absent trauma in comparison to those fractures resulting from high-energy trauma. In addition to the mechanism, the Charlson comorbidity index and the performance of the intervention on an urgent basis were identified as independent factors associated with higher all-cause mortality in this group of patients.

The findings emphasize the need for personalized patient assessment, particularly in cases involving patients with elevated risk profiles. The selection process and preoperative optimization must be prioritized.
