Characteristics and Correlates of Mirror Movements in Parkinson’s Disease
Authors: Pritish Gupta, Manavjot Singh, Birinder Singh Paul, Manraj Singh, Sidakbir Singh Paul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:This study aimed to assess the frequency, characteristics, and clinical associations of mirror movements in individuals with Parkinson’s disease, considering factors such as age, side of symptom onset, disease severity, and motor asymmetry.Methods:This cross-sectional observational study included 87 individuals with Parkinson’s disease, diagnosed according to Movement Disorder Society criteria. Exclusion criteria included atypical Parkinsonism, dementia, and refusal to consent. Mirror movements were assessed using video-recorded standardized tasks and scored with the Woods Teuber scale. Clinical parameters, including the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS-III), Hoehn and Yahr scale (H and Y staging), and lateralized motor severity, were analyzed. Statistical correlations were performed using SPSS version 26.Results:Mirror movements were observed in 65.5% of participants, predominantly affecting the upper limbs (98% in fingers). The frequency of mirror movements increased with age (84.6% in those aged 70–90 years, P = 0.04) and was significantly higher in individuals with left-sided symptom onset (74.1% vs. 51.5%, P = 0.03). The severity of mirroring correlated with the side of disease onset and greater lateral differences in UPDRS motor scores (>30, P = 0.015). A rare subtype of discordant mirror movements (non-homologous movements) was identified in 8.2% of individuals.Conclusions:Mirror movements are a frequent and underrecognized feature in individuals with Parkinson’s disease. Their clinical associations indicate a greater frequency in older individuals, those with left-sided onset, and those with more asymmetric motor involvement. These findings highlight the potential utility of mirror movements as a clinical biomarker for disease lateralization and progression. Further research, including functional imaging studies, is warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Introduction

Human movements are broadly categorized as voluntary or involuntary. Unilateral voluntary movements require coordinated activation of the contralateral premotor cortex, along with inhibition of ipsilateral motor neurons, to ensure the smooth and isolated execution of motor commands.[1] Mirror movements (MMs) are involuntary, unintended movements that occur in homologous muscles on the side opposite to the voluntary movement, effectively “mirroring” the intended action. While physiologically present during infancy, the persistence of MMs beyond early development is considered pathological. They are observed in several congenital or acquired neurological conditions but often go unnoticed in clinical practice due to their subtlety and exclusion from routine neurological assessments.[2] Most previous studies aim to establish the pathophysiology of MMs, revealing disrupted basal ganglia output that results in impaired interhemispheric inhibition and aberrant activation of corticospinal pathways during voluntary motor tasks.[13] These movements may serve as a clinically useful sign, especially in distinguishing idiopathic Parkinson’s disease (PD) from atypical parkinsonian syndromes such as corticobasal degeneration, where their distribution differs. Espay et al,[4] in their study of twenty-seven individuals, and Vidal et al,[5] in their study of twenty-one individuals with PD, described the occurrence of MMs predominantly in the early stages of the disease.[3] However, they remain underrecognized in clinical evaluation and are still underexplored in the literature. We undertook this study to delineate the factors associated with the occurrence of MMs and establish any relationship between MMs and disease characteristics in a large cohort of PD patients from northern India.

Methods

Subjects

We conducted a cross-sectional observational study involving 112 individuals diagnosed with PD according to the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) Clinical Diagnostic Criteria.[6] The participants were evaluated at the movement disorder clinic of a tertiary care center in North India. All consecutive individuals meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period were included. Inclusion criteria required participants to be adults (age > 18 years) and able to follow verbal instructions to complete motor tasks. Exclusion criteria included atypical parkinsonian syndromes (multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal syndrome, and dementia with Lewy bodies), patients with advanced PD (stage 5), or those with severe dyskinesia, and Parkinson’s patients with dementia (Montreal Cognitive Assessment score <26; and <19 for those with less than 9 years of formal education.[78] All ethical guidelines were followed, and study approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee. Prior to enrollment, written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients for video recording of their movements. Demographic data and clinical disease characteristics were collected, including age at onset, handedness, disease duration, side and nature of symptom onset, and gender. Neurological examination was performed using Part III (motor subsection) of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS), and Hoehn and Yahr (H and Y) staging was recorded. The UPDRS motor examination was conducted by a neurologist in the defined “ON” medication state, at a consistent time after the participant’s usual dopaminergic dose to ensure uniformity. Asymmetry in motor symptoms was determined by comparing the total right and left limb scores of the UPDRS-III and categorized as <15, 15–30, or >30 point differences.

Assessment of mirroring movements

MMs were assessed using a structured motor task protocol adapted from Espay et al.[4] Participants performed four voluntary movements: (1) thumb–index finger tapping, (2) hand opening and closing, (3) hand pronation–supination, and (4) ankle dorsiflexion–plantarflexion. Each movement was performed five times per limb, with a rest period of at least one minute between tasks. While the contralateral limb executed the movement, the ipsilateral limb was kept at rest: in the lap for upper limb tasks and lightly resting on the ground for lower limb tasks. A neurologist, blinded to the UPDRS scoring, side of disease onset, and other disease characteristics, scored the MMs. Participants were not informed that the focus was on MMs to minimize bias.

Scoring of mirror movements

MMs were evaluated using a combination of two validated approaches: the Woods and Teuber Scale. This classical scale was used for the initial qualitative assessment of MMs. It is a descriptive scale that grades mirroring from 0 (no mirroring) to 4 (continuous mirroring closely mimicking the voluntary movement). Each motor task was rated on three components: Amplitude (0–4): degree of excursion of the mirrored movement; Distribution (0–3): spatial similarity to the voluntary movement; and Proportion (0–3): duration of mirroring during the task. The total score for each task could range from 0 to 10, with a maximum total score of 40 (three hand tasks + one foot task).

Statistical analysis

The collected data was entered into Microsoft Excel sheets for organization and analysis. Descriptive statistics, including mean, median, and standard deviation, were used to summarize continuous variables, while frequency distributions and percentages were used to summarize categorical data. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov or Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of distribution for continuous variables. For group comparisons, the Chi-Square or Fisher’s Exact test was used for categorical variables, depending on cell counts. For continuous variables, Pearson’s correlation was applied to examine the strength and direction of the linear relationship between normally distributed variables, while Spearman’s rank correlation was used for non-normally distributed variables. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical calculations were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.

Results

Out of the 112 screened persons with PD (PwPD), 87 were finally included in the study. Twenty-five were excluded; of these, 14 were not willing to participate in the study and 11 were diagnosed with atypical parkinsonism (7 had progressive supranuclear palsy and 4 had multiple system atrophy). The mean age of the 87 PwPD included in the cohort was 62.95 + 9.845 years, and 55 (63.2%) were males. With respect to disease characteristics, 59 PwPD (67.8%) had tremor as their first symptom, while 10 (11%) were uncertain about their first symptom. Bilateral disease was observed in 64 (73.6%), whereas 26.4% had only unilateral disease. Among the participants, 54 (62.1%) experienced their first symptoms on the left side. Table 1 represents the demographic and disease-related characteristics of the cohort. In this study cohort, MMs were observed in 57 (65.5%) of the PwPD [Video 1]. MMs were observed in 40% of participants under 50 years of age, 65.6% in those aged 50–70 years, and 84.6% in the 70–90 age group. A significant linear correlation exists between the occurrence of MMs (P = 0.04) and the increasing age of onset of PD [Figure 1]. Notably, a significantly higher proportion of individuals with left-sided symptom onset of PD exhibited MMs (74.1%) compared to those who had right-sided symptoms (51.5%; P = 0.03). The mean of the mirroring score was 4.9 ± 5.4. In addition, there was significant laterality of MMs in those who exhibited bilateral occurrence of MMs; the severity score was higher on the side of the start of the disease of PD. Individuals with higher lateralized UPDRS Part III motor scores (difference >30 points) had a higher amplitude of MMs on the same side, which also corresponded to the side of initial symptom onset (P = 0.015). Five (8.2%) PwPD exhibited MMs that did not correspond to the movement of the limb executing the task (the fingers extended when performing the flexion task using the opposite limb). This discordant type of MM was also more common on the side of the symptom onset [Video 2]. Discordant MMs were defined as involuntary movements in the contralateral limb that did not match the type of voluntary movement performed, with no other explanation. A significant difference was observed in the mirroring score (P = 0.04) and the side of occurrences of the first symptoms. Patients with a score >5 were comparatively older than those with a score <5. All participants were receiving levodopa therapy at the time of recruitment. The mean Levodopa Equivalent Daily Dose (LEDD) was 433 ± 187.1 mg/day.

Discussion

There is a paucity of research describing the clinical severity and distribution of MMs across both early and late stages of PD. This study from North India aims to address the knowledge gap regarding the mirroring pattern in PwPD. We observed a high prevalence of MMs (65.5%) in PwPD, consistent with previously reported ranges (29–95.7%).[49]

The majority of MMs were observed in the upper limbs, especially in the fingers (98%), with far fewer occurrences in the arms (2%) or lower limbs (feet: 12%; legs: 0%) [Figure 2]. The predominant occurrence of MMs in the upper limbs may be related to the fact that in most PwPD, the disease typically starts in the upper limbs. In addition, the MMs stem from the abnormal activation of the hemisphere contralateral to mirroring, and the hands and fingers have a larger homunculus representation in the motor cortex compared to the legs and feet. A novel and notable observation in our study was the presence of discordant MMs in 8.2% of participants, where the involuntary movement did not mirror the voluntary task (finger extension during contralateral flexion) [Video 2]. Although rare, these “reverse MMs” still qualify as mirroring phenomena due to their absence at rest and specificity to contralateral voluntary movement. Furthermore, these movements were observed only as mimicking the voluntary motor activity performed by the contralateral hand and fingers. Surprisingly, this finding has not been previously reported as a mirroring phenomenon in persons with PD. A plausible explanation for this is that there was abnormal motor output from the ipsilateral motor cortex to the voluntary movements performed, representing an abnormal cortical enhancement of physiological mirroring. This over-activation of the insula or increased brain activity in the precuneus and motor cortex resulted in the occurrence of reverse MMs as a compensatory effort to enhance motor function due to the degenerative disease.[9] Poisson et al[10] described how the insula’s overactivation in PD may aim to improve motor performance but can lead to MMs due to its bilateral involvement. This increased activation could result from a compensatory effort to enhance motor function, and its broader brain engagement might disrupt normal lateralized motor control, promoting MMs.

Our findings also revealed a linear relationship between age and MM prevalence, with the highest frequency seen in participants aged 70–90 years. This may reflect age-related deterioration in intracortical inhibitory mechanisms, particularly short-interval intracortical inhibition, which is known to decline with aging and neurodegeneration. Additionally, a higher proportion of individuals with left-sided symptom onset exhibited MMs (74.1%) compared to those with right-sided onset (51.5%). Although the mechanisms underlying this lateralization remain unclear, hemispheric differences in motor control and interhemispheric inhibition likely contribute. Our findings concur with other studies from India, which also reported that left-predominating PD patients demonstrate a significantly higher prevalence of MMs.[111213]

MMs typically occurred asymmetrically and were more commonly observed on the more affected side (58.5%) than the less affected side (41.5%) [Table 2]. This finding contradicts a previous study by Espay et al.,[11] which showed that MMs were observed in the less affected or unaffected hand when voluntary movements were performed with the more affected hand. This difference may relate to the inclusion criteria of that study, as they recruited only twenty-seven patients with early PD compared to our larger cohort representing early (<2; 70%) and late (>3; 30%) stages of the disease (H and Y). The authors suggested that rigidity and bradykinesia on the less affected side were reasons for this manifestation of MMs, as motor impairment was less on the other side. Electrophysiological and imaging studies have shown that pathological impairment of dorsal premotor cortex activity and transcallosal inhibition is the most plausible mechanism mediating MMs in PD. Espay et al.[11] described this neurological sign in early asymmetrical parkinsonism when the overall motor impairment is relatively minor. They found no association between this distribution and the UPDRS score or duration of disease. According to the authors, this phenomenon is not necessarily a feature of early disease but instead a characteristic of the asymmetrical nature of the disease process.[14]

The present study found no correlation between the levodopa equivalent dosage and MMs. However, previous studies have suggested a potential role of dopaminergic therapy in the occurrence of MMs in PD patients. Espay conducted a study examining those with minimal UPDRS improvement, who demonstrated fewer MMs.[11] In another case-control study by Chatterjee et al, the number of patients with MMs was higher during the ON phase of levodopa therapy compared to the OFF phase.[1215] As the neurodegenerative process progresses, motor disability also increases (indicated by rising UPDRS part 3 scores), and MMs become more apparent. It was noted that in later stages of the disease, when the UPDRS score difference exceeded thirty, MMs became more noticeable on the contralateral side as well. This observation was noted in present study, and the findings should be further corroborated with functional imaging of the brain.

The mechanisms underlying MMs in PD patients remain unclear, but researchers have proposed several theories, none of which is conclusive. One possibility is that MMs may result from abnormalities in the corticospinal tract, which is responsible for transmitting motor signals from the brain to the spinal cord.[4] Another possibility is that MMs may result from abnormalities in the corpus callosum, which coordinates motor function between the two hemispheres of the brain.[9]

Interestingly, the presence of MMs in PD could be associated with increased brain activity in areas like the precuneus/ Post Cingulate Cortex (PCC), which is involved in proactive inhibition.[16] Boulinguez and colleagues have suggested a “proactive inhibition” model, where the precuneus/PCC plays a key role in preventing inappropriate behaviors.[171819] Thus, the overactivation of the precuneus/PCC node might reflect an effort to inhibit abnormal MMs. Similarly, the overactivation of both the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex and the inferior frontal cortex may also represent attempts to suppress these abnormal movements, given their roles in response inhibition and interference suppression.[2021]

This study contributes to the existing literature by providing a detailed clinical characterization of MMs in a large cohort of PwPD across different disease stages in a real-world outpatient setting. Unlike prior studies that focused mainly on early-stage PD, our data capture MM patterns in both early- and later-stage disease, highlighting a broader clinical spectrum and novel features such as task-mismatched mirroring. Clinically, identifying MMs, especially subtle or discordant ones, may help detect disease asymmetry, track progression, and distinguish typical PD from atypical forms like corticobasal degeneration. These findings support the use of MM assessment as a practical, low-cost bedside tool to enhance neurological evaluation. From a research standpoint, MM characteristics may reflect disrupted interhemispheric motor control, warranting further study through neurophysiological or imaging methods to explore their potential as clinical biomarkers in PD.

The present study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, and as a single-center study, this may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the lack of an age-matched healthy control group restricts the interpretation of the absolute frequency of MMs in PD relative to normal aging. We did not perform multivariate analyses adjusting for confounders such as disease duration, H and Y stage, or medication dose, which should be addressed in future studies. Lastly, functional imaging was not performed to confirm the pathophysiological process.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study from North India showcasing the correlation of MMs with age, side of symptom onset, motor-UPDRS scoring, and phenotype of PD. Limited data is available worldwide on this particular subject, which is an often overlooked neurological sign; this highlights the need for extensive, wide-ranging studies including meta-analyses.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that MMs are a common phenomenon in PwPD and are associated with more severe motor impairment. Additional studies are needed to investigate the mechanisms underlying MMs in PwPD and to determine their clinical significance. This study, despite its limited number of participants, sheds light on the characteristics of MMs in PD patients.
