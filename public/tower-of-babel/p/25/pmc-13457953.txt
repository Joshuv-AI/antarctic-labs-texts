A pilot translational study of neoadjuvant fulvestrant plus abemaciclib in women with advanced low-grade serous carcinoma

ABSTRACT
Low-grade serous carcinoma of the ovary (LGSOC) is relatively resistant to chemotherapy. Given its biological parallels to hormone receptor-positive breast cancer, including responsiveness to anti-estrogen therapies, we conducted a pilot phase II study to assess clinical benefit of neoadjuvant fulvestrant and abemaciclib in women with advanced, unresectable LGSOC (NCT03531645). Imaging assessments were performed every 8 weeks until resectable. The primary endpoint was clinical benefit rate (CBR). Exploratory objectives included evaluation of safety profile, accessing biomarkers related to clinical benefit, and description of tumor phenotypic changes. Fifteen patients were enrolled and evaluable for efficacy. CBR was 100%, with 1/15 patients (7%) achieving complete response (CR), 8/15 patients (53%) achieving partial response (PR), and 6/15 (40%) exhibiting stable disease (SD). Interval cytoreductive surgery (ICS) was performed in 9 patients (60%), with 7 (78%) achieving complete or optimal resection. Fourteen tumor samples underwent transcriptomic and proteomic profiling. Tumors from long-term survivors showed significantly higher baseline expression of cell-cycle-related programs and estrogen signaling-related genes, both suppressed upon treatment. Neoadjuvant fulvestrant and abemaciclib was well tolerated, achieved high response rates, and enabled optimal surgical resection in most patients. Tumor proliferative activity and estrogen signaling dependency may predict therapy response.

Introduction

Low-grade serous carcinoma of the ovary (LGSOC) is a rare histologic subtype of epithelial ovarian cancer, representing approximately 5–10% of all serous ovarian carcinomas1. While high-grade serous carcinomas (HGSOC) are usually very chemo-responsive, conventional chemotherapy has demonstrated more limited activity in LGSOC in the neoadjuvant, adjuvant, and recurrent settings2–5. Accordingly, primary surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy is preferred over neoadjuvant therapy for the treatment of LGSOC6. In the adjuvant setting, Grabowski and colleagues performed an exploratory case-control study of the AGO-metadatabase of 4 randomized phase 3 trials, which included 145 patients with primary stage IIIB to IV LGSOC and compared them to patients with HGSOC. Thirty-nine (27%) of these LGSOC patients had residual disease > 1 cm after primary debulking surgery, allowing evaluation of response to adjuvant therapy, and of those, only nine (23%) patients had a complete or partial response to chemotherapy compared to 90% of matched patients with HGSOC4.

Select patients with extensive, unresectable tumors or significant co-morbidities may be candidates for neoadjuvant therapy. Our group previously reported outcomes for 36 patients with advanced, unresectable LGSOC treated with neoadjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy. The median number of chemotherapy cycles was four (range 3–9), with an overall response rate of only 11%. Among the 29/36 LGSOC patients who proceeded to interval surgery, the rate of optimal interval cytoreductive surgery (ICS) was 38%, markedly lower than the 75% observed in a comparator cohort of patients with HGSOC3.

LGSOC has been noted to have more biological similarities with luminal breast cancer than with its HGSOC counterpart. Both LGSOC and luminal breast cancer demonstrate high expression of estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR), significantly worse outcomes in women under the age of 35, and clinical benefit from endocrine therapy in the maintenance and recurrent settings7–9. These parallels have informed the clinical management of LGSOC; hormonal therapies are now established treatments in the recurrent setting, associated with a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 7.4 months and overall survival (OS) of 78.2 months. In a study by Gershenson and colleagues, 61% of hormonal treatment regimens (including anastrozole, fulvestrant, letrozole, leuprolide, megestrol acetate, tamoxifen, raloxifene, and leuprolide + anastrozole/letrozole/tamoxifen) resulted in a PFS duration of at least 6 months7. The role of hormonal maintenance has been assessed in a retrospective cohort study of 203 patients with advanced LGSOC who underwent primary cytoreductive surgery followed by platinum-based chemotherapy and then received either hormonal maintenance therapy or proceeded with observation. The patients who received hormonal maintenance therapy had significantly longer PFS compared with those who were observed without therapy (64.9 vs. 26.4 months, p < 0.001.)8 In luminal breast cancer, cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) 4 signaling plays a critical role in tumor proliferation, and CDK 4/6 inhibitors—targeting the G1-to-S phase transition of the cell cycle—have emerged as effective and well-tolerated therapies10. Based on improvement in both PFS and OS seen with antiestrogen plus CDK4/6 inhibitor combination therapies in HR + breast cancer, we hypothesized that a similar strategy could be effective in LGSOC11–16.

Here, we show the clinical and translational results from a study evaluating CDK4/6 inhibition (abemaciclib) combined with anti-estrogen therapy (fulvestrant) as neoadjuvant treatment for patients with advanced, unresectable LGSOC. The combination therapies demonstrated a clinical benefit rate (CBR) of 100% in the study cohort, with an acceptable safety profile. Translational analyses further suggest that baseline levels of tumor proliferation and estrogen signaling dependency may predict therapy responses. Together, our results demonstrate the clinical importance of neoadjuvant abemaciclib/fulvestrant therapy in LGSOC, providing a basis for further efficacy evaluation and mechanistic studies in larger cohorts.

Results

Patients

A total of 18 patients were enrolled in the study between September 2019 and October 2021 (Fig. 1). The median age at diagnosis was 58 years (range 25–79 years). One patient was not evaluable for efficacy and was removed from the study due to progression of disease prior to completing two cycles of therapy (patient #7). One patient was initially consented but ultimately did not start treatment due to a TP53 mutation noted on confirmatory next-generation sequencing (patient #15), suggesting high-grade serous histology. An additional patient was initially consented after undergoing diagnostic laparoscopy with unresectable disease; however, CT imaging did not demonstrate measurable disease per RECIST v1.1 criteria, and hence the patient was not eligible for the study (patient #16). One patient (patient #10) electively withdrew from the study after the first imaging assessment (and therefore was still evaluable). Of the 15 patients who were evaluable for efficacy, eight had stage III disease at diagnosis, and seven had stage IV disease. Efficacy

The primary endpoint of this study was clinical benefit rate (CBR.) Of the 15 evaluable patients in this pilot study, the best overall response rate was 60% (8 patients had a PR and 1 patient had a CR); the remaining (6/15) patients (40%) had SD, leading to a CBR of 100%. Of note, the patient with CR was also confirmed with pathologic CR at the time of ICS. The median time to partial response for those who responded was 4.6 months. For patients who underwent interval cytoreductive surgery, the median time to surgery was 8.4 months. As of the data cutoff date (April 24, 2024), all patients had completed investigational therapy. Safety

The safety analysis population included 16 patients (all patients who received at least one cycle of the study drugs). Over the course of the study, there were no deaths or any serious adverse events attributed to the study drugs. One patient had a recurrent grade 2 increase in creatinine, and ultimately the dose of abemaciclib was reduced to 100 mg PO BID with improvement of creatinine. An additional patient underwent dose reduction of abemaciclib to 100 mg PO BID due to recurrent grade 3 vaginal hemorrhage from disease (although the bleeding was not likely related to study treatment). Ten patients (63%) experienced grade 1 anemia, and three patients (19%) experienced grade 3 anemia. While 63% of patients experienced grade 1 diarrhea, there were no grade 2 or 3 instances of diarrhea. Three patients experienced grade 3 neutropenia or grade 3 decrease in white blood cell count. There were no grade 4 events in the study. Molecular analysis reveals potential mechanisms of response and resistance

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) with a targeted panel17 was performed on 21 samples from 15 patients (Fig. 5). As expected for LGSOC, all samples exhibited low tumor mutational burden. Consistent with prior studies by our group and others18,19, activating mutations in the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway—specifically, BRAFV600E, KRASG12D—were among the most common mutations observed. Additional somatic mutations, including in CDKN2A and BRCA1, were found in a subset of patients. To explore the relationship between tumor phenotypes and therapeutic response, we performed transcriptomic and proteomic profiling on 14 samples from 8 patients enrolled in the trial, including six patients with long-term PFS and two with short-term PFS (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Table 1). Patients were defined as having “long-term” PFS if PFS was greater than 18 months and “short-term” PFS if PFS was less than 18 months, based on the median after excluding Acc10 who had withdrawn from the study. Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of three long-term survivors (Acc2, Acc9, Acc13) revealed transcriptional changes following treatment with abemaciclib and fulvestrant. Notably, we observed upregulation of the TNF-α/NFκB signaling pathway and hypoxia response upon treatment (Fig. 6b). These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating crosstalk between hypoxia and inflammatory pathways, with NFκB directly implicated in hypoxia signaling20,21. Concurrently, we observed an upregulation of the glycolysis pathway, indicating dynamic metabolic reprogramming. In addition, cell cycle and interferon response-related pathways were downregulated. Corroborating the transcriptomic data, we observed an upregulation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), and downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)-related enzymes, pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) and NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1), along with downregulation of the p38/MAPK pathway, in RPPA data (Fig. 6c). To better understand which tissue compartments contributed to the observed phenotypic changes, we performed GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiling whole transcriptome atlas (DSP-WTA) on nine samples from six patients (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2). We analyzed a total of 47 regions of interest (ROIs) with high-quality data, which were further segmented into 41 tumor- and 46 stromal-enriched areas of illumination (AOIs) (Supplementary Table 2). First, longitudinal comparisons were performed between on-treatment and pre-treatment samples from all profiled patients or long-term survivors (Fig. 6d–g and Supplementary Fig. 2a–d). Consistent with RNA-seq data, we observed an upregulation of genes related to inflammatory and stress responses, such as IL6, EGR1, JUNB and DUSP1, in the tumor compartments, along with a downregulation of markers related to mitochondrial electron transport chain (e.g., NDUFB6, UQCR10), and cell cycle checkpoints (e.g., CDKs). (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 2a). These transcriptomic changes were also reflected at the pathway level by upregulation of the TNF-α/NFκB signaling, the hypoxia pathway, and stress response, with concurrent downregulation of cellular respiration and OXPHOS, in the tumor compartment (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Figs. 2b, 3a,b). Specifically, GREB1, an ER-responsive gene as well as a key ER cofactor that facilitates estrogen-driven tumor growth22–25, was downregulated in the tumor compartment with investigational therapies. This was accompanied by downregulation of MYC-driven oncogenic programs26, along with multiple cell cycle-related signatures, such as G2M checkpoint and E2F target, in the tumor compartments (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Figs. 2b, 3a,b), indicating an inhibition of cancer cell proliferation. In contrast, we observed an upregulation of ER response-related genes in the stromal compartment with treatment, exemplified by ESR1 (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 2c). These observations suggest that as fulvestrant may have effectively inhibited estrogen signaling in tumor cells, it may have concurrently induced compensatory responses in the tumor microenvironment. Taken together, longitudinal comparisons suggest that combined abemaciclib and fulvestrant treatment led to intricate yet coordinated global phenotypic reprogramming of tumor cells, represented by diminished estrogen response, MYC signaling, proliferative capacity, and energy generation by OXPHOS, which, collectively, may contribute to the observed reduction of tumor volume with therapy27.

To further validate key biomarkers potentially related to therapy responses, as observed in transcriptomic data, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) staining on tissue sections from pre- and on-treatment tumor samples (Supplementary Table 3). In line with findings from GeoMx data, a trend of reduced estrogen receptor (ER) protein expression in tumor cells was observed following treatment with abemaciclib and fulvestrant (Fig. 7a). Interestingly, we observed the highest percentages of ER + tumor regions in the pre-treatment biopsies of two patients with long PFS (85% moderate to strong staining in Acc11, 80% weak to moderate in Acc13), and both cases exhibited significant reduction in ER expression upon treatment (15% weak staining in the on-treatment sample from Acc11, 20% weak staining in the on-treatment sample from Acc13, Supplementary Table 3). Indeed, downregulation of ER expression was also observed in breast cancer treated with fulvestrant28,29, and the effect was thought to be associated with ER degradation30. Furthermore, we also evaluated the expression of progesterone receptor (PR), one of the downstream targets of ER signaling (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Table 3). Although both pre- and on-treatment samples from Acc13 were PR-, we observed a significant reduction in PR expression in tumor cells from Acc11 with treatment (25% moderate-to-strong staining in pre-treatment versus 0% in the on-treatment sample), consistent with effective suppression of ER signaling by the investigational therapy. Finally, a recent study showed that high p16 levels (encoded by cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A [CDKN2A]) are associated with a lack of response to CDK4/6 inhibitors in ER + breast cancer31. We therefore sought to identify potential markers of therapeutic response to abemaciclib in our cohort and further examined p16, Cyclin D1, Cyclin E1 and Rb. Each of these markers showed normal expression patterns in both pre- and on-treatment samples of Acc11 and Acc13, though a reduction in Cyclin D1 (binding partner of CDK4)–positive tumor area was observed in both cases with treatment (Supplementary Table 3). In addition, Acc8 showed aberrantly negative staining for Cyclin D1, Cyclin E1 and Rb at baseline, consistent with the lack of response and short PFS in this case (Supplementary Table 3). All evaluable cases exhibited non-aberrant staining of p16 in both pre- and on-treatment timepoints, indicating that it was not a useful biomarker for predicting response to the combination of abemaciclib and fulvestrant in our cohort. Beyond single biomarkers analyzed by IHC, we next sought to characterize the cellular functional states of tumors from short- and long-term survivors using whole transcriptome GeoMx profiling. Cross-sectional comparisons were performed with samples collected at baseline to identify potential predictors of response to therapy. Compared with patients with short PFS, pre-treatment tumor samples from long-term survivors demonstrated higher baseline levels of certain estrogen-responsive genes, alongside higher proliferation capacity and dependency on OXPHOS (Fig. 7d, e). When examining the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in detail, CDK4 was significantly upregulated in the tumor compartment of pre-treatment samples from long-term survivors. In addition, genes encoding OXPHOS-related enzymes, such as UQCR11, FH, NDUFA6; and key genes related to ER signaling, such as GREB1, PGR, and FKBP4, were also upregulated at different levels in certain tumor regions from long-term survivors (Fig. 7d, f and Supplementary Fig. 4a–c). Taken together, transcriptional signatures indicative of high proliferative capacity and baseline CDK4 expression, along with stronger dependency on estrogen signaling, may confer greater sensitivity to combined abemaciclib and fulvestrant therapy in LGSOC tumor cells. Finally, to better understand the transcriptomic features associated with short-term survivors, we performed gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) against two published gene sets of cancer-related metaprograms32,33. Based on these gene sets, we observed an upregulation of interferon response alongside expression of transcriptional programs associated with multiple epithelial lineages — including the ciliated cell lineage — in both tumor and stromal compartments of short-term survivors, (Fig. 7d, e and Supplementary Fig. 5a–c), suggesting active interferon pathway stimulation and greater lineage plasticity in these tumors. Indeed, chronic interferon stimulation has been shown to be immunosuppressive and tumor-promoting34. Further studies will be needed to fully elucidate mechanisms associated with resistance to the current therapies.

Discussion

This study represents an evaluation of a non-chemotherapy regimen for the neoadjuvant treatment of LGSOC. While primary cytoreductive surgery followed by adjuvant therapy is preferred over neoadjuvant therapy for treatment of LGSOC6, there are patients who may be candidates for neoadjuvant treatment due to extensive disease burden, medical comorbidities, or patient preferences (e.g., lack of acceptance of the possibility of a colostomy). Our group has previously reported that in patients with advanced, unresectable LGSOC, the response rate to neoadjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy was only 11%3 as opposed to 60% observed in this study (at the same institution). In addition, the rate of optimal interval cytoreductive surgery following platinum-based chemotherapy was only 38% for patients with LGSOC3 as opposed to 55.5% of patients achieving complete gross resection and an additional 22.2% having optimal cytoreduction with this neoadjuvant combination therapy. While the present study included a small number of patients, this response rate and the rates of complete gross resection and optimal resection are unprecedented in this patient population.

This strategy of CDK4/6 inhibition with endocrine therapy has been evaluated in hormone receptor positive recurrent endometrial cancer: a phase II study of fulvestrant plus abemaciclib demonstrated an objective response rate of 44% and clinical benefit rate of 78%35, and the randomized phase II PALEO trial demonstrated improved PFSwith palbociclib plus letrozole compared with placebo plus letrozole (median PFS 8.3 vs 3.1 months, HR 0.57, p = 0.044)36. In recurrent LGSOC, the phase II trial of ribociclib plus letrozole (GOG 3026) reported an objective response rate of 30.6%, clinical benefit rate of 84%, and median PFS of 14.5 months37. An earlier phase II study of ribociclib and letrozole in relapsed ER-positive ovarian and endometrial cancers demonstrated particularly promising activity in LGSOC, with all three LGSOC patients achieving at least a partial response lasting over 2 years38. These encouraging results in the recurrent setting, combined with our findings in the neoadjuvant setting, strongly support further clinical investigation of this approach in the frontline treatment of patients with LGSOC.

The observed tolerability of abemaciclib with fulvestrant in this study aligns with the known side effects of both agents. The MONARCH 2 trial (NCT02107703)15 was a phase III study comparing abemaciclib with fulvestrant versus fulvestrant alone in patients with advanced breast cancer who had progressed while receiving endocrine therapy. In this study, the most frequent adverse events of any grade in the combination arm included diarrhea (86.4%) and neutropenia (46%). The MONARCH3 trial (NCT02246621)39 evaluated abemaciclib with a nonsteroidal aromatase inhibitor (physician’s choice of anastrozole or letrozole) versus a nonsteroidal aromatase inhibitor alone in women with hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer who had no prior systemic therapy in the advanced setting. Similar to the MONARCH 2 study, diarrhea was the most frequent adverse event in the combination arm (due to the abemaciclib), but was mostly grade 1. These adverse events are comparable to those seen in our pilot study, with diarrhea and neutropenia being frequent side effects (Table 2). With these similarities in adverse event incidence and tolerability, further exploration in LGSOC is safe and feasible.

In this study, we also performed multi-omics profiling and in-depth integrative analysis on available paired pre- and on-treatment samples, shedding light on potential mechanisms of response and resistance to the investigational therapies. Multiple transcriptional programs were identified to be associated with response to abemaciclib and fulvestrant therapy, including stronger dependency of tumor cells on CDK4 as well as estrogen signaling for proliferation and OXPHOS as an energy source in the baseline. Specifically, we identified higher expression levels of CDK4 and GREB1 in pre-treatment tumor samples of long-term survivors. Though the relationship between cell cycle-related gene expression and response to CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy remains controversial40,41, a recent study in melanoma showed the efficacy of dalpiciclib, another CDK4/6 inhibitor, in tumors with CDK4 amplifications42. Previous studies demonstrated that GREB1 interacts with estrogen receptor-α and promotes its downstream effects on target gene transcription22–24. Furthermore, GREB1 mediates cellular proliferation in ovarian and hormone-sensitive breast cancer cells upon estrogen stimulation25,43, and high expression has been associated with better response to endocrine therapy in breast cancer23,24. In line with this, GREB1, along with other putative biomarkers, including CDK4, warrant evaluation in larger confirmatory studies to assess their utility in identifying patients most likely to benefit from CDK4/6 inhibition and endocrine therapy in LGSOC.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The single-arm design and small sample size restrict the ability to draw definitive conclusions. In addition, the discretionary nature of deciding whether to proceed with neoadjuvant chemotherapy and when to intervene for an attempt at interval cytoreductive surgery by the treating physician poses a potential source of bias impacting the reported clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, the unprecedented response rate and notably high rates of optimal cytoreduction (exceeding those observed with conventional platinum-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy) support the urgent need for further investigation of this combination therapy in larger trials. This approach holds transformative potential to redefine the treatment paradigm for LGSOC by providing a chemotherapy-free and efficacious alternative in the frontline neoadjuvant setting.

Methods

Human research participants

This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the MD Anderson Cancer Center Institutional Review Board. The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. This trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03531645).

Study design

We conducted an open-label, single-arm, translational pilot study of neoadjuvant abemaciclib with fulvestrant (and goserelin if premenopausal with ovaries in situ) in 15 patients with advanced LGSOC who were deemed unlikely to achieve benefit from primary cytoreductive surgery by the treating physician based on imaging and/or minimally invasive surgical evaluation. This was a single-institution study performed at the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center. The primary objective of this study was to estimate the clinical benefit rate (partial response (PR), complete response (CR), and stable disease (SD)) of this regimen using RECIST v1.1 criteria based on the patient’s best overall response in the neoadjuvant setting. Exploratory objectives included evaluation of toxicity as assessed by CTCAE (Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events) v5.0. Additional exploratory objectives included accessing biomarkers related to clinical benefit and description of tumor transcriptional and proteomic expression changes induced by the abemaciclib and fulvestrant. (Supplementary Note)

Patient eligibility

Patients were required to have a clinical or surgical determination of stage III or IV LGSOC with measurable disease by RECIST v1.1 and recommended to receive neoadjuvant therapy after being assessed as unlikely to derive benefit from primary cytoreduction by the treating physician. Histologic diagnosis required surgical or core biopsy (rather than cytology or fine needle aspiration), with all diagnoses reviewed and confirmed by gynecologic pathologists at MD Anderson Cancer Center. Pre- and on-treatment tissue was collected by either diagnostic biopsy, research biopsy, or laparoscopic assessment with biopsy. Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status was required to be 0-1. In addition, patients were required to have adequate renal, hepatic, and hematologic function. Patients were ineligible if they had current use of potent CYP3A4 inducers and drugs known to prolong the QT interval, if they had been diagnosed with another malignancy within 3 years, or if they were known to have serious pre-existing medical conditions. Complete protocol details are available in the Supplementary Note.

Treatment and response evaluation

All patients on study received fulvestrant as a 500 mg intramuscular (i.m.) injection on days 1 and 15 of Cycle 1, followed by day 1 of all subsequent cycles. Abemaciclib was administered at a dose of 150 mg orally twice daily continuously, initiated on day 1 of cycle 1. Patients received 8 weeks of neoadjuvant therapy followed by assessment of response using RECIST v1.1 criteria, with a CT scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis. Those with stable disease or partial response but still unresectable continued neoadjuvant therapy with repeat CT assessment of disease every 8 weeks. Those with complete response or partial response and deemed resectable then underwent interval cytoreductive surgery. Those with progressive disease were removed from the study and treated per standard of care. Dose modification was performed in a standard fashion and is described in the protocol (Supplementary Note).

Determination of surgical resectability was made by the patient’s gynecologic oncologist based on the risks, benefits, and alternatives to the proposed cytoreductive surgery. The patient’s other medical comorbidities, wishes, risk tolerance, and attitudes toward potentially indicated procedures were also considered.

After interval cytoreductive surgery, patients received adjuvant abemaciclib with fulvestrant until progression or for a maximum of 16 weeks. Patients who reached maximum protocol-directed therapy received letrozole or another aromatase inhibitor as maintenance therapy per the discretion of the treating physician. Patients were followed for disease progression and survival every 3 months. Patients who discontinued therapy for reasons other than progressive disease continued to have post-treatment follow-up for disease status until disease progression, initiating a non-study cancer treatment, withdrawing consent, or becoming lost to follow-up.

Statistical design

Given the rarity of LGSOC and the lack of prior studies on this combination therapy in the neoadjuvant setting, we designed this pilot study to provide preliminary efficacy data that could inform future larger studies, with planned accrual of 15 evaluable patients (non-evaluable patients were replaced but included in safety reporting). The sample size was deemed appropriate, balancing the need to assess clinical outcomes while considering feasibility and the limited number of patients with advanced unresectable LGSOC. The evaluable patient population consisted of all patients enrolled on study and who received at least 2 cycles of abemaciclib and fulvestrant and had a baseline (screening) and follow-up tumor assessment. The population evaluated for safety included all patients who received at least one dose of either study drug. The primary endpoint was the clinical benefit rate (complete response, partial response, and stable disease) assessed using RECIST v1.1 based on the patient’s best overall response in the neoadjuvant setting. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the clinical characteristics of the patients, and their objective response by imaging assessment. Best overall response rate, clinical benefit rate, and surgical outcome status were calculated along with 95% Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals. OS and PFS were estimated with the methods of Kaplan and Meier. Overall survival was estimated from treatment initiation to the earliest date of death or last follow-up. PFS was estimated from treatment initiation to the earliest date of progression or death. For those who were alive and progression-free at the time of analysis, the PFS time was censored at the date of last imaging assessment. Adverse events were summarized using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (version 5.0). Frequencies for the entire population and the maximum degree of toxicity were reported. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/MP v17.0 (College Station, TX).

Clinical sample collection and processing

Pre-treatment image-guided core biopsies were collected prior to the first dose of study drug. On-treatment tissue sampling was performed at the time of interval cytoreductive surgery. When possible, on-treatment biopsies were obtained from the same area of the tumor as pre-treatment biopsies. Samples were collected, labeled, processed, and stored by the MD Anderson Cancer Center Gynecologic Oncology Tumor Bank for translational studies. Sample identification and associated patient-level data was entered into a secure database. Sample collection for translational analysis was based on the maximal availability of patient specimens. All collected samples were submitted for independent pathological quality control by a board-certified pathologist, including initial assessment of tumor cellularity, tissue integrity, and adequacy for downstream assays. Only samples that met predefined pathology QC criteria were then used for subsequent molecular and translational profiling. Due to the limited tissue availability and variability in specimen yield, not all assays could be performed. Thus, individual analyses were conducted on all qualifying specimens with sufficient materials.

RNA-seq library preparation and sequencing

RNA-seq was performed at the MD Anderson Cancer Genomics Core Laboratory, following the procedures we previously described44. Briefly, total RNA was extracted from patients’ frozen tissues using the Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantification was performed with the Quant-iT™ RiboGreen™ RNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RNA quality was assessed using the Agilent RNA 6000 Nano Kit and the 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the extracted RNA using the SureSelect cDNA Module (Agilent) and purified with AMPure XP beads. The cDNA was chemically fragmented using 2X Priming Buffer (Agilent Inc.). Library preparation was conducted with the SureSelect XT Low Input Reagent Kit (Agilent Technologies) using indexes 1–96 and automated on the Sciclone G3 NGSx Workstation (PerkinElmer, Inc.). Individual libraries were hybridized to Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon v.4 probes (Agilent Technologies), with hybridization and post-hybridization steps automated on the Sciclone G3 NGSx Workstation. Agilent captures were performed as single-sample reactions using 500–1000 ng of prepared library input, adhering to Agilent’s protocol. Captured libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 2 × 150 paired-end reads and an 8-nt index read, utilizing Cycle Sequencing v3 reagents (Illumina). Raw RNA-seq data were processed using an in-house RNA-seq analysis pipeline, which included tools such as STAR aligner45 for aligning reads to the hg19 human reference genome, featureCounts46 for quantifying aligned reads to generate raw counts, Oncofuse47 for filtering and prioritizing fusion candidates, and FastQC and QualiMap48 for quality control of raw reads and feature counts.

RNA-seq data analysis

RNA-seq FASTQ files were processed through FastQC (v0.11.5), a quality control tool to evaluate the quality of sequencing reads at both the base and read levels and RNA-SeQC (v1.1.8) to generate a series of RNA-seq related quality control metrics49. All samples passed the quality check for this study. STAR 2-pass alignment (v2.7.0 f)19 was performed with default parameters to generate RNA- seq BAM files. HTSeq-count (v0.11.0)50 tool was applied to aligned RNA-seq BAM files to count for each gene how many aligned reads overlap with its exons. In the initial assessment of transcriptomic level similarity and distinctions of all the samples, 2 samples shown as significant outliers were removed from further analysis. Differential gene expression analyses were then performed using the R package DESeq2 (version 1.42.1)51. A ranked list of genes was generated based on the Wald statistic in DESeq2 for all coding genes and processed by Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)52 using the R package clusterProfiler (version 4.6.2)53 against the hallmark gene sets from Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB)54 to identify significantly enriched signaling pathways. The false discovery rate (FDR) q-values were used for multiple hypothesis testing55. The results from the above analyses were visualized with the R package ggplot2 (version 3.4.2), and the top 10 (or the maximum numbers if less than 10) significantly enriched pathways were shown. All the downstream analyses were performed in the R statistical environment (version 4.3.0, https://www.r-project.org/).

Targeted DNA sequencing

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from samples using the QIAamp® DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) and quantified with the Quant-iT™ PicoGreen™ dsDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quality assessment of gDNA was conducted using the Genomic DNA ScreenTape and Reagents on the TapeStation 4200 (Agilent Technologies). Up to 200 ng of gDNA per sample, as determined by PicoGreen quantification, was mechanically fragmented using the E220 Focused-ultrasonicator (Covaris) under the following parameters: Peak Incident Power 200, Duty Cycle 25%, Cycles per Burst 50, and duration 10 s for 70 iterations. Fragmentation quality was confirmed on the TapeStation 4200 with the DNA High Sensitivity kit (Agilent Technologies). Sheared DNA proceeded to library preparation using the SureSelect XT Low Input Reagent Kit with indexes 1–96 (Agilent Technologies), automated on the Sciclone G3 NGSx Workstation (PerkinElmer, Inc.). The process included three enzymatic reactions: end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation, followed by barcode insertion via PCR using Herculase II Fusion DNA Polymerase (8–14 cycles, depending on DNA input quality and quantity). PCR products were purified using the Agencourt AMPure PCR Purification kit (Agencourt Bioscience Corporation). Library quality and quantity were evaluated on the TapeStation 4200 using the DNA High Sensitivity kit, confirming appropriate fragment sizes and the absence of primer dimers. Prepared libraries were hybridized to custom-designed probes targeting 322 cancer-relevant genes, including regions of interest for global copy number analysis (Agilent Technologies)44. Hybridization steps were automated on the Sciclone G3 NGSx Workstation (PerkinElmer, Inc.) and performed as single-sample reactions using 500–1000 ng of input library. Hybridization was conducted at 65 °C for up to 24 h, following Agilent’s protocol. Captured DNA fragments were isolated using streptavidin beads, washed, and enriched by PCR amplification. The quality and quantity of enriched libraries were assessed on the TapeStation 4200 with the DNA High Sensitivity kit. Captured libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a TruSeq paired-end v3 flow cell. Sequencing was performed to generate 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads with a 12 nt index read using Cycle Sequencing v3 reagents (Illumina). Sequence data were demultiplexed using CASAVA 1.8.2 with no mismatches, generating fastq.gz files. All target regions achieved > 20 × read coverage. For data analysis, reads were aligned to the hg19 human reference genome using BWA and duplicates were removed with Picard. Variants, including single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small indels, were identified using an in-house pipeline and classified as somatic, germline, or loss of heterozygosity based on variant allele frequencies in tumor and matched normal tissues.

DNA sequencing data analysis

The analysis of the targeted DNA sequencing data followed the methods we previously reported17. Raw output of Illumina exome sequencing data was processed using Illumina’s Consensus Assessment of sequence and Variation (CASAVA) tool (v1.8.2) for demultiplexing and conversion to FASTQ format. The FASTQ files were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using BWA (v0.7.5)56 with 3 mismatches (2 mismatches must be in the first 40 seed regions) for a 76-base sequencing run. The aligned BAM files were then subjected to mark duplication, realignment and base recalibration using Picard (v1.112) and GATK (v3.1-1) software tools57. The generated BAM files were then used for downstream analysis. A genotyping quality check was performed to rule out any possible sample swapping or contamination. MuTect (v1.1.4)58 was applied to identify somatic point mutations, and Pindel (v0.2.4)59 was applied to identify small insertions and deletions (Indels). The MuTect and Pindel outputs were then run through our pipeline for filtering and annotation. Briefly, only MuTect calls marked as “KEEP” were selected and taken into the next step. For both substitutions and Indels, mutations with a low variant allelic fraction (VAF < 0.02) or had a low total read coverage ( < 20 reads for tumor samples; < 10 reads for germline samples) were removed. In addition, Indels that had an immediate repeat region within 25 base pairs downstream towards its 3’ region were also removed. After that, common variants reported by ExAc (the Exome Aggregation Consortium), Phase-3 1000 Genome Project, or the NHLBI GO Exome Sequencing Project (ESP6500) were further removed. The intronic mutations, mutations at 3’ or 5’ UTR or UTR flanking regions, silent mutations, in-frame small insertions and deletions were also removed. Subsequently, the mutational profile of the cohort was visualized using the R package ComplexHeatmap (version 2.18.0).

RPPA assays and data analysis

The RPPA assay was performed at the MD Anderson Functional Proteomics Reverse Phase Protein Array (RPPA) Core, following previously described procedures44. In brief, frozen tumor tissues from patients were analyzed to assess protein expression and phosphorylation statuses. Antibodies targeting specific proteins and their phosphorylated forms were used, as most antibodies employed in RPPA analyses are preselected for their relevance to signaling pathways implicated in tumor development (Supplementary Table 4). Samples were probed collectively with these antibodies, and signal intensities from the RPPA arrays were quantified using MicroVigene software (VigeneTech). The data were subsequently processed with the R package Super-Curve (version 1.01; https://bioinformatics.mdanderson.org/public-software/archive/oompa/), yielding relative log2 expression values for each protein. After the initial assessment, two samples were removed as outliers. The RPPA values were then compared between pre-treatment and on-treatment groups using the Wilcoxon test. Subsequently, proteins showing significantly different expression levels between two timepoints were retained, and the RPPA values were scaled and visualized as a heatmap using the R package pheatmap (version 1.0.12).

GeoMx assays

To perform spatial gene expression analysis utilizing the GeoMx digital spatial profiler (DSP) platform (Bruker Spatial Biology), we selected formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) biopsy samples. H&E-stained slides from biopsy samples were reviewed by a pathologist to confirm the presence of tumor tissue and an adequate number of cells for DSP RNA profiling. FFPE tumor tissues were sliced into 5 µm-thick sections and then deparaffinized, rehydrated, and processed with the Leica Bond RX Stainer (Leica Biosystem). The standard RNA protocol was utilized for the whole transcriptomic atlas (WTA) assay. For tissue visualization and segmentation, slides were processed the following immunofluorescence biomarkers: SYTO-13 (Nuclei), and PanCytokeratin (Tumor cells) (GeoMx Solid Tumor TME Morphology Kit, Catalog number 121300301), CD3e (Clone UMAB54, AF647, catalog number: UM500048, and CD45 (Immune cells) (GeoMx Solid Tumor TME Morphology Kit, Catalog number 121300301). Visualization biomarkers were previously optimized using tonsil control tissue to achieve the highest signal to noise ratio. After scanning, a selection of 3 to 6 ROIs was placed on the images, in areas with viable tumor surrounded by tumor microenvironment, avoiding necrosis, tissue artifacts and non-tumoral tissue. Each ROI had an area of up to 600 by 600 µm with an adequate number of viable cells per assay requirement (200 cells at a minimum for each segment). ROIs were segmented into biological compartments: “Tumor” (PanCk + , which included malignant cells and intra-epithelial immune cells) and “Stroma” (PanCk-, which included cells of the tumor microenvironment surrounding the malignant cells), and whole ROI selection was performed in patients without residual tumor present, thus capturing the ROI without tissue segmentation. Segments were exposed to ultraviolet light to decouple the linked barcode, which was collected for library preparation and quantified using next-generation sequencing using the Ilumina NGS workflow (Illumina) to then be mapped to individual ROIs. (Supplementary Fig 1 and Supplementary Table 2)

GeoMx data analysis

FASTQ files of DSP WTA assay were processed into digital count conversion (DCC) files using GeoMx NGS pipeline (https://university.nanostring.com/geomx-dsp-ngs-readout-user-manual/1193408). Quality controls of the DCC files were performed using GeomxTools package in R (version 4.3.1) and segments were filtered based on minimum number of reads (1000), minimum % of reads trimmed (80%), minimum % of reads stitched (80%), minimum % of reads aligned (75%), minimum sequencing saturation (50%), minimum negative control counts, maximum counts observed in NTC well (9000), minimum number of nuclei estimated (100), minimum segment area (1000). A probe was removed globally from the dataset when the geometric mean of that probe’s counts from all segments divided by the geometric mean of all probe counts representing the target from all segments was less than 0.1 or the probe is an outlier according to the Grubb’s test in at least 20% of the segments. Segments with less than 5% of the genes detected were removed. Qualified digital counts were normalized using RUVg normalization using housekeeping genes (FCF1, DHX16, ABCF1, EDC3, ZNF143, HDAC3, CNOT10, SAP130, AGK, AMMECR1L, SF3A3, COG7, TMUB2, ZC3H14, DDX50, MRPS5, ZNF346, MTMR14, ERCC3, EIF2B4, TLK2, NUBP1, USP39, PRPF38A. NOL7, CNOT4)60. Digital counts were used to find differential gene expressions using limma-voom linear mixed model61 with limma batch effect correction across slides. P-values were corrected for multiple hypothesis testing with the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). GSEA was performed against the hallmark gene set (MSigDB)54 or published cancer metaprograms32,33 using the fgsea package. The results were visualized with the R (version 4.5) package ggplot2. The top 10 (or the maximum numbers if less than 10) significantly enriched pathways on each side were shown. Normalized read counts were also visualized on spatial DSP images with SpatialOmicsOverlay (https://github.com/Nanostring-Biostats/SpatialOmicsOverlay).

Immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissue samples were submitted to the institutional CLIA-certified immunohistochemistry laboratory at MD Anderson Cancer Center for analysis of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and p16, while samples were submitted to the MD Anderson Anatomic Pathology immunohistochemical clinical lab for analysis of Cyclin D1, Cyclin E1, and Rb expression. IHC staining was performed using validated protocols with the following antibodies, all used in ready-to-use (RTU) formulations: anti-ER (clone 6F11, Leica) with ER1 antigen retrieval for 20 min on the Leica Bond III platform using the Bond detection kit; anti-PR (clone 16, Leica) with ER2 antigen retrieval for 20 min= on the same platform and detection system; and anti-p16 (clone E6H4, Ventana) with CC1 antigen retrieval for 48 min at 95 °C on the Roche Ventana platform using the Ventana detection kit; anti-Cyclin D1 (clone EP12, Leica). In addition, anti-Cyclin E1 (clone EP126, Bio SB) and anti-Rb (clone G3-245, BD Biosciences) antibodies were used at 1:200 dilution. ER and PR expression were assessed based on the percentage of tumor nuclei exhibiting positive staining, and p16 was interpreted as aberrant when strong, diffuse nuclear and cytoplasmic staining or when complete null staining was observed. Cyclin D1/E1 expression was interpreted as abnormal when noted for strong diffuse staining; additionally, cases that were completely negative were also noted. Rb expression was interpreted as abnormal when the staining was completely negative. All slides were reviewed by board-certified pathologists blinded to clinical and treatment outcomes.
