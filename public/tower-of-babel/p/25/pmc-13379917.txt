Coronary Intramural Hematoma in a Young Athlete After Direct Ball Impact to the Chest
Authors: Hichem Sakhi, Laurette Kalifa, Sahbi Fradi, Vincenzo Palermo, Haifa Remili, Salma Chaher, Virgile Chevance, Iyad Mallak, Said Ghostine, Arshid Azarine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary intramural hematoma (IMH) is a rare cause of acute coronary syndrome that may occur after blunt chest impact.Case SummaryA 19-year-old competitive athlete presented with sudden retrosternal chest pain after intense exertion and direct ball-related impact to the chest during a football match. Troponin peaked at 20,400 ng/L. Coronary angiography showed a nonobstructive ostial left anterior descending artery (LAD) lesion. Cardiac magnetic resonance demonstrated near-transmural ischemic injury with microvascular obstruction in the basal-mid septum. Coronary computed tomography (CT) angiography revealed hyperdense mural thickening and positive remodeling of the proximal LAD, suggestive of IMH. Optical coherence tomography confirmed a focal subadventitial IMH in the proximal LAD, near the ostium of the first septal branch.DiscussionThis case illustrates traumatic IMH as a potential cause of myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries in a young athlete and underscores the value of cardiac CT.Take-Home MessagesCoronary IMH should be considered after blunt chest trauma. Noncontrast CT aids IMH diagnosis.Key Words: acute coronary syndrome, cardiac magnetic resonance, computed tomography, intravascular ultrasound

History of Presentation

A 19-year-old previously healthy competitive athlete experienced sudden-onset, constrictive, typically anginal retrosternal chest pain during intense exertion and after a direct ball-related impact to the chest while playing football. The pain lasted approximately 1 hour, was associated with vomiting, and completely regressed thereafter.

He presented shortly afterward to a community emergency department. On arrival, his blood pressure was 125/84 mm Hg and heart rate 73 beats/min. Physical examination was unremarkable, with no signs of heart failure. The initial electrocardiogram (ECG) showed early repolarization with prominent T waves in V3-V4, without reciprocal changes. High-sensitivity troponin returned elevated at 22,000 ng/L (normal <14 ng/L).

The patient was transferred to the coronary care unit of our tertiary center for further evaluation. Because of the persistent mild residual chest discomfort, the on-call interventional cardiologist proceeded with urgent coronary angiography, which revealed a nonobstructive proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) plaque (<30%) (Figure 1).

Past Medical History

There was no prior medical, surgical, or family history. The patient was an active smoker.

Differential Diagnosis

Given this clinical context—exertional, typically anginal chest pain, marked troponin elevation, nonspecific ECG changes, and a nonobstructive coronary angiogram—we considered the differential diagnosis of myocardial injury with no obstructive coronary artery, including myocardial infarction (MINOCA), myocarditis, takotsubo cardiomyopathy, and other forms of cardiomyopathy (such as arrhythmogenic or inflammatory cardiomyopathy).

Investigations

Initial transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no evident regional wall motion abnormalities, with a normal left ventricular ejection fraction, no significant valvular disease, normal right-sided chamber dimensions, and a nondilated proximal aorta without features of dissection. There was no pericardial effusion.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) was conducted at 1.5-Tesla with a comprehensive protocol including T2 mapping for myocardial edema, cine SSFP sequences for ventricular function assessment, first-pass perfusion imaging, and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) for scar and necrosis characterization (Figure 2). Using this protocol, CMR identified near-transmural LGE in the basal to mid-interventricular septum, associated with rest perfusion defects, distal microvascular obstruction, edema, and matching regional hypokinesia. The overall findings are therefore consistent with a recent myocardial infarction.

(C and D) First-pass perfusion imaging with corresponding basal septal hypoperfusion. (E and F) Late gadolinium enhancement revealing transmural basal septal enhancement with associated microvascular obstruction.

A contrast-enhanced cardiac computed tomography (CT) scan was performed using a protocol that included an initial noncontrast acquisition, followed by an ECG-synchronized arterial-phase acquisition with a triphasic contrast injection (Figure 3). This protocol subsequently revealed a hyperdense appearance of the proximal LAD wall on the noncontrast images, consistent with spontaneous intramural hyperattenuation, and demonstrated a 25% to 49% luminal narrowing on the contrast-enhanced series. Together, these findings strongly raised suspicion of a coronary intramural hematoma (IMH).

(A and B) Contrast-enhanced acquisition showing the proximal left anterior descending artery lesion with positive remodeling and an estimated 25% to 49% stenosis. (C and D) Noncontrast acquisition demonstrating spontaneous hyperattenuation of the lesion, confirming the presence of an intramural hematoma (red arrow).

Management

Management included initiation of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin and prasugrel, complemented by a β-blocker and a low-dose angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, together with structured smoking-cessation counseling. Given the absence of flow-limiting stenosis and the stability of the patient, a conservative, noninterventional strategy was adopted.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient experienced a complete clinical recovery, with no residual functional complaints during follow-up. A repeat coronary angiograph at 1 month with optical coherence tomography (OCT) examination was performed, under the guidance of the prior coronary CT imaging. OCT was intentionally conducted at a distance from the acute event to avoid extending the lesion and to document the natural evolution and healing of the arterial wall. This delayed intracoronary imaging confirmed a well-demarcated subadventitial hematoma with interval signs of stabilization (Figure 4).

DAPT was continued for 1 month and subsequently de-escalated to aspirin monotherapy after follow-up imaging demonstrated stabilization of the IMH and absence of significant stenosis.

CMR performed 3 months later demonstrated full resolution of myocardial edema, a substantial reduction in the extent of LGE consistent with focal residual necrosis, and complete normalization of regional wall motion. Left ventricular ejection fraction remained within the normal range, confirming full restoration of systolic function.

Given the patient’s athletic profile and the absence of clear recommendations in the literature regarding return-to-sport evaluation after traumatic coronary IMH or spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD)–like coronary injury, a cautious and individualized strategy was adopted. At 6-month follow-up, clinical reassessment including resting ECG, transthoracic echocardiography, and exercise stress testing showed reassuring findings, without symptoms, inducible ischemia, arrhythmias, or functional limitation. A gradual and carefully monitored return to sporting activity was therefore authorized.

Discussion

SCAD encompasses a spectrum of nonatherosclerotic coronary injuries characterized by separation within the arterial wall. Among its presentations, isolated IMH represents a distinct phenotype in which blood accumulates within the medial or medial-adventitial layers without a visible intimal flap, making angiographic detection particularly challenging.1

Although SCAD is increasingly recognized as a cause of acute coronary syndrome in young and otherwise healthy individuals, an important proportion of cases appear temporally linked to vigorous physical exercise, suggesting a direct mechanistic contribution of sport-induced hemodynamic stress.2 Previous studies report that between 15% and 30% of SCAD events are preceded by high-intensity exercise, heavy lifting, or abrupt increases in intrathoracic pressure.1,2

It is important to note that although sport may act as a precipitating factor, it does not imply that these individuals have structurally normal vessels. Most SCAD patients demonstrating exercise-associated events harbor an underlying predisposing substrate—fibromuscular dysplasia, postpartum medial degeneration, connective tissue fragility, or arteriopathy of uncertain origin—rendering them more vulnerable to the mechanical stresses of high-intensity training.

Nevertheless, our patient exhibited none of these identifiable predisposing features. In contrast to classical SCAD, our patient sustained a direct thoracic impact immediately before symptom onset, suggesting a distinct initiating mechanism. The patient reported being only a few meters from the shooter and sustaining a direct chest impact from a close-range shot during active play, while the ball was still in its acceleration phase. Although the kinetic energy of the impact could not be precisely quantified, this short-distance direct anterior chest impact supports a plausible traumatic mechanism. The close temporal relationship between the impact, the onset of anginal chest pain, and subsequent imaging findings further reinforces this hypothesis. In fact, blunt chest trauma may induce coronary injury through direct mechanical stress, potentially causing localized intimal disruption or rupture of the vasa vasorum, ultimately leading to IMH formation. However, these cases typically involve intimal disruption with visible dissection flaps or significant luminal compromise. In contrast, our case demonstrated an isolated IMH without angiographic evidence of dissection, confirmed by OCT as a subadventitial hematoma. This supports a distinct phenotype of coronary wall injury, where trauma may induce intramural bleeding without overt intimal tear. Recognizing this distinction is clinically relevant, as it broadens diagnostic reasoning in patients presenting with acute coronary syndrome and atypical triggers such as chest trauma.3,4

The CMR findings were initially challenging because the septal LGE pattern was atypical for a conventional myocardial infarction. Myocarditis classically produces subepicardial or midwall LGE, whereas ischemic injury usually begins in the subendocardium and may extend transmurally. However, small infarctions involving septal perforator branches can show a more focal intramyocardial septal pattern and therefore mimic nonischemic injury. In our case, the basal-mid septal location, associated perfusion defect, microvascular obstruction, and proximity of the proximal LAD hematoma to the first septal perforator supported a focal ischemic mechanism rather than myocarditis. Therefore, this case finally highlights the importance of a structured diagnostic reasoning pathway, where each imaging modality contributes complementary information.5

Nevertheless, coronary CT angiography could have been informative earlier, even before the first coronary angiography. In young patients with MINOCA presentations, and when logistics permit, a first-line CT—including noncontrast acquisition (IV−) to detect mural hyperattenuation—may reveal an IMH before any invasive procedure, potentially accelerating diagnosis and reducing procedural risk. Previously reported cases have highlighted the usefulness of coronary CT angiography in this diagnosis.6,7 In select patients, it may obviate the need for immediate invasive angiography, emphasizing the importance of this imaging modality.

The optimal antithrombotic strategy in coronary IMH and SCAD-like coronary wall injury remains highly debated, especially in conservatively managed patients without stent implantation.8 Although antiplatelet therapy may reduce thrombus formation within the true lumen, it may theoretically exacerbate intramural bleeding or promote hematoma extension. In our case, DAPT was initiated because of the acute coronary syndrome presentation and marked troponin elevation.

Conclusions

Coronary IMH is a very rare but important cause of acute coronary syndrome and MINOCA in young athletic individuals. This case highlights the importance of considering IMH in the diagnostic work-up of chest pain and troponin elevation occurring after blunt chest trauma, even when initial coronary angiography shows no obstructive lesion. Multimodality imaging—particularly coronary CT with a noncontrast acquisition—can provide key diagnostic clues by identifying mural hyperattenuation and guiding subsequent intracoronary imaging. Early recognition of this traumatic coronary wall injury is essential to avoid unnecessary intervention, support conservative management when appropriate, and ensure tailored follow-up.
