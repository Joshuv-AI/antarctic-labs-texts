Syndromic Cleft Lip and Palate in an Infant With Suspected Trisomy 13: Ethical Surgical Decision-making
Authors: Simeon Mulugeta Mengistu, Harom Gari Wakjira, Dagmawi Geremew Zinaw, Mekonen Eshete
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome) is a rare, life-limiting chromosomal disorder characterized by severe multisystem anomalies and profound neurodevelopmental impairment. Surgical correction of cleft lip and palate in these infants presents complex clinical and ethical challenges, especially when compounded by malnutrition, which significantly increases perioperative risks and influences overall prognosis. This case report is presented in accordance with the CARE guidelines. A 5-month-old male infant presented with multiple congenital anomalies, including bilateral cleft lip, right-sided cleft palate, bilateral anophthalmia, postaxial polydactyly, and an umbilical hernia, and was clinically diagnosed with trisomy 13 due to the absence of genetic testing. He was diagnosed with severe acute malnutrition at 3 months of age and admitted for nutritional rehabilitation, leading to subsequent improvement. A multidisciplinary evaluation addressed prognosis, operative feasibility, nutritional vulnerabilities, and ethical considerations surrounding surgical intervention. Given the limited survival prospects, heightened surgical risk due to malnutrition, and anticipated quality-of-life outcomes, the surgical team and family, after informed consent, agreed on supportive management focused on feeding optimization, psychosocial support, and continued follow-up, rather than proceeding with cleft repair. In infants with trisomy 13 complicated by malnutrition, decisions regarding cleft repair require nuanced ethical and clinical deliberation. Prioritizing nutritional stabilization, realistic prognostic counseling, and shared decision-making is essential, particularly in resource-limited settings where surgical risk–benefit balance is even more critical.

Trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome) is a rare chromosomal disorder with an incidence of approximately 1 in 10,000–20,000 live births and is characterized by severe multisystem anomalies and high early mortality.1 Multidisciplinary management of cleft lip and palate in these infants is complex, particularly in the presence of malnutrition, which increases perioperative risk and affects prognosis.2,3 Ethical deliberations—balancing beneficence, nonmaleficence, and family-centered decision-making—play a central role in determining the appropriateness of surgical intervention.4,5 We present the case of a 5-month-old male infant with syndromic cleft lip and palate associated with suspected trisomy 13 and complicated by malnutrition, highlighting clinical, nutritional, and ethical considerations guiding management.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 5-month-old male infant was referred for evaluation of syndromic cleft lip and palate. He was born at term via vaginal delivery to nonconsanguineous parents. Pregnancy and delivery were reportedly uncomplicated. There was no family history of congenital anomalies. Feeding initially consisted of both expressed breast milk and formula. By 3 months of age, feeding had transitioned to formula-only using a specialized cleft-feeding bottle. At that time, the infant was diagnosed with severe acute malnutrition (SAM) and admitted for nutritional rehabilitation. Following treatment, his weight improved, edema subsided, and he was in stable condition by 5 months. The mother reported significant psychological distress and social isolation resulting from the child’s condition. The father had abandoned the family during the infant’s hospitalization.

Upon physical examination, anthropometric measurements at 5 months were as follows: weight, 6.0 kg (weight-for-age Z score < −2 SD; underweight); length, 63 cm (normal); and head circumference, 41 cm (ninth percentile, normal) (Table 1).

CARE PLAN

After detailed counseling, the family elected supportive, comfort-focused care with continued optimization of feeding. Surgical repair of the cleft lip and palate was deferred. The infant was discharged in stable condition with scheduled follow-up.

The mother received psychosocial counseling and linkage to social support services to address caregiver distress, social isolation, and the father’s abandonment during hospitalization. The mother’s social isolation and lack of familial support further contextualized the principle of justice, highlighting the ethical responsibility to avoid placing disproportionate caregiving and medical burdens on already vulnerable families. Ongoing follow-up included nutritional counseling and referrals for psychosocial support.

The care plan was intentionally framed as dynamic rather than absolute. Should the infant demonstrate sustained physiological stability, improved nutritional reserve, and family-aligned goals of care over time, the multidisciplinary team (MDT) would reconsider the appropriateness of limited palliative cleft lip repair focused on feeding facilitation and caregiver bonding.

DISCUSSION

Trisomy 13 is primarily diagnosed clinically in settings without genetic testing. Recognition of the constellation—bilateral cleft lip and palate, anophthalmia, polydactyly, cardiac defect, and umbilical hernia—guided the clinical diagnosis. Early recognition enables informed, family-centered decision-making. Trisomy 13 is associated with profound neurodevelopmental impairment and severe multisystem anomalies, resulting in markedly limited survival; fewer than 10% of affected infants live beyond their first year.1,6 This expected trajectory framed all subsequent discussions regarding the role and timing of cleft repair.

Surgical repair may improve feeding efficiency, reduce nasal regurgitation, and facilitate better oral hygiene. It also carries potential psychosocial benefits such as enhanced parental bonding and reduced caregiver stress.7 However, these potential advantages must be interpreted cautiously in the setting of a life-limiting diagnosis where long-term functional improvement is unlikely to be realized.

SAM is common in infants with cleft lip and palate due to impaired oral intake.8 In this case, the weight dropped to 4.3 kg at 3 months of age, requiring hospitalization. Although edema resolved and weight improved following nutritional rehabilitation, the infant remained underweight with a weight-for-age Z score of –2 (underweight) at the age of 5 months, indicating persistent nutritional vulnerability, which, in isolation, is not a contraindication to cleft surgery. Malnutrition increases the likelihood of postoperative complications by impairing immune function, delaying wound healing, and increasing metabolic instability.3 These risks are not meaningfully mitigated by short-term nutritional recovery. More importantly, the MDT decision was not driven by nutritional status alone but by the underlying syndromic physiology, including anticipated airway instability, neurological impairment, and cardiopulmonary fragility characteristic of suspected trisomy 13.8 Despite an unremarkable cranial ultrasound, the constellation of external anomalies carried greater diagnostic and prognostic weight, as cranial ultrasound has limited sensitivity for detecting cortical and midline malformations. In a resource-limited setting with constrained access to pediatric intensive care and advanced airway rescue, these factors collectively rendered elective cleft repair disproportionately high risk relative to the anticipated functional benefit. Ethical principles guiding care in syndromic, medically fragile infants include beneficence and nonmaleficence: weighing the potential benefit of cleft repair against operative risk4,9; autonomy: respecting parental values and preferences; and justice: responsible allocation of limited healthcare resources.

Although this infant demonstrated short-term clinical stability and spontaneous closure of a small atrial septal defect, the MDT did not base its recommendation on immediate lethality. Rather, the concept of futility was applied in terms of proportionality of benefit: the anticipated limited neurodevelopmental trajectory and systemic vulnerability associated with suspected trisomy 13 rendered elective cleft repair unlikely to provide durable functional benefit relative to cumulative anesthetic and perioperative risk.

In otherwise healthy infants, cleft lip repair is typically performed at approximately 3–6 months of age, with cleft palate repair commonly undertaken between 9 and 12 months to support feeding, speech development, and oronasal separation. In the present case, these standard indications were weighed against syndromic physiology, anticipated neurodevelopmental trajectory, and cumulative perioperative risk, leading to deferral of elective repair.

Shared decision-making ensured that the family was empowered to prioritize feeding, comfort, and supportive care over surgical repair, reflecting ethically responsible care.10 Multidisciplinary counseling, including ethical guidance, is essential, especially in resource-limited settings.

LIMITATIONS

We acknowledge the ethical risk of diagnostic uncertainty in the absence of genetic confirmation. Although alternative syndromic diagnoses (eg, Meckel–Gruber syndrome) may mimic aspects of the phenotype, the constellation observed in this infant strongly favored trisomy 13. Importantly, the MDT decision was not anchored solely to a diagnostic label but to the overall multisystem disease burden and anticipated physiological vulnerability, which would remain relevant even in phenotypically similar syndromes with comparably poor prognostic trajectories.

CONCLUSIONS

In children born with syndromic conditions such as suspected trisomy 13 and SAM, cleft lip and palate repair requires careful ethical and clinical deliberation. Nutritional rehabilitation and supportive care are often more appropriate priorities than elective surgery. Shared decision-making, MDT involvement, and respect for parental values are central to ethically sound care.
