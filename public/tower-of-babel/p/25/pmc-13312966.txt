Stabilizing and directional selection shape the genetic variation of gene expression in the transcriptome of Chlamydomonas
Authors: Eniolaye J Balogun, Jacqueline L Sztepanacz, Rob W Ness, Jeffrey Townsend
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGene expression has proven to be a source of evolutionary novelty and is important to phenotypic evolution. However, we still have a poor understanding of the degree to which mutation contributes to variation in segregating and diverging populations. Here, we aim to investigate the evolutionary forces that shape the transcriptome in 24 wild strains of the green alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, originating from two subpopulations and four closely related species. We employ a comparative analysis of expression variation in the presence (ie genetic variance, VG) and absence (ie mutational variance, VM) of selection, in conjunction with the phylogenetic comparative model—Expression Variance Evolution, to identify the selective forces that may be shaping variation. We found that gene expression displays moderate heritability, with genetic changes accounting for 17% of the differences in expression. The transcriptome evolves under the dominant influence of stabilizing selection, with a minor role of directional selection, which was present in 7% to 14% of genes, supporting the strong conservation of expression across species that had diverged over 10 MYA. Indeed, we found that genes with high within-species variation also displayed high expression differences between species.Keywords: gene expression, mutation, evolution, heritability, Chlamydomonas reinhardtii

Introduction

Heritable variation lays the foundation for evolution via natural selection. Mutation introduces variation in a trait, which is subsequently shaped by selection and genetic drift. However, the interaction of mutation with selection and drift over longer evolutionary timescales remains an open question. The amount of variation in a population is determined by the joint action of mutation, which generates variation and selection and drift, which tend to reduce variation. Therefore, disentangling the relative influence of mutation from selection and drift is challenging without direct estimates of these processes. Furthermore, a reduction in variation from either directional or stabilizing selection can manifest as reduced variation within species, but these possibilities can be distinguished based on the rate of divergence between species. Yet, the relationship between mutational variance, standing variation, and divergence has been explored in only a few traits, such as wing morphology (Houle et al. 2017) and mitotic spindle-associated traits (Farhadifar et al. 2015), with mixed conclusions. Understanding the interplay between variation introduced by mutation each generation (VM), standing variation in a natural population (VG), and interspecific divergence is therefore central to predicting both short- and long-term evolutionary change as well as the repeatability of evolutionary responses. Given the varied genetic architectures linking genotype to phenotype, it is critical that we investigate these processes in a larger diversity of traits.

One such trait that is important to investigate is gene expression, as it creates a link between the genotype and phenotype. It has long been hypothesized that gene expression may provide as much evolutionary novelty as protein sequence changes (Britten and Davidson 1969; Britten and Davidson 1971; King and Wilson 1975), and this idea now has a large body of supporting evidence (Abzhanov et al. 2004; Schulte 2004; Wray 2007). Despite this, the joint dynamics of VM, VG, and divergence in gene expression remain poorly understood.

Gene expression heritability has been used to assess the extent of genetic variation across multiple taxa (reviewed in Whitehead and Crawford 2006) and tissue types (Wheeler et al. 2016), revealing a wide range of estimates—from as low as 0.02 in human tissues (Wheeler et al. 2016) to as high as 0.84 in yeast (Brem et al. 2002). The transcriptome is commonly believed to evolve under stabilizing selection (Lemos et al. 2005; Hodgins-Davis et al. 2015; Hill et al. 2021), where there are stronger fitness penalties for expression changes that deviate from the optimum. Although this is the prevailing hypothesis, there is also evidence of expression evolution under both neutral (Oleksiak et al. 2002; Khaitovich et al. 2004; Whitehead and Crawford 2006) and directional selection (Rifkin et al. 2003; Nourmohammad et al. 2017; Catalán et al. 2019), thus estimating their relative contribution to evolution is essential. The neutral model for expression variation is complicated by the fact that, unlike the effects of mutation on sequence, the impact of mutation on expression is less clear. For example, the overall rate of change in expression must include the cumulative contribution of up- and downregulatory mutations (Metzger et al. 2016). Regulatory mutations may also have widespread pleiotropic effects on numerous genes and traits (ie trans-acting mutation) (Vande Zande et al. 2022), and these expression changes themselves may have knockout effects mediated by the cell’s regulatory network.

Several methodologies have been developed to investigate gene expression evolution. There have been direct attempts to model the fitness–expression relationship, where selection differentials are calculated from the relative fitness of gene expression level and are used to identify selective forces (Groen et al. 2020). Where this is not possible, a common approach is to compare inter- and intraspecific variation with their neutral expectations. Using this method, Oleksiak et al. (2002) found that most genes within and between Fundulus species evolved neutrally, while a few genes exhibited expression conservation indicative of stabilizing selection. However, over long evolutionary spans, expression divergence may saturate, obscuring our ability to discern between neutral change and directional selection. Therefore, sampling related species that are analyzed in a phylogenetic framework (Hansen 1997; Butler and King 2004; Brawand et al. 2011; Rohlfs et al. 2014; Rohlfs and Nielsen 2015; DeGiorgio and Assis 2021) can help alleviate this problem by explicitly accounting for the effect of phylogeny when estimating rates of expression divergence between species. Using this approach, directional selection can be modeled as an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process (Uhlenbeck and Ornstein 1930), in which the expression value evolves according to a Brownian motion process with a deterministic pull toward an optimal value, at a speed determined by the strength of selection toward the optimum. Lineage-specific shifts in the optimal value of expression can be interpreted as the result of directional selection altering the optimal expression value. Overall, although these approaches have proven helpful in identifying how adaptive and nonadaptive forces shape gene expression, their mixed conclusions raise questions about how much of the variation in the strength and form of selection on gene expression across species is biological or technical in nature.

Another challenge in interpreting patterns of within or between species expression change is that differential input of mutational variation for expression can obscure whether the amount of expression variation is caused by selection or variable mutation input. To address this, expression variation generated by mutation in the absence of selection can be directly measured and then subsequently compared to naturally segregating variation, which has been subject to selection. Although standing genetic variation (VG) can be estimated using samples of natural isolates, mutational variance (VM), which represents the increase in genetic variance introduced by mutation each generation, is more challenging to measure due to the rarity of mutations. Mutation accumulation experiments circumvent this challenge by subjecting inbred lines or clones to severe bottlenecks, thus allowing the observation of spontaneous mutations and their effects in the absence of selection (Keightley and Halligan 2009). The ratio of VM by VG is compared to neutral expectations, with any deviations indicative of different selective modes (Lynch and Hill 1986). A small number of studies have employed this strategy for gene expression evolution, specifically in Drosophila (Rifkin et al. 2005; McGuigan et al. 2014a, 2014b; Huang et al. 2016) and Caenorhabditis elegans (Denver et al. 2005). In both species, they concluded that strong stabilizing selection is removing much of the variation generated by mutation (Hodgins-Davis et al. 2015). Comparing VM to standing variation can be extended to comparisons of the rate of expression divergence between species, which can provide insights into how selection shapes expression over longer timescales. For example, Rifkin et al. (2005) compared expression profiles among Drosophila species to the neutral expectation (VMt, where t is the divergence time) and found fewer expression differences than neutrality predicts. Unfortunately, there is a lack of breadth in the species studied. Most previous works were based on dated technology, limiting the scope of the transcriptome studied, and surprisingly, did not incorporate phylogeny.

To investigate the interplay of mutation, selection, and drift on gene expression, we will use the unicellular, haploid green alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii. Chlamydomonas reinhardtii is a well-established model used to study cellular, molecular, and genomic biology (Harris 2001; Grossman et al. 2003). Its ploidy, short generation time, and low maintenance have perpetuated its efficiency in studying mutation. Chlamydomonas reinhardtii has become a model for mutation rate evolution (Ness et al. 2012, 2015, 2016; López-Cortegano et al. 2021), base spectrum (Flynn et al. 2018), and distribution of fitness effects (Böndel et al. 2019; Böndel et al. 2022). Through comparative genomic analysis, we find that C. reinhardtii exhibits strong population structure and high genetic diversity of ∼3% (Craig et al. 2019), making it an interesting model for studying the degree to which a mutation generates variation within segregating populations. Quite recently, it has joined the small number of studies that have investigated the effects of mutation on gene expression, the associated fitness effect of expression change, and the distribution of expression effects, making it a useful model organism for our purposes.

Here, we aim to determine the magnitude and heritability of intra- and interspecific expression variation. In this study, we sequenced the transcriptomes of 24 genetically distinct natural isolates from two subpopulations isolated across North America (Craig et al. 2019). Additionally, we included four Chlamydomonas species from the core-Reinhardtinia clade, one of which is C. reinhardtii's closest known relative: C. incerta (Craig et al. 2021). To measure the evolutionary force shaping the transcriptome, we are among the first to combine the Expression Variance Evolution (EVE) model (Rohlfs and Nielsen 2015), which considers species phylogeny, with pairwise comparisons of expression variation and mutational variance (VM) (Balogun and Ness 2024). VM was previously estimated from 28 well-characterized MA lines derived from two independent wild-type genotypes (CC-2344 and CC-2931) (Morgan et al. 2014). Each MA line was grown for 800 to 1,100 generations and carries approximately 76 mutations, including structural mutations (López-Cortegano et al. 2023). In this study, we address the following questions: (i) What is the heritability of intraspecific expression variation? (ii) How does expression divergence relate to pairwise sequence divergence? (iii) What proportion of genes are subject to stabilizing and directional selection? (iv) How does mutation contribute to variation in segregating and diverging populations? (v) Do genes within and between species reflect similar patterns in variation? Below, we present evidence of strong conservation across the transcriptome, with stabilizing selection playing a dominant role, and directional selection having a small but significant effect.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and growth

Chlamydomonas reinhardtii is a unicellular, haploid green alga that is capable of vegetative asexual growth, but is also facultatively sexual. Sex can be induced in the lab through nitrogen deprivation and occurs between mt+ and mt− cells encoded by a mating type locus on chromosome 6. To estimate genetic variation within a natural population of C. reinhardtii, we examined the gene expression of 24 natural isolates from two subpopulations: NA1 (n = 14) and NA2 (n = 10). These strains have been characterized for sequence similarity (Craig et al. 2019) and represent related strains within each subpopulation. Three replicates of each strain were grown on Bold's agar medium at 25 °C in a shaking incubator at 100 RPM until a dense culture was reached. Cells from each replicate were subsequently frozen at −80 °C until total RNA extraction via the Maxwell RSC Plant RNA Kit. The quality and quantity of the RNA were assessed using gel electrophoresis and a Qubit fluorometer. Novogene conducted mRNA isolation, library preparation, and sequencing. Sequencing was completed on the NovaSeq X Plus Series with 150-bp paired-end reads and 20 M read pairs per sample.

We followed the protocol outlined by Balogun and Ness (2024) for read alignment, quantification, and normalization. For detailed protocols, see Supplementary Methods.

Quantifying standing genetic variation in expression variation using natural isolates

To estimate variation across natural isolates (ie genetic variation, VG), we fitted a generalized linear mixed model following the Poisson distribution (lme4 package, Bates et al. 2015).

In our model, each raw read count Yijk of gene j, strain i, and replicate k is normalized by DESeq2-derived size factors, by fitting ln(1sizefactor) as a fixed effect to account for differences in sequencing depth. The strain and subpopulation are fit as random effects to account for genetic differences among strains that could contribute to expression variation. Genetic variance is estimated from the among-strain variance, represented by the variance component of the random effect of “strain.” The observation-level random effect bk ∼ N(0, σ2b) accounts for overdispersion, and any residual variation is denoted by εijk (Harrison 2014).

Estimating broad-sense heritability of gene expression

Broad-sense heritability (H2 = VG/VP) was calculated from the variance components of Equation (1). Genetic variance (VG) was estimated from the variance component of strain (ie among-strain variance), whereas phenotypic variance (VP) was defined as the sum of genetic and residual variance. Genes with a median expression level less than 10 were excluded from the analysis.

Estimating SNP heritability of gene expression between intraspecific pairs

To estimate SNP heritability, we constructed a kinship matrix based on genome-wide SNPs (GQ > 30 in all samples) via Linkage-Disequilibrium Adjusted Kinships (Speed et al. 2020) and quantified the pairwise Euclidean distances (Glazko and Mushegian 2010) of their genome-wide expression profiles. We then regressed the Euclidean distance of gene expression on SNP similarity to estimate whether genetic relatedness predicts phenotypic differences. We used the coefficient of determination as our estimate of heritability, and we determined the statistical significance of the correlation using a Mantel test (v2.2.2) in Python with 1 million permutations for each subpopulation. It tests the null hypothesis that there is no relationship between the SNP-based kinship matrix and gene expression distance matrix.

Estimating expression divergence

Orthologous gene pairs were identified using reciprocal best blast hits and filtered by a percent identity and query coverage ≥ 60%. To estimate expression divergence, the read counts for each species pair were initially Fragment Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped fragments normalized, then subsequently normalized using the trimmed mean of M values method. The normalized expression counts of each species pair were transformed by the following equation adapted from Tirosh et al. (2006), where the log10-transformed read counts of 1:1 orthologs (ie xi and xj) for species i and j are used.

Estimating selection across the transcriptome

The strength of selection acting upon each gene was estimated by the formula s ∼ VM/VG (Barton 1990). VM represents the mutational variance, the increase in genetic variance due to a single generation of mutation, VG is the standing genetic variation, and s is the selection coefficient of the average mutant genotype. The mutational variance of each gene in C. reinhardtii was previously estimated by Balogun and Ness (2024) from mutation accumulation (MA) lines derived from two genotypes: CC-2344 (n = 15) and CC-2931 (n = 13). The median VM values are as follows: VM CC-2344 = 8.2 × 10−4, VM CC-2931 = 6.1 × 10−4, VM Average = 7.2 × 10−4. The standing genetic variation was estimated in Equation (1). According to Lynch and Hill (1986), genetic variation in the absence of selection should be within the range of NeVM and 2NeVM. Genes under stabilizing selection are expected to display VG < NeVM, where Ne is the effective population size, while genes under disruptive selection displayed the reverse trend (VG > 2NeVM). Based on genetic diversity and the mutation rate, we used estimates of Ne for NA1 and NA2 populations, respectively, as 4.91 × 107 and 6.35 × 107 (Craig et al. 2019).

The EVE model was used to distinguish between genes evolving under directional versus stabilizing selection (Rohlfs and Nielsen 2015). The phylogenetic tree used in this model was constructed by aligning the translated coding sequences of all 1:1 orthologs, such that for each gene, there is a corresponding ortholog in each Chlamydomonas species. The newly aligned sequence was reverse-translated back into its nucleotide sequence, and the software MEGA12 (Kumar et al. 2024) was used to generate a phylogenetic tree using the Maximum Parsimony method.

Identification of recent paralogous gene pairs

To test for altered expression divergence after gene duplication, we identified recent duplicates from within-species paralogs. For every C. reinhardtii–species pair, we ran a BLASTx (-evalue 1e-10 -max_hsps 1 -num_alignments 5) search for the top five hits per query sequence. These top hits were considered putative duplicates. Each Chlamydomonas species within the pair was used as a query and blasted against the other, resulting in two BLASTx outputs per pair. Within each species, we retained all putative gene duplicates with a query coverage of over 20% and a percent identity of over 50%. After the reciprocal best blast hit was removed from each paralogous set, the remaining putative gene duplicates were classified by their proximity to each other into three bins: tandem repeats, distant tandem repeats, and dispersed. Duplicates exclusive to one species per pair were used for further analysis. For expression analysis between paralogous and orthologous gene pairs, the expression level for C. reinhardtii was based on the geometric mean of all 24 natural isolates. We found the following paralogous pairs: nC. incerta = 142, nC. malaysian = 114, nC. schloesseri = 134, nJG5-1 = 169, nC. reinhardtii = 117.

Results and discussion

Moderate heritability of gene expression within species

We characterized gene expression variation in 24 natural isolates sampled from two genetic subpopulations—NA1 (n = 14) and NA2 (n = 10) (Fig. 1). We found that expression variation was significantly correlated across the two subpopulations (Fig. 2, Pearson's R = 0.49, P < 1 × 10−5). We observed that genes in NA1 exhibited slightly higher variation than those in NA2, as indicated by the linear regression slope of 0.54. To estimate SNP heritability (h2SNP), we regressed a kinship matrix on a matrix of pairwise expression distances, represented by Euclidean distance, across all genes (reviewed in Zhu and Zhou 2020). We found a h2SNP of 17% across all samples (Fig. 3, Mantel's Test R2 = 0.17, P = 2 × 10−6). Genetic kinship contrasts between individuals from different subpopulations had negative relatedness, indicating they are unrelated. Such negative values are often associated with strong population structure as observed in our data (Manichaikul et al. 2010). When analyzed independently, the two subpopulations exhibited SNP-based heritability estimates of 26% in NA1 (P = 3.1 × 10−4) and 32% in NA2 (P = 1.2 × 10−4). These values fall within the range of past estimates completed in an assortment of taxa (Whitehead and Crawford 2006; Viñuela et al. 2012; Wright et al. 2014; Huang et al. 2015; Lloyd-Jones et al. 2017) spanning from 0.06 in C. elegans (Zhang et al. 2022) to 0.7 in yeast (Brem and Kruglyak 2005). Structural variants and insertions/deletions, which are not captured by the SNP-based genetic distance, may also explain the variation unaccounted for by SNPs (Loegler et al. 2025). However, we have no reason to think that SNPs do not capture the relative differences among strains. Nonetheless, as genetic differences accumulate, it is not necessarily true that expression will continue to diverge if compensatory mutations or buffering of expression change are inherent to the regulatory network (Signor and Nuzhdin 2018). For example, Coolon et al. (2014) found that although cis-regulatory mutations contribute to differences between species and accumulate over divergence time, they are poorly correlated with expression differences.

Unexplained phenotypic variation in gene expression may be attributed to uncaptured or rare modifier alleles, dominance, environmental variation, pleiotropy, and gene–gene interactions (Mackay 2014; Mackay and Anholt 2024). Many of these nonadditive genetic effects are captured in broad-sense heritability, and comparing it with SNP-heritability can provide insight into the contribution of nonadditive effects (see Bloom et al. 2013). In our study, C. reinhardtii samples are haploid and grown in uniform environments, meaning that most of the nonadditive effects should be from epistasis. However, our median broad-sense heritability estimates (H2NA1 = 0.27; H2NA2 = 0.24) are roughly similar to SNP heritability, providing relatively little evidence of gene–gene interactions. Broad-sense heritability estimates between subpopulations were only moderately correlated across genes (Pearson's R = 0.34, P = 6.34 × 10−257), suggesting population-specific genetic architectures. Our heritability estimates fall within the range of previous studies of gene expression across taxa (0.08 to 0.84), including rice (H2 = 0.36; Groen et al. 2020), nematodes (H2 = 0.30; Viñuela et al. 2012; Volkers et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2022), fruit flies (H2 = 0.08; Huang et al. 2015), mice (H2 = 0.11; Chesler et al. 2005), and yeast (H2 = 0.84; Brem et al. 2002). It is challenging to make generalities about why gene expression heritability varies so much across species, given that the species reported have variable ploidy, genome size, and are both multi- and unicellular. The distinction between uni- and multicellular organisms might be quite important given that multicellular organisms are comprised of many different cell types that are most often sampled in aggregate, which might obscure variable patterns between cells and create false non-genetic variation depending on the frequency of cell types in extractions (Fu et al. 2012; van der Wijst et al. 2018). In contrast, unicellular organisms are generally sampled as large cultures of clonal cells sampled in a synchronized life stage in a tightly controlled environment, which effectively averages among cell expression variation, and reduces the contribution of environmental variance. Generalities about the broad-scale determinants of heritability across species will necessitate much denser sampling of species in a comparative framework to disentangle the many potential contributing factors. However, it is clear that despite these complexities, expression is heritable confirming its potential to play a significant role in adaptive evolution.

Strong stabilizing selection across the transcriptome

It is generally thought that the transcriptome evolves under strong stabilizing selection. To examine the type and strength of selection on gene expression, we compared the amount of variation introduced by mutation in the absence of selection (ie mutational variance, VM) to the amount of standing genetic variation present in a natural population (ie genetic variance, VG). The VM (Balogun and Ness 2024) and VG were both estimated using a similar Poisson loglinear model shown in Equation (1), where VM and VG were defined as the among-MA line and among-strain variance, respectively. We found that the median gene-wise VG was 0.45 and 0.15 for NA1 and NA2, respectively, with corresponding interquartile ranges (IQR) [0.22 to 0.85], [1.65 × 10−9 to 0.63] (Figure S4). NA2 appears to show less genetic variance in expression patterns than NA1, though the inverse is true based on polymorphisms (Figure S2). Although this disparity could be explained by the higher environmental noise in NA2, measured by the variance among replicates, we did not find a difference in the distribution of VE between subpopulations (see Figure S15). Averaging the median VG of both populations (VG = 0.3), our estimate falls within the range of past genetic variance estimates of gene expression from 7.7 × 10−3 in D. melanogaster (Huang et al. 2015), 0.101 in S. cerevisiae (Fay et al. 2004), 0.21 in C. elegans (Zhang et al. 2022), to 0.34 in D. serrata (McGuigan et al. 2014b). These studies vary widely in both sample size (ranging from 9 to 207 samples) and methodology. Several relied on microarray technology, which has reduced sensitivity at low expression levels and can bias estimates of expression variation (Draghici et al. 2006; Jaksik et al. 2015). Moreover, only a fraction of the transcriptome is captured by microarrays. In contrast, RNA sequencing overcomes this limitation by providing a comprehensive view of all expressed genes. The mutational variance (VM) was previously estimated in Balogun and Ness (2024) in two genotypes, and the average VM of both genotypes will be used for further analysis. The VG/VM ratio can be compared to neutral expectations, which is treated as the null (Lynch and Walsh 1998, see Methods), with the expectation that a ratio with a value less than 5.63 × 107 (VG << NeVM) is evidence of stabilizing selection. We concluded that the transcriptome primarily evolves under purifying selection, consistent with similar MA studies in D. melanogaster (Rifkin et al. 2005; Huang et al. 2016), D. serrata (McGuigan 2014a, 2014b), and C. elegans (Denver et al. 2005).

The VM/VG ratio approximates the intensity of selection against new deleterious mutations (Crow 1979). To examine the range of selection intensities, we binned gene-specific VM/VG ratios into intervals of 3.3 × 10−3 and observed an inverse L-shaped distribution, with approximately 74% to 77% of genes falling into the two highest bins—consistent with strong purifying selection (Fig. 4). Individuals in NA2 appear to have less expression variation, possibly due to stronger selection than those in NA1, evident by the 14% increase in the last bin than in NA1 (median VM/VG (NA1) = 3.3 × 10−3, Median VM/VG (NA2) = 9.8 × 10−3). The inverse of the VM/VG is proportional to the persistence time of new mutations. Our estimates imply that most new mutations are removed within 300 generations, consistent with efficient selection arising from the large effective population size and exposure of all haploid mutations to selection. This also suggests that these deleterious mutations impose significant fitness costs, which is consistent with the negative association between total expression change and fitness observed in Balogun and Ness (2024). McGuigan et al. (2014a, 2014b) and Denver et al. (2005) similarly observed skewed distributions and concluded that most genes in the transcriptomes of D. serrata and C. elegans were under strong selection. Interestingly, D. serrata is a diploid outcrosser, which means that mutations are effectively purged despite the potential for heterozygosity to mask recessive alleles. Since selection is removing the majority of the variation introduced by mutation each generation, the mean phenotype within and between subpopulations remains relatively unchanged compared to neutral expectations.

Signatures of weak neutral evolution of gene expression between species

To study the forces shaping gene expression between species, we examined the divergence of expression among four additional Chlamydomonas species (see Table 1), including C. reinhardtii's closest relative, C. incerta (diverged <10 MYA; Coleman and Mai 1997; Liss et al. 1997; Popescu et al. 2006). Under a simple neutral model, genetic differences are expected to accumulate with divergence time, and if expression diverges over time, a correlation between genetic and expression divergence might be observed (Khaitovich et al. 2004; Yanai et al. 2004; Hodgins-Davis and Townsend 2009; Catalán et al. 2019). Deviations from this pattern may be indicative of adaptive forces, such as negative or positive selection. While stabilizing selection can maintain expression despite genetic divergence, positive selection would promote a rise in the frequency of an expression phenotype at a faster rate than expected by both VM and drift, leading to excessive expression divergence. However, changes in expression as mutations accumulate may not be linear, such that expression divergence could reach a point of saturation, or up- and downregulatory mutations could offset each other (Balogun and Ness 2024). When we correlated expression divergence of orthologs with their respective genetic distance, we only found a significant correlation in 2 of 10 species pairs (Fig. 5, Pearson's R2 = 4 × 10−4, P ≥ 0.085). For pairs C. schloesseri × C. reinhardtii and JG5-1 × C. reinhardtii, we found a weak correlation (Pearson's R2 = 0.0064, P ≤ 2.7 × 10−12), which might be explained by the accumulation of neutral changes. When we correlated expression and sequence divergence for all 10 species together, treating species pair as a random effect, we detected a weak but significant relationship (Figure S17; Pearson's R = 0.0832, P = 4.3 × 10−123). Given that we used only synonymous divergence, it suggests that an element of gene expression is evolving neutrally, possibly through differences in mutation rate. Our results are consistent with another study investigating divergence in two or more species (Wang et al. 2025).

The obvious explanation for a weak signal between neutral sequence and expression divergence is that stabilizing selection is removing many expression-modifying mutations. However, the existence of a correlation also implies a role for mutational input in the rate of expression divergence. The connection between the rate of sequence and expression change is made complex due to compensatory cis–trans regulatory mutations (reviewed in Signor and Nuzhdin 2018) and network buffering that may maintain the mean expression despite genetic or non-genetic perturbations. It would be ideal if we could focus on sequence divergence in regulatory elements (Holloway et al. 2007; Lawniczak et al. 2008); however, the regulatory sites in many species are not well annotated. For example, Staubach et al. (2010) found a weak, significant correlation between sequence (upstream regions) and expression polymorphisms when comparing two house mouse subspecies. Overall, it may be challenging to distinguish between the presence of adaptive versus nonadaptive forces on the transcriptome, given the complex nature of expression changes. To elucidate this issue, we compared the transcriptomes of each species with that of C. reinhardtii to determine whether they exhibited similar expression patterns. Indeed, we found that gene expression was conserved between every C. reinhardtii × species pair (Fig. 6, average Pearson's R = 0.77, P < 10−5). This trend remained true for correlations between all combinations of Chlamydomonas species (Figure S3, average Pearson's R = 0.80, P < 10−5), indicating that they all share similar evolutionary forces acting on expression. Since C. reinhardtii is already established to evolve predominantly by strong purifying selection, though some genes may evolve neutrally, it is likely that the other species evolved under similar selective pressures (Rifkin et al. 2003; Lemos et al. 2005; Bedford and Hartl 2009) or underwent directional selection.

A significant and moderate correlation was found between all species pairs (Pearson's correlation R2 ≥ 0.75, P < 10−5).

Evidence for directional selection on gene expression in Chlamydomonas species

Although we have shown evidence that gene expression evolves primarily under stabilizing selection, it is interesting to quantify the extent and targets of directional selection on expression. It is challenging to distinguish between the appearance of stabilizing vs directional selection, as both reduce VG and therefore exhibit lower VG/VM ratios than expected under neutrality. If a gene is subject to directional selection in C. reinhardtii, it should have low intraspecific variation, but a relatively high expression divergence between other Chlamydomonas species. To differentiate between the two forces, we employed a phylogenetic comparative model (ie EVE model) that assumes gene expression evolves according to an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process toward an optimum θA with strength α, and a constant variance (drift) σ2 (Rohlfs and Nielsen 2015). In the EVE model, analogous to the Hudson-Kreitman-Aguadé test, the ratio of within-species variance to between-species divergence is compared to identify genes under directional selection as those that are diverging faster than expected based on the available variation. The remaining genes are therefore consistent with those subject to purifying selection or neutrality. Using EVE, we used the phylogeny of the five Chlamydomonas species to control for shared ancestry and evolutionary time. Any lineage-specific shifts in mean phenotype are indicative of directional selection. Considering all 1:1:1:1:1 orthologs across the five species, we found that 14% of orthologous genes (415 genes of 2,867) were evolving under directional selection (Fig. 7). The median expression divergence of genes under directional selection was 0.097 [IQR: 0.074 to 0.13], with a genetic variance of 0.21 [0.13 to 0.32], while the remaining genes assumed to be evolving under neutral or stabilizing selection were 0.089 [0.069 to 0.11] and 0.31 [0.18 to 0.50], respectively. Our estimate of expression does not account for variation in expression throughout the day, over the life cycle, or across environments in C. reinhardtii (Strenkert et al. 2019). Therefore, the fraction of genes under directional selection might change with different conditions (Rifkin et al. 2003; Nourmohammad et al. 2017) and is likely to vary across tissue types (Blekhman et al. 2008; Romero et al. 2012). For example, Catalán et al. (2019) found that 81% of the brain and eye evolution in Heliconius butterflies resulted from drift, 3% from stabilizing selection, and 16% from directional selection.

Correlation of intra- and interspecies variance. A scatterplot showing the correlation of intra- and interspecies variance for 2,867 genes. The regions of directional (14%, n = 415, green) and stabilizing (86%, n = 2452, blue) selection are shown.

Our analyses relied on 2,867 of the 5,812 orthologous gene pairs found across all five species. When all 1:1:1:1:1 orthologs were included in the EVE analysis, these genes often had no between-species divergence, and extremely high estimates of within-species variation, ∼10 orders of magnitude higher than the mean, indicating that the model was not fitting these genes correctly (Figure S10a). When we plotted a PCA of expression in the 5,812 orthologs for all samples, it is evident that C. reinhardtii samples clustered together on PC1 and PC2, and each of the other four species was distinct (Figure S11a). However, when we conducted the PCA using only the genes that returned extreme values in EVE, it was evident that the C. reinhardtii samples overlapped the other species, indicating that there was as much or more within-species expression variance as there was between-species divergence (Figure S11c). This is confirmed by the fact that the median VG of these excluded genes is higher (Mann–Whitney U P = 7.7 × 10−30) and their expression divergence is lower (Mann–Whitney U P = 3.0 × 10−52) than other included genes (Figure S16). The fact that these excluded genes return extreme values in EVE likely reflects that they violate the phylogenetic assumptions of the model, because expression divergence is so low as to be indistinguishable from within-species variance. When the expression of a given gene deviates from the phylogenetic expectation, it can lead to poor model fit (personal correspondence with author Lars Gronvold). Further analyses on excluded genes are provided in Figure S16 and Table 1. Since the excluded genes are likely evolving neutrally or under stabilizing selection, as they exhibit a relatively high median genetic variance and lower expression divergence, our estimate of 14% of genes evolving under directional selection is likely an overestimate. The value would be closer to 7.1%. Overall, we conclude that the dominant role of stabilizing selection on the transcriptome is evident; however, there is also a significant role for directional selection, with 7% to 14% of genes subject to directional evolution.

Mutation variation does not predict expression divergence

Under a simple neutral model, the amount of variation created by mutation (VM) should determine the pace of trait divergence. However, this simple connection can be undermined by several well-known processes that weaken the relationship between VM and divergence. We investigated this link by correlating the VM of each gene measured from MA lines of C. reinhardtii to the expression divergence observed between all pairs of Chlamydomonas species. We found that across pairs, VM only weakly correlated with expression divergence (R2 ranged 7 × 10−4 to 0.0294; Fig. 8), but given the large sample size of our analysis (n > 7,000 genes per pair), the significance of some of these correlations may not be biologically meaningful. The most likely cause of the weak correlation, based on our other results, is that stabilizing selection on expression selects against new mutations and prevents the rise or fixation of expression modifiers, therefore eroding the neutral expectation. It is also possible that the neutral expectation for expression divergence may not be linear, such that over evolutionary time, up- and downregulatory changes may offset one another, reducing the correlation of mutational input and trait divergence. Given that the species included in our study are quite distantly related (KS > 0.34 (Craig et al. 2021), and that we previously showed that up- and downregulating mutations are equally common (Balogun and Ness 2024), it is possible that the connection between mutation and divergence has been obscured over time. Our results are in contrast to Rifkin et al. (2005), who found a clearer correlation between VM and divergence in Drosophila species. That correlation may have been stronger because they only included genes with significant VM estimates or because the species are more closely related (KS ∼ 0.12 to 0.30) and therefore have less time to saturate. Similarly, strong correlations have been found in other traits, including wing shape in Drosophila (Houle et al. 2017) and a recent study in nematodes that showed the rate of divergence of vulval cell fates best explained by mutational input (Picao-Osorio et al. 2025). However, expression is a highly complex trait where a small number of expression quantitative trait loci can influence the expression of many genes (Schadt et al. 2003; Hubner et al. 2005; Petretto et al. 2006; Majewski and Pastinen 2011). Moreover, regulatory feedback in the co-expression network can potentially buffer expression change, reducing the variance in expression (Goncalves et al. 2012; Schaefke et al. 2013; Bader et al. 2015; Verta et al. 2016; Metzger et al. 2017). Although there are so few studies connecting VM to divergence that it is difficult to make generalizations, there is evidence from VM–VG comparisons that suggests that stabilizing selection is the dominant force shaping expression divergence.

Consistent with the limited predictive power of mutational variance for expression divergence, VM also exhibited a weak correlation with standing genetic variance (Pearson's R2 = 6 × 10−4, P = 3.32 × 10−3; see Figure S7). This contrasts sharply with the strong VM–VG correlations reported for other traits (Houle 1998; Lynch et al. 1998; Farhadifar et al. 2016; Houle et al. 2017; Dugand et al. 2021). Our mutational variance estimates (Balogun and Ness 2024) were derived from mutation-accumulation (MA) lines generated in two distinct genotypes, which showed only a weak—though statistically significant—correlation (Pearson's R = 0.19, P = 4.54 × 10−127). In contrast, Farhadifar et al. (2016) reported a much stronger correlation (R = 0.9) between VM estimates derived from two independent MA line sets for 19 traits associated with the first mitotic division. These differing results suggest that mutation-led expression variation is genotype-dependent, or that some traits may exhibit a more reproducible or stable mutational architecture than gene expression. Indeed, unlike many other traits, gene expression is highly dynamic, fluctuating across daily cycles (Strenkert et al. 2019) and responding sensitively to environmental conditions (Cheviron et al. 2008; Hodgins-Davis and Townsend 2009; Aguilar et al. 2010; Morris et al. 2014). Consequently, mutational effects measured in a single genetic background or environmental context may not be generalized across genotypes or conditions. Moreover, it has been shown that expression of many genes can be regulated by trans-acting loci (Albert et al. 2018; Hill et al. 2021; Vande Zande et al. 2022) and therefore allelic variation in these large-effect loci may have a strong impact on mutational variance in different genetic backgrounds. However, we do see that there is a correlation of VG with expression divergence (Fig. 9, Pearson's R2 = 0.12, P = 6.74 × 10−162), which indicates the amount of variation segregating in the population is correlated with the extent of divergence. A similar pattern was observed in mice, where genes with high within-species variation also displayed high expression differences between species (Khaitovich et al. 2004; Staubach et al. 2010). Since our mutation data does not explain this connection between VG and divergence, it is reasonable to infer that the variable strength of selection against new variation drives the correlation, further supporting the predominant role of stabilizing selection.

Too early to tell the fate of gene duplicates

Gene duplicates are commonly thought to provide a source of evolutionary novelty. Generally, gene copies are eliminated soon after their introduction due to drift and purifying selection against imbalanced gene products. However, gene duplicates that persist may evolve under two main processes. Either multiple ancestral functions are subdivided among duplicated genes (ie subfunctionalization), or one gene copy maintains the original function, allowing others to acquire a new function (ie neofunctionalization) (Ohno 2013). It follows that in addition to the function of the protein changing, its regulation may also be modified to its new role. To study the evolution of gene expression specifically in smaller-scale duplication events, we identified recent gene duplications for each Chlamydomonas species, such that a gene duplicate is found in only one species but not the other. We confirmed that our within-species paralogous gene pairs (gene duplicates) had lower neutral divergence than orthologous pairs between species (Figure S12). In this set of genes, we found that gene expression evolves at a faster rate in gene duplicates than in orthologs (Fig. 10, Mann–Whitney U test, P = 4.14 × 10−23), which supports either selection for increased expression change or reduced maintenance of expression. We found that duplicates tended to decrease in expression as they evolved (Figure S14), indicated by a slope less than one in read count comparisons of paralogs versus orthologs. Our previous data showed that regulatory mutations tended to drive expression up and down to a similar extent; therefore, the general reduction in duplicate expression cannot be simply explained by the neutral accumulation of mutations. It could therefore be interpreted as a reduction in the expression of gene copies as they are in the process of being eliminated. Alternatively, the overall downward trend could be explained by gene dosage compensation, where one or both copies of a functional gene are reduced in expression to cumulatively maintain the ancestral expression level. Reliable gene annotations would also be needed to discriminate between the theories. All in all, given the recency of these duplicates, it may be too early to determine their fate. Several theories explain its current evolutionary path.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that gene expression is a heritable trait with variability among subpopulations. Expression is highly conserved across Chlamydomonas species, with patterns of expression variation maintained over more than 10 million years of divergence. We observed that genes with high expression variation within C. reinhardtii exhibited greater variation between species, indicating that patterns of selection are generally consistent over long evolutionary timescales. Despite the predominance of purifying selection, 7% to 14% of the transcriptome shows evidence of directional selection, with accelerated divergence in expression relative to expectation. Finally, analyses of recent gene duplicates revealed that paralogs evolve more rapidly and are more frequently associated with reduced expression. While gene expression is predominantly conserved, a moderate fraction of positively selected expression levels indicates that modification of expression offers novelty that provides paths for evolutionary innovation sustained by the influx of new mutations.
