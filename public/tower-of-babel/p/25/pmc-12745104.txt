Decoding intertumoral heterogeneity in multifocal ground-glass nodule lung cancers: Clonal evolutionary insight for precision management
Authors: Chu-Long Xie, Xiao-Yang Hua, Qi Zhao, Ji-Bin Li, Zi-Hui Tan, Peng Li, Jie-Tian Jin, Lin Gao, Zai-Xian Tai, Jing-Sheng Cai, Xue Hou, Hao-Xian Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe management of multifocal ground-glass nodule (GGN) lung cancers remains hindered by insufficient knowledge of intertumoral heterogeneity, which complicates accurate diagnosis and effective treatment strategies. This study aims to characterize intertumoral heterogeneity of multifocal GGN lung cancers and to inform an evidence-based framework for diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making.MethodsWe performed multiregion whole-exome sequencing (WES) on 60 surgically resected lung GGNs and their matched normal tissues. Clonal evolution analysis (CEA), focusing on subclonal expansion, was compared with comprehensive histologic assessment (CHA) to evaluate clonal relationships among multifocal lung GGNs. Longitudinal follow-up was conducted to validate the findings' robustness.ResultsA total of 43 tumor pairs were included in the analysis. CEA classified 41 pairs (95.3%) as independent primary tumors (IPTs) and 2 pairs (4.7%) as intrapulmonary metastases (IMs). CHA initially identified 37 pairs (86.0%) as IPTs and 6 pairs (14.0%) as IMs. However, CEA reclassified 4 of the 6 CHA-identified IM pairs (66.7%) as IPTs, demonstrating superior diagnostic accuracy. Over a median follow-up of 57.5 months, 1 patient with IMs experienced recurrence and progression, whereas no such events occurred in patients with IPTs.ConclusionsCEA reveals noteworthy intertumoral heterogeneity in multifocal GGN lung cancers, highlighting the added diagnostic value of genetic profiling. Although most cases represent independent primaries, a subset aligns with metastatic processes, underscoring the need for vigilant assessment. These findings support a precision framework that integrates molecular data to optimize surveillance and therapeutic strategies, contributing to addressing the intertumoral heterogeneity challenges outlined in current management paradigms.Key Words: multifocal ground-glass nodule lung cancers, clonal evolution analysis, intertumoral heterogeneity

Central Message

Clonal evolution analysis enhances diagnostic accuracy in multifocal GGN lung cancers and supports a precision management framework by revealing intertumoral heterogeneity.

Perspective

Multifocal GGN lung cancers exhibit noteworthy intertumoral heterogeneity. Although most cases represent independent primaries, a subset aligns with metastatic processes, underscoring the need for vigilant assessment. Leveraging molecular signatures allows for tailored surveillance and therapeutic strategies, ultimately mitigating current management challenges and improving patient outcomes.

Pulmonary ground-glass nodules (GGNs) have become a significant health concern, serving as an early warning sign of lung cancer. The advent of low-dose computed tomography (CT) for lung cancer screening has significantly increased the detection of multifocal GGN lung cancers.1 A key challenge in managing multifocal GGN lung cancers is intertumoral heterogeneity, which complicates accurate diagnosis and effective treatment strategies. Determining whether multifocal GGN lung cancers represent independent primary tumors (IPTs) or intrapulmonary metastases (IMs) is critical for accurate diagnosis and optimized therapy. Traditionally, multifocal GGN lung cancers were regarded as IPTs, implying independent origins of each nodule.2 However, emerging evidence suggests the possibility of IMs within multifocal GGN lung cancers,3, 4, 5 although a robust diagnostic framework remains unavailable.

Comprehensive histologic assessment (CHA) has been used to differentiate IPTs from IMs by analyzing tumor architecture, cytologic, and stromal features.6,7 Despite its utility, CHA struggles to distinguish tumors with similar histologic appearances,7, 8, 9 highlighting the need for more reliable molecular approaches. Targeted sequencing has advanced our understanding of the heterogeneity of multifocal GGN lung cancers, yet limitations persist. Single-site sequencing often fails to capture the entire genomic landscape,10,11 and reliance on a limited set of driver mutations may compromise diagnostic accuracy.12

To address these gaps, we developed a clonal evolution analysis (CEA) model using multiregion whole-exome sequencing (WES) to minimize sampling bias and intratumoral heterogeneity effects.10,11 Subclonal expansion analysis was further integrated to refine clonal relationships, because subclonal expansion is a recognized hallmark of early metastasis in non−small cell lung cancer.13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 This approach offers a more comprehensive understanding of intertumoral heterogeneity, enabling precise differentiation between IPTs and IMs and informing tailored management strategies for patients with multifocal GGN lung cancers.

Methods

Patients and Samples

Between January 2019 and January 2021, 60 GGN samples from 26 patients who underwent surgical resection were analyzed. All of the resected lesions were clinically considered malignant on imaging (Lung-RADS category ≥4A).19 To reduce the potential influence of intratumor heterogeneity,10,11 at least 3 different regions were sampled from each lesion. Tumor regions were selected on the basis of spatial distribution: peripheral and central areas were sampled while avoiding necrotic or hemorrhagic zones. Multiregion tumor tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen within 20 minutes after resection,20,21 and paired peripheral blood samples were collected preoperatively. This study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (approval number: B2018-054-02; approved on June 15, 2018). This study was registered in the Research Registry from https://www.researchregistry.com (UIN: researchregistry10691). All patients participating in this study provided informed written consent specifically for the publication of their deidentified study data.

Comprehensive Histologic Assessment

CHA was performed to classify the tumor pairs as IPTs or IMs according to the criteria proposed by Girard and colleagues.6 Following these criteria, we classified tumor pairs as definite IPTs when they exhibited distinct histologic types or when 1 of the tumors presented with inflammation, atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH), adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), or minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA). Conversely, tumor pairs were classified as high-probability IPTs when they shared the same histologic type but displayed significantly different histopathologic characteristics, including architectural patterns, cytological features, and stromal features. Tumor pairs with similar histopathologic characteristics were considered indeterminate IMs. Histopathologic features were independently reviewed by 2 experienced pathologists (P.L. and J.T.J.) according to the 2021 World Health Organization classification of thoracic tumors.22 In cases with divergent or indeterminate diagnoses, a consensus was reached after consultation with a third pathologist.

Whole-Exome Sequencing

DNA was extracted from surgical fresh-frozen tumor specimens using the TIANamp Genomic DNA kit (TIANGEN Biotech) following the manufacturer's instructions. DNA was also isolated from peripheral blood or normal lung tissues as control to distinguish somatic from germline mutations. We used blood or normal tissue DNA depending on data availability. The DNA data were normalized by removing differences between blood and normal tissue. The purity and concentration of DNA were determined using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer with Quanti-iT dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Library construction was performed using the xGen Exome Research Panel (Integrated DNA Technologies). WES was performed using GeneSeq-2000 (GenePlus-Beijing), with 100-bp paired-end sequencing. Detailed information on data filtering and quality control, and somatic variant and copy number alteration calling are described in the Appendix E2 and E3.

Phylogenetic Analysis

Phylogenetic tree is a visual representation of potential evolutionary relationships between multiple entities.23 Meskit (R package)24 was applied to reconstruct phylogenetic trees of GGNs from the same patient using all nonsynonymous mutations identified by WES, and the maximum parsimony algorithm was used. In this study, trunk event was defined as ubiquitous mutations shared by all GGNs from the same individual, whereas branch event was accordingly defined as heterogeneous mutations shared only in specific GGNs. Branch event was further defined as private event when exclusively identified in a single GGN. The length of each branch was proportional to the number of nonsynonymous mutations.25

Clonal Evolution Analysis

PyClone (v0.13.1),25 a Bayesian clustering method, was used to analyze the clonal structure in the study. The variant calls generated by Mutect2, and the copy number variation results obtained from Sequenza were parsed and provided as input to PyClone for analysis. The cancer cell fractions of mutations were estimated using PyClone, which adjusted the variant allele frequencies of somatic mutations on the basis of the local copy numbers of the mutated loci and the tumor purity information obtained from Absolute (R package).26

CEA was used for tumor pair classification at the molecular level, with its usage based on the concordance of somatic mutations and subclonal expansion. This approach was derived from the criteria outlined by Mansuet-Lupo and colleagues12 as follows: (1) definite IPT, when tumor pairs had no somatic mutations in common; and (2) undetermined, when tumor pairs shared 1 or more identical somatic mutations. Undetermined tumors were further classified as high-probability IPTs or IMs on the basis of the occurrence of subclonal expansion. High-probability IPTs were defined as tumor pairs lacking evidence of subclonal expansion, whereas high-probability IMs were characterized by the presence of subclonal expansion. Subclonal expansion in cancer refers to the process in which seeding clusters of primary tumor cells acquire specific genetic mutations and proliferate, leading to the formation of a metastatic tumor13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 (Figure 2, A). Clonality is determined on the basis of the cancer cell fractions, which is considered clonal when it reaches a threshold of 0.6 or greater.27

Follow-up

Patients were scheduled for follow-up surveillance at 3-month intervals during the first 2 years, biannually from years 3 to 5, and annually thereafter. The follow-up assessment included a physical examination, thoracic and upper abdominal thin-section CT scans, and tumor marker testing. If a patient had specific symptoms, an expedited examination was conducted to facilitate a thorough assessment.28 Follow-up data were updated in December 2024.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Normally distributed measurement data are expressed as mean and standard deviation, and non-normally distributed data are described using median and interquartile ranges. Categorical data were compared using either the Pearson χ2 test, Fisher exact test, or McNemar test. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0 and R 4.0.2 software).

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 26 patients with 60 surgically resected GGNs were included in the study. The median age of the cohort was 56.5 years (range, 28-78 years). A predominance of women (76.9%) and nonsmokers (84.6%) characterized the patient demographics. The GGNs presented with a median diameter of 9 mm (range, 6-38 mm). Among the types of GGNs, pure GGNs were more common, representing 56.7%, whereas mixed GGNs accounted for 43.3%. The clinical characteristics of the 26 patients are summarized in Table E1.

In this cohort, 19 patients each presented with 2 tumors, 6 patients each had 3 tumors, and 1 patient had 4 tumors, yielding a total of 43 tumor pairs for comparison (Figure 1, B).

Mutational Profile

After we performed WES on 26 patients with multifocal GGN lung cancers, a total of 1857 somatic variations were detected across the 60 lesions. The analysis revealed the top 5 frequent cancer-related mutated genes in the 60 tumors: EGFR (45%), KRAS (8.33%), TP53 (6.67%), MAP2K1 (5%), and BRAF (5%). Among the detected variations, missense mutations were the most common type (80.2%), with C >T substitutions being the most prevalent single-nucleotide variation (36.1%).

The median tumor mutational burden of the multifocal GGN lung cancers was 0.46 mutations per megabase, markedly lower than the tumor mutational burden of 4.52 mutations per megabase observed in the lung adenocarcinoma cohort from The Cancer Genome Atlas database. This significant difference is largely attributable to the high incidence of solid tumors in The Cancer Genome Atlas cohort, which generally present with a greater mutational burden.

Heterogeneity Among Tumor Pairs

In this study, 14 of the 43 tumor pairs (32.6%) exhibited no common somatic mutations, underscoring the genetic heterogeneity inherent in multifocal GGN lung cancers. Conversely, the remaining 29 pairs (67.4%) shared 1 or more identical somatic mutations (Table E2), indicating a likelihood of clonal relatedness. However, the significance of these findings remains to be determined. In further analyses, we performed CHA and CEA to better evaluate the clonal relationships among the tumors.

Outcomes of CHA Among Tumor Pairs

According to the CHA, 22 of 43 tumor pairs (51.2%) were classified as definite IPTs on the basis of the diagnosis of at least 1 nodule in each pair as either inflammatory, AAH, AIS, or MIA. Fifteen tumor pairs (34.9%) exhibiting markedly distinct histopathologic characteristics were classified as high-probability IPTs. Six tumor pairs (13.9%) were classified as indeterminate IMs because of their similar histopathologic characteristics. The detailed outcomes of CHA in the 43 tumor pairs are shown in Table E2.

Outcomes of CEA Among Tumor Pairs

A total of 14 tumor pairs (32.6%) without overlapping somatic mutations were classified as definite IPTs. In contrast, the remaining 29 tumor pairs (67.4%) shared 1 or more identical somatic mutations. Among these 29 tumor pairs, only 2 pairs exhibiting subclonal expansion were classified as high-probability IMs, as depicted in Figure 2, B and C. The other 27 pairs, without evidence of subclonal expansion, were classified as high-probability IPTs.

Notably, in the IM tumor pair (P22T1 vs P22T2) classified by CEA, a TIE1 mutation was identified within the cluster exhibiting subclonal expansion. This mutation has been reported to be associated with angiogenesis and metastasis.29, 30, 31 The detailed CEA outcomes for the 43 tumor pairs are listed in Table E3.

CHA Versus CEA in Identifying IMs and IPTs

A comparison between CHA and CEA in identifying IMs and IPTs is shown in Figure 3, A and B. Overall, 4 of the 43 tumor pairs (9.3%) showed discordant results between CHA and CEA. All 4 tumor pairs were misclassified as IMs by the CHA because of their similar histopathologic characteristics. However, after CEA review, these 4 tumor pairs were reclassified as IPTs because of the lack of shared somatic mutations or subclonal expansion. Although the differences in diagnostic outcomes between the 2 methods did not reach statistical significance (Figure 3, B, P = .125), the fact that 4 of 6 tumor pairs (66.7%) that classified as IMs by CHA were corrected as IPTs by CEA suggested a strong power of CEA in discriminating IMs and IPTs.

Follow-up Outcomes

The median follow-up duration for the entire cohort was 57.5 months (range, 47-66 months). During follow-up, none of the patients with IPT tumor pairs alone experienced disease recurrence or progression. Conversely, 1 of the 2 patients with IM tumor pairs experienced disease recurrence and progression. Four small new lesions were identified in this patient 19 months after surgery, and these were considered as malignant based on a CT scan assessment (Lung-RADS category ≥4A, Figure 4, A). The fact that new lesions considered to be malignant occurred during this short period of follow-up indicates the highly aggressive nature of the primary tumors. Furthermore, at 58 months after surgery, we observed progression in the 2 lesions identified before surgery. The size of the 2 lesions had increased from 5 mm to 10 mm and from 8 mm to 11 mm, respectively (Figure 4, B). The emergence of new malignant lesions and the progression of existing lesions underscore the high-risk and aggressive behavior of this case.

Discussion

In this study, we developed the integration of multiregion WES and clonal expansion analysis to precisely evaluate intertumoral heterogeneity and clonal relationships in multifocal GGN lung cancers. The outcomes revealed that multifocal GGN lung cancers exhibit substantial intertumoral heterogeneity; although the majority represent independent primary tumors, a subset demonstrates metastatic potential. These findings not only bridge a critical gap in understanding intertumoral heterogeneity but also inform strategies for the personalized management of multifocal GGN lung cancers.

To the best of our knowledge, this is a novel study to systematically apply CEA for clonal relationship assessment in multifocal GGN lung cancers. The methodology adopted in this study has 2 advantages. First, multiregion WES performed in this study circumvents selection bias inherent in single-site sequencing and provides a comprehensive genomic landscape.10,11 Second, subclonal expansion analysis, validated by the TRACERx study,13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 offers a superior paradigm for diagnosing metastasis compared with traditional criteria reliant on common clonal clusters. Although the presence of common clonal clusters between 2 lesions is important for the diagnosis of metastasis, this approach is more applicable to advanced solid tumors where dominant clonal mutations present in primary tumor lesions also manifest in metastatic lesions.13,14 In contrast, GGNs are typically regarded as early-stage tumors, in which clonal diversification remains nascent. The TRACERx study revealed that dominant clonal mutations presenting in primary lesions are consistently absent in early metastatic lesions.13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Instead, the subclonal seeding clusters within primary lesions, which are capable of metastasis, may undergo subclonal expansion and proliferate at the metastatic site13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 (Figure 2, A). By integrating spatial genomic mapping with clonal dynamics, this study establishes a novel paradigm for differentiating true metastases from multicentric origins in early-stage disease.

The analysis of the tumors' molecular characteristics uncovered 2 significant insights into intertumoral heterogeneity. First, multifocal GGN lung cancers harboring distinct prevalent driver mutations are not necessarily IPTs, challenging previous assumptions in studies of multiple lung tumors.12,32, 33, 34 For instance, the tumor pair of patient 1 (P1T2 vs P1T3) in this study harbored different driver mutations, which, according to earlier principles, would traditionally be classified as IPTs.12,32, 33, 34 However, this tumor pair was reclassified as IM rather than IPT because further CEA identified subclonal expansion in the tumors (Figure 2, B). Second, multifocal GGN lung cancers harboring a single frequent driver mutation are not necessarily IMs. This was exemplified by 3 tumor pairs that shared a single frequent driver mutation (Table E2; EGFR p.L858R in the tumor pairs of patients 3, 10, and 18). In the present study, CEA classified them as high-probability IPTs rather than IMs. Audrey and colleagues12 suggested that tumors exhibiting a common driver mutation (such as EGFR del 19, EGFR p.L858R, or KRAS p.G12X) can be classified as IPTs. This classification is based on the observation that the concurrent presence of these common driver mutations across different tumors is not rare. Nevertheless, the foundation of their hypothesis, which rests solely on the likelihood of mutation occurrence, appears arbitrary. In contrast, this study provided more definitive evidence to substantiate the classification of these tumors as IPTs by demonstrating the absence of subclonal expansion. Taken together, subclonal expansion analysis provides a more robust framework for differentiating IMs from IPTs.

The investigation of heterogeneity and clonal relationships among multifocal GGN lung cancers provoke our thinking about the management of this specific disease. The treatment strategy for multifocal GGN lung cancers still lacks consensus.35 It is usually impossible to remove all lesions without compromising the pulmonary function for these patients. Is it feasible to resect 1 or 2 primary lesions for pathologic and molecular analysis, then administer molecular-targeted therapies to the residual lesions on the basis of the molecular features of the resected lesions? A recent study demonstrated that the response rate of postoperative tyrosine kinase inhibitor treatment for unresected GGN lesions was only as low as 33.3%, even if the resected main lesion harbored a tyrosine kinase inhibitor−sensitive EGFR mutation,36 suggesting that the clinical efficacy of this strategy was limited. These results can be explained by the findings of the present study, which revealed significant intertumoral heterogeneity among multifocal GGN lung cancers. On the basis of these findings, we propose that it is unreasonable to guide treatment regimens for the remaining lesions merely on the basis of the molecular features of the resected lesion(s).

The prevalence of IMs in our cohort (2/26 patients, 7.7%) aligns with previous reports showing IM rates of 7.7% to 10.5% in multifocal lung adenocarcinomas presenting as GGNs.4,37 Although this did not reach statistical significance as the result of our sample size, the clinical relevance is evident from the aggressive behavior observed during follow-up, with 2 patients with IM developing recurrence and progression. The identified IM tumor pairs were derived from patient 1 and patient 22. Patient 1 developed tumor recurrence 19 months after surgery, manifested by 4 new GGNs with a high probability of malignancy on CT scan (Figure 4, A). Furthermore, at 58 months after surgery, we observed progression of 2 other lesions that were identified before surgery. The size of the 2 lesions had increased from 5 mm to 10 mm and from 8 mm to 11 mm, respectively (Figure 4, B). The emergence of new malignant lesions in the short follow-up period, along with the progression of the existing lesions, underscores the high-risk and aggressive behavior of this case. Patient 22 had not yet experienced recurrence or progression after a follow-up of 44 months. However, we propose an intensive surveillance for this patient for 2 reasons. First, given the tumor's onset at a young age (28 years), an extended follow-up period is imperative. Second, a TIE1 mutation associated with metastasis29, 30, 31 was identified in the IM tumor pair from this patient, warranting closer monitoring.

It is noteworthy that CHA also exhibited acceptable performance in identifying IPTs in some cases. Our findings demonstrated that 22 of 43 tumor pairs (51.2%) were classified as definite IPTs by CHA. This classification was predicated on the diagnosis of at least 1 nodule in each pair as either inflammatory, AAH, AIS, or MIA; all of which were reported to have a curative prognosis.38 This highlights specific histologic scenarios in which CHA is more suitable than CEA for identifying IPTs, given that CHA is both more convenient and less costly in clinical practice.

However, CHA also presented limitations, as evidenced by 6 tumor pairs initially suspected of being IMs because of their similar stromal and cytologic features and predominant architectural patterns. Nevertheless, a subsequent analysis by CEA reclassified 4 of these pairs (66.7%) as IPTs (Figure 3, B) rather than IMs, because they had no overlapping mutations or lacked evidence of subclonal expansion. This finding supports the notion that similar histologic appearances alone are insufficient to define IMs.7, 8, 9

Although CEA allows a powerful distinction between IPTs and IMs for multifocal GGN lung cancers, it also encounters several limitations, such as cumbersome technical steps and high costs. In recent years, deep sequencing has been widely used in clinical practice; however, it still requires a substantially longer time than a pathologic review. The complete histomolecular assessment requires approximately 3 to 4 weeks (3-5 days for CHA, plus an additional 12-17 days for CEA when indicated), which—although longer than routine pathology—remains acceptable for treatment planning in multifocal GGN cases, particularly for patients classified as high-probability IM. To address these challenges, we propose a comprehensive histomolecular algorithm that integrates CHA and CEA for robust classification of multifocal GGN lung cancers, thereby enhancing clinical decision-making (Figure 3, C).

This study has several limitations. First, the requirement for fresh-frozen tissue and multiregion sampling may limit clinical applicability. Second, the computational complexity and cost of multiregion WES and CEA analysis may pose challenges for routine clinical implementation. Third, our small sample size limits statistical power for subgroup analyses. Finally, validation in larger, multi-institutional cohorts is needed to confirm our findings and establish standardized protocols.

Conclusions

Clonal evolution analysis reveals significant intertumoral heterogeneity in multifocal GGN lung cancers and highlights the pivotal role of genetic analysis in improving diagnostic accuracy. Although most multifocal GGN lung cancers are IPTs, vigilance is warranted to identify potential IMs. Our findings provide valuable insight into intertumor heterogeneity of multifocal GGN lung cancers, underscoring the importance of tailoring diagnostic and therapeutic strategies to accommodate the intertumoral heterogeneity in the management of multifocal GGN lung cancers.

All of the resected ground-glass nodules were identified preoperatively on thin-section computed tomography, which were clinically considered malignant on imaging (Lung-RADS category ≥4A). After surgical resection, the tumors were bisected along their largest axial plane for histopathologic and molecular analysis. (1) Histopathology half: One half of the tumor was entirely submitted for routine formalin fixation and paraffin embedding. Serial step sections were prepared to ensure that all histologic growth patterns were represented and margins could be evaluated. (2) Molecular half: From the remaining half, ≥3 spatially distant regions (peripheral, intermediate, and central zones whenever possible) were dissected under sterile conditions. The matched normal lung tissues were dissected with a new scalpel blade to prevent cross-contamination. These samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen within 20 minutes after resection and stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction for whole-exome sequencing. Matched peripheral blood samples were collected preoperatively. This bisect approach conforms to other multiregion sequencing protocols, maintains diagnostic integrity for pathology, and still provides high-quality genomic material even in nodules <10 mm.

Appendix E2. Data Filtering and Quality Control

In the raw sequencing data, a minority of reads contain adapter sequences, low-quality bases, or undetermined bases. These low-quality data can significantly impact the accuracy of subsequent data analysis. To mitigate this, we employed the fastp softwareE1 to perform meticulous filtering on the raw reads postsequencing, yielding high-quality clean reads. All subsequent analyses were conducted exclusively on these clean reads. The data-filtering process primarily entailed the removal of reads falling into 1 of 3 categories in a paired-end manner: (1) Reads with a length shorter than 35 base pairs after trimming adapter sequences and low-quality bases from both ends; (2) Reads with an N (representing undetermined bases) content exceeding 10% of the total number of bases; (3) single-end reads exhibiting a proportion of low-quality bases (quality value Q ≤ 5) exceeding 50% of the entire read length.

Appendix E3. Somatic Variant and Copy Number Alteration Calling

Clean paired-end reads were aligned to the human genome build hg38 (University of California Santa Cruz) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (version 0.7.17).E2 Aligned SAM files of each library were sorted and formatted to BAM files using samtools (version 1.5).E3 Duplicate reads were identified and the base quality scores were recalibrated using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, version 4.3.0).E4

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were used as the germline control whenever available because they provide a reliable source of constitutional DNA largely devoid of somatic alterations; when peripheral blood mononuclear cells were unavailable, normal lung tissue from the same patient served as the substitute germline reference. To minimize batch effects between blood- and tissue-derived normals, both sample types were processed through a unified pipeline that removed mutations linked to environmental exposure (based on public databases), enforced a stringent variant-allele-frequency threshold of >5% for somatic calls, cross-referenced all variants against germline databases to exclude polymorphisms, and included manual curation of putative driver mutations to ensure accuracy.

Somatic variants, including somatic single-nucleotide variants and small insertions and deletions (indels), were called using MuTect2.E5 Subsequently, we performed mutation imputation on each tumor sample from the same patient. The process entailed the extraction of alignment information for each mutation in every sample from the BAM files. For each position of interest, the reference and alternate reads were recalculated. If the alter reads exceeded 2 and the coverage of reads at the locus was greater than 10, the mutation at that position for the respective sample was retrieved. A set of stringent filtering criteria was applied to the initial pool of variant candidates in order to identify reliable single-nucleotide variants and indels. For single-nucleotide variants, the following criteria were enforced: (1) Tumor samples had to exhibit a minimum total coverage of 10 × and variant coverage of 3 × , ensuring sufficient sequencing depth for reliable variant detection in the tumor. (2) The matched normal sample from each patient was required to have a minimum coverage of 10 × , enabling accurate comparison with the tumor sample.

Regarding indels, the following stringent criteria were implemented: (1) Only calls marked with “PASS” in the Variant Call Format file were retained, indicating that these calls met predetermined quality standards. (2) Tumor samples were subjected to a minimum total coverage of 10 × and variant coverage of 3 × , ensuring reliable detection of indels with adequate sequencing depth. (3) The matched normal sample for each patient required a minimum coverage of 10 × , and the absence of mutated reads, thereby minimizing the possibility of germline variants being mistaken as somatic mutations.

DNA copy number variations were detected using Sequenza version 3.0.E6 To identify focal copy number gain and loss events, the GISTIC 2.0E7 algorithm was employed. This allowed for the identification of specific regions of the genome exhibiting significant copy number alterations.
