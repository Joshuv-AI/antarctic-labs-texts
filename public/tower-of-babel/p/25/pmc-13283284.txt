Biased and inflexible interpretations of social situations predict depressive symptoms and relational outcomes in parent-adolescent dyads
Authors: Reuma Gadassi-Polack, Reut Zabag, Michael V Bronstein, Jutta Joormann, Jonas Everaert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdolescence is a critical period for social-emotional development, characterized by increased risk for psychopathology and disruptive changes in the parent-adolescent relationship. Biased and inflexible interpretations of social situations have been linked to psychopathology and adaptive social functioning, but these associations are rarely studied in adolescence. To address this gap, the present study examined whether interpretation bias and cognitive inflexibility are associated between parents and adolescents, and whether these factors relate to depressive symptoms and relational perceptions. A sample of 112 parents and adolescents recruited online from the general population completed at baseline the Bias Against Disconfirmatory Evidence task and measures of perceived partner responsiveness and insensitivity, followed by a 28-day diary. Parents’ and adolescents’ negative biases were moderately and positively correlated, but not their positive bias or inflexibility. Negative bias and inflexibility in both negative and positive interpretations predicted higher levels of depressive symptoms. Regarding relational perceptions, an interesting picture emerged: positive bias was related to perceived responsiveness, negative bias was related to perceived insensitivity, and inflexibility was associated with both perceived responsiveness and insensitivity. Extending beyond their established role in psychopathology, our results demonstrate that interpretation biases and inflexibility may influence the maintenance of close relationships through their effects on how parents and adolescents perceive each other’s responsiveness and insensitivity in daily interactions. These findings suggest that therapeutic interventions should assess biased and inflexible interpretations in both parents and adolescents and address them when present to improve depressive symptoms and relationship quality.Keywords: Interpretation flexibility, Adolescence, Parenting, Depression, Perceived responsiveness and insensitivity, Dyadic designs, Daily diaries

Introduction

Adolescence is a critical period for social-emotional development that often presents a multitude of novel social situations, as it coincides with transferring schools and the emergence of romantic relationships (Meeus, 2016; Meuwese et al., 2017). Simultaneously, the adolescent-parent relationship goes through normative, yet disruptive, changes, as it becomes more conflictual and less close (Branje, 2018; Marceau et al., 2015), and adolescents become more independent from parents (Blakemore, 2018; Crone & Dahl, 2012; Hadiwijaya et al., 2017; Ho et al., 2025; Nelson et al., 2005). Despite these changes, parents continue to exert critical influence on adolescents’ social-emotional development and mental health (e.g., interactions with parents [vs. peers] are more strongly related to youth depressive symptoms; Gadassi Polack, Sened et al., 2021; see also Barthel et al., 2018; Ho et al., 2025; Klimes-Dougan et al., 2007; Laursen & Collins, 2009). Indeed, poorer relationships with parents are longitudinally related to poorer child mental health (Morgan et al., 2012), whereas secure attachment to caregivers is negatively associated with mental health issues (Spruit et al., 2020).

This combination of changes—expectations for increased independence, heightened emotionality, decreased parental involvement, and multiple novel stressors—makes adolescence a challenging developmental period and is thought to contribute to the increases in mental health issues, including depressive symptoms, that characterize this stage (Rapee et al., 2019; Solmi et al., 2022). Adolescent-onset depression often has long-lasting effects, including worse academic and interpersonal outcomes, a more chronic and severe course of psychopathology, and increased risk of suicidality (Zisook et al., 2004). Thus, there is theoretical and practical importance for the identification of modifiable risk factors. Biased and inflexible interpretations of social information have emerged as such risk factors that can serve as targets for investigation and intervention (Gadassi Polack, Bronstein et al., 2023; Platt et al., 2017).

The role of biased and inflexible interpretations in adolescent and adult depression

Interpretation is a semantic process in which aspects of a situation are integrated into a mental representation that clarifies the meaning and implications of an event (Blanchette & Richards, 2010). Biased interpretations, the habitual selection of overly negative or insufficiently positive interpretation of ambiguous events, has been argued to contribute to the development of depression by inspiring pessimistic beliefs about the self, others, and the world (e.g., Beck & Bredemeier, 2016). Indeed, empirical findings show that depression is cross-sectionally associated with more negative and fewer positive interpretations (Gadassi-Polack, Bronstein et al., 2023; Haller et al., 2016; Lau & Waters, 2017; Leigh & Clark, 2018; Mobach et al., 2019; Oliver et al., 2019; Stuijfzand et al., 2018). Supporting the role of interpretation biases as risk factors are studies showing their predictive value (for a meta-analysis, see Vos et al., 2025), as well as the presence of such biases in never-depressed adolescents who are at high risk for developing depression (Dearing & Gotlib, 2009; Sfärlea et al., 2021). Finally, evidence from the Cognitive Bias Modification literature shows that decreases in negative interpretations and increases in positive ones are related to decreases in depressive symptoms (e.g., Krebs et al., 2018; Platt et al., 2017; cf., Sicouri et al., 2024).

Although biased interpretations may have adverse mental health consequences, it is important to keep in mind that situations are not static – and neither are interpretations. Ideally, initial interpretations are updated to reflect additional information gathered as situations evolve (Mehu & Scherer, 2015). Revisions that reflect accumulated evidence, therefore, are crucial for final interpretation accuracy. Indeed, research has increasingly focused on interpretation flexibility, defined as the degree to which an initial interpretation is revised (or updated) to reflect accumulated information which suggests the initial interpretation was incorrect (e.g., Everaert et al., 2018; Gadassi-Polack, Bronstein et al., 2023; Vernimmen et al., 2025).

A recent study showed that interpretation inflexibility was positively correlated with adolescents’ depressive symptoms and social anxiety, and moderated the effect of negative social interactions with family and friends on adolescents’ depressive symptoms (Gadassi Polack, Bronstein et al., 2023). Further support for the importance of interpretation inflexibility comes from studies on adults that show its association with higher levels of depression, social anxiety, posttraumatic stress symptoms, and suicidality (Deng et al., 2022, Deng et al., 2023a,b; Everaert et al., 2018, 2020, 2021; Haim-Nachum et al., 2024; Kube & Rozenkrantz, 2021; Zabag et al., 2022). While the evidence supporting the role of biased and inflexible interpretations in psychopathology is substantial, a crucial question remains regarding their developmental origins. Theoretical models suggest that parents play a significant role in shaping how children and adolescents interpret social information (Aktar, 2022; Goodman & Gotlib, 1999; Perlman et al., 2022).

The intergenerational transmission of interpretation bias

Developmental theories propose that, among other influences (e.g., peers; Dishion & Tipsord, 2011), exposure to parental biased information processing contributes to the development of child social information processing (Perlman et al., 2022). Empirical findings show support for this proposition. For example, a recent meta-analysis shows that parent and child interpretation biases are positively correlated (Subar & Rozenman, 2021). Similarly, a study on parents and adolescents in the context of parental depression history showed a positive association between parent and adolescent negative interpretation bias (Sfärlea et al., 2019).

While this research establishes that interpretation biases can be transmitted from parents to children, less is known about how these cognitive patterns might influence the quality of ongoing parentadolescent interactions. Given that adolescence is marked by significant changes in the parent-adolescent relationship (Branje, 2018; Marceau et al., 2015; Zapf et al., 2024), understanding whether and how interpretation biases and inflexibility affect how parents and adolescents perceive each other becomes particularly important. If biased and inflexible interpretations contribute to distorted perceptions of social interactions more broadly, they may also color how family members view each other’s behavior and intentions during daily interactions.

The role of biased and inflexible interpretations in the perception of caregivers

Indeed, research suggests that while caregivers’ actual behavior influences how children later perceive them, child characteristics and cognitions also shape these perceptions (Cook et al., 2018). Theoretical models suggest that interpretation biases and inflexibility—which lead to less accurate social interpretations—have real-world consequences for forming and maintaining close relationships (Everaert et al., 2018; Kashdan & Rottenberg, 2010; Mehu & Scherer, 2015; Stange et al., 2017). Empirical evidence supports this link: Cognitive Bias Modification interventions can change how both children and adults perceive their parents (De Winter et al., 2017; Doolan et al., 2021). Additionally, research with adults demonstrates that interpretation inflexibility is associated with poorer self-reported social functioning, both cross-sectionally and over time (Deng et al., 2023a; Zabag et al., 2024).

Interpretation bias and parenting

Just as adolescents’ interpretations may shape how they perceive their parents, parents’ interpretation processes likely influence how they perceive and respond to their adolescent children. Indeed, parental perceptions of their young children has garnered much attention (Garon-Bissonnette et al., 2024; King et al., 2021; Mudiam et al., 2023). The handful of studies that examined parental interpretations during adolescence show that more negative interpretation is positively associated with parental psychopathology and less adaptive (e.g., harsh) parenting (Chen et al., 2009; Mudiam et al., 2023; Sheeber et al., 2009; Wang & Wang, 2018).

Gaps in the literature

Despite the growing evidence for the importance of interpretation processes in psychopathology and relationships, several critical gaps remain. First, research has predominantly focused on interpretation bias rather than flexibility. This gap is particularly significant during adolescence, a period of rapidly changing and novel social situations. Second, while theoretical models suggest connections between interpretation flexibility and relationship perceptions, empirical research rarely examined this link, and only in adults. The current study will focus on a type of relationship perception called perceived responsiveness, the degree to which one feels cared for, understood, and valued by their relationship partner (Reis et al., 2004). Examining perceived responsiveness is important considering its critical for building and maintaining close relationships (Cook et al., 2018; Crasta et al., 2021; Gordon & Chen, 2016; Reis, 2017), which play a role in supporting mental health (Gadassi & Rafaeli, 2015; Spruit et al., 2020). Third, research on parents’ interpretation processes during adolescence remains limited (Mudiam et al., 2023), despite the importance of the parent-adolescent relationship for both parents’ and adolescents’ mental health and the normative disruptions that characterize this relationship during this developmental period (Branje, 2018; Gadassi Polack, Sened et al., 2021; Marceau et al., 2015; Zapf et al., 2024).

The present study

The current study addresses the aforementioned gaps in the literature by utilizing data from the Parent-Adolescent Interpersonal emotion Regulation Study (PAIRS; Gadassi Polack, 2024) which included the emotional Bias Against Disconfirmatory Evidence (BADE; Everaert et al., 2018) task at baseline followed by an intensive longitudinal design (a 28-day daily diary) completed online. The BADE comprises of a series of self-referential scenarios with themes of social failure and rejection presented in three stages. In each stage, additional information about an unfolding ambiguous social situation is provided. After viewing each stage, participants rate the plausibility of four different interpretations of the information acquired thus far in that scenario. The first stage guides the participant to one direction of interpretation (negative or positive), but by the third and final stage this initial direction is discovered to be erroneous. Thus, the BADE requires participants to revise their initial interpretation of the situation, either from negative to positive or vice-versa. The task uniquely teases apart initial interpretation biases and the ability (or lack thereof) to change the initial interpretation (i.e., interpretation inflexibility). Because social scenarios in the original BADE may not be relevant for children and adolescents, we used the adolescent BADE (Gadassi-Polack, Bronstein et al., 2023) for adolescents, and the original, adult version, BADE (Everaert et al., 2018) for parents.

The current investigation examined how parents’ and adolescents’ biased and inflexible interpretations of social situations are cross-sectionally and longitudinally associated with psychopathology and relational perceptions. The use of daily diaries (structured daily measurements), a highly ecologically valid method, is recommended for studying social-emotional processes in adolescence (aan het Rot, 2012; Russell & Gajos, 2020). The design, sample size, analytic plan, and the first hypothesis was pre-registered at https://osf.io/cmuv2/?view_only=71ba7088968a4e5ca63722c74733dda0.

We tested the following hypotheses (pre-registered hypotheses are indicated with a *):

H1*. We hypothesized that there would be a positive association between parent and adolescent interpretation biases and inflexibility

H2. Lower interpretation inflexibility and positive bias, and higher negative interpretation bias would be cross-sectionally associated with higher levels of perceived partner insensitivity and lower levels of perceived partner responsiveness, as measured at baseline.

H3. Lower interpretation inflexibility and positive bias, and higher negative interpretation bias would predict higher levels of depressive symptoms and perceived partner insensitivity, and lower levels of perceived partner responsiveness, as measured at the daily diary.

In the pre-registration, we also hypothesized that the BADE would be associated with depressive and social anxiety symptoms at baseline, as such replicating prior work in adults and adolescents. These models are reported in the supplementary materials to reduce complexity (Table S1). This study focused on extending prior work.

Method

The current study uses data from PAIRS, a larger investigation of emotional processes in parent-adolescent dyads (Gadassi Polack, 2024). Only measures relevant to the current study are described. The study was approved by the Yale University Institutional Review Board (Human Investigation Committee #2000022492).

Participants

Parent-adolescent dyads were recruited via advertisements inviting parents and adolescents to participate in a study examining how they react to each other’s emotions. Advertisements were posted online (e.g., Facebook) or emailed to parents of teenagers who had participated in our previous studies. Eligible dyads (1) had an adolescent aged between 12 and 18 years old who resided with the participating parent for at least half the study period, (2) both partners were fluent and literate in English (3) resided on the East coast of the United States, and (4) had daily access to a device connected to the Internet. To ensure data quality, we adopted several attention checks in the initial survey and included only participants who failed one or less of the attention checks; however, no dyads were excluded for this reason. Additionally, because the study was conducted online, we took measures to ensure participants were parent and child (e.g., conducting the initial video call with both parent and child present; N of excluded dyads = 24), and that they indeed reside in USA and/or participate only once (N of excluded dyads = 65). Finally, as pre-registered and recommended for studies using intensive longitudinal assessment (Trull & Ebner-Priemer, 2020), we included only dyads in which both members completed at least 14 diary entries; this criterion further ensured participants were committed to the study, thus indirectly supporting data quality (N of excluded dyads = 3). Detailed information regarding data quality procedures can be found in the supplementary materials and in Gadassi-Polack et al. (2025).

Our final sample consisted of 112 adolescent-parent dyads (i.e., 112 adolescents and 108 parents, as parents were allowed to participate consecutively with different children) who completed the background session and daily diary. Adolescent ages ranged from 12 to 18 years old (Mean = 14.13, SD = 1.74, Median = 14), and parent ages ranged from 33 to 64 years old (Mean = 43.99, SD = 6.03, Median = 43). Sixty-one adolescents (54.5 %) identified as female, 49 (43.8 %) identified as male, and two (1.8 %) identified as “other.” Ninety-six parents (88.9 %) identified as female and 12 (11.1 %) identified as male. Participants were largely White (64.3 % of the adolescents; 75 % of the parents) and non-Hispanic (92 % of the adolescents; 91.7 % of the parents). Most parents (75.9 %) were married and lived with their child 100 % of the time (90.4 % of the dyads). Overall, parents completed a mean of 26.63 diary entries (SD = 2.37, Median = 27, Range = 19–32) and adolescents completed a mean of 25.27 diary entries (SD = 3.45, Median = 26, Range = 14–31); this resulted in 5442 data points.

Procedure

As can be seen in Fig. S1, the study started with an onboarding Zoom session with the parent and the adolescent. A research assistant explained study procedures and reimbursement structure and reviewed all the daily diary questions to ensure comprehension. If both parent and adolescent expressed interest in participating, they provided separate email addresses and chose a time at which they wanted to receive the daily-diary questionnaire (approximately an hour prior to regular bedtime). Then, participants were sent emails with links to the baseline assessment, including consent and assent forms, demographic questionnaires, the emotional BADE task, and questionnaires assessing depression and perceived partner responsiveness and insensitivity. After both dyad members completed the baseline session, they started receiving daily emails with links to the diary survey. Daily emails were sent for 28 consecutive days. Participants were instructed to complete daily surveys before going to bed each night, independently from their dyad member. The link expired after 14 h. In cases in which a participant missed a diary entry due to technical issues or another reason we deemed justified (e.g., illness), the diary period was extended by a day for both dyad members. Both the baseline session and the daily diary were completed on a secure website (Qualtrics). Participants each received $15 for the completion of a baseline session, and an additional $70 if they completed at least 90 % of the surveys, $50 if they completed 60–90 %, and $10 if they completed fewer than 60 %.

Sample size

Sample size was based on a power analysis for PAIRS, focusing on sufficient power for daily-diary based analyses. Briefly, we used the EMAtools (Kleiman, 2017) R package which suggested that a sample of 100 individuals who completed at least 75 % of the daily diary entries would allow us to detect small effects (d = 0.2) with a power of β = 0.8. Thus, we aimed to recruit 100 dyads. Because we continued, as pre-registered, to onboard new dyads until we reached the goal of 100 dyads in which both dyad members completed at least 60 % of the diary (i.e., for ~17 days after the onboarding of the 100th dyad), our final sample size was 112 dyads.

Transparency and openness

Study procedures, sample size justification, statistical analyses, and the first hypothesis were pre-registered. We deviated from the preregistration in two ways. First, we examined hypotheses that were not pre-registered. Second, we had originally planned to examine biased and inflexible interpretations as predictor across parents and adolescents. However, we reconsidered this decision and opted for a more appropriate model which included, in addition to interpretation biases and inflexibility, a variable that distinguishes between parents and adolescents (see Data Analytic Strategy below). Pre-registration and a PDF copy of the daily diary can be found at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/C7H98. Data can be obtained by contacting the first author.

Baseline measures - adolescents

Relational outcomes.

Relational outcomes were measured using the 8-item version (i.e., 4 items for each subscale) of the Perceived partner Responsiveness-Insensitivity scale (PRI; Crasta et al., 2021). Respondents rate to what degree they perceive their relationship partner as responsive (e.g., My partner really listened to me) and insensitive (e.g., My partner ignored my side of the story) to them on a six-point scale ranging from “Not at all” to “Completely”. The instructions asked that responders focus on a specific timeframe (vs. a specific interaction) when assessing responsiveness and insensitivity. At baseline, we opted to asked participants to focus on the past two weeks.

The PRI scales show high internal consistency, strong convergent validity with existing measures of perceived partner responsiveness, strong incremental validity over global satisfaction, ability to detect meaningful change over time, and sensitivity to partners’ behaviors in the relationship (Crasta et al., 2021). To adapt items to the parent-adolescent relationship, we changed the word “partner” to “parent/caregiver.” In the present study internal consistency was ωtotal = 0.88 for the responsiveness scale and ωtotal = 0.77 for the insensitivity scale.

Interpretation bias and inflexibility.

Interpretation bias and inflexibility were measured using the adolescent version of the emotional Bias Against Disconfirmatory Evidence (BADE) task (Gadassi-Polack, Bronstein et al., 2023). The adolescent BADE task includes scenarios that are social, emotional, and self-referential to capture depression and anxiety-related concerns regarding rejection and personal failure. The adolescent BADE task consists of scenarios that describe plausible social situations from daily lives of adolescents (e.g., joining classmates for lunch in the school cafeteria). Participants were instructed to imagine each scenario as if it were happening to them at that moment. Each scenario consisted of three statements that provided information about the unfolding scenario, which were presented progressively. Within each trial, for each progressive statement, participants were asked to rate the plausibility of four interpretations of the information acquired thus far in that scenario on a 13-point rating scale (1 = poor to 13 = excellent). The task requires participants to revise their interpretations for a given scenario by integrating the disconfirmatory information provided by each of the latter two statements. The task includes two types of scenarios: disconfirming the negative scenarios (requiring revisions of negative interpretations based on new positive information) and disconfirming the positive scenarios (requiring revision of positive interpretations based on new negative information). Fig. S2 illustrates an example sequence of the adolescent BADE task.

Version 1 of the adolescent BADE (Gadassi-Polack, Bronstein et al., 2023) comprised of 10 scenarios (4 disconfirming-the-negative and 6 disconfirming-the-positive). As reported by Gadassi Polack, Bronstein, et al. (2023), the version 1 of the adolescent BADE included an unbalanced number of scenario types because some of the original disconfirming-the-negative scenarios deviated from the expected task structure (e.g., the absurd statement was deemed as highly plausible throughout the task, the true statement remained less plausible compared to the lure statements in the third and final stage of the task, etc.). Therefore, to ensure a balanced number of scenarios, we developed three novel disconfirming-the-negative scenarios. Supplementary Figs. S3-S15 visualize the pattern of all version 2 scenarios; Figs. S3-S5 show the new scenarios). Two of the three novel scenarios adhered to the canonical pattern of BADE task scenario ratings (consistently low Absurd explanation ratings, escalating True explanation ratings, and falling Lure explanation ratings). Thus, we discarded one (“Smell”, Fig. S5 scenario, and the adolescent BADE – version 2 included 12 scenarios.

To further support the validity of the adolescent BADE – version 2, we conducted a factor analysis for each scenario type, and confirmed that task performance is adequately represented with three principal components – one representing interpretation inflexibility, one representing negative interpretation bias, and a third representing positive interpretation bias (Everaert et al., 2018, 2020; Gadassi-Polack, Bronstein et al., 2023). As can be seen in Table S2, the components we obtained were very similar to those derived from the adult task and to version 1 of the adolescent task. The first component for each scenario type featured positive loadings on the final Lure explanation ratings, and a negative loading for the final True explanation ratings, suggesting that the component captured Interpretation Inflexibility. The second component for each scenario type featured strong positive loadings on average Lure explanation ratings over the course of the scenarios, suggesting that it captured a bias toward endorsing the initially-plausible interpretations, which were either positive or negative depending on the scenario type. The final component for each scenario type featured negative loadings for True interpretations, which were also either positive or negative depending on the scenario type. Similar to findings regarding version 1 of the adolescent BADE, Absurd ratings did not load strongly onto the Interpretation Inflexibility component as they do for adults. Thus, our final scoring scheme was in line with the previous version of the adolescent BADE: Negative Interpretation Inflexibility was scored as: LureA3+LureB3-True3. Positive Interpretation Inflexibility had the same formula, but with the addition of True1 ratings. The two interpretation bias components were scored as: True1+True2+- True3 and LureA1+LureA2+LureB1+LureB2. In these formulas, the number (i.e., the “2” in “True2”) represents the statement in the scenario after which the rating was provided. The word (i.e., the “True” in “True2”) describes the type of explanation rating (e.g., ratings of True explanations) that were averaged across scenarios. In this study, split-half reliabilities were calculated using the Spearman-Brown corrected coefficient; reliability was good for disconfirming-the-negative scenarios (r = .84) and for disconfirming-the-positive scenarios (r = .83).

Baseline measures - parents

Relational outcomes.

Relational outcomes were measured using the PRI (Crasta et al., 2021). In the parents’ PRI, the word “partner” was changed to “child”. Internal consistency was ωtotal = 0.85 for the responsiveness scale and ωtotal = 0.82 for the insensitivity scale.

Interpretation bias and inflexibility

Interpretation bias and inflexibility were measured using the adult version of the emotional Bias Against Disconfirmatory Evidence (BADE) task (Everaert et al., 2018). The differences between the adolescent and adult versions of the emotional BADE Task are (1) scenario content (e.g., work-related scenarios for adults vs. school-related scenarios for adolescents), (2) adults’ plausibility rating scale ( 21-point scale for adults vs. 13-point scale used by adolescents), and number of scenarios (24 scenarios for adults vs. 12 for adolescents).

We calculated task scores for disconfirming-the-negative scenarios: negative interpretation inflexibility (Absurd1 + Absurd2 + Absurd3 + LureA3 + LureB3 – True3), positive interpretation bias (True1 + True2 + True3), and negative interpretation bias (LureA1 + LureB1 + LureA2 + LureB2). For disconfirming-the-positive scenarios: positive inflexibility (Absurd1 + Absurd2 + Absurd3 + LureA3 + LureB3+True1 – True3), negative interpretation bias (True1 + True2 + True3), and positive interpretation bias (LureA1 + LureB1 + LureA2 + LureB2). In these formulas, numbers in variable names represent the statement after the rating was made, and the remainder of the name denotes the category of explanation being rated.

In this study, split-half reliabilities were calculated using the Spearman-Brown corrected coefficient; reliability was good for disconfirming-the-negative scenarios (r = 0.92) and for disconfirming- the-positive scenarios (r = 0.93).

Daily-diary measures – parents and adolescents

Depressive symptoms.

Parent and adolescent daily depressive symptoms were measured using the Short Mood and Feelings Questionnaire (SMFQ; Angold et al., 1995). Respondents rate how true 13 statements that reflect various aspects of depression (e.g., I feel miserable or unhappy) applied to them using a three-point scale (“True”, “Some-what true”, or “Not true”). Scores range from 0 to 26, with higher scores reflecting more depressive symptoms. Studies assessing the psychometric properties of the child version of the SMFQ have found high internal consistency and criterion validity when used in adolescents and young adults (e.g., Angold et al., 1995; Eyre et al., 2021). Ratings are summed to get the final scores, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms.

To adapt the scale for daily diary use, instead of asking participants to rate the statements in regards the last two weeks, we asked them to rate how they felt at the moment of filling out the diary entry. We calculated the between- and within-participant reliabilities using procedures outlined by Shrout and Lane (2012). The between-subject reliability coefficient is the expected between-subject reliability estimate for a single typical day. The within-subject reliability coefficient is the expected within-subject reliability of change within individuals over the daily-diary entries. The between-person and within-person reliabilities in our sample were 0.94 and 0.82 for adolescents and 0.85 and 0.73 for parents. These reliabilities are considered very good for within-individual measures (Nezlek, 2017). In the present sample, average sum score across the diary period was M = 1.85 for parents and M = 4.24 for adolescents.

Relational outcomes.

Relational outcomes were measured using the 8-item version of the PRI (Crasta et al., 2021) as was done in the baseline session, only altering the timeframe from the last two weeks to between last night and right now. Adaptation of the PRI for daily diaries has been previously suggested by the questionnaire’s authors (Crasta et al., 2021) and used in research on adult relationships (e.g., Lemay et al., 2025). In the present study, the between-person and within-person reliabilities for the perceived partner responsiveness subscale in our sample were 0.88 and 0.81 for adolescents and 0.86 and 0.82 for parents. The between-person and within-person reliabilities for the perceived partner insensitivity subscale in our sample were 0.80 and 0.78 for adolescents and 0.79 and 0.82 for parents. These reliabilities are considered very good for within-individual measures (Nezlek, 2017). In the present sample, average sum scores across the diary period for responsiveness were M = 7.20 for parents and M = 7.89 for adolescents, and average sum scores for insensitivity were M = 2.06 for parents and M = 2.03 for adolescents.

Data Analytic Strategy

Data were analyzed using the nlme package (Pinheiro et al., 2014) of the statistical programming software RStudio. Our data have a multilevel structure (persons nested within dyads; Bolger & Laurenceau, 2013). Therefore, we used multilevel regression models. As our hypotheses were regarding between-individual effects only, scores from the diary were averaged across the whole diary period before being entered into analyses.

We ran all analyses with and without two covariates strongly associated with adolescent social-emotional: age and biological sex (Rapee et al., 2019). Results are presented in the supplementary materials.

We ran a series of such models in which interpretation inflexibility, positive bias and negative bias were associated with depressive symptoms and relational outcomes. In line with prior work (e.g., Everaert et al., 2018; Gadassi-Polack, Bronstein et al., 2023), interpretation bias and inflexibility from each scenario type were analyzed separately. Additionally, although not included in the pre-registration, we included relationship role (i.e., being the parent or the adolescent) as a moderator in all models, so that we could determine whether models varied between parents and adolescents. The model used to assess person-level results included a random intercept for dyads.1 Below as an example model predicting depressive symptoms from BADE indices based on disconfirming-the-negative scenarios:

Yij (Average depressive symptoms across the diary period for person j in dyad i) = β0 + β1*(Negative Interpretation Bias) + β2*(Positive Interpretation Bias) + β3*(Negative Interpretation Inflexibility) + β4*(Role 2)5*(Role * Negative Interpretation Bias) + β6*(Role * Positive Interpretation Bias) + β7*(Role * Negative Interpretation Inflexibility) + bi (random effect for dyad) + rij

Model assumptions were evaluated through examination of residual plots. Normality of residuals was assessed using Q-Q plots, homoscedasticity was evaluated through plots of residuals versus fitted values, and outliers were identified using standardized residuals (∣z∣ > 3.29). Across ten models, seven cases were identified as outliers but retained as they did not appear to be data entry errors. Visual inspection confirmed that all assumptions were adequately met for all models.

Discussion

The present study investigated the associations between biased and inflexible interpretations of social situations, depressive symptoms, and relational outcomes in parent-adolescent dyads. Through our innovative methodological approach—combining a task that distinctly measures bias and inflexibility with a 28-day daily diary—we examined whether parents’ and adolescents’ interpretation patterns were correlated, and how these patterns related to depressive symptoms and perceptions of partner responsiveness and insensitivity. By analyzing these questions separately for positive and negative scenarios, we uncovered a nuanced pattern of relationships. Most notably, our results demonstrate that biased and inflexible interpretations are not only related to individuals’ depressive symptoms but are even more consistently associated with how individuals perceive their relationship partner. Four major findings emerged from our analysis that advance our understanding of cognitive processes in both psychopathology and relationship dynamics.

Biased and inflexible interpretations predict depressive symptoms

Our first set of findings addresses the well-established but still evolving understanding of how interpretation processes – and particularly inflexibility – relate to depressive symptoms. As predicted, higher levels of negative (but not positive) interpretation bias longitudinally predicted depressive symptoms for adolescents and parents. This pattern of results is in line with previous research on interpretation biases, and it highlighted the more robust role of overly negative (vs. not enough positive) interpretation bias in emotional disorders (Chen et al., 2020; Gadassi Polack, Bronstein, et al., 2023; Oliver et al., 2019; Stuijfzand et al., 2018), and with theoretical models that emphasize the role of interpretation biases in depression (Beck & Bredemeier, 2016). Going beyond the majority of prior studies that focused on cross-sectional associations of interpretation bias, our study uniquely examines whether interpretation bias predicts depressive symptoms in everyday life (Gadassi Polack, Bronstein, et al., 2023).

Similarly, inflexible negative and positive interpretations predicted higher levels of depressive symptoms. These results replicate previous findings on inflexibility in adolescents (Gadassi Polack, Bronstein et al., 2023), further supporting the robustness of this association and the validity of the second version of the adolescent BADE. For parents, inflexible positive – in addition to negative – interpretations predicted depressive symptoms, similar to findings in adolescents. This finding diverges from prior work that shows that inflexible negative (vs. positive) are more consistent predictors of psychopathology (Everaert et al., 2018, 2020; Zabag et al., 2025). This difference from prior research may be related to the fact that our sample of adults had low levels of depressive symptoms compared to prior studies. Future studies are needed to replicate and explore this finding.

Positive and negative bias are differentially related to perceived responsiveness and insensitivity

Our results show that biases in interpreting fictional social situations – unrelated to the parent-adolescent relationship - were significantly associated with real-life relational outcome: the degree to which parents and adolescents perceived each other as responsive and insensitive both cross-sectionally and longitudinally. Specifically, positive bias was positively associated with perceived responsiveness, whereas negative bias was positively associated with perceived insensitivity. These results are in line with prior research on children and adults showing that biased cognitions are associated with perception of attachment figures (De Winter et al., 2017; Doolan et al., 2021). Going beyond prior investigations, we focused on two dimensions of relational perceptions – i. e., responsiveness and insensitivity – whereas most prior studies focused mostly on responsiveness (Crasta et al., 2021). This choice helped uncover unique roles to positive vs. negative biases in close relationships and lends further support for the finding that perceived responsiveness has a two-factorial (vs. unidimensional) structure – (Crasta et al., 2021).

Moreover, our findings have important implications to the study of positive biases in psychopathology. Most studies that examine the association between biases and psychopathology focus on negative interpretation bias because of its more robust associations with psychopathology symptoms compared to positive biases (Gadassi Polack, Bronstein, et al., 2023). Indeed, the current study also found stronger ties between negative (vs. positive) bias and depressive symptoms. However, our results suggest that although positive bias – or lack thereof – is not directly related to symptoms of psychopathology, it is strongly tied to relational factors that contribute to wellbeing, such as perceived responsiveness. Considering the robust role of perceived responsiveness in the formation and maintenance of close relationships (Cook et al., 2018; Crasta et al., 2021; Gordon & Chen, 2016; Reis, 2017), and the role of close relationships in ameliorating the adverse effects of psychopathology (Gadassi & Rafaeli, 2015; Spruit et al., 2020), our findings suggest that research endeavors, particularly those examining protective factors, should give more attention to positive interpretation bias.

Inflexibility in interpretation of scripted social situations is related to relational perceptions

As previously found in adults (Deng et al., 2023a; Zabag et al., 2024), more inflexible interpretations were associated – cross sectionally and longitudinally – with more perceived insensitivity and less perceived responsiveness for both adolescents and parents. These findings represent the first evidence that the detrimental effects of inflexibility extend beyond personal well-being to influence how individuals perceive their close relationships in everyday interactions. We extend previous findings from adults to adolescents, and to the parent-adolescent relationship. By combining a daily diary assessment with a task that examines interpretations of scripted social situations our study adds to the literature by being one of the first to examine the longitudinal correlates of inflexibility (cf., Zabag et al., 2024) and the first to do so using a highly ecological-valid method (Berenson, Nynaes, et al., 2018). Interestingly, unlike prior studies on adults, that found that depression is consistently linked to inflexibility in positively updating negative interpretations, but not vice-versa (e.g., Deng et al., 2023a; Zabag et al., 2024; Zabag et al., 2025), we found that inflexibility in updating positive – in addition to negative – social situations is important for relational well-being.

The connection between interpretation inflexibility and relationship perceptions merits deeper exploration, particularly given our finding that inflexibility in updating positive scenarios was more consistently detrimental than inflexibility in updating negative scenarios. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses, we offer tentative interpretations that require replication in future confirmatory studies. We speculate that interpretation flexibility may facilitate a more nuanced and integrated view of relationship partners. This flexibility could potentially have allowed individuals to accommodate both positive and negative aspects of others without rigid categorization, similar to the integrative complexity described in personality research (Berenson et al., 2018a, 2018b). Indeed, more integrative perceptions of significant others are found to be linked to better relational outcomes (Showers & Zeigler-Hill, 2004). One possibility is that inflexible interpretations may prevent individuals from revising initial impressions when faced with contradictory evidence, leading to relationship perceptions that are fragmented rather than holistic. This rigid cognitive style might partially explain why individuals with personality disorder characteristics struggle with dichotomous views of close others (Berenson, Johnson, et al., 2018) and could provide a cognitive mechanism underlying relationship difficulties in various forms of psychopathology. These proposed mechanisms should be directly tested in future studies.

Parent-adolescent similarity in the interpretation of scripted social situations

Partly supporting our hypotheses that parents’ and adolescents’ biases and inflexibility will be associated, we found a moderate positive correlation between parents and adolescents’ negative interpretation bias in initially negative situations. These results are in line with prior studies that examined similarity between parents’ and adolescents’ negative interpretation biases (Subar & Rozenman, 2021). This finding supports theoretical models suggesting that children learn from their parents how to interpret social situations, and that parents may contribute to the development of children’s negative biases (e.g., Aktar, 2022; Goodman & Gotlib, 1999; Perlman et al., 2022). Indeed, recent studies suggest that specific parental behaviors (e.g., verbal communication) serve as a mechanism in the transmission of interpretation bias (Aktar et al., 2022; Zeytinoglu et al., 2025). We extend prior research by having the parents and adolescents complete tasks that are conceptually and structurally similar, but with different content, as the adult and adolescent BADE included different, age-appropriate, scenarios. The fact that similarity in negative interpretations was found beyond the specific situations suggests that a process of generalization may occur. Future research directly examining generalization of negative bias tendency is needed.

However, the use of tasks with different content likely increased noise and contributed to the null findings regarding positive interpretation bias and interpretation inflexibility. Indeed, prior studies used the same task for parents and their children when assessing similarity (e.g., Sfärlea et al., 2019). Future studies could address this by developing tasks that are appropriate for examining interpretation flexibility across a larger age range, as well as by using larger samples to compensate for the measurement noise.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

The present study is the first to examine how biased and inflexible interpretations are related to depressive symptoms and perception of close others using a dyadic design and utilizing a combination of a structured task with a highly ecologically valid daily diary. Moreover, this is one of the first studies to examine interpretation flexibility in adolescence (cf., Gadassi-Polack, Bronstein et al., 2023), as prior investigations focused on adulthood. The current research has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, we rely on a homogenous community sample recruited online, thus limiting the generalizability of our results to diverse or clinical samples. Future studies should examine these effects in diverse and clinical samples. Second, because of the challenge of recruiting dyads, our sample size was relatively small compared to other studies using the BADE task and may have limited our ability to detect effects. Future studies with larger samples are needed to replicate and extend our findings. Third, as we did not use an experimental design, our ability to deduce causality is limited. Although the use of longitudinal design helps establish temporal precedence, future studies that experimentally manipulate biases and inflexibility are needed.

In addition, our definition of interpretation flexibility as the degree to which interpretation is updated to reflect accumulated information which suggests the initial interpretation was incorrect is one optional definition of flexibility. Indeed, interpretation flexibility can be considered as a specific aspect of cognitive flexibility. Cognitive flexibility is a multifaceted construct that encompasses various aspects and definitions (Ionescu, 2012; Stange et al., 2017). Additional related constructs, such as prediction error processing, expectation violation, or inhibitory learning, might lead to different updating patterns (e.g., Kube, 2023). Future research may aim to elaborate on the way interpretation flexibility is defined and computed in order to generalize the results of the current study. Moreover, the role of individual motives for interpretation updating—hence how useful or desirable individuals perceive their interpretations—was not examined in the current study but is an important topic for future research (Bromberg-Martin & Sharot, 2020; Sharot et al., 2023). Finally, our hypotheses regarding the association between biased and inflexible interpretations and perceived partner responsiveness and insensitivity were not pre-registered and are therefore considered exploratory. Future pre-registered studies replicating these effects are needed. Relatedly, because of absence of relevant hypotheses, we underutilized the daily-diary data and simply averaged across the whole diary period. Future studies should explore how biased and flexible interpretations predict or moderate temporal dynamics of psychopathology and relational outcomes (e.g., Gadassi-Polack, Bronstein et al., 2023; Hohensee et al., 2025).

Clinical implications

Our finding that biased and inflexible interpretations of positive and negative social situations are associated both with depressive symptoms and with perceived partner responsiveness and insensitivity should be recognized by clinicians. Although interventions for adolescents and adults already focus on modifying interpretation biases (Krebs et al., 2018; Platt et al., 2017), our findings suggest that a stronger emphasis needs to be on interpretation flexibility. In particular, it may be counterintuitive for clinicians to encourage flexibly changing positive – in addition to negative – interpretations. Still, our findings replicate and extend our prior work (Gadassi Polack, Bronstein et al., 2023) by showing that flexibly revising positive interpretations is as important – if not more so – than interpreting initially negative interpretations. While our findings are preliminary and require replication, they suggest that future therapeutic approaches might benefit from targeting interpretation flexibility as a skill, rather than focusing exclusively on the valence of interpretations. This could involve exercises where clients practice generating multiple interpretations (both positive and negative) for everyday events or hypothetical scenarios, then evaluate which interpretation best fits the available evidence. Our findings that bias and inflexibility are associated with relational factors also suggest that information processing should receive more attention in treatment focusing on relational well-being (De Winter et al., 2017; Doolan et al., 2021), or on disorders associated with relational disturbances, such as borderline and avoidant personality disorders (Berenson et al., 2018a, 2018b; Duda et al., 2024).

Summary

The present study represents a significant advancement in our understanding of how social-cognitive processes shape both mental health and relationship quality during adolescence—a developmental period marked by heightened risk for psychopathology and transformative changes in parent-child relationships. By employing a methodologically sophisticated approach combining structured tasks with ecologically valid daily assessments, we demonstrated that interpretation flexibility plays an important role in mental health and relational well-being for both adolescents and their parents.

Our findings extend existing theories of psychopathology by highlighting the importance of positive interpretation bias and flexibility, which has received considerably less attention than negative bias in prior research. Moreover, we establish a clear link between interpretation processes and relationship perceptions, bridging cognitive and interpersonal domains that have often been studied separately. This integration suggests that interpretation flexibility represents not only an intrapersonal capacity important for emotion regulation but also an interpersonal asset that facilitates relationship satisfaction and stability. Finally, our results emphasize that psychopathology and well-being are related but distinct constructs (Mason et al., 2025), which has important implications for future research.

Future research should build upon these findings by examining whether targeted interventions to enhance interpretation flexibility improve both psychological symptoms and relationship quality, particularly during key developmental transitions. By continuing to investigate these processes across different relationship types and developmental periods, we may develop more effective prevention and intervention strategies that address both mental health and interpersonal functioning—two deeply interconnected aspects of human well-being.
