Monetary evaluation of quality-adjusted life year in patients with malignant tumor in China: Willingness-to-pay analysis
Authors: Li Huang, Xiaochen Peng, Jingdong Zhang, Lihua Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The objective of our study was to investigate the willingness to pay (WTP) per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) among people with malignancies in China, and the associated factors that significantly affect WTP/QALY ratios.Methods:We conducted a cross-sectional survey using the contingent valuation method and employed the EuroQol-5 Dimensions (EQ-5D), European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Utility Measure-Core 10 Dimensions (QLU-C10D), and WTP questionnaires to extract information on demographic characteristics, health utility, and WTP for full recovery from the respondents. Furthermore, we conducted stepwise regression analyses to identify factors affecting the WTP/QALY ratios.Results:We received 1012 valid questionnaires, comprising 588 patients and 424 family members. The mean age of the patients was 57.05 (11.48) years. There were 483 male patients and 529 female patients; the numbers of urban and rural patients were 576 and 436, respectively. The mean scores of health utility (standard deviation) measured by EuroQol Five-Dimensional Five-Level Questionnaire and QLU-C10D were 0.75 (0.20) and 0.74 (0.16). For the overall sample, the mean and median WTP/QALY values based on the EQ-5D instrument were 336,236 renminbi (RMB) (4.67 times gross domestic product [GDP] per capita) and 177,814 RMB (2.47 times GDP per capita), and 344,233 RMB (4.78 times GDP per capita) and 152,386 RMB (2.12 times GDP per capita) for the QLU-C10D instrument. There was no significant difference between the WTP/QALY values based on different cancers. In stepwise regression models, the WTP/QALY value was significantly correlated with health utility, income level, employment status, regular physical examination, etc.Conclusion:This paper evaluated the monetary value of QALY among people with malignancies in China. Our analysis reveals an inverse relationship between national income levels and the ratio of WTP per QALY to GDP per capita, suggesting that lower-income countries may adopt relatively higher cost-utility thresholds in proportion to their GDP per capita. Additionally, the ratios of WTP/QALY to GDP per capita for patients with malignant tumors are similar to those of the general population with life-threatening conditions; patients with non-life-threatening diseases have comparable WTP/QALY to GDP per capita ratios with the general population in a quality-of-life improvement scenario, providing empirical reference for decision-making and resource allocation in the stratified disease management. Future studies with more representative samples across a broader range of countries are warranted to enable a more comprehensive and systematic discussion.

1. Introduction

According to the Global Cancer Observatory, China accounts for 23.7% of newly diagnosed cancer cases and 30.2% of cancer-related deaths worldwide.[1] In China, cancer incidence and mortality have increased by an average of 3.9% and 2.5% per year in the past 10 years, and malignant tumors have become the leading cause of death, triggering a major public health issue.[2] Moreover, the disease burden of malignant tumors is inevitably growing, as China is the most populous country in the world and has an aging society[3]; thus, the economic burden on patients is compounded by increasing demands for antitumor treatments and costly innovative drugs. Since 2017, the adjustment of China’s national reimbursement drug list has been undertaken year by year, and a series of antitumor drugs have been covered by the national medical insurance with varying price cuts, which has eased the economic burden on patients and improved the accessibility and affordability of antitumor drugs to some extent. However, a large number of antitumor drugs remain unaffordable as they have not yet entered the national reimbursement drug list.

Pharmacoeconomics, particularly cost-utility analysis, has been broadly applied as an economic evaluation tool to optimize healthcare resource allocation. Cost-utility analysis uses the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) as an evaluation index to reflect the incremental cost associated with 1 additional unit of clinical outcome; if the ICER is below the threshold, the intervention or treatment can be considered cost-effective. In recent years, there have been several studies on ICER thresholds from the “supply” and “demand” perspectives in China. He[4] and Ochalek et al[5] used the opportunity cost method to estimate the opportunity cost of health resources, yielding values of 38,100 to 45,500 renminbi (RMB) per disability-adjusted life year and 27,923 to 52,247 RMB per disability-adjusted life year, respectively; Ye et al[6] estimated the threshold of 113,120 RMB in a general population-based study. Zhao et al[7] and Gao et al[8] conducted studies on patients with chronic prostatitis and epilepsy, respectively, and found that the willingness to pay (WTP) for a quality-adjusted life year (QALY) was 49,681 to 50,374 RMB and 54,734 to 58,790 RMB. In addition, Cai et al[9] estimated the threshold as 1.45 times gross domestic product (GDP) per capita using the value of a statistical life method. However, there is still a lack of consensus on the threshold in China, and it is unclear whether the same threshold should be used for patients with malignant tumors and other common diseases.

Meanwhile, patients, as well as their families’ WTP for treatments, has a meaningful impact on the accessibility and affordability of innovative treatments; therefore, it is necessary to measure the WTP from the perspective of insured patients. Therefore, we conducted a population-based WTP/QALY analysis using a questionnaire whose respondents were patients with malignant tumors and their family members from 3 Chinese cities with high, medium, and low GDP levels, based on the GDP per capita of these cities in 2019.

2. Method

2.1. Study design

A cross-sectional survey was conducted in 3 tertiary hospitals in different cities (Ningbo, Zhejiang; Shenyang, Liaoning; and Huaihua, Hunan) from November 2020 to February 2022, and the study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of each hospital (Ningbo First Hospital, Zhejiang Province, Liaoning Cancer Hospital & Institute, and Huaihua First People’s Hospital, Hunan Province).

2.3. Questionnaire design

This study mainly involved the following 3 aspects: health utility measurement, WTP estimation, and subgroup analysis based on demographic characteristics.

2.3.1. Health utility measurements

The EuroQol Five-Dimensional Five-Level Questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L) and Quality of Life Utility Measure-Core 10 Dimensions (QLU-C10D) instruments were used to measure the health utility of patients with malignant tumors. The EQ-5D-5L is a general multi-attribute health status measurement tool that includes 5 dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activity, pain or discomfort, and anxiety or depression. Each dimension is described at 5 levels: “no problem, some problems, moderate problems, more serious problems, and extremely serious problems.” The health utility scores in our study were calculated using a Chinese population-based value set from published studies.[11] The QLU-C10D is a malignant tumor-specific utility measurement tool with dimensions that include physical functioning, role functioning, social functioning, emotional functioning, pain, fatigue, sleep, appetite, nausea, and bowel problems. Therefore, patient-reported health-related outcomes were collected by the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire Core 30 questionnaire; disease-specific utility scores were calculated based on published preference weights and value sets. Here, we adopted Australia’s population-based value sets due to the lack of availability of China’s population-based value sets.[12]

2.3.2. WTP measurement

A payment card combined with open-ended questions was used in the questionnaire, and its feasibility was verified through a pilot survey. The range of offered bids on the payment card was in accordance with multiples of China’s per capita GDP in 2019, namely 0 RMB, 30,000 RMB, 60,000 RMB, 90,000 RMB, 120,000 RMB, 150,000 RMB, and 180,000 RMB. Each respondent was told about the assumptions under a hypothetical treatment scenario on which the questions were based. A patient could be fully restored from their current health status by taking a novel drug, which is painless and nontoxic with no side effects. Long-term use is required, and life expectancy is said to reach 80 years, which is China’s average life expectancy. Because the drug needs to be constantly taken to maintain its efficacy, respondents were informed that the expenses would be paid annually, and then were asked about their WTP for this drug. If the respondent wanted to pay for the new drug, the range of bidding amounts was tested first by payment card, and then the maximum bidding amount was assessed through open-ended questions. If the respondent said that they would not pay for the new drug, the reason was further examined through questions.

2.3.3. Demographics

Demographic variables included sex, age, residence, marital status, education level, annual household income per capita, healthcare insurance, and other information. Additionally, we inquired about the health status of the patient and their family, such as whether the patient underwent regular physical examinations, whether they had chronic diseases, whether the patient had been hospitalized for other diseases in the past year, whether the patient had a family member who was critically ill or had passed away in the past year, and whether their family members suffered from similar or other diseases.

A P-value of 5% was considered statistically significant. IBM SPSS 23 (IBM Corporation, Armonk) and Stata 17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station) were used for the statistical analysis.

3. Results

3.1. Characteristics of patients

A total of 1264 questionnaires were returned in the survey conducted, 47 of which were nonresponse questionnaires; 9 respondents failed to give the maximum bidding amount; 160 respondents were not willing to take the new treatment altogether, making their bidding amount 0. The total number of respondents with a utility score of 1 was 35. Additionally, 4 and 31 respondents did not answer health-related questions on the EQ-5D-5L and QLU-C10D questionnaires, respectively. In summary, 1012 questionnaires were valid and included in the analysis; respondents included 588 patients and 424 family members. Because all patients were being treated and were paying for current treatments, and considering the definitions of the true 0 values and protest responses in publications,[13–15] the aforementioned 0 values of the bidding amount were categorized as protest responses.

Based on the 1012 valid questionnaires, the proportion of female patients in our survey was larger than that of males, with women accounting for 52.3% of the respondents (529 in total). This differed from the female-to-male proportion in the “2019 China Cancer Annual Report,” in which women only accounted for 44.4% of the patients.[16] In our survey, the proportion of rural to urban patients was 43.1% to 56.9%, which was close to the proportion of rural to urban populations in China (45.98% rural to 54.02% urban). The average age of the patients in the study was 57.05 years, which was slightly lower than that of Chinese patients with malignant tumors (63.47 years old).[17] The income level distribution of the patients approximated the distribution of Chinese household incomes in 2019, but the percentage of the “20,000 to 35,000 RMB” and “35,000 to 60,000 RMB” subgroups both exceeded 20% and were slightly larger than other income-level subgroups.

Regarding the tumor types involved in our study, the major types were colorectal cancer, lung cancer, breast cancer, gastric carcinoma, cervical cancer, and lymphoma, and the number of patients with liver cancer was relatively small (one of the top 5 cancers in China). The education level of the patients was primarily junior high school and below; two-thirds of the patients had this education level. The most common type of healthcare insurance was national basic medical insurance for urban and rural residents, accounting for 50.3% of the patients in the study. Furthermore, approximately 1.3% of the patients were covered by 2 types of insurance together, including 1 commercial medical insurance. Approximately 88.5% of patients were married. Full-time employees, housewives, farmers, and retirees comprised the majority of occupation statuses, accounting for 71.6% of the respondents. Moreover, it was worth noting that 113 respondents were unemployed due to malignant tumors, accounting for about 11.2% of the patients. More than one-third of the patients underwent regular physical examinations, about one-third of them had other chronic diseases, approximately 5.7% of the patients were hospitalized due to other diseases, and 5.6% of the patients had a family member who was critically ill or had passed away in the past year. We also asked about the health status of the patients’ families. We found that the proportion of patients whose family members had similar cancers was 1/4 and those whose family members had other diseases was 1/5. The mean and median utility measured by EQ-5D-5L were 0.75 and 0.79, respectively; the mean and median utility measured by QLU-C10D were 0.74 and 0.77, respectively. 3.2. WTP

Our study included 1012 respondents, consisting of 558 patients and 424 family members of patients. The mean and median WTP of the overall sample were 47,985 RMB and 30,000 RMB, 42,801 RMB and 30,000 RMB for patient subgroup and 55,174 RMB and 40,000 RMB for family subgroup. According to the Mann–Whitney test, there was a significant difference in WTP between the patient subgroup and family subgroup (Z = −6.110, P < .05).

3.4. Multiple regression analysis

WTP/QALY(EQ-5D) and WTP/QALY(QLU-C10D) were set as dependent variables in the stepwise regression model. WTP/QALY was positively correlated with health utility scores (EQ-5D-5L, QLU-C10D), annual household income per capita, and regular physical examination. The ratio of WTP to QALY was significantly lower in the patient subgroup than in the family subgroup. Furthermore, the WTP/QALY value for full-time patients was lower than that for patients in other occupations, including self-employed (WTP/QALY[EQ-5D], WTP/QALY[QLU-C10D]) and students (WTP/QALY[EQ-5D], WTP/QALY[QLU-C10D]) at a P-value of 5%. When WTP/QALY(EQ-5D) alone was set as the dependent variable in the regression model, the WTP/QALY value was negatively correlated with the “social functioning” dimension of the QLU-C10D scale. 4. Discussion

This study estimated the monetary value for a QALY in the hypothetical scenario of maintenance treatment for patients with malignant tumors in 3 cities with different GDP levels in China. As shown in the results section, the mean and median WTP/QALY(EQ-5D) values were 336,236 RMB (4.67 times GDP per capita) and 177,814 RMB (2.47 times GDP per capita) for the overall sample; 311,742 RMB (4.33 times GDP per capita) and 156,799 RMB (2.18 times GDP per capita) for the patient subgroup, and 370,204 RMB (5.14 times GDP per capita) and 197,628 RMB (2.74 times GDP per capita) for the family subgroup; and the mean and median WTP/QALY(QLU-C10D) values were 344,233 RMB (4.78 times GDP per capita) and 152,386 RMB (2.12 times GDP per capita) for the overall sample; 336,237 RMB (4.67 times GDP per capita) and 138,112 RMB (1.92 times GDP per capita) for the patient group, and 355,321 RMB (4.94 times GDP per capita) and 170,697 RMB (2.37 times GDP per capita) for the family subgroup, respectively. We used median values in the analysis where the WTP/QALY values had a skewed distribution.[19]

We compared the ratio of WTP/QALY to GDP per capita of similar studies in different countries based on the literature available. For life-threatening conditions, the ratio of the WTP/QALY value to Vietnam’s GDP per capita (11,301 USD, 4.4 times GDP per capita of Vietnam in 2017), according to the study in lung cancer patients in Vietnam,[20] was larger than the ratio of the WTP/QALY value to China’s GDP per capita in our study (EQ-5D: 2.47 times GDP per capita, QLU-C10D: 2.12 times GDP per capita). The ratios of WTP per QALY to GDP per capita measured in patients with malignant tumors in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (1–1.5 times the GDP per capita)[21] were smaller than the ratios in our study for the overall sample (EQ-5D: 2.47 times GDP per capita, QLU-C10D: 2.12 times GDP per capita). And for other non-life-threatening diseases, the ratios of WTP/QALY to GDP per capita among Chinese patients – 1.03 to 1.11 times the city’s GDP per capita in 2009 (GDP per capita of Beijing in 2009: 71,059 RMB, https://nj.tjj.beijing.gov.cn/nj/main/2022-tjnj/zk/indexch.htm, GDP per capita of Kunming in 2009: 29,355 RMB, https://tjj.km.gov.cn/2011tjnj/indexch.htm) for chronic prostatitis[7] and 0.74 to 0.75 times the city’s GDP per capita in 2012 (GDP per capita of Wuhan in 2012: 79,482 RMB, http://tjj.wuhan.gov.cn/tjfw/tjnj/202004/P020200426455966002438.pdf) for epilepsy[8] – were higher than those reported in more developed countries, such as 0.17 times the GDP per capita in 2015 among Greek outpatients,[22] 0.44 times the GDP per capita in 2011 among Spanish patients in a health center,[23,24] and 0.56 to 0.74 times the GDP per capita in 1995 among Swedish patients receiving hormone replacement therapy.[25,26] The comparisons showed that ratios of WTP per QALY to GDP per capita in more developed countries tend to be smaller than those in less developed countries (https://data.worldbank.org.cn/indicator/NY.GDP.PCAP.CD?locations=SA-CN-VN), suggesting that lower-income countries may rationally set higher cost-utility thresholds relative to GDP. Further study should be conducted to verify the correlation.

For studies of the Chinese population, the median WTP/QALY for the overall sample (EQ-5D: 2.47 times GDP per capita, QLU-C10D: 2.12 times GDP per capita) in our study was close to the WTP/QALY for life extension scenarios (177,761 RMB, 2.47 times GDP per capita of China in 2020) and higher than the WTP/QALY for quality-of-life (QOL) improvement scenarios (78,907 RMB, 1.10 times GDP per capita of China in 2020) of the general population in China, which was performed from October 2020 to January 2021.[6] And the ratios of the median WTP/QALY to GDP per capita were higher than the threshold (1.45 times the GDP per capita) estimated using the value of a statistical life method.[9] Similarly, the median and the ratios of median WTP/QALY to GDP per capita in our study were higher than the results in the chronic prostatitis patients (49,681–50,374 RMB, 1.03–1.11 times the city’s GDP per capita [GDP per capita of Beijing in 2009: 71,059 RMB, https://nj.tjj.beijing.gov.cn/nj/main/2022-tjnj/zk/indexch.htm, GDP per capita of Kunming in 2009: 29,355 RMB, https://tjj.km.gov.cn/2011tjnj/indexch.htm]),[7] and the epilepsy patients (54,734–58,790 RMB, 0.74–0.75 times the city’s GDP per capita [GDP per capita of Wuhan in 2012: 79,482 RMB, http://tjj.wuhan.gov.cn/tjfw/tjnj/202004/P020200426455966002438.pdf])[8] as reported by previous studies in China.

In general, the WTP for a QALY measured in China was <3 GDP per capita, and the WTP/QALY for people with malignant tumors/general population in life-threatening condition scenario was higher than that for non-life-threatening chronic conditions/general population in QOL improvement scenario. This is a similar conclusion to our previous study.[19] Furthermore, we compared existing research in China by considering the research population and hypothetical scenarios. The ratio for malignant tumors was close to that of life-threatening condition in the general population, as well as the ratio for non-life-threatening conditions (e.g., prostatitis and epilepsy) was close to that of QOL in the general population. This may support more refined decision-making.

According to the regression analysis, factors including health utility, annual household income per capita, patient or family member status, whether the patient underwent regular physical examinations, and occupation had a statistically significant impact on the WTP/QALY value. Higher scores of health utility implied better health status of the patients and higher WTP for the treatments, which was consistent with the findings from studies regarding WTP for lung cancer patients in Vietnam[20] and WTP for patients with cardiovascular disease in Iran.[27] Consistent with the findings of previous studies, WTP/QALY was positively correlated with household income. It showed that the WTP/QALY of the patient group was significantly lower than that of the family member group, suggesting that family members were willing to pay more compared with the patients themselves. Similar results were reported in the research conducted by Takelu[28] in Australia, South Korea, Japan, and the United Kingdom (in life-threatening conditions, family members of the patients gave the higher WTP values), 1 possible explanation for this difference was that the patient may be more conservative, and family members were willing to pay more due to the fact that people were more willing to sacrifice for family members. In addition, “regular physical examination” had a positive impact on WTP/QALY, which was similar to the findings of Li et al’s[29] study on Shanghai urban residents’ WTP premiums for critical illness insurance, suggesting that such patients care more about their own health and may be more inclined to spend more money on it. Furthermore, when compared with full-time employed patients, WTP/QALY had a higher value for self-employed and student patients, probably due to the limited impact of their illness on household income (which was consistent with our previous study[19]). Finally, WTP/QALY(EQ-5D) was negatively correlated with the “social function” of the QLU-C10D instrument, indicating that the WTP/QALY value decreased as the social function of patients was increasingly limited.

There are some limitations in our study. First, respondents who were measured as being in “perfect health” (e.g., utility score = 1) gave positive values of WTP in our survey, which means that the calculation will produce undefined results (given a zero value in the denominator in Eq. [2]). To avoid this situation, we excluded these samples by referring to the processing methods used in previous studies.[30] Second, though the sample in our study essentially represents the demographic characteristics of the Chinese malignant tumor population, there are still some differences. Therefore, the underrepresentation of certain cancers may introduce bias into the results. For instance, the proportion of liver cancer patients in our study was relatively low, which could be due to more patients seeking care in specialized hospitals or being unwilling to discuss their illness given the disease’s high malignancy; the proportion of female patients was slightly higher, while the mean age of the sample was slightly lower. This might be attributed to the study’s exclusion criteria, which prevented super-elderly patients from being included in our study – with some elderly prostate cancer patients also excluded. Additionally, Australia’s population-based value sets were adopted in calculating the health utility by the QLU-C10D instrument due to unavailability of China’s population-based value sets, and the potential culture bias in attribute weights (e.g., pain, anxiety, etc) may affect the interpretation of the regression analysis. Despite these limitations, our study aimed to explore WTP/QALY thresholds in patients with malignancy and provide a reference for future population-based cost-utility threshold studies and decision-making in China.

5. Conclusion

In summary, this study empirically estimated the WTP/QALY in the hypothetical scenario of maintenance treatment for people with malignancies in cities with different GDP levels. Our analysis reveals an inverse relationship between national income levels and the ratio of WTP per QALY to GDP per capita, suggesting that lower-income countries may adopt relatively higher cost-utility thresholds in proportion to their GDP per capita. Additionally, there appears to be a similarity between the ratios for patients with malignant tumors and the general population with life-threatening conditions, as well as between those with non-life-threatening diseases and the general population with improvement in QOL, providing insights for informing decision-making and resource allocation in stratified disease management. Acknowledging the limitations of this study, future research should encompass a larger range of diseases and improve the representativeness of the sample. More relevant findings from future studies conducted in different countries will contribute to systematic discussions.
