Ageing of the Oral Mucosa: Mechanisms and Consequences
Authors: Chongsheng Qiu, Weiping Jie, Yinjie Qian, Xiao Lu, Yaqian Chen, Misi Si
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral mucosal ageing represents a fundamental reprogramming of the tissue microenvironment, a dynamic process that underlies the functional decline and heightened disease susceptibility observed in the elderly. This review synthesises current evidence to reconceptualise oral mucosal ageing as an active reprogramming of the tissue microenvironment, delineating the interplay between structural, molecular, and immunological changes, and exploring how these alterations drive functional decline and increase susceptibility to age-related oral diseases. Through a comprehensive analysis of experimental and clinical studies from human and animal models, we demonstrate that the ageing process fundamentally transforms the oral mucosa. Key findings include structural changes such as epithelial atrophy, extracellular matrix remodelling, and salivary gland degeneration, driven molecularly by genomic instability, accumulation of proinflammatory senescent cells, stem cell exhaustion, and dysregulated stress responses. These are compounded by an immunological state of ‘inflammaging’ and functional decline in innate and adaptive immunity, further exacerbated by shifts in the oral microbiome. Collectively, these deficits lead to impaired regeneration, diminished sensory function, and reduced salivary secretion, creating a permissive landscape for chronic oral diseases. In conclusion, oral mucosal ageing is a dynamic process of microenvironmental reprogramming driven by cellular senescence, immunosenescence, and structural decay. This actively underpins the heightened vulnerability to oral disease in the elderly, providing a mechanistic foundation for developing targeted interventions to preserve oral health in ageing populations.Keywords: Aging, Oral mucosa, Histology, Cytology, Oral diseases

Introduction

The global population is undergoing a marked trend of ageing. According to the United Nations' World Population Prospects 2022 report, the number of people aged 65 and above worldwide reached 771 million in 2022, accounting for 10% of the total population, and is projected to rise to 1.6 billion by 2050. This demographic shift poses emerging challenges for health care systems, including a growing prevalence of age-related oral health issues, particularly an increased incidence of oral mucosal diseases.

The oral mucosa, comprising the masticatory, lining, and specialised regions, is a fundamental structure within the oral cavity. It plays a critical role not only as a physical barrier protecting the orofacial region but also in facilitating sensation, immune defence, and the mechanical processes of chewing and swallowing.

As individuals age, the oral mucosa undergoes both visible structural changes and subtle molecular disruptions. Clinically, ageing is associated with symptoms such as dry mouth, weakened immune responses, and diminished taste and smell, all of which increase the risk of opportunistic infections, delay wound healing, and contribute to the development of conditions like periodontitis and precancerous lesions.1

Therefore, a deep understanding of the biological mechanisms underpinning oral mucosal ageing is crucial for guiding the maintenance of oral health in the elderly and for developing early intervention strategies against age-related oral diseases. Despite the increasing number of older adults worldwide, the underlying mechanisms of oral mucosal ageing remain poorly understood. This review aims to synthesise current knowledge on the biological processes driving oral mucosal senescence and to explore potential strategies for preserving oral health in ageing populations.

Structural changes in ageing oral mucosa

Epithelial layer

Ageing of the oral mucosa is predominantly characterised by epithelial thinning and atrophy, accompanied by alterations in epithelial cell morphology (Figure 1). The epithelial rete ridges become shorter and flatter, leading to a reduced interface between the epithelium and the underlying connective tissue. However, this change is variable between anatomic sites. For instance, in contrast to other oral sites, the thickness of the palatal masticatory mucosa tends to increase with age.2

Histological changes in oral mucosa ageing. A, Young oral mucosa. In young tissue, the epithelial rete ridges are prominent, the cells are arranged in an orderly manner, the blood vessels are elastic, and the connective tissue fibres are abundant. B, Aged oral mucosa. In aged tissue, the epithelial rete ridges become shorter, the total amount of connective tissue in the lamina propria decreases, fibroblasts shrink, collagen fibres degrade, hyaline degeneration occurs, and elastic fibres increase. The minor salivary glands in the mucosa undergo atrophy, the number of fat cells increases, and vascular elasticity decreases.

These architectural changes are driven by age-related alterations in keratinocytes, the principal epithelial cells. Morphologically, ageing keratinocytes demonstrate reduced numbers and layers, with abnormal keratinisation such as hyperorthokeratosis and parakeratosis. The intercellular spaces within the prickle cell layer become enlarged, and cell-to-cell adhesion weakens, leading to acantholysis and further undermining barrier function.3 Edematous changes are also frequently observed.4

Site-specific modifications are evident. Besides the above changes, the gingiva is more fragile because of the loss of stippling, and the tissue often assumes a paler or greyish appearance with advancing age.5 The dorsal tongue epithelium presents much more pronounced hyperkeratosis than other sites.6

Lamina propria

Concurrent with epithelial changes, the lamina propria exhibits a decrease in connective tissue volume. This is characterised by notable fibroblast shrinkage, nuclear elongation, cytoplasmic reduction, collagen fibre fragmentation, hyaline degeneration, and an accumulation of elastic fibres.

The ageing of fibroblasts, the workhorses of the connective tissue, is central to these alterations. Morphologically, senescent gingival fibroblasts exhibit substantial cellular enlargement and flattening. This is accompanied by cytoskeletal disorganisation, specifically the mislocalisation of vinculin from focal adhesions to a perinuclear region and reduced colocalisation with actin, resulting in impaired contractile function.7

With ageing, the microvessel density in the gingival lamina propria decreases.8,9 Furthermore, the lips, cheeks, and tongue frequently develop vascular lesions over time, and a progressive decline in mucosal extensibility and moisture content is observed, particularly beyond the fourth decade of life.10 Some studies of oral mucosa have also documented reduced capillary density and altered microvascular morphology, including signs of basal membrane disruption, which may contribute to this increased vascular lesion burden.11

Submucosa and associated structures

The impact of ageing extends to the submucosa, where changes in the minor salivary glands are particularly critical. Histologically, the average volume of salivary gland acini decreases with age. Normal salivary parenchyma is progressively replaced by adipose tissue, and fibrous connective tissue gradually increases.12 These degenerative changes in glandular structure are a primary structural basis for the quantitative and qualitative decline in saliva secretion observed in many elderly individuals. This age-related salivary hypofunction is a key contributor to xerostomia, which leads to difficulties in speaking, chewing, and swallowing, thereby reducing the overall quality of life in the ageing population.

Molecular mechanisms of oral mucosal ageing

Genomic instability and epigenetic alterations

Ageing imposes significant stress on the genetic and epigenetic integrity of oral mucosal cells (Figure 2). In oral keratinocytes, replicative senescence is linked to decreased telomerase activity and reduced expression of human telomerase reverse transcriptase.13 Furthermore, endogenous stressors such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) promote keratinocyte senescence through epigenetic modifications, notably the demethylation of the p16^INK4a^ promoter, which leads to cell cycle arrest.14 Transcriptomic analyses corroborate these shifts, revealing widespread changes in gene expression in ageing normal human oral keratinocytes (NHOKs), including the upregulation of genes involved in cell cycle regulation, immune responses, and inflammation, such as IL-1β, S100A8, S100A9, and various matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs).15 To better understand such age-related molecular dysregulation, recent developmental studies using spatial transcriptomics and single-nucleus transcriptomics have established baseline expression profiles of key epithelial and mesenchymal markers (eg, KRT6A, KRT13, and COL3A1) during embryogenesis, thereby providing a developmental reference for the systematic comparison of age-associated alterations.16 The identification of 16 commonly upregulated genes, such as Cathepsin B and p21^WAF1/CIP1^, reveals epithelial-specific ageing signatures that are highly expressed in ageing NHOKs but not in fibroblasts.17

Hallmarks and drivers of oral mucosal ageing. With advancing age, oral mucosal cells experience an accumulation of genomic damage and epigenetic alterations, which disrupt normal gene expression patterns. A key consequence is the induction of cellular senescence, accompanied by a proinflammatory SASP, which actively shapes a dysfunctional ageing microenvironment. Furthermore, the regenerative capacity of the mucosa is diminished due to the exhaustion of stem and progenitor cell pools. These changes are compounded by a pervasive dysregulation of metabolic pathways and a decline in cellular stress responses, collectively leading to a critical loss of tissue homeostasis in the aged oral mucosa.

Cellular senescence and chronic inflammation

The accumulation of senescent cells is a hallmark of ageing tissues, and the oral mucosa is no exception. Senescent human oral keratinocytes extensively secrete inflammatory cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases, a phenomenon known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). The development of the SASP in keratinocytes is mediated by the activation of multiple inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB and interferon signalling, which drive the secretion of factors like IL-6, IL-8, and MMP-1.18 This is not limited to epithelial cells, senescent gingival fibroblasts also exhibit elevated expression of cell cycle inhibitors (p16, p21, p53) and enhanced secretion of SASP factors, including IL-6, IL-8, and TGF-β.19 It is noteworthy that senescent gingival fibroblasts may not exhibit a classical proinflammatory SASP but a distinct one characterised by elevated TNF-α and reduced IL-8, potentially due to an impaired NF-κB signalling pathway.7 Differentiation processes influence keratinocyte biology during ageing. However, the pathways leading to differentiation can be distinct. Calcium-induced differentiation upregulates keratinocyte markers without inducing senescence, revealing that differentiation and replicative senescence are governed by divergent molecular pathways.20 Additionally, overexpression of Bmi-1 has been shown to delay the onset of senescence in NHOKs by inhibiting the TGF-β/Smad signalling axis and downregulating cell cycle inhibitors such as p15^INK4b^ and p57^KIP2^, suggesting potential anti-ageing mechanisms.21

Cellular stress and loss of homeostasis

Ageing is associated with a profound dysregulation of metabolic and stress-response pathways, leading to a loss of cellular homeostasis. The mTOR pathway drives fibroblast senescence by dysregulating cellular metabolism. Rapamycin, an mTOR inhibitor, counteracts this by restoring catabolic-anabolic balance and attenuating pro-senescent outputs, thereby delaying senescence and suppressing ageing markers.22 Concurrently, heightened oxidative stress is a common feature. The senescence of fibroblasts is frequently associated with an increased inflammatory cytokine profile and oxidative stress, which can be mitigated by natural antioxidants such as quercetin and resveratrol through the downregulation of NF-κB activity.23 External environmental factors, such as chronic exposure to e-cigarettes, can exacerbate this state by increasing oxidative stress and promoting inflammatory responses, thereby accelerating cellular senescence.24 The regulation of autophagy, a critical cellular clearance mechanism, is also impaired. Modulation of autophagic pathways has been shown to reverse oxidative stress-induced senescence, suggesting its pivotal role in maintaining mucosal cellular homeostasis during ageing.25 Furthermore, agents like erythritol may reverse ageing phenotypes in fibroblasts by suppressing p16/p21 and modulating metabolism, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting these pathways.26 Other interventions, such as capsaicin and all-trans retinoic acid, have also demonstrated potential in modulating age-related immune decline and extending keratinocyte lifespan.27,28

Immunological shifts in the ageing oral mucosa

Systemic inflammaging and oral mucosal inflammation

Systemic chronic inflammation is a fundamental hallmark of the ageing process. This state, termed ‘inflammaging’, arises from a poorly regulated immune response and contributes to the pathogenesis of numerous age-related diseases.29 This systemic phenomenon is reflected within the oral mucosa, where a proinflammatory microenvironment is established and sustained. A primary driver of this local inflammation is the SASP produced by senescent cells, as detailed in section Cellular Senescence and Chronic Inflammation. The accumulation of these cells exacerbates tissue deterioration by amplifying inflammation and impairing regenerative capacity.30 Consistent with this, gingival tissues in the elderly show elevated expression of proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β and TNF-α, and genes associated with M1 macrophage polarisation.31,32

Neutrophils: While neutrophil counts remain stable, their antimicrobial functions are notably impaired. Aged neutrophils exhibit reduced chemotactic and migratory ability, particularly within the gingival sulcus. A critical defect lies in the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps, which severely compromises bacterial clearance and heightens susceptibility to infection.33,34

Macrophages: Age-related alterations similarly impair macrophage phagocytic and immunoregulatory functions. Studies suggest that this functional decline, rather than a change in cell number, plays a key role in the development of oral inflammatory conditions in the elderly.33 This creates a complex scenario where in vivo tissues show inflammatory activation, while in vitro assays often reveal diminished phagocytic capacity, indicating context-dependent dysfunction.35

Langerhans cells and dendritic cells: These sentinel antigen-presenting cells are particularly vulnerable to ageing. A significant reduction in the number and dendritic complexity of Langerhans cells points to a failure in frontline immune surveillance.36 The function of dendritic cells (DCs) is also compromised, with an observed increase in immature DCs in the lesions of elderly patients, which likely undermines effective antigen presentation.4 Pathogens can exploit this vulnerability; for instance, Porphyromonas gingivalis can infect DCs and induce cellular senescence, thereby propagating local immune dysfunction via the SASP.37

Adaptive immunosenescence

The adaptive immune system undergoes profound changes with age, collectively known as immunosenescence, which disrupts targeted immune responses.

T cells: T-cell populations are significantly dysregulated. The function of specialised subsets like IL-17A^+Foxp3^+ regulatory T cells (Treg17) is disrupted via the IL-1β/MyD88/mTOR signalling axis, leading to exaggerated inflammatory responses during infection.38 Furthermore, T cells in aged individuals exhibit hallmarks of senescence, including elevated expression of inhibitors like PD-1 and CTLA-4, reduced proliferative capacity, and increased levels of senescence biomarkers (p16, p21).39,40 This creates an immunosuppressive milieu and weakens antitumour and antipathogen surveillance.

B cells: The humoral immune arm also shows signs of dysregulation with age. Transcriptomic analyses reveal an upregulation of genes linked to B-cell activation, proliferation, and differentiation in the elderly, particularly in those with inflammatory conditions, suggesting a state of dysregulated B-cell activity.41

Microbiome shifts in the ageing oral mucosa

The composition of the oral microbiome evolves with age, and these shifts interact bidirectionally with the host's immune system to shape the ageing mucosal environment. Culture-based studies have documented a significantly higher prevalence of subgingival Enterobacteria and Pseudomonas in elderly individuals compared to their younger counterparts, indicating a broader change in microbial community structure.42 This dysbiosis often involves an increased abundance of Gram-negative anaerobic bacteria, which can modulate host inflammatory gene expression and metabolic pathways, thereby actively reshaping the local immune microenvironment.43 P gingivalis can directly accelerate immune cell senescence, creating a feedback loop that perpetuates inflammation and tissue dysfunction.37

Functional alterations in the ageing oral mucosa

Impaired regenerative capacity

With advancing age, the oral mucosa exhibits a marked decline in regenerative capacity, clinically manifested as delayed wound healing and a prolonged epithelial turnover cycle under homeostatic conditions (Figure 3).3 This functional deterioration reflects underlying cellular and molecular deficits, chief among them being the functional impairment of differentiated cells and a reduction in stem and progenitor cell populations in specific niches.44

Age-related functional impairments of the oral mucosa. The regenerative capacity of the oral mucosa is significantly impaired with age, leading to clinically observable outcomes such as delayed wound healing and slowed cellular turnover. Concurrently, sensory functions undergo a marked decline, including a multifaceted deterioration of taste perception and a reduction in tactile sensitivity within the oral cavity. Furthermore, salivary function is frequently compromised in the elderly, primarily due to salivary gland degeneration and the common issue of polypharmacy. These collective functional deficits profoundly impact oral homeostasis, nutritional status, and overall quality of life in the ageing population.

At the molecular level, a key driver of this regenerative impairment is the exhaustion of stem and progenitor cells. This phenomenon is strikingly evident in the ageing taste bud, where animal models demonstrate a coordinated loss of basal, progenitor, and mature taste receptor cells. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses have further elucidated this process, revealing a specific decline in the progenitor pool and disruption of intercellular communication networks, thereby providing direct molecular evidence for stem cell exhaustion in a defined oral mucosal tissue.45 The functional capacity of these cellular constituents is critically dependent on a properly structured microenvironment. Ageing disrupts the synthesis and organisation of essential extracellular matrix (ECM) components, including collagen and fibronectin, leading to a loss of mucosal integrity and an impaired remodelling capacity.46

Ultimately, the failure of mucosal regeneration in ageing is an integrative process. It arises from the synergistic dysfunction of aged fibroblasts with diminished contractile and migratory ability, the accumulation of senescent keratinocytes, and a dysregulated immune response. This confluence of factors disrupts the highly coordinated cellular dialogue imperative for effective tissue repair.44

Decline in sensory function

Taste impairment

Taste perception undergoes a significant and clinically relevant decline with ageing. Epidemiological data from the U.S. National Health Interview Survey show a dramatic age-related increase in taste disturbances, from fewer than 0.1% of individuals aged 18-24 to 1.7% of those aged 85 and above.47 This subjective experience is corroborated by objective human psychophysical studies, which demonstrate elevated detection thresholds for salty, sour, bitter, sweet, and umami tastes in older adults, indicating a broad decline in gustatory function.48 This functional deficit is underpinned by progressive structural degeneration of the taste apparatus, including a decline in fungiform papillae density, taste bud number, and vascular supply.49, 50, 51 As noted in section Cellular Stress and Loss of Homeostasis, the decline in taste bud progenitor cells and impaired intercellular communication are key molecular mechanisms underlying this degenerative function.45 At present, therapeutic strategies remain limited, though nutritional interventions such as vitamin D supplementation show promise for mitigating sensory decline.52

Tactile sensitivity loss

In parallel, ageing also diminishes tactile sensitivity within the oral mucosa. Electrophysiological studies have shown that elderly individuals require higher stimulation thresholds, and tactile sensitivity scores are significantly lower compared to those of younger individuals.53,54 This sensory decline exhibits regional variation, with the tip of the tongue and the soft palate being most affected.55

Compromised salivary function

Salivary function is frequently impaired in the elderly, leading to the common complaint of dry mouth, which causes difficulties in speaking, chewing, and swallowing.56 The structural basis for this, as noted in section Submucosa and Associated Structures, is the age-related degeneration of salivary glands, characterised by acinar atrophy, replacement of parenchyma by adipose cells, and increased fibrosis.12 However, it is critical to note that significant salivary hypofunction in the elderly is not solely an intrinsic ageing process. While unstimulated and stimulated salivary secretion can decrease with age, longitudinal studies of healthy, non-medicated individuals show that salivary flow rate does not necessarily decline significantly solely due to ageing.57 This highlights that the high prevalence of xerostomia in the elderly is mainly attributable to polypharmacy (eg, anticholinergics, antihypertensives) and systemic age-related diseases, rather than to ageing as a single factor.58

Clinical consequences of oral mucosal ageing

Periodontitis

The incidence and severity of periodontitis increase markedly with advancing age. Data from the Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey in China confirm this trend, showing a rise in gingival bleeding from 58.4% at age 12 to 82.6% among those aged 65-74, with a high prevalence of deep periodontal pockets and attachment loss in the elderly population.59 These findings illustrate the age-related susceptibility of the periodontium, highlighting how ageing of the oral microenvironment can exacerbate chronic inflammatory conditions such as periodontitis.

The structural thinning of the epithelium, the fragmentation of connective tissue, and the reduced microvascular density detailed in section Structural Changes in Ageing Oral Mucosa collectively compromise the first line of defence against the subgingival biofilm, and periodontal pathogen-derived outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) may further aggravate this vulnerability by mediating bacteria-host interactions and promoting local inflammatory responses.60 The ageing process fundamentally alters the cellular and molecular response to this bacterial challenge. The accumulation of senescent fibroblasts and other senescent cells within the periodontal tissue is now recognised as a pivotal driver of disease progression. These cells are not merely dormant; through their potent SASP, they secrete a cascade of proinflammatory cytokines, MMPs, and growth factors.19 This SASP creates a local environment of chronic, nonresolving inflammation—‘inflammaging’—that disrupts extracellular matrix homeostasis, impairs the function of surrounding cells, and heightens the tissue's sensitivity to bacterial toxins.61,62 Inflammatory mediators and stress-related factors, such as RANTES/CCR5 and ROS, are also upregulated in aged periodontal tissues, accelerating alveolar bone destruction.63,64 This conceptual framework redefines periodontitis in the elderly as a maladaptive host response rather than a purely infection-driven process, driven by the accumulation of senescent cells. The crosstalk between the SASP and the subgingival microbiome creates a vicious cycle, aggravating tissue destruction and alveolar bone loss. Promisingly, antiageing interventions such as rapamycin, SIRT6 activators, and bromelain have shown potential in targeting senescent cells or the SASP to modulate the oral microenvironment.65, 66, 67

Oral squamous cell carcinoma

The strong association between ageing and OSCC risk underscores the role of the aged mucosal microenvironment as a tumour-promoting ‘soil’. According to the Global Cancer Observatory 2020 data, there were approximately 377,713 new cases of OSCC worldwide, with Asia accounting for the largest share.68 The National Cancer Institute found that nearly half of oral cancer cases occur in individuals over the age of 65, highlighting the significant contribution of oral mucosal ageing. While cellular senescence can act as an initial tumour-suppressive mechanism, the chronic presence of senescent cells in aged tissue ultimately facilitates carcinogenesis through multiple mechanisms.

In the established pre-malignant or tumour microenvironment, the SASP from senescent stromal cells, particularly cancer-associated fibroblasts, becomes a powerful engine for tumour promotion. The SASP factors enhance cancer cell proliferation, induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition, stimulate angiogenesis, and remodel the extracellular matrix to promote invasion and metastasis.69 Concurrently, age-related immunosenescence (Section Immunological Shifts in the Ageing Oral Mucosa) cripples the body's immune surveillance. The decline in antigen presentation by Langerhans cells and dendritic cells, coupled with the accumulation of dysfunctional, senescent T-cells that exhibit exhausted markers, creates an immunosuppressive milieu that allows emerging tumour cells to evade detection and destruction.40 Thus, the aged oral mucosa presents a ‘perfect storm’ for carcinogenesis: genomically unstable epithelial cells are bathed in a SASP-rich, growth-factor-laden fluid from senescent stroma, while the very immune system tasked to eliminate them is aged and inefficient. This collectively drives the initiation and progression of OSCC in the elderly. Therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways, such as Piperlongumine, Xanol, and senolytics (Sabutoclax, ABT-737), are showing promise in preclinical models.70, 71, 72

Xerostomia

With advancing age, unstimulated salivary secretion declines markedly. Xerostomia in elderly patients is initially characterised by quantitative and qualitative alterations in saliva, accompanied by abnormal sensations of the oral mucosa.73 Studies have demonstrated that the prevalence of xerostomia among elderly individuals without cancer is approximately 38%, and this rate is further elevated in those with systemic diseases.74

As elaborated in section Structural Changes in Ageing Oral Mucosa, ageing is associated with a reduction in the average volume of salivary gland acini, replacement of normal salivary gland parenchymal cells by adipocytes, progressive accumulation of fibrotic tissue, and alterations in the proportional distribution of ductal volume.12 The SASP-mediated chronic low-grade inflammatory microenvironment can suppress the proliferation and differentiation potential of salivary gland stem/progenitor cells through upregulation of inflammatory cytokines.75 This impairment of salivary gland tissue regenerative capacity ultimately leads to compromised salivary secretory function. Furthermore, the superimposition of pathological xerostomia (eg, Sjögren’s syndrome) with physiological ageing effects exacerbates these symptoms.76 In addition to directly impairing the quality of life of the elderly by causing masticatory, swallowing, and speech dysfunctions, xerostomia disrupts the homeostasis of the oral microecology.77 This disruption results in increased mucosal fragility, elevated risk of candidal infection, and heightened incidence of dental caries. Notably, intervention strategies targeting salivary gland ageing—including pilocarpine administration to stimulate salivary secretion, supplementation with salivary substitutes, and anti-inflammatory interventions targeting the SASP—have demonstrated efficacy in mitigating symptoms in clinical settings.78

Halitosis

Halitosis is a prevalent yet underappreciated condition in the elderly, exhibiting a substantially high prevalence rate.79 The high incidence of halitosis in the elderly challenges the misconception that oral mucosal ageing is solely linked to severe pathological conditions, highlighting the extensive impact of ageing on oral function and quality of life.

Oral mucosal ageing drives halitosis through multidimensional regulatory mechanisms. Firstly, the age-related decline in salivary secretion impairs the oral self-cleansing mechanism. This leads to the accumulation of food debris in interdental spaces and on the tongue's dorsum, which in turn provides a nutritional foundation for microbial growth.80 Concurrently, diminished levels of antibacterial constituents (eg, lysozyme, lactoferrin) in saliva further weaken the suppression of pathogenic microorganisms. Secondly, age-related dysbiosis of the oral microbiota is a key driver of halitosis, characterised by the overgrowth and colonisation of periodontal pathogens (eg, P gingivalis) and tongue coating-associated anaerobic bacteria. These bacteria metabolise sulphur-containing amino acids to produce high levels of volatile sulphur compounds (VSCs), which directly induce and exacerbate halitosis.81 Furthermore, as discussed in Sections Structural Changes in Ageing Oral Mucosa and Immunological Shifts in the Ageing Oral Mucosa, immune senescence impairs immune surveillance function, failing to efficiently eradicate abnormally proliferating oral pathogens and thereby exacerbating oral microecological imbalance. Diminished epithelial renewal capacity and compromised barrier function enhance mucosal permeability, facilitating the diffusion of bacterial metabolites and further intensifying odour perception. Halitosis and periodontitis exert a reciprocal promotional effect. Deep periodontal pockets induced by periodontitis offer a cryptic niche for anaerobic bacteria, while VSCs produced by these bacteria aggravate periodontal inflammation, forming a vicious cycle.82 Clinically, halitosis in the elderly can be effectively alleviated through improved oral hygiene practices (eg, tongue dorsum cleaning, interdental brush use), oral microbiota modulation (eg, probiotic formulations), and targeted management of underlying oral conditions.83

Other oral mucosal diseases

In addition to the aforementioned diseases, the elderly population is also susceptible to a variety of mucosal lesions associated with oral mucosal ageing, including recurrent aphthous ulcer (RAU), burning mouth syndrome (BMS), and denture stomatitis (DS). Despite their distinct aetiologies, these diseases are all linked to age-related alterations in the oral mucosa to a certain extent.

Contrary to common perceptions, the incidence rate of RAU in the elderly is slightly lower than that in young and middle-aged adults.84 One hypothesis attributes this phenomenon to immunosenescence, which impairs the function of innate immune cells in the elderly and thus prevents the formation of the local high-intensity inflammatory infiltration required for RAU onset. Meanwhile, the core pathology of RAU lies in T cell-mediated autoimmune damage to the oral mucosa, and immunosenescence suppresses this pathological process by remodelling T cell subsets and their functions.85 Beyond RAU, BMS is particularly prevalent among elderly females, and is mainly characterised by burning pain in the oral mucosa without obvious organic lesions. In addition to endocrine changes, neurological disorders, and psychological factors, age-related neurodegeneration of oral mucosal nerve fibres, alterations in salivary secretion and composition, and oral dysbiosis also contribute to the development of BMS.86 DS is another common mucosal disease in the elderly. The core inducing factors include age-related impairment of the barrier function of the oral mucosa, coupled with excessive proliferation of Candida caused by improper denture cleaning, which triggers mucosal erythema, oedema, and burning pain, and can affect denture wearing in severe cases.87

Interventions for these age-related oral mucosal diseases should take both aetiological factors and ageing mechanisms into account. In addition to conventional local anti-inflammatory and analgesic treatments, nutritional interventions such as supplementation with B vitamins and zinc can enhance mucosal repair capacity and alleviate RAU symptoms.88 For patients with BMS, combination therapy involving saliva replacement therapy and psychological counselling is required.89 For DS, the primary interventions are strengthening denture cleaning and administering local antifungal therapy, while attention should also be paid to the mucosal tolerance of elderly patients to avoid treatment-related damage.90 All these intervention strategies should be tailored to the physiological characteristics of oral mucosal ageing in the elderly to achieve personalised treatment.

Conclusion

In summary, this review synthesises evidence establishing oral mucosal ageing not as a passive decline, but as a dynamic process of microenvironmental reprogramming. Driven by the core pillars of cellular senescence, immunosenescence, and structural decay, the aged mucosa transitions from a resilient barrier to a proinflammatory, vulnerable tissue landscape. This altered landscape fundamentally explains the heightened susceptibility to chronic oral pathologies in the elderly.
