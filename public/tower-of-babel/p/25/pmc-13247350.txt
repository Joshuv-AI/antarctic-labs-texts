Introduction

The transition to adulthood is marked by developmental transformations that have lasting impact on the trajectory and well‐being of the young adult (Schwartz et al. 2011). This transition period is critical for young adults with developmental disabilities due to the unique challenges they face in achieving independence. The transition to adult healthcare services is especially critical for autistic young adults as they have higher rates of co‐occurring chronic physical and mental health conditions compared to the general population (Cheak‐Zamora et al. 2021; Cashin et al. 2018; Croen et al. 2015). Additionally, between 70% and 80% of autistic individuals experience mental health challenges, predominantly depression and anxiety (Lever and Geurts 2016). Supporting autistic young adults’ independence requires a comprehensive assessment of their current levels of independence and future goals to develop tailored support systems (White et al. 2023; Gilmore et al. 2023).

Healthcare Transition (HCT) services help youth in the transition to adult‐oriented healthcare (Schwartz et al. 2011). Central to these services, HCT readiness assessments evaluate an individual's readiness to transition from paediatric to adult‐oriented care and manage their healthcare needs independently (Schwartz et al. 2014). These assessments play a vital role in guiding a collaborative effort toward independence for the young adult, caregiver and provider (Schwartz et al. 2011; Schwartz et al. 2014; Jensen et al. 2017; Cheak‐Zamora, Teti et al. 2020).

Among the most widely used HCT readiness tools are the Self‐Management and Transition to Adulthood with Rx = Treatment (STARx) and the Transition Readiness Assessment Questionnaire (TRAQ) (Zhang et al. 2014; Ferris et al. 2015; Sawicki et al. 2011). While these measures address core competencies such as provider communication, medication management and appointment‐keeping, their limited scope overlooks additional challenges often faced by autistic young adults (Davis et al. 2014; Kuhlthau et al. 2016). Recognizing these limitations, the Health‐Related Independence (HRI) measure was developed to provide a broader assessment of transition needs for autistic young adults (Cheak‐Zamora et al. 2021; Cheak‐Zamora, Teti et al. 2020). The HRI was designed based on qualitative interviews with both caregivers and autistic young adults. However, later phases of its development included only caregiver validation, thus it is not currently designed for autistic young adults’ self‐administration.

The current study aims to resolve the need for a broader and more inclusive measure by refining the HRI measure to include the perspectives of autistic young adults as they transition into adulthood. Specifically, this study had two key objectives: (1) to use qualitative interviews to identify HRI concepts that are meaningful and relevant to autistic young adults and (2) to conduct cognitive interviews to assess the importance, clarity, readability and ease of use of existing HRI questions. By integrating insights from these qualitative approaches, the study seeks to better understand autistic young adults’ perspective on independence.

Methods

Eligibility: Young adults aged 18–26 with a self‐reported autism spectrum diagnosis were eligible to participate if they could complete an English‐language interview, provide informed consent and actively engage in the interview session.

Recruitment: Purposive sampling was used to recruit autistic young adults from urban and rural communities in the Midwest. Recruitment efforts included advertisements on social media platforms and flyer distribution on college campuses and autism specialty clinics. Additionally, patients from autism specialty clinics who provided prior consent were contacted via mail and email.

Autistic young adults interested in participating contacted the research team to complete a screening questionnaire to determine eligibility. During the initial conversation, the first author described the study and encouraged questions. Autistic young adults were informed that participation was voluntary and would not impact their medical care. Eligible participants received a $25 compensation after completing the study. The research team obtained institutional review board approval from their university.

Quantitative Data Collection

All participants completed two short surveys. One collected information about the participant's demographic background, diagnosis/medical history and perceived level of independence. Participants self‐reported their level of independence by selecting one of four categories (Table 1). This question was designed to provide a general sense of day‐to‐day functioning and living arrangements, rather than a comprehensive assessment of skills across multiple domains. Examples provided to participants included managing daily routines, transportation and personal care. Responses were used descriptively to contextualize qualitative findings rather than to assign clinical labels. The second survey asked participants to rank healthcare topics on a scale from 1 (most important) to 10 (least important) based on personal relevance. If participants had not completed the surveys prior to their interview, the interviewer provided time during the session for completion.

Qualitative Data Collection

Autistic young adults participated in 30 to 60‐min‐long semi‐structured interviews via Zoom. Interviews were automatically transcribed using Zoom's transcription feature and subsequently reviewed for accuracy by members of the research team. Several strategies were used to facilitate the interview and reduce participant stress during the interview process. Individual interviews were conducted as this method has been shown to elicit in‐depth information in a less threatening and more productive manner for autistic young adults than methods such as focus groups. (Cheak‐Zamora and Teti 2015, Walsh et al. 2017) Additionally, participants were allowed to invite an advocate (e.g., caregiver, family member, or another trusted individual) to attend the interview with them. Four participants chose to include an advocate in their interview. Advocates were asked to only assist in answering questions or provide additional information if requested by the autistic young adults. Additionally, participants were emailed interview questions beforehand. (Cheak‐Zamora et al. 2017) Participants were encouraged to take breaks as needed and to join the interview from their preferred location, with the option to turn the camera off.

Interview Training

The first and second authors conducted all interviews. The first author, who has over 15 years of experience conducting qualitative studies, led most of the interviews and attended other interviews as a secondary facilitator. The second author was trained in strategies for qualitative interviewing and how to use the interview guide. This was followed by interviewing practice sessions between the first and second authors to ensure methodological consistency and interviewer confidence.

Interview Guide

Individual interview guides were developed based on a social‐ecological model of readiness for transition to adult care, previous findings from HCT studies and caregiver and self‐advocate recommendations. (Schwartz et al. 2011, First et al. 2019) Two interview guides were utilized to address three main aims: (1) Understand the importance of HRI topics, (2) understand how young adults implement HRI skills when managing healthcare needs and (3) assess specific questions from the previous HRI survey for relevance, understandability and clarity.

Each interview guide started off with the same general questions, asking participants to describe what they thought managing their own health and health care needs meant and how they managed these tasks in their own lives. The first interview guide then asked participants to review (1) how they ranked the HRI topics in the quantitative survey (Table 2), (2) discuss why they rated some topics as important and others as less important and (3) discuss what skills they would need to do the activity address in the HRI topic. Interviewers utilized pre‐survey data to identify which HRI topics participants rated as most and least important and provided the participants with these answers if needed. For example, participants were reminded of their highest, middle and lowest‐rated HRI topics and asked to elaborate on their rankings.

The second interview guide employed a cognitive interviewing method to assess the readability, clarity and validity of individual HRI questions. This structured approach allowed for the examination of survey items, assessing possible item confusion, evaluating the appropriateness of response options and assessing the relevance of the item and construct (Dumas et al.; Peterson et al. 2017). During the cognitive interview, the questions were read aloud and, when appropriate, also displayed on the screen to support participant comprehension. After the initial interview questions (described above) participants were asked (1) read one of the HRI questions (based on the HRI topics), (2) describe what the question meant to them and how they would answer it, (3) discuss the importance of the topic and (4) discuss what skills they would need to do the activity address in the HRI question.

All interviews ended with the participant being asked to describe what was the most important thing about becoming independent and if there were any other topics they wanted to talk about. The majority of the data used for this analysis came from the participants' general discussion about HRI topics and their examination of if they could do an HRI activity and why or why not. Five autistic young adults participated in interviews using this initial guide. Nine autistic young adults participated in interviews using the second interview guide. Preliminary analysis of data, based on the concept of data saturation, was used to determine the final sample size (Guest et al. 2006; Saunders et al. 2018).

Data Analysis

Quantitative data analysis: Survey data were analysed using descriptive statistical methods to summarize and synthesize demographic information and rating scale responses. The mean and standard deviation were calculated to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics (Table 1). The HRI ranking data was initially averaged separately for autistics in both interview groups; however, no differences were noted across groups, thus the data was combined.

Qualitative data analysis: Interview transcripts were reviewed by a minimum of two authors to ensure accuracy. The research team employed an inductive thematic analysis approach to identify recurring patterns, categories and themes across the dataset. This approach was employed to code and organize data, including initial and axial coding, analytical memos and organizational matrices and reports (Guest et al. 2011; Merriam and Tisdell 2015), Interview data were analysed through an iterative, multi‐stage process involving discussion, coding, categorizing, theme development and review (Campbell et al. 2013; Miles et al. 2018). Throughout the coding process, the analytical team worked to stay close to the data and true to our objective of giving autistic young adults a forum to define independence and their journey towards adulthood on their terms (Guest et al. 2011; Merriam and Tisdell 2015).

A software platform was utilized to code and analyse participants’ transcripts (Dedoose, Version 8.3.35). The three members of the analytic team independently reviewed transcripts and formulated initial impressions of the data and initial codes. Over the course of several meetings, initial impressions of thematic categories were discussed, leading to the development of a preliminary structure of primary codes. The second and third authors then began coding the data utilizing an iterative consensus‐building process (Guest et al. 2011; Merriam and Tisdell 2015). To enhance the reliability of the coding process and findings, both reviewers coded sections of four transcripts to establish interrater reliability. Subsequently, all team members discussed the codes to review discrepancies and refine the coding structure. The examination of the code agreement resulted in 80% agreement. The team clarified differences and coded several additional transcripts separately. After this, the coder agreement obtained was 95%. Any discrepancies in the coding were reviewed, after which the second and third authors divided the remaining transcripts and coded them independently. The analytic team continued to meet weekly to discuss coding, preliminary findings and analysis memos.

After all transcripts were coded, data visualization of code frequency and co‐occurrence was used within Dedoose to identify connections between codes and to inform synthesis of data into interpretable thematic categories. The final set of themes and subthemes met the recommended criteria for internal homogeneity and external heterogeneity (Patton 1990; Rossman and Rallis 2017). Except for minor edits to improve clarity, all quotes were provided verbatim. To protect participant confidentiality, all names used in this manuscript are pseudonyms.

Results

Demographic Information: Interviews were conducted with 14 autistic young adults, with an average duration of 44 min. Five of the interviews were qualitative and nine were cognitive interviews. Most of the participants were male, Caucasian and graduated from high school (Table 1).

Quantitative results

Autistic young adults were asked to rank the six topics identified as important in previous studies. Table 2 outlines the six topics with plain language descriptions created for this study. Participants unanimously agreed that the six topics evaluated were relevant to advancing HCT services and developing a transition measure (Figure 1). They also indicated that no additional topics needed to be added to the HRI measure.

Participant ranking of importance on each of the health‐related independence constructs.

Although participants demonstrated some variability in their rankings, several consistent patterns emerged. ‘Understanding and being able to talk about your physical and mental health challenges’ was ranked as the most important topic across participants, followed by ‘feeling safe in your home, community and on the internet’. In contrast, ‘knowledge about sex, reproduction and safe relationships’ was most frequently ranked as least important. These results suggest that autistic young adults prioritize topics that directly relate to their immediate safety, self‐understanding and ability to communicate about personal health needs, while placing less emphasis on topics that may seem distant or less relevant to their current stage of independence.

Qualitative results

The qualitative portion of the study explored the challenges faced by autistic young adults as they navigate the complexities of achieving independence, particularly in the context of managing their health decisions and daily responsibilities. Participants defined independence as the ability to make decisions, manage daily activities on their own or with limited support and perform tasks autonomously. As Jim stated when discussing independence, ‘That's honestly a word for me doing stuff by yourself, or meeting other people by yourself, not having any person around to like tell you what to do.’ Similarly, Ashley stated that the most important part of learning to be independent was, ‘Being safe in your home, in the Community, on the Internet’.

We identified four major themes related to the importance and challenges of gaining independence. These themes included (1) need for memory aids, (2) learning behaviours and gaining skills, (3) barriers to independence and (4) feeling like I can't do it. Across all conversations, autistic young adults expressed a desire for independence, along with a spectrum of experiences ranging from strong self‐reliance to heavy dependence on family members for support.

Theme 1: Memory Aids

Participants described challenges with memory and needing support remembering everything from important activities like doctors’ visits to daily activities such as brushing teeth. Many discussed using memory aids as essential in helping them achieve higher levels of independence. Memory aids are tools or strategies designed to assist individuals in remembering tasks, appointments, or activities. Subthemes included self‐imposed memory aids (1a) and memory aids a support person helped develop and manage (1b).

Subtheme 1a: Self‐Imposed Memory Aids

Self‐imposed memory aids were initiated and managed by autistic young adults themselves to improve their independence and daily functioning. Autistic young adults discussed the utilization and helpfulness of automatic and manual self‐imposed memory aids. Automatic memory aids included electronic or digital apps that provided reminders, while manual aids consisted of static or physical tools. Autistic young adults described automatic reminders as essential for staying organized and ensuring the timely completion of tasks. As Amber described, routine daily activities were often neglected ‘I mean I've got an APP on my phone that tells me, it's 10 am go brush your teeth’.

Autistic young adults described manual reminders as imperative to keep them abreast of daily tasks. When describing taking medication, Lee described using manual reminders saying, ‘I will have like a piece of paper to tell me how much I can take in one day or two [to help me remember to take pills]’. Ashley often expressed utilizing only automatic reminders or utilizing physical reminders in conjunction with automatic reminders. Notably, no autistic young adults discussed relying exclusively on manual reminders as their preferred memory aid.

Subtheme 1b: Supportive System Memory Aids

Supportive system memory aids were initiated by an individual who is part of the young adult's support system. Young adults described the importance of these memory aids but acknowledged that the utilization and successful application of the memory aid often relied on their support system to provide the reminder or tool. As Ashley described, ‘Also, just like having other people remind me about deadlines …’ Ashley goes on to further detail that having this type of supportive system memory aid encourages her and helps keep her on track. Ryan also discussed ‘Yeah, I have the memory of a goldfish. I have to have constant reminders with a lot of stuff’. Our findings suggest that memory challenges significantly impact the daily lives of autistic young adults. These challenges necessitate the use of various memory aids, often developed in collaboration with caregivers, to maintain independence and manage daily tasks.

Theme 2: Methods of Learning Behaviours and Gaining Skills

Autistic young adults shared numerous general and specific examples of their journey toward self‐reliance and independence, often describing it as a gradual and evolving process. This included learning social behaviours to expand their social network and gain deeper self‐awareness of their strengths and areas for improvement. Interestingly, participants noted that becoming independent sometimes involved learning to use trusted people for support and encouragement. Participants centred these conversations on the strategies participants used to develop new skills, including observing and mirroring the behaviours of others to enhance their social skills (Subtheme 2a) and gaining independence through directly asking and investigating (2b).

Subtheme 2a: Observation and Mirroring

When recalling adolescence and time in school several participants recounted observing their peers and mirroring their behaviours to learn social norms. As Amber described, ‘I think that [interaction with others] was just something I learned over time by kind of people watching might be the best way to explain it was watching the way that people interact with each other from an outside perspective.’ Ashley recalled a similar strategy when she described looking back on middle school experiences, ‘I definitely remember. Like I said sitting in school, just like hyper systemizing, I think everything everyone said and like especially paying attention to like the reaction from the receiving end of certain phrases or tones of voice, because I just like did not know how to come up with that myself.’ Participants described this ‘mirroring’ as a way of learning social norms and fit in. As Ashley stated, ‘I would do pretty well in social situations and I realize now it's because I did a lot of mirroring.’

Subtheme 2b: Asking for and Searching out Information

Participants also described purposefully seeking information through various means. Amber described checking in with her parents and role‐playing activities to learn new skills. She describes this process saying, ‘It was less of the like I can't do this and more [like] I'm not sure what to ask.’ She described another lesson on communication skills she learned from her parents stating,

I was taught that when you call somebody is [to] just smile because they hear it in your voice and I don't know if that's been helpful at all, I feel like I get a positive response regardless just because I tried to be as polite as possible.

Several participants and caregivers, serving as advocates, described using the Internet as a tool to learn skills and gain independence. For instance, when asked how he learned about his medical condition and ASD, Jim responded, ‘Chrome. The internet’. Similarly, Mary, the mother of Ryan described how Ryan used online tools to enhance his reading and communication skills: ‘He's figured out how to cut and paste and he cuts and paste things that he wants read to him into Google translate and has it read it back to him’.

In the same tenor, Ashley talked about being raised in an immigrant family and her parents not understanding her challenges as a child. This led her to turn to the internet for help. As she states,

Having access to the Internet, probably taught me everything, because I like to ask a lot of questions about things I learned and I like finding out those answers so just looking up facts in here and there was something I did often. I naturally did that about sex and relationships and I'd read those articles about how to get a boyfriend and stuff like that and I just in retrospect realize like I'd not naturally know how to socialize or was always very bad at reading other people's nonverbal cues.

In each of these discussions, participants seemed to actively seek out information and apply strategies to navigate the world more fully. While challenges persisted, participants seemed to reflect positively on the initiatives they took to be more independent.

Theme 3: Barriers to Health‐Related Independence

Independence in healthcare refers to a patient's ability to understand their condition(s), manage their healthcare needs and participate in decision‐making regarding their well‐being. While a few participants described managing their health and healthcare needs with minimal assistance, most reported relying heavily on caregivers. to oversee some or all of their healthcare needs. When talking about strengths and challenges regarding HRI, participants reported experiences largely fit into appointments (Subtheme 3a), medications (3b) and explaining and understanding ASD and health (3c).

Subtheme 3a: Appointments

Participants often expressed needing support to make and keep track of medical appointments. Many attributed this to overlapping family events, which made it more practical for their parents to manage scheduling. As Lee described, ‘Sometimes my mom puts it on (the schedule) because she gets lots of calls and stuff’. Similarly, when discussing scheduling doctors’ visits, Noah stated, ‘Mom usually does because I never remember all the other appointments or stuff we're doing. She kind of acts like the House Secretary in a way’. He went on to discuss his ability to do the task but reluctance to take over, saying, ‘I suppose I probably could make appointments on my own, it's just easier this way without having to worry about everyone else's (schedules) interfering’. Some participants described trying to use electronic methods to remember appointments but eventually deferring to others. As Kevin describes, ‘Oh, I was keeping reminders on my phone, but then I forgot to put reminders back on my phone’. His mother, serving as an advocate, added to the conversation saying, ‘He gets a lot of reminders from mom but that's okay’. Others described their inability to keep track of appointments, as Ryan stated, ‘It's usually my mom who makes those appointments’.

Subtheme 3b: Medications

While not all participants in this study took medications, many described managing multiple medications daily. Participants often underscored how much easier it was to navigate this responsibility with another's help. For example, Kevin described worrying about overtaking his medications so he always ‘checks with mom’. When asked if he could take more ownership of this task, he responded, ‘No not right now, I used to have a pill organizer but it was thrown away’.

Other participants expressed a desire for increased independence around medication management. As Lee commented, ‘Well I think I need my mom to do it but I think it would be nice to do it on my own’. Another participant, Jim, who described taking his medications independently, noted challenges with obtaining refills when needed. He stated, ‘I know when it's running low. Or we both know’. His mom interjected, ‘He's totally dependent on Mom, Because, mom, Mom does refills’.

Subtheme 3c: Explaining and Understanding ASD and Health

Participants were asked to discuss how well they understood their ASD diagnosis and health conditions as well as how well they could talk to others about health‐related concerns. Participants described a variety of sources where they learned about their ASD or health conditions including ‘talking to parents,’ ‘doctors,’ ‘therapists,’ and ‘internet search’.

Participants displayed different levels of understandings of their diagnosis. Some expressing insight into their healthcare needs while others reported difficulties understanding and reliance on caregivers. Amber described what she would need to achieve greater independence in managing her health, stating: ‘Understanding the how maybe understanding the why. Being able to properly vocalize issues that you're having like all have things wrong with my body and be [able to say] this is what's happening’. When asked about his understanding of his health condition Ryan thoughtfully expressed:

I have ADHD and depression, which causes a unique mess, for me, others might not have that and others might have other combinations I think that ASD [is] an important thing to look at, but I think it also needs to be looked at in combination with what else is present. I think it's rarely going to be the only factor and if you just focus on it, you might miss something else that could cause other issues…Because I don't think my memory issues are from autism, I think they're from the ADHD. But I'm not sure.

Other participants reported difficulty understanding their diagnosis and struggled to explain associated feelings and symptoms. Kevin reported, ‘I don't know how to talk about my mental health because I can't understand it. Sometimes, I don't know how to explain things. Sometimes I prefer mom to be there to talk’. Kevin's mom added, ‘He explains things to me, and I translate, so I translate both ways a lot. He is really pretty dependent. Pretty dependent on me’. Similarly, Lee described, ‘[Needing help] knowing the basics and knowing what we think it is. Basically, tell them what the problem is and what I need’. Mary, Ryan's mom, described her part in her son's lack of independence when she described,

This is a kind of a rude awakening to me because as a mother you just sort of get used to doing things for [people] and people let you do things for them and it had not occurred to me like this whole, what do you want to do with yourself, you know autonomy. I want to know what he thinks…I'm not sure he gets as much choice, as some people would get….So this was sort of a you need to pay attention to what they're thinking…And I never done it, but it occurs to me that I can take some of the limit off. You know if he's feeling like you can talk to the doctor now, he's old enough to talk to the doctor without me.

The autistic young adults’ experiences reveal a wide range of understandings and challenges related to their ASD diagnosis and health conditions.

Theme 4: Feeling Like I can't do it

When discussing their abilities to manage health‐related tasks independently, participants often expressed hesitation or uncertainty about being fully independent in any specific areas. Participants often had difficulty articulating or pinpointing the exact reason they lacked independence or a particular skill (Subtheme 4a). When they did describe what was holding them back the barrier was often related to communication challenges or a specific complex task (4b).

Subtheme 4a: Unsure of What Is Needed

Asked to identify any health‐related tasks he could handle on his own, Lee responded ‘I don't think so’. Answering a similar question, Noah said, ‘I'm honestly not sure…I almost assume at this point, [I will need] some sort of like assistant, or something. I really don't know it's something we've been trying to figure out’.

Other participants seemed to have a bit more confidence in their developing independence but continued to express the desire for caregiver support. As Amber said, ‘I can make my own medical decisions. I just prefer to have my mom in the room, because she also works in healthcare, so I want her opinion’. Similarly, when asked about talking to the doctor about health issues, Kevin said, ‘sometimes I can do it sometimes I can't’. When asked what would help him do more of it on his own, he responded, ‘I don't know’. This uncertainty reflects the transitional nature of independence for many autistic young adults, who may recognize their growing capabilities while simultaneously feeling unsure about when or how to fully assume responsibility for their own health‐related decisions.

Subtheme: 4b: Communication and Other Limitations

Several participants specifically described wanting assistance because of communication challenges or anxiety around communicating with others. When discussing mental health needs, Jim expressed being ‘totally dependent’ on his parents and stated, ‘Just some communication between me and them (parent) would probably be the best’. Kevin also described challenges being independent saying ‘It (ASD) affects some of my life but I'm okay at home, but outside of the house, I am very much (in) need of help from my mom because I don't know how to explain things’. To address communication challenges and anxiety due to not knowing what to say, Amber worked with her mother to develop a script saying, ‘My mom helped me… it's like yeah I can call them up and say ‘one doctor's appointment, please’, but what do I need to ask in a set of, instead of asking this say this, then say the service, and then you're done’.

Participants described needing additional support in several areas, including an inability to drive (Noah), needing reminders about medications (Kevin, Sara), and making decisions independently. Regarding making decisions, Michael stated, ‘More often than not, I would just go with what looks to be the simpler option rather than what would be the best option…so I would probably need someone by my shoulder helping me out with it’. While some participants described specific barriers preventing them from completing a task or taking on more independence, many seemed to lack confidence rather than skill or ability.

Discussion

Within this study, we used a mixed‐method approach to understand how autistic young adults are transitioning into adulthood and the knowledge and skills that would aid in their transition. Our findings highlight the significant challenges autistic young adults face in achieving independence and their dependence on caregivers. While this study did not include a typically developing comparison group (Bartoszuk et al. 2021), a previous study assessing the transition to adulthood for college students and their caregivers found that they, too relied on their caregivers or support and did not feel fully independent. While the challenges of this sample were more general and financial and guidance‐based, some similarities emerged. Additionally, research comparing the transition needs and barriers of autistic young adults, young adults with medical conditions and typically developing adults would expand our understanding of this developmental period across seemingly. distinct groups.

Despite the previous validation of the HRI measure for caregivers, this study illustrates the importance of incorporating the individual needs and perspectives of autistic young adults into the measure. The survey data demonstrated the importance of all HRI topics to the transition to adulthood. The qualitative data identified four major themes that encapsulated the views and concerns of the autistic young adults interviewed. These included (1) Need for and use of memory aids, (2) Methods of learning behaviours and skills (3) Barriers to HRI and (4) Feeling like I can't do it. While these themes are related to the HRI constructs previously identified, they provide a broader understanding of autistic young adults’ experiences and needs.

The quantitative findings reveal that autistic young adults prioritized understanding and being able to talk about their physical and mental health challenges as the most important skill in becoming independent, followed by feeling safe in one's home, community, and online environments. These findings are consistent with previous work developing the HRI measure, which identified these same topics as critical aspects of independence and the HCT process for both caregivers and autistic young adults (Cheak‐Zamora, Teti et al. 2020).

Findings from both quantitative and qualitative data suggest that autistic young adults emphasize safety and self‐awareness as central components of independence. Incorporating additional HRI questions related to physical and mental health challenges may therefore enhance the measure's sensitivity to real‐world needs and help inform the design of HCT services. Within this and the previous HRI study, knowledge about sex, reproduction and safe relationships was consistently ranked as less important (Cheak‐Zamora, Teti et al. 2020). While this may not be a current priority for autistic young adults, future research should explore the timing, content and delivery methods for effective sexual health education to support safe and healthy relationships in adulthood.

Early in our conversations with participants, they described the role of memory aids in helping manage daily tasks and appointments. This is not surprising given that executive functioning including prioritizing and managing daily tasks are well‐documented challenges for autistic individuals (St. John et al. 2022). This study adds to the previous research by documenting strategies autistic young adults use and those that would be helpful to implement. The reliance on self‐imposed memory aids, particularly automatic digital reminders, indicates a proactive approach to mitigate these challenges. In contrast, the frequent use of supportive system memory aids underscores the critical role of external support in ensuring the execution of important tasks. This duality suggests that while autistic young adults strive for autonomy, some still depend on their support networks to compensate for memory difficulties. Our findings highlight the need for integrated approaches that combine personal strategies with external support to effectively address memory challenges in this population. Implementation of additional automatic reminders or prompts with faded support from others could be an ideal solution (Marcotte et al. 2020).

While the interview included questions regarding independence in general and specifically related to autistic young adults’ healthcare needs, many participants talked more specifically about how they gained skills needed to live in the world. For instance, autistic young adults described how they learned to communicate with others and ‘fit in' with their peers. Previous studies have shown that both autistic and typically developing individuals use camouflaging (i.e., working to blend in with a larger peer group) to reduce stigma and gain membership in peer groups (Miller et al. 2021; Cook et al. 2021). While camouflaging may have benefits related to acceptance, it has negative short and long‐term effects including exhaustion, depression, and feelings of loss of identity (Miller et al. 2021; Cage and Troxell‐Whitman 2019) Females discussed mirroring or practicing interactions more often than their male counterparts. The limited literature examining social engagement and coping strategies in autistic females supports our findings that autistic females often use camouflaging and imitation to establish friendships and relationships with peers. (Cage and Troxell‐Whitman 2019; Tierney et al. 2016) Together these findings underscore the need for gender‐based assessments and interventions to assist with friendship development and mental health support.

Participants talked about areas in which they struggled to gain independence and described deferring to their caregivers to do or help with tasks. These included appointments, taking medication and talking about their autism. Participants seemed to lack confidence in their ability to manage these aspects of their health. Several participants reported deferring to caregivers because it was easier, safer, or too complex for them to manage. Caregivers frequently reiterated their involvement. This is similar to findings in studies on service availability, childhood interventions and support for autistic teens, college students and adults (Cheak‐Zamora et al. 2017; Chun et al. 2023; Teti et al. 2019). Caregivers often reported a lack of needed services and fear that without their influence, their child would not be successful or safe (Cheak‐Zamora et al. 2017; Chun et al. 2023). In one study, caregivers assessed their autistic young adult's self‐determination. Within this study, caregivers rated the opportunities for self‐determination as high at home (i.e., opportunities for youth to be independent) but rated autistic young adults' capacity (i.e., ability to be independent) as low (Cheak‐Zamora, Maurer‐Batjer et al. 2020b). Other studies identify differences in caregiver and youths' evaluation of what the autistic young adults could do, where youth consistently rated their ability higher than their caregivers did (Cheak‐Zamora et al. 2017). While the current study did not assess levels of independence, it appears that autistic young adults are interested in increasing their autonomy but lack opportunities to gain these skills.

Interestingly, nearly 80% of participants self‐reported being independent or mostly independent. However, the qualitative data revealed nuanced challenges and ongoing support needs that complicated this self‐assessment. This disparity may reflect differences in how participants conceptualized ‘independence’, with some emphasizing autonomy in decision‐making or daily routines while still requiring substantial support in other areas. It may also highlight the limitations of broad self‐report categories for the complexity of lived experiences. Recognizing this distinction is important for both research and practice, as it underscores the need for more refined measures that capture variations in independence across different life domains. Data was not collected on participants’ current living situations; this will be discussed further in the limitations section.

Additionally, participants reported debilitating lack of confidence in their ability to manage their healthcare needs, often saying that they, ‘can't do it’. Participants described not having the skills needed to be independent in general or related to a specific activity. A few participants discussed having the ability to do things independently but desiring support. In all cases, the rationale for needing support focused on communication challenges. These mixed feelings about independence reflect a developmental stage in which young adults may want to take on more responsibility yet continue to rely on trusted caregivers for reassurance and guidance. Recognizing these tensions is important, as it can inform intervention strategies that help young adults build confidence and communication skills while honouring their need for ongoing support.

Reports of communication challenges are very common, especially within the medical setting (Nicolaidis et al. 2015; Doherty et al. 2022). Providers' lack of knowledge about autism, assumptions about autistic individuals’ abilities, lack of communication assistance or support, overstimulating office settings and a rushed and complex healthcare system can compound this problem for autistic young adults. As discussed previously, caregivers, understandably, work to compensate for these challenges and ensure their needs are met by talking or interpreting for the autistic young adults. More work is needed to empower autistic individuals, support caregivers as they transition from a lead to a supporter role, adequately train providers and increase healthcare accessibility.

Limitation

The current study is an expansion of previous work examining the health‐related transition needs of autistic young adults and their caregivers. We observed saturation between themes but acknowledged that our sample size was limited. The results are not generalizable to all autistic young adults as we used qualitative data to explore the experience of a small sample. Further, while autistic individuals, caregivers, and clinicians provided input on the study design and interview guide questions, these groups were not included in the data analysis or manuscript's development. Although the first author has a neurodevelopmental disability, no one on the research team is autistic.

Since the study required autistic young adults to be able to participate verbally, participants who used alternative forms of communication were excluded. Verbal and nonverbal forms of participation may have enhanced our ability to recruit a more diverse sample. The sample was equally split between individuals identifying themselves as female or male, but the majority of the sample was white and young (mean age 23). This lack of diversity may affect the results identified in this study.

Another limitation is that autism spectrum diagnoses were self‐reported and not independently verified by the research team. This raises the possibility that some participants may not have met full diagnostic criteria. However, because the majority of recruitment occurred through clinical settings and professional listservs, it is likely that most participants had received formal evaluation by a qualified clinician. In addition, data on participants’ living situations was not collected, which limits the interpretation of self‐reported independence. Living arrangements, such as residing with family, in supported housing, or independently, may influence how individuals conceptualize and report their level of independence. Future studies should include this information to provide a more comprehensive understanding of contextual factors influencing independence.

In several interviews, caregivers often offered unsolicited perspectives and elaborated on the autistic young adults’ statements. The caregiver's presence during the interview may have influenced autistic young adults' answers and/or led them to engage less in the interview‐ deferring to their caregivers for answers or explanations. More work is needed to develop a better data collection method to capture the perspectives of autistic individuals with various communication abilities and preferences as well as those with and without intellectual disabilities. Future studies should consider the input of both autistic young adults and caregivers without influencing each other's perspectives.

Conclusion

This study used qualitative methods to gain insight into how autistic young adults are transitioning into adulthood especially as it relates to managing health care needs. Participants identified knowledge about their physical and mental conditions and safety as important areas for their transition into adulthood. Qualitative findings centred around what resources and support helped autistic young adults become more independent, and barriers limiting their progress. Autistic young adults often used electronic reminders, observation, or discrete trial training to learn skills needed to gain autonomy. Participants also reported challenges, reliance on caregivers, and feeling incapable of reaching independence.

Future research and service provision should focus on empowering autistic young adults and reconceptualizing independence as self‐determination with needed support. Self‐determination interventions can be initiated early, starting at 12–13 years of age, to capitalize on the built‐in support system, peer modelling and provide the youth with opportunities to utilize skills in self‐management and safe in achievable ways as they move into adulthood. Providing structured interventions with caregiver support can help youth capitalize on new opportunities for autonomy in a way that makes sense to them and their goals. Caregivers, healthcare providers, and educators should incorporate self‐determination and independent skill‐building across home, school, community and healthcare settings for all youth and young adults.
