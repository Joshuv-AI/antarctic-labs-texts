The future seen: Does episodic future thinking influence health decision-making in adolescents?
Authors: Haide Chen, Zixuan Liu, Enjin Zheng, Yu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although prior research has clearly demonstrated the general impact of episodic future thinking (EFT) on behavioral decision-making, there has been a scarcity of studies investigating how different features of EFT might variably affect health-related intertemporal choices in adolescents. This lack of research hinders our comprehension of the functional specificity of EFT. The present research aimed to examine how three specific characteristics of EFT (i.e., temporal distance, health relevance, and simulation perspective) influence health-related intertemporal decision-making in adolescent populations. Three experiments were designed and conducted with 248 adolescents aged 15 to 17 years. The decision-making was measured by health-related intertemporal decision-making task. Study 1 experimentally manipulated temporal distance through an episodic thinking imagination task, comparing intertemporal choice between a near-term EFT condition and a long-term EFT condition. Study 2 manipulated the content of EFT (specifically for long-term EFT), comparing intertemporal choice between a health-relevant EFT condition and a health-irrelevant EFT condition. Study 3 manipulated the perspective adopted during EFT simulation (specifically for long-term EFT within health-relevant content), comparing intertemporal choice between a first-person perspective EFT condition and a third-person perspective EFT condition. Study 1 found that ‌long-term‌ EFT was more conducive to health decisions than near-term‌ EFT. Study 2 found that ‌health-relevant‌ EFT was more effective than ‌health-irrelevant‌ EFT. Study 3 found that ‌third-person‌ perspective simulation promoted better health decisions compared to ‌first-person‌ perspective simulation. These findings highlight the functional specificity of EFT and suggest that its impact on intertemporal decision-making can be optimized by manipulating temporal distance, content relevance, and simulation perspective.

Introduction

The formation of health behaviors exerts profound and enduring effects on both immediate and long-term well-being. With the development of autonomy, adolescents aged 10 to 19 years have more opportunities to take charge of their own health behaviors, and engage in more self-directed behaviors (e.g., eating behaviors; Ziegler et al., 2026). When making health-related decisions, individuals often need to trade off the immediate costs against the delayed health benefits (Lange-McPherson & Halpern-Felsher, 2023), which is defined as intertemporal decision-making. Adolescents routinely encounter contexts involving health-related intertemporal decision-making. For instance, adolescents are often confronted with dental treatment or orthodontic issues. Adopting a treatment regimen that demands more time and effort in the present stage can help them obtain a more durable and long-term health status in the future.

However, adolescents’ intertemporal decision-making capacity is still under development (De Water et al., 2014), and the prefrontal cortex that supports this cognitive function has not fully matured either (Anandakumar et al., 2018; Figner et al., 2010). Coupled with their immature self-regulation ability (Mayangsari et al., 2024) and underdeveloped mental time travel capacity (Yuan et al., 2025), adolescents are more susceptible to impulsive influences and immediate rewards, which makes them prone to decisions characterized by high delay discounting (Ikink et al., 2023; McFadden et al., 2022). Given these, the present study aimed to address the challenge of improving intertemporal decision-making regarding health-related behaviors among adolescents.

Intertemporal decision-making refers to decisions oriented toward future outcomes. Episodic Future Thinking (EFT), being inherently future-oriented, is defined as the cognitive process of mentally simulating or vividly pre-experiencing future personal events based on past or present experiences (Atance & O’Neill, 2001). Empirical research has demonstrated the role of EFT in shaping intertemporal decisions across various domains, including reducing caloric intake (Daniel et al., 2015), promoting healthier snack selections (Dassen et al., 2016), decreasing cigarette consumption (Liu et al., 2024), increasing motivation for smoking cessation (Chen et al., 2025), and lowering substance use-related risk outcomes (Collado & Stokes, 2023). Research has also found that EFT training enhances time value discounting in populations with obesity or diabetes (e.g., Colton et al., 2024; Ding et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023) and also reduces delay discounting among individuals who use marijuana (Sofis et al., 2020). Most above relevant studies take adults as research participants. Beyond adult populations, empirical evidence has also documented the significant effect of EFT on intertemporal decision-making among adolescent groups. For instance, two studies employing regression analysis ‌demonstrated that EFT served as a stable and moderate-level predictor of delay discounting behaviors in adolescents, even after controlling for a set of potentially relevant confounding variables (Bromberg et al., 2015; McCue et al., 2019)‌. A subsequent study conducted by Burns and colleagues (2022) further found that EFT exerted a significantly stronger effect on delay discounting among adolescents aged 12 to 14 years, rather than among children aged 8 to 11 years (Burns et al., 2022).

Existing studies often compare EFT with semantic future thinking and emphasize that the core function of EFT is to facilitate individuals to construct detailed and vivid future scenarios, so as to vividly pre-experience events that have not yet occurred (Schacter et al., 2017). However, the effect of EFT is not robust, and its influence on intertemporal decision-making will vary with the changes in its own characteristics (Rösch et al., 2022). As the future can be conceptually delineated into the near-term future (encompassing timeframes within several days to weeks) and the long-term future (spanning several months to years), EFT can be operationally categorized as near-term EFT (NT-EFT) and long-term EFT (LT-EFT). While prior research has predominantly contrasted EFT with standardized episodic thinking paradigms (e.g., Turnquist & Juliano, 2025), the distinct behavioral effects of NT-EFT versus LT-EFT remain empirically unexamined.

Construal Level Theory (Trope & Liberman, 2003) posits that individuals mentally represent events along a psychological continuum ranging from low to high levels of construal. Low-level construal is characterized by concrete or context-dependent representations, while high-level construal involves abstract or decontextualized representations that broaden cognitive perspective to encompass overarching goals (Crouzevialle et al., 2023). According to this theory, compared with NT-EFT, LT-EFT facilitates the reduction of psychological distance for more distant future events, shifting construal from abstract high-level to detailed low-level representations. More specifically, under the NT-EFT condition, individuals can only generate concrete low-level construal for near-future events, while maintaining abstract high-level construal for distant future events, failing to form corresponding concrete low-level construal for these temporally remote events. By contrast, under the LT-EFT condition, individuals are also able to generate concrete low-level construal for distant future events. As LT-EFT shortens the psychological distance of future events (including rewards), individuals tend to place less weight on the superficial details of immediate options, shift their focus to the value of delayed rewards, and exhibit greater willingness to wait for long-term benefits (Kim & Zauberman, 2019). Given these, it can be speculated that LT-EFT is more conducive to individuals making more future-oriented intertemporal decisions than NT-EFT.

Beyond temporal distance, the content imagined during EFT tasks can also vary. A substantial body of empirical evidence has consistently demonstrated that the facilitative effect of EFT on intertemporal decision-making is significantly stronger when the content of the imagined future events is closely aligned with the inherent nature of the presented choice options. Athamneh and colleagues (2021) found that health-goal related EFT did not demonstrate more effectiveness in reducing monetary discounting compared to general content EFT. Nevertheless, integrating a health-goal into standard EFT protocols yielded significantly stronger effects on demand intensity and elasticity for cigarettes and fast-food relative to EFT without such a goal (Athamneh et al., 2021). Chang and Ladd (2023) observed that participants exposed to non-alcohol EFT conditions exhibited significantly lower future reward discounting than those in alcohol EFT conditions (Chang & Ladd, 2023). Rösch and colleagues (2022) conducted a meta-analysis and showed that the effect of EFT on delay discounting was significantly stronger for content-specific future simulations than for general future simulations (Rösch et al., 2022).

Theoretically, health-relevant EFT (HR-EFT) may confer greater benefits than health-irrelevant EFT (HI-EFT) for individuals’ health-relevant intertemporal decisions. The Goal Systems Theory (Kruglanski et al., 2002) emphasizes the core role of goals in behavior regulation, positing that the activation of behavioral goals prompts individuals to engage in top-down processing. Ajzen and Kruglanski (2019) further proposed the Theory of Reasoned Goal Pursuit, which argues that a specific behavioral goal of an individual will be activated under certain conditions or situations, and behaviors or objects that facilitate the achievement of this goal are more likely to be selected by the individual (Ajzen & Kruglanski, 2019). According to these theoretical frameworks, individuals often maintain multiple concurrent behavioral goals. Under the HR-EFT condition, health-related goals will be activated, which in turn generates a priming effect on health-related decision-making. In contrast, HI-EFT activates non-health goals and exerts no priming effect on health-related decision-making. It is therefore reasonable to speculate that HR-EFT is more conducive to individuals making more optimal health-relevant intertemporal decisions than HI-EFT.

The impacts of EFT may further be shaped by the perspective adopted during the mental simulation. The perspectives can be divided into first-person perspective and third-person perspective (Shoko & Tamami, 2023). From a first-person perspective, an individual may view an imagined scenario through a field perspective, which is understood to be internal, first-personal, and preserves the individual’s original field of view; from a third-person perspective, an individual may adopt an observer perspective, in which one sees oneself from the outside in the imagined scenario (McCarroll & Cosentino, 2020). Taking basketball dribbling as an example, from the first-person perspective, the individual sees their own hands slapping the basketball; from the third-person perspective, the individual sees their entire complete body performing the dribbling action. Empirical studies suggest that the third-person perspective may exert a greater influence on psychological responses than the first-person perspective. For instance, Christian et al. (2016) demonstrated that third-person perspective simulation exerted a greater effect on reducing individuals’ craving, willingness to pay, and consumption of tempting foods compared to first-person perspective simulation (Christian et al., 2016). Similarly, Saine et al. (2018) found that simulating one’s own product or service use from a third-person perspective was more likely to induce changes in consumption behavior than simulation from a first-person perspective (Saine et al., 2018).

Compared with first-person perspective EFT (FP-EFT), third-person perspective EFT (TP-EFT) may facilitate more concrete mental imagery via a broader scope of imagined content. Operationally, the first-person perspective enables intuitive scene information acquisition, but its 180-degree front-facing field of view is limited, making it hard to grasp the overall scenario structure and its relation to the self. The third-person perspective breaks this visual constraint, allowing individuals to adopt an observer vantage point to fully perceive their torso, facial expressions, actions, and the self-overall scenario relationship (Shoko & Tamami, 2023). Taking mountain climbing event as an example, the first-person view only captures steps and surrounding scenery, while the third-person view adds the full-body climbing movements and the individual’s position on the staircase. According to the previous studies of EFT (Schacter et al., 2017), more detailed imagination optimizes EFT functioning. And according to Construal Level Theory (Trope & Liberman, 2003), EFT for distal abstract events concretizes these entities and enables low-level construal comprehension. Thus, the TP-EFT might help capture more behavior-relevant details and form low-level construal representations for abstract future events.

Beyond generating more concrete details, TP-EFT may also elicit holistic and meaning-focused processing of the imagined event, which in turn promotes the formation of high-level construal cognition in individuals (Niese et al., 2019). A recent study not only found that individuals tend to use third-person photos to capture the meaningful aspects of an event, but also discovered that such photos triggered stronger recollections of the event’s core meaning relative to first-person photos (Niese et al., 2024). This pattern seemingly contradicts the hypothesis that the functional theory of EFT and Construal Level Theory place more emphasis on the effects induced by low-level construal. In fact, the two mechanisms are not mutually exclusive. TP-EFT generates detailed cognition of specific action information at the low construal level, while simultaneously fostering abstract and in-depth understanding of behavioral goals at the high construal level. That is, TP-EFT can establish more extensive associations between the meaning of an action and its specific implementation plan. When individuals possess both a clear goal and a specific, operable plan that matches the goal, they are more likely to make behavioral decisions consistent with the goal (Ajzen & Kruglanski, 2019). In view of this, it can be hypothesized that TP-EFT is more conducive to promoting health-related intertemporal decision-making than FP-EFT.

The present research aimed to systematically examine the influence of EFT on health-related intertemporal decision-making in adolescents, with a specific focus on three characteristics: temporal distance (NT-EFT vs. LT-EFT), health relevance (HI-EFT vs. HR-EFT), and simulation perspective (FP-EFT vs. TP-EFT). To address these aims, we conducted three experiments employing a health-related intertemporal choice paradigm to assess decision-making tendencies. Study 1 experimentally manipulated temporal distance through an episodic thinking imagination task, comparing intertemporal choice between NT-EFT condition and LT-EFT condition. It was hypothesized that participants in the LT-EFT condition would exhibit lower delay discounting rates than those in the NT-EFT condition (H1). Based on Study 1, Study 2 further manipulated the content of EFT (specifically for LT-EFT) to examine whether HR-EFT exerted a stronger influence on intertemporal choice than HI-EFT. It was hypothesized that participants in the HR-EFT condition would exhibit lower delay discounting rates than those in the HI-EFT condition (H2). Finally, Study 3 extended Study 2 by manipulating the perspective adopted during EFT simulation (specifically for LT-EFT within health-relevant content), to test the distinct effects of FP-EFT and TP-EFT on intertemporal decision-making. It was hypothesized that participants in the TP-EFT condition would exhibit lower delay discounting rates than those in the FP-EFT condition (H3).

Study 1

Method

A 2 (Group: NT-EFT group vs. LT-EFT group) × 2 (Time: pretest vs. posttest) two-factor mixed experimental design was adopted in this study. The two independent variables included the between-subjects factor of group and the within-subjects factor of test time. The dependent variable was intertemporal decision-making performance, operationalized as the individual delay discount rate k derived from the intertemporal choice task. All of the experiments reported in this article have received full approval from an institutional ethics committee at a local university.

Participants

The required sample size was determined using GPower 3.1.9.7, with an effect size of f = .25, an alpha level of .05, and a desired statistical power of .80, test type of F test (i.e., ANOVA: Repeated measures, within-and-between interaction), yielding a calculated sample size of 66 participants. Prior to participant recruitment, we had full communication with the school administrators and class teachers, and formally launched the recruitment only after obtaining their informed consent. Participants were recruited via class-based recruitment notices with voluntary sign-up. A total of 96 adolescents were recruited from a senior high school in Zhejiang Province, China. Their inclusion criteria included: (1) aged between 10 and 19 years; (2) no clinically diagnosed health conditions; (3) no history of mental health disorders or neurological injuries; (4) normal or corrected vision. Participants were randomly assigned to either the NT-EFT group or the LT-EFT group using a ‌paired randomization‌ procedure, where the pairing was based on the participants’ sequential registration order. Nine participants were excluded due to exhibiting patterned or consistent responding patterns on the measurement tools or failing to meet the operational requirements of the imagination task of EFT. The final sample consisted of 87 participants aged 15 to 17 years, with 44 in the NT-EFT group and 43 in the LT-EFT group. Descriptive statistics of the sample were shown in Table 1. Materials and measures

Future scenario thinking and imagination task

EFT was manipulated by scenario thinking and imagination tasks (Ciaramelli et al., 2019). Each participant required to complete three rounds of imagination tasks, which differed in the specified future time points (e.g., 1 month, 1 year, and 10 years). The imagination was induced by instructions. For example, for the “1 month later” time point, the instructions were: “Please sequentially imagine positive events that will happen to you within one month. Clearly construct a vivid scene in your mind containing the environment, people, and actions, and try to record the imagined content in writing as detailed as possible. When imagining the events for each time point, you need to answer the following five questions: (1) Location; (2) Specific event; (3) Involved people; (4) Your feelings (please invoke multiple senses for detailed description); (5) A summary of the event.” The primary reason for requiring participants to imagine positive events rather than negative ones is that, compared with negative content, EFT targeting positive events exerts a larger and more robust effect size on intertemporal decision-making (Rösch et al., 2022).

The manipulation validity of the imagination task was tested using a self-report questionnaire, in which participants rated the vividness, emotional valence, and emotional experience intensity of the just imagined events on a 7-point Likert scale (Snider et al., 2016). The three items were respectively: “Please rate the vividness of the event you just recalled (1 = not vivid at all, 7 = extremely vivid)”, “Is the event you just recalled positive or negative? (1 = completely negative, 7 = completely positive)”, and “How intense is the emotional experience brought by the event you just recalled? (1 = very weak, 7 = extremely intense)”.

Intertemporal decision task

The present study developed a health-related intertemporal decision task suitable for the adolescent population by integrating existing standard monetary and health-related intertemporal decision tasks, as well as the results of the pre-experiment in this study. The health-related decision-making scenario and its corresponding instructions were developed based on the study by Van Der Pol and John Cairns (2001). The formal instruction was presented as follows: “Imagine you are managing a chronic illness (e.g., chronic gastroenteritis). Two treatment options for symptom relief are presented. Please select the one that aligns with your true preference. Take time to consider your decision carefully before selecting.”

Then, we developed 3 sets of items following the standard compilation method for intertemporal decision task items (e.g., Wang et al., 2024). Each item was a binary choice task containing one SS (smaller-sooner) option and one LL (larger-later) option. Both the SS and LL options explicitly specified the treatment delay and the corresponding health benefit duration. Based on the behavioral data collected from 20 adolescent participants, we finally set the treatment delay for the SS option and LL option as 2 years and 5 years respectively. In the first set of 8 choice items, the SS option for each item was fixed as “A 2-year treatment regimen that yields a 10-week symptom relief state”. For the corresponding LL option in 8 items, the duration of symptom relief state was set to 110%, 120%, 130%, 140%, 150%, 160%, 170%, or 180% of the SS option’s duration — that is, 11 to 18 weeks. For example, in the first item, SS option: “2 years treatment – 10 weeks symptom relief”, and LL option: “5 years treatment – 11 weeks symptom relief”. In the second set of 8 choice items, the SS option for each item was fixed as “A 2-year treatment regimen that yields a 20-week symptom relief state”. For the corresponding LL option in 8 items, the duration of symptom relief state was set to 22, 24, 26, 28, 30, 32, 34, or 36 weeks respectively. In the third set of 8 choice items, the SS option for each item was fixed as “A 2-year treatment regimen that yields a 30-week symptom relief state”. For the corresponding LL option in 8 items, the duration of symptom relief state was set to 33, 36, 39, 42, 45, 48, 51, or 54 weeks respectively.

Another 20 adolescent participants were recruited to complete these 24 items, and interviewed them about their comprehension of the task scenarios. Most participants reported full understanding of the items. And the results showed that the average proportion of LL choices across the three sets was around 30%, with no ceiling or floor effects observed. These three sets of items were finally adopted in the formal experiment. Further analysis of the formal experimental data also revealed that the proportion of LL choices among participants ranged from 30% to 60%, which further confirmed the absence of ceiling or floor effects.

The intertemporal decision-making task was administered via computers. Participants first read the standardized on-screen instructions, then completed 3 sets of 8 choice items, totaling 24 items in all. For each choice item, a white “+” fixation point was presented at the center of a black screen for 500 ms, followed by a pair of options displayed on the left and right sides of the screen. Participants were instructed to select their preferred option by pressing the “F” key for the left-side option and the “J” key for the right-side option. Immediately after a valid response was registered, a red selection frame appeared around the chosen option and remained visible for 500 ms, to explicitly confirm the participant’s selection. During the task, participants were allowed to raise questions freely if they failed to understand the general instructions or the meaning of specific items. The experimenter would then provide illustrative examples to ensure their full comprehension of the task.

The consideration of future consequences scale

Future orientation was assessed using the Consideration of Future Consequences Scale, which was originally developed by Strathman et al. (1994). The scale consisted of 12 items (e.g., “I consider how things might be in the future, and try to influence those things with my day-to-day behavior”). Participants were required to rate on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Extremely uncharacteristic, 5 = Extremely characteristic), with higher total scores indicating a higher level of future orientation. The Cronbach’s α coefficient this scale in present study was 0.78.

Brief self-control scale

Self-control was measured using the Brief Self-Control Scale, which was developed by Morean et al. (2014). The scale consisted of 7 items (e.g., “I can work efficiently toward a long-term goal”). Participants were required to rate on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree), with higher total scores indicating a higher level of self-control. The Cronbach’s α coefficient this scale in present study was 0.81.

Positive and negative affect scale

Affective states were assessed using the Positive and Negative Affect Scale (PANAS), which was originally developed by Watson et al. (1988). The scale consisted of 10 positive affective words and 10 negative affective words. Participants were required to rate how often they have felt such affection over the past two weeks on 5-point Likert scales (1 = Not at all, 5 = Extremely). ‌The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the two subscales were .84 and .84, respectively.

Sociodemographic and anthropometric data

Participants were instructed to report their sex, date of birth, body height, and weight, and to indicate whether they had any serious physical illness, mental disorder, or history of brain injury. Subsequently, subjective socioeconomic status was assessed using a 10-level ladder graphical representation (Adler et al., 2000).

Procedure

The experiment was conducted in a sound-insulated and secluded small classroom, and the total duration of the experimental procedure was approximately 30 min. The overall experimental procedure consisted of seven steps. First, after participants finished reading the informed consent form and provided their explicit voluntary consent, the formal research procedures were initiated. Second, participants filled out a sociodemographic questionnaire, along with completing baseline measures (e.g., the consideration of future consequences scale). Third, participants were briefed on the experimental instructions and familiarized themselves with the experimental procedure through practice sessions. Fourth, the formal experiment began with participants completing the pre-test of the health-related intertemporal decision-making task. Fifth, participants conducted all three rounds of EFT imagination task and completed a manipulation validity check. Sixth, participants completed the post-test of the health-related intertemporal decision-making task. Seventh, participants were debriefed and received ¥10 cash reward or a small gift of equivalent value as a token of appreciation.

Experimental manipulation

The manipulation of EFT for all participants was implemented via a standardized imagination task. For the NT-EFT group, the three future time points assigned for the three rounds of imagination were 1 day, 2–3 days, and 4–5 days respectively. For the LT-EFT group, the three corresponding future time points for the three rounds of imagination were set as 1 month, 1 year, and 10 years respectively.

Each round of imagination task included four steps. First, the instructions for the imagination task presented above the screen. Second, participants followed the instructions to vividly imagine future event, record the key words of imagined content in writing, and answer five questions about an event (e.g., location). Third, the screen initiated a 30-second timer, during which participants were required to close their eyes and mentally reconstruct and review the previously recorded event details. Fourth, an operational validity check was conducted. Participants were asked to rate the vividness, emotional valence, and emotional experience intensity of the events they had just imagined on a 7-point Likert scale (e.g., Snider et al., 2016). If the vividness rating of the event was <3, participants were prompted to imagine another, more vivid event and returned to the initial page. If the vividness rating of the event was still <3 on the second attempt, the participant was defined as invalid according to the exclusion criteria.

Statistical analyses

The calculation of the delay discount rate k in this study followed the procedure established by Kirby and colleagues (Kirby & Marakovic, 1996; Kirby, 2009). First, the indifference k value for each item was calculated based on the discounting formula V=A/(1+kD), where V denoted the present subjective value of a delayed reward A that was available after a delay of D, and k was the discount rate parameter. Second, the k value for each participant on each set of choice items was computed as the geometric mean of the two k values corresponding to the two adjacent items at the point where the participant switched their preference between the immediate and delayed reward options. For instance, if a participant consistently selected the immediate reward for the first two items, and switched to the delayed reward from the third item onwards, their k value would fall approximately between the indifference k value for second item and third item. The geometric mean of these two boundary k values was taken as the participant’s raw delay discount rate k for that specific item set. A larger k value indicated a steeper delay discounting, meaning the individual was more inclined to prioritize immediate rewards over larger delayed ones. Third, the arithmetic mean of the k values derived from the three independent sets of choice items was calculated as the participant’s final raw delay discount rate. Fourth, since the raw k values in the current study violated the normality assumption, a natural logarithmic transformation was applied to the data, generating the transformed variable denoted as Ln(k). Finally, a 2 × 2 mixed-design repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted on the Ln(k) values to test the main effects of group and test time, as well as their interaction effect.

Study 2

Method

A 2 (Group: HI-EFT group vs. HR-EFT group) × 2 (Time: pretest vs. posttest) two-factor mixed experimental design was adopted in this study. The indicator of the dependent variable of intertemporal decision-making was delaying discount rate k.

Participants

The required sample size for Study 2 was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.7, with parameters consistent with those in Study 1, resulting in 66 participants. A total of 94 adolescents were recruited from a senior high school in Zhejiang Province, China. The recruitment criteria for participants were the same as those in Study 1. And all participants had not been involved in previous experiments. Participants were randomly assigned to either the HR-EFT group or the HI-EFT group. Thirteen participants were excluded due to exhibiting response patterns on measurement tools or failing to meet the operational requirements of the imagination task of EFT. The final sample consisted of 81 participants aged 15 to 17 years, with 40 in the HR-EFT group and 41 in the HI-EFT group. Descriptive statistics of the sample were shown in Table 1. No significant differences between the two groups were observed in terms of sex ratios, age, and subjective socioeconomic status.

Materials and procedure

To test the effect of health relevance, the EFT in Study 2 was adjusted to a long-term EFT format (i.e., participants were required to imagine events occurring 1 month, 1 year, and 10 years in the future). Subsequently, the imagined content was manipulated as being either health- relevant or health- irrelevant.

Specifically, for the HR-EFT group, participants were required to imagine events related to their health behavior goals. Taking the “1 month” time point as an example, the instructions were augmented with the statement: “Please sequentially imagine positive events that will happen to you within one month. This event must be related to health behaviors (for example: playing basketball, taking a walk after meals, playing badminton and other physical exercises that can make me physically stronger, and maintaining a good eating habit that helps keep my body healthy). Clearly construct a vivid scene in your mind containing the environment, people, and actions, and try to record the imagined content in writing as detailed as possible. When imagining the events for each time point, you need to answer the following five questions: (1) Location; (2) Specific event; (3) Involved people; (4) Your feelings (please invoke multiple senses for detailed description); (5) A summary of the event.”

For the HI-EFT group, participants were required to imagine events unrelated to their health behavior goals. The instructions were similar to those for the HR-EFT group, with the only difference being that participants were asked to imagine general daily events. The corresponding instruction reads: “Please sequentially imagine positive events that will happen to you within one month. This event should be a general daily event (for example: passing an exam, having dinner with family and sharing daily stories, spending a pleasant lunch break with friends, receiving unexpected praise or thanks, discovering a new favorite song).”

After completing the imagination task, participants completed the manipulation check. The manipulation check was adapted from the version used in Study 1, with one additional item added (i.e., “To what extent is the event you just imagined related to health-related behaviors? 1 = completely irrelevant, 7 = highly relevant”) to assess the health-relatedness of the imagined events.

The measurement of intertemporal decision-making and baseline variables used in Study 2 were identical to those used in Study 1. Also, the experimental procedure and statistical analyses followed the same protocol as Study 1.

Study 3

Method

A 2 (Group: TP-EFT group vs. FP-EFT group) × 2 (Time: pretest vs. posttest) two-factor mixed experimental design was adopted in this study. The indicator of the dependent variable of intertemporal decision-making was delaying discount rate k.

Participants

The required sample size for Study 3 was calculated with parameters consistent with those in Study 1, resulting in 66 participants. A total of 90 adolescents were recruited from a senior high school in Zhejiang Province, China. The recruitment criteria for participants were the same as those in Study 1. And all participants had not been involved in previous experiments. Participants were randomly assigned to either the TP-EFT group or the FP-EFT group. Ten participants were excluded due to exhibiting response patterns on measurement tools or failing to meet the operational requirements of the imagination task of EFT. The final sample consisted of 80 participants aged 15 to 17 years, with 40 in the TP-EFT group and 40 in the FP-EFT group. Descriptive statistics of the sample were shown in Table 1. No significant differences were observed in ‌sex ratios‌, age or ‌subjective socioeconomic status.

Materials and procedure

Study 3 employed the scenario-based imagination tasks used in the HR-EFT group of Study 2 to operationalize EFT, with the critical addition of an imagery perspective manipulation. ‌For the FP-EFT group‌, participants were instructed to imagine the scenarios from a ‌first-person perspective‌. The instructions were: “During the imagination process, please adopt a ‌first-person perspective‌. Under this perspective, you view the event from the same vantage point as if you were experiencing it firsthand, seeing the surroundings through your own eyes and clearly constructing a vivid mental scene in your mind.” ‌For the TP-EFT group‌, participants were instructed to imagine the scenarios from a ‌third-person perspective‌. The instructions were: “During the imagination process, please adopt a ‌third-person perspective‌. Under this perspective, you view the event as an outside observer, allowing you to see both yourself and the surrounding scene.” To enhance participants’ understanding of first-person and third-person perspectives, we presented them with two sets of illustrative images.

The image for the first-person perspective depicted a hand reaching for a document from a box, which corresponded to the typical perceptual experience that individuals usually saw their own specific actions and nearby objects when viewing the world from a first-person standpoint. The image for the third-person perspective showed a complete person (including the head, torso and hand) reaching for the document from the box, illustrating that when individuals adopted a third-person perspective to observe themselves, they perceived themselves as a fully integrated individual performing the target action. The manipulation check was adapted from Study 2 by adding one additional item (i.e., During the imagination process, I consistently used . A. First-person perspective; B. Third-person perspective) ‌to assess‌ the imagery perspective.

All other measurements for intertemporal decision-making and baseline variables were identical to those used in Study 2. The experimental procedure and statistical analyses also followed the same protocol as Study 2.

Discussion

Three experiments were conducted with adolescent participants to examine the impact of EFT on health-related intertemporal decision-making. Study 1 found that participants in the LT-EFT condition exhibited significantly more health-promoting decisions compared to those in the NT-EFT condition, thereby supporting Hypothesis 1. A possible explanation for this result is that the functional effect of EFT requires a temporal match between the occurrence time of the imagined event and the expected occurrence time of the decision-making event. Existing studies have posited that the fundamental mechanism underlying EFT’s functioning is to render abstract future behaviors concrete and vivid (Atance & O’Neill, 2001). If the occurrence time of the imagined event in EFT aligns with the expected occurrence time of the decision event, individuals will develop a concrete and clear perception of the future behavior at this temporally consistent point, thus forming a low-level construal. However, if the EFT is a NT-EFT while the expected occurrence time of the decision event is distant, individuals who undergo this NT-EFT can only generate low-level construal for events expected in the near future, and fail to form low-level construal for distant future events. The health-related decision-making in the present study involves relatively distal future events, thus LT-EFT is required to facilitate and influence such decision outcomes.

Similarly, Study 2 revealed that participants in the HR-EFT condition made more health-promoting choices than those in the HI-EFT condition, providing support for Hypothesis 2. Previous studies have also demonstrated that content-specific EFT exerts a stronger effect on intertemporal decision-making than general future simulations (Rösch et al., 2022). One plausible explanation is that health-relevant EFT (HR-EFT) activates the goal system, which in turn initiates and guides subsequent health-related decision-making and action (Kruglanski et al., 2002). By focusing on health-related future scenarios, individuals become more attuned to health goals, enhancing their motivation to pursue health-promoting behaviors (Ajzen & Kruglanski, 2019). An alternative explanation is that HR-EFT renders health-related goals more concrete, explicit and operable. Health-related goals are inherently abstract, especially the health behaviors involving the relatively distant future in the present study. If the EFT is not targeted at health-related content, individuals will generate concrete perceptions for other irrelevant contents, while failing to form specific perceptions of the distant future health behaviors. According to Construal Level Theory (Trope & Liberman, 2003), transforming abstract high-level construal into concrete low-level construal is more conducive to individuals’ behavioral decision-making. Therefore, the functioning of EFT requires a content congruence between the EFT task and the decision-making target.

Study 3 showed that participants in the TP-EFT condition engaged in more health-promoting decision-making than those in the FP-EFT condition, supporting Hypothesis 3. Although there is still a lack of direct empirical evidence that systematically examines how EFT with different perspectives influences intertemporal decision-making, existing studies have also demonstrated that third-person perspective simulation exerts a stronger influence on behavior intention than first-person perspective simulation (e.g., Christian et al., 2016). A plausible reason for this phenomenon is that TP-EFT provides individuals with more abundant and more specific information than FP-EFT. Compared with FP-EFT, individuals in the TP-EFT condition can not only observe the action details and corresponding scenarios when performing certain health-related behaviors, but also perceive their own facial expressions, torso, and complete coherent whole-body movements (McCarroll & Cosentino, 2020; Shoko & Tamami, 2023). With access to more and more specific information, individuals are likely to form low-level construal of abstract future events (Trope & Liberman, 2003), which exactly aligns with the functions and characteristics of EFT, thus facilitating the effect of EFT on health decision-making.

Another possible explanation is that TP-EFT strengthens the association between the meaning of a specific health behavior and the corresponding actions for implementing that behavior. Individuals in the TP-EFT condition not only perceive the fine-grained details of the behavior, but also process the behavior holistically, especially gaining a thorough understanding of the meaning and purpose of the behavior (Niese et al., 2024). In the present experiment, adolescents in the TP-EFT condition not only constructed the details of a certain health behavior, but also comprehended the purpose of this health behavior. As a result, they formed not only low-level construal but also high-level construal for distal abstract health events. For instance, when an individual imagines themselves running on a treadmill in TP-EFT, they not only perceive the fine details of the running action, but also recognize that the purpose of the running behavior is to maintain a healthy physical state. In this case, the paradigm simultaneously brings the positive effect of low-level construal generated by EFT and the motivational drive derived from the salient goal, which explains why TP-EFT exerts a more pronounced effect than FP-EFT.

Apart from the hypothesis-consistent results mentioned above, the present study observed no significant pre-post difference in the HI-EFT condition, which deviates from the findings of previous studies (e.g., Dassen et al., 2016). The potential explanations for this unexpected result are listed as follows. First, health-related intertemporal decisions are harder to modify than the classic intuitive, quantifiable monetary decision paradigm, and the single-session short EFT intervention conducted in the present study is far less powerful than long-term protocols. Second, the HI-EFT content is fully decoupled from the health decision context, failing to trigger target priming, while the pre-test of health-related intertemporal choices further anchors participants to health-related mental representations and weakens the EFT effect. Third, as the referenced meta-analysis reveals, unpublished EFT studies have a pooled effect size of g = 0.307, 95%CI = [−0.096, 0.71], SE=0.2, with the confidence interval including zero, indicating null EFT effects are not rare underreported phenomena (Rösch et al., 2022).

The present study makes several meaningful contributions to the existing literature. ‌ The present study identifies that the boundary conditions for EFT to modulate decision-making are closely related to three key characteristics: temporal distance, health relevance, and simulation perspective of the EFT task. More importantly, it innovatively reveals that TP-EFT exerts a significantly stronger effect on health-related decision-making among adolescents than FP-EFT. These findings highlight the functional specificity of future-oriented thinking, and provide new empirical evidence for more nuanced theoretical accounts of its underlying mechanisms. Furthermore, this study offers practical implications for adolescent health behavior interventions. Future-oriented thinking training could be integrated into school-based health education programs. By guiding adolescents to imagine future health scenarios, strengthening their cognitive representation of long-term well-being, and reducing the allure of immediate rewards, such interventions may foster more rational health decision-making. Importantly, the findings suggest that enhancing the temporal distance, increasing health-related content, and adopting a third-person perspective mental simulation may optimize the effectiveness of these interventions.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the present study. First, the present study did not differentiate or compare intertemporal decision-making across different types of health behaviors. The emergence of distinct types of health behaviors is governed by their unique underlying mechanisms, such as high-calorie food intake (Chen et al., 2026), bedtime procrastination (Wang et al., 2025), and physical exercise (Hu et al., 2025). Future research should examine how EFT influences intertemporal choices across distinct health domains. ‌ Second‌, the current intertemporal decision task only included 2-year and 5-year delay options, without shorter time-horizon conditions. The timescale match between EFT and decision tasks is a likely critical moderator of EFT intervention effectiveness. Future studies can add short-horizon decision tasks (e.g., 2-day vs. 5-day comparisons) to systematically test how this timescale matching effect shapes EFT outcomes. Third, the absence of a no-EFT induction control condition in the present study limits the interpretability of the current findings, as it precludes direct inference of whether different EFT paradigms outperform a neutral baseline. Future research should add control group to verify each EFT condition’s measurable benefits relative to the unmanipulated baseline. Fourth, the delay discounting measurement in the present study was conducted after the concentrated EFT manipulation. Future research can adopt the EFT-cued delay choice paradigm (e.g., Burns et al., 2022) to accumulate more empirical evidence. Fifth‌, the underlying mechanisms through which different types of EFT influence health intertemporal decisions remain insufficiently explored. The present study did not directly measure key psychological variables (e.g., low or high-level construal) are theorized to mediate this effect. Future studies should test the mediating roles of these constructs to clarify the causal pathways involved.

Conclusion

The present research systematically examined the effects of three characteristics of EFT (i.e., temporal distance, health relevance, and simulation perspective) on health-related decision-making. The results indicated that ‌long-term‌ EFT was more conducive to health decisions than ‌near-term‌ EFT, ‌health-relevant‌ EFT was more effective than ‌health-irrelevant‌ EFT, and ‌third-person‌ perspective simulation promoted better health decisions compared to ‌first-person‌ perspective simulation. These findings highlight the functional specificity of EFT and suggest that its impact on decision-making can be optimized by manipulating temporal distance, content relevance, and simulation perspective.
