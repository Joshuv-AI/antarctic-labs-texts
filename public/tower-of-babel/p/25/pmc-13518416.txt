Iron dysregulation in the central nervous system: implications for autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Geir Bjørklund, Hasmik Harutyunyan, Zaven Mkrtchyan, Ella Baghdasaryan, Tamara Avetisyan, Nagwa A. Meguid, Fatma Hussein, Konstantin Yenkoyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by deficits in social interaction, impaired communication, and restricted, repetitive behaviors. Its pathophysiology involves a complex interplay of genetic, epigenetic, metabolic, and environmental factors. Among these, iron metabolism has emerged as a critical component due to its essential role in brain maturation. Iron supports oxygen transport, mitochondrial ATP production, neurotransmitter synthesis, DNA replication, myelination, and enzyme regulation processes vital for neurodevelopment. Dysregulation of iron homeostasis, both iron deficiency and iron overload, has been frequently observed in individuals with ASD and may contribute to core neurobehavioral symptoms through mechanisms involving neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and altered synaptic plasticity. Genetic variants affecting iron-regulatory proteins such as transferrin receptor, ferritin, ferroportin, and ceruloplasmin highlight the link between iron imbalance and ASD. Iron deficiency during critical windows of brain development may impair cognitive and behavioral maturation, whereas excessive iron accumulation may trigger glial activation, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptotic death of neurons. Therapeutic strategies differ across developmental stages: normalizing maternal iron status in pregnancy, correcting early-life deficiencies through supplementation of iron-fortified foods, and applying chelation therapies to prevent iron overload. This review integrates molecular, developmental, and clinical evidence to highlight the role of iron dysregulation in ASD with a focus on iron imbalance at different stages of neurodevelopment. Understanding the key mechanisms of iron dysregulation may grant new opportunities to restore iron homeostasis at specific time windows, supporting healthy neurodevelopment.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition affecting a substantial portion of the population, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 1 in 36 children in the United States, a figure that continues to grow annually (Maenner et al. 2023; Salehi et al. 2025) and 1 in every 127 people worldwide (Santomauro et al. 2025). The main symptoms of ASD are manifested as persistent deficits in social communication and restrictive and repetitive behaviors, as defined by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). Generally, ASD is characterized by heterogeneity of clinical manifestations and several co-occurring conditions, such as epilepsy and sleep disorders. (American Psychiatric Association 2013; Yenkoyan et al. 2017; Zhuang et al. 2024). The multifactorial nature of ASD makes it challenging to pinpoint a single causative factor, as its onset and phenotypic variability arise from the interplay of genetic, epigenetic, environmental, and metabolic influences (Ayoub 2025). The main directions to search for the etiology of ASD are commonly centered around prenatal synaptogenesis, neurotransmitter systems, energy metabolism, neuroimmune signaling, antioxidant systems, and metal homeostasis (Bjørklund et al. 2018, 2020; Masini et al. 2020; O’Grady & Grabrucker 2025; Yenkoyan et al. 2024).

Iron deficiency is one of the most common nutritional deficiencies in the world, affecting up to 30% of the global population (Kumar et al. 2022). Iron is widely involved in cellular respiration and energy production, hydroxylation and neurotransmitter synthesis, neurogenesis, and myelin synthesis (Obeagu 2025). Iron is involved in redox regulation and is crucial for epigenetic enzyme activity, such as demethylases (Behl et al. 2020). In addition, iron serves as an essential cofactor for numerous enzymes involved in neurodevelopment and neuronal signaling, including tyrosine hydroxylase and tryptophan hydroxylase involved in dopamine and serotonin synthesis, cytochromes participating in mitochondrial electron transport and energy production, catalase and peroxidases contributing to antioxidant defense, and ribonucleotide reductase required for DNA synthesis and cellular proliferation (Kulaszyńska et al. 2024; Lei et al. 2019; Porras & Rouault 2022).

Dysregulations in iron metabolism can negatively affect neurodevelopment, highlighting iron as a potential contributor to ASD etiology (Gao et al. 2025). Recent studies show that both iron deficiency and overload may occur in people with ASD, manifested as impaired iron uptake, storage, and export within the central nervous system. Both iron deficiency and overload were described in people with autism. These alterations are associated with genetic and environmental factors, including mutations and polymorphisms in genes involved in iron homeostasis, including TFRC (encoding transferrin receptor 1), SLC40A1 (encoding ferroportin), CP (encoding ceruloplasmin), and FTL (encoding ferritin light chain), as well as maternal iron status during gestation (Rabaya et al. 2023; Skogheim et al. 2021).

At the cellular level, iron deficiency can disrupt mitochondrial ATP synthesis, reduce activity of tyrosine hydroxylase, monoamine oxidase, and other iron-dependent enzymes, compromise myelin synthesis, maturation of axons, and monoaminergic neurotransmission, affecting neuronal connectivity and cognitive development (Cheli et al. 2020; Duan et al. 2022; Roemhild et al. 2021). Iron overload may induce excessive generation of reactive oxygen species, causing lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic cell death (Liu et al. 2022a).

Several studies support the presence of iron dysregulation in children with ASD, including low serum ferritin levels and abnormal magnetic susceptibility signals in the basal ganglia and cortical regions, indicating atypical iron distribution in the brain (Chen et al. 2022; De Giacomo et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2025). These findings suggest that systemic and neural iron imbalance may contribute to synaptic dysfunction, oxidative stress, and behavioral phenotypes associated with autism (Table 1). Clinical evidence of altered iron status and iron-related biomarkers in ASD

This review examines the possible involvement of iron in the etiology of ASD, focusing on the effects of impaired iron homeostasis in the prenatal and postnatal brain development.

Iron metabolism in the fetus

Iron contributes to mitochondrial respiration and cellular energy production through its role in cytochromes and electron transport chain complexes (Dutt et al. 2022). In addition, iron is required for heme synthesis and for the function of oxygen-binding proteins such as hemoglobin and myoglobin, which support oxygen transport and storage. Iron also contributes to cytochrome P450 activity and hormone (Günther 2023). Furthermore, iron is essential for cell proliferation and growth in epithelial tissue, immune cells, neurons, and other cell types (Teh et al. 2024), as well as for DNA replication and repair through iron-dependent enzymes such as ribonucleotide reductase and helicase cofactors (Federico et al. 2022). In the nervous tissue, iron participates in neural stem cell proliferation and differentiation, axonal growth, myelination, and maintenance of oligodendrocyte functional activity (Cheli et al. 2020). Iron is also involved in mitochondrial energy metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis and receptor function (Kulaszyńska et al. 2024), antioxidant defense and neuroprotection, modulation of microglial activity, and synaptic plasticity, including pathways associated with brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) signaling (Tran et al. 2020). Large amounts of iron are found in the basal ganglia, substantia nigra, and cerebellum, likely reflecting the high metabolic demand and active neurotransmitter synthesis in these regions (Ghaderi et al. 2023; McCann et al. 2020). Excess intracellular iron is primarily stored in ferritin and can be exported from the brain through ferroportin-1-mediated pathways (Kotla et al. 2022). From the first days of prenatal development, the fetus needs iron. The maternal organism is the only source of iron for the developing fetus, providing iron through placental transport. Iron transport from mother to fetus through the placenta is a tightly regulated process. Data suggests a hierarchy of iron usage prioritizing the fetus first, followed by maternal hematocrit, and lastly, maternal iron reserves (Sangkhae et al. 2023).

Iron in the maternal blood is bound to transferrin. The placenta has transferrin receptors (TfR1) on the surface of syncytiotrophoblasts, which bind to transferrin (Sangkhae & Nemeth 2019), enabling receptor-mediated endocytosis and facilitating the entry of transferrin (Guo et al. 2025). Inside the endosome, ferrireductase (STEAP3) reduces Fe3+ in transferrin to Fe2+, which is transported into the cytoplasm via divalent metal transporter (DMT1) (Fig. 2) (Cao & O’Brien 2013). Iron in the syncytiotrophoblast can be stored in ferritin, used for placental metabolism, or transported to fetal circulation (Khorami-Sarvestani et al. 2024; O’Brien 2022). The latter occurs via ferroportin, which is regulated by hepcidin, a liver-derived peptide (Nemeth & Ganz 2021). In pregnancy, hepcidin is down-regulated, promoting iron transfer to the fetus, especially in the third trimester, when the fetus needs iron most (Sangkhae et al. 2020). In maternal iron deficiency, placental ferroportin expression increases to enhance iron transport. In iron overload and inflammation, hepcidin levels rise, which reduces ferroportin-mediated transport (Nemeth & Ganz 2023).

In fetal circulation, most iron is bound to transferrin in Fe3+ form. In full-term neonates, transferrin has a high saturation level (approximately 50%) (Cross et al. 2020), and transferrin receptors are expressed predominantly in erythropoietic organs, the liver, and the CNS (Guo et al. 2025). A small fraction of iron can circulate in a non-transferrin-bound form. In the fetus, the liver ferritin is the main storage of iron (Jatavan et al. 2022). In the third semester, the liver also accumulates extra iron in ferritin, ensuring a postnatal growth rate in the limited availability of dietary iron (Kämmerer et al. 2020). Most of the iron in the fetal period is used for hem synthesis and erythropoiesis at 3–8 (synthesis of embryonic hemoglobins by primitive erythroblasts), 8–24 (fetal erythropoiesis in the liver), and from 24 weeks (red blood cells are predominantly produced in the bone marrow) of gestation (Zych-Krekora et al. 2025). The iron is incorporated into the structure of the heme in the last step of the synthesis, catalyzed by ferrochelatase (Dutt et al. 2022).

The transport of iron from the fetal circulation to the fetal brain is carefully regulated by the BBB. Iron (Fe2+) is transported through ferroportin 1 from the luminal membrane of endothelial cells of the BBB to the abluminal membrane (Baumann et al. 2019). Endothelial cells further regulate transferrin-bound iron intake by altering the expression and integration of transferrin receptor 1, as well as modulating vesicular transport (Campos-Escamilla 2021). Once iron reaches the brain's interstitium, it is oxidized to a safer Fe3+ form by hephestin or ceruloplasmin and incorporated into transferrin for uptake by neurons and glia (Chen et al. 2025a). Iron can cross the BBB via lactoferrin receptor, glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored melanotransferrin receptor, transcytosis, or cerebrospinal fluid. Neurons and glia take up iron through transferrin 1 receptor or DMT1 (Fig. 1) (Wang et al. 2025b).

This process is mediated by astrocytes, a key component of the BBB, by buffering iron. They take up transferrin-bound iron using transferrin receptor 1 and non-transferrin-bound iron mainly through DMT1 (Cheli et al. 2020). Astrocytes also express ceruloplasmin and hephaestin, facilitating the active Fe2+ to Fe3+ conversion, enabling iron distribution across nervous tissue and reducing the risk of reactive oxygen species formation (Cheli et al. 2023; Yanase et al. 2020). The iron is stored in ferritin and further distributed to neurons. In case of iron intake deficiency, export and distribution to oligodendrocytes and neurons are reduced (Kulaszyńska et al. 2024), disturbing energy metabolism, antioxidant system, neurotransmitter synthesis in the neurons, and myelin synthesis in the oligodendrocytes (Chen et al. 2025a).

Neurodevelopment in maternal and early postnatal iron imbalance

Recent works have reported that maternal and early postnatal iron deficiencies are associated with a high risk of ASD, suggesting iron dysregulation as a modifiable environmental contributor to its etiology (Benson et al. 2022; Georgieff 2023b). Thus, the disturbed prenatal and postnatal iron metabolism may have long-lasting effects on neurodevelopment (Zych-Krekora et al. 2025).

The iron imbalance in the fetal brain may be associated with either maternal iron deficiency or maternal or fetal single or combined deficiency of iron intake and utilization systems. Altered expression and polymorphisms of genes involved in iron transport, storage, export, and utilization have been reported in ASD, potentially contributing to impaired iron availability, abnormal intracellular iron accumulation, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis in the developing brain. TF (transferrin) deficiency affects iron transport across the BBB (Chiou et al. 2019). Mutations in the FTH1/FTL (ferritin heavy and light chains) impair storage of the iron and increase oxidative vulnerability (Srivastava et al. 2023). Dysregulation of SLC40A1 (ferroportin) affects the efflux of iron, causing intraneuronal accumulation of iron and ferroptotic cell death (Uguen et al. 2023). Animal models prove that reduced activity of ceruloplasmin (CP), involved in iron export, impairs iron metabolism and increases oxidative stress (Kedari et al. 2026). Genetic polymorphisms of TFRC (Transferrin receptor 1) alter iron availability in the developing brain (Petralla et al. 2024). GPX (glutathione peroxidase) prevents lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, while SOD2 (mitochondrial superoxide dismutase) is an antioxidant enzyme that regulates ROS production (Forcina & Dixon 2019). Collectively, these alterations suggest that dysregulation of iron transport, storage, export, and antioxidant defense systems may contribute to impaired iron homeostasis, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis-related neurodevelopmental alterations in ASD (Table 2). Children with ASD have been shown to exhibit lower serum ferritin and hemoglobin levels, as well as altered transferrin saturation (De Giacomo et al. 2022; Gunes et al. 2017). In addition, MRI studies demonstrate atypical iron distribution in the basal ganglia, cortex, and cerebellum, regions involved in social cognition, sensory processing, and motor control (Du et al. 2024; Spence et al. 2020). Elevated magnetic susceptibility associated with iron accumulation has been reported in children with ASD in the temporal, parietal, and occipital gyri, right insula, and rostral anterior cingulate gyrus compared with typically developing children (Xu et al. 2025), as well as in the right substantia nigra, where these alterations correlate with social and communicative deficits and reward-seeking behavior (Itahashi et al. 2025).

Microglia are highly involved in iron recycling. As resident macrophages of the central nervous system, microglia recycle iron derived from degraded cells and myelin (Nnah & Wessling-Resnick 2018). ron content influences microglial activation status: iron-rich microglia exhibit a predominantly pro-inflammatory phenotype (M1), whereas low-iron microglia are associated with an anti-inflammatory phenotype (M2) (McIntosh et al. 2019). Alterations in iron levels may therefore induce abnormal microglial activation, increasing hypersensitivity and promoting chronic neuroinflammation. Dysregulated microglia may also fail to appropriately eliminate or remodel synapses during neurodevelopment, contributing to synaptic pruning abnormalities described in ASD (Michels et al. 2025; Ward et al. 2022).

Neurons are almost entirely dependent on astrocytes and indirectly on microglia for iron. The impaired uptake of iron by astrocytes affects neuronal metabolism. Iron participates as a cofactor in the synthesis and metabolism of dopamine, serotonin, and noradrenaline. The poor supply of iron reduces the activity of tyrosine and tryptophan hydroxylases, monoamine oxidase, disturbing the balance and regulation of neurotransmitters (Baumann et al. 2024), as described in the pathogenesis of autism. Animal studies have shown that an iron-deficient diet downregulates dopamine transporter gene expression (Rhoten et al. 2025) (SLC6A3). Iron is a cofactor for fatty acid desaturases, essential for myelin synthesis. Iron deficiency reduces myelination, weakening neuronal connectivity (Cheli et al. 2020).

Iron deficiency primarily impairs DNA replication and repair, considering the wide involvement of several iron-dependent enzymes, such as ribonucleotide reductase, demethylases, helicases, glycosylases, and nucleases into these processes. DNA polymerase stability relies on Fe-S (iron-sulfur) clusters found in its cysteine-rich motifs (Zhang 2014). Beyond replication, iron is involved in epigenetic regulation. In this context, the Jumonji family and Ten-Eleven Translocation (TET1-3) family proteins (histone demethylases) can be highlighted. These iron and alpha-ketoglutarate-dependent proteins mediate histone demethylation, sequentially oxidizing 5-methylcytosine to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, 5-formylcytosine, and 5-carboxycytosine, enabling thymine DNA glycosylase to excise modified bases and restore cytosine. In iron deficiency, the activity of Jumonji and TET proteins is reduced, altering the expression of the neurodevelopmental factor genes (Barks et al. 2022, 2021; Sarah & Fujimori 2023). The outcome of impaired histone demethylation includes a smaller neuronal pool and premature differentiation of neural progenitor cells. Maternal iron deficiency is associated with reduced hippocampal volume, lower serum BDNF concentrations, and established correlations between maternal hemoglobin, hippocampal volume, and BDNF levels (Basu et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2025a).

Iron participates as a cofactor for tyrosine and tryptophan hydroxylases; its deficiency affects the synthesis of dopamine and serotonin. Indirectly, iron imbalance affects several neurotransmitter synthesis enzymes due to impaired energy metabolism, enzyme regulation, gene expression, and oxidative stress (Berthou et al. 2022; Harutyunyan et al. 2021).

The high oxygenation of nervous tissue is associated with the high-energy demands of neurons. For energy production, the electrons are transported between four complexes. I, II, and III complexes contain Fe-S clusters; III and IV complexes contain cytochrome (hemoproteins) b, c1, c, a, and a3 (Cogliati et al. 2021). Iron deficiency reduces ATP production, which negatively affects synaptogenesis and neurogenesis (Gao et al. 2021). High oxygenation level of nervous tissue increases production of reactive oxygen species, which, in the deficiency of iron, cannot be effectively neutralized, due to reduced functioning of iron-dependent superoxide dismutase, found in autism patients (Yenkoyan et al. 2018). As a result, reactive oxygen species damage membranes, DNA, and increase the risk of apoptosis and reduce cell survival (Galaris et al. 2019).

On a structural level, several brain regions show altered development. The development of the hippocampus, a brain region implicated in learning, memory, and social behavior, is a key event in a normally developing fetus. The hippocampus, and particularly the subgranular zone, has a high rate of cell proliferation (Jurkowski et al. 2020). It was revealed that children with ASD have increased hippocampal volume compared with typically developing individuals. Moreover, right versus left hippocampal volume differences were greater in ASD. Differences between males and females were not identified (Reinhardt et al. 2020). A similar pattern was observed for the amygdala, showing an early increase in volume in ASD compared with typically developing individuals, in which it has longer postnatal volumetric maturation. At the age of three, the amygdala is significantly larger in children with ASD, compared to typically developing individuals (Amaral & Nordahl 2022).

Brain region-specific changes in altered metabolism of iron in autism

Animal models of autism show shorter, disorganized CA1 apical dendrites and reduced expression of mitochondrial and synaptic genes (Bastian et al. 2016). Resulting deficits include impairments in episodic memory, poor spatial navigation, reduced neurogenesis and synaptogenesis, as well as social avoidance.

In autism, several region-specific effects were identified encompassing the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, amygdala, striatum, basal ganglia, and cerebellum. Iron deficiency impairs chromatin modifications, affecting CREB/BDNF (cAMP response element binding protein/brain-derived neurotrophic factor) signaling, which, in turn, impacts dendritic growth, synapse formation, and the complexity of dendritic arbors (Mahmoud et al. 2024). Key transcription factors that are downregulated include NeuroD1, which negatively affects the transition of neuronal progenitor cells into post-mitotic neurons and disrupts dentate gyrus development (Pavlinkova & Smolik 2024). Pax6 downregulation affects the proliferation of progenitor cells, cortical and hippocampal layering, and the balance of glutamate and GABA (Zhang et al. 2025). Sox2 downregulation affects neuronal progenitor cells' multipotency and proliferation via HIF-1 and mitochondrial function (Mercurio et al. 2022).

On the level of the prefrontal cortex, energy deficits, impaired neurotransmitter synthesis, and reduced myelination converge with several molecular disruptions. Iron-dependent phosphorylation of FOXP2 impairs its transcriptional activity and affects genes involved in language acquisition and social communication (Yalcintepe et al. 2021). Disruption of MEK/ERK signaling inhibits synaptic pruning and causes cortical thinning, causing cortical inflexibility (Murari et al. 2023). The altered RNA demethylation reduces the stability of FMR1 mRNA, showing phenotypes of impaired learning, adaptation, and synaptic remodeling (Greenblatt & Spradling 2018).

The striatum expresses high ferritin levels for the buffering of iron. Iron deficiency alters ferritin expression, increases free iron, and risk of oxidative damage. Dysregulated mTORC1 signaling impairs cell growth, synaptic plasticity, autophagy and pruning, resulting in excessive dendritic spines and hyperactive circuits (Shang et al. 2020; Szwed et al. 2021). Links to ASD-risk genes (TSC1/TSC2, PTEN, FMR1) amplify these effects, producing social deficits, repetitive behaviors, rigidity, and motor incoordination (DeSpenza et al. 2021; Pivina et al. 2019).

In the cerebellum, iron imbalance disrupts calcium homeostasis, arborization, GSK3b activity, and Wnt signaling in Purkinje cells, impairs cerebellar patterning, motor learning, coordination, and posture (Armstrong et al. 2022).

The connection between iron imbalance during prenatal and early postnatal development and increased ASD risk is supported by convergent epidemiological, neuroimaging, experimental, and mechanistic evidence. The developing brain undergoes rapid iron-dependent processes, including neurogenesis, neuronal migration, synaptogenesis, myelination, and maturation of dopaminergic pathways, during which even marginal iron deficiency may induce persistent neurodevelopmental alterations (Felt & Tran 2025; Georgieff 2023a; Zhao et al. 2025).

Maternal iron deficiency anemia diagnosed before 30 weeks of gestation has been associated with significantly increased ASD risk in offspring, particularly in cases accompanied by intellectual disability. Low maternal dietary iron intake and insufficient supplementation may further increase ASD susceptibility, especially in the presence of advanced maternal age or maternal metabolic conditions, including obesity, diabetes, and hypertension (Benson et al. 2022; Moyle 2025). These associations remain significant after adjustment for socioeconomic status, maternal education, and other nutritional factors (Rezaeizadeh et al. 2024).

As illustrated in Fig. 3, iron demand peaks during late prenatal (25–40 weeks) and early postnatal (0–6 months) developmental periods across multiple brain regions involved in ASD-relevant neural circuits. During these developmental windows, the fetal and neonatal brain accumulates approximately 80% of its total iron content (Zhao et al. 2025). Iron deficiency during these periods may preferentially affect several vulnerable brain regions and neurodevelopmental processes. In the basal ganglia and substantia nigra, impaired tyrosine hydroxylase activity may disrupt dopamine synthesis and signaling, potentially contributing to repetitive behaviors and attention deficit (Fiani et al. 2025). In the hippocampus, iron deficiency has been associated with altered BDNF expression, impaired dendritic arborization, and disruption of excitatory/inhibitory balance linked to social and cognitive impairments (Bhamidimarri et al. 2026). Neuroimaging studies in children with ASD frequently demonstrate altered iron content in deep gray matter nuclei, including the substantia nigra and globus pallidus, measured using quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM), with some studies reporting correlations with symptom severity. Mendelian randomization analyses additionally support a potential causal relationship between reduced iron availability and ASD risk (Zhou et al. 2025).

Importantly, perinatal iron deficiency may induce long-lasting neurodevelopmental alterations. Animal models of gestational iron restriction demonstrate persistent abnormalities in excitatory signaling, social behavior, and prepulse inhibition even after postnatal iron repletion, suggesting incomplete functional recovery following early iron deficiency (Sangkhae et al. 2019).

Collectively, these findings suggest that suboptimal iron status during critical developmental windows may act as a neurodevelopmental programming factor that increases vulnerability to ASD, particularly in the presence of genetic susceptibility or additional environmental stressors. These observations further support the importance of early screening and optimization of iron nutrition from preconception through early postnatal development as potential modifiable strategies for reducing neurodevelopmental risk.

Iron deficiency is prevalent in individuals with ASD (Masini et al. 2020) and represents a modifiable risk factor for autism. Maternal iron deficiency driven by dietary insufficiency, genetic variants of iron transport and/or storage or inflammation-induced hepcidin upregulation can affect fetal neurodevelopment across multiple pathways (Fig. 4). Addressing gestational iron status may offer a preventive strategy for autism, which requires further investigation. A reduced iron supply to the fetus can be caused by mutations in transferrin, its receptor, and ferroportin. The deficits of iron in the fetus affect energy metabolism, reducing hemoglobin synthesis and oxygen supply and synthesis of cytochromes, directly involved in the energy production and hydroxylation (Gao et al. 2019). Deficits of hydroxylation may induce neurotransmitter imbalance, affecting levels of dopamine and serotonin (Marotta et al. 2020). In the third trimester of prenatal development, the fetal organism increases iron storage, which is primarily associated with increased iron need in the first months of life and reduced amounts of iron in the food (Sangkhae et al. 2023). Iron deficiency is common in preterm infants. Along with maternal iron deficiency, iron deficiency in the neonatal and postnatal period is associated with impaired functioning of iron transport in the gastrointestinal tract, associated with deficits in DMT1 and ferroportin functioning (Settanni et al. 2021). Genetic deficits in transferrin and its receptor may prevent sufficient iron transfer to the brain (Campos-Escamilla 2021). In later postnatal periods, infants may avoid certain types of iron-rich foods, causing iron deficits (Yanagimoto et al. 2020).Fig. 4Key points of iron dysregulation and associated autism manifestations in prenatal and postnatal periods.

Individuals with both ASD and iron deficiency may often experience heightened difficulties in social interactions, increased restlessness, and irritability (Ferreira et al. 2019; German & Juul 2021). For instance, a child with ASD who experiences iron deficiency may find engaging in social activities or communicating effectively with others even more challenging (Marinov et al. 2024).

Iron overload and ASD: implications of excess brain iron

Although iron deficiency is more frequently observed in individuals with autism, certain subgroups of ASD also show iron overload or region-specific iron accumulation in the brain. Iron overload is associated with oxidative stress, cytotoxicity, ferroptosis, and neuroinflammation, accompanied with further reduction in executive function (Spence et al. 2020).

While iron is essential for normal brain function and development, an excess can have significant implications for individuals with ASD. Excessive iron levels in the brain can lead to oxidative stress and neuroinflammation (Cheng et al. 2022), both of which have been implicated in the pathophysiology of ASD. Elevated iron levels can produce ROS and reactive nitrogen species, leading to cellular damage and inflammation. Neuroinflammation is increasingly recognized as a contributing factor in ASD, potentially linked to the observed behavioral and cognitive symptoms (Liu et al. 2022b).

Excess iron accumulation can damage neurons, interfere with synaptic plasticity, and disrupt neurotransmitter systems (Sousa et al. 2020). These effects may contribute to the neurological and cognitive challenges often observed in individuals with ASD. Iron overload in the brain can lead to altered connectivity patterns, potentially affecting the neural circuits involved in social interactions and communication, which are areas of particular concern in ASD (Rabaya et al. 2023). Changes in connectivity can exacerbate the difficulties individuals with ASD face in understanding and responding to social cues. Elevated brain iron levels can result in changes in behavior and cognition. In individuals with ASD, these changes may be particularly relevant as they can further exacerbate existing symptoms. Such changes may include increased impulsivity, irritability, and cognitive difficulties (Chen et al. 2022; Spence et al. 2020).

In children with autism, neural progenitor cells show accumulation of iron and high levels of oxidative stress, as well as increased risk of ferroptosis, which is iron-dependent programmed cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation. Surprisingly, these cells resist ferroptosis, possibly through upregulated glutathione peroxidase 4, promoting excessive proliferation and brain overgrowth. This explains disproportionate megalencephaly in ASD children with iron overload (Chen et al. 2025b). Ferroptosis-related genes are differentially expressed in ASD, forming distinct molecular clusters with immune infiltration differences. Higher ferroptosis profile correlates with immune activation, supporting that dysregulated iron-driven cell death contributes to neuroinflammation in ASD (Liu et al. 2022a).

Excess iron induces the Fenton reaction, which contributes to the production of ROS, damaging DNA, lipids, and proteins. The consequence in the form of lipid peroxidation depletes glutathione and glutathione peroxidase, triggers ferroptosis, neuronal cytotoxicity, and mitochondrial dysfunction (Ru et al. 2024). Animal models of autism have shown that ferroptosis can be inhibited by selenium supplementation, which activates the Nrf2/GPX4 pathway, reducing hippocampal ferroptosis and autistic-like behaviors, such as repetitive actions and social deficits (Wu et al. 2022).

Summarizing, it can be highlighted that systemic iron overload is not common in autism, hence region-specific accumulation may promote oxidative stress and ferroptosis, damaging neurons and causing inflammation.

Iron dysregulation management

The therapeutic approaches can be subdivided into iron excess and deficiency management strategies. For each developmental period, a specific strategy may be considered.

Iron deficiency management in the prenatal period is primarily limited to maternal iron deficiency management. The common therapeutic strategy involves iron supplementation (Abu-Ouf & Jan 2015). The common forms include ferrous sulfate, ferrous fumarate, and ferrous gluconate. These therapeutic forms are commonly involved in vitamin supplements for the prenatal period (Falahati et al. 2022). However, careful monitoring is required to avoid iron overload. There is a concern that an overdose of iron supplements may induce the release of non-protein-bound iron, which may cause reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Recently, the safety of ferrous sulfate not producing ROS was demonstrated (Brittenham et al. 2014). Even in maternal iron overload, there is a regulation of intestinal absorption of iron. The transfer of iron from enterocytes to the blood occurs via ferroportin, which is downregulated via hepcidin, a liver-derived hormone (Ginzburg 2019). The iron absorbed in the enterocytes is accumulated in the ferritin, which can further be removed during desquamation (Anderson et al. 2012). The expression of hepcidin is increased in inflammation and infections, which increases the importance of maternal follow-up (Wang & Babitt 2016).

In early-stage iron deficiency or in reduced maternal iron stores, dietary interventions may help recover deficiency. This includes the consumption of iron-fortified foods. In ASD, a balanced diet may have several benefits in symptom management. In the late stages of the pregnancy, the fetal organism initiates increased iron accumulation in the ferritin. This ensures the iron balance in the neonate in the lack of dietary diversity during the first months of life (Amadi et al. 2022). In preterm infants, lower amounts of iron are stored in ferritin, which increases the chances of iron dysregulation, causing disturbances in iron metabolism (Fisher & Nemeth 2017). Recently, the delayed umbilical cord clamping has been used in preterm infants to continue blood flow between the placenta and the newborn. Results obtained support the hypothesis that from 30 s to 5 min of delayed umbilical cord clamping reduces the risk of anemia and further need for blood transfusions (Rabe et al. 2019).

To ensure iron homeostasis in newborns, neonatal iron screening can be a good practice, which prevents the consequences of iron imbalance. If required, the iron supplementation can be initiated directly in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) (McCarthy et al. 2022). In the postnatal period, gastrointestinal issues may cause iron dysregulation; thus, the timely diagnosis and treatment of such diseases can prevent further complications. In mild to severe iron deficiency, iron supplementation may be prescribed for children with autism (Katz et al. 2021).

In the approved diagnosis of ASD, the complex treatment includes dietary interventions, use of chelators, and treatment of diseases causing iron imbalance, which may improve the symptoms of autism and prevent iron and other dietary factor-associated disturbances in nervous tissue and periphery (Al-Beltagi 2024; Entezari et al. 2022).

Iron excess is less common than iron deficiency; therefore, it is a potentially dangerous state that affects the organism's antioxidant balance. Chelation therapy is a common intervention for managing iron and other metal overload (Entezari et al. 2022). The principle of chelation therapy is based on the use of chelation agents that bind to excess iron in the body, facilitating its removal. While chelation therapy has been used for other conditions associated with metal toxicity, primarily lead and mercury, its application in ASD remains controversial (Alum & Uti 2025; Balali-Mood et al. 2021).

In recent years, personalized approaches in the treatment of diseases, such as autism, have been gradually coming to the forefront. These include determining suitable dosages based on biomarkers or clinical indicators necessary for effective supplementation (Kittana et al. 2023). Further research is needed to assess the impact of iron supplementation on ASD-specific symptoms, such as communication deficits and repetitive behaviors.

It is important to recognize that iron-based interventions should be integrated into a comprehensive care plan that addresses the multifaceted nature of ASD. Therapies and interventions related to behavioral, communication, and sensory challenges should continue to play a central role in managing ASD. Integrating iron-based interventions with these existing therapies can form a more holistic approach to addressing ASD symptoms (Prakash et al. 2021).

Conclusion

The relationship between iron within the CNS and ASD represents a complex and multifaceted area of research with significant implications for understanding ASD etiology, symptomatology, and potential therapeutic strategies. This review focused on the role of iron in prenatal, neonatal, and postnatal neurodevelopment, highlighting how disturbances in iron homeostasis and related metabolic pathways may contribute to ASD pathophysiology.

Iron plays an essential role in neural development and function, influencing mitochondrial energy metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis, myelination, antioxidant defense, and neural plasticity. Consequently, iron imbalance may disrupt these processes and contribute to cognitive, behavioral, and neurodevelopmental alterations associated with ASD.

Current evidence suggests that iron deficiency is more prevalent in individuals with ASD than in the general population, supporting the importance of monitoring iron status during critical developmental periods. Potential interventions may include nutritional screening, iron supplementation, dietary modification, and the use of iron-fortified foods, with individualized approaches tailored to each patient’s metabolic and clinical profile.

At the same time, iron overload may promote oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, ferroptosis, and neuronal injury, potentially exacerbating ASD-associated symptoms and comorbidities. Interactions between iron and other trace elements, including copper, further emphasize the complexity of metal homeostasis within the developing brain. Neuroimaging findings demonstrating altered regional brain iron distribution additionally support the potential involvement of iron dysregulation in ASD-related behavioral and cognitive alterations.

Nevertheless, currently available findings remain heterogeneous and do not support a singular pathogenic role of iron dysregulation in ASD. Autism is a complex multifactorial disorder in which iron imbalance likely interacts with broader genetic, epigenetic, metabolic, neuroimmune, and environmental factors. Further mechanistic, longitudinal, and translational studies are required to clarify whether iron dysregulation represents a causal factor, secondary consequence, or modifying component of ASD pathophysiology.

Despite these complexities, continued investigation of iron homeostasis in ASD may contribute to improved biomarker identification, earlier detection of metabolic vulnerabilities, and development of more targeted preventive and therapeutic approaches for individuals affected by ASD.
