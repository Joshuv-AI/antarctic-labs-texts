Response to “Enhancing the Study of Long-Term Pulmonary Sequelae After COVID-19: Methodological Perspectives on Imaging and Clinical Integration”
Authors: Jiyoung Song, Soon Ho Yoon
Published: 2026

We appreciate the interest of Dong et al. [1] in our recent publication and their thoughtful comments on future research directions for post-COVID-19 pulmonary sequelae [2]. While we agree that quantitative CT, closer integration of imaging with clinical outcomes, artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted standardization, and refined radiological phenotyping may strengthen future prospective studies, these approaches were beyond the scope of our study, which was designed to provide a study-level meta-analysis of the currently available long-term follow-up literature.

Their letter [1] suggested the use of quantitative CT descriptors and deep learning-based reconstruction algorithms to detect subtle parenchymal abnormalities. We agree that quantitative CT metrics may improve sensitivity, reproducibility, and comparability across studies [3]. However, most studies included in our review relied on qualitative visual assessments, and quantitative CT variables were not reported in a sufficiently standardized manner to permit pooled analysis. Because our meta-analysis was based on published study-level data, retrospective application of quantitative AI methods was not feasible.

The authors also proposed linking radiological and physiological findings with clinical outcomes using machine learning models. We agree that such an integration would enhance clinical relevance, and recent longitudinal studies support associations between persistent abnormalities and symptoms or physiological impairments [4]. Because our analysis was based on aggregated study-level data, rather than individual patient data, direct evaluation of associations between imaging features and clinical outcomes was not feasible in our study.

We also agree that AI-driven tools may help standardize CT interpretation and reduce inter-reader variability in future multicenter studies. However, our meta-analysis synthesized heterogeneous follow-up studies published between 2020 and 2024, and AI-driven approaches were neither consistently applied nor reported in a standardized form across the included literature. Given these constraints, our analytical approach is methodologically appropriate for evidence synthesis using available data.

While AI-based approaches are promising, no widely validated framework currently exists for harmonized applications across heterogeneous multicenter datasets specifically addressing long-term post-COVID-19 outcomes. When such tools become available, rigorous standardization and external validation will be required to permit meaningful incorporation into evidence synthesis. These suggestions thus remain aspirational rather than operational within the constraints of the currently available published data.

Regarding radiological interpretations, we concur that persistent radiological abnormalities may reflect heterogeneous pathological processes rather than a uniform “fibrosis.” Prior studies have shown a spectrum of post-COVID-19 residual lung abnormalities on CT, including organizing lung injury, residual inflammation, reticulation, parenchymal bands, bronchial dilatation, and fibrotic changes, and fibrotic-like findings should not be automatically interpreted as irreversible progressive fibrosis [5,6]. Our conclusion was not that all persistent CT abnormalities represent irreversible fibrosis, but rather that abnormalities described as fibrosis in the literature tended to persist over time with limited evidence of resolution, suggesting a stable, non-progressive pattern at the population level. As the included studies rarely provided pathological correlations or sufficiently granular phenotypic subclassifications, a more detailed distinction among these entities was not possible in the present meta-analysis.

In conclusion, future primary research would benefit from standardized quantitative imaging, clinically integrated longitudinal outcomes, and more refined radiological phenotyping. Our study aimed to provide a methodologically sound synthesis of the currently available long-term evidence on post-COVID-19 pulmonary sequelae, which will serve as a useful baseline for future prospective investigations.
