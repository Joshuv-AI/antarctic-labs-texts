Continuous Monophasic Production of Pharmaceutical-Grade 5‑Hydroxymethylfurfural from Sugar Syrup
Authors: Nattee Akkarawatkhoosith, Kritsanalak Thongkan, Attasak Jaree, Tiprawee Tongtummachat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A continuous-flow, monophasic catalytic process for the direct production of 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (5-HMF) from sugarcane syrup was developed using a microreactor platform to enable sustainable, pharmaceutical-compatible manufacturing. In contrast to conventional mineral-acid or biphasic systems, green citric acid was employed as a sustainable catalytic alternative. Low-toxicity solventsdeionized water, acetone, n-propanol, and isopropanolwere systematically evaluated to balance reaction performance and downstream compatibility. The results were benchmarked against a conventional solvent system. Key process parameters, including reaction temperature, residence time, catalyst loading, organic-to-aqueous (O:A) ratio, initial syrup concentration, and salt addition, were investigated and optimized using response surface methodology. Under the optimized conditions (15 wt % citric acid, 170 °C, O:A ratio of 0.25:1, and a residence time of 80 min), a maximum 5-HMF selectivity of 43.9% was achieved in a monophasic acetone-based system. A simplified downstream strategy combining sequential selective adsorption with solvent evaporation enabled purification of 5-HMF to 99% purity without complex extraction or biphasic separation steps. Compared with related systems, superior production and purification performance was observed. By combining green catalysis, continuous microreactor processing, direct syrup utilization, and simplified downstream purification, the proposed strategy offers a promising pathway to pharmaceutical-grade 5-HMF production, with reduced separation complexity and enhanced process intensification.

Introduction

5-Hydroxymethylfurfural (5-HMF) is a versatile biomass-derived platform chemical with broad applications in biobased polymers, fuel precursors, food additives, and pharmaceuticals. , In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward its pharmaceutical potential. When administered in appropriate doses, 5-HMF has been reported to be nontoxic and suitable for use as an active pharmaceutical ingredient (API). , Several pharmacological studies have demonstrated its diverse biological activities. For example, Li et al. (2015) reported that 5-HMF protects the liver from alcohol-induced oxidative damage, while Ya et al. (2012) found that it prolonged survival in rats with cerebral ischemia. Other investigations have revealed additional therapeutic potential, including anticancer, antisickling, and antiallergenic properties, as well as beneficial effects on exercise performance and surgical outcomes when used as a dietary supplement. , Collectively, these findings highlight 5-HMF as a promising biobased molecule with significant potential for pharmaceutical and biomedical applications.

In recent years, the development of 5-HMF production processes has primarily focused on improving yield and selectivity by modifying and optimizing catalysts and organic solvents. However, most reported systems employ reaction media or catalysts that are unsuitable for pharmaceutical applications. Conventional biphasic systems commonly use mineral acids, such as hydrochloric acid (HCl) and sulfuric acid (H2SO4), along with toxic organic extractants, including methyl isobutyl ketone (MIBK), dimethylacetamide (DMAc), and dimethylformamide (DMF). These reagents pose considerable environmental, health, and safety concerns due to their corrosivity, volatility, and potential carcinogenicity, making them incompatible with pharmaceutical manufacturing standards. In addition, biphasic systems often require multistep downstream operations, including solvent recovery and phase separation, to isolate 5-HMF from both aqueous and organic phases. Such operations not only increase process complexity and cost but also generate additional waste streams. Indeed, Zheng et al. (2020) noted that downstream separation and purification of 5-HMF account for the largest share of overall production costs (60–70%) and are more challenging and energy-intensive than the synthesis itself.

To address these limitations, homogeneous monophasic systems have recently emerged as a promising alternative. In a homogeneous medium, all reactants, catalysts, and solvents coexist in a single phase, ensuring uniform mixing and efficient mass transfer without interfacial limitations. Although monophasic systems typically yield lower 5-HMF yields and selectivity compared with biphasic systems, they offer significant advantages, including simpler process design, reduced environmental impact, and compatibility with continuous-flow operation.

In this study, a homogeneous, catalytic, monophasic system was developed, with a focus on pharmaceutical compatibility and sustainability. Environmentally benign solvents that meet pharmaceutical safety standardsacetone, 1-propanol (1-PrOH), 2-propanol (IPA), and tetrahydrofuran (THF)were examined for their ability to form homogeneous media and facilitate the efficient dehydration of sugars. The use of low-boiling-point solvents also offers practical advantages for downstream separation, as they reduce the energy consumption required for solvent recovery and product purification. Furthermore, citric acid, a naturally occurring, nontoxic organic acid, was selected as a catalyst to replace mineral acids, thereby enabling a fully green, biocompatible route for 5-HMF production suitable for pharmaceutical and nutraceutical applications.

5-HMF can be produced by dehydrating various C6-based carbohydrates, including fructose, glucose, and sucrose. Among these, fructose is often considered the preferred feedstock, as it predominantly exists in the more reactive furanose form compared with other hexoses. However, because of the high cost of fructose, several studies have investigated alternative, less expensive sugars, such as glucose and sucrose. Lignocellulosic biomass, including corn stover, wheat straw, sugarcane bagasse, rice husk, and woody residues, has also been explored as a low-cost feedstock for producing 5-HMF. Nevertheless, their conversion efficiency is limited by the complex, heterogeneous composition of these materials, which hinders catalytic performance and increases processing difficulty. For pharmaceutical applications, syrup represents a suitable and cost-effective feedstock, as it consists mainly of fructose, glucose, and sucrosesimple sugars that are inexpensive and readily available. Previous studies have demonstrated the efficient conversion of syrup into 5-HMF under various catalytic systems. Additionally, syrup is widely used in pharmaceutical formulations as an oral dosage form to enhance the taste, viscosity, and stability of active ingredients. Therefore, in this work, syrup was selected as the raw material for producing 5-HMF.

Compared to conventional batch reactors, particularly at large scales, continuous-flow processing in microreactors offers several advantages, including markedly enhanced heat and mass transfer rates, leading to higher product yields. In recent years, microreactors have been extensively investigated for the synthesis of 5-HMF in both monophasic and biphasic systems, consistently demonstrating superior performance relative to traditional reactors. One of the key benefits is the extremely short residence time required to achieve high conversion and selectivity. For example, in our previous study, the conversion of glucose to 5-HMF in a biphasic microreactor system catalyzed by hydrochloric acid achieved a 5-HMF yield of 81.7% within only 3 min. In contrast, conventional systems typically require 30–120 min to reach comparable yields. Therefore, in this work, a continuous-flow monophasic microreactor system was selected for green, pharmaceutically compatible conditions.

The addition of inorganic salts was also investigated to enhance 5-HMF production. In biphasic systems, salts can induce salting-out, promoting phase separation and enhancing extraction efficiency. In monophasic systems, however, their role is primarily to modify the reaction environment, influencing factors such as acidity, ionic strength, and sugar-dehydration kinetics to optimize 5-HMF yields. In this work, the effects of sodium chloride (NaCl) and ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) as additives were examined.

This study aimed to develop a continuous-flow monophasic process for 5-HMF production in a microreactor designed for pharmaceutical applications. The work focused on achieving a nontoxic, nonhazardous, cost-effective, and efficient approach that delivers high yield and selectivity. Key process parameters, including reaction temperature, residence time, solvent volume, feed concentration, and catalyst loading, were systematically optimized to maximize 5-HMF yield. The production performance of the developed system was compared with that of conventional techniques. Furthermore, downstream processing yielded a high-purity 5-HMF product (99%), confirming the feasibility of this approach for pharmaceutical-grade applications.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Concentrated sugarcane syrup was purchased from Mitr Phol Group. The syrup contained approximately 24% sucrose, 37% glucose, and 38% fructose. 5-HMF standard was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Acetone, 1-propanol (1-PrOH), 2-propanol (IPA), and tetrahydrofuran (THF), used as organic solvents, were supplied by RCI Labscan. Deionized water and acetonitrile were used to prepare the mobile phase for HPLC analysis. Citric acid, used as the catalyst, was purchased from Kemaus. Sodium chloride (NaCl) and ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) were obtained from QRëC and Loba Chemie, respectively. Amberlyst-21 (A21), employed for impurity removal during purification, was supplied by Thermo Scientific.

5-HMF Production

Syrup solutions at the studied concentrations, along with the organic solvent, were separately fed into a T-shaped micromixer (0.5 mm) using HPLC pumps. The volumetric flow rates of the syrup and organic solvent were adjusted to achieve the desired residence time and organic-to-aqueous (O:A) phase volumetric ratio. After exiting the micromixer, the mixed stream was introduced into a microreactor (1/16 in. ID, 6 m length, 4 mL volume) placed in a convection oven maintained at the studied temperature. A back-pressure regulator was used to maintain the system pressure at 1000 psi. The reactor effluent was collected for subsequent product analysis. The experimental setup is shown in Figure . Product Analysis

The quantification of 5-HMF was conducted using High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) equipped with a Photodiode Array Detector (PDA). The analytical conditions were as follows: separation was performed on a C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm particle size; ACE Excel, Avantor). The mobile phase was a 90:10 mixture of deionized water and acetonitrile, delivered at 1 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and detection was performed at 320 nm to monitor 5-HMF. An analytical standard was used to determine the retention time and identity of the detected compounds. Byproduct analysis, including formic acid and levulinic acid, was performed using the same HPLC method, except that the detection wavelength was set to 280 nm.

The sugar content in the raw material and product was analyzed using an HPLC system equipped with a Refractive Index Detector (RID). The analysis was conducted under the following conditions: separation was performed on an NH2 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm particle size; ACE Excel, Avantor). The mobile phase was a mixture of deionized water and acetonitrile (18:82 v/v), with a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The column temperature was set to 40 °C, and analytical standards were used to determine retention times and identify the detected compounds.

Downstream Processes

In this work, a simple and effective downstream process for the purification of 5-HMF was demonstrated, as illustrated in Figure . The crude 5-HMF solution obtained under optimal reaction conditions was directly used as the feed stream for purification. A fixed-bed adsorption column (1/4″ OD, 3.3 mL) was employed for selective adsorption. Amberlyst 21 and activated carbon were used sequentially as adsorbents to remove impurities and recover 5-HMF. Ethanol and isopropanol were applied as selective desorbents to elute the adsorbed 5-HMF from the adsorbent beds. Finally, the desorption solvent was removed using a vacuum evaporator, yielding purified 5-HMF.

Results and Discussion

Screening of Solvents in a Monophasic System

The selection of the organic solvent is a critical factor influencing the efficiency, selectivity, and safety of 5-HMF production. This study aims to develop a nontoxic and safe production process for 5-HMF suitable for pharmaceutical applications. Simple downstream purification steps enabled the achievement of a high-purity 5-HMF product (99%). To this end, monophasic solvents with low boiling points were selected to facilitate solvent removal by evaporation and to ensure that no residual solvents remain in the final product. The green solvents selected in this study included deionized water (DI water), acetone, 1-propanol (1-PrOH), and 2-propanol (IPA). These solvents are classified as Class 3 under the ICH Q3C guideline, indicating low toxicity and suitability for use in pharmaceutical and environmentally benign processes. Tetrahydrofuran (THF) was used as a benchmark solvent because it has been reported as a promising organic solvent for 5-HMF synthesis; however, THF is classified as a Class 2 residual solvent and therefore requires strict control of its residual level in pharmaceutical products. Therefore, identifying alternative green solvents that can achieve comparable selectivity and purity is of significant interest.

To evaluate the effectiveness of each solvent, the 5-HMF production process was conducted using a 20 g/L syrup, 15 wt % citric acid as the catalyst, an O:A ratio of 0.5:1, a temperature of 190 °C, and a residence time of 60–100 min. The results are presented in Figure .

Effect of solvent types on 5-HMF performance: (a) syrup conversion, (b) 5-HMF yield, and (c) 5-HMF selectivity.

All solvents exhibited high syrup conversion, as shown in Figure a, with conversion levels exceeding 90% and remaining comparable across all solvent systems. In contrast, the solvent type had a pronounced effect on 5-HMF yield (Figure b). At a residence time of 80 min, acetone achieved the highest 5-HMF yield (32.02%), followed by THF (30.23%). In comparison, 1-PrOH and DI water produced moderate yields of approximately 20%, while IPA consistently performed the worst. Similar trends in solvent effectiveness were also observed at shorter residence times (<60 min), as reported in our previous study. However, further prolonging the residence time beyond 80 min led to a significant decline in the 5-HMF yield, likely due to secondary reactions involving both reactants and the formed 5-HMF under extended reaction conditions.

THF provided relatively high yields due to its moderate polarity and weak hydrogen-bond-donor ability, which enhances 5-HMF stability by limiting rehydration and condensation reactions within the monophasic medium. Similar behavior of THF as an efficient solvent for 5-HMF production has been reported in solvent-screening studies. Acetone also offered the highest yield among the green solvents; its intermediate polarity, aprotic nature, and low viscosity facilitated efficient dehydration and good mass transfer, while minimizing alcohol-involved side reactions, consistent with earlier reports of high 5-HMF yields in acetone-based systems.

Water gave a slightly lower yield than acetone but still higher than the alcohol systems, because it strongly promotes proton transfer and acid dissociation of the organic acid catalyst, enabling rapid dehydration of fructose and glucose despite concurrent rehydration of 5-HMF to levulinic and formic acids. In contrast, 1-propanol and isopropanol resulted in substantially lower 5-HMF yields, likely due to their strong hydrogen-bonding and protic character, which decreases the effective acidity of the medium and promotes secondary degradation pathways such as rehydration and humin formation, consistent with solvent-effect trends in mixed aqueous–alcohol systems. Overall, solvents with intermediate polarity and limited hydrogen-bonding capability (THF and acetone) provided the most favorable balance between dehydration activity and 5-HMF stability under the studied conditions, in agreement with broader analyses of solvent effects in 5-HMF chemistry. When THF is excluded, acetone yielded the highest 5-HMF productivity among the green solvents tested, attributable to its balanced polarity, low viscosity, and volatility, which facilitate efficient dehydration and simple postreaction solvent removal.

In addition, 5-HMF selectivity followed a similar trend (Figure c): extended reaction times led to decreased selectivity, consistent with the findings of Guo et al. (2021), who attributed this decline to side reactions that form undesired byproducts. A comparative analysis of different solvent systems relative to deionized water showed that certain organic solvents, notably THF and acetone, yielded higher 5-HMF yield and achieved higher selectivity. In contrast, isopropanol consistently yielded the lowest values for both yield and selectivity, likely due to increased byproduct formation.

Among the solvents evaluated, acetone emerged as the most effective and environmentally favorable green solvent under the studied conditions. Its high volatility (boiling point of 56 °C) minimizes the risk of residual solvent contamination in the final 5-HMF product, which is a critical requirement for pharmaceutical applications. A comprehensive cost-performance evaluation of the investigated solvents has been reported in our previous work. Among them, acetone emerged as a cost-effective solvent that offers an optimal balance between process efficiency and cost. Taken together, these advantagesalong with its superior 5-HMF yield, cost-effectiveness, and compliance with ICH Class 3 solvent guidelinesestablish acetone as the preferred solvent for subsequent optimization of reaction parameters, providing an optimal balance among economic viability, process efficiency, and regulatory suitability for pharmaceutical-grade 5-HMF production.

Screening of Key Process Variables on 5-HMF Performance

In this study, five key process variablesreaction temperature, residence time, O:A volumetric ratio (acetone-to-water), initial syrup concentration, and citric acid concentrationwere systematically investigated. A one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) approach was employed to determine the individual influence of each variable on 5-HMF performance (yield and selectivity), thereby defining the appropriate operating ranges for subsequent response surface methodology (RSM) optimization.

Influence of Reaction Temperature

Temperature is widely recognized as a critical parameter influencing the efficiency and selectivity of 5-HMF production from sugar-based feedstocks. To investigate the effect of temperature under controlled conditions, reaction temperatures of 150 to 190 °C were evaluated in this study using a fixed syrup concentration of 20 g/L, 15 wt % citric acid as the catalyst, acetone as the solvent, and an O:A ratio of 0.5:1. As shown in Figure a, increasing the reaction temperature from 150 to 190 °C markedly improved the conversion of syrup and the yield of 5-HMF. Syrup conversion rose steadily from 37.9% at 150 °C to 93.5% at 190 °C, reflecting accelerated kinetics at higher temperatures. Likewise, the 5-HMF yield increased from 10.8% to 32.0%, peaking at 190 °C. Figure b shows that 5-HMF selectivity initially improved with temperature, peaking at 39.8% at 170 °C, then declined to 38.1% at 180 °C and 34.3% at 190 °C. Notably, the product solution became visibly darker at ≥180 °C (embedded Figure ), indicating the formation of undesired byproducts at more severe conditions.

Influence of reaction temperature on 5-HMF performance; (a) 5-HMF yield and (b) 5-HMF selectivity.

These results suggest a trade-off caused by temperature-dependent reaction pathways. Higher temperatures greatly accelerate the acid-catalyzed dehydration of sugars to 5-HMF (Arrhenius behavior), explaining the sharp rise in conversion and in the rate of 5-HMF formation. However, 5-HMF is a reactive intermediate that can undergo secondary reactions once formed. At elevated temperatures, especially above 170 °C, 5-HMF participates in degradation pathways: it can rehydrate in the presence of water to form levulinic and formic acids, or undergo condensation/polymerization to form insoluble carbonaceous species (humins). The drop in 5-HMF selectivity above 170 °C is consistent with these side reactions consuming a larger fraction of the sugars/5-HMF. Hu et al. (2011) observed that humin polymer formation is favored at high temperatures and long residence times, whereas milder conditions suppress polymerization. The pronounced darkening of the reaction mixture at 180–190 °C (embedded Figure ) aligns with increased humin formation, corroborating the selectivity decline. In contrast, at lower temperatures (150–160 °C), humin formation was less evident, but the slower reaction kinetics led to incomplete conversion and lower 5-HMF yields.

Our findings are consistent with the literature on the temperature sensitivity of 5-HMF production. Hao et al. (2021) observed that raising the reaction temperature (within an optimal range of around 160–170 °C) enhanced 5-HMF yields, whereas further increases or prolonged heating decreased yields. Sayed et al. (2020) likewise reported that very high 5-HMF yields can be attained at higher temperatures (e.g., ∼87% yield at 200 °C) only if the residence time is extremely short to prevent 5-HMF loss. This underscores that, although elevated temperatures markedly accelerate 5-HMF formation, maintaining strict control over reaction time under these conditions is essential to prevent excessive 5-HMF degradation. Moreover, the O:A ratio is another key variable that can suppress undesired side reactions by limiting 5-HMF rehydration or polymerization via solvation effects. Based on the overall performance metrics, 170 °C was identified as the optimal reaction temperature in the citric acid/acetone system and was selected for further investigation in subsequent experiments.

Influence of O:A Ratio

The effects of O:A ratios ranging from 0.125:1 to 1:1 on 5-HMF production were examined under controlled conditions (20 g/L syrup, 15 wt % citric acid, and 170 °C) (see Figure ). This parameter markedly influenced syrup conversion, 5-HMF yield, and selectivity, consistent with prior reports. In the monophasic acetone–water medium, varying the O:A ratio changes not only the solubility and interactions of reactants and products, but also the physicochemical properties of the reaction medium, including polarity, hydrogen-bonding behavior, and the effective acidity of the weak organic acid catalyst. , These solvent-composition effects strongly influence both sugar dehydration and the stability of the formed 5-HMF.

Influence of O:A ratio on 5-HMF performance; (a) 5-HMF yield and (b) 5-HMF selectivity.

At the lowest O:A ratio (0.125:1, highly aqueous medium), conversion reached 55%, but 5-HMF yield and selectivity were limited (20% and 36%), indicating dominant side-product formation. Increasing the organic content to 0.25:1 reduced conversion slightly (48.9%) but markedly increased yield (23.7%) and selectivity (48.5%), indicating a more favorable water-to-acetone balance. This intermediate ratio provided sufficient water for acid-catalyzed dehydration while allowing acetone to stabilize nascent 5-HMF, thereby suppressing secondary reactions. Previous work on fructose dehydration in acetone–water systems has shown that water assists intermolecular hydrogen transfer and accelerates the dehydration rate, whereas acetone suppresses HMF degradation reactions and can increase the population of more reactive fructofuranose-type species.

At higher O:A ratios (0.5:1 and 1:1), overall sugar conversion increased to 53% and 68%, respectively; however, 5-HMF yield and selectivity declined significantly (<20% and 25%). This reduction is attributed to the acetone-rich environment, which reduces the efficiency of acid-catalyzed dehydration. The lower solvent polarity and reduced water content likely diminish transition-state stabilization and weaken the dehydration pathway. In addition, because citric acid is a weak organic acid, its degree of dissociation is expected to decrease as the organic fraction increases, owing to the lower ability of the mixed solvent to stabilize ionic species. As a result, the effective Brønsted acidity available for dehydration becomes weaker at higher O:A ratios. Therefore, although acetone can help suppress 5-HMF degradation, excessive acetone content may reduce the rate of the desired dehydration pathway and shift the system toward unselective side reactions, including condensation and polymerization of intermediates. These findings are consistent with those reported by Zhu et al., who observed similar trends in acetone–water systems, where increasing the organic fraction beyond an optimal threshold led to lower HMF yields.

Mechanistically, these results highlight that the effect of O:A ratio in the present system is governed by a balance between solvent-mediated stabilization and solvent-dependent acidity. A moderate acetone content improves the reaction environment by enhancing 5-HMF stabilization, while still retaining enough water to maintain effective acid-catalyzed dehydration. In contrast, excessively high acetone fractions reduce the effective acidity of citric acid and limit water’s beneficial role in the dehydration pathway, thereby lowering 5-HMF selectivity. Among the tested conditions, an O:A ratio of 0.25:1 offered the optimal compromise between conversion and selectivity and was therefore selected for subsequent experiments.

Influence of Initial Syrup Concentration

The influence of syrup concentrations at 10–25 g/L was examined under controlled reaction conditions, which include the use of 15% citric acid as a catalyst, a reaction temperature of 170 °C, acetone as the solvent, and an organic-to-aqueous (O:A) phase ratio of 0.25:1. The experiment was done under 40 to 80 min of residence times. The results (Figure ) indicate that increasing the starting syrup concentration from 10 to 25 g/L consistently increased syrup conversion. The highest conversion was achieved at the highest concentration (25 g/L), reaching about 69.6% after 80 min. This demonstrates that greater substrate availability, especially when combined with a longer reaction time, significantly enhances sugar conversion under these conditions. By contrast, more dilute feeds (10–15 g/L) exhibited considerably lower conversions (even at extended residence times), due to an insufficient substrate availability.

Influence of initial syrup concentration on 5-HMF performance; (a) 5-HMF yield and (b) 5-HMF selectivity.

While higher concentrations promoted more complete conversion of the syrup, the 5-HMF yield/selectivity did not increase monotonically with concentration (Figure ). The maximum 5-HMF yield and selectivity were 27.03% and 42.82% at 20 g/L and 80 min. Increasing the concentration beyond this point, such as to 25 g/L, did not further improve the yield. This suggests that above a certain threshold, additional sugar promotes competing side reactions (humin formation and condensation of intermediates), thereby reducing the efficiency of conversion toward 5-HMF. Thus, excessive substrate loading shifts the reaction away from the desired dehydration pathway.

These results highlight a clear trade-off between maximizing conversion and maintaining high 5-HMF yield and selectivity. Higher initial sugar concentrations enhance conversion but also accelerate side reactions, diverting a significant portion of the substrate toward undesired byproducts and thereby diminishing both yield and selectivity. Conversely, low sugar concentrations limit overall productivity due to insufficient substrate availability. Based on the combined performance metrics, an initial syrup concentration of 20 g/L was identified as the optimal condition and was therefore selected for further investigation.

Influence of Citric Acid Concentration

Under fixed reaction conditions (20 g/L sugar syrup, 170 °C, O:A 0.25:1), varying the citric acid concentration from 5 to 20 wt % had a pronounced impact on 5-HMF production (Figure ). Increasing the acid loading led to markedly higher sugar conversion, up to 70.5% at 20 wt % citric acid at 80 min, consistent with the acceleration of dehydration kinetics at greater proton availability. However, 5-HMF yield did not rise monotonically with acidity. Instead, it peaked at an intermediate citric acid concentration (15 wt %), reaching a maximum yield of 26.9% at 80 min. Thus, although more severe acidic conditions drive greater sugar conversion, beyond a certain point, this does not translate into higher 5-HMF formation. Excess acid (20 wt %) appears to divert a portion of the sugar, as well as some of the formed 5-HMF, into side-products rather than the desired 5-HMF, as reflected by the drop in HMF selectivity at the highest acid loadings.

Influence of citric acid concentration on 5-HMF performance; (a) 5-HMF yield and (b) 5-HMF selectivity.

Mechanistically, these trends can be rationalized by the acid catalyst’s dual role in promoting both dehydration and side reactions. At low citric acid concentrations (5–10 wt %), the effective Brønsted acidity is insufficient to efficiently dehydrate the sugars to 5-HMF. Because citric acid is a weak organic acid, its catalytic behavior in the present monophasic acetone–water medium depends not only on acid loading but also on solvent-dependent dissociation behavior. , Studies on citric acid in aqueous–organic mixed solvents, as well as broader pK a investigations in aqueous–organic media, have shown that acid dissociation is sensitive to solvent composition and that less polar media generally reduce stabilization of ionic species. , Consequently, the effective proton-donating ability of citric acid in the acetone–water medium is expected to be lower than in pure water, meaning that a minimum acid concentration is required to achieve appreciable dehydration. This interpretation is consistent with reports that weaker acids generally provide lower 5-HMF yields unless used at sufficiently high concentrations.

Increasing the citric acid concentration to 15 wt % likely compensates for the reduced degree of dissociation in the mixed solvent by increasing the total amount of proton-donating species in the system, thereby enhancing dehydration to 5-HMF. However, at higher acid concentrations (>15 wt %), the stronger overall acidic environment accelerates not only the desired dehydration pathway but also undesired secondary reactions. Under overly harsh acidic conditions, 5-HMF is prone to further degradation through rehydration and condensation/polymerization reactions. Taken together, these results demonstrate that an optimal citric acid concentration exists that maximizes 5-HMF yield by balancing effective dehydration and product stability. This insight highlights that the effect of citric acid concentration in the present system should be interpreted as a combined acid-loading and solvent-dependent acidity effect, rather than as a simple increase in proton concentration alone.

The Effect of Salt Addition

This study examined the influence of salt additives on 5-HMF production efficiency by incorporating sodium chloride (NaCl) and ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) into the reaction system. Experiments were conducted under previously determined optimal conditions. As shown in Figure , the addition of either salt did not improve 5-HMF yield or selectivity. In fact, both NaCl and NH4Cl reduced 5-HMF yield and selectivity, despite a modest increase in syrup conversion. This divergence indicates that while salts may promote sugar breakdown, they simultaneously accelerate side reactions that divert intermediates away from the desired 5-HMF pathway.

Influence of salt addition on 5-HMF performance and HPLC chromatogram of 5-HMF products.

Chromatographic analysis further confirmed this effect: the area corresponding to the byproducts increased significantly in the salt-containing systems compared with the salt-free control. This suggests that the presence of salts in the monophasic acetone–water medium acted more as a cocatalyst promoting undesired side reactions rather than serving a beneficial role, such as phase separation or product stabilization. Unlike biphasic systemswhere salts like NaCl are known to induce phase separation and improve in situ 5-HMF extractionthis monophasic system offered no such advantage. Consequently, the incorporation of salts under the present conditions was detrimental to 5-HMF production and is not recommended for performance enhancement in this solvent regime.

Process Comparison

In this work, production performance across various aspects was compared with literature data obtained using the same organic solvent (acetone) for the synthesis of 5-HMF. Both batch/continuous processes and homogeneous/heterogeneous catalysts were involved, as summarized in Table . Note that 5-HMF production from syrup has been demonstrated for the first time in this work, making it difficult to compare with the same raw material. Fructose and glucose were used instead. The results indicate that fructose feedstock is readily converted to 5-HMF (>90% selectivity) under both homogeneous (Table ; No. 1) and heterogeneous (Table ; No. 2) catalysts, owing to its intrinsically favorable direct dehydration pathway. However, when glucose was used (Table ; No. 4), only moderate selectivity (67.9%) was observed in the presence of strong homogeneous acids, which decreased to 27.5% with a heterogeneous catalyst (Table ; No. 5). This is due to the more complex glucose pathway, which requires prior isomerization to fructose before dehydration. In heterogeneous systems, mass-transfer limitations, limited active-site accessibility, and diffusion constraints impair efficient catalytic operation, thereby decreasing 5-HMF selectivity. When the mild homogeneous catalyst used in this work was employed, better selectivity (43.9%) was achieved than with the solid catalyst.

From the viewpoint of renewability and environmental impact, the catalyst used in this work also offers an important advantage. Citric acid is biomass-derived, environmentally benign, and safer to handle than strong mineral acids. In addition, its use is more consistent with green chemistry principles, as it reduces catalyst-related hazards and avoids generating more problematic secondary waste associated with harsher catalytic systems (Table ; Nos. 1 and 4).

Although the 5-HMF yield and productivity obtained in this study were lower than those reported for fructose-based processes (Table , Nos. 1 and 2), the proposed process showed clearly superior 5-HMF production performance (Po) compared with other glucose-based 5-HMF processes, achieving 0.54 kg5‑HMF/kgcat-h versus ≤0.2 kg5‑HMF/kgcat-h, despite the relatively low yield observed. These results highlight the promising potential of this technique for producing 5-HMF from glucose-based feedstocks. Furthermore, when the raw material costs, including sugar, solvent, and catalyst, were compared, the proposed process exhibited the lowest cost among the glucose-based systems.

Moreover, the proposed process offers an additional advantage in its simplified continuous production–purification scheme, which can deliver 5-HMF with 99% purity. For pharmaceutical-grade applications, product purity and downstream process simplicity are highly important considerations. In this context, the reduced purification complexity of the present system may partially offset its lower crude 5-HMF yield. Therefore, the main strength of this process lies in its simple overall process design and easier purification. Nevertheless, the current 5-HMF yield remains a limitation and should be improved in future studies. A detailed techno-economic analysis should also be conducted to verify whether the benefits of simplified purification and high product purity can offset the limitation of relatively low yield at a larger scale. One promising approach is to introduce a low-toxicity Lewis acid cocatalyst to enhance glucose-to-fructose isomerization, thereby improving overall 5-HMF yield and selectivity.

5-HMF Purification Processes

The 5-HMF product obtained under optimal conditions (initial syrup concentration of 20 g/L, citric acid concentration of 15 wt %, O:A ratio of 0.25:1, reaction temperature of 170 °C, and residence time of 80 min) was used to demonstrate a simple and effective continuous purification process. The purification strategy consisted of sequential adsorption using commercial adsorbents (A21 and AC), followed by selective desorption with isopropanol and ethanol. Final solvent removal yielded 5-HMF with 99% purity. The experimental procedures and performance of each purification step are described in the following sections.

Adsorption with A21

The crude 5-HMF stream was fed into a fixed-bed adsorption column (1/4″ OD, 3.3 mL) packed with A21 to remove organic impurities, primarily levulinic acid and formic acid. Adsorption experiments were conducted at 30 °C for 40 min. Under these conditions, an impurity removal efficiency of 68.68% was achieved, corresponding to a final 5-HMF purity of 97.0%. To further enhance purification, a two-stage fixed-bed adsorption configuration was evaluated under identical operating conditions. The two-stage system provided a marginal improvement, with an impurity removal efficiency of 70.43% and a corresponding 5-HMF purity of 97.4%, as shown in Figure . However, the improvement was not statistically significant, indicating that a single fixed-bed adsorption column is sufficient for effective impurity removal in this step.

Adsorption–Desorption Process

The 5-HMF-containing stream after A21 treatment was introduced into a fixed-bed adsorption column (1/4″ OD, 3.3 mL) packed with AC for further purification. In this step, selective adsorption was used to retain organic compounds, including residual organic impurities and 5-HMF, while unadsorbed syrup components and acetone were discharged in the effluent. The experiments were demonstrated at 30 °C with a residence time of 10 min.

After passage through the AC column, the effluent contained mainly syrup-derived components and solvent, whereas 5-HMF, together with a portion of the organic impurities, was retained on the AC, as confirmed by the chromatogram of the effluent. Breakthrough analysis of 5-HMF under continuous-flow conditions indicated that the AC bed approached saturation after 80 min of operation, at which point desorption was required.

Desorption of the adsorbed species was carried out stepwise. IPA was first used to remove residual organic acids and byproducts due to its moderate polarity. However, IPA also caused partial desorption of 5-HMF, resulting in an estimated loss of approximately 15% of the adsorbed 5-HMF. This result indicates that the current impurity-elution step is not yet fully selective and that product loss during this stage remains a significant limitation of the present downstream protocol.

Following IPA treatment, ethanol was used as the desorption solvent to recover 5-HMF. Ethanol desorbed 80% of the retained 5-HMF from the AC bed. Therefore, considering both the IPA-associated loss and the subsequent ethanol recovery step, the current adsorption–desorption protocol yielded only a limited overall downstream recovery of 5-HMF, despite its effectiveness in removing impurities. This result highlights a clear trade-off between purification efficiency and product recovery in the present system.

The recovered 5-HMF solution was subsequently subjected to solvent removal using a rotary evaporator at 90 °C under reduced pressure (40 mbar). After solvent evaporation, the final 5-HMF purity reached approximately 99%, exceeding the purity levels commonly reported for other downstream purification processes (85–95%). ,, Nevertheless, the present desorption protocol still requires further optimization to reduce 5-HMF loss and improve overall recovery. Parameters such as solvent composition, mixed-solvent systems, desorption temperature, elution volume, solvent-to-adsorbent ratio, and stepwise desorption sequence should be systematically investigated in future work.

Conclusion

A sustainable, process-intensified, continuous-flow conversion of sugarcane syrup to 5-HMF was successfully demonstrated in a microreactor using citric acid as a green catalyst in a monophasic system. Systematic solvent screening revealed that although the THF-based system delivered the highest yield, acetone provided the optimal balance between reaction efficiency, cost-effectiveness, downstream compatibility, and regulatory suitability. Comprehensive optimization of key operating variablesincluding temperature, residence time, catalyst loading, O:A ratio, initial syrup concentration, and salt additionhighlighted the strong interdependence between reaction environment and 5-HMF performance. Under optimized conditions (15 wt % citric acid, 170 °C, O:A ratio 0.25:1, 80 min), a maximum selectivity of 43.9% was achieved.

Importantly, a simplified downstream strategy integrating sequential selective adsorption (A21 followed by AC) with solvent evaporation enabled the production of high-purity 5-HMF (∼99%) without extraction-intensive or biphasic separation steps. This integrated reaction–separation framework significantly reduces process complexity and enhances scalability. Overall, this study demonstrates a sustainable manufacturing strategy for pharmaceutical-grade 5-HMF, achieved through the synergistic integration of green catalysis, low-toxicity solvents, direct utilization of biomass feedstocks, and continuous-flow process intensification. Future work should focus on enhancing production efficiency by rationally designing cocatalyst and cosolvent systems to further improve reaction kinetics, selectivity, and overall process performance.
