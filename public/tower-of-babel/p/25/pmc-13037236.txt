Short-term changes in clinical status, cardiovascular function and heart rate variability after staphylectomy and alarplasty in French Bulldogs with brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome
Authors: Anak Agung Gde Fandhiananta Widyanjaya, Chollada Buranakarl, Krishaporn Kradangnga, Nawat Sannamwong, Thasinas Dissayabutra, Saikaew Sutayatram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractShort-term effects of elongated soft palate correction on cardiovascular function and heart rate variability (HRV) were studied in eleven French Bulldogs with brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome (BOAS). The oxygen saturation (SpO2), blood pressure (BP), electrocardiography (ECG), echocardiography, short-term HRV, and modified BOAS questionnaire were carried out before and one month after surgical correction of BOAS. Significant reduction in the respiratory rate was reported after surgery (P < 0.05, 95% CI [-16, -2]). No significant differences were observed in SpO2, BP, ECG, and echocardiographic parameters both before and after surgery. Most of the questionnaire scores were improved after surgery. The HRV results after surgery showed a significant reduction in vagally mediated indices and a relative shift toward low frequency (LF) components. These changes were correlated to the clinical improvement from modified BOAS questionnaire. Although surgery improved questionnaire scores, the short-term changes in HRV likely reflect altered breathing patterns rather than definitive relief of ‘parasympathetic overactivity’. Introduction

Brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome (BOAS) is a respiratory distress condition caused by occlusion in the upper airway. It has high prevalence in the brachycephalic breeds such as Pugs, English Bulldogs, and French Bulldogs [48–50]. The primary BOAS abnormalities are aberrant nasopharyngeal turbinate, tracheal hypoplasia, stenotic nares, and soft palate elongation [29, 40]. These abnormalities cause increased negative pressure and turbulent airflow, leading to inflammation of surrounding soft tissue and progression of secondary lesions such as laryngeal collapse, everted laryngeal saccules, tonsillar hyperplasia, and pharyngeal hyperplasia [14, 40]. The BOAS pathophysiology involves several body systems leading to several clinical signs including increased respiratory effort and sounds, exercise and heat intolerance, gastrointestinal tract problems, and sleep apnea [14, 29, 40]. In humans, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) causes autonomic nervous system (ANS) imbalance and increases risk of cardiovascular diseases [7, 33]. Brachycephalic dogs with BOAS mostly present with sinus arrhythmia, sinus arrest, or sinus block [17]. They have higher systolic blood pressure (SBP), fractional shortening (FS), and left atrium to aorta ratio (LA/Ao) with lower tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion index (TAPSEI) and left ventricular internal diameter in diastole index (LVIDdI) [9, 11, 22], when compared to healthy non-brachycephalic dogs.

Heart rate variability (HRV) is a non-invasive tool to reflect cardiac ANS activity [55]. It is used for prognosing and determining the progression of cardiovascular diseases [24]. Moreover, vasovagal tonus index (VVTI) has been associated with severity of cardiac diseases in dogs and with the ANS tones in brachycephalic dogs [12, 16, 45]. However, limited data is available regarding the ANS activity and VVTI alterations in dogs with BOAS.

Surgical correction of BOAS to restore the airway diameter improves the respiratory rate (RR) and breathing pattern [17]. Nonetheless, short-term changes in the clinical status, cardiovascular functions and HRV have not been well studied in dogs with BOAS after surgical treatment. Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the short-term changes in the clinical status, cardiovascular function, and cardiac ANS activity using short-term HRV measurement in French Bulldogs with BOAS before and 1 month after staphylectomy and alarplasty.

Materials and methods

Animals

A total of 11 client-owned French Bulldogs that met the following criteria were included in the study: 1) diagnosed with BOAS that required staphylectomy and alarplasty, 2) age above 6-month-old, 3) weight above 8 kg, 4) normal hematology and biochemistry, and 5) normal thoracic radiograph using breed specific cardiac vertebral scale system (vertebral heart score; VHS) [25] and tracheal diameter to thoracic inlet (TD/TI) ratio < 0.12 in Bulldogs for tracheal hypoplasia [27]. The exclusion criteria were 1) obesity, 2) systemic diseases including structural heart diseases, pathological arrhythmias, heartworm disease, infectious pulmonary diseases, hepatic and renal dysfunction, severe eye diseases, and severe pre-existing gastrointestinal diseases, and 3) currently receiving anti-inflammatory or analgesic drugs (i.e., washout time less than 10 times of drug elimination half-life duration).

Experimental protocol

This study was conducted at the Small Animal Teaching Hospital, Faculty of Veterinary Science, Chulalongkorn University. The BOAS severity was determined prior to surgery using functional grading system [32]. Data collection at before surgery timepoint were performed within 7 days before surgery, including body temperature, blood pressure (BP), oxygen saturation (SpO2), electrocardiography (ECG), echocardiography, Holter monitoring for HRV evaluation, and information from modified BOAS questionnaire. All measurements and questionnaire outcomes were repeated one month after the BOAS surgical correction.

Modified BOAS questionnaire

The modified BOAS questionnaire was developed and modified from the previous studies [2, 3, 32, 50]. The questions were adapted (without formally validation) to align with the climate and dog care practices in Thailand (Supplementary Table 1). English language was translated to Thai language by Thai veterinarians who had good English proficiency. Clarification of the questions was performed by direct conversation between owners and veterinarians. The questionnaire comprised items on abnormal breathing sound, exercise and heat intolerance, and eating and sleeping problems, with total scores ranging from 0 to 41.

Blood pressure measurement

Blood pressure including SBP, diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and mean arterial pressure (MAP) were measured without sedation with minimal restraint using oscillometric device (SunTech Vet20, SunTech Medical, Inc., USA). The dogs were acclimatized in a quiet and air conditioning examination area (setting temperature at 25 ˚C) for at least 15 min, then placed on the table in the right lateral recumbency while the appropriate cuff size (approximately 40% of limb circumference) applied around the left front leg. The result of the first measurement was discarded, and the results of subsequent 3 measurements with less than 10% variability, were averaged.

Oxygen saturation measurement

The SpO2 measurement was performed using pulse oximeter probe placed on the dog’s nonpigmented areas of the mucosa (i.e., buccal mucosa) which was connected to the pulse oximeter device (SOARMED H-100B, Soar Medical Tech. Co., Ltd., Taiwan).

Electrocardiography

The ECG was recorded using ECG machine (CardiMax FX-7102, Fukuda Denshi Co., Ltd., Japan). The six limb leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, and aVF) were recorded while the dog was placed on the right lateral recumbency without sedation. The ECG parameters (P-wave duration and amplitude, RR interval, R-wave amplitude, PQ interval, QRS duration, QT interval, T-wave duration and amplitude) were averaged from 12 consecutive beats in lead II ECG tracing [21]. The corrected QT interval (QTc) was calculated using Van de Water formula: QTcV = QT – 0.087 × (RR – 1,000) [59].

Echocardiography

Echocardiography was performed by an experienced veterinarian in conscious dogs without sedation in right and left lateral recumbency using an echocardiographic machine (M9, Shenzhen Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., China) with a phased array cardiac probe (frequency range: 1.0–5.0 MHz) (Mindray SP5-1s, Shenzhen Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., China). Two-dimensional (2-D) mode, motion mode (M-mode), tissue Doppler, pulsed-wave Doppler, and continuous-wave Doppler echocardiographic parameters with simultaneous ECG monitoring were quantified according to the standard protocol of imaging planes and orientations as described by Thomas et al. [56], Lang et al. [31], Boon [4], and Boon [5]. Echocardiographic parameter measurement and calculation were also performed according to previous publications [19, 54, 58, 60]. The right ventricular longitudinal strain (RVSL) was obtained in the same view using 2-D speckle tracking echocardiography (2D-STE) software through RV free wall analysis (6 segments), as previously described [64]. The tissue Doppler on the left apical four chamber view focused on the right heart was applied to assess the peak systolic myocardial velocity of the lateral tricuspid annulus (S’), as well as the RV myocardial performance index (RV MPI) [61, 64]. M-mode echocardiography in the left apical four chamber view was performed to measure TAPSE [61]. The pulsed-wave Doppler on the right parasternal short axis view was performed to assess velocity–time integral of the pulmonary artery flow (PV VTI), and acceleration time to peak pulmonary artery flow velocity-to-ejection time of pulmonary artery ratio (AT/ET) [36]. End-diastolic and End-systolic frames were selected based on ECG [53]. Echocardiographic volume index and weight-dependent dimensional echocardiographic index were normalized by body surface area (BSA) and body weight, respectively [9, 13]. Details on standard echocardiographic parameters and indexing were presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Ambulatory electrocardiographic (Holter) monitoring

The short-term HRV was recorded by a 3-channel transthoracic ambulatory electrocardiographic machine (Holter, Fukuda Denshi Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) with bipolar lead ECG Holter recording for approximately 1-h monitoring between 09:00 am to 4:00 pm, in the quiet and air conditioning examination room with the present of the owner who was instructed not to initiate dog physical activity. Before- and after-surgical HRV recordings of each dog were performed at similar times of the day (i.e., within 4 h difference) to reduce circadian effects. During all HRV recording, the same veterinarian observed and recorded dog behavior in the time log data sheet. After recording, the ECG data was analyzed using SCM-510 Holter software (Fukuda Denshi Co., Ltd., Japan). The signals were checked, filtered, then converted for spectral analysis according to previous study [47]. The frequency components of the HRV were set as described by Pirintr et al. [46]. The data were analyzed from three separated 10-min segments, which were chosen based on the dog’s behavior (during resting period, relax, and not excited), and the signal quality of the recording. The results from three segments were averaged. The HRV parameters included time-domain parameters (i.e., standard deviation of RR intervals (SDNN), the square root of the mean difference between adjacent normal RR intervals (rMSSD), and number of NN50 divided by total NN count × 100 (pNN50)) and frequency domain parameters (i.e., low frequency (LF), high frequency (HF), total power (TP), low frequency to high frequency ratio (LF/HF), normalized LF (LFnu), and normalized HF (HFnu)). The calculation of LFnu was done by using the formula: LF/(LF + HF), while the HFnu was calculated by using the formula: HF/(LF + HF) [34]. Also, VVTI was calculated and averaged from the same selected 10-min segments using 20 RR formula [41].

Surgical correction procedure

An advanced staphylectomy technique using a bipolar sealing device (LigaSure™ Exact Dissector, Medtronic Ltd., USA) was performed to correct the elongated soft palate, and a vertical wedge excisional alarplasty was performed for surgical correction of stenotic nares as described previously [3].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistic version 28 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). The normality test of data was performed by Shapiro–Wilk test. The parametric data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) with 95% confidence interval (CI) and effect size (Cohen's d). The ordinal data were presented as median and interquartile range (25th to 75th percentile) with effect size (Matched-pairs rank biserial correlation coefficient). The effect sizes were calculated with before surgery – after surgery direction and reported as absolute value. The data before and after surgical correction was compared using Paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The relationships between HRV parameters and modified BOAS questionnaire scores were performed using Spearman correlation. GraphPad Prism 10.6.1 (GraphPad Software, Inc., Boston, USA) was used to generate graphic figures. The P value less than 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Electrocardiography

No clinically relevant [62] or statistically significant changes were observed in standard ECG (Supplementary Table 3). Before surgery, sinus rhythm was found in 7 dogs (64.3%), and respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) was found in 4 dogs (36.4%). After surgical correction, the 2 dogs that previously had RSA changed to sinus rhythm. There was no ectopic beat, sinus arrest, or heart block at any time points.

Echocardiography

No abnormalities of the cardiac structure and function [4–6, 9, 15, 36] or statistically significant differences were observed in echocardiographic parameters (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

This study evaluated the short-term effect of surgical correction of elongated soft palate and stenotic nares, on clinical status, cardiovascular functions, and short-term HRV in dogs with BOAS. In terms of the dogs’ characteristics, all of 11 dogs in this study were French Bulldogs and were within the young-adult age range. The breed and average ages of all dogs in this study were similar to previous clinical reports [22, 49, 50]. BOAS is clinically observed in dogs aged between 2–3 years [14]. However, in this study, there were 3 dogs aged under 2 years. This earlier clinical presentation could be due to the hot and humid environment that can aggravate the poor thermoregulation of the dogs with BOAS [50]. According to previous study, male French Bulldogs had higher prevalence risk ratio of BOAS compared to female French Bulldogs [32]. Confounding factor may be related to larger muscle mass surrounding upper airway (i.e., neck and chest areas), which can further compress the narrowing upper airway [50]. The obesity may not be a risk factor in this study since the BCS was normal, similar with findings from previous study [22]. All dogs in this study exhibited elongated soft palate and stenotic nares, without secondary abnormality. Since VHS and TD/TI ratio were normal in all dogs, which was consistent with previous study [11], thus, neither heart enlargement nor tracheal hypoplasia were commonly found in the BOAS dogs.

In this study, resting RR was significantly reduced after surgical correction, consistent with the findings in previous BOAS study [17]. These might indicate that with improved airway resistance after surgical correction, the dogs can breathe more comfortably, resulting in reduction of compensatory respiratory efforts through increasing RR and/or respiratory depth. However, other parameters such as body temperature and SpO2 did not exhibit abnormal value or differences after surgical correction, in line with the findings in French Bulldogs with moderate BOAS [2]. This might be attributed to adequate compensatory responses in terms of ventilation and heat elimination through respiration during resting state.

In terms of cardiovascular functions, the SBP, DBP, and MAP did not differ significantly after surgical correction. Also, in a previous study, the SBP was not significantly different between dogs with BOAS and healthy non-brachycephalic group [11]. Likewise, the SBP, DBP, and MAP were similar among groups of pugs with different severity of BOAS [35]. However, one study showed that healthy brachycephalic dogs were presented with higher SBP, DBP and MAP compared with non-brachycephalic dogs [22]. Elevation of the BP in the OSA dog model, as well as in patients with OSA were also found [10, 42]. Increased BP may be due to exacerbate vasoconstriction from the hypoxia-induced sympathetic nervous system (SNS) activation, and increased renin, angiotensin II, and endothelin-1, along with reduction in nitric oxide synthesis [44]. In this study, most of the dogs before surgery had SBP values that were classified as prehypertensive level, and 1 dog was classified as hypertensive level. After surgery, 4 out of the 11 dogs showed SBP level reduction. Nevertheless, no evidence of target organ damage was found. Several studies observed that environmental settings and stress levels also impact BP values [23, 26, 35]. Higher BP in the individual case before surgery might be influenced by stress and difficulty breathing. After surgery, the lower BP might be due to familiarization with the settings, as well as improved respiratory flow. The HR was also not significantly different after surgical correction. Similarly, continuous positive airway pressure therapy in humans with OSA did not alter HR, despite the significant alteration in HRV. This might be because the OSA influences the variance of HR rather than the natural change in HR during a specific time point [30].

BOAS primarily affects respiration and thermoregulation but can also impact other systems. In this study, all dogs exhibited BOAS-related clinical signs, such as abnormal breathing sounds, gastrointestinal problems, cyanosis, and/or exercise intolerance. After surgical correction, all owners reported improvement in the modified BOAS questionnaire scores. All these findings align with previous studies that also reported the improvement in the BOAS-related clinical signs after surgical treatment [28, 49, 57]. The modified BOAS questionnaire scores showed significant difference in most question parts after the surgical correction (except eating and sleeping problems). Previous study showed that BOAS-related clinical signs were still improved in long-term follow up (6 months) after surgery [48]. These findings indicate that the surgical correction of BOAS could restore airflow leading to beneficial effects in resolving the clinical signs both in short-term and long-term periods.

In the present study, all ECG parameters both before and after surgical correction were within normal reference range without significant difference. Previous study in BOAS dogs showed unremarkable abnormal ECG compared to normal non-brachycephalic dogs except PR interval [11]. The majority of dogs in the present study exhibited sinus rhythm recorded before surgical correction. Some dogs had RSA which disappeared after surgery. The absence of RSA in the ECG recording in some cases may be attributable to stress from restraint, whereas RSA was detected in all dogs during short-term HRV monitoring in the resting state. Higher incidence of RSA was found in BOAS [17]. The RSA is associated with elevated parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) activity [39] and the activation of vagal tone can be calculated from the magnitude of RSA [20]. Increased inspiratory resistance found in BOAS can augment fluctuations in intrathoracic pressure leading to higher magnitude of RSA [12]. However, equal presentation of RSA and sinus rhythm was also reported in French Bulldogs with BOAS [16].

In the echocardiographic examination, all values were within normal limits with unremarkable findings in both left and right sides either before or after surgical correction. Similar findings were found in BOAS compared with the healthy non-brachycephalic dogs [11]. However, the differences of IVSsI between French Bulldogs with and without BOAS [9], and cardiac function improvement (e.g., significantly larger LA/Ao ratio and LVPWdI, smaller RVID at basal index, and lower late diastolic interventricular annular velocity) in dogs with BOAS after 6 to 12 months of surgical treatment were reported [8]. Since both duration and severity of OSA affected the severity of cardiovascular problems [18, 38, 63], the unremarkable changes in cardiac functions in this short-term study might be due to differences in dog population, BOAS severity, and duration of follow-up, compared to other studies.

The short-term HRV results showed that pNN50 and VVTI decreased significantly after the surgical treatment while SDNN and rMSSD exhibited trends of reduction. Decrease in SDNN, rMSSD, and cardiovagal index at 1 month and 2 months after surgical correction was also reported in dogs with BOAS [17]. This suggested that surgical correction of BOAS was consistent with reductions of PNS activity to the heart and bradyarrhythmia frequency. Long term SDNN recording can be influenced by thermoregulation, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), baroreflex, circadian rhythm, and peripheral vasomotor tone while short-term recordings indicates PNS activity [52]. The rMSSD and pNN50 also reflect alterations in vagal activity [55]. In terms of VVTI, one study found that VVTI was not different between brachycephalic and mesocephalic dogs [16] while another study reported that brachycephalic dogs had higher VVTI than other dog breeds [12]. Also, stress during clinical examination evaluated by behavior responses did not affect VVTI values in adult dogs [41]. The contradictory VVTI results might be due to small sample size, variation in BOAS severity, VVTI methodology, and other confounding factors.

For frequency-domain parameters, HFnu was significantly reduced after surgical correction. Conversely, the LF/HF and LFnu increased significantly. Spectral analysis for frequency-domain is reproducible and more accurate than the time-domain methods in short-term studies of ANS activity on the heart [37]. Both SNS and PNS can modulate the LF value. However, the HF component represents a vagal modulation under the effect of the respiration, most importantly RR and tidal volume [43]. Respiratory mechanics modulate PNS activity to the heart through several mechanisms: chemorecptor activtion in response to hypoxia and hypercapnia, and mechanoreceptor activation in the lungs triggered by negative intrathoracic pressure during dyspnea [65, 66]. Although, LF/HF has been considered as the overall balance between SNS and PNS activity [55], it has several limitations related to the complexity of HRV analysis due to interactions among SNS, PNS, and other factors (especially respiration and resting HR), as well as recording duration [51]. Also, the RR in this study was significantly reduced after surgical correction and the respiratory mechanics were highly influenced by BOAS severity [32]. Thus, reduction in HFnu with increase in LF/HF and LFnu found in this study should be interpreted with caution, as they are influenced by relative spectral distribution and other confounding factors. Moreover, with high influence of respiration on PNS activity and HRV parameters [16, 17], interpretation of HRV analysis in dogs with chronic respiratory problems should be integrated with other parameters, especially RR and respiratory pattern during HRV monitoring.

In this study, the %change of abnormal breathing sound, exercise and heat tolerance, and total scores from the modified BOAS questionnaire strongly and positively correlated with %change of HFnu while negatively with %change of LF/HF. Moreover, the %change of exercise and heat tolerance had strong negative correlation with %change LFnu. These findings suggested that alteration in short-term HRV analysis could be considered as supportive, but not definitive, marker of clinical improvement.

The evaluation of multiple physiological endpoints (i.e., vital signs, ECG, echocardiography, and HRV indices) used in this study was exploratory in nature. Accordingly, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, as the primary aim was comprehensive physiological characterization and the endpoints were inherently interrelated. However, multiple comparisons in the exploratory HRV analysis may lead to an inflated type-I error rate. Nevertheless, the results indicated that improvements in RR, owner-report clinical scores, and short-term HRV indices may serve as adjunctive tools for assessing short-term surgical correction response and guiding clinical management in dogs with BOAS.

The limitations of this study were small sample size, absence of a control group (brachycephalic without clinical BOAS and/or non-brachycephalic dogs), short follow-up (1 month), lack of standardized respiratory monitoring during Holter recording (e.g., absence of RR and tidal volume measurements, or tidal volume measurements or continuous respiratory inductance plethysmography), short-term (1 h) HRV analysis, lack of questionnaire formal validation, and potential owner-report bias in the questionnaire.

Conclusions

One month after upper airway surgical correction, dogs showed lower resting RR and improved owner-reported clinical scores. Short-term HRV indicated a reduction in variability amplitude with a relative spectral shift (increase LFnu and LF/HF with decrease HFnu). Given the strong influence of breathing and HR on HRV and the limitations of LF/HF as a balance marker, these findings are consistent with altered autonomic modulation accompanying improved ventilatory pattern, rather than direct evidence of ‘parasympathetic overactivity relief’. Longer follow-up with respiratory control, or comparison among brachycephalic breeds is warranted.
