Neuroimaging transcriptomic analyses of Parkinson’s disease highlight molecular, cellular, and neurobiological mechanisms
Authors: Xavier Bledsoe, Michael J. Betti, Eric R. Gamazon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Parkinson’s disease is a debilitating disorder with a profound symptomatic burden. While genomic studies of PD have made advances in recent years, there remains a gap in our understanding of the relationship between PD genes and the neurologic changes involved in disease progression. In this work, we aim to characterize the mechanisms by which germline susceptibility variants affect gene expression and the downstream effects on neurobiology. We perform a transcriptome-wide association study of PD (N = 482,730) and identify 74 transcript-level associations with the disease. These findings derive from gene expression models trained in bulk tissue samples from 13 different brain regions, and 6 tissues with clinical relevance to the brain, including whole blood. We explore the resulting associations using eQTL colocalization, the chromatin-state discovery approach CoRE-BED, and our NeuroimaGene atlas of neuroimaging transcriptomics. In this work, we implicate enhancer disruption for STX1B and HIP1R in neurons and oligodendrocytes respectively in PD pathophysiology and show that reduced expression of PD risk genes broadly is predictive of cortical thinning and dysregulation of somatomotor circuitry.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a debilitating disorder with a progressive and wide-reaching symptomatic burden. It is estimated that 5% of seniors older than 85 are living with PD1,2. The classic hallmarks of the disease are forms of motor dysfunction including resting tremor, postural instability, akinesis, and limb rigidity. Additionally, virtually all patients with PD will present with non-motor symptoms during their illness1,3–6. PD is thus characterized by diverse symptoms centered on canonical motor dysfunction and extending well beyond.

Despite considerable progress in disease evaluation, there is still much that we do not know about the molecular basis of PD. Efforts to identify these pathways have been hampered by highly polygenic and heterogenous disease architectures. The most common form of PD is idiopathic PD (iPD)7. Individuals with this condition develop classical Parkinsonism in the absence of rare, high-effect size, PD causing variants. The largest and most recent genome wide association study (GWAS) of iPD has identified 90 independent genome-wide significant risk signals and reports 53 candidate PD risk genes8. Capturing 16–36% of heritability, these data represent a major step forward in understanding the molecular profile of the disease. While some findings are directly interpretable, such as the alpha synuclein and LRRK2 loci, a disproportionately high number of trait-associated SNPs fall in genomic regulatory regions and do not demonstrate clear protein correlates. These findings suggest that PD risk variants may contribute to disease through dysregulation of gene transcription9. In addition to iPD, familial forms of PD have been identified that demonstrate Mendelian patterns of inheritance10. Inheritance studies have mapped instances of familial PD (fPD) to rare variants affecting several genes7. Different forms of PD can be modeled by complex polygenic genetic architectures as well as rare-variant, Mendelian patterns. Lastly, moving from molecules to organ-level biology, clinical MRI studies demonstrate significant disease-related alterations in the brains of PD patients11. Together, the presence of both genetic and neuroimaging markers for this condition indicates that it is a strong candidate for molecular neuroimaging studies12–14.

In this work, we assess the impact of PD genetic risk factors on multiple intermediate phenotypes including gene expression, gene regulatory elements, and grey/white matter structure. First, we assess the role of genetically regulated gene expression (GReX) in PD through transcriptome wide association studies (TWAS). We also evaluate familial PD genes and their biophysical interactors as potential risk factors in PD pathophysiology. We refine these hypotheses according to a regimen of computational molecular analyses to prioritize a set of high-confidence findings (Fig. 1). We then assess the role of PD genes on the brain by testing for gene-level associations with neuroimaging features using our NeuroimaGene resource. By assessing the endogenous relationship between PD genes and the structure of grey and white matter features in the brain, we identify the putative structures most impacted by PD risk genes. Results

TWAS of idiopathic Parkinson’s disease

We performed TWAS of PD using summary statistics of the largest, most recent GWAS. We leveraged gene expression models for 19 neuro-relevant tissues that were trained on Genotype Tissue Expression (GTEx) data via JTI (Fig. 2a). To reflect the neurological nature of PD, we employed all 13 brain models from GTEx. Each model was derived from tissue samples taken from 13 standardized cortical areas of donor brains15. We employed 6 additional models on the basis of their clinical relevance to the brain and prior neurological relevance discussed in the literature16,17. These include the adrenal gland and pituitary which participate in neuroactive hormone regulation and the liver which helps deactivate blood toxins and can lead to hepatic encephalopathy when dysfunctional. The sigmoid and transverse colon participate in the brain-gut axis, and whole blood is involved in the nourishment of the brain as well as a host of immunologic and metabolic processes relevant to brain health. Using a false discovery rate threshold of 0.05, we identified 210 significant associations between GReX and PD. These findings span all 19 tissue models and implicate the GReX of 74 different genes (Fig. 2b). There is a special interest in the utility of whole blood. Unlike solid organ biopsies, whole blood is routinely gathered from patients in a non-invasive, low-risk procedure. Prior studies suggest that the transcriptomic profile of whole blood is less predictive of brain features than imputed transcriptomic profiles derived from brain-based models16. In our TWAS of PD, the whole blood model generated 13 significant gene-level associations (Fig. 2b). This number is greater than both the mean of all other models (t test p = 0.0119) as well as the mean across brain-derived models (t test p = 0.0218). The absolute number of identified genes is greater than the mean of other models. However, we must note that the whole blood model was trained on far more samples, likely owing to the ease of collecting blood (Fig. 2d). There are significantly fewer associations in whole blood than for the other models when adjusting for the number of samples used for model training (t test, p = 4.46e–8) (Table S1). We noted further that only 6 of the 13 genes (46%) detected by the whole blood model are detected by other gene models meaning that 53% of the genes are specific to the whole blood model (Fig. S1). This is greater than the average percentage of model-specific findings across the other models (t test, p = 2.64e–9). Observing that the whole blood model yielded fewer TWAS results than expected under a null hypothesis and produced fewer results that were replicated in other models, we assessed the overall correlation in TWAS associations across all models. The mean correlation in z-scores between all models and the whole blood model is 0.200, significantly less than the corresponding mean of 0.399 across all other models (t test p = 2.39e–15). The TWAS using whole blood demonstrates greater dissimilarity with the other models than do the brain models.

Sensitivity analyses of TWAS of PD

Nalls et al. report eQTL-driven analyses of their PD GWAS using multi-SNP summary-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR)8. This method identifies associations between a phenotype and GReX using a formalized approach to Mendelian randomization that differs from PrediXcan in its statistical handling of potential confounding variables. Seventeen of our 74 TWAS results (23%) were also identified by the SMR approach. We also performed a TWAS of the same PD GWAS using the FUSION methodology. While PrediXcan uses Elastic-Net as a penalized regression model for associating SNPs with gene expression, FUSION employs a Bayesian sparse linear mixed model. Both evaluate associations between GReX and a trait of interest. In contrast to the degree of overlap with SMR, 4 of the JTI-significant GReX associations tested in FUSION reached a nominal significance threshold of 0.05, a rate of 5.4%. These genes, SNRNP200, ORC4, RBM12B, and ASF1A, are each involved with maintenance and modification of nucleic acids18–21.

Tractography-wide analysis of white matter in idiopathic PD

We identified associations between named white matter tracts and the 74 genes identified in the PD discovery TWAS using our NeuroimaGene resource (Fig. 3a). NeuroimaGene is a major compendium of associations between GReX and MRI-derived brain measurements from a representative sample of ~33,000 European individuals. The affiliated R tool enables association testing between GReX for specific genes or gene sets and over 3535 neuroimaging derived phenotypes (NIDPs) across multiple MRI modalities. The tool functions as an interface to query the NeuroimaGene brain atlas. This allows users to identify neurological consequences of changes in genetically regulated gene expression across the transcriptome. Using this resource, we identified seven white matter tracts whose that were associated with PD GReX (Table S2). Each of these measures surpassed a study-wide statistical significance threshold of 0.05. Each association identifies a white matter tract whose microstructure is associated with expression of PD genes. These findings are derived from patients without documented PD, implicating the white matter tracts as potentially endogenous targets of PD genes. These white matter tracts involve two cortical projection fibers (the medial lemniscus and left superior thalamic radiation) as well as four association fibers (bilateral superior longitudinal fasciculus, right uncinate fasciculus, and left inferior frontal occipital fasciculus) (Fig. 3b). Of note, the projection fiber associations are involved in somatomotor signaling22,23. These findings reached study-wide significance in the most tissue models relative to the other white matter tracts (Fig. 3c). Cortex-wide evaluation of grey matter in idiopathic PD

We performed similar analysis of grey matter features with PD-associated GReX using the Desikan cortical atlas and Freesurfer’s automated segmentation of the subcortical regions. These associations were derived from the NeuroimaGene tool described above using a study-wide significance of 0.05, adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate. This analysis identified 151 associations involving 15 genes and 40 cortical and subcortical measures (Figs. 3e, f, S2, Table S3). These represent neuroimaging-derived characterizations of cortical and subcortical morphology that are associated with GReX of PD genes. The cortical measure with the greatest mean effect size magnitude from PD-associated GReX describes the global cortical thickness of the right hemisphere (Fig. 3e). Additionally, 75% of the associations for NIDPs reflecting cortical area demonstrate a negative effect size. This finding is in opposition to associations with cortical thickness measures in which 86% of GReX-NIDP associations have a positive effect size. Thus, reductions in gene dosage for the PD genes lead to both a reduction in cortical thickness and an increase in surface area. Volume measures skew negatively with 63% of associations having a negative effect size (Fig. 3g). The genes EFNA1 and SMCR5 are associated with both white matter and grey matter associations.

Colocalization of PD genes and multi-modal prioritization

We performed colocalization analyses of PD-associated genes and eQTLs from the 13 GTEx brain regions. This analysis prioritizes genes that have eQTLs implicated in the iPD GWAS taking linkage disequilibrium into account. There were 265 genes that surpassed a nominal significance threshold of 0.05. Following prioritization based on statistical significance in both gene expression and PD, we identified 208 eQTLs for 39 genes as our top candidates (Table S4). We used eQTpLot to visualize the relationship between eQTLs and PD GWAS SNPs (Fig. 4a–d)24. For each gene-tissue-SNP triad, we assessed SNP localization within gene regulatory elements using CoRE-BED (Fig. 4e)25. CoRE-BED uses chromatin-based annotations to determine the overlap of SNPs with gene regulatory elements such as promotors and enhancers. We restricted our analysis to regulatory element predictions derived from cell and tissue models trained on the brain (Table S5). Using this resource, we annotated each of the eQTL/PD colocalization SNPs by the context-specific gene regulatory elements with which they are associated. Our strongest finding involved the previously identified effect of rs10847864 on the expression of hypoxia inducible factor 1r (HIP1R)27. The posterior probability for this gene was 0.999 and the SNP itself is predicted to localize to an active enhancer according to CoRE-BED chromatin annotations. HIP1R is most highly detected in oligodendrocyte precursor cells, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes (Fig. S3, Table S6). Our next strongest finding involves the effect of rs12930545 on expression of Syntaxin 1b. The posterior probability for this gene was 0.953 and the SNP localizes to an active enhancer according to CoRE-BED. STX1B is most highly detected in neuronal cells including horizontal cells, excitatory neurons, and inhibitory neurons (Fig. 4f, Table S7).

We leveraged cNIDPs for PD from the ENIGMA neuroimaging study, which identified 43 T1-derived brain measures that differ significantly between individuals with PD (2357) and healthy controls (1182). We draw on these data as a set of empirical PD cNIDPs for cross-examination of our transcriptomic analyses. Specifically, we identified the total number of cNIDPs that are captured by the neuroimaging TWAS approach. We used NeuroimaGene to quantify the associations between PD-TWAS genes and the 43 cNIDPs. NeuroimaGene assesses associations using 19 different tissue models (Fig. 5b). These tissues have shared regulatory architecture and thus the tissue models ought not be treated as independent tests. Accordingly, we used principal component analysis to derive the number of effectively independent tissue-brain pairs in the data (Fig. 5c–e). The first two principal components largely reflect the hemisphere of the brain and the measurement type. Annotating NIDP/tissue pairs by the region of the brain demonstrated visual clustering as well. Notably, we did not observe clear clustering across the first two principal components on the criteria of tissue model (Fig. S4). Of the 817 NIDP/tissue pairs assessed, 95% of NeuroimaGene variance was captured by the first 384 principal components. We thus used 384 as the number of effectively independent NIDP/tissue pairs for multiple testing correction (Methods).

We next performed an enrichment analysis to assess whether the clinically ascertained cNIDPs overlap more with the NIDPs derived from PD-TWAS genes than random gene sets. We first quantified the number of associations between the 74 PD genes and the empirical cNIDPs using the modified Bonferroni significance threshold of 0.05. We then repeated this analysis 1000 times using random sets of 74 genes to generate a null distribution. The number of cNIDP associations tagged by PD genes was less than both the 95th percentile and the median for the null distribution (Fig. 5g; Methods). The number of unique NIDPs tagged by PD-TWAS genes independently of tissue model context demonstrated a similar data distribution (Fig. S5).

Assessment of familial PD interaction genes

We next assessed the consequences of genes associated with familial Parkinson’s disease. It has been suggested that the molecular mechanisms of familial Parkinson’s disease are different than those of idiopathic PD28,29. Familial PD typically arises from rare, large-effect variants in select protein-coding genes. By contrast, causal TWAS associations result from the aggregation of multiple SNPs with low to moderate effect sizes. We proposed that both familial and idiopathic PD result from disruption of the same biochemical networks. Specifically, we suspect that familial PD genes constitute critical components in these networks which cause PD when lost. The PD TWAS analysis may capture generalized disruption of molecular moieties that interact with these core PD genes such that similar phenotypes are observed.

We test this hypothesis by first using protein-protein interaction data to identify 159 genes with evidence for interaction with 6 well-described familial Parkinson’s genes: PARK2, PARK7, VPS35, PINK1, SNCA, and LRRK2 (Fig. 6A). The STRING database contains information on interactions between proteins derived from a wide variety of studies and reports30. Restricting our analysis to those with ‘high’ levels of evidence (see Methods), we identify networks of protein-protein interactions for the 6 described fPD genes. The smaller, highly connected network is centered on VPS35 and involves 13 interactors, 11 of which are part of the retromer complex. This gene network includes sorting nexins, vacuole protein sorting proteins, and a TBC1 domain protein, all of which are involved in endosomal and vesicular regulation. Protein degradation mechanisms including ubiquitin and the proteasome are of interest given the pathological build-up of protein aggregates in PD. We then assessed the fPD interaction genes for overlap with genes associated with iPD via the TWAS methodology. These two different categories of PD did not share any gene-level associations (Fig. 6b). While the gene-level bases of fPD and iPD may differ, we continued to assess for convergence at other levels of biology. We performed NeuroimaGene analysis of the fPD interaction genes and compared the resulting NIDPs with those identified by iPD TWAS genes. The fPD network genes were associated with relatively few NIDPs. Two NIDPs were associated with both iPD genes and fPD network genes, both of which are occipital regions (Fig. 6c). In fPD, the area of the right lateral occipital cortex was associated with GReX of alpha-synuclein interacting protein (SNCAIP). Alpha-synuclein is the protein aggregate most associated with protein inclusions known as pick bodies that often present in individuals with PD. The second duplicated NIDP is the left pericalcarine cortex which demonstrated an association with LRP5 (Fig. 6d, e). Both the genes, protein interactors, and neurological consequences of iPD and fPD transcriptomics constitute largely non-overlapping sets. We did not observe evidence supporting a convergence of fPR and iPD on the same biochemical networks. Visualization of the fPD network associated brain regions highlight a predominately posterior distribution of NIDPs (Fig. 6f).

Discussion

We extend upon the TWAS methodology to further characterize the molecular features of Parkinson’s disease and their downstream consequences. TWAS findings constitute associations between genetically determined gene expression and disease. Through our multi-modal colocalization approaches, we supplement several of these associations with promising molecular mechanisms. The PD-associated SNP, rs12930545, is a strong eQTL for STX1B and occurs in an enhancer less than 200 kb from the gene’s transcription start site. Single-cell RNA sequencing data highlights neurons as the dominant cellular context for STX1B expression. Given its proposed function of regulating the exocytosis of vesicles at the neuronal synapse, syntaxin 1B may contribute to the pathophysiology of Parkinson’s disease by disrupting the homeostatic regulation of release of specific neurotransmitters involved in PD such as dopamine or serotonin. Consistent with formal Mendelian randomization, the high correlation and the magnitude of association p-values for rs12930545 in STX1B expression and PD suggests a causal role for the expression of STX1B in PD. Additionally, we provide evidence in support of a role for HIP1R in PD. We replicate findings implicating rs10847864 as a causal SNP that affects the expression of HIP1R by modifying a predicted enhancer for the gene. We extend these data by additionally highlighting oligodendrocytes and astrocytes as the primary cell type in which the gene is expressed. HIP1R is a component of clathrin coated pits involved in endocytosis31. Given these data, we suspect that HIP1R mediated dysregulation of the process by which glial cells interact with neurotransmitters in the synapse may contribute to the molecular basis of PD.

When we analyzed the subset of NIDPs that are modified secondary to GReX of PD-associated genes, the findings implicated elements of the somatomotor circuits of the brain. STBD1 is associated with the organization of key white matter tracts responsible for sensory and motor communication between the brain and body. The laterality of this finding remains an open question as the association is with the left hemisphere. The effect of STBD1 expression on the right superior thalamic radiation is significantly less (Fig. S6). The top cortical finding involves the global mean thickness of the right hemisphere and the same measure for the left hemisphere passes statistical significance as well. Together, these NIDP level analyses point to broad, relatively non-specific effects of PD genes on the cortical grey matter alongside a narrower, symptom-relevant set of white matter tract associations.

We noted further that across the idiopathic data, the effect of PD-associated GReX on the brain is consistent with cortical degeneration. The median effect size of PD genes on significant cortical thickness measures was strongly positive. This finding indicates that reduction in the expression of PD genes is predictive of a thinner cortex. The effect of PD genes on cortical area is negative, indicating that a reduction in the expression of these genes predicts greater surface area. Collectively, these findings suggest that the aggregate effect of reduction in the expression of the PD TWAS genes is a thinner cortex with greater surface area, reflective of a degenerative pattern at the cortical level.

Additionally, the effect sizes of PD TWAS genes on white matter organization as measured through fractional anisotropy are uniformly positive. These findings suggest that reduced expression is associated with greater disorganization of the white matter bundles. Because these results are derived from patients without Parkinson’s disease, we believe that these data linking genes and the brain represent intrinsic, homeostatic relationships that are disrupted or exacerbated in the context of PD.

Our study illustrates that transcriptomic risk factors of PD affect the neuromorphology of the brain. It has been hypothesized that the changes in the brain that occur in the context of PD reflect a combination of genetic factors, environmental exposures, and the progression of disease pathology. We assess the relative contribution of PD GReX to a clinically derived PD neuroimaging profile (Fig. 5). It appears that the neurological effects of PD GReX constitute a small proportion of the brain changes that occur in individuals with PD. This is unsurprising given the profound downstream effects that Parkinsonism itself has on the brain. In the course of PD, there are many non-genetic drivers of neurological change that occur alongside genetic risk factors. These include environmentally mediated neurodegeneration, compensatory remodeling, the accumulation of alpha synuclein aggregates, neurologically active medications, and more. These changes, many of which are volatile and externally mediated, can overwhelm the relatively subtle effects of GReX. This finding differs from conclusions drawn about schizophrenia in a prior publication16. NeuroimaGene analyses of schizophrenia associated gene expression were significantly more predictive of schizophrenia cNIDPs neuroimaging than random gene sets. Schizophrenia is a highly heritable disease with heavy involvement of axonal projections and cellular connections. These features appear to be well characterized by genetic variation. Our data illustrate that PD genes are no more predictive of PD cNIDPs than random gene sets. We interpret these findings as evidence that the neurological consequences of having active, manifest PD overwhelm the gene-driven brain changes that predicate the disease.

We did not observe evidence of convergence between the protein interactors of fPD genes and iPD TWAS results. This may be for several reasons including a failure due to an insufficiently powered test. The STRING protein-protein interaction database captures only a fraction of protein-protein interactions. Many relevant interactions may only occur in specific cell types under specific circumstances, many of which have yet to be documented. In this scenario, there may yet be interactions that have not been identified. Alternatively, there may be no true common genetic convergence for fPD and iPD. These two forms of the condition may be mediated by pathways that converge at a higher level of biology. While our data are not strong enough to merit rejecting the null hypothesis, neither do they rule out the potential for convergence. The diversity of different proteins and cell contexts needed for an experimental evaluation of protein-protein interactions is likely prohibitively demanding, however, methods in computational modeling may enable the broad screening such an analysis would require.

These findings are subject to several important limitations. We do not yet know which of the examined tissue contexts is causal. GTEx has shown that there is substantial sharing of regulatory architecture across tissues25 which may lead to the misattribution of gene-disease findings to closely related tissues. As such, we avoid emphasizing that our discovered associations are specific to unique tissues. A benefit of shared tissue architecture is that a substantial component of brain-specific pathophysiology may be captured in non-brain tissues, further supporting the relevance of the results we describe above. Additionally, findings derived from whole blood may be more clinically relevant owing to the ease and safety with which patient blood can be acquired. TWAS findings from whole blood generally differed from those produced by brain-based models. The biological mechanisms by which changes in brain expression impact the brain are likely to differ from the mechanisms by which hematogenous factors affect the brain. We hypothesize that the observed discrepancy in blood vs brain transcriptomics in the context of PD is representative of this biological difference. Nevertheless, additional analyses would be needed for proper assessment of this hypothesis as these findings could be representative of false positive associations mediated by tissue misattribution. Although the role of NIDPs in mediating the association between GReX and disease will require additional studies, we demonstrate stringent evidence for both associations between GReX and NIDPs as well as GReX and disease. We acknowledge the absence of a gold standard neuroimaging signature of Parkinson’s disease against which we could assess the specificity of the NIDP associations described above. Additionally, we use a strict significance threshold that may introduce type II error. As a result, it is not possible to rule out the potential that the neurological effect of PD GReX is actually diffuse neurodegeneration and we are only capturing a subset of these NIDPs as a result of our significance threshold. While further analyses across computational, cohort, and biological methods are necessary to better distinguish between these systems, both scenarios support our overarching claim that transcriptomic variation in PD is intrinsically associated with neurological changes. Relatedly, while cell type specificity analyses aid in contextualization of specific genes, these cell types reported by the Human Protein Atlas are broad and thus are unable to distinguish between the hundreds of subtypes and cell states that may be of relevance to PD. Expression models trained on specific groups of cells such as the dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra would be of particular interest. Nevertheless, the identification of gene-specific effects in PD to individual cell types represents an unprecedented level of resolution regarding neuroimaging-based transcriptomics in this context.

Regarding fine mapping, the localization of a SNP to a gene regulatory element is not proof that the SNP has an effect. However, we identify cases where eQTLs for genes lie within nearby enhancers; given that these are known eQTLs with significant disease associations and gene expression associations, we consider these mechanistic insights to be compelling. The neuroimaging findings represent associations between imputed GReX and NIDPs that were measured in individuals without PD. It remains possible that these endogenous associations are not relevant or descriptive of brain-gene relationships that characterize PD. Assessing this question would necessitate further PD-specific transcriptomic and neuroimaging studies. Lastly, the source data for these analyses were largely derived from populations of European ancestry. While the similarity of the genomic background helps avoid ancestrally mediated genetic confounding, our findings may be less generalizable to individuals from other genetic ancestries. Replication of these analyses using genetic datasets that reflect the broader sweep of humankind is crucial for improving the utility and generalizability of our findings.

Altogether, the analyses presented in this work provide further evidence for the significance and biological context of specific genes in the progression of PD. STX1B and HIP1R represent two genes both involved in vesicular regulation31,32. Vesicular regulation is central to homeostasis of neurotransmitters such as dopamine and serotonin, two primary drivers of PD pathology and treatment. Furthermore, our analysis of neuroimaging transcriptomics brings together molecular and neuroimaging endophenotypes in PD to promote a unified arc of associations from SNPs through enhancers to genes, neuroimaging regions, and ultimately symptom-associated brain networks.

Methods

TWAS of idiopathic Parkinson’s disease

We performed a TWAS of idiopathic Parkinson’s disease using summary statistics from the largest available GWAS meta-analysis (Table S8)8. A discovery TWAS was performed using the S-PrediXcan pipeline applied to gene expression models for 19 neurologically relevant tissues33. Thirteen tissues are brain specific and the other 6 demonstrate clinical relevance to neurologic health (Fig. 2). These models were generated using the joint tissue imputation (JTI) approach described in Zhou et al. whereby similarities in gene expression across tissues were leveraged to improve predictive power of individual models34. Significance was assessed via the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate of 0.05 across all transcript-tissue-disease associations.

FUSION TWAS of idiopathic Parkinson’s disease

For TWAS confirmation, we applied the FUSION protocol developed by Gusev et al. to the summary statistics from the iPD GWAS by Nalls et al. (Table S9)8,35. To perform expression predictions in the FUSION framework, we relied on the standard protocol, implementing gene expression models trained on GTEx consortium transcriptome data. We matched these models to the 19 tissues from the PrediXcan analysis15. We used a nominal p-value significance threshold of 0.05 as confirmation threshold.

Image-agnostic NeuroimaGene analysis of idiopathic PD genes

We identified neuroimaging features associated with iPD TWAS genes using NeuroimaGene (Tables S2-4)16. NeuroimaGene is an atlas of associations between NIDPs and GReX derived from a cohort of healthy individuals from the UK biobank. We selected fractional anisotropy measures for well-described white matter regions identified via probabilistic tractography (ProbtrackX). For cortical measures, we relied on regions captured via the Desikan atlas. In both cortical and white matter association studies, we used an atlas-wide false discovery rate of 0.05 as the significance threshold. We refer to these MRI based neuroimaging derived phenotypes as NIDPs. For visual depiction of brain regions, we utilized the ggseg and ggseg3d family of packages in R36.

Identification of clinical Parkinson’s associated NIDPs

We identified an empirical set of iPD-associated NIDPs from a clinical neuroimaging study conducted by the ENIGMA-PD Working Group (Table S10)37. T1-weighted MRI scans had been collected between 2016 and 2019 across 19 sites and 20 countries. These data were generated from 2357 PD patients and 1182 controls. All results were adjusted for age, sex, and cohort. Laansma et al. report differences between patients and controls for neuroimaging measurements reflecting subcortical volume and regional cortical subcortical area and thickness37. Regional parcellations were conducted using Freesurfer 5.3. These NIDPs reflected 34 regions of interest based on the Desikan–Killiany atlas as well as 8 subcortical regions of interest. We focused only on those regions which demonstrated statistically significant differences across patients and controls (p ≤ 0.001, Table S10). We refer to these clinically derived NIDPs as cNIDPs. The same Desikan–Killiany atlas parcellation is present in the NeuroimaGene resource. Accordingly, we selected these results as a set of PD-associated cNIDPs for further analysis.

Neuroimaging enrichment analysis in idiopathic PD

We performed a NeuroimaGene analysis to identify all associations between GReX from our PD TWAS and the PD-associated NIDPs from the ENIGMA PD neuroimaging study. We corrected for multiple hypothesis testing (Table S11) using a modified Bonferroni significance threshold. The standard Bonferroni correction assumes that each test is independent. Because our analysis tests for the same GReX-NIDP associations in multiple correlated tissue contexts, this assumption of independence is not met. Specifically, the unique genes from the PD TWAS were tested for associations with 43 NIDPs in 19 different tissue contexts (817 NIPD/tissue pairs). Given the non-independence of the NIDP/tissue pairs, we applied principal component analysis to the full set of all GReX effect sizes for all 817 NIDP/tissue pairs. We then identified the number of principal components accounting for 95% of the variation in the data as the number of effectively independent NIDP/tissue pairs. The final threshold was determined as follows:

0.05 / (67 PD GReX * 384 NIDP/Tissue pairs) = 1.76E-6

Of note, only 67 of the 74 genes were testable using NeuroimaGene as reflected in the equation. Using this threshold, we then identified all significant associations between iPD genes and the NIDPs from the ENIGMA study. We generated a null distribution to define how many gene-level associations we would expect to identify by chance. We selected 67 random genes (1000 times) and assessed the number of significant associations with the ENIGMA NIDPs. We then estimated the 95th percentile of the estimated null distribution as the threshold above which we would consider an enrichment to be statistically significant.

Single-cell transcription validation analysis of PD-neuroimaging genes

We assessed the cell-type specificity of the measured transcription of the PD neuroimaging genes using the single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from the Human Protein Atlas. This scRNA-seq dataset comprises mRNA expression as measured in 69 different cell types. We accessed the data in the form of transcripts per million using the online web portal38.

Colocalization analysis of eQTLs and iPD SNPs

We used the ‘coloc’ package to perform colocalization analyses between SNPs from the Nalls et al. PD GWAS meta-analysis and the eQTLs from the 19 tissues used in NeuroimaGene39. Prior to colocalization, we performed SNP harmonization and imputation of the GWAS according to the summary GWAS imputation tools created by the Im lab and hosted on GitHub, leveraging the 1000 genomes reference panel40. Using ‘coloc’, we calculated the posterior probability of shared causal variants for PD and gene expression41.

Gene prioritization after colocalization

We selected all genes from the colocalization study for which the posterior probability was >0.5 and that had a nominally significant p-value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document}≤0.05. For these target genes, we sought to identify top causal SNPs driving both expression and PD risk. We identified all eQTLs in a window 200 kb upstream and downstream of the gene that were colocalized with PD GWAS SNPs. We assigned each of these SNPs a p-value score defined as the sum of the negative log10 p-values for both gene expression and PD. For each gene-tissue pair with a posterior probability greater than 0.5, we prioritized the top ten SNPs according to the p-value score for further analysis (Table S4).

eQTpLot analysis of TWAS hits

We applied the eQTpLot package in R to visualize the relationship between PD GWAS SNPs and eQTLs for the genes prioritized by the TWAS analysis24. This package generates a locus-zoom style plot with accompanying panels detailing nearby genes, eQTL enrichment in GWAS SNPs, and relative significance values for SNPs associated with both phenotypes (gene expression and PD).

CoRE-BED annotation of gene regulatory elements

The gene prioritization steps described above generate a table of eQTLs that colocalize with iPD GWAS loci, inform expression of iPD TWAS genes, and occur within 200 kb of the target gene. We then used CoRE-BED chromatin-based annotations to determine the overlap of these SNPs with gene regulatory elements. CoRE-BED uses epigenomic features in multiple cell and tissue types to predict specific regulatory functions25. We restricted our analysis to regulatory element predictions derived from cell and tissue models trained on the brain (Table S5). Using this resource, we annotated each of the eQTL/PD colocalization SNPs by the context-specific gene regulatory elements with which they are associated.

Gene interaction identification in familial PD genes

We identified a set of genes associated with familial PD from a survey on the molecular genetics of PD42. These genes demonstrate evidence from family studies for being monogenic causes of PD. All 6 genes are protein-coding. We then determined a set of interactor proteins that have evidence for directly interacting with the proteins from the familial PD genes. To identify interactor genes, we leveraged the STRING protein-protein interaction database30. We subset findings to include those with either “experimental” or “database” evidence and a minimum interaction score of 0.7. We then collected the fPD genes and the genes for their protein interactors into a single set of fPD-related genes.

NeuroimaGene analysis of familial PD interaction genes

We performed NeuroimaGene analysis to identify neuroimaging features associated with the familial PD interaction genes. We used the Desikan cortical atlas features using a multiple testing correction threshold of 0.05 according to the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate. Plots were generated using the embedded plotting functions in the NeuroimaGene R package.
