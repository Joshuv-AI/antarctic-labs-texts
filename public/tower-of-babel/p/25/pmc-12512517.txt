Seroprevalence and molecular detection of human t-cell lymphotropic virus type 1 among multi –transfused patients in Sana’a City, Yemen
Authors: Yasser Ghaleb, Arwa Mohammed Othman, Rowa M Assayaghi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prevalence of Human T-Cell Lymphotropic Virus Type 1 (HTLV-1) represents a major public health challenge, threatening the safety of blood transfusions for patients globally, particularly in Yemen due to the lack of routine pre-transfusion screening for this type of virus. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and associated factors of HTLV-1 among multi-transfused patients (MTPs) in Sana’a city.MethodsAn analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among MTPs at medical treatment centers in Sana’a city. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a questionnaire that included sociodemographic information and predisposing factors of infection. A quantitative ELISA kit was used to detect HTLV-1 antigen in serum/plasma samples and a positive ELISA test was confirmed using real-time PCR. All patients were also screened for HIV, HBsAg and HCV. Bivariate and multivariate analysis were performed to identify associated factors.ResultsA total of 361 MTPs were enrolled. Of them, male 51.8% and aged less than 20 years 73.7%. Seroprevalence was higher in males, married individuals, and the 20–29 age group. Out of 361 samples, 12 (3.3) were initially reactive to HTLV1/2 antigen. The PCR results confirmed 10 samples (2.8%) were positive for HTLV-1. Only one patient had co-infection of HTLV-1 with HBsAg. Patients who have a low income (AOR:12.23;95%CI: 1.37-109.37), a medical history of blood transfusion of more than 30 units (AOR: 8.14; 95%CI:1.48–44.95), acupuncture (AOR:6.66;95%CI: 1.25–35.56), and dental clinic visits (OR = 5.84;95%CI:1.01–33.74) were significantly associated with HTLV-1.ConclusionsAlthough the prevalence rate of HTLV-1 was not high in Sana’a city, it remains an important problem among MTPs. Therefore, HTLV-1 screening should be mandatory in blood centers, and community awareness of risk factors must be increased.Keywords: Seroprevalence, Human t-cell lymphotropic virus type 1, ELISA, PCR, Yemen

Introduction

Blood transfusions save millions of lives worldwide each year, particularly among people who require regular blood transfusions, such as those with hematological disorders or cancer therapy. However, blood transfusion is among the essential vehicles for transmitting infections. These infections include viral, bacterial, parasitic, and prion. The most prominent among these are the Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) and Hepatitis C Virus (HCV), Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), Human T-cell Lymphotropic Virus (HTLV-I/II), syphilis, and malaria [1].

HTLV belongs to the family Retroviridae and the genus Deltaretrovirus with four types, where HTLV-1 and HTLV-2 are the most studied subtypes of HTLV. The HTLV-1 is an enveloped, single-stranded RNA and is associated with some severe diseases such as HTLV-1 associated myelopathy/tropical spastic paraparesis (HAM/TSP) and adult T-cell leukemia (ATL), whereas the HTLV-2 was not yet clearly associated with any human disorder. HTLV-1 is transmitted primarily through infected bodily fluids, including blood, breast milk, and semen. Injecting drug use is also a risk factor for HTLV-1 infection [2–4].

Mandatory HTLV-1 screening of all blood donations has been implemented in 23 countries. Screening tests for HTLV-1 should be followed by confirmatory tests for the diagnosis of HTLV-1. Several studies have proposed transitioning from using western blot for confirmation in routine testing to using line immunoassay or nucleic acid test [4]. Real-time PCR or quantitative PCR (qPCR) has been used to determine the real status of HTLV infections and quantify the proviral load (PVL) [5].

Globally, HTLV-1 is estimated to be between five and 10 million individuals are infected with HTLV-1 [6]. Published studies reveal a scarce distribution of the virus in the African countries of Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMRO), while a lower prevalence is denoted in the Asian countries of EMRO, except in Iran, where the prevalence is high [7]. The overall HTLV1 prevalence in Saudi Arabia was 1.7/100,000 donations and prevalence among blood donors in Qatar and Egypt was 0.18% and 1.11%, respectively [7–9].

In Yemen, there are no published studies and a lack of information of HTLV-1 prevalence among patients and healthy populations. Regarding hemoglobinopathy, previous studies mentioned that thalassemia and sickle cell disease (SCD) are a serious problem in Yemen, where the prevalence of sickle-cell trait (HbAS) is 2.2%, and the prevalence of thalassemia was 26.4% [10–13]. On the other side, leukemia is one of the most common cancers and the main cause of mortality in children with a prevalence of 5.7% [14, 15].

Furthermore, Transfusion-Transmissible Infections (TTIs) in Yemen are the greatest threats to blood safety for multi-transfused patients (MTPs) who are suffering from haemoglobinopathies, bleeding disorders, bone marrow failures and leukaemias and pose a serious public health problem due to the war consequences and conflict [16, 17]. HTLV-1 antibody screening of blood donations has not been implemented in Yemeni blood transfusion services. Since both leukemic patients and patients with hemoglobinopathies are more prone to blood multi-transfusion, this puts them at higher risk for HTLV infection. This study aimed to determine the seroprevalence and associated risk factors for HTLV1 among multi-transfused patients (thalassemia, SCD and leukemia) in Sana’a city.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting and population

An analytical cross-sectional study was conducted on multi-transfused patients in Sana’a city from September 2023 to June 2024. This study was conducted among attendants of the Yemeni Society for Thalassemia and Genetic Blood Disorders, National Oncology Center (NOC), and the Childhood of Leukemia unit at Al Kuwait University Hospital in Sana’a city. Sana’a is the capital of the Republic of Yemen and is located in the western part of the country at an elevation of more than 7,200 feet (2,200 m) above sea level and has a population of approximately 3,292,497 people.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All patients with sickle cell anemia, thalassemia, and leukemia who attended the medical centers requiring regular transfusions at any age and who had received at least three units of blood or blood products and agreed to give their written consent were included in this study. The exclusion criteria were patients who had been transfused less than three units of blood or blood products, had a history of other hematological disorders, and who received non-allogeneic blood transfusions. Furthermore, leukemia patients who received chemotherapy and who do not agree to give their written consent were excluded.

Sample size and sampling procedure

The sample size was calculated using Epi- Info assuming a population size of 6000 patients according to the last register, a margin of type I error of 5%, confidence level of 95%, an estimated HTLV-1 prevalence of 50%, and a design effect 1. The sample size was calculated to be 361 participants. This sample size of participants would be required to detect a significant association of infection with 80% power. According to data analysis results of NOC and Yemeni Society for Thalassemia and Genetic Blood Disorders, the sample was selected using the Population Propionate Sampling (PPS) technique where 53 Thalassemia patients, 275 Sickle cell anemia patients and 33 Leukemia were chosen from the study population. PPS technique used when population units vary in size and these sizes are known as well as units with larger sizes have more chance to be selected. Patients who were available during the data collection period were selected sequentially.

Data collection

Data was collected through face-to-face interviews with patients aged above 18 years and from mothers or caregivers for patients below 18 years using a predesigned questionnaire. It was developed based on previously published studies to obtain a comprehensive validated [16–22]. Both closed and open-ended questions covered demographic, socioeconomic characteristics, and associated risk factors of HTLV-1 infection were included in the questionnaire. Before data collection, we piloted the study questionnaire with 20 patients to assess its comprehensibility and timeliness of administration and not included in the final analysis of data. All identified gaps were addressed and some questions are removed.

Sample collection

Peripheral blood (5 ml) was collected from each patient in tube with anti-coagulant which used for provirus DNA extraction and 5 ml in plain tube. The samples were transported to the virology laboratory of National Blood Transfusion and Research Center. Plasma/serum was separated by centrifugation at 3000–4000 rpm for 5–10 min and stored at frozen at − 40◦C until serological testing by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and molecular confirmation by Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) were performed.

Serological assay

The ELISA Kit is a Sandwich enzyme immunoassay technique for the in vitro quantitative determination of HTLV antigens concentration in serum (ELISA Kit; HTLV I + II, Wuhan Feiyue Biotechnology Co. Ltd) was used. The sensitivity of the Kit was 1.85 U/ml and used for laboratory scientific research only. Manual ELISA device (Bio-Rad, France) was used to perform the test steps and read the results.

The micro-ELISA plate provided in the kite has been precoated with an antibody specific to HTLV. Standards or samples are added to the plate wells and combined with the specific antibodies. Then Horseradish Peroxides (HRP) labeled detection antibodies is added to each micro plate and incubate. And then. TMB substrate solution is added and incubate. An enzyme-catalyzed reaction generates a blue color in the solution, thereafter, stop solution is added to stop the substrate reaction and the color turn yellow. The yellow solution is read at the wavelength of 450 nm. The concentration of Human of HTLV in the sample is then calculated from the OD value by the establishing a standard curve that determined by regression analysis. The sample with a concentration of HTLV (concentration related to viral particle per volume) ≥ 65U/ml was considered positive. All steps of testing were performed according to the instructions sheet inside the kits.

Molecular assay (real-time PCR)

Any positive sample was confirmed by Real-Time PCR. Extraction of HTLV-I proviral DNA was performed by using Kit Ribo virus (Sacace, Biotechnologies company). Real-Time PCR (TIAN LONG, Chain) was used for the Qualitative and Quantitative detection of proviral DNA in samples by reagent kit from (Wuhan Feiyue Biotechnology Co.Ltd) for scientific research. This reagent uses TagMan probe method for real-time fluorescence PCR technology, and designs specific Tax gene primers of the HTLV-1(Forward Primer:

5’-CGGATACCCAGTCTACGTGT-3’ and Reverse Primer: 5’-GAGCCGATAACGCGTC CATCG-3’). The kit contains, 2* Probe qPCR Master Mix, special template dilution, ultrapure water, proviral PCR primer probe dry powder and HTLV-1 proviral PCR positive control (1*107 copies/µl). The qPCR reactions were prepared after preparation of standard curve samples as: 10 µl of 2* Probe qPCR Master Mix, 3 µl of HTLV-1 provirus PCR probe Mixture, 7µl DNA templates, 7µl of ultrapure water and 7µl dilution solution of standard curve sample. TIAN LONG thermocycler was used to amplify LTR region with the following amplification cycling program: one cycle of 95 °C for 5 min, 95 °C for 15 s and 40 cycles of 45 °C for 60 s.

Statistical analysis

Data was entered and analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 26). Data was presented as frequencies and percentages. Cross-tabulation was used to identify associated factors with HTLV1 using the Chi-squared test for categorical and T-test for continuous variables. Odds ratios (OR), Adjusted Odd Ratios (AOR), and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated as well as all variables with p ≤ 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression. The cut-off for statistical significance was P-value < 0.05.

Discussion

HTLV-1 is an unrecognized issue in Yemen due to underestimation of the total number of infected individuals, lack of data and screening policies and guidelines of HTLV-1 testing. The estimation of the magnitude of HTLV-1 infections and its associated factors among MTPs will help to determine the prevalence and establish effective national blood screening strategies to protect these vulnerable groups from infection.

The worldwide prevalence of HTLV-1 infection is highly heterogeneous, with a global prevalence of 0.91%. In high human development index (HDI) countries, the observed prevalence was 0.41% and 1.18% in low HI countries. In the general population, the observed prevalence was 1.65% and 0.34% in pregnant women and in blood donors 0.04% [18]. Higher prevalence of HTLV-1 among MTPs was found in our study 2.8%. This finding is nearly similar to a prior study in Jiroft, Iran 2.5% [19] but lower than studies conducted in Tehran 11.3% [20] and Sudan 6.6 [21]. Furthermore, our result is higher than the prevalence reported among receiving blood in Zahedan 1.1% [22] and Iraq 1.2% [23]. The large differences in the prevalence rate of HTLV-1 among MTPs in different countries might be due to the differences in prevalence in geographical locations, mean age of patients, different screening methods as well as type of study population.

In the current study, the findings revealed a high prevalence of HTLV-1 among males, married, illiterate and low-income patients, these results agree with studies conducted in Iran [21], Sudan [24] and disagree with the study performed in Southeast Brazil [25]. Lower literacy and income may be associated with limited access to health care and education, which leds to the increasing the risk of HTLV-1 transmission. Furthermore, the highest prevalence of the infection was observed among patients from Al Mahwit governorate. This may be attributed to a lack of awareness among the population, low income of family members and more research are needed to explain the increase in HTLV-1 infection in this governorate.

Thalassemia patients are at risk for infection with blood-borne pathogens, including human T-cell lymphotropic viruses (HTLV) that are transmitted by transfusion of cellular blood products [26]. The prevalence among thalassemia patients in the current study was 3.8% which is consistent with recorded findings in Kermanshah 3.6% [27]. In contrast, a high prevalence was reported by a previous study 6% and lower prevalence 1.3% of HTLV revealed by Switzer et al. [26, 28],. In addition, patients with sickle cell anemia (SCA) had an abnormal susceptibility to blood-borne infections [29]. In the current study, the prevalence of HTLV-1 among SCA was 2.9%. High prevalence was reported from New York City 4%, Martinique 10% and lower prevalence was reported in West Indies 0.0% [28–30].

HTLV − 1 is a well-established risk for developing Hematological malignancies, including ATL [21]. Studies conducted in Sudan [21], Iraq [23] and Tehran [31] found the prevalence among leukemia patients was 6.6%, 1.2%, and 20%, respectively. This disagreement with our findings which showed negative results among leukemia patients. The difference in the results may be attributed to the small sample size of leukemia patients in this study.

To minimize the risk of HTLV-1 transmission to laboratory and other healthcare workers as well as patients, providing regular training and education on HTLV-1 handling and safety protocols. Previous study found that most of the investigated blood donors underwent invasive procedures, such as surgery, transfusion, acupuncture, or teeth washing, and one of them received a blood transfusion, which can help in HTLV infection [32]. Regarding associated factors with HTLV-1 in this study, our finding presented HTLV-1 was significantly higher among patients who had a previous history of blood receiving more than 30 units, acupuncture, dental visits or had low income. These findings are consistent with study in Mashhad [22]. The significant association between transfusion blood and infection may be the absence of HTLV screening tests in centers and blood banks in Yemen. Furthermore, acupuncture and dental visits findings could be affected by recall or reporting bias, which are considered the main limitations to this study.

Breastfeeding longer than six months is one of the HTLV-1 transmission routes [33]. The present study showed high frequencies of breastfeeding and positivity rate for > 6 months of MTPs compared with less than 6 months, but this difference was not statistically significant. Lack of variability about prolonged breastfeeding might be a reason that we could not find a significant association between the mode of feeding in the infancy period and the prevalence of the infection [24].

Multiple transfusions are required frequently in patients with thalassemia, sickle cell anemia, and leukemia which present them with an increased risk of TTIs. Those patients have a high risk of acquiring HBV, HCV, HIV and other blood-borne infections [34]. The seroprevalence of HBsAg, anti-HCV and HIV in this study was 3.4%, 2.9% and 0%, respectively. This prevalence was lower than that reported by a previous study in Sana’a, Yemen [35]. The decrease in prevalence could be due to the improved screening techniques of HBV and HCV in blood transfusion centers and blood banks. Furthermore, the low HIV in our study also may reflect the low HIV prevalence among Yemeni blood donors.

This is the first study conducted in Yemen, which is a suitable design to determine the prevalence of HTLV-1 among MTPs and the findings will be used to raise awareness among decision makers and health service providers regarding HTLV infection and its associated factors and to ensure the safety of blood transfusion services. However, the main limitation of this study was the assessment of predisposing factors of HTLV-1 infection conducted through self‑reporting which may have led to recall and social desirability bias. In additional, certain behavioral risk factors are not researched, such as intravenous drug use, paid sex, and sex with multiple partners due to societal customs and traditions, which are found among the Yemeni community, and the patients who were chosen in the pilot study refuse to answer these inquiries. Furthermore, the study was conducted in Sana’a City; the results cannot be generalized to the whole country.

Conclusion

Our findings raise an alarm to the existence of a high risk of HTLV-1 among MTPs and high seroprevalence among males, aged between 20 and 30 years, married, and illiterate patients. In addition, patients who received more than 30 blood units, had low income, had a history of acupuncture, or visited dental clinics were significantly associated with HTLV-1 infection. Therefore, HTLV-1 screening should be performed mandatorily in blood centers to reduce its spread and improve economic status (i.e. income) to decrease vulnerability of HTLV infection. Establishment of HTLV infection surveillance system, increasing awareness among communities with risk factors of transmission and future studies in the whole country of patients and healthy individuals are strongly recommended to improve prevention and control strategies and to protect patients from potential risks.
