Multimodal PET–MR segmentation for glioblastoma: complementarity for treatment planning and recurrence definition
Authors: Alejandro Mora-Rubio, Dejan Kuhn, Carolina Bravo-Vergara, Tobias Fechter, Melissa Mauer, Maria Beser-Robles, Irene Marín Radoszynski, Rocío Catalán Flores, Johannes Fostitsch, Pablo García Verdú, Michael Mix, Dimos Baltas, Luis Martí-Bonmatí, Anca L Grosu, Ilinca Popp, Montserrat Carles

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn patients with Glioblastoma (GBM), Magnetic Resonance (MR) is used for tumour diagnosis and treatment planning. Positron Emission Tomography (PET) with O-(2)-18 F-Fluoroethyl-L-Tyrosine (FET) has been recommended to distinguish local recurrence from radiogenic alterations. However, clinical practice remains hindered by the time and expertise required for tumour and organs-at-risk (OARs) segmentation and the limited evidence of the added value of PET and its restricted availability across clinical centres. This study presents automatic segmentation models and a comprehensive evaluation of PET/MR complementary biological information for recurrent disease definition.MethodsThe nnU-Net was employed for segmentation using manually defined contours on 1,610 patients from 33 institutions. Model performance was evaluated by Dice-Sørensen-Coefficient (DSC). PET/MR recurrence complementarity was evaluated in 185 patients by Wilcoxon-Signed-Rank test (WSRT), DSC and radiomic features (RF). In RF analysis, MR-Enhancing subregions were classified as MR∩PET or MR-Only, based on overlap with PET. For MR-RF showing significant MR∩PET/MR-Only differences (WSRT), discrimination was further assessed by classifying RF for 3 × 3 × 3-voxel subregions within MR-Enhancing in two volumes (greater/less than the RF cohort median) and evaluating Positive-Predictive-Value and Sensitivity with MR∩PET/MR-Only.ResultsModels’ performance in the test set resulted in DSC(MR-Enhancing) = 0.76 ± 0.24, DSC(MR-Edema) = 0.69 ± 0.23, DSC(PET-Uptake) = 0.71 ± 0.20, DSC(Planning-Target-Volume) = 0.93 ± 0.05, DSC(OARs) = 0.70 ± 0.13. Manual MR and FET-PET based GBM recurrence delineations differed significantly in size (p = 0.0497) and location (DSC = 0.45 ± 0.20). From the 37 MR-RF showing significant differences between MR∩PET and MR-Only (p < 0.05), none of the MR-Enhancing based RF-maps allowed spatial identification of PET findings (positive-predictive-value and sensitivity < 0.6).ConclusionsThe resulted segmentation models could facilitate PET/MR integration in GBM treatment. Background

In patients with Glioblastoma (GBM), diagnosis, staging and treatment have traditionally relied on magnetic resonance (MR) imaging. In particular, contrast-enhanced T1-weighted (T1wCE) sequences are the preferred choice for tumour and organs at risk (OARs) delineation [1–3], offering great anatomical and superior soft-tissue contrast with respect to Computed Tomography (CT). However, T1wCE images are influenced by changes induced by surgery, radiation therapy (RT), and chemotherapy, complicating the differentiation between true tumour progression and pseudo-progression due to alterations in blood-brain-barrier (BBB) integrity [4–6]. Amino-acid Positron Emission Tomography (PET) has emerged as a valuable complementary tool, in which amino-acid tracers such as O-(2-[18 F]fluoroethyl)-L-tyrosine (FET) exploit the increased uptake of amino acids by tumour cells, providing metabolic information largely independent of BBB status [4, 6]. This may allow more accurate identification of active tumour tissue and superior discrimination of true progression from treatment-related changes [7–9]. Currently, RT remains a key therapeutic option both for primary and recurrent disease [10, 11], although other treatment approaches have been recently proposed [12]. In RT, accurate delineation of tumour extent and OARs plays a particularly decisive role in ensuring that the prescribed dose is delivered to the target while maintaining adherence to radiation constraints for critical structures.

Currently, the delineation of OARs and tumour extent is performed manually, a laborious process that is prone to inter-observer variability [13–15]. These limitations have motivated research into automated segmentation. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [16] have shown promising results [1, 17, 18]. However, few studies have focused on the delineation of the RT planning–specific volumes, such as the gross tumour (GTV), clinical target (CTV) and planning target (PTV) volumes, for which overlap with OARs must be avoided. The geometrical expansion margins recommended for CTV/PTV delineation differ between primary and recurrent disease [10, 11] and, in the latter case, the recommended margins—where no mandatory expansion from GTV to CTV is defined (optional 3–5 mm), and the CTV-to-PTV expansion is recommended up to 3 mm—are applied heterogeneously across institutions, leading to a lack of standardization in their definition.

Furthermore, previous publications have emphasized the complementary value of FET-PET and MR in terms of tumour identification [9, 19]. However, these findings are yet to be validated for larger multi-centre cohort sizes, and more complex considerations, such as texture-based characterisation are yet to be investigated.

This study involved a multi-centre, multi-stage, multimodality imaging dataset to develop and evaluate nnU-Net–based automatic segmentation models for OARs and tumour volumes, required by treatment planning of GBM, in both primary and recurrent disease. Additionally, the complementary evaluation of PET/MR imaging is enhanced by incorporating radiomics features (RF) in addition to the conventional analysis of tumour volume and location. In RF analysis, MR-Enhancing regions without PET-Uptake overlap (MR-Only) and with PET-Uptake overlap (MR∩PET) were differentiated to evaluate the capability of T1wCE-derived features to discriminate PET-Uptake.

Materials and methods

Patient cohorts

This study involved a multi-centre, multi-stage, multimodality imaging dataset. In this section, we first describe the different stages of treatment for which imaging data were available and their corresponding nomenclature. Second, we briefly outline the characteristics of each cohort and the data collected. Finally, we present how these data were partitioned to generate the datasets used for model development and evaluation.

The general timeline for patients and key stages of neuroimaging follow-up for GBM is depicted in Fig. 1a, distinguishing between: image at Diagnosis (D), image for RT planning after Surgery (S), image at Recurrence (R#), and image for recurrence RT planning after re-surgery (R#S), where # denotes the ordinal number of the recurrence (e.g., R1 for first recurrence).

Prospective cohort (PC) GLIAA

The prospective, multi-centre, randomized clinical trial GLIAA (NCT01252459 12/02/2010, DRKS00000634 10/10/2014, EudraCT-No. 2012-001121-27) involved patients with histologically confirmed recurrent GBM who had been previously irradiated. The GLIAA trial was conducted in fifteen institutions in Germany between November 2013 and August 2021. Detailed description of procedures and main outcome results have been previously reported [6, 20].

This study involved 185 patients from GLIAA. The imaging data available for this cohort included MR T1wCE and T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and FET-PET/CT exams. All images corresponded to the recurrence stage (R1 or R1S). Images were co-registered using the Eclipse Treatment Planning System. A radiation oncologist delineated the contrast-enhancing tumour (MR-Enhancing) and OARs on T1wCE, peritumoral Edema (MR-Edema) on FLAIR, and their corresponding recurrence PTV (rPTV). Additionally, a nuclear medicine specialist delineated the PET-Uptake and PET-rPTV. All segmentations in accordance with European recommendations [10, 11, 21]. The OARs segmentations included the brain, brainstem, hippocampus, eyes, lenses, and retinas [11].

The indicated protocol for MR imaging consisted of a cranial scan performed at 1.5 Tesla, including axial and 3D native T1-weighted contrast-enhanced (T1wCE) sequences. For T1wCE, the following parameters were used: repetition time (TR) of 2200/2050 ms, echo time (TE) of 3.02/2.15 ms, slice thickness of 1 mm, and isotropic voxel size of 1 mm³. On the other hand, PET scans were acquired 20 min after intravenous administration of 3 MBq/kg of FET, with a scan duration of 20 min. Data were attenuation corrected using low-dose CT and reconstructed isotropically using iterative reconstruction algorithms (OSEM, 3D-RAMLA). The PET/CT scanners used were Philips Gemini TrueFlight 64 and Philips Gemini Big Bore 16.

Retrospective cohorts (RC)

The MR Retrospective Cohort (MR-RC) involved 68 patients with histologically confirmed GBM (WHO Grade IV) treated in one single institution in Spain between 2007 and 2024. The MR images available for this cohort included T1wCE and FLAIR at different disease stages (43 at D, 25 at S, and 15 at R1S). MR scans were acquired using either 1.5 or 3 T MR machines from GE Healthcare (Signa 1.5/3 T HDxt; Optima MR360) or Siemens (MAGNETOM Vida). Areas of MR-Enhancing on T1wCE and MR-Edema on FLAIR, and the PTV were manually delineated by a neuroradiologist and a radiation oncologist.

The PET Retrospective Cohort (PET-RC) involved 107 patients with histologically confirmed GBM (WHO Grade IV) treated in one single institution in Germany between 2021 and 2024. The imaging data available for this cohort includes FET-PET at different disease stages (61 at S, 42 at R1S, 2 at R2S, and 2 at R3S). FET-PET scans were acquired using Philips PET/CT machines (GEMINI TF 16 Big Bore, Vereos). The PET-Uptake was manually performed by a nuclear medicine specialist.

All segmentations were performed in accordance with European guidelines [11].

Open-source external cohort

The BraTS 2021 dataset is an open repository comprising MR imaging from 1,250 patients with low- and high-grade gliomas, retrospectively collected from multiple institutions. Imaging was acquired at the time of initial diagnosis under standard clinical conditions but using different scanners and imaging protocols [22]. For the present study, we used T1wCE and FLAIR images together with the corresponding MR-Enhancing and MR-Edema contours.

Patients from these cohorts were split into training and test sets. A representation of the cohorts’ division is shown in Fig. 1b, where rows represent the different treatment stages. Each patient had only one image per sequence/modality, so each patient contributed to only one of the sets. For PC GLIAA, the dataset was split into training and test sets based on the medical centre of origin, ensuring that images from the same centre are contained in only one of the sets. nnU-net framework for medical image segmentation

Based on our previous comparison of the performance and training times of four different open source CNN architectures for MR GBM segmentation [23], the Plain (no Residual Encoder) 3D full-resolution variant of the nnU-Net [24] was selected. The values of the batch size, the patch size, the number of epochs and folds employed in each segmentation task are detailed in Supplementary Material A.

For the PTV segmentation models, the clinical practice workflow was replicated by considering as input, not only the imaging data, but also the manually defined Enhancing and Edema for primary PTV and only Enhancing for rPTV in MR, and Uptake for PTV in PET. This approach would allow the incorporation of manually reviewed contours as inputs to the PTV models, thereby reflecting their intended integration into the clinical workflow. Furthermore, symmetric OARs (Eyes, Lenses, and Retinas) required both pre- and post-processing steps. During preprocessing, all images were settled to the same orientation, while in postprocessing, left- and right-side organs were labelled according to image orientation. Additionally, for lenses and retinas, eye segmentation was provided as an auxiliary input to simplify the task and reduce processing time. Segmentation performance evaluation

Model performance was evaluated using the Dice Sørensen Coefficient (DSC), positive predictive value (PPV), sensitivity, and maximum Hausdorff Distance (HD) [25].

For each task, a single validation result averages the performance metrics across all folds. For the test set, each patient’s segmentation mask was derived from an ensemble of the five cross-validation models by averaging their predicted probabilities. The final reported test metrics are thus derived from these ensemble predictions, leveraging the best checkpoints from each fold.

PET/MR Complementarity Evaluation using RF

For the 185 patients in PC GLIAA, manually delineated FET-PET tumour contours were compared with those obtained from MR. Comparisons were performed in terms of size (relative volume difference) and spatial agreement (DSC and PPV).

Additionally, the capability of MR-RF to discriminate PET-Uptake was evaluated. MR-Enhancing manual contour was subdivided into two subregions (see Fig. 3): MR∩PET, representing areas overlapping with PET manual contour; and MR-Only, representing areas without PET overlap. For each patient, T1wCE-derived RF were extracted separately from the MR∩PET and MR-Only subregions. A description of the radiomic extraction settings is provided in the Supplementary Material C.

Diagram and example of the MR contour categorization based on PET: MR∩PET, representing areas without PET overlap. For each patient, T1wCE-derived RF were extracted separately from the MR∩PET and MR-Only subregions

The analysis of the MR-RF obtained from the MR∩PET and MR-Only subregions was divided into three parts. First, an evaluation using an experimental uniformity phantom was performed to eliminate region size as a potential confounding factor by excluding RF whose values are intrinsically dependent on the region size. A detailed description of this procedure is provided in the Supplementary Material D. Second, for the RF that were not volume dependent, significant differences between MR∩PET and MR-Only features were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, applying the Bonferroni correction to account for multiple comparisons. Finally, to confirm the discriminative capability of MR based RF between MR∩PET and MR-Only regions, for each of the RF showing statistically significant differences in the previous step the median value across the entire patient cohort was calculated and will be referred to as RF-threshold. RF were then computed on 3 × 3 × 3 voxel subregions [26] across the whole MR-Enhancing contour, and binary-maps were created by classifying subregions into two groups: greater or less than the RF-Threshold. PPV and Sensitivity were then computed between the two RF map–based groups and the corresponding subregion MR∩PET or MR-Only. RF binary-maps were considered capable of distinguishing this categorization (MR∩PET vs. MR-Only) using MR data alone if the PPV and Sensitivity values exceeded 0.6.

PET/MR Complementarity

Results showed statistically significant differences (p = 0.0497, WSRT) in tumour size when derived from PET (12 ± 13 ml) and from MR (11 ± 12 ml) and low degree of agreement in spatial position (DSC = 0.45 ± 0.20), independently of the time difference between scans (p = 0.006, Pearson = -0.2), which ranged from same-day acquisitions to 18 days. The median relative volume difference () showed that PET contours were larger than MR by 42% (IQR [22%, 75%]).

When PET-Uptake was used to subdivide the MR-Enhancing region into MR∩PET and MR-Only components, the MR-Only volume was reduced by an average of 47% (5 ml) compared with the total MR-Enhancing volume. Statistically significant volume difference between MR-Only and MR∩PET (p = 4.17 × 10⁻⁹, MWUT) was observed, with MR-Only volume greater than MR∩PET. To prevent the differences on volume from leading to an incorrect interpretation of the results in the MR-RF evaluation, only the 86 RF intrinsically independent of the size were considered. These features were selected according to the uniformity phantom evaluation presented in Supplementary Material D.

A total of 37 RF showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05, MWUT with Bonferroni correction) between MR∩PET and MR-Only. They are listed in Supplementary Material E. From these 37 RF, binary-maps were computed on the whole MR contour and resulted in two volumes. No RF binary-map satisfied the criteria of spatial differentiation (PPV > 0.6 and Sens.>0.6) between MR∩PET and MR-Only. These thresholds were used as indicative reference values, as robust performance would be expected to approach values close to one. Discussion

We have successfully developed nnU-Net based models for automatic segmentation of tissue volumes required for treatment planning of GBM, achieving moderate to excellent performance, and demonstrating generalization across different protocols and imaging modalities, as well as in primary and recurrent disease. The employed framework [24] and the resulting models (https://github.com/MATTO-GBM-TRANSCAN/Segmentation) are provided as open source. Furthermore, the complementarity of PET/MR imaging has been confirmed for GBM recurrence definition in prospective cohort of 185 patients from 13 different institutions, and MR-RF mapping was investigated for the identification of FET-PET Uptake regions, but no significant discriminative performance was observed.

Regarding GBM tumour segmentation, performance of all developed models (DSC-Enhancing = 0.76 ± 0.24, DSC-Edema = 0.69 ± 0.23, DSC-Uptake = 0.71 ± 0.20) was on par with state-of-the-art approaches, however improved generalization across heterogeneous datasets could be inferred. For MR-Enhancing and Edema previous models have been developed based on the four MR sequences (T1w, T1wCE, T2w and FLAIR) at the diagnosis stage of BraTS dataset, resulting in performance for MR-Enhancing of DSC = 0.78 [14], DSC = 0.85 [27], DSC = 0.78 [18], DSC = 0.91 [28] and for MR-Edema of DSC = 0.81 [14]. MR-Enhancing models based on single-centre cohort at postoperative stage have also been developed, resulting in DSC(T1wCE) = 0.68 with 340 patients [29] and DSC(T1w, T1wCE, T2w, FLAIR) = 0.72 ± 0.12 for 62 patients [30]. Similarly, our median-DSC for 185 patients from 13 centre cohort of 0.79 (interquartile range, 0.66–0.85) for PET-Uptake, is on par with models previously reported in the literature: single-centre cohort median-DSC = 0.80 (interquartile range, 0.68–0.88) for 555 patients [31]; median-DSC = 0.81 (interquartile range, 0.70–0.92) for 174 patients [32]. Overall, for our tumour segmentation models, the diverse patient populations and imaging protocols acquired at different stages of clinical management, ranging from initial diagnosis and surgical intervention to post-treatment recurrence, constituted a heterogeneous dataset that enhances the generalization of the provided models against different protocols and imaging systems. Furthermore, we trained MR-Enhancing and Edema models specifically using only the imaging modalities recommended for clinical practice, T1wCE images for segmenting enhancing tumour regions and FLAIR images for peritumoral Edema detection [11, 33]. This approach facilitates the implementation of the resulting model in clinical practice, where complete multi-sequence MR datasets may not always be available and are often affected by greater protocol variability. Lastly, to the best of our knowledge, no published studies have focused on PTV segmentation from either imaging modality and primary and recurrence stage. Our planning volume segmentation models on MR (DSC = 0.98 for PTV and DSC = 0.91 ± 0.08) and on PET (DSC = 0.90 ± 0.06 for PTV/rPTV) are therefore very advantageous for a robust and less time-consuming incorporation of tumour definition in treatment planning at distinct stages.

In terms of segmentation performance for diagnosis and recurrence stage cases, Table 4 shows a notable decrease for recurrence stage cases in MR-Enhancing and MR-Edema. While recurrence imaging is inherently more complex, we have evaluated the differences in lesion volume, using the number of voxels in the segmentation mask, and have found that there are significant differences between the lesion volumes (p < 0.05, MWUT), with lesions at diagnosis being larger for both cases. Additionally, a positive correlation between lesion volume and segmentation performance (DSC) was found for MR-Enhancing (p < 0.05, Spearman = 0.40) and MR-Edema (p < 0.05, Spearman = 0.50), indicating that segmentation accuracy tends to decrease for smaller lesions (see supporting data in Supplementary Material G). These findings support the notion that lesion volume has a measurable impact on model performance. However, we also consider that treatment-related changes could introduce additional variability that affects segmentation accuracy. As for the clinical implications, the impact of this performance decrease on treatment planning is mitigated within a semi-automated workflow. In this approach, the radiation oncologist uses MR-Enhancing, MR-Edema, and PET-Uptake regions as initial inputs for contour definition. Following physician review and manual refinement, the validated contours are then provided to automatic PTV models, which generate the initial inputs for radiotherapy planning.

Regarding performance of OARs segmentation models in large structures (DSC-Brain = 0.96 ± 0.02, DSC-Brainstem = 0.80 ± 0.18 and DSC-Eyes = 0.82 ± 0.08), the comparison with respect to previous publications has shown similar or higher DSC values while considering our larger multicentre sample (N = 125 patients from 13 institutions). Single-centre T1w cohorts from previous publications resulted in DSC-Brain = 0.96, DSC-Brainstem = 0.89 and DSC-Eyes = 0.89 for N = 44 [34]; DSC-Brain = 0.96 and DSC-Brainstem = 0.90 for N = 30 [35]; DSC-Brainstem = 0.85 for N = 12 [36] and DSC-Brainstem = 0.86 for N = 60 [37]. Our results for large structures support the potential of our models for clinical deployment in GBM radiotherapy planning for different magnetic field scanners and protocols. For smaller structures, the models obtained low performance (DSC-Hippocampi = 0.58 ± 0.18, DSC-Retinas = 0.53 ± 0.13 and DSC-Lenses = 0.51 ± 0.18) with respect to the values reported previously DSC-Hippocampi = 0.71 and DSC-Lenses = 0.58 for N = 44 on T1wCE [34] and DSC-Lenses = 0.86 for five-centres cohort of 131 patients on T2w [38]. As far as we are aware, no studies to date have concentrated on automatic segmentation of the retinas. We attribute the decrease in performance for small OARs, to the volume of the structures, which as mentioned previously is positively correlated to the segmentation performance. Additionally, the size of the contours also has an impact on the DSC metric, with equivalent displacements or deformations having a much larger impact for smaller contours. Despite the non-optimal performance for small structures, the models have been provided considering their release valuable as a reference and starting point for future research. These models may serve as a foundation for the development of more robust CNN-based segmentation algorithms through retraining with larger and more diverse patient cohorts. Furthermore, although the segmentation performance was relatively low, the generated segmentations may still be clinically valuable at their current stage. No statistically significant differences (p > 0.2, WSRT) were observed in maximum or mean doses between manual and automatic contours, suggesting no direct impact on the dose criteria used for treatment plan evaluation. In this context, OAR segmentation models may help alleviate the demanding and time-consuming process of manual delineation by providing an initial set of contours that are subsequently reviewed and refined by the radiation oncologist prior to incorporation into the treatment planning workflow.

Regarding time investment in segmentation for radiotherapy treatment, studies report that the manual delineation of a typical brain MR dataset takes on average 80 min [39, 40] while the editing and correction of automatically generated contours takes on average 22 min [41]. Based on these reference values, the use of our proposed automatic segmentation models would require approximately 32 min, including manual revision of the generated contours, which may correspond to time savings of up to 60% compared to manual delineation. However, the actual time required will depend on multiple factors, including technical deployment and clinical workflow, and has yet to be formally evaluated.

All our resulting models can be downloaded from GitHub and, together with the open-source nnU-Net framework, can be used for further refinement with local datasets.

In terms of the PET/MR comparison, our findings of statistically significant differences in the size and spatial position of tumour manual contours derived from PET and from MR are aligned with previous results [9, 19]. However, the clinical impact of these differences on patient outcomes remains an open question that should be addressed in future research. From our knowledge, no previous studies have evaluated the potential of T1wCE-derived features to discriminate PET-Uptake. Although 37 RF showed significant differences between MR∩PET and MR-Only, our evaluation on 185 patients did not result in a robust method for the identification of FET-PET regions based on RF-maps derived from MR-Enhancing. These findings support the complementarity of FET-PET and therefore the recommendation of the 2023 ESTRO-EANO guidelines [11] regarding the use of FET-PET imaging to improve target delineation in GBM patients and to guide more efficient and personalized treatment planning.

Limitations and future work opportunities

Among the limitations of the current study, we find: [1] no QA/consensus process was applied in the definition of ground truth segmentations of GLIAA, MR- and PET-RC; [2] segmentation models were trained on the entire heterogeneous dataset with no distinction between diagnosis and recurrence stage cases, and it may be possible to further optimize segmentation performance at the recurrence stage with specific or more complex training strategies; [3] no harmonization strategies was applied to RF to mitigate the effect of potential centre-biases for MR∩PET and MR-Only discrimination analysis; [4] RF-based MR∩PET and MR-Only discrimination could be enhanced by using machine learning classifiers instead of the threshold-based approach used in this study, and by incorporating RF from other MR sequences such as FLAIR or ADC.

Regarding the degree of clinical usability of the contours and the effectiveness of a semi-automatic segmentation pipeline, in which the radiation oncologist/nuclear medicine specialist uses the model output as initialization for the contour definition, we are preparing a prospective cohort evaluation to assess the clinical implications of segmentation performance, in which the radiation oncologists/nuclear medicine specialists will be asked not only to validate the quality of the output, but also to provide insight into the time needed to edit the contour and if it is ultimately valuable in a clinical setting.

Conclusions

The moderate to excellent performance of the automatic segmentation models demonstrates generalization across different protocols and imaging systems, as well as primary and recurrent disease, supporting their use to reduce time investment and increase robustness. The MR and PET comparison in this cohort supports the notion that PET provides complimentary information to MR for GBM recurrence definition, and, while MR-derived RF showed statistically significant differences between MR∩PET and MR-Only regions, MR-RF alone are not sufficient for the proper identification of these regions.
