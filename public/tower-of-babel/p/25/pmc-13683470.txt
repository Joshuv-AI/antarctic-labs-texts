Long-term hydroxyurea therapy alters bone marrow megakaryocyte morphology and associates with prognosis in myeloproliferative neoplasms
Authors: Zhikang Zheng, Yingqing Xu, Tian Zeng, Rumeng Li, Bingbing Wen, Qingmei Han, Xiaojing Bao, Jian Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydroxyurea (HU) is a first-line treatment for myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs), but a quantitative, standardized framework for assessing its impact on megakaryocyte (MK) morphology and prognosis remains lacking. This retrospective, two-center study analyzed 22,097 MKs from 280 patients (118 polycythemia vera [PV], 162 essential thrombocythemia [ET]), classified into 12 subtypes using the 5th edition WHO classification in conjunction with objectively derived thresholds (minimum P-value approach). Our primary contribution is to establish a quantifiable scoring framework that translates descriptive WHO categories into reproducible prognostic metrics. Using this framework, HU significantly reduced normal MK proportion in JAK2-ET (P = 0.0189), TN-ET (P = 0.0233), and JAK2-PV (P = 0.0427), while increasing abnormal subtypes (bulbous, separated nuclear MKs); conversely, IFN was associated with preserved normal MKs and reduced abnormal forms. Multivariate Cox regression, applied to our empirically derived cutoffs, identified protective thresholds (normal MK ≥ 58% in ET, ≥ 53% in PV; emperipolesis ≥ 2% in ET) and adverse cutoffs (Naked nuclei ≥ 13% in ET, ≥ 7% in PV; staghorn like ≥ 6% in ET; separated nuclear ≥ 3% in ET) for myelofibrosis progression (all P < 0.05). Our quantitative findings corroborate prior qualitative observations that long-term HU is associated with adverse MK remodeling, while IFN correlates with lower progression risk as captured by our threshold-based system. This standardized scoring approach—not the identification of new prognostic factors—provides a reproducible tool for monitoring treatment-related morphological changes and may inform risk-adapted therapy in MPN patients.Keywords: Myeloproliferative neoplasms, Hydroxyurea, Interferon, Megakaryocyte morphology, Prognosis

Introduction

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are a group of hematological malignancies characterized by clonal bone marrow hematopoietic cell proliferation [1]; diagnosis and management rely on bone marrow morphological assessment and driver gene mutation detection, with hydroxyurea (HU) and interferon (IFN) recommended as first-line cytoreductive agents for long-term disease control. However, the effects of these agents on bone marrow microstructure—particularly megakaryocyte (MK) morphology—and their clinical relevance to prognosis remain incompletely elucidated [2–5], partly due to the absence of standardized, quantitative criteria for objectively capturing and comparing morphological shifts under different therapeutic regimens. With improved MPN patient survival, dynamic bone marrow morphological changes associated with long-term pharmacotherapy have gained increasing attention. While abnormal MK morphology is a key MPN diagnostic criterion and correlates with adverse outcomes such as myelofibrotic(MF) progression and acute leukaemic transformation(AML), these correlations have largely been established through qualitative descriptive approaches, and no clear clinical guidelines address the differential MK morphological remodeling induced by distinct treatment regimens based on reproducible metrics. This study aims to apply a minimum P-value approach, threshold-based quantitative scoring system—grounded in the standardized WHO-defined morphological categories—systematically evaluate changes in MK morphological subtype distribution in MPN patients receiving long-term HU or IFN therapy, with the primary objective of providing evidence to optimize first-line pharmacotherapeutic selection and administration timing in clinical practice.

Previous studies have reported that MPN patients with increased proportions of Naked nuclei and hypolobated MK carry a significantly higher risk of MF and AML [6, 7]. However, these prior observations remain subjective and lack operational thresholds for routine application. Therefore, the principal novelty of the present study is to establish a quantifiable, standardized scoring framework (using WHO categories with cutoffs) to objectively assess how different cytoreductive agents modulate these morphological subtypes. This methodological advance provides direct, reproducible morphological evidence to support clinical drug selection, distinguishing it from prior descriptive reports.

Our findings suggest that alterations in specific MK morphological subtypes as captured by our threshold-based system, may contribute to MPN progression; notably, in patients with ET, increased proportions of certain abnormal MK phenotypes above cutoffs may indicate heightened risk of progression to MF or AML. We propose that MK morphology could not only serve as a tool for differentiating MPN subtypes but also be incorporated into prognostic scoring systems as a reproducible parameter to guide clinical pharmacotherapeutic decisions.

Methods

Study cohort

This retrospective analysis included 280 newly diagnosed and follow-up MPN patients from The First Affiliated Hospital and The Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, enrolled between October 2014 and August 2025. A total of 22,097 MKs were microscopically enumerated. The cohort comprised 162 ET patients (14,727 MKs), of whom 35 received HU, 27 IFN, and 95 no cytoreductive therapy (only blood cell monitoring and aspirin); five patients were excluded due to other treatment regimens. The remaining 118 PV patients (7370 MKs) included 22 treated with HU, 11 with IFN, and 59 with no cytoreductive therapy; 26 patients with JAK2-negative status and decreased erythropoietin (EPO) were excluded. All cases were re-reviewed by two blinded pathologists (Z Kang and X Qing) and classified per the 5th WHO criteria [8]. Subjects with significant reduction or absence of megakaryocytes were excluded as poor bone marrow dry aspiration or sampling. Interobserver agreement was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa on a randomly selected 25-case subset; κ values for the 12 subtypes ranged from 0.802 to 1.000 (median 0.886), all P < 0.001, indicating excellent reproducibility.

BM smear

All 280 specimens were bone marrow smears, stained with Wright-Giemsa stain (Zhuhai Best Group Co., Ltd.) consisting of Solution A (staining solution) and Solution B (phosphate buffer solution). Bone marrow smears were prepared using a standardized protocol: each smear had a marrow area of approximately 1.5 × 3.0 cm with appropriate, uniform thickness, and staining was performed for 15 min. MKs were initially identified under a low-power objective lens (10× magnification, total 100×), followed by morphological assessment using an oil immersion objective lens (100× magnification, total 1000×). Additional exclusions included bone marrow smears with unidentifiable cells due to staining fading from prolonged storage.

Bone marrow smears were classified and counted

MKs were categorized into 12 morphological subtypes per the 5th WHO criteria (Fig. 1). MK enumeration was standardized based on proliferative status to ensure sufficient sampling: ≥100 MKs were counted for MK hyperplasia, 50 MKs for moderate counts, and all MKs on the entire bone marrow smear for reduced MK numbers. The proportion of each morphological subtype was calculated as a percentage, with median values used for statistical analyses. Nuclear morphology was prioritized as the key classification criterion: (i) MKs with staghorn-like nuclei (regardless of accompanying leukocyte cytoplasmic extensions); (ii) MKs with a giant size but staghorn-like nuclear morphology were categorized as staghorn-like MKs; (iii) small MKs with hypolobated nuclei were classified as hypolobated MKs, respectively. To ensure classification objectivity, a strict double-blinded method was implemented: Two experienced pathologists independently classified MKs morphologically, without access to patients’ baseline or diagnostic data; clinical and diagnostic details were recorded only after completion of this independent classification.

Statistical analylsis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 and Prism 10 software. Numerical variables were summarized as median (range), and categorical variables as count and relative frequency (%). Between-group comparisons of numerical variables were conducted via the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test. Optimal cut-off values for specific MK morphological subtype proportions were determined using the “surv_cutpoint” function in the survival package of R software, with patients stratified accordingly. Multivariate analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards regression models. To account for sparse data and potential separation, we applied Firth’s penalized likelihood correction to the Cox models. P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The morphological differences of megakaryocytes in ET patients under different treatment regimens

MK morphology in JAK2-ET, CALR-ET, and TN-ET was influenced by driver mutations and therapeutic modalities (Tables 2, 3 and 4). For normal MK, statistically significant differences were observed among control, HU, and IFN groups: JAK2-ET showed lower proportions in HU vs. control (P = 0.0189) and IFN (P = 0.0012), TN-ET had reduced normal MK in HU vs. control (P = 0.0233; Fig. 2a), and no differences were seen in CALR-ET. Among abnormal MK subtypes, staghorn like MK had significant differences across all three groups in JAK2-ET (P = 0.0009, P = 0.0031) and lower levels in IFN vs. control in TN-ET (P = 0.0339; Fig. 2b), with no changes in CALR-ET; emperipolesis MK was lower in HU vs. control in JAK2-ET (P = 0.0439; Fig. 2c) but unchanged in CALR-ET/TN-ET; Naked nuclei MK was higher in HU vs. control (P = 0.0009) and IFN (P = 0.0455; Fig. 2d) in TN-ET with no changes in other subgroups. For pleomorphism MK, cluster MK was reduced in IFN vs. control in JAK2-ET (P = 0.0251; Fig. 2e) and large MK was lower in HU vs. control in CALR-ET (P = 0.0074; Fig. 2f), with no other subgroup differences. Defects in maturation MK included bulbous MK, which was higher in HU vs. control/IFN in JAK2-ET (P = 0.0027, P = 0.0085) and in HU/IFN vs. control in TN-ET (P = 0.0058, P = 0.0078; Fig. 2g), and hyperchromatic MK, which was lower in HU/IFN vs. control in TN-ET (P = 0.0194, P = 0.0089; Fig. 2h). Dysplastic MK showed separated nuclear MK was higher in HU vs. control in JAK2-ET (P < 0.0001) and CALR-ET (P = 0.0250), with JAK2-ET also having higher levels in HU vs. IFN (P = 0.0009), while CALR-ET/TN-ET had higher separated nuclear MK in IFN vs. control (P = 0.0391, P = 0.0124; Fig. 2i); hypolobated/non-lobated MK was higher in HU vs. control in CALR-ET (P = 0.0465; Fig. 2j) with no differences in JAK2-ET/TN-ET.

Discussion

In routine clinical practice, MKs are primarily classified into four subtypes: immature megakaryocytes, granular megakaryocytes, platelet-producing megakaryocytes, and bare-nucleated megakaryocytes. However, this basic classification only allows assessment of platelet production function and is insufficient for the prognostic evaluation of MPN. Therefore, we adopted the 12-subtype MKs morphological classification system as defined in the 5th edition of the WHO Classification of Tumours of Haematopoietic and Lymphoid Tissues to evaluate the prognostic impact of different therapeutic regimens in MPN patients. Rather than merely reaffirming the known associations between abnormal MK morphology and disease progression, our primary contribution lies in establishing a standardized, quantifiable scoring approach that translates descriptive WHO categories into reproducible metrics for treatment monitoring.

Notably, in our study, MKs morphological characterization using an oil immersion lens (100 × 10 magnification) enabled more detailed assessment compared to high-power microscopy (40 × 10 magnification) of bone marrow biopsies. Univariate analysis confirmed significant associations between alterations in multiple MKs subtypes and different therapeutic interventions. For instance, compared with the control group, HU treatment significantly reduced the median percentage of normal MKs in both JAK2-ET and TN-ET patients (P = 0.0189 and P = 0.0233); additionally, JAK2-ET patients treated with HU had a significantly lower proportion of normal MKs than those receiving IFN (P = 0.0012). Cohort analysis demonstrated that reduced normal MK proportions were significantly associated with ET progression to MF. In contrast, normal MK percentages in CALR-ET patients remained unchanged regardless of HU or IFN treatment. Collectively, these findings regarding normal MK proportions suggest that HU treatment may increase the risk of MF progression in JAK2-ET and TN-ET patients.

In our cohort study analysis, univariate analysis identified significant associations between normal, emperipolesis, staghorn like, Naked nuclei, separated nuclear MK and ET transformation to MF (P = 0.0092, P = 0.0211, P = 0.0157, P < 0.0001, P = 0.0003). Using the Mann-Whitney U test, we found that HU treatment significantly reduced the proportions of normal MK and emperipolesis MK in JAK2-ET patients compared with the control group (P = 0.0189 and P = 0.0439). This suggests that JAK2-ET patients with normal MK < 58% and emperipolesis MK < 2% have an increased risk of MF transformation; accordingly, HU should be discontinued and replaced with IFN. Compared with HU treatment, IFN therapy significantly decreased the proportions of staghorn like MK and separated nuclear MK in JAK2-ET patients (P = 0.0031 and P = 0.0009), leading to a reduction in MF risk. In TN-ET patients, the IFN‑treated subgroup appeared to have higher proportions of staghorn like, Naked nuclei, and separated nuclear MKs compared with the control group (P = 0.0339, P = 0.0455, P = 0.0124). However, given the very small number of IFN-treated TN-ET patients in our cohort (n = 3), these findings should be interpreted as descriptive observations rather than definitive evidence of elevated MF risk. In CALR-ET patients, IFN treatment had no apparent impact on the proportions of staghorn like, Naked nuclei, or separated nuclear MKs in our limited sample. Nevertheless, HU treatment significantly increased the proportion of separated nuclear MK (a marker of high MF risk) in CALR-ET patients compared with the control group (P = 0.0250), although this finding also warrants cautious interpretation due to the small subgroup size.

In JAK2-PV, univariate analysis demonstrated that normal MK, Naked nuclei MK, and hyperchromatic MK were significantly correlated with the progression of PV to MF (P = 0.0061, P = 0.0047, P < 0.0001). Compared with the control group, HU treatment tended to decrease the proportion of normal MK (P = 0.0527) while markedly increasing the proportion of naked nuclei MK (P = 0.0216); these findings were consistent with those observed in JAK2-ET. In contrast to JAK2-ET, IFN therapy significantly reduced the proportion of hyperchromatic MK in JAK2-PV patients compared with the control group (P = 0.0198). Collectively, these data suggest that the presence of normal MK < 53%, Naked nuclei MK ≥ 7%, and hyperchromatic MK < 3% during HU treatment identifies JAK2-PV patients at elevated risk of progression to MF. Using a murine model of MPN, Crespiatico et al. [9]. observed overexpression of myocyte enhancer factor 2 C (Mef2c) — a transcription factor involved in MKs differentiation. This overexpression resulted in MK morphological abnormalities reminiscent of those seen in human MPNs, characterized by dysplastic MKs, which is consistent with the findings of our cohort study. Additionally, separated nuclear MKs may be associated with leukemic transformation in MPNs [3, 4]. Previous studies conducted in China have demonstrated a correlation between Naked nuclei MKs and MF [10], a finding that also aligns with our results. However, no prior reports have linked staghorn like MKs to MF. This discrepancy may stem from the lack of a clear morphological definition of staghorn like MKs among pathologists, with no consensus on the minimum number of nuclear lobes required to classify a MK as hypersegmented (staghorn like). In the present study, we defined staghorn like MKs as those with more than 10 nuclear lobes and a staghorn like appearance, which we propose as a reference for future research.

Beyond the cohort analysis, we identified several additional MKs morphological subtypes associated with MPN therapy, such as cluster MKs. Our findings demonstrated that in JAK2-ET patients, IFN therapy significantly reduced the proportions of staghorn like MKs and cluster MKs compared with no cytoreductive therapy (P = 0.0009; P = 0.0251), However, in JAK2-PV patients, there was no significant difference in cluster MK among the three treatment regimens. Both domestic and international studies have indicated that clustered MKs are associated with an increased risk of thrombosis and MF [10, 11]. Our data revealed that hydroxyurea treatment led to a significant increase in the proportion of bulbous MKs in both JAK2-ET and TN-ET patients relative to the control group (P = 0.0027; P = 0.0058). In contrast, IFN therapy only increased the percentage of bulbous MKs in TN-ET patients compared with no cytoreductive therapy (P = 0.0078); again, this observation from the IFN-treated TN-ET subgroup requires confirmation in larger cohorts. Among JAK2-PV patients, the proportion of Bulbous MK treated with HU was significantly increased compared with that treated without cell reduction (P = 0.0015), which was the same as the result of JAK2-ET. Previous research has shown that the presence of bulbous MKs in the bone marrow is accompanied by a mild increase in reticulin [12], suggesting that bulbous MKs may be linked to an elevated risk of MF — a finding consistent with our results. Our study also found that IFN therapy was associated with a significant decrease in the proportion of hyperchromatic MKs in TN-ET and JAK2-PV patients compared with no cytoreductive therapy (P = 0.0089 and P = 0.0198), with the TN-ET finding again limited by the small subgroup size. According to the 5th edition of the WHO Classification, hyperchromatic MKs are classified as “defects in maturation,” and relevant literature has reported that MKs with maturation defects are more common in pre-fibrotic primary myelofibrosis pre-PMF and overt PMF patients [13, 14]. This indicates an association between hyperchromatic MKs and MF.

Our study further demonstrated that in CALR-ET patients, the proportion of multinucleated round MK was increased regardless of treatment with hydroxyurea or interferon, which was associated with elevated risks of MF and AML. This finding may be related to the predilection of CALR mutations in MF and AML [15–20]. However, given the limited number of CALR-ET patients (HU, n = 22; IFN, n = 4; control, n = 17), these observations are also preliminary and require validation in larger CALR-mutated cohorts. Our data also revealed that in CALR-ET patients, HU treatment led to a significant increase in large MKs among pleomorphic MKs compared with no cytoreductive therapy (P = 0.0074). Previous studies have indicated that myelofibrosis is characterized by an increase in pleomorphic MKs in the bone marrow, which typically contributes to the development of osteosclerosis [21]. In summary, treatment of CALR-ET patients with either HU or IFN results in an increase in MK morphological subtypes associated with elevated risks of progression to MF and AML.

Based on these data, we propose incorporating the median percentage changes of these MKs morphological subtypes into prognostic scoring models, which may enhance the accuracy of determining the optimal timing of therapeutic intervention for ET and PV patients with different driver mutations. Interobserver agreement for the 12-subtype classification was excellent (κ median 0.886). Additionally, while our definition of staghorn like MKs (> 10 nuclear lobes) was consistently applied by two pathologists, its reproducibility across different centers and pathologists remains to be validated. We also acknowledge that certain small subgroups preclude robust inference; these findings are descriptive and require validation in larger cohorts. External validation in independent multicenter cohorts is therefore warranted before these thresholds can be adopted in routine clinical practice.
