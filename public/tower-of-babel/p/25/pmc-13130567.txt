Comparative analysis of multimorbidity prevalence and lifestyle factors among older adults in frigid versus non-frigid zones of China
Authors: Feng Lu, Xue-Ying Li, Tao Sun, Xin-Yu Duan, Xiao-Jing Yang, Yu Shi, De-Pin Cao, Shu-E Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultimorbidity, defined as the coexistence of at least two chronic diseases in an individual, exhibits spatial heterogeneity in its association with lifestyle factors across different climatic zones. Older adults in frigid zones–defined as areas where the average temperature in the coldest month is ≤ -10℃, or where daily mean temperatures ≤ 5℃ persist for ≥ 145 days per year-face distinct environmental exposures compared to those in non-frigid zones. Nevertheless, few studies have explored multimorbidity and lifestyle differences between frigid and non-frigid zones. This study aims to: (1) identify sociodemographic, lifestyle, and regional factors linked to multimorbidity in Chinese older adults and further to compare them across zones; and (2) assess lifestyle diversity related to multimorbidity in each zone.MethodsA total of 10,619 participants aged ≥ 60 years were included from China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) collected in 2020. All participants met the inclusion criteria were classified with reference to the 2016 GB50176 climate standard for frigid zone and non-frigid zone. Chi-square test, regression analyses, and subgroup analysis were used to describe and analyze risk factors and difference in multimorbidity between frigid zone and non-frigid zone among Chinese older adults.Results(1) The prevalence of multimorbidity among older adults is significantly higher in frigid zone (72.69%) than in non-frigid zone (65.63%) and the national level (66.32%). (2) Living zones and lifestyles have association on multimorbidity in older adults. (3) The effect of Internet use on multimorbidity in the older adults demonstrated significant heterogeneity in terms of living zone(I2 = 66.1%).ConclusionsMuch attention should be paid to older adults with multimorbidity residing in frigid zone. Especially, targeted interventions for unhealthy lifestyle in frigid zone and non-frigid zone among older adults to decrease multimorbidity prevalences and their catastrophic consequences. For older adults in frigid zone. In parallel, study indicated that appropriate sleep duration, reducing problematic internet use, smoking cessation and alcohol restriction and other healthy lifestyle may contribute to multimorbidity prevention among older adults especially in frigid zone.Keywords: Multimorbidity, Frigid zone, Lifestyle factors, Older adults

Background

Multimorbidity is one of the greatest challenges for public health worldwide [1], which has led to adverse outcomes and even catastrophic consequences such as more frequent and longer hospitalizations, increased healthcare costs, readmissions, mortality and reduced quality of life [2–4]. More importantly, multimorbidity has caused increased healthcare utilization and multiple pharmacological treatments, which further places financial strain on household and healthcare systems [5]. Multimorbidity, defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as the co-existing of at least two chronic diseases within the same individual, is characterized by its patient-centered perspective where all conditions hold equal importance [6]. In contrast, comorbidity is a clinical, disease-centered concept, referring specifically to additional disorders co-occurring with a pre-specified index disease, with analytical focus on their effects on the primary condition’s outcomes.

Adults with multimorbidity were over one-third of individuals worldwide. In most high-income countries, multimorbidity is considered highest prevalence [7], which also appears to be increasingly prevalent in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) [7]. Miserably, in LMICs, there were more than half of all adults with chronic conditions [8–12]. Compared with the general adult population, multimorbidity prevalence is higher among the older population group--a population segment defined by WHO standards as those aged 60 years or older [13]. An evidence indicated that a 30% prevalence of multimorbidity among those aged 45–64, 65% among those aged 65–84 and 82% among those aged 85 and over [14]. Several studies have observed a sex disparity in the prevalence of multimorbidity, with reports from LMICs indicating that multimorbidity is more common in female [15]. Numerous studies have confirmed that demographic characteristics are important influencing factors of multimorbidity. However, due to differences in sociocultural backgrounds or disease spectrums, these conclusions may not be directly generalizable to Asian populations, particularly in China. Therefore, it is necessary to specifically investigate the true impact of demographic characteristics on multimorbidity among Chinese populations.

Lifestyle factors may differ in each zone. What is not clear is how factors affecting multimorbidity vary across zone. Substantial differences in multimorbidity prevalence exist across geographical environments and climate zones, underscoring their importance as key determinants of multimorbidity [16, 17]. Previous studies suggest that regional variations in temperature may contribute to heterogeneous health outcomes by shaping local lifestyles (e.g., dietary patterns and physical activity), which in turn differentially influence the development of various chronic diseases [18, 19]. Consequently, the pathogenic effects of these factors on multimorbidity may vary significantly across different climatic zones. Consistent with the environment–behavior–health framework, cold climate functions as a persistent structural stressor that modifies the health effects of individual-level risk factors. Under colder environmental conditions, the adverse impacts of unhealthy lifestyles and socio-economic disadvantage are likely to be amplified through constrained physical activity, increased physiological stress, and reduced adaptive capacity, leading to stronger associations with multimorbidity. Especial in differences between frigid and non-frigid zones for notable differences in climate, ecology, and culture, while multimorbidity prevalence among older adults indirectly influenced by diverse lifestyle and pathogenesis. Population distribution of multimorbidity and their health damage severity were influenced by determine factors such as the built and natural environments, social and cultural context, public health infrastructure, health and social services, and demographic and regional vulnerabilities [20, 21]. Frigid zone, characterized by low temperatures, heavy snowfall, and long winters, possess a distinct natural environment that profoundly impacts residents’ lifestyles and dietary habits [22]. Prolonged exposure to cold environments necessitates higher energy consumption to maintain adults body temperature, which may cause an increased likelihood of consuming high-calorie and high-fat foods, consequently elevating the risk of chronic diseases like obesity, hypertension, and hypercholesterolemia [23, 24]. Furthermore, cold weather also made a contribution to adults’ physiological index such as intervening circulation and metabolism, further exacerbating the occurrence and progression of chronic diseases [25].

In contrast, non-frigid zone is featured by milder climates with four distinct seasons and diverse ecosystems. This does not imply that residents who living in non-frigid zone face a lower risk of multimorbidity compared to those in frigid regions. However, non-frigid zone dwellers may encounter health challenges for extreme weather conditions such as high temperatures and humidity. For instance, high temperatures can lead to acute health issues like heat stroke and dehydration [26], while prolonged high humidity can contribute to chronic conditions like rheumatism and arthritis [27]. Additionally, irrational diets consisting of high-calorie and high-sugar foods among non-cold-weather residents can also result in chronic diseases like obesity and diabetes [28, 29]. Apart from climatic and dietary factors, disparities in living habits and socio-cultural backgrounds between frigid and non-frigid regions can impact the risk of developing multimorbidity. In other words, the association between lifestyle and multimorbidity may vary by region. Social culture and natural environment may alter the impact of specific behaviors on health. Frigid zone residents may engage in less outdoor activities during winter due to the chilly weather, leading to insufficient exercise [24], which means that the negative health effects of a sedentary lifestyle in cold regions may be magnified. This is because such behavior will be compounded by seasonal activity restrictions. Conversely, non-frigid zone inhabitants may neglect exercise due to busy schedules and a fast-paced lifestyle [30]. Cultural practices specific to each region can also influence residents’ health concepts and behaviors, consequently impacting the incidence of multimorbidity [31].

As the aging population continues to grow rapidly, multimorbidity prevalence challenging among older adults are becoming prominent for physiological decline, combined with barriers to self-care and worsens this deterioration [32], especially in cold conditions. Older adults with multimorbidity living in distinct regions were both similarities and differences. Those residing in frigid zones may face more pronounced climate-related health obstacles. However, the influencing factors of multimorbidity in frigid zones compared to non-frigid zones are likely to vary significantly due to differences in climate, environment, and lifestyle. Existing studies have primarily focused on studying the impact of extreme temperatures on multimorbidity in the older adults [33–35]. Yet, a comparative analysis of multimorbidity between frigid and non-frigid zones remains unclear. To address this gap, this study was conducted to: (1) identify the sociodemographic, lifestyle, and zone characteristics associated with multimorbidity among Chinese older adults, contrasting these differences residing in frigid zone and non-frigid zone; and (2) assess the lifestyle diversity of multimorbidity among older adults in these respective zones. This study will in turn help inform future research on underlying mechanisms, optimize healthcare resource allocation, and guide the design of targeted interventions.

Materials and methods

Data sources

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), which is a survey of Chinese residents aged 45 and older conducted by Peking University. As of today, the CHARLS is one of the most representative, up-to-date databases available with the widest coverage [36]. The baseline national assessment of CHARLS was carried out in 2011, and residents were selected from 150 counties and 450 communities (villages) of 28 provinces in China using a stratified multi-stage probability-proportional-to-size random sampling strategy. The Cohort Profile reported the detailed methodology description of the CHARLS [37]. The CHARLS questionnaire includes basic information about the individual, family structure and financial support, physical measurements, health status and functioning, work, retirement and pensions, income, consumption, assets, and basic community information. Although CHARLS is a longitudinal study, our analysis focuses on the 2020 wave. As the most recent data available, it offers a timely and critical snapshot of population health in the immediate post-pandemic period. Understanding health disparities, such as multimorbidity across regions, requires an analysis grounded in the contemporary healthcare landscape that has been significantly influenced by the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite some adjustments in the data collection mode, existing research shows that indicators related to health status and lifestyle variables still demonstrate good robustness at the analytical level [19, 38].

According to the purpose of the study, the following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) Participants under 60 years of age; (2) Those without basic demographics data or lifestyle information. After applying these criteria,10,619 valid samples were finally obtained. The data screening process is presented in Fig. 1.

Analytical sample

Independent variables

The building climate zoning standard adopted in this study is determined in accordance with the national standard “Thermal Design Code for Civil Buildings (GB 50176)” [2016].According to this code, the criteria for defining frigid zone are: areas where the average temperature of the coldest month does not exceed − 10℃ for consecutive years, or where the number of days with an average daily temperature not exceeding 5℃ is generally no fewer than 145 days. Based on this criterion and considering the unique lifestyle patterns in Northeast China, the defined cold regions include Heilongjiang Province, Jilin Province, Liaoning Province, Inner Mongolia [39]. In the 2020 CHARLS, the independent variable was assessed using the community ID which divide the area into frigid zone and non-frigid zone, where the frigid zone includes Heilongjiang, Jilin, Liaoning, Inner Mongolia and non-frigid zone is comprised by other cities besides those included in CHARLS. A value of 1 was assigned if the respondent’s community is in the frigid zone; otherwise, it was 0.

Outcome variables

In this study, the dependent variable was multimorbidity which was measured by the multiple chronic diseases reported by older adults in the survey. Multimorbidity status was measured by the question “Has a doctor ever told [XRName] has [XChroDisType[𝑖]]?”( [XRName] indicates the name of the participant and [XChroDisType[𝑖]] indicates the type of chronic disease). The measurement method for each disease is consistent, relying on self-reported physician diagnosis. The analysis included 15 chronic diseases derived from the CHARLS questionnaire, which cover the most common conditions affecting older populations in China: 1) Hypertension, 2) Dyslipidemia, 3) Diabetes and high blood sugar, 4) Cancer and malignant tumor, 5) Chronic lung diseases, 6) Liver disease, 7) Heart problems, 8) Stroke, 9) Kidney diseases, 10) Digestive diseases, 11)Psychiatric problems, 12) Memory-related disease, 13) Arthritis and rheumatism, 14) Asthma, 15) Parkinson’s disease. Participants who had two or more of the 15 chronic diseases mentioned above were defined as having multimorbidity. The responses were combined into dichotomous variables categorized as (0 = none or single), (1 = more than two chronic diseases) to study the prevalence of multimorbidity.

Control variables

Many studies have shown that lifestyles are important factors on multimorbidity. Therefore, this study involves a list of sociodemographic characteristics and lifestyle factors as control variables. For demographics, we included age (60–69 years old for the low age group, 70–79 years old for the middle age group, and 80 years old and above for the high age group), sex (male vs. female), residence (urban and suburban vs. rural), marital status and education. Marital status was dichotomized into ‘with spouse’ (including those currently married and cohabiting with their spouse, or married but living apart temporarily due to work or other reasons) versus ‘without spouse’ (including those who were separated, divorced, widowed, or never married). This categorization is based on the presence of a core spousal support union. We categorized education into four levels: illiterate, primary, secondary (junior high school, high school, secondary school graduation), college and above (junior college graduation, Bachelor’s Degree, Master’s Degree, Doctorate Degree).

Lifestyle factors considered were: (1) Smoking, (2) Drinking, (3) Sleep duration, (4) Physical activity, and (5) Internet use. The inclusion of Internet use as a lifestyle variable in this study is motivated by the health empowerment theory and social support theory. From the perspective of health empowerment, it enables older adults to access health information and improve self-management, potentially directly affecting chronic disease trajectories. Simultaneously, aligned with social support theory, it helps sustain social ties and reduce loneliness—key psychosocial factors linked to chronic disease development. As Internet use becomes increasingly common among older populations, examining its role is critical for understanding contemporary health determinants.

Smoking equals 0 if an individual has never smoked; if an individual used to smoke but now quits, smoking equals 1, and 2 if an individual is a current smoker. Drinking was treated as a binary variable. Participants who reported consuming any alcoholic beverage in the past year, regardless of frequency, were coded as 1. Those who reported drinking “nothing” in the past year were coded as 0. Sleep duration was primarily assessed by self-reported nighttime sleep, measured with the question: “During the past month, how many hours of actual sleep did you get at night (average hours for one night)?” Based on the responses, participants were categorized into three groups: short sleep duration (< 6 h/night), medium sleep duration (6–9 h/night), and long sleep duration (> 9 h/night). Moreover, a binary variable for daytime napping was created to capture compensatory sleep in older adults. Physical activities were divided into three types, vigorous, moderate and light. Vigorous physical activities made participants short of breath, such as lifting heavy objects, digging, ploughing, aerobics, fast cycling, carrying loads on a bike. Moderate physical activity that makes participants breathe faster than usual, including lifting light objects, cycling at a regular pace, mopping the floor, doing tai chi, walking briskly. Light physical activity such as walking (walking includes walking from one place to another at work or at home, and other walks you take for leisure, sport, exercise or recreation). The Internet use was assessed using the question “In the past month, have you used the Internet for any purpose, including but not limited to instant messaging, news consumption, video streaming, online gaming, or financial management? ” Responses were divided into two categories: 0(no) and 1(yes). In subgroup analyses, those who quit smoking and still smoking were grouped together, and those who slept less than 6 hours and more than 9 hours were grouped together. If the participant consistently does physical activity for at least ten minutes per week, for yes; otherwise, for not.

Statistical analyses

Variables were described by their absolute and relative frequencies (n (%)). This study applied the chi-square test (χ2) to see the association between multimorbidity and its covariates. We then performed the logistic regression to study the determinants of multimorbidity and the association between multimorbidity and area among older adults in China. The results of the logistic regression models are presented using the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). In this study, statistical significance was set at P<0.05(two-tailed). Furthermore, we investigated whether the relationship between lifestyle factors and multimorbidity varied by zone. Subgroup analyses were performed by residential zone. The subgroup-specific odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are displayed in forest plots. In the forest plots, effect estimates for the frigid and non-frigid zone subgroups are presented as squares with horizontal lines denoting 95% confidence intervals (CIs), while the combined meta-analysis estimate is displayed as a diamond with its width representing the 95% CI. The extent of between-subgroup heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic( I²=100%×(Q–df)/Q), where Q is Cochran’s heterogeneity statistic and df the degrees of freedom [40]. The I² statistic describes the percentage of total variation across subgroup estimates that is due to genuine heterogeneity rather than random chance. It ranges from 0% to 100%, where 0% denotes the absence of heterogeneity and increasing values correspond to greater degrees of heterogeneity [41]. Subgroup analysis statistical significance was set at P<0.1 and I2>50%. All of the above analyses were conducted using stata17.0.

Of the whole sample, 7042 individuals presented multimorbidity, corresponding to an overall prevalence of 66.32%. The prevalence differed by region, being higher in the frigid zone (72.69%) than in the non-frigid zone (65.63%). Among samples with multimorbidity, the percentage of non-smokers and non-drinkers was 60.17% and 70.95%, respectively. Approximately 46.61% of the sample with multimorbidity slept 6–9 h. Most of the participants with multimorbidity engaged in light activities (75.11%), while the participation with vigorous-intensity activities (26.91%) was lower. Moreover, 77.65% of the study population with multimorbidity did not use the Internet. Figure 2 compares the population composition of multimorbidity in frigid and non-frigid zones, stratified by demographic and lifestyle characteristics. In the frigid zone sample, 55% of individuals who engaged in light exercise had multimorbidity, which was higher than the 49.25% observed in the non-frigid zone sample.

Multivariable analysis

Table 3 depicts the association of demographic and lifestyle factors with multimorbidity in older adults. Risk of multimorbidity was higher in 70–79 years older adults (OR = 1.251, 95%CI = 1.074–1.363) as compared to the older adults aged 80 and above who are having the lower odds of having multimorbidity (OR = 0.842, 95%CI = 0.721–0.984. Female were more likely than men to have multimorbidity (OR = 1.210 95%CI = 1.074–1.363). Participants who quit smoking are more likely to suffer from multimorbidity (OR = 1.691, 95%CI = 1.462–1.957) compared to those who never smoked, while those who still smoke are less likely to suffer from multimorbidity (OR = 0.883, 95%CI = 0.779–1.001). Participants who drinking were less likely to develop multimorbidity than non-drinkers (OR = 0.706, 95%CI = 0.642–0.778). Compared to those slept less than 6 h per day, who slept 6–9 h and more than 9 h were less likely to have multimorbidity (OR = 0.608, 95%CI = 0.556–0.665; OR = 0.631, 95%CI = 0.541–0.735). Taking daytime naps was identified as a risk factor for multimorbidity when compared to no napping. (OR = 1.272, 95%CI = 1.166–1.387). Vigorous physical activity was found to decrease the risk of multimorbidity (OR = 0.778, 95%CI = 0.707–0.856). Internet use was negatively associated with multimorbidity (OR = 1.254, 95%CI = 1.122–1.401). Moreover, living in frigid zone increases the risk of multimorbidity (OR = 1.315, 95%CI = 1.928–2.692). However, no significant association was found between residence, marital status and moderate physical activities, and multimorbidity in this study.

Discussion

Comparative analysis of multimorbidity prevalence among older adults in frigid versus non-frigid zone

The present study showed that the prevalence of multimorbidity among the older adults is 72.69% in the frigid zone, which is higher than in the non-frigid zone and in the country as a whole. Cold weather in frigid zone lasts a long time. For older adults, exposure to the cold is particularly dangerous and may include shivering, drowsiness and cold skin. Cold exposure can lead to a loss of plasma fluid, which can lead to increased thrombosis and cardiovascular risk. During the winter months in the Northeast in China, some fresh fruits and vegetables are not as available, thus affecting vitamin intake and dietary balance, and this dietary change may contribute to the high prevalence of multimorbidity in older adults in frigid zone [42].

Comparative analysis of lifestyle factors associated with multimorbidity among older adults in frigid versus non-frigid zone

The results of this study showed that the difference in factors affecting multimorbidity between older adults in frigid zone and non-frigid zone was small. This may be due to the fact that centralized heating is provided in winter in frigid zone of China and for a longer period of time, which reduces the difference in exposure to extreme cold temperatures between frigid zone and non-frigid zone. In addition, some older adults in frigid zone choose to spend the winter in warmer places such as Hainan Province which also leads to the fact that older adults in frigid zone are not specialized in terms of the factors influencing multimorbidity. In frigid zone, in addition to routinely making poor lifestyle changes, more attention needs to be paid to multimorbidity in older adults who drink and quit smoking. At the same time, there is a focus on maintaining optimal sleep and on Internet use behavior among older adults in frigid zone.

Subgroup analysis suggested that the effect of Internet use on the multimorbidity of older adults demonstrated heterogeneity in terms of living zone. The effect of Internet use on multimorbidity in frigid zone was more pronounced. Frigid zone is less economically developed than non-frigid zone in China. Older adults living in more deprived areas may be more vulnerable to the negative effects of Internet use. As a result, older adults in frigid zone have higher prevalence of multimorbidity than those in non-frigid zone. There is a clear need to raise awareness of the rational use of the Internet among older adults, particularly in the frigid zone, in order to change the negative impact of Internet use on multimorbidity.

Sociodemographic factors associated with multimorbidity among Chinese older adults

Shift in disease patterns coupled with the aging of the large baby boomer cohort, means multimorbidity is increasingly becoming a serious public health problem. In view of the health risks of multimorbidity in older adults and the differences in risk factors for multimorbidity in different regions, this study examined the prevalence and risk factors of multimorbidity in older adults using data provided in the CHARLS2020. We found that the risk of multimorbidity was greater among those aged 70–79 years, but older adults aged 80 and above are less likely to be multimorbid. Multimorbidity is not worse with age, possibly because some older adults with multimorbidity die before age 80 [43]. A finding that lends weight to this possibility is China’s latest reported average life expectancy of 78.6 years, implying that many individuals with severe multimorbidity might not live beyond the eighth decade, thereby attenuating the observed association between age and multimorbidity severity in the oldest-old population. Among the demographic factors, we find that female sex is a risk factor for multimorbidity in the older adults. This result supported by studies that find older female have a higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes as estrogen levels decline with age [44]. An additional contributing factor is women’s longer life expectancy. Research indicates that by 2035, women in mainland China will have a life expectancy of 85.1 years, 7 years longer than men’s 78.1 years. This longevity advantage paradoxically exposes women to a longer period of health vulnerability, thereby elevating their lifetime risk of accumulating multiple chronic conditions [45]. The results suggested that we should pay more attention to the multimorbidity status of female, while focusing on prevention in the early stages of life. As for marital status, no significant association was observed between marital status and multimorbidity in this study. One possible explanation lies in the buffering effect of the traditional “multi-generational household” model in China. Even though this model is gradually declining overall, living with children remains the most common living arrangement for older adults in China [46]. This implies that older adults without spouses often receive daily care and emotional comfort from their children within multigenerational households. Such support may, to some extent, substitute for the functions provided by a spouse, thereby helping to maintain their health and reduce the risk of multimorbidity.

Lifestyle characteristics associated with multimorbidity among Chinese older adults

Our results showed the significant association between multimorbidity and lifestyle factors like smoking, drinking, sleep duration, vigorous physical activity and Internet use. In previous studies, unhealthy lifestyle habits such as smoking and drinking was associated with a higher likelihood of multimorbidity. Our study found that older adults who had quit smoking were more likely to have multimorbidity than those who had never smoked. One possible explanation given in a Portuguese study is that most participants only quit smoking after being diagnosed with certain diseases [47]. This may also explain why older adults who still smoke was less likely to develop multimorbidity, the participants were not diagnosed and continued to smoke. Multimorbidity is a condition in which a person suffers from more than one disease at the same time. Some studies have shown that lifestyle factors are associated with specific chronic diseases. They found current cigarette smoking was found to increase the hazards of many of the selected chronic diseases, and it was found that the associations for type of smoker (e.g. current smoker) were stronger for certain diseases. And our study found participants who drinking were less likely to develop multimorbidity, which corroborate with the earlier findings that non-drinkers are at increased risk of certain chronic diseases [48]. However, this association is likely a reverse causal relationship—it is not drinking that leads to a lower risk of disease, but rather the deterioration of health itself that causes people to abstain from or reduce their alcohol consumption. This result may be related to China’s wine culture that says “small drinks are good, big drinks hurt” [49]. At the same time, drinking in moderation has very different health effects than excessive drinking. In China, where alcohol consumption is often accompanied by table culture and social interaction, moderate citations can help to relax. There may also be residual confounding among participants who drink alcohol, as this may be a heterogeneous group including excessive drinking and moderate drinking older adults [48]. Moderate alcohol consumption has been shown to be associated with lower rates of chronic diseases such as coronary heart disease and diabetes [33, 50]. Our analysis revealed the significant and positive relation between multimorbidity and sleep duration. Epidemiological studies have found that long sleep duration is a risk factor for a variety of diseases, including cardiovascular disease [51], stroke [52], hypertension [53], cancer [54] and cognitive impairment [55]. There are also studies that showed both prolonged nighttime sleep duration and short nighttime sleep duration are associated with the number of diseases [56, 57]. However, our study found that participants who slept more than 9 hours were less likely to suffer from multimorbidity. One possible reason is that prolonged sleep duration may be a consequence or an incidental phenomenon of one or some of the chronic diseases in the multimorbidity, and may not be a risk factor for the causation of the disease [58]. While the conventional view holds that napping is beneficial for health, our study observed that daytime napping was a significant risk factor for multimorbidity. This association may be attributable to emerging evidence that prolonged napping can disrupt circadian rhythms and elevate inflammatory markers, suggesting a complex relationship between napping patterns and long-term health outcomes [59, 60]. Consistent with previous studies [19], our results demonstrate that engaging in vigorous physical activities is associated with a decreased risk of multimorbidity. Physical activity is associated with increased life expectancy and reduced risk of coronary heart disease, heart stroke, diabetes, hypertension and obesity [61]. There is also study that show that those who engage in vigorous activity are less likely to develop multimorbidity than those who engage in moderate activity [62]. In our study, the proportion of participants who engaged in vigorous physical activities was much lower than the proportion of participants who did not engage in vigorous physical activities. This may be due to the fact that older adults who participate in vigorous physical activities tend to be in better health condition, e.g. less likely to have arthritis, knee pain, fear of falling, to support their strenuous activities. One of the significant findings of this study is the significant positive correlation between Internet use and multimorbidity, which diverges from some earlier studies that adults who use the Internet have a lower prevalence of chronic disease [49, 63]. Multimorbid older adults face worse physical health than older adults with a single chronic condition, which means they need more rest and care. Physical health conditions are one of the main reasons that limit older adults’ access to the Internet. Some studies have shown that Internet use is associated with decreased contact with family, shrinking social networks, and increased feelings of loneliness and depression [64]. In addition, Internet use can even be combined with other unhealthy factors such as sedentary lifestyles, mental illnesses and neurological disorders (e.g. depression), which further influence the development of multimorbidity in older adults [65]. We could not further infer the temporal and causal relationships between lifestyle factors and multimorbidity as this study is a cross-sectional design.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several advantages. First, data were derived from the latest CHARLS2020, which can reflect the latest multimorbidity status of older adults in China and has a high representativeness and timeliness [36]. Second, this study is the first to analyze the multimorbidity status of older adults in frigid zone and non-frigid zone in a comparative manner. Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional nature of the 2020 data limits causal interpretation. Furthermore, our multimorbidity measure was restricted to 15 chronic diseases based on the CHARLS questionnaire, potentially omitting other relevant conditions. Future studies with a more comprehensive set of diseases are warranted to fully understand multimorbidity patterns in this population. Additionally, unmeasured confounders (e.g., income) may have influenced the results.

Conclusions

This study revealed a significantly higher prevalence of multimorbidity among older adults in China’s frigid zone compared to non-frigid zone and national levels. Meanwhile living zones and lifestyles were significantly associated with multimorbidity in older adults. Notably, the effect of Internet use on multimorbidity in the older adults demonstrated significant heterogeneity in terms of living zone. These findings underscore the need for zone-specific prevention strategies: prioritizing multimorbidity screening and management in frigid zone populations while differentiating interventions for frigid zone versus non-frigid zone multimorbid older adults based on different lifestyles. Future longitudinal research is warranted to establish causal relationships between lifestyle modifications and multimorbidity prevention.
