Prevalence and risk factors of preoperative anxiety among obstetric patients undergoing cesarean section under spinal anesthesia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Geresu Gebeyehu Ganamo, Selamawit Shiferaw Jimma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreoperative anxiety is a common and significant concern among obstetric patients scheduled for cesarean section (CS). This anxiety may negatively affect perioperative outcomes for both mother and child. We aimed to determine the incidence of and identify risk factors associated with preoperative anxiety in this population.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among women scheduled for elective CS under spinal anesthesia at two specialized hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, from October 2024 to January 2025. Pregnant women (≥ 18 years) were systematically recruited. The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) was used to measure anxiety. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression were used, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsThe prevalence of clinically significant preoperative anxiety was 54.0% (95% CI: 49.2–58.8%). Independent predictors identified by multivariable logistic regression included: younger age (< 25 years) (AOR = 2.1;95%CI:1.3–3.5;p = 0.002), lower educational attainment (AOR = 1.8;95%CI:1.1–2.9;p = 0.016), previous negative surgical experience (AOR = 2.5;95%CI:1.4–4.6;p = 0.004), lack of preoperative counseling (AOR = 3.2;95%CI:2.0–5.1;p < 0.001), and poor social support (AOR = 2.0;95%CI:1.2–3.3;p = 0.008).ConclusionPreoperative anxiety is highly prevalent. Anxiety in this population is highly preventable through targeted, cost-effective interventions. The strong predictive capacity of modifiable factors (counseling and social support) provides a clear blueprint for clinical action, particularly in settings with resource limitations. Implementation should focus on establishing a standardized anesthetist consultation, a supportive social network or pathway, and maintaining educational accessibility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03945-2.Keywords: Preoperative anxiety, Cesarean section, Spinal anesthesia, Cross-sectional study, Prevalence, Risk factors

Introduction

Cesarean section (CS) is a major surgical procedure performed globally, primarily to ensure the safety and well-being of both the mother and the fetus when vaginal delivery carries significant risks. Over the past few decades, the rate of cesarean deliveries has increased steadily worldwide, reflecting evolving obstetric practices and advances in surgical and anesthetic techniques [1, 2]. While CS is often a life-saving intervention, it is associated with a range of emotional responses, among which preoperative anxiety is particularly prevalent [3, 4].

Preoperative anxiety is a complex emotional state characterized by feelings of apprehension, nervousness, and fear prior to undergoing a surgical procedure [5, 6]. These feelings can be profoundly intensified in obstetrics’ settings by concerns regarding the mother’s health, safety of the unborn child, anticipated pain or discomfort, previous negative experiences with childbirth or surgery, and uncertainties about the surgical process [7–10].

Specifically, the anticipation of spinal anesthesia, the preferred technique for CS, can heighten anxiety due to fears related to loss of sensation or awareness during surgery and potential complications [9, 11].

The reported prevalence of clinically significant anxiety among women scheduled for CS is notably high, with rates ranging widely from 40% to 80% in various populations [3, 12, 13]. High preoperative anxiety not only impairs the patient’s psychological well-being but also triggers adverse physiological responses, including increased heart rate, elevated blood pressure, and altered stress hormone levels. These changes can complicate anesthetic management and surgical outcomes potentially leading to greater intraoperative and postoperative complications [4–7]. Moreover, maternal anxiety may also negatively influence neonatal outcomes, affecting fetal heart rate patterns and contributing to less favorable birth experiences [4, 14].

Anxiety levels are heterogeneous and are influenced by a variety of factors including the sociodemographic characteristics (age, educational attainment, socioeconomic status); obstetric history (parity, previous surgical experiences); social factors (the availability and quality of social support); and the extent and quality of preoperative counseling and information provided to the patient [3, 12, 15, 16].

Identifying patients at heightened risk for significant preoperative anxiety is therefore critical for healthcare providers, as it enables the implementation of targeted, pre-emptive interventions to support psychological well-being, improve the perioperative experience, and potentially enhance surgical outcomes [3, 6, 15].

Despite the recognized importance of preoperative anxiety, there remains a need for robust, context-specific studies to quantify its incidence and systematically identify associated risk factors in diverse obstetric populations, particularly in resource-constrained settings [12, 13].

The aim of this study was to determine the prevalence and associated risk factors of preoperative anxiety in women undergoing elective cesarean section under spinal anesthesia at major teaching hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, from October 01, 2024, to January 25, 2025. The study sites were Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH), a tertiary teaching hospital, and Gandhi Memorial Hospital (GMH), a specialized obstetric and gynecologic hospital in the city. Both facilities serve a large volume of obstetric patients scheduled for CS with a comparable case flow rate.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The source population comprised all obstetric patients scheduled for CS at the study hospitals during the data collection period. The study population included all eligible patients undergoing elective CS under spinal anesthesia.

Inclusion criteria were: pregnant women aged ≥ 18 years, scheduled for elective CS under spinal anesthesia, and willing and able to provide written informed consent and communicate effectively.

Exclusion criteria were: patients undergoing emergency CS, those with a known history of psychiatric disorders or on psychotropic medications (to avoid confounding on STAI results), and those with a contraindication to spinal anesthesia or who refused to participate.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The sample size (n) was determined using the single population proportion formula, assuming a 50% expected population proportion (p = 0.5) of preoperative anxiety (as no local studies existed), a 95% confidence interval (CI), and a 5% margin of error (d = 0.05) [17], as follows:

Accounting for a 10% non-response rate, the final calculated sample size was N ≈ 422.

A systematic random sampling technique was employed. We conducted a situational analysis of a total number of C/S in four months and found it to be 615 in both settings (326 from GMH and 289 from TASH). After using a proportional allocation of samples to a respective set ups, 224 participants were obtained from GMH and 198 participants were included from BLH. Eligible patients were selected from the operating theatre’s elective surgical schedule using an interval of (K = 1.5 ≈ 2) based on the expected patient flow during the study period, until the required sample size was met. Thus, every second patient from a random start was recruited from the random start point from the daily surgical schedule. The random start of the first sample was selected from the given samples using a lottery method.

Data collection tools and measurements

The data collection tool consisted of a structured questionnaire and the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI). The questionnaire captured data on sociodemographic (Age, education, income, etc.), obstetric/clinical (Parity, previous CS/surgery, comorbidities), and psychosocial/care factors (Social support status, receipt of preoperative counseling, family history of mental illness). The social support status was assesses using MSPSS (multidimensional scale of perceived social support), which is a 12 item tool to evaluate a support from the sources of support and it has a 7 point Likert scale points with 1 corresponds to a very strong disagreement, 2-strongly disagree, 3-mildly disagree, 4-neutral, 5-mildly agree, 6-strongly agree, and 7-very strongly agree. The mean MSPSS of 1-2.9 indicates low perceived support, 3–5 indicate moderate and 5.1-7 indicates a high social support [18].

Preoperative anxiety was measured using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-S Form) which consists of 20 items and scores ranging from 20 to 80. A STAI-S score of ≥ 40 indicates the presence of clinically significant preoperative anxiety, consistent with established literature for surgical patients [15, 19].

Data were collected by trained BSc Nurse Anesthetists and Midwives through face-to-face interviews. The interview was conducted in a private, quiet setting (e.g., pre-operative holding area) after the patient had been confirmed for surgery, but prior to the commencement of any pre-medication or transfer to the operating room. The time of assessment was standardized to approximately 30–60 min before transfer to the operation theatre. The questionnaire was initially developed in English, translated to Amharic, and back-translated to ensure consistency.

Data processing and analysis

Data were checked for completeness and consistency, then coded and entered using SPSS version 22. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were used to summarize participant characteristics and the prevalence of anxiety.

Binary logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with preoperative anxiety. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were candidates for the final multivariable logistic regression model to control for potential confounders. The Hosmer-Lemeshow Goodness-of-Fit test was used to check the model’s fitness. Adjusted Odds Ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Ethical review board of college of health sciences, Addis Ababa University assessed the eligibility of a study and approved with a reference number; CHS//Anes/43////2024/25. Informed consent was obtained after fully explaining the study’s purpose, their right to withdraw at any time, and the assurance of confidentiality. The principles of declaration of Helsinki were applied.

Discussion

This study found an alarmingly high prevalence of clinically significant preoperative anxiety (54.0%; 95% CI: 49.2–58.8%) among obstetric patients scheduled for elective Cesarean Section (CS) under spinal anesthesia. This figure underscores the high emotional burden experienced by this specific population, even within a resource-constrained setting where anxiety management protocols might be less established.

Our result falls within the broad range reported internationally (i.e., 40 − 80%) [3, 6, 12, 13]. However, it is higher than figures reported in some western high-income countries, such as a study in Turkey which reported 43% anxiety using the same STAI tool [20], or lower rates found in certain European cohorts [21]. The high prevalence in our study may be a true reflection of the environment, where factors such as lower health literacy, inconsistent access to high-quality antenatal education, and potential cultural anxieties surrounding surgical birth contribute significantly.

Conversely, our figure is lower than the 65% reported in some Asian or other African studies [22]. This variability necessitates a critical analysis of the context. The homogeneity of the study population provides a robust snapshot for this specific context, highlighting an urgent need for local intervention.

Our multivariate analysis systematically identified five independent predictors, the strength of which emphasizes the combined role of preparedness, personal history, and social context.

The finding that the absence of preoperative counseling is the single strongest predictor (AOR = 3.2 or 220% increased odds) represents a key actionable insight. This magnitude of association significantly exceeds what is reported in some literature where counseling’s effect is more modest [23, 24]. We hypothesize that in settings where patient-physician communication may be rushed or standardized protocols for emotional preparation are weak, the complete lack of counseling creates a disproportionately large vacuum of fear and uncertainty.

Similarly, poor social support (AOR = 2.0) reinforces established psychosocial models. Social support acts as a critical buffer against stress [25, 26]. Our result suggests that whatever metric was used to define “poor support” in our setting, its absence doubles the risk of clinical anxiety, underscoring the necessity of actively involving support persons in the perioperative journey.

The predictive power of a previous negative surgical experience (AOR = 2.5) is a consistent finding across surgical subspecialties [27]. However, its prominence in this obstetric cohort requires specific attention. For patients undergoing repeat CS, the negative experience may relate not just to pain or complication, but to perceived loss of control, poor communication, or emotional distress during the prior event.

Our finding that younger age (≤ 25 years; AOR = 2.1) is a strong independent predictor aligns with several reports [15, 16]. Younger women may have fewer established coping mechanisms, lack prior major medical experience, and face greater life transitions simultaneously. This factor serves as a robust indicator for vulnerability, suggesting that young patients should be prioritized for extended counseling sessions.

The association of lower educational attainment (AOR = 1.8) with increased anxiety is also consistent with the literature [28]. This factor critically intersects with the lack of counseling. Individuals with lower literacy may struggle to process complex medical information or standard printed materials, leading to greater feelings of uncertainty and fear. Clinicians must criticize their own communication methods and shift from reliance on written documentation to verbal, visual, and simplified explanations, ensuring true comprehension.

Interestingly, several factors commonly discussed in the literature, such as ASA physical status, parity, and history of previous C/S, were not found to be independent predictors in our adjusted model. This suggests that the psychological burden of anxiety in this cohort is primarily driven by psychosocial and care-related factors, rather than inherent clinical risk (ASA status) [29]. The non-significance of ASA status suggests that the fear is centered on the unknowns of the anesthetic process (spinal anesthesia) and the baby’s well-being, rather than the mother’s baseline physical health [29].

Furthermore, while previous C/S history itself was not significant, the strong significance of a previous negative surgical experience (AOR = 2.5) highlights the importance of the quality and memory of the prior event, emphasizing the need for targeted screening that goes beyond a simple ‘yes’ or ‘no’ on the surgical history [30]. Similarly, the effect of marital status and occupation appears to be absorbed by the stronger, more proximal predictors of poor social support and lower educational attainment in the multivariable analysis [31].

The strength of this study was its prospective nature and conducted on a homogenous population and explored the most important clinical factor among obstetric patients. However, its cross-sectional nature and involving only elective obstetrics patients are the major limitations of this study.

Clinical implications and future directions

The major conclusion is that anxiety in this population is highly preventable through targeted, cost-effective interventions. The strong predictive capacity of modifiable factors (counseling and social support) provides a clear blueprint for clinical action, particularly in settings with resource limitations. Implementation should focus on:

Standardizing Anesthetist Consultation: Ensuring that the consultation is not merely technical but specifically addresses fears related to consciousness, pain, and the specific sensations of spinal anesthesia.

Conclusion and recommendations

Preoperative anxiety is highly prevalent among obstetric patients undergoing elective cesarean section under spinal anesthesia. Modifiable factors specifically lack of preoperative counseling and poor social support were identified as the strongest independent predictors, alongside non-modifiable factors such as younger age and lower educational attainment.

Further research must move beyond prevalence studies. Interventional randomized controlled trials are warranted to rigorously test the impact of structured counseling packages (e.g., cognitive preparation, relaxation techniques) on anxiety reduction and subsequent perioperative outcomes, including analgesic requirements, patient satisfaction, and recovery time. Such trials are crucial for translating our correlational findings into definitive, evidence-based policy.

Recommendations

Based on these findings, we recommend the following clinical and research actions:

Routine Screening: Implement routine, brief screening for preoperative anxiety (e.g., using a short, validated tool like the STAI) for all obstetric surgical patients to identify high-risk individuals before the day of surgery.
