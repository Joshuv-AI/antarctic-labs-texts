New deep sea terebellid polychaete with sucker like ventral pads adapted to a sediment free environment
Authors: Naoto Jimi, Geminne G Manzano, Natsumi Hookabe, Hiroki Kise, James Davis Reimer, Sau Pinn Woo, Yoshihiro Fujiwara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA new species of terebellid polychaete, Lanice spongicola sp. nov., is described from deep-sea sponges in the northwestern Pacific Ocean off Minamidaito Island, Okinawa, Japan. This species is associated with the sponge Walteria cf. leuckarti at a depth of 843 m. It is characterized by a deeply grooved, sucker-like mid-ventral pad on segments II–VI, three pairs of arborescent branchiae, distinct notochaetae and neurochaetae morphology and arrangement, and its ecological association with sponges. The anterior area of ventral pad is used as an adhesion organ, facilitating attachment to the host sponge. This species is a terebellid that differs from the typical sediment-dwelling lifestyle, having adapted to a sediment-free environment through a relationship with sponges and the development of specialized sucker structures for attachment.Subject terms: Taxonomy, Evolution

Introduction

Suckers are a morphological feature independently acquired by some organisms including fishes, leeches, and octopuses1–3. While the evolutionary pathways leading to sucker development vary4, some examples suggest their acquisition is linked to symbiotic relationships. For instance, nemerteans of the genus Malacobdella are thought to have developed suckers as an adaptation for symbiosis with bivalves5, while parasitic flatworms such as Monogenea use suckers to attach to the gills of their hosts (Rhode 1994). These examples underscore how novel traits like suckers can facilitate entry into new ecological niches, and thus create novel biodiversity. However, such instances remain rare4, and further studies are required to fully understand the role of suckers in fostering symbiotic relationships and enhancing biodiversity.

Members of the family Terebellidae are predominantly sediment-dependent polychaetes6. They typically inhabit tubes buried in sediment or occupy spaces between rocks and sediment. They construct their tubes using collected sediment particles and feeding on suspended particles captured by their tentacles. This dependence on sediment has largely restricted Terebellidae from colonizing high-flow and sediment-deficient environments. Furthermore, apart from cases where the family serves as hosts7,8, symbiotic behavior has not been documented within Terebellidae9.

Steep, sediment-scarce deep-sea environments, such as seamounts, ridges, and trenches, are widespread across the globe. It is estimated that there are more than 25 millions of these structures in the world’s oceans, representing significant marine habitats and hosting high levels of biodiversity10. These environments are known to generate ocean currents, creating conditions where sediment accumulation is minimal compared to abyssal plains11.

Submerged karst-derived deep-sea environments, formed from coral reef structures, are particularly prone to developing steep topography and sediment-poor conditions. The challenges posed by such environments have rendered traditional benthic sampling methods like dredges and grab samplers largely ineffective. Consequently, biological exploration in these regions had been scarce until the advent of remotely operated vehicle (ROV) technology. Minamidaito Island, a karst island originating from coral reefs, exemplifies this steep and sediment-deficient habitat, resembling the characteristics of seamount ecosystems12. Despite their prevalence, the benthic communities in these unique and dynamic environments remain largely unexplored and offer significant opportunities for advancing our understanding of deep-sea biodiversity and ecological adaptations.

To address these gaps, the Deep-sea Archaic Refugia in Karst (D-ARK) project, led by the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC), has been exploring these unique environments in the waters around Japan. During project surveys, we discovered a terebellid polychaete associated with tree-like glass sponges growing on rocky substrates. Remarkably, this terebellid species has evolved a sucker-like ventral pad, a specialized morphological adaptation that facilitates attachment to its sponge host. Based on these unique characteristics, we describe the polychaete as a new species. Furthermore, we explore the evolutionary implications of the sucker-like ventral pad’s development in conjunction with symbiosis, discussing how this innovation enables sediment-dependent organisms to adapt to sediment-deficient environments. This study sheds light on the processes of morphological innovation, ecological expansion, and their roles in promoting biodiversity in challenging habitats.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and preservation

Terebellid worms were collected from off Minamidaito Island, Okinawa (25°52.081´ N, 131°12.483´ E) (Fig. 1A), found attached to a glass sponge (Fig. 1B and C). The host sponge (NSMT Po-2515, GenBank accession number: PQ565536) was previously examined and identified as Walteria cf. leuckarti Ijima, 1896 (Japanese name: suginoki-kaimen) in Hookabe et al. (2025)5. After removing the worms from their hosts (Fig. 2), one parapodium from the holotype was excised and preserved in 99.5% ethanol for DNA extraction, while the remaining body was fixed in formalin and then transferred to 70% ethanol. Preserved specimens were examined under stereomicroscopes (SMZ18 and ECLIPSE Ni). Scanning electron microscope (SEM) observations followed the protocols described by Jimi et al. (2024)12. All specimens were deposited in the National Museum of Nature and Science, Tsukuba (NSMT).

DNA extraction, sequencing, phylogenetic analyses

Genomic DNA was extracted from a small piece of the parapodium of the holotype (NSMT-Pol H-999) using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Partial gene sequences for mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI), and nuclear 18S ribosomal RNA (18S) and 28S ribosomal RNA (28S) were amplified and sequenced according to the methods described in the previous study13. The newly obtained sequences have been deposited in GenBank (PV067165, PV069109, PV069110) (Table 1). Data enquiries should be addressed to corresponding author (beniimo7010@gmail.com). The phylogenetic analyses were also carried out according to the previous study13.

Habitat and distribution

Specimens of Lanice spongicola sp. nov. were collected from the external surface of the sponge Walteria cf. leuckarti (Fig. 1B and C). The host sponge was found off Minamidaito Island in the northwestern Pacific Ocean at a depth of 843 m. The host’s habitat consisted entirely of solid rock, with no surrounding sediment. While most terebellids typically inhabit sediment, this species constructed its tube on the lower part of the sponge, which was growing like a tree. When extracted from its tube, it was found to be tightly adhered to the inner wall of the tube using its ventral pad, which is deeply grooved and modified into a sucker-like structure. This suggests that the ventral pad functions as an adhesion organ. This adhesive behavior likely allows the worm to anchor itself securely to the sponge surface during tube construction or when exposed to ambient water currents in a sediment-free environment (= on Walteria tree sponge).

Phylogenetic analysis

In the COI-only tree (Fig. 6A), the new species grouped with Lanice conchilega. In the four-gene tree (Fig. 6B), it grouped with Loimia minuta. Both topologies showed low support (bootstrap < 50).

Discussion

Taxonomic status and phylogenetic position of Lanice spongicola sp. nov.

This species is clearly distinguished from all known congeners by the unique morphology of its ventral pad, which is deeply grooved in the anterior body (Segments II–VI), forming a sucker-like structure with elevated margins.

Molecular phylogenetic analyses were conducted using both COI-only and concatenated four-gene datasets (Fig. 6). In the COI analysis, L. spongicola sp. nov. clustered with Lanice conchilega, while in the four-gene analysis it grouped with Loimia minuta. However, both placements received very low bootstrap support, leaving the phylogenetic position of the new species unresolved.

Given this uncertainty, we have chosen to provisionally assign this species to the genus Lanice. Should future studies discover additional species possessing similarly modified ventral pads, a more comprehensive phylogenetic and taxonomic re-evaluation may be warranted. At that time, it will be possible to determine whether L. spongicola sp. nov. represents a highly derived member of Lanice, or instead belongs to a separate and hitherto unrecognized lineage within the Terebellidae.

The current lack of comprehensive genetic data for Terebellidae hinders deeper phylogenetic insights. Most data in GenBank consist of COI and 16S gene sequences14, emphasizing the need to expand datasets with other commonly used markers such as 18S and 28S ribosomal DNA. While these markers improve phylogenetic resolution, integrating genomic datasets would further strengthen evolutionary inferences. Genome-scale data, including ultra-conserved elements (UCEs), whole-genome sequences, and transcriptomic datasets, provide robust frameworks for resolving deep phylogenetic relationships and detecting adaptive evolution. Expanding these datasets will clarify phylogenetic relationships and illuminate the evolutionary pathways of unique traits such as the sucker-shaped ventral pad.

Association between sucker morphology and symbiotic ecology

Sucker-like structures have evolved independently across many taxa, yet the mechanisms behind their evolution remain poorly understood4,15. These structures are generally used for adhesion, but their acquisition specifically for host attachment in symbiotic relationships is rare. In the case of the new species described here, the evolution of its sucker-like ventral pad appears to be closely tied to its symbiotic relationship with sponges. This highlights that morphological innovations such as suckers can play an important role in expanding biodiversity by enabling novel ecological strategies.

The sucker-like structure in this species likely represents a specialized modification of the ventral pad commonly observed in Terebellidae (Fig. 3C and D). This adaptation is unique to the new species and provides an opportunity to study the evolutionary processes behind sucker development. Future studies on the genetic, neural, and muscular underpinnings of this structure could offer valuable insights into its evolutionary origins.

Although the precise nature of the relationship between this species and its sponge host remains to be elucidated, preliminary field observations support the assumption of a symbiotic association. Individuals were never observed within sediment, beneath rocks, or attached to other types of sponges in the surrounding environment. Instead, they were consistently found only on the specific tree-shaped Walteria sponge species. Based on this host specificity and consistent spatial association, we regard the relationship as symbiotic for the purpose of this study.

Evolution of sediment-dependent organisms in steep, sediment-scarce deep-sea environments

Deep-sea submerged karst landscapes, with their steep slopes and minimal sediment accumulation, present significant challenges for sediment-dependent organisms. These environments are often inaccessible using traditional sampling tools such as dredges or grab samplers, and biological research in these areas was limited until the development of ROV technology12. With the increasing availability of ROV technology, the observation of benthic organisms in steep, sediment-poor environments has recently advanced significantly. As a result, cases like the present species, where organisms typically inhabiting sediments have adapted to sediment-lacking environments, have been documented16. This raises a compelling question: How do sediment-dependent organisms adapt and thrive in such sediment-scarce environments?

Members of the family Terebellidae are typically reliant on sediments, constructing burrows or tubes within sediment or in spaces between rocks and sediments to support their survival6. Furthermore, terebellids are known to select specific sediment types for settlement during their larval stage17. While exceptions to this pattern are known—for example, some polycirrids that construct only mucous sheaths or lack tubes altogether, and the free-swimming terebellid Biremis—most described terebellids are still associated with sediment-based habitats. Our discovery of a new terebellid species inhabiting a sediment-free sponge surface suggests that terebellids can also colonize sediment-deficient environments under certain conditions. Notably, this species was not found in areas with surrounding sediment, implying that, if capable of selecting their settlement substrate as previous studies have suggested, this species would choose sponges as its substrate instead of sediment.

The host sponge for this new species resides on bare rock devoid of any sediment, in an environment characterized by strong currents (Fig. 1B and C). The unique sucker-like ventral pad observed in this new species likely facilitates adhesion to its sponge host, enabling the polychaete to inhabit this otherwise inaccessible niche. This represents an example of how morphological innovation can drive ecological expansion.

In addition to the adhesion mechanism, other aspects of the worm’s biology may also reflect adaptations to this sediment-deficient habitat. Although direct observations of feeding or tube-building behavior are lacking, we hypothesize that Lanice spongicola sp. nov. may engage in suspension feeding, capturing fine particulate organic matter or detritus from the ambient water. The elevated position and tree-like morphology of the host Walteria sponge may facilitate this mode of feeding by increasing access to water flow and suspended material. Moreover, the worm appears to construct its tube directly on the sponge surface, likely using secreted mucus combined with intercepted particles, rather than incorporating surrounding sediment as typical terebellids do. These potential adaptations further support the notion that L. spongicola sp. nov. has shifted away from traditional sediment dependency and is utilizing alternative strategies for both feeding and tube construction.

The transition of a sediment-dependent organism to a sediment-scarce habitat through the acquisition of novel morphological traits offers key insights into the interplay between morphology, ecology, and evolution. This new species not only demonstrates how terebellids can overcome habitat constraints, but also serves as a model for exploring the evolutionary history underlying habitat transitions and adaptive innovation.
