Reassessing the Rinne Test: Importance of a Three‐Frequency Tuning Fork Evaluation
Authors: Martin B C da Silva, Rudolfh B Arend, Natan L Lima, Daniel B Zatt, Arthur H da Silva, Elisa C Nauck, Joel Lavinsky, Syriaco A Kotzias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective(1) To evaluate the accuracy of the Rinne test in the diagnosis of conductive hearing loss (CHL) at the frequencies of 512, 1024 and 2048 Hz comparing to standard audiometry. (2) To define a possible linear relationship between the frequencies of the tuning forks and the proportional increase in the air‐bone gap (ABG).Study DesignRetrospective cross‐sectional.SettingOutpatient otology clinic.MethodsOne hundred and twenty‐one patients diagnosed with otosclerosis in at least 1 ear were evaluated between 1998 and 2019 and underwent the Rinne test with 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz tuning forks followed by an audiometry prior to surgery. Then, the Rinne test results were compared with the ABG found in audiometry.ResultsThe sensitivity of the Rinne test in the diagnosis of otosclerosis was 97.3%, 91%, and 48% at 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz, respectively. The test showed high specificity at all frequencies (90.7% at 512 Hz, 100% at 1024 Hz, and 100% at 2048 Hz). Logistic regression used to calculate the Rinne transition point revealed that the probability of a negative Rinne test result reached 50% from an ABG of 13.9 dB, 18.7 dB, and 31.3 dB when using 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz, respectively.ConclusionThe Rinne test has a high sensitivity at the frequencies of 512 and 1024 Hz, being an excellent test for otosclerosis screening. At the same time, the use of the 3 tuning fork frequencies is an excellent method for estimating the severity of pure conductive hearing loss.Level of EvidenceLevel 3.Keywords: air‐bone gap, conductive, hearing loss, otosclerosis, rinne test, tuning fork

In 1855 Heinrich Adolf Rinne (1819‐1868) showed that a tuning fork is heard better by air than by bone in normal ears. 1 August Lucae and Friedrich Bezold introduced his test into routine practice in the 1890s. 2 , 3 Today the quick, inexpensive bedside Rinne test supports hearing‐loss screening, audiometric confirmation, grading and typing of loss, and selection of otosclerosis candidates for stapedotomy. 4 Classical texts claim that a negative Rinne at 512 and 1024 Hz indicates ossicular fixation warranting surgery, and negativity at 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz identifies an excellent candidate 5 ; however, these thresholds lack solid evidence.

Despite long use, contemporary studies are few and heterogeneous, differing in the air‐bone gap (ABG) that defines abnormality and in how ABG varies with fork frequency, material, technique, or patient cohort, hampering standardisation. Robust data are needed to clarify the test's limitations and ideal conditions. We therefore assessed the diagnostic accuracy of Rinne tests at 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz versus pure‐tone audiometry and analysed how abnormal results at each frequency predict the magnitude of conductive hearing loss.

Methods

A retrospective cross‐sectional study was carried out in which 121 patients (242 ears) were evaluated between 1998 and 2019 in a public service, Hospital Governador Celso Ramos, and another private otolaryngology clinic in the city of Florianopolis, Santa Catarina state, Brazil. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hospital Governador Celso Ramos (CAAE: 27892920.0.0000.5360).

We included patients aged 16 to 65 years with otosclerosis in at least 1 ear who underwent stapedotomy, had an intact tympanic membrane, unequivocal Rinne test results, and audiometry performed within 1 week of the tuning fork test. All Rinne tests were performed by the same experienced otologist. Patients with neuropsychiatric disorders or other ear diseases that could affect the results were excluded.

All patients underwent Rinne test with 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz aluminum tuning forks in both ears using the loudness comparison technique. 6 , 7 The tuning fork was activated by percussion on the proximal or middle interphalangeal joint, then it was positioned firmly on the mastoid (position of the BC) for a few seconds, and then it was moved in front of the external auditory canal (position of the AC), being positioned parallel to it and approximately 3 to 4 cm from the tragus. 8 Although the exact force applied to the mastoid and the precise distance between the tuning fork and the external auditory canal were not formally measured or standardized, reflecting routine clinical practice, in which such parameters are rarely quantified.

The patient was asked to compare sound intensity at each position. Results were classified as a positive Rinne test when sound was louder in front of the ear than on the mastoid (AC > BC), negative when louder on the mastoid than in front of the ear (BC > AC), and equivocal when no clear difference was perceived or responses were inconsistent on retesting (AC = BC). Patients with equivocal results were excluded from the study.

The ABG of each patient was defined from an average of the ABGs at the frequencies of 500, 1, 2, and 3 kHz. 9 A four‐frequency averaged ABG was chosen to represent the overall severity of conductive hearing loss, as the Rinne test reflects a global comparison between air and bone conduction rather than a strictly frequency‐specific phenomenon. The “transition point” of the Rinne test was defined as the specific ABG present when the Rinne test transitions from normal to an abnormal result in most patients. 4

Statistical Analysis

To characterize the patients and their ears evaluated, descriptive statistics was applied, presenting absolute and relative frequency for nominal variables, and mean with the respective standard deviation for continuous variables. ABG, expressed in decibel (dB), was analyzed as a continuous variable. The possible results of the Rinne test were grouped into a nominal variable, resulting in the following groups and their respective labels: (a) Positive Rinne at all frequencies [(+) 512 Hz (+) 1024 Hz (+) 2048 Hz]; (b) Rinne negative at 512 Hz and positive at other frequencies [(−) 512 Hz (+) 1024 Hz (+) 2048 Hz]; (c) Rinne negative at 512 and 1024 Hz, but positive at 2048 Hz [(−) 512 Hz (−) 1024 Hz (+) 2048 Hz]; (d) negative Rinne at all frequencies [(−) 512 Hz (−) 1024 Hz (−) 2048 Hz].

The diagnostic validation of the Rinne test was evaluated by comparing the test result at 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz with the diagnosis of conductive hearing loss (CHL) given by audiometry, the reference method. It is classified as CHL when the average audiometry ABG is 15 dB or greater at least one frequency. 10 To compare the accuracy of the test applied with different tuning forks, we used the parameters of sensitivity, specificity and positive and negative predictive values.

The description of the ABG interval found when the Rinne reversal occurs at 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz was expressed between the 25th and 75th percentiles (P25; P75), and between the 5th and 95th percentiles (P5; P95) of the sample. The first and second intervals include, respectively, 50% and 90% of the sample around the median.

The mean ABG in ears with normal hearing thresholds and ears with Rinne test reversal with 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz tuning forks was estimated from simple linear regression and then compared using contrasts. Results were presented in predicted averages and comparisons were tested by adopting a significance level of 5% (P < .05) with Sidak adjustment. The regression model was evaluated for the assumptions of homoscedasticity and normality of the residues.

The probability of a negative Rinne test result according to ABG was estimated from binary logistic regression models. Three regression models were computed, one for each of the following outcomes: (a) Rinne negative at 512 Hz; (b) Rinne negative at 1024 Hz; (c) Negative Rinne at 2048 Hz. Estimated parameters were used to construct logistic curves showing the negative Rinne probabilities as a function of mean ABG and the respective 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). All analyses were conducted on the Stata SE statistical package version 15 (StataCorp LLC).

Results

Sample Characterization

The study included 121 patients diagnosed with otosclerosis between 1998 and 2019 in 2 otorhinolaryngology services in Florianopolis, SC, Brazil (Table 1). The patients (mean age 43 years) were mostly female (69%). All patients had both ears evaluated, resulting in a final sample of 242 observations. From the total ears evaluated (mean ABG of 25.1 dB), it was observed that 77% were diagnosed with CHL from the audiometric evaluation. The proportion of ears diagnosed with CHL from the Rinne test was 78%, 71%, and 36% using 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz tuning forks, respectively.

Both 512 Hz (97.3%; 95% CI: 93.9; 99.1) and 1024 Hz (91%; 95% CI: 85.9; 94,6) tuning forks presented higher sensitivity than the 2048 Hz tuning fork (46.8%; 95% CI: 39.5; 54.2). The low sensitivity observed for the 2048 Hz tuning fork suggests that the instrument has a higher proportion of false‐negative diagnoses compared to the lower‐pitch tuning forks. No differences were observed between 512 Hz (90.7%; 95% CI: 79.7; 96.9), 1024 Hz (100%; 95% CI: 93.4; 100), and 2048 Hz (100%; 95% CI: 93.4; 100) tuning forks for specificity. Both instruments presented high specificity, which suggests a low proportion of false positive diagnoses.

The Rinne test at the 3 frequencies had a similar positive predictive value and greater than 95%. However, the negative predictive value decreased with increasing frequency of the tuning fork used in the test [512 Hz (90.7%; 95% CI: 79.7; 96.9), 1024 Hz (76.1%; 95% CI: 64.5; 85.4), and 2048 Hz (35.1%; 95% CI: 27.6; 43.2)].

Results of Transition Point

Transition point of the Rinne test is the specific ABG present when the Rinne test transitions from normal to an abnormal result in most patients. 4 Different statistical methods have already been used to determine the transition point as shown by some previous studies. 7 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14

Logistic regression was the method used in our study. It indicates the probability of a negative Rinne test as a function of ABG. Therefore, we observed that the probability of a negative Rinne test result was greater than 50% in 13.9, 18.7, and 31.3 dB when using 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz tuning forks, respectively (Figure 2).

Probability of negative Rinne test as function of air‐bone gap. Note: dotted lines refer to the confidence intervals of 95% for their respective curve.

Kelly et al 4 investigated the transition point in 10 studies in a systematic review published in 2018. For the 512 Hz Rinne test, the transition points ranged from 17.5 dB to 40 dB (mean, 26 dB). However, the method used to calculate the transition points in these studies was not standardized, with 6 different methods being verified among the 10 studies evaluated.

Range of ABG Expected for the Negative Rinne at 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz

Anecdotal observations regarding the range of ABG expected for a negative Rinne at 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz vary widely. In clinical practice, it is more common to see an ABG between 15 and 20 dB when we have a negative Rinne at 512 Hz, an ABG between 20 and 35 dB when negative Rinne at 512 and 1024 Hz and an ABG greater than 35 dB when we have a negative Rinne in the 3 frequencies.

In this study, when considering the interval between the 25th and 75th percentiles, we found a range of ABG of 13.8 to 18.8 dB when negative Rinne at 512 Hz, 22.5 dB to 31.3 dB when negative Rinne at 1024 Hz, and 32.5 dB to 40.6 dB when negative Rinne at 2048 Hz (Figure 3).

Discussion

Characteristics of the Rinne Test Technique: Tuning Fork Frequency, Materials, Masking, Rinne Method, Positioning, Strength, and Effect of Practitioner Experience

The 128 and 256 Hz forks generate overtones an octave above the fundamental, especially in bone‐conduction testing. 15 Clinic low‐frequency noise further reduces the accuracy of 256 Hz assessments, 16 and Sheehy et al showed that its vibrations can be mistaken for sound, contraindicating its use. 17 Most earlier studies (mainly pre‐1990s) relied on 256 and 512 Hz forks. In 2015 Stevens et al 18 analysed 256, 512, and 1024 Hz forks struck on 4 surfaces; only the 2 lower frequencies produced nonharmonic, potentially misleading overtones when hit on wood or metal, whereas 1024 Hz remained pure. A 2018 systematic review by Kelly et al 4 included 16 Rinne‐test studies, all using 256 or 512 Hz forks. Our study evaluated 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz forks; 256 Hz was not tested due to its known susceptibility to vibrotactile perception and overtone generation, which can confound bone conduction assessment and reduce diagnostic accuracy.

Regarding fork material, we chose aluminium because it is corrosion‐resistant, lighter and cheaper than steel. 11 MacKechnie et al nevertheless showed that steel forks detect an ABG 8 dB smaller, making them more sensitive. 11 Masking was not applied. Kelly et al's review found only 2 studies that compared masked and unmasked Rinne in the same patients, 4 and masking is rarely used in everyday practice. 12 , 19

The Rinne test may be performed using loudness‐comparison or timed‐threshold techniques; we used the former, which Kelly et al 4 considered superior for detecting CHL. Browning et al 20 reported 24% higher sensitivity with loudness comparison for an ABG ≥ 20 dB using a 256 Hz fork, while Golabek and Stephens 13 found that the timed‐threshold method increased the transition point by 5 dB with a 512 Hz fork.

All tests were performed with the fork tines parallel to the external auditory canal (EAC). Butskiy et al measured a 2.5 dB sound‐level gain with this orientation in mannequin experiments, 8 yet a 2018 clinical study of 50 patients showed identical diagnostic accuracy for parallel versus perpendicular positions, 14 indicating orientation does not affect real‐world outcomes.

Distance from fork to EAC is seldom measured. Kelly et al noted most studies place the fork 2 to 2.5 cm from the canal, 4 whereas Canadian otolaryngologists prefer 3 to 4.9 cm. 8 We did not measure this distance formally but typically position the fork about 3 to 4 cm away.

Force during Rinne testing is seldom standardised; a recent review highlighted this gap. 4 Johnston 14 compared light (400 g/3.9 N), routine (intermediate), and firm (2400 g/23.5 N) mastoid pressure with a 512 Hz fork: firm pressure raised sensitivity by 20% but lowered specificity by 6.7%. We likewise did not standardise force.

Examiner skill also affects results—sensitivity improves with experience. 4 Burkey et al 21 found that a 512 Hz Rinne performed by a fellow was 15.8% less sensitive when equivocal results were included, and 29.7% less when excluded, than tests by an experienced otologist. In our study, the examiner had >30 years' experience.

Rinne Test Accuracy Outcomes

Our study showed Rinne‐test sensitivity above 90% with 512 and 1024 Hz forks but only 46.8% with the 2048 Hz fork. Kelly et al 4 reviewed studies at 256 and 512 Hz: among 10 papers using 512 Hz, sensitivity ranged 16% to 87% (median 54%), under 75% in 8 and under 90% in all. Thus, our otosclerosis cohort achieved markedly higher sensitivity, implying fewer false negatives. Specificity was likewise high—90.7% (512 Hz) and 100% (1024 and 2048 Hz)—whereas Kelly et al 4 reported 55%‐100% (median 98.5%).

Their review suffered from heterogeneous samples, dated studies, bias risk and inconsistent ABG definitions. All 3 forks in our series produced positive predictive values >95%, but negative predictive value declined with frequency, dropping to 35.1% at 2048 Hz, indicating many false negatives at that pitch.

Results of Transition Point

The transition point of the Rinne test is the ABG at which results change from normal to abnormal in most patients. 4 Different statistical methods have been used to determine this transition point in previous studies. 7 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14

We used logistic regression to model the probability of a negative Rinne test as a function of ABG. The probability exceeded 50% at ABGs of 13.9, 18.8, and 31.3 dB for 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz tuning forks, respectively. This analysis estimates the degree of conductive hearing loss at which the Rinne test reverses, providing clinically useful guidance when audiometric confirmation is unavailable or delayed.

Kelly et al 4 investigated the transition point in 10 studies in a systematic review published in 2018. For the 512 Hz Rinne test, the transition points ranged from 17.5 to 40 dB (mean, 26 dB). However, the method used to calculate the transition points in these studies was not standardized, with 6 different methods being verified among the 10 studies evaluated.

Range of ABG Expected for the Negative Rinne at 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz

Anecdotal reports of the ABG associated with a negative Rinne at 512, 1024, and 2048 Hz vary widely. Clinically, a negative Rinne is typically observed at ABGs of 15 to 20 dB for 512 Hz, 20 to 35 dB for 512 and 1024 Hz, and >35 dB when all 3 frequencies are negative. In our study, the interquartile ranges were 13.8 to 18.8 dB for 512 Hz, 22.5 to 31.3 dB for 1024 Hz, and 32.5 to 40.6 dB for 2048 Hz.

Limitations

This retrospective two‐centre review is susceptible to incomplete data capture and selection bias, limiting external validity. As the cohort included only patients with otosclerosis, findings may not generalise to other causes of conductive hearing loss. All Rinne tests were performed by a single otologist with >30 years' experience; accuracy may differ with other examiners. Masking was not used and mastoid force was not standardised, which may have influenced results, particularly in asymmetric loss, although this reflects routine clinical practice. Similarly, the exact distance between the tuning fork and the external auditory canal during air‐conduction testing was not formally controlled, which may have introduced additional variability in sound perception. Excluding patients with equivocal Rinne results may have inflated diagnostic performance, but was necessary for the binary classification required for sensitivity, specificity, and regression‐based transition point analyses; this should be considered when interpreting accuracy estimates. Conductive hearing loss was defined using a four‐frequency averaged air–bone gap, which reflects overall conductive status and reduces variability but may obscure frequency‐specific relationships between tuning fork frequencies and audiometric thresholds. Finally, data collected between 1998 and 2019 span evolving audiometric equipment and protocols, introducing temporal bias.

Conclusion

The Rinne test proved to be an excellent screening tool for otosclerosis, particularly at 512 and 1024 Hz. Using tuning forks of 3 frequencies allows prediction, with reasonable statistical reliability, of the minimum air–bone gap associated with an abnormal (negative) result. These findings may extend to other otologic conditions and support the continued clinical relevance of the Rinne test. Well‐designed, contemporary studies aimed at standardizing the test are needed to better define its accuracy and inform future guidelines.
