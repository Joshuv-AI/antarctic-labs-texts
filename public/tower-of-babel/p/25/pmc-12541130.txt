A Case of Steroid‐Dependent Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor Pneumonitis Treated With Subcutaneous Tocilizumab
Authors: Nicole Chang, Alexa Meara, Kevin Ho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 57‐year‐old male with a history of urothelial cancer status post neoadjuvant cisplatin and gemcitabine, cystoprostatectomy and adjuvant nivolumab developed immune checkpoint inhibitor pneumonitis (ICI‐p) following nivolumab treatment. He was treated with steroids but developed recurrent ICI‐p during multiple steroid tapers. Given accumulating side effects from prolonged steroid treatment and the desire to minimise frequent visits to the clinic and infusion center, tocilizumab, which can be administered subcutaneously at home, was considered for steroid‐dependent pneumonitis. Ultimately, he received intravenous followed by subcutaneous tocilizumab, and after a few months of treatment, he was weaned off steroids with sustained resolution of ICI‐p, had improvement in steroid‐related side effects, and had no progression or recurrence of his underlying bladder cancer. To our knowledge, this is the first case of subcutaneous tocilizumab being utilised for steroid‐dependent ICI‐p.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint proteins regulate the immune system response and prevent autoimmune‐mediated tissue damage [1]. Tumour cells can evade detection by activating these proteins, leading to unregulated tumour cell growth and proliferation. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are monoclonal antibodies that block tumour cells from activating immune checkpoint proteins, restoring the immune system's ability to detect and destroy cancer cells. While ICIs are an effective cancer treatment modality, there are potential autoimmune‐mediated side effects, with immune checkpoint inhibitor pneumonitis (ICI‐p) being the most common debilitating immune‐related adverse event (iRAE) [2]. While most ICI‐p cases are steroid responsive, a subset of patients have no response (steroid‐refractory) or recurrent symptoms while weaning systemic corticosteroids (steroid‐dependent). Society guidelines outline management recommendations for steroid‐refractory ICI‐p, but no recommendations are made for steroid‐dependent ICI‐p [3, 4], which results in prolonged steroid courses that can significantly impair quality of life. Furthermore, many non‐steroid treatment options for steroid‐dependent ICI‐p are inconvenient to administer (intravenously—intravenous immunoglobulin [IVIG], infliximab) or associated with significant side effects with unknown impacts on cancer control (mycophenolate). Tocilizumab, which can be administered subcutaneously by patients at home, can be effective for ICI‐p and may also have an anti‐tumour effect [5]. However, to our knowledge, subcutaneous tocilizumab has not been evaluated in steroid‐dependent ICI‐p. Here, we present a case of a patient with steroid‐dependent ICI‐p who was treated with subcutaneous tocilizumab resulting in complete resolution of ICI‐p, cessation of corticosteroids and no progression of underlying malignancy.

Case Report

A 57‐year‐old male with a history of chronic kidney disease and urothelial cancer received neoadjuvant cisplatin and gemcitabine before cystoprostatectomy and adjuvant nivolumab. He received 10 cycles of nivolumab before developing shortness of breath and acute hypoxemic respiratory failure (new oxygen requirement of 5 L/min). His initial workup included a computed tomography angiogram of the chest that showed right upper lobe pulmonary emboli without right heart strain as well as diffuse ground glass opacities (Figure 1). He was admitted to the hospital, treated with heparin for pulmonary emboli, and received antibiotics for presumed bacterial community‐acquired pneumonia with no improvement in symptoms or oxygen requirement. Subsequently, he underwent a bronchoscopy and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) with a cell differential of 28% lymphocytes, 44% polymorphic neutrophils and 28% macrophages. Trichoderma was isolated from a respiratory fungal culture, which was thought to be a non‐pathogenic coloniser. Cytology from the BAL was negative for malignancy. Ultimately, given adequate exclusion of alternate possibilities for respiratory failure, he was diagnosed with ICI‐p related to nivolumab and started on high‐dose corticosteroids (100 mg prednisone [1 mg/kg]). His oxygen requirement improved with steroid treatment (maximum oxygen requirement during hospitalisation: 6 L/min at rest, discharge oxygen requirement after steroid initiation: 3 L/min with exertion only and none at rest), and he was discharged on a prolonged steroid taper (10 mg per week taper starting from initial 1 mg/kg dosing).

As steroids were being weaned to between 10–20 mg prednisone, he had recurrent dyspnea and escalating oxygen requirements (maximum oxygen requirement during steroid wean: 4 L/min at rest) necessitating multiple increases in prednisone over the course of several months. Repeat infectious workup, including (1,3)‐β‐D‐glucan, procalcitonin, histoplasmosis and blastomycoses antigens, was negative. During this time, he developed steroid‐induced diabetes, worsening mood disorders, generalised weakness, and orthostasis. Due to accumulating steroid side effects, non‐steroid immune suppressants were considered. Mycophenolate and IVIG were deferred given unknown impact on urothelial cancer, concerns for fluid overload with chronic kidney disease, and history of venous thromboembolism, respectively. After discussion with the patient, he was started on intravenous tocilizumab (800 mg [8 mg/kg] every 4 weeks), and after five doses, transitioned to subcutaneous tocilizumab (162 mg every 2 weeks) due to the distance from his residence to the infusion center. With the initiation of tocilizumab, prednisone was tapered off (20 mg prednisone at tocilizumab initiation, tapered to 7 mg daily after five infusions of tocilizumab, off steroids completely after six injections of subcutaneous tocilizumab), oxygen requirement improved (no oxygen requirement at rest and with exertion), inflammatory changes resolved on chest imaging (Figure 2) and steroid side effects improved. To date, he remains on subcutaneous tocilizumab (over 13 months of treatment, off systemic corticosteroids for 11 months) with no recurrence of ICI‐p and is in surveillance for urothelial cancer without evidence of cancer recurrence. Given the lack of pneumonitis recurrence and stability of cancer control, subcutaneous tocilizumab treatment was continued after steroid discontinuation and tocilizumab discontinuation will be considered 15–18 months from the last steroid treatment.

Discussion

Steroid‐dependent ICI‐p can be a source of significant patient morbidity, resulting in persistent dyspnea, prolonged steroid use, and significant steroid‐related side effects. Society guidelines have limited management recommendations for steroid‐dependent ICI‐p [3, 4], and existing research mainly focuses on the role of mycophenolate as a steroid‐sparing agent for steroid‐dependent disease [6], with limited research on the other non‐steroid therapeutic options.

As cancer patients often have clinic visits every 3–4 weeks for ongoing cancer treatment, cancer surveillance or management of treatment‐related toxicities, identifying effective non‐steroid treatment options is of paramount importance. Mycophenolate, which may increase the risk for certain malignancies, is poorly tolerated, especially in cancer patients who often have significant gastrointestinal side effects from cancer or other cancer therapies. IVIG, which is typically given daily for consecutive days, is hard to administer as an outpatient due to the need for daily infusion center visits. Tocilizumab, which has an established role in systemic autoimmune disease [7] and in reducing lung inflammation in patients with severe COVID‐19 infections [8], is an intriguing therapeutic for mitigating immune‐mediated lung inflammation from ICI treatment. Additionally, tocilizumab can be administered at home subcutaneously without a clinic visit, along with its potential anti‐tumour effect [5], is an attractive option, particularly for cancer patients with high‐frequency medical visits.

In our patient, treatment with tocilizumab decreased the frequency of clinic and infusion center visits, improved his dyspnea and oxygen requirements, and increased his quality of life through tapering and eventual discontinuation of systemic corticosteroids. Additionally, treatment with tocilizumab did not have any obvious detrimental impact on his cancer control as he remains in cancer surveillance almost 2 years from the last systemic cancer treatment. Further study with large cohorts of patients with steroid‐dependent ICI‐p is needed to evaluate whether subcutaneous tocilizumab is safe and efficacious for steroid‐dependent disease.
