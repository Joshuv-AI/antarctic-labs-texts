Evolutionary trajectory of the NPF6.3(NRT1.1/CHL1)-centered nitrate signaling regulatory network in plant roots
Authors: Ran Zhao, Jianyu Wang, Xin Yi, Jinpeng Wang, Peng Xu, Xueqian Duan, Hanying Wu, Yuannian Jiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNitrogen not only influences plant growth and development but also participates in numerous signaling pathways. Plant evolved an efficient and sophisticated nitrate signaling regulatory network (NSRN) in roots for adapting to terrestrial environments. However, our understanding of when and how a complete NSRN was established remains limited.ResultsHere, we systematically identified the Homologous genes encoding 20 key components involved in the NPF6.3(NRT1.1/CHL1)-centered NSRN and elucidated their evolutionary histories. Further analysis of six core components (NPF6, CIPK, CPK, NLP, TCP, NRT2) revealed that most clades containing functional genes appeared at the ancestral node of seed plants. Moreover, the putatively functional clade members exhibit root-preferential expression patterns in gymnosperms. Protein interaction predictions indicate that, except for TCP, the interactions among other components were already established in gymnosperms and conserved in angiosperms. These results suggest that a relatively complete NSRN was likely formed at the ancestral node of seed plants. Meanwhile, the appearance of putatively functional clade members of non-core components at different evolutionary nodes likely contributes to the increasing complexity of NSRN.ConclusionsOur study provides better understanding of the evolutionary trajectory of the NPF6.3(NRT1.1/CHL1)-centered NSRN in land plants and the adaptive mechanisms of nitrogen utilization since plant terrestrialization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07417-6.Keywords: Land plant, Nitrogen transport system, Regulatory network, Gene family, Origin and evolution

Introduction

Nitrogen plays a crucial role in plant life, influencing growth, development, and participating in various signaling pathways [1]. Early-diverging land plants primarily relied on atmospheric deposition and symbiotic nitrogen fixation for nitrogen acquisition. However, with the emergence of roots during the Devonian period, plants gradually evolved root-mediated nitrogen uptake [2, 3]. In seed plants, the well-developed root system has become the primary means of obtaining nitrogen. Thus, the establishment of efficient nitrogen uptake and transport systems in roots has been essential for plant adaptation to terrestrial environments. Nitrate is the principal form of nitrogen absorbed by most plant roots. During the evolutionary transition from algae to land plants, the nitrate transporter gene family, NPF (NITRATE TRANSPORTER 1/PEPTIDE TRANSPORTER FAMILY), underwent rapid expansion and functional divergence [4]. Concurrently, the continuous recruitment of multi-level regulatory components enhanced the complexity of nitrate signaling regulatory network (NSRN), ultimately evolving an efficient and sophisticated nitrate transport system in plant roots [5–7].

NPF6.3, also known as NRT1.1 and CHL1 (CHLORATE RESISTANT 1), is a member of the NPF family and functions as a dual-affinity transceptor that initiates and dominates the entire NSRN [8–11]. This network involves numerous transcription factors, protein kinases, and other regulatory factors. In Arabidopsis thaliana, CIPK23 and CBL1/9 regulate the phosphorylation status of NPF6.3(NRT1.1/CHL1), thereby modulating its nitrate affinity [10]. Upon nitrate perception, NPF6.3(NRT1.1/CHL1) triggers the accumulation of IP3 and Ca²⁺. The calcium signal is subsequently decoded by CPK10/30/32, which phosphorylates the master regulator NLP7, facilitating its nuclear retention and activation of nitrate responsive genes [12]. NLP7 further interacts with TCP20 [13, 14], and NRG2 [15] to coordinately regulate the expression of more downstream nitrate responsive genes. In addition to NPF6.3(NRT1.1/CHL1), NLP7 and NRT2.1 has been proposed as a nitrate sensor contributing to NSRN [16–20]. Furthermore, myriad TFs have been identified as key regulators of nitrate responsive gene expression or signal integration, including: bZIP1 [21], TGA1/4 [17, 22, 23], SPL9 [24], FIP1 [25], CPSF30 [25], LBD37/38/39 [26], HHO1/HRS1 [27, 28], ANR1 [29, 30], BT1/2 [31, 32], NAC4 [33, 34], AFB3 [33], and ARF8 [35], etc. (Fig. 1).

A summary of the NPF6.3-centered NSRN. Grayish blue boxes represent core components. Deep yellow boxes represent non-core components. Arrows denote positive regulation. Blunted lines denote negative regulation

Previous studies have demonstrated that NPF orthologs were present in chlorophytes and streptophytes with limited copy numbers, while their abundance markedly increased in land plants through multiple lineage-specific duplication events [36, 37]. Due to the complexity of terrestrial environments, NPFs have undergone neofunctionalization in substrate- or tissue-specificity, which benefits plants [4, 36, 38]. The NRT2 gene family existed throughout Archaeplastida, with the ancestral ζ whole-genome duplication (WGD) event in seed plants giving rise to two clades [37]. In A. thaliana, members of these two clades subsequently differentiated into inducible and constitutive high-affinity transport systems (iHATS and cHATS), exhibiting distinct expression patterns [39, 40]. CPK orthologs are widely distributed among plants and have undergone extensive duplications. An ancestral duplication event in charophytes led to the formation of five clades in streptophytes, and subsequent WGDs in seed plant ancestors likely further increased copy numbers within these clades [37]. The orthologs of TCP20 and NRG2, which originated in charophytes, have also undergone expansion and functional divergence in land plants [37]. While the origins and evolutionary histories of several gene families involved in nitrogen transport and signal transduction have been extensively studied, it remains unclear whether these genes have acquired the conserved functions characteristic of model plants, especially A. thaliana, in algae or early-diverging land plants, and when they were integrated into NSRN.

Here, we systematically identified orthologs of 20 key components of the NPF6.3(NRT1.1/CHL1)-centered NSRN in plant roots and elucidated their evolutionary histories across land plant evolution. Our analyses demonstrated that the three major evolutionary innovations of six core components have promoted the gradual optimization of NSRN: (1) the emergence of putatively functional clade, (2) the acquisition of root-preferential expression patterns, and (3) the establishment of protein-protein interactions. Ultimately, we speculate a relatively complete NSRN was established at the ancestral node of seed plants. Additionally, the ongoing recruitment of non-core components might further reshape the complexity of NSRN. These findings provide new insights into the molecular adaptations underlying nitrogen utilization since plant terrestrialization.

Materials and methods

Collection of genome and transcriptome data

A total of 24 plant species representing the major Lineages of land plants were selected as the subjects of our study, including three bryophytes, two lycophytes, three ferns, four gymnosperms, one Amborellales, one Nymphaeales, two magnoliids, four monocots and four eudicots. Genomic protein and CDS sequences of 24 species were obtained from the publicly available database (Table S1), and transcriptome sequences of four representative species, include Isoetes sinensis, Ginkgo biloba, Gnetum montanum, and Amborella trichopoda, were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and National Genomics Data Center (NGDC, https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa/browse/) (Table S2).

Identification of gene family

To identify the orthologs of 20 key components, we performed BLASTP analysis using well-annotated functional genes (Table S3) as queries and conducted profile hidden Markov model (HMM) searches using domains (Table S3) from the Pfam database [41] as seeds. These analyses were carried out against the genome-wide amino acid sequences in 24 species, utilizing BLASTP [42] (E-value < 10−5) and hmmsearch in HMMER v3.3 [43] (--domE 0.001) respectively. The identified putative orthologs were further manually inspected and screened using InterProScan [44].

Phylogenetic analysis of gene family

To construct the phylogeny of these gene families, amino acid sequences were initially aligned using MAFFT v7.312 [45]. The corresponding nucleotide sequences were then aligned to fit the amino acid alignment using PAL2NAL v14 [46]. Poorly aligned regions were removed with trimAL v3 [47] using the parameter “-gt 0.3”. The phylogenetic tree was constructed using maximum likelihood in RAxML v8.2.12 [48] under the “GTRGAMMA” model with 1000 bootstrap replicates, and the algal sequence was used as the outgroup. The topology of the consensus trees was visualized using iTOL [49]. Notably, in our phylogenetic analysis of gene families, we introduced a key term—“putatively functional clades”—which refers to gene clusters that exhibit significant phylogenetic grouping with experimentally validated Arabidopsis genes.

Synteny and gene duplication type analysis

To identify potential homologous gene pairs within and between genomes, an all-versus-all local BLASTP search was conducted using protein sequences, with an E-value threshold of < 10−5. The MCScanX algorithm was employed to detect syntenic blocks using default parameters [50]. We extracted all gene pairs of a certain gene family involved in NSRN from the syntenic blocks and mapped the synteny evolution patterns. Gene duplication types were classified using the duplicate_gene_classifier package within MCScanX.

Protein-Protein interaction prediction

To predict potential protein-protein interaction networks, we utilized the online STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) [51], based on known Arabidopsis homologs. This approach enabled us to execute and visualize potential interaction networks effectively.

Gene expression analysis

Raw reads of transcriptomes were preprocessed using Trimmomatic [52] to remove adaptor sequences and low-quality reads with the parameters of “PE -phred33 ILLUMINACLIP: Trimmomatic-0.36/adapters/TruSeq3-PE.fa: 2:30:10 LEADING: 20 TRAILING: 20 SLIDINGWINDOW: 4:20 MINLEN: 50”. The clean reads were then mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 with default parameters. Gene expression levels were quantified as TPM values using Stringtie [53] with options “-e -A”. Heatmaps were generated by TBtools v2.003 [54].

Results

Identification of 20 gene family members and their duplication types

To explore the evolutionary histories of 20 key components involved in NSRN (Fig. 1), we performed a comprehensive ortholog identification across 24 representative species. Our findings revealed that all 20 gene families emerged before plant terrestrialization and were widely distributed across all major land plant lineages (Table S4 and S6). By comparing copy numbers, we observed that most gene families in early-diverging land plants had fewer copies, with some even being lost entirely. For example, we found a complete absence of BT orthologs in Anthoceros angustus and Pinus tabuliformis; a loss of bZIP orthologs in both A. angustus and Selaginella moellendorffii (Table S4). In contrast, seed plants—particularly monocots and eudicots—exhibited significant gene family expansions, likely resulting from multiple WGD and whole-genome triplication (WGT) events in their respective ancestral lineages [55]. Analysis of gene duplication types revealed species- or lineage-specific family expansions driven by distinct mechanisms: Populus trichocarpa and Glycine max showed WGD-driven expansions across multiple gene families (Table S5 and S6); P. tabuliformis exhibited exceptional copy number amplification in CIPK orthologs (n = 58), with 60% (35/58) originating from tandem duplication events (Table S5 and S6).

Origin and evolution of core components involved in NSRN

To understand the establishment process of NSRN, we conducted a detailed study on the evolutionary histories of six core components constituting the backbone of nitrate signaling [7]. We identified a total of 1,181 putative NPFs across 24 representative species. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that NPFs are divided into eight subgroups, which is largely consistent with previous studies (Fig. S1) [4, 37, 38]. Among these, NPF6 subgroup holds a special position as it harbors NPF6.3 (NRT1.1/CHL1), the first molecularly characterized and most thoroughly studied plant nitrate transporter [56]. Intriguingly, although plants have evolved sophisticated NSRN, only a single nitrate sensing system has been described to date, relying on the NPF6.3 transceptor [57]. In this study, we focused on NPF6 subgroup for further exploring the origin of nitrate-related “transceptor” functions. We reconstructed the phylogenetic tree using 85 NPF6 members, which included basal orthologs from bryophytes, lycophytes, and ferns. Obvious duplication events occurred at the ancestral node of seed plants, resulting in the formation of three clades (I, II and III). Each clade includes gymnosperms, amborellales, magnoliids, monocots, and eudicots, with nymphaeales exclusively retained in clade III (Fig. 2a). Notably, AtNPF6.3 (Atha|AT1G12110.1) and two other functionally similar members—OsNPF6.5 (Osat|LOC_Os10g40600.1) from rice and ZmNPF6.6 (Zmay|GRMZM2G161459_P01) from maize—are all classified within clade III [58–60], suggesting that this may be a putatively functional clade (see methods). Additionally, synteny analysis between species showed strong collinearity among members within each clade, further implying their functional conservation (Fig. 2b). Integrating these results suggests that following duplication at the ancestral node of seed plants, functional divergence among clades may have facilitated clade III in acquiring nitrate-related “transceptor” functions.

We employed the same methods to investigate the evolutionary histories of the other five core component gene families. For CIPK, we identified 513 putative orthologs. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that CIPKs are divided into four distinct clades (I, II, III and IV) (Fig. S2). Duplication events in the ancestor of flowering plants led to the formation of two subclades (I-a and I-b) within clade I, with AtCIPK8 belonging to clade I-a. Similarly, duplication events in the ancestor of seed plants allowed clade II to further differentiate into three subclades (II-a, II-b and II-c), with AtCIPK23 belonging to clade II-a (Fig. S2). For CPK, we identified 551 putative orthologs. The phylogenetic tree showed that CPKs are divided into five clades (I, II, III, IV and V), with each clade containing all land plant lineages, except for clade III (Fig. S3). Clade IV further formed three subclade (IV-a, IV-b and IV-c) due to the obvious duplication events in the ancestor of seed plants. AtCPK10, AtCPK30 and AtCPK32 belong to clade IV-a and clade IV-c, respectively, implying that nitrate-related functions may have originated in the ancestor of seed plants (Fig. S3). Subsequent functional divergence likely led to the loss of these functions in CPKs of clade IV-b.

For NLP, we identified 127 putative orthologs, and phylogenetic analysis showed that it is monophyletic. Notably, obvious duplication events at the ancestral node of seed plants led to an increase in gene copy numbers within clades I, II, and III (Fig. S4), a finding consistent with previous research [37]. AtNLP7 and AtNLP6 belong to clade III, which may serve as a putatively functional clade playing a core regulatory role in NSRN of plant roots [61–63]. For TCP, we identified 188 putative orthologs. The phylogenetic tree revealed that duplications in the ancestor of lycophytes resulted in two clades (I and II). Of which, clade I contains all vascular plant lineages, with AtTCP20 present in clade I-c (Fig. S5). For NRT2, we identified 129 putative orthologs. The phylogenetic tree indicated that a clade-specific duplication led to the formation of two clades (I and II) in the ancestor of seed plants, with AtNRT2.1 present in clade II (Fig. S6). The emergence of putatively functional clades for most core components at the ancestral node of seed plants suggests that it may be a pivotal node in establishing the backbone of nitrate signaling.

In addition to the core components mentioned above, we also identified orthologs of other 14 components and constructed their phylogenetic trees. Phylogenetic analyses revealed that the putatively functional clades containing AtSPL9 (Fig. S7), AtCPSF30 (Fig. S8), and AtLBD37/38/39 (Fig. S9), emerged before plant terrestrialization. The putatively functional clade containing AtHHO1/HRS1 arose from the common ancestor of vascular plants (Fig. S10). The putatively functional clades containing AtCBL9 (Fig. S11), AtANR1 (Fig. S12), and AtTGA1/4 (Fig. S13) emerged in the ancestor of ferns. The putatively functional clades containing AtNRG2 (Fig. S14), AtFIP1 (Fig. S15), and AtBT1/2 (Fig. S16), arose from the ancestor of seed plants. Finally, the putatively functional clades containing AtNAC4 (Fig. S17), AtAFB3 (Fig. S18), AtbZIP1 (Fig. S19), and AtARF8 (Fig. S20), arose from the ancestor of angiosperms. These results suggest that during plant evolution, the complexification of NSRN in land plant roots may have been achieved through the stepwise recruitment of various regulatory factors at different phylogenetic nodes (Fig. 2c).

Gene expression profiles of core components involved in NSRN

Gene expression is a key prerequisite for genes to perform biological function and participate in plant-specific regulatory networks. To investigate whether the backbone of nitrate signaling was functional at the common ancestral node of seed plants, we conducted gene expression profiling analysis of three representative species. Our analysis revealed that different clades of NPF6 subgroup exhibit distinct expression patterns. In putatively functional clade III, genes such as Gmon|TnS000847209t03 and Atri|evm_27.model.AmTr_v1.0_scaffold00086.97 are widely expressed in all tissues, including roots. Conversely, members of clades I and II are not expressed in roots of these three species (Fig. 3a). Notably, CIPKs and CPKs also display similar expression patterns (Fig. 3b and c). For NLPs, all members of putatively functional clade III show root-preferential expression patterns in these three species (Fig. 3d), whereas members of clade I have low expression levels in roots. Similar expression patterns were also observed in NRT2s, members of putatively functional clade II, such as Gbil|Gb_15864, Gmon|TnS000097081t07, and Atri|evm_27.model.AmTr_v1.0_scaffold00140.55, exhibited root-specific expression patterns (Fig. 3f). The putatively functional clade of TCPs arose from the ancestor of lycophytes, but these orthologs exhibit minimal expression in roots of I. sinensis (Table S7). However, the putatively functional clade members, Gbil|Gb_23937 was widely expressed in all tissues in G. biloba, and Atri|evm_27.model.AmTr_v1.0_scaffold00016.212 showed root-preferential expression patterns in A. trichopoda (Fig. 3e). Overall, the results suggest that the backbone of nitrate signaling may have already performed specific functions in the common ancestor of seed plants.

Evolutionary history of protein-protein interaction of these core components

In A. thaliana, there are complex interactions among core components of NSRN. In order to further explore the establishment process of the backbone of nitrate signaling, we predicted protein-protein interactions (Fig. 4 and Table S8). Our analyses revealed that the interaction between NPF6s and CIPKs from putatively functional clades occurred in gymnosperms and has been conserved across all angiosperms. Notably, another three groups of interaction, NPF6s and NLPs, NLPs and CPKs, NLPs and NRT2s, also share a similar evolutionary history. Additionally, we found that the interaction between NLPs and CPKs appears to be lost in Nymphaea colorata. In contrast, the interactions between NPF6s and TCPs, as well as between NLPs and TCPs, were found to exist in A. trichopoda but specifically lost in Sorghum bicolor, Aristolochia fimbriata, and Vitis vinifera. These findings suggest that the interactions likely originated in the ancestors of angiosperms. Alternatively, it is also plausible that these interactions existed earlier but were subsequently lost in gymnosperms. In conclusion, the interactions among core component members from putatively functional clades appear to have been conserved at two key evolutionary nodes: the ancestor of seed plants and the ancestor of angiosperms. Presumably, these interactions may have facilitated the establishment of a complete regulatory network within the backbone of nitrate signaling.

Potential evolutionary trajectory of the NPF6.3-centered NSRN

Following the divergence of major lineages of land plants, we compiled information on all relevant orthologs and constructed a putative evolutionary model for the establishment of the NSRN (Fig. 5). Orthologs of the components comprising the NSRN were found to be present in all land plant species studied. Furthermore, these putatively functional clades, where the functionally verified Arabidopsis gene located, emerged in a stepwise manner. For instance, putatively functional genes of CPSF30, LBD37/38/39, and SPL9 were identified across land plants; HHO1/HRS1 and TCP20 were found in vascular plants; while ANR1, CBL1/9, and TGA1/4 originated at the ancestral node of ferns and seed plants. Notably, a large number of key genes—such as NPF6, CIPK23, CPK10/30/32, NLP6/7, NRT2, NRG2, BT1/2, and FIP1—appeared prior to the divergence of seed plants. Genes including bZIP1, NAC4, AFB3, and ARF8 emerged with the origin of angiosperms, by which time all major components of the NSRN were present.

Solid circles represent members of putatively functional clades, while hollow circles denote gene family members. A dotted line around a circle signifies expression in roots. Lines connecting the circles illustrate interaction relationships

By integrating expression data and predicted protein–protein interactions, we further investigated the potential origin of the NSRN. The results indicated that bryophytes, lycophytes, and ferns lack a complete set of the core components of NSRN. Moreover, no interaction relationships could be predicted among their corresponding orthologs, suggesting that a major evolutionary transition may have occurred around the ancestral node of seed plants. Conserved expression and potential interactions—such as between NPF6 and CIPK, NPF6 and NLP, NLP and CPK, and NLP and NRT2—were observed in both gymnosperms and angiosperms. However, interactions involving NPF6-TCP and NLP-TCP were predicted only in angiosperms, indicating increased complexity or functional specialization of the NSRN following the evolution of seed plants.

Our findings illustrate how the stepwise recruitment of key components, changes in expression patterns, and the establishment of protein interaction relationships have collectively shaped this complex regulatory network in plant roots. We acknowledge that additional phylogenetic and experimental evidence would further substantiate the model. Nonetheless, this framework offers valuable insights into the evolutionary trajectory of the NSRN.

Discussion

The establishment of the NPF6.3-centered NSRN in plant roots is an evolutionary strategy for adapting to complex terrestrial environments. In Arabidopsis, the functions of core components and their regulatory relationships have been well studied. However, when they acquired the conserved functions and participated in NSRN in roots remains to be further investigated. For NPF6, a duplication event occurred in the ancestor of seed plants, likely corresponding to the ζ-WGD event [55], resulting in the formation of three clades. Among these, putatively functional clade III contains three root-specific nitrate transporter-encoding genes (AtNPF6.3, OsNPF6.5, ZmNPF6.6) that have been confirmed to possess dual-affinity nitrate transport capabilities, regulating NSRN in plant roots. Nevertheless, clade I contains AtNPF6.4, which is specifically expressed in the parenchyma and affects polyamine transport and metabolism in shoots [64]. Clade II contains AtNPF6.2, which is specifically expressed in the petiole and plays a key role in regulating leaf nitrate homeostasis to promote leaf development [65]. Consequently, at the common ancestor of seed plants, the three clades appear to have undergone significant functional divergence in terms of both substrate- and tissue- specificity, reflecting the adaptation of duplicated genes to the increasing morphological complexity of seed plants [4, 37, 38]. Changes in expression patterns are often significant indicators of subfunctionalization and neofunctionalization following gene duplication [66]. Further analysis of gene expression patterns revealed that putatively functional clade III members of NPF6 are expressed in roots of gymnosperms, whereas other clade members are not. This suggests that putatively functional clade members of NPF6 may have played nitrogen-related roles in roots at the ancestral nodes of seed plants.

For CIPK, they emerged before plant terrestrialization and underwent duplication and diversification in the common ancestor of land plants, aligning with previous studies [37, 67]. Phylogenetic analyses suggest that putatively functional clade II-a (containing AtCIPK23) and I-a (containing AtCIPK8) are derived from duplication events of the common ancestral nodes of seed plants and angiosperms, respectively. These events may coincide with the ζ-WGD and ϵ-WGD events [55]. Currently, due to the lack of reports on the involvement of other CIPKs in NSRN, we speculate that further functional divergence may have occurred among different subclades at the common ancestral node of seed plants and angiosperms, respectively. For CPK, members of putatively functional clades IV-a and IV-c display broad expression patterns across multiple tissues in gymnosperms and angiosperms, suggesting that they may have diverse tissue-specific functions. In A. thaliana, AtCPK10 is expressed in leaves and plays a role in ABA-mediated stomatal regulation in response to drought stress [68]. AtCPK32 is expressed in various aboveground tissues, including floral organs and the shoot apex, and is involved in the autonomous regulation of flowering time [69]. Based on protein-protein interaction predictions, the interaction between CPK and NLP orthologs from putatively functional clades exists in several representative gymnosperm species. We speculate that, as NLP root-preferential expression patterns evolving, CPK may have been recruited into the NSRN, performing specific functions in roots.

For NLP, the ζ-WGD event led to the formation of three duplicated clades, consistent with previous studies [37]. Within these clades, ZmNLP6 (Zmay|GRMZM2G475305_P01) and ZmNLP8 (Zmay|GRMZM2G375675_P01), located in a putatively functional clade, have been identified as master regulators involved in the primary response to nitrate [70]. Further analysis revealed that, compared to clade I, most members of putatively functional clade III exhibit root-preferential expression patterns in three representative species from gymnosperms and angiosperms. Meanwhile, the interactions between putatively functional clade members of NLP and NPF6, CPK, and NRT2 are also predicted to exist in gymnosperms, suggesting that they may have been involved in NSRN in roots at the ancestral stage of seed plants. Similarly, for NRT2, the phylogenetic tree indicates that the ζ-WGD event resulted in the formation of two duplicated clades in the ancestor of seed plants, aligning with earlier research [37]. AtNRT2.1, AtNRT2.2, and AtNRT2.4, from putatively functional clade II, and AtNRT2.5, from clade I, have separately formed the iHATS/cHATS [39, 40], demonstrating clear functional divergence. In this study, compared to clade I, most putatively functional clade II members of NRT2 also exhibit root-preferential expression patterns in gymnosperms. This suggests that the NRT2 gene families may have undergone functional divergence at the ancestral node of seed plants. Moreover, protein-protein interaction predictions indicate that the interactions between NRT2 and NLP orthologs from putatively functional clades are widespread in seed plants, implying that root-preferential expression may have facilitated the origin of their interaction within the same tissue, thereby participating in NSRN and playing a specific role in the root.

We also acknowledge several limitations of this study and further in-depth study could help us gain a more comprehensive understanding of the evolutionary trajectory of the NSRN. First, the identification of the putatively functional clades relies mainly on phylogenetic relationships. Further functional genomic validation would provide a more comprehensive assessment of functional conservation within these clades. Second, although core components of the putatively functional clades show a root-preferential expression pattern in seed plants—hinting at a potential role in the NSRN. However, such interpretation would be strengthened by broader species sampling. Third, the current evidence regarding protein-protein interactions is based solely on computational predictions, and it might worth to perform additional experiment validations. In addition, it will be interest to understand about how sequence, domain, and spatio-temporal expression changes helped the formation of interactions and thereafter gaining functions.

Conclusion

Our analyses show that the homologs of core components of the NPF6.3-centered NSRN have ancient origins, but their functions seem to progressively specialized through root-specific expression patterns and protein-protein interactions during plant evolution. This evolutionary trajectory might be associated with the selective pressures imposed by the complex nutrient availability encountered in terrestrial environments. We speculate that the well-developed NPF6.3-centered NSRN in seed plants may represent an efficient and rapid adaptive strategy for responding to fluctuations in environmental nitrate levels, closely related to their complex plant morphology. Overall, our study proposes a potential evolutionary trajectory of the NPF6.3-centered NSRN in land plants, providing new insights into the adaptive evolutionary mechanisms of plant-specific functional regulatory networks.
