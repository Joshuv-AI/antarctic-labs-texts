Assessing diaphragm dysfunction in ICU patients with COVID-19 pneumonia: A prospective observational cohort study
Authors: Nazire Ates Ayhan, Sinem Bayrakci, Ahmet Firat, Yurdaer Bulut, Gulsah Seydaoglu, Emre Karakoc, Oya Baydar Toprak, Ezgi Ozyilmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveUltrasonography has been commonly employed during the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic to assess lung and heart conditions; however, diaphragm ultrasonography (DU) has not been as widely acknowledged. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of diaphragm dysfunction (DD) in non-intubated critically ill patients with COVID-19 pneumonia on intensive care unit (ICU) admission and to examine the correlation between DD and various prognostic factors during their ICU stay.MethodsThis prospective observational cohort study included 79 consecutive patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) over an eight-month period. DU was performed within 24 h of admission by the same ultrasonographer. Diaphragm excursion was measured, and the diaphragmatic thickening fraction (DTF) was calculated.ResultsA total of 79 patients were included in the study. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis identified a diaphragmatic thickening fraction (DTF) threshold of ≤25% for predicting ICU mortality (area under the curve [AUC] 0.87, sensitivity 85.1%, specificity 71.9%). Based on this cutoff, diaphragmatic dysfunction (DD) was present in 50.6% of patients on ICU admission and was associated with higher mortality (65.0% vs. 15.4%, p<0.001), fewer ICU-free days, and higher intubation rates. Survivors had significantly higher DTF values than non-survivors (30.7% vs. 18.8%, p<0.001). Multivariate analyses showed that DD on ICU admission was an independent predictor of mortality (HR 3.6, 95% CI 1.2–10.5, p=0.02), along with ferritin levels and the presence of septic shock.ConclusionsDD was common at ICU admission and was associated with higher intubation rates, fewer ICU-free days, and increased mortality. Early assessment of diaphragm function may help guide clinical management in non-intubated critically ill COVID-19 patients with acute respiratory failure, and a lower diaphragmatic thickening fraction may serve as a potential predictor of poor outcomes in these patients.Keywords: diaphragm ultrasonography, diaphragmatic dysfunction, diaphragmatic thickening fraction, COVID-19 pneumonia, intensive care, prognosis

1. Introduction

COVID-19 pandemic, driven by the novel Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2, has emerged as a global health crisis affecting populations worldwide. 1 The clinical presentation of COVID-19 varies widely, with cases ranging from asymptomatic to critically ill. 2 Approximately 81% of cases are classified as mild, while about 5% progress to critical conditions, including respiratory failure, septic shock, and multiple organ dysfunction. The overall case fatality rate was 2.3%, with critical cases experiencing a significantly higher rate of 49.0%. Due to variations in study design, patient demographics, and geographic factors, ICU admission rates for COVID-19 patients range from 4 % to 32 %, and mortality rates vary widely from 0.7 % to 52.4 %. 3 The morbidity and mortality associated with COVID-19 are predominantly due to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) resulting from viral pneumonia. 4

The diaphragm is the main respiratory muscle and plays a key role in ventilation. Diaphragm dysfunction (DD) is defined as the failure of the diaphragm to generate normal maximal force. Several risk factors for critical illness-associated DD have been identified, including sepsis, systemic inflammation, mechanical ventilation, and several medications. This creates a “multiple-hit mechanism” whereby the interplay of various factors results in alterations to respiratory mechanics, ultimately leading to respiratory failure. The frequency of DD in ICU patient is 60-80 % under mechanical ventilation and two times more frequent than limb weakness.5,6 Additionally, DD may preexist in critically ill patients; Demoule et al. showed that 64 % of the patients had DD on ICU admission. 7 Even preexisting or developing during follow-up, it is clearly associated with increased ICU mortality, difficult weaning, and prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation.7,8 The gold standard method for evaluating DD is the measurement of transdiaphragmatic pressure (Pdi) with magnetic or electrical stimulation. However being an invasive method with low repeatability and requiring special equipment and expertise in addition to patient cooperation, it’s not practical to use during routine clinical practice in the ICU. 9

Diaphragm ultrasound (DU) is a noninvasive and easily available tool for assessing DD that allows repeated measurements at the bedside. Although the DU does not directly measure diaphragm function, that is, Pdi, it provides a surrogate measure of diaphragm activity, which makes it a good option for Pdi measurement. It has been widely used in critically ill patients for many purposes, such as predicting weaning failure, evaluating patient–ventilator interaction, and diagnosing DD. Two measurements are used to diagnose DD with DU: diaphragm thickness (DT) measured by a linear probe and diaphragm excursion (DE) measured by a convex probe. Diaphragm excursion less than 1.1 cm during tidal breathing or a thickening fraction of diaphragm (TFdi) below 20% are valuable diagnostic measures for diaphragm weakness.10,11 However, the diagnostic value of DT, TFdi and DE significantly decreases under high positive pressure and measurements under spontaneous ventilation are known to be much more valuable to interprete. 12 Thus, there is currently no standard values in the selection of ultrasonography markers and thresholds for DD. 13

Despite the high frequency and significant impact of DD diagnosed with these measurements on prognosis, most trials have mainly focused on the effects of mechanical ventilation and critical illness on the diaphragm during ICU stay.14–18 Remarkably, the current knowledge on the frequency and impact of DD in pneumonia is extremely limited. However, it should be of particular interest owing to the fact that the diaphragm is noted for its resistance to fatigue due to its reliance on aerobic metabolism, which primarily utilizes type 1 or slow-twitch muscle fibers that are abundant in its composition. It has been reported that the interplay of hypoxemia and dyspnea, along with muscle involvement, is commonly observed in patients hospitalized with COVID-19 a few days after the onset of the illness. 19 Hypoxia hinders energy production, leading to a shift towards anaerobic metabolism, which decreases the diaphragm’s contraction strength and heightens fatigue. Based on this hypothesis, it can be proposed that COVID-19 infection leads to multisystem involvement, including the diaphragm. Moreover, early detection of DD may be crucial, as early intervention can improve prognosis. Thus, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of diaphragmatic dysfunction (DD) in non-intubated critically ill patients with COVID-19 pneumonia on ICU admission and to evaluate the relationship between DD and various prognostic factors during ICU stay.

2. Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted over an 8-month period, from May 2020, to December 2020, in two (specially designed for COVID-19 patients, similar technical features and patient profiles, and followed up by the same intensive care team) 25-bed intensive care units at a university hospital (Cukurova University, Adana, Turkey). The reporting of this study conforms to STROBE guidelines for observational studies. 20

2.1. Patients

All patients who met the inclusion criteria were consecutively enrolled during the study period. All patients who were hospitalized in the ICU due to suspected COVID-19 pneumonia and respiratory failure were screened within 24 h of admission.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)-negative patients; age less than 18 years; need for invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) and hemodynamic instability on admission; known pregnancy; preexisting diaphragm or neuromuscular disorders; home mechanical ventilator use; history of diaphragm surgery; morbid obesity; pneumothorax; subcutaneous emphysema; subphrenic abscess; patients who had already undergone tracheostomy before ICU admission; absence of adequate initial ultrasound images; and denial of informed consent.

A total of 465 patients who were suspected of having COVID-19 pneumonia and admitted to the ICU with respiratory failure were screened during the 8-month trial period. Patients with negative nasal and pharyngeal swab PCR assay findings (n=216) were excluded. Of the 249 patients who remained, 170 were eliminated because they were hemodynamically unstable (n=20), under IMV at admission (n=32), intubated during the first 24 h after ICU admission (n=38), or had technical reasons (n=80). 2.2. Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the Çukurova University Faculty of Medicine Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval number 98-42) on April 10, 2020. All patients or their legally authorized representatives provided written informed consent prior to their participation. All procedures performed in this study involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the hospital, national research committee, and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration, as revised in 2024. All patient data were de-identified to ensure that no individual could be identified in any way.

2.3. Clinical data collection

Demographic data, comorbidities evaluated using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), 17 severity of illness (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II [APACHE II]) score, 18 Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, 19 ROX index (ratio of oxygen saturation as measured by pulse oximetry SpO2/FiO2 to respiratory rate), 21 NUTRIC score, 22 physiologic data, presence of septic shock, 23 and arterial blood gas analysis results were prospectively documented upon ICU admission. Records were maintained for ICU and hospital mortality, the necessity for intubation, the requirement for mechanical ventilation (MV), the duration of MV, the number of ICU-free days, MV-free days, and the length of ICU and hospital stays.

2.4. Diaphragm assessment

Diaphragm ultrasound (DU) was performed within 24 h of ICU admission by the same intensivist with more than five years of experience in critical care ultrasonography, who was blinded to the patients’ clinical status. All measurements were conducted in spontaneously breathing patients at ICU admission; none of the assessments were performed while the patients were receiving noninvasive ventilation. At the time of measurement, the patients were on conventional oxygen therapy or high-flow nasal cannula as clinically indicated. Diaphragmatic motility was assessed in the supine position with an average inclination of 45° using a bedside B-mode ultrasound device (GE Logic e R7 Pro, USA) connected to a 4–12 MHz linear probe. For diaphragm thickening fraction (DTF) measurements, the probe was positioned at the zone of apposition between the anterior and mid-axillary lines at the 8th–10th intercostal space at the junction of the diaphragm and rib cage. The diaphragm was identified as a three-layer structure consisting of a relatively hypoechoic muscle layer bordered by two echogenic lines representing the diaphragmatic pleura and peritoneal serosa. Diaphragm thickness was measured at end-inspiration and end-expiration using M-mode ultrasonography during quiet breathing. DTF was calculated as follows: DTF = (End-inspiratory thickness – End-expiratory thickness)/End-expiratory thickness × 100. 24

Diaphragm excursion was measured using a 3.5 MHz convex probe on the right side in the supine position. The probe was placed between the midclavicular and anterior axillary lines and directed medially, cranially, and dorsally. After visualization was corrected using B-mode, the inspiratory and expiratory craniocaudal displacements of the diaphragm were recorded in M-mode during quiet breathing. The distance between maximal and minimal echogenic lines was measured on frozen images from the M mode tracing. 25

All measurements were performed three times on the right side of the diaphragm. Measurements were repeated at least once until they were consistently within 10% of the variation; the mean of the three measurements was used for analysis. All patients were followed up until ICU and hospital discharge.

2.5. Statistical analyses

Possible alternative cut-off points for DTF were evaluated using area under the curve (AUC) statistics. The prognostic ability of DD was evaluated for overall survival (OS) using both univariate and multivariate Cox regression models. OS was defined as the time from study entry to any documented clinical progression or death from any cause. The Kaplan-Meier method and Cox proportional regression model were used to estimate the mean-median survival rate and hazard ratios (HR). The log-rank test was used to compare the survival distributions between the groups. The correlation between continuous variables was analyzed using the Spearman correlation test. The chi-square test or Student’s t-test was used to compare the groups. A logistic regression model was used to evaluate the independent risk factors for mortality. The results are reported as mean±SD, median, number (n), and percentage (%). No formal sample size calculation was conducted because of the exploratory nature of this prospective study. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using the statistical package SPSS v. 22.0.

3. Results

A total of 79 patients were included in this study. The mean age was 62.5±13.7 years, and 65.8% of the patients were male. A smoking history was present in 30.4% of patients. The mean APACHE II, SOFA, NUTRIC, and CCI scores were 19.3±8.6, 5.1±3.1, 4.1±2.1, and 3.6±2.5, respectively.

The optimal DTF threshold associated with ICU mortality was determined using ROC curve analyses. The cutoff value of ≤25% yielded an AUC of 0.87, with a sensitivity 85.1% and specificity of 71.9 % (Figure 2). This threshold was used to define DD at the time of ICU admission. The prevalence of DD on ICU admission was determined to be 50.6% based on this cutoff value. Age, sex, duration of COVID-19 symptoms, vital signs (apart from heart rate), SOFA and NUTRIC scores, C-Reactive Protein (CRP), serum ferritin, and lymphocyte levels were similar between the groups; however, APACHE II and CCI Scores were higher, and the PaO2/FIO2 ratio and serum phosphorus levels were lower (p<0.05) in the DD group. 4. Discussion

The presented study showed that DD is common among critically ill COVID-19 pneumonia patients on ICU admission. Using the criteria DTF ≤25% to define DD, more than half of the patients in this study population exhibited DD upon ICU admission. Ferritin was revealed to be an independent predictor of mortality in addition to having a lower DTF. In addition, the survival time of ICU stay was significantly longer in patients without DD than in patients with DD. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explicitly investigate the prevalence of DD in individuals with viral pneumonia.

DD has been frequently investigated among critically ill patients during their ICU stay and mostly during the weaning process. DD has been reported in 60-80% of patients on mechanical ventilation, causing an increased duration of MV, prolonged weaning, and mortality.26,27 However, the frequency of DD on ICU admission and its effect on prognosis have received less attention to date. Demoule et al. demonstrated a 64 % prevalence of diaphragmatic dysfunction in a diverse cohort of general medical ICU patients using bilateral anterior magnetic phrenic nerve stimulation. 11 The same study group subsequently demonstrated that 53 % of patients exhibited DD upon the commencement of MV. 27 In another study performed in patients with severe sepsis, nerve conduction abnormality was found to be 63% within 72 hours of ICU admission. 28 The current study is substantially different from earlier trials. First, all these trials involved a mix of ICU patients. In this study, we focused on patients with severe pneumonia. Second, previous studies included both intubated and non-intubated patients. In contrast, we exclusively included non-intubated critically ill patients to investigate DTF and DE. It is generally understood that DE measurement is unreliable under MV. 29 Thus, this is the first study to explicitly examine the frequency of DD among critically ill patients with pneumonia who were not intubated.

There is relatively insufficient evidence on diaphragm function in COVID-19 patients. The first experiment, which included 27 patients, found that lower DTF (21.4%) was significantly associated with CPAP failure among patients with COVID-19 pneumonia. 30 The same group also evaluated the association between diaphragm thickness and adverse outcomes in individuals with COVID-19 pneumonia. As a result, decreased end-expiratory diaphragm thickness and lymphocyte count were identified as independent predictors of poor outcomes. 31 However, none of these investigations examined the frequency of DD in critically ill patients with COVID-19 pneumonia on ICU admission. In addition, for the first time, this study demonstrated a clear connection with mortality.

Previous research has shown that DD is a well-known risk factor for mortality and morbidity.26,32 Supinski et al. found that patients with DD had significantly greater ICU mortality (49 % vs. 7 %, p < 0.05). 14 Another study found that diaphragmatic weakness was a far better predictor of ICU mortality than the degree of organ failure, severity of lung function abnormalities, age, sex, or comorbidities. 33 The lack of a uniform definition of DD complicates comparisons across multiple trials. In this study, we determined DD using the ROC curve’s greatest sensitivity and specificity levels. This study found that DD (<25%) was an independent risk factor for ICU mortality in severely ill patients with COVID-19 pneumonia. Furthermore, the study’s findings indicated that DD is related to ICU survival time and hospital stay. Individuals without DD had longer ICU survival times and hospital stays than those with DD.

Diaphragmatic dysfunction in patients with COVID-19 pneumonia is attributed to several multifaceted mechanisms. Experimental evidence suggests that infections can impair diaphragm strength by up to 80% within the first 24 h, regardless of the underlying pathogen.34,35 Severe abdominal and thoracic infections, such as peritonitis, pancreatitis, and pneumonia, are established precursors to respiratory failure, potentially damaging the diaphragm due to anatomical proximity.36–38 In COVID-19, specifically, the interplay of oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cytokine storms (notably interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α) can trigger the diaphragm’s catabolic pathways. Beyond these molecular mechanisms, lung aeration levels have been closely linked to diaphragm contractility. 39 In addition to these clinical and pathophysiological pathways, recent post-mortem evidence from mechanically ventilated COVID-19 patients has revealed significant structural alterations, including fiber atrophy, hypertrophy, altered myosin fiber composition, and increased fibrosis. 40 These changes appear to be modulated by both the modality and duration of mechanical ventilation, suggesting that DD in this population arises from a synergistic combination of direct viral effects, systemic inflammatory cascades, and ventilator-associated factors. Clinically, this is exacerbated by the prevalence of hypotension, hypoxia, and sepsis in critically ill patients with pneumonia. Consistent with these findings, all patients in our cohort were hospitalized for hypoxemic respiratory failure, and those with DD faced a significantly higher risk of septic shock upon admission. However, diaphragmatic dysfunction and septic shock were observed concurrently at the time of ICU admission; therefore, this finding should be interpreted as an association rather than a causal relationship.

Diaphragm excursion (DE) is mostly influenced by inspiratory volume and can vary depending on the patient’s position.41,42 In a study by Barbariol et al., DE was used to define diaphragmatic dysfunction (DD) in a heterogeneous group of surgical and medical patients. They found a higher prevalence of DD in surgical patients, but when its effect on noninvasive ventilation (NIV) success was evaluated, no statistically significant difference was observed. 43 Another study reported that diaphragm thickening fraction (DTF) is a more reliable tool than DE for measuring respiratory effort and diaphragmatic contractile function, and that DE has lower sensitivity and specificity than DTF in predicting ventilator weaning success. 44 In our study, we focused on a homogeneous cohort of medical patients primarily hospitalized with respiratory failure and assessed both DTF and DE in non-intubated individuals. Consistent with the literature, although DTF was significantly higher in survivors than in non-survivors, DE was comparable between the two groups. Furthermore, as expected, APACHE II, SOFA, NUTRIC, CCI, and ROX indices, septic shock on admission, and procalcitonin levels were higher in non-survivors; however, multivariate analysis revealed that these factors were not independently associated with mortality in this cohort.

Serum ferritin was reported to be an independent predictor of severe COVID-19 in previous studies. 45 Higher ferritin levels have also been associated with cytokine storm and poor outcomes in patients with SARS-CoV-2 infection.46,47 Consistent with these findings, ferritin levels were identified as an independent predictor of mortality in the multivariate analysis of the present study.

Several limitations of this study should be considered. We did not use bilateral phrenic nerve stimulation, which is considered the gold standard for assessing diaphragm activity. However, it has already been demonstrated that DU is equally effective as diaphragm electrical activity for assessing diaphragm function. 9 Our decision to designate the DD cutoff point as DTF ≤25% is worth discussing. Several studies have reported varying figures for the definition of DD, with most reported values being close to 25%. There is no universal definition for DD. We used ROC curve analysis to establish the cutoff value for DTF associated with mortality. Another restriction is that we omitted individuals who were already on MV because we evaluated the DE and DTF simultaneously. DE measures have been reported to have certain disadvantages in patients with MV. In addition, radiological severity scoring based on chest CT findings was not systematically available for all patients, which may have limited the evaluation of the relationship between lung involvement and diaphragmatic dysfunction. Furthermore, we do not know whether the patients in the research population already had DD before enrollment; nevertheless, we do not believe that this had a substantial impact on our results because we excluded patients who already had well-known illnesses that damaged the diaphragm. Another potential restriction could be that patients with DD had higher CCI scores; however, of the comorbidities measured in the CCI, only chronic lung disease may have a major impact on the diaphragm. Thus, we did not infer that this element had a substantial effect on our results. The study data cannot be extrapolated to the full population because it only included non-intubated critically ill patients with no major comorbidities. Further research is needed to investigate this link.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this prospective observational study provides detailed information on DD in non-intubated critically ill patients with COVID-19 pneumonia admitted to the ICU. DD was common at ICU admission and may be associated with higher intubation rates, fewer ICU-free days, and increased mortality rates. A lower diaphragmatic thickening fraction may be a potential predictor of poor outcomes. These findings suggest that early assessment of diaphragm function may help guide clinical management and risk stratification in this patient population.
