Safe Proton Beam Re-irradiation With a Bioabsorbable Spacer in Pancreatic Cancer: A Case Report
Authors: Shintaro Shiba, Koichi Tokuuye, Kazuya Koizumi, Toshiyasu Kawahara, Noriko Fujiwara, Hiroki Yamaue, Shoichi Fujii, Motoko Omura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeProton beam therapy (PBT) re-irradiation for pancreatic cancer is challenging because of accumulated dose limits in surrounding gastrointestinal organs. Bioabsorbable polyglycolic acid (PGA) spacers can reduce radiation exposure to adjacent organs. This report aims to detail the safe execution of PBT re-irradiation for recurrent pancreatic body cancer adjacent to the stomach using a PGA spacer and physically verify the spacer's proton beam blocking effectiveness via auto-activation positron emission tomography (AAPET).Patients and MethodsA 77-year-old female with pancreatic body cancer experienced local residual disease 6 months after an initial PBT course (67.5 Gy (RBE)/25 fractions). To enable a second PBT, a 1-cm-thick PGA spacer was successfully placed between the stomach and the pancreas. The second PBT delivered 67.5 Gy (RBE) in 25 fractions using 2 posterior beams to avoid gastric passage. AAPET-CT was performed 3 times during the second PBT. Quantitative analysis compared the average Becquerel (Bq)/ml values in the planning target volume, the stomach (ventral to the spacer), and abdominal regions outside the field.ResultsMaximum doses to the stomach and duodenum were limited to 16.1 Gy (RBE) and 16.7 Gy (RBE), respectively. Quantitative AAPET analysis showed the average Bq/ml value in the planning target volume was significantly higher than in other regions. This visually and quantitatively confirmed the spacer's effectiveness in stopping the proton beams. Three months after the second PBT, the irradiated site was controlled, and no adverse events related to PBT or PGA spacers were observed.ConclusionIn a case of recurrent pancreatic body cancer adjacent to the stomach, safe proton beam re-irradiation was successfully performed by placing a PGA spacer. PGA spacer placement may represent an effective strategy for safely achieving PBT re-irradiation for recurrent pancreatic cancer near the gastrointestinal tract.Keywords: Proton beam therapy, Pancreatic cancer, Re-irradiation, Spacer, Auto-activation PET

Introduction

In radiotherapy for pancreatic cancer, delivering curative doses to pancreatic targets while limiting exposure to the surrounding gastrointestinal (GI) tract remains challenging. Conventional X-ray radiotherapy cannot adequately spare these organs, restricting target dose delivery. Proton beam therapy (PBT), with its Bragg peak, allows high doses to the tumor while sparing adjacent organs through distal falloff,1 improving clinical outcomes.2, 3 However, re-irradiation within the initial field is difficult because nearby healthy organs often reach their tolerance dose during the first course of PBT.

Bioabsorbable polyglycolic acid (PGA) spacers were developed to reduce radiation to healthy organs in tumors near the GI tract, and their usefulness has been demonstrated.4, 5, 6 In carbon-ion radiotherapy, auto-activation positron emission tomography (AAPET) confirmed that PGA spacers effectively stopped the carbon-ion beams at the predicted distribution.7

In this case report, we safely performed PBT re-irradiation for recurrent pancreatic body cancer adjacent to the stomach by placing a PGA spacer. AAPET also confirmed that the spacer effectively stopped the proton beams during re-irradiation.

Case presentation

Diagnosis to first PBT

A 77-year-old female was diagnosed with clinical T4N0M0 Stage III pancreatic body cancer,8 11 months before PBT (Figure 1A and, B). The pathological diagnosis was invasive ductal adenocarcinoma. The tumor was diagnosed as unresectable because its infiltration extended beyond 180 degrees into the celiac artery and superior mesenteric artery. The patient initially received gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel (GnP) for conversion surgery. After 4 months of GnP (7 months before PBT), computed tomography (CT) showed stable disease (Figure 1C). As surgery was not indicated, the regimen was switched to fluorouracil (5-FU)/leucovorin, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin (FOLFIRINOX). After 4 months of FOLFIRINOX (2 months before PBT), CT again showed stable disease (Figure 1D, E), confirming that surgical ineligibility due to tumor infiltration exceeding 180 degrees into the celiac artery and superior mesenteric artery remained unresolved, and she was referred for PBT.

The first PBT delivered 67.5 Gy (relative biological effectiveness [RBE]) in 25 fractions with gemcitabine (GEM). Details of immobilization, target delineation, dose constraints, and respiratory gating have been reported previously.9 Figure 1F shows the dose distribution. Informed consent was obtained from the patient before starting the treatment. Treatment was completed as scheduled without non-hematologic toxicities during or after PBT. FOLFIRINOX was resumed afterward.

Second PBT

A 2-Deoxy-2-[18F]fluoro-D-glucose (FDG)-PET-CT performed 6 months after the first PBT showed local residual disease (Figure 2A, B). Ovarian enlargement without abnormal FDG uptake was also noted, but an additional magnetic resonance imaging did not strongly suggest pancreatic cancer metastasis. Thus, only local residual disease was confirmed, making the patient eligible for PBT. As the second PBT was a re-irradiation, and tolerable dose limits of surrounding organs were a concern, spacer placement was planned before treatment.

Radiological images. (A) CT at local residual diagnosis (yellow arrow, tumor). (B) FDG-PET at local residual diagnosis (yellow arrow, abnormal uptake). Abbreviations: CT, computed tomography; FDG-PET, 2-deoxy-2-[18F]fluoro-D-glucose positron emission tomography.

Spacer placement surgery was performed 8 months after the first PBT. A 1-cm-thick bioabsorbable PGA spacer was shaped and positioned between the stomach and pancreas. The second PBT began 9 months after the first PBT, delivering 67.5 Gy (RBE) in 25 fractions with GEM. Figure 3A shows the dose distribution. For target delineation, the clinical target volume was the gross tumor plus a 5-mm margin, and the planning target volume (PTV) was the clinical target volume plus a 3-mm margin. Unlike the first PBT, the prophylactic area was excluded. To limit the stomach dose, 2 posterior beams were used to avoid gastric passage. Maximum doses to the stomach and duodenum were 16.1 and 16.7 Gy (RBE). Dose-volume histogram parameters are listed in Table 1. Figure 3B and C show the cumulative dose distribution. Informed consent was obtained from the patient before starting the treatment. During the second PBT, AAPET was performed 3 times to confirm that the PGA spacer stopped the proton beam.

Local residual lesions were delineated in the treatment planning CT scans for the second PBT. The treatment planning CT scans for the first and second PBT were fused based on the location of the pancreas. Dose-volume histogram analysis using the dose distribution from the first PBT revealed that the V67.5Gy(RBE) for the residual tumor was 36.5%, V60 Gy(RBE) was 67.5%, and V50 Gy(RBE) was 93.2%. The dose in the tumor region adjacent to the stomach might be insufficient.

Treatment was completed without PBT-related non-hematologic toxicities. However, ovarian enlargement progressed during therapy. Bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy 1 week after PBT confirmed ovarian metastasis and peritoneal dissemination. Consequently, FOLFIRINOX was discontinued, and GnP was initiated 3 weeks after PBT.

Three months after the second PBT, CT and FDG-PET-CT showed progression with worsening peritoneal dissemination and pancreatic tail and lung metastases, although the irradiated sites remained controlled (Figure 4A, B). Chemotherapy was switched from GnP to liposomal irinotecan plus 5-FU/leucovorin (NAPOLI). NAPOLI was administered for 2 months; however, the treatment was discontinued at the patient's request, and follow-up was discontinued. No adverse events related to PBT or PGA spacers were observed.

AAPET

A clinical PET-CT scanner (Biograph Horizon; Siemens Healthineers, Bayern, Germany) was used to assess positron emitter distribution after PBT. AAPET-CT was performed on the 5th, 15th, and 24th fractions of the 25-fraction course. After each irradiation, the patient was transferred to a nearby PET room, positioned supine, and scanned 10 minutes after PBT. PET and CT images were obtained simultaneously and fused with a coordinate-matching technique to compare with the treatment plan dose distributions. Average and maximum Becquerel (Bq)/ml values were measured in the PTV, PGA spacer, stomach (ventral to the spacer), and right and left abdomen (outside the field). Figure 5A illustrates these regions. Bq/ml values were compared with t tests, with significance defined as P < .05.

(A) CT contours for Bq/ml calculations: PTV (blue), PGA spacer (pink), stomach (yellow, ventral to spacer), right abdomen (purple), and left abdomen (orange). (B) AAPET images at the 5th fraction. The positron-emitting region did not extend beyond the spacer (pink outline). Abbreviations: AAPET, auto-activation positron emission tomography; CT, computed tomography; PGA, polyglycolic acid; PTV, planning target volume.

Figure 5B shows the AAPET-CT results. The average and maximum Bq/ml values in the PTV, PGA spacer, stomach, right abdomen, and left abdomen were 270.1 and 736.7, 128.9 and 584.8, 10.4 and 119.0, 13.9 and 143.5, and 12.0 and 95.0, respectively. Values in the PTV were significantly higher than those in the stomach and abdominal regions (each P < .05). Average Bq/ml values did not differ significantly between the stomach and either abdominal side (P = .29 and .57). Maximum Bq/ml was significantly lower in the stomach than in the right abdomen (P < .05) but higher than in the left abdomen (P < .05). These AAPET findings confirm that the PGA spacer effectively stopped proton beams.

Discussion

In this case of recurrent pancreatic body cancer after PBT, placement of a PGA spacer enabled safe re-irradiation despite proximity to the GI tract. AAPET further confirmed that the spacer effectively stopped the proton beams. Although follow-up was short, no PBT-related adverse events occurred. Peritoneal dissemination and multiple metastases developed after the second PBT; while the effect of proton beam re-irradiation on overall prognosis is unclear, we believe optimal management choices were made at each stage.

Reports of pancreatic cancer re-irradiation using carbon-ion radiotherapy show favorable outcomes,10 including a 1-year local control rate of 67% and overall survival of 48%. However, eligibility requires tumors to be ≥3 mm from the GI tract. In contrast, this case demonstrates that when tumors abut the GI tract, safe re-irradiation can still be achieved using a spacer. Thus, spacer placement should be considered for re-irradiation of tumors near the GI tract. Moreover, although 8 months had elapsed between the initial PBT and spacer placement in this case, no adhesions were observed, and surgery was completed without complications.

AAPET confirmed that the spacer effectively stopped the proton beams. Quantitative analysis showed average Bq/ml values in the stomach were comparable to those in abdominal regions outside the field, and maximum stomach Bq/ml values lay between those of the right and left abdomen. Additionally, AAPET signal intensity depends on the cross-section of the nuclear reaction. Therefore, the proton energy reaches its minimum at the end of the beam path (near the Bragg peak), where the proton energy falls below the nuclear reaction threshold. In this case, we confirmed low AAPET signals within the spacer region. This physically indicates that the high-dose region (Bragg peak) of the proton beam is either stopped within the spacer or appropriately controlled just before the spacer, preventing high-energy protons capable of inducing nuclear reactions from reaching critical organs at risk, such as the GI tract located behind the spacer. These findings confirm spacer effectiveness. Although Bq/ml values cannot be directly converted to doses, they provide evidence of proton beam stoppage consistent with dose distribution. In contrast, PGA decomposition into carbon dioxide may reduce spacer effectiveness. For verification, when PBT and PET/CT are available nearby, AAPET should be considered.

Recent reports have demonstrated that endoscopic injection and placement of absorbable hydrogel spacers into the pancreaticoduodenal groove in patients with pancreatic head cancer results in an average space creation of 7.7 mm.11, 12 The PGA spacer in this report also successfully created a space; thus, both PGA and hydrogel spacers can be considered potential treatment strategies for pancreatic cancer. The advantages and limitations of both methods are as follows. While the PGA spacer has the disadvantage of being invasive due to placement via open surgery, it allows for secure placement by fixing the spacer to surrounding tissues. In contrast, hydrogel spacer placement is endoscopic and less invasive. However, while not reported in previous studies, a potential risk of hydrogel leakage into the abdominal cavity exists, which could compromise secure placement. Furthermore, all reported cases involved injection and placement into the pancreaticoduodenal groove in patients with pancreatic head cancer; thus, whether it is suitable for pancreatic body or tail cancer remains unclear. Ideally, both spacers should be implantable based on the case.

Conclusion

In a case of recurrent pancreatic body cancer adjacent to the stomach, safe proton beam re-irradiation was successfully performed by placing a PGA spacer and confirmed its effectiveness in stopping proton beams using confirmed using AAPET. This case suggests that re-irradiation using spacers may be a promising option; however, establishing its safety and efficacy warrants further long-term follow-up and accumulation of cases.
