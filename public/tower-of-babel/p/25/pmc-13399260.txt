Comparison of efficacy of ultrasound-guided obturator nerve block versus pericapsular nerve group block in preventing adductor jerk in patients undergoing transurethral resection of postero-lateral bladder mass under spinal anaesthesia: A randomised, single-blinded, controlled study
Authors: Swati Vijapurkar, Subrata K Singha, Jakkireddy Sravani, Gade Sandeep
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:Adductor jerk during transurethral resection of bladder tumour (TURBT) is a common complication caused by obturator nerve stimulation, particularly in tumours located on the lateral or posterolateral bladder wall. Obturator nerve block (ONB) is widely used to prevent this complication. The pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block is a newer regional technique that may also reduce adductor jerk by blocking branches of the obturator and accessory obturator nerves. This study compared the efficacy of ultrasound-guided PENG block and ONB in preventing adductor jerk during TURBT.Methods:This prospective, single-blinded, randomised study included patients undergoing TURBT for posterolateral bladder tumours under spinal anaesthesia. Patients received either ultrasound-guided ONB or PENG block. The primary outcome was the prevention of adductor jerk. Secondary outcomes included conversion to general anaesthesia, bladder perforation, postoperative pain scores, and quadriceps muscle strength.Results:Sixty-two patients were analysed, with 31 patients in each group. The incidence of adductor jerk was similar between groups using Fisher’s exact test (P = 1.000). Numerical Rating Scale scores at 6 hours were significantly lower in the PENG block group (median: 3.00 [2.00–3.00]) compared to the ONB group (median: 4.00 [3.00–4.00]), and this difference was statistically significant (Mann–Whitney U = 224, P < 0.001). Quadriceps muscle strength at 6 hours was comparable between groups with a median score of 5.00 in both groups, and the difference was not statistically significant (Mann–Whitney U = 418, P = 0.130). No bladder perforation was observed in either group.Conclusion:Ultrasound-guided PENG block is comparable to ONB in preventing adductor jerk during TURBT and provides better postoperative analgesia.

INTRODUCTION

Transurethral resection of bladder tumour (TURBT) is a common procedure used to treat cancer of the bladder. These tumours occur in the elderly population. Every measure should be taken to avoid serious complications from both anaesthesia and surgery in this population.[1] The surgery can be performed under spinal anaesthesia or general anaesthesia, but spinal anaesthesia offers many advantages, such as technical ease of performing the procedure, reduced risk of bleeding, and early recognition of bladder perforation.[2] The obturator nerve originates from the lumbar plexus and follows the lateral wall of the pelvis along the psoas muscle up to the obturator canal. Resection of a mass in the posterolateral region of the bladder directly stimulates the obturator nerve, leading to adductor jerk despite adequate motor blockade with spinal anaesthesia.[34] Adductor jerk is a prominent issue for anaesthesiologists and urologists because of its complications, like bladder rupture or injury secondary to adductor muscle contraction (obturator reflex) during TURBT.

Obturator nerve block (ONB) is a regional technique that is frequently employed as an adjunct to spinal anaesthesia to prevent adductor jerk, thereby improving surgical safety outcomes and avoiding conversion to general anaesthesia.[5] Although ONB is widely used, reported success rates of around 78% indicate a clinically relevant failure rate of approximately 22%, highlighting the need for alternative or adjunctive techniques to effectively prevent the obturator reflex.[6]

Pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block is a regional technique that targets the femoral, accessory obturator, and obturator nerves between the anterior inferior iliac spine and the iliopubic eminence. It is routinely used for postoperative analgesia following hip surgery. The evidence regarding the use of PENG block in preventing adductor spasm in TURBT surgery is sparse.

This study aimed to compare the efficacy of PENG block and ONB in preventing the adductor jerk in patients undergoing transurethral resection of posterolateral mass in the bladder.

METHODS

This single-centre, single blind, randomised trial was conducted between November 2025 and February 2026 after approval from the institute ethics committee (AIIMSRPR/IEC/2025/2087) and Clinical Trials Registry–India (CTRI/2025/10/096511). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki[7] and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment in the study for research and educational purposes.

Male and female patients aged between 18 and 75 years, belonging to the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I-III,[8] and scheduled to undergo transurethral resection of a posterolateral bladder mass were included in the study. Patients who refused to participate or had a known allergy to the study drug, a deranged coagulation profile or a platelet count of less than 80,000/mm3, infection at the site of block placement, sepsis, bladder mass not located in the posterolateral region, or any other contraindication to spinal anaesthesia were excluded from the study.

The primary outcome was the prevention of adductor jerk. Secondary outcomes included conversion to general anaesthesia, bladder perforation, postoperative pain scores, and quadriceps muscle strength.

Using data from José R Soberón et al.,[9] the sample size was calculated using the formula:

N = 2(Z1–α/2 + Z1–β)² p̄(1 − p̄)/(p1 − p2)²

where N = required sample size; Z1–α/2 = standard normal deviate corresponding to the desired level of significance (for α = 0.05, Z1–α/2 = 1.96); Z1–β = standard normal deviate corresponding to the desired statistical power (for 80% power, Z1–β = 0.84); p1 = proportion in Group 1 (4.2%); p2 = proportion in Group 2 (20.8%); p̄ = pooled proportion, calculated as (p1 + p2)/2 = 0.125; α = Type I error rate (5%); β = Type II error rate (20%).

Using the proportions derived from the reference study (p1 = 4.2%, p2 = 20.8% and p̄ = 0.125), with an alpha error of 5% and a study power of 80% (β = 0.20), corresponding to a Zβ value of 0.84, the calculated sample size was 62.3. This was rounded up to 63 participants, with approximately equal allocation between the two groups (31 participants per group).

Guidelines issued by the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials[10] were followed for the conduct of this study [Figure 1]. Computer-generated randomisation was performed by the investigator who was not involved in patient care, intra- or postoperatively using a block randomisation technique to allocate the participants to two groups, that is, group O and group P. To reduce the selection bias, random block sizes were used, and the investigator was kept blind to the size of each block. The surgeon was also blinded to the group allocation.

Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) flow chart

Group allocation was concealed in a sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelope before the study began, which the patient picked up in the preoperative area and the anaesthesiologist opened in the operating room (OR) before performing the block. Thus, the patients were blinded to group allocation.

The patients were assessed for medical and surgical history, general and systemic examination, airway examination, and investigations such as complete blood picture, renal function test, liver function test, and coagulation profile.

On the day of surgery, in the preoperative area, baseline vitals such as heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) were noted. The patient was then taken inside the OR, and standard ASA monitors were connected. After noting down the baseline vitals, spinal anaesthesia was given, and the level of the sensory and motor blockade was noted. The onset of sensory blockade was assessed by the appearance of tingling and numbness sensation, and the maximum level of sensory blockade was assessed using pin prick test. The motor blockade was assessed using the Modified Bromage scale.

After achieving adequate sensory and motor blockade, under all aseptic precautions, group P received an ultrasound guided PENG block with 20 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine and group O received an ultrasound-guided ONB with 20 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine.

Intraoperatively, the presence of adductor jerk was noted by the surgeon and the anaesthesiologist. The jerk was categorised into mild resulting in slight twitching or brief interruption of the surgical field. The risk of perforation is present but low as the surgeon pauses to reassess or adjust the electrical current. However, the severe jerk causes forceful adduction and external rotation of the thigh. This violent spasm significantly increases the risk of bladder perforation as the sudden movement drastically alters the position of the resectoscope relative to the bladder wall. The patient was followed up for 6 hours postoperatively, and quadriceps muscle strength was assessed using Medical Research Council (MRC) grading at 6 hours postoperatively and was documented. Complications like perforation of the bladder, conversion to general anaesthesia, and falls due to quadriceps weakness were noted. Any adverse event before, during, and after the procedure was noted and treated as per institutional protocol. We defined the occurrence of adductor jerk as block failure. However, conversion to general anaesthesia was performed only when the adductor jerk was severe enough to interfere with surgical safety or continuation of the procedure. Postoperative pain was assessed in the postoperative period at 0 h, 2 h, 4 h, and 6 h by the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) method.

Statistical analysis was performed using Jamovi software version 2.7.15. Continuous variables were summarised using mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data and median with interquartile range (25th–75th percentile) and range (minimum–maximum) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage.

Since most of the variables did not follow a normal distribution (P < 0.05), non-parametric tests were used for between-group comparisons. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables between the PENG block group and the ONB group. Categorical variables such as gender, adductor jerk, conversion to general anaesthesia, and bladder perforation were compared using Fisher’s exact test. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

RESULTS

A total of 62 participants were included in the study, with 31 participants in the PENG block group and 31 participants in the ONB group. The demographic parameters like age, weight, and height were comparable between both the groups. The difference in gender distribution between the two groups was not statistically significant (Chi-square test, χ² = 0.00, df = 1, P = 1.000), indicating that both groups were comparable in terms of gender distribution [Table 1].

In group P, one patient exhibited quadriceps weakness of MRC grade 3 at 6 hours, whereas no patients in group O demonstrated grade 3 weakness. Additionally, five patients in group P had quadriceps weakness of MRC grade 4, compared to two patients in group O with grade 4 weakness. However, quadriceps muscle strength at 6 hours was similar, with a median score of 5.00 in both groups, and the difference was not statistically significant (Mann–Whitney U = 418, P = 0.130).

Conversion to general anaesthesia was required in only 1 participant (3.2%) in each group as the jerk was deemed to be severe by the operating team. The difference was not statistically significant (P = 1.000), indicating comparable procedural success rates between the PENG block and ONB groups. Bladder perforation was not observed in any participant in either group; statistical comparison was not applicable.

DISCUSSION

This randomised controlled trial was conducted to compare the efficacy of ultrasound-guided PENG block with ONB for the prevention of adductor jerk during TURBT. The primary outcome of this study was the prevention of adductor jerk during TURBT. The ONB directly targets the obturator nerve (derived from the L2-L4 lumbar plexus) as the nerve exits the pelvis via the obturator canal. Injecting local anaesthetic in the interfascial plane between the pectineus and obturator externus muscles blocks the motor and sensory fibres. On the other hand, the PENG block, an interfascial block, targets the anatomical space between the psoas tendon anteriorly and the superior pubic ramus posteriorly to block the articular and motor branches of the obturator nerve. The PENG block provides easily identifiable ultrasound landmarks and requires a single-injection technique, making it technically simpler to perform compared to the ONB. The application of PENG block for the prevention of adductor jerk during TURBT is an application of the block which has been explored in the current study.

No statistically significant difference in the incidence of adductor jerk between the PENG block and ONB groups (9.7% vs 9.7%, Fisher’s exact test, P = 1.000) was found in the present study, suggesting that ultrasound-guided PENG block is comparable to ONB for preventing adductor jerk during TURBT.

TURBT is a surgical procedure performed for the diagnosis, staging, and treatment of bladder tumours.[4] TURBT is not without risk, one of the most serious of which is perforation of the bladder wall by the resectoscope loop.[11] Perforation of the bladder in the lateral wall is because of the sudden nerve stimulation by the electrocautery and mechanical jolt of the resectoscope within the bladder. The sudden movement during tumour dissection in the vicinity of the lateral wall leads to perforation. The obturator nerve running close to the lateral wall of the bladder may be stimulated during TURBT, which may result in obturator nerve reflex and unpredictable movement of the ipsilateral thigh, resulting in a powerful adductor jerk of the leg known as an “obturator jerk”. Therefore, appropriate anaesthesia should be provided for adequate surgical conditions and complete resection during TURBT. Spinal anaesthesia alone does not prevent the obturator nerve reflex, requiring an additional nerve block.[12]

ONB is an established regional technique used alongside spinal anaesthesia to prevent the adductor jerk during TURBT.[21314] Limited literature is available for the PENG block in preventing adductor jerk in TURBT.[15] Because of the close proximity of the obturator nerve to the inferomedial acetabulum, the local anaesthetic injected during a PENG block spreads via the fascial planes to selectively block the articular and motor branches of the obturator nerve (as well as the accessory obturator and femoral nerves).[1617] The block is typically used to provide analgesia following surgeries of the hip or thigh, such as acetabular fractures, femoral neck or mid-shaft fractures, hip replacement, hip arthroscopy, medial thigh lesions, knee surgery, and vascular operations, such as stripping to target several dermatomes.[18]

In a study, the incidence of adductor jerk in TURBT reduced from 46% to 22% when the ONB was used.[5] In our study, adductor jerk was found in 3 of the 31 patients (9.7%) in group O, which was similar (9.7%) in group P, suggesting that PENG block is comparable to ONB in preventing adductor jerk in TURBT.

Quadriceps muscle strength at 6 hours was similar between the groups in the present study, with a median score of 5.00 (5.00–5.00) in both groups, and the difference was not statistically significant (Mann–Whitney U = 418, P = 0.130).

Quadriceps weakness following the PENG block is attributed to the unintended spread of local anaesthetic to the femoral nerve. A study by Huang Y et al.[19] reported the occurrence of quadriceps weakness with 20 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine and therefore recommended reducing the volume to 10 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine to minimise motor blockade. In their randomised controlled trial, Lin et al.[20] demonstrated that the PENG block preserved motor function and quadriceps strength, thereby promoting postoperative mobilisation and improving recovery quality. Although a small number of patients in the current study experienced quadriceps weakness, no falls were reported in either group.

The reduction in pain score at 6 hours was significantly greater in the PENG block group compared to the ONB group in the present study. This may be attributed to the more proximal spread of the PENG block and the coverage of articular branches of other nerves supplying the anterior hip. By providing more comprehensive coverage of the articular branches[21] and reducing somatic pelvic pain as well as associated muscle jerk, the PENG block likely contributes to the lower 6-hour NRS scores observed when compared with the ONB alone.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the incidence of bladder perforation decreases significantly from 53% to 11% with ONB administration.[5] However, in our study, no cases of bladder perforation were observed in either group because of the meticulous dissection and caution exercised by the surgeon.

There are no well-defined criteria for conversion to general anaesthesia. Although adductor jerk was observed in some patients in the current study, only those with clinically significant movement requiring interruption of surgery were converted to general anaesthesia. Based on this criterion, only one participant in each group required conversion to general anaesthesia in our study; however, there are not much data/established criteria for the scenarios considered to be converted to general anaesthesia.

The present study has certain limitations. It was conducted at a single centre with a relatively small sample size. Block success was evaluated solely by the presence or absence of the adductor jerk, whereas sensory blockade could not be assessed because spinal anaesthesia was in use. A comprehensive evaluation of both sensory and motor blockade would have provided better insight into the onset time and duration of the block. The difference in mean age between the groups (PENG group being younger) is not statistically significant but may be clinically relevant given the small sample size. Older age may be associated with differences in tumour characteristics and surgical complexity, potentially influencing intraoperative outcomes and acting as a confounding factor.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, no statistically significant difference in adductor jerk incidence was observed between PENG and ONB in this randomised study. PENG block patients demonstrated lower postoperative pain scores at 6 hours and earlier mobilisation without evidence of clinically significant quadriceps weakness. Larger adequately powered multicentric trials are needed to confirm these findings.
