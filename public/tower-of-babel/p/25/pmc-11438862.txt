Nanopore signal deviations from pseudouridine modifications in RNA are sequence-specific: quantification requires dedicated synthetic controls
Authors: Amr Makhamreh, Sepideh Tavakoli, Ali Fallahi, Xinqi Kang, Howard Gamper, Mohammad Nabizadehmashhadtoroghi, Miten Jain, Ya-Ming Hou, Sara H Rouhanifard, Meni Wanunu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemical modifications to mRNA respond dynamically to environmental cues and are important modulators of gene expression. Nanopore direct RNA sequencing has been applied for assessing the presence of pseudouridine (ψ) modifications through basecalling errors and signal analysis. These approaches strongly depend on the sequence context around the modification, and the occupancies derived from these measurements are not quantitative. In this work, we combine direct RNA sequencing of synthetic RNAs bearing site-specific modifications and supervised machine learning models (ModQuant) to achieve near-analytical, site-specific ψ quantification. Our models demonstrate that the ionic current signal features important for accurate ψ classification are sequence dependent and encompass information extending beyond n + 2 and n − 2 nucleotides from the ψ site. This is contradictory to current models, which assume that accurate ψ classification can be achieved with signal information confined to the 5-nucleotide k-mer window (n + 2 and n − 2 nucleotides from the ψ site). We applied our models to quantitatively profile ψ occupancy in five mRNA sites in datasets from seven human cell lines, demonstrating conserved and variable sites. Our study motivates a wider pipeline that uses ground-truth RNA control sets with site-specific modifications for quantitative profiling of RNA modifications. The ModQuant pipeline and guide are freely available at https://github.com/wanunulab/ModQuant.Subject terms: Nanopores, Nanometrology

Introduction

The diverse landscape of chemical modifications in cellular RNA implies a myriad of functional roles. For example, the heavily modified transfer-RNAs (tRNAs) and ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs)1 have functions that heavily depend on chemical modifications in their sequences. Interrogation of these highly expressed RNAs has revealed over 100 different types of RNA modifications, characterized using analytical tools such as mass spectrometry2 and thin-layer chromatography3. As sequencing technologies have evolved into practical use, many of these modifications have also been identified in messenger RNAs, for example inosine4, N6-methyladenine (m6A)5,6 and pseudouridine (ψ)7,8. Although its presence is sparse in mRNAs, accounting for only 0.2–0.6% of total uridines9, it has been shown to extend mRNA half-life10 and enhance translational efficiency11. During the COVID-19 pandemic, ψ gained significant attention due to the incorporation of its methylated analog, N1-methylpseudouridine, in the Moderna12 and Pfizer13 mRNA vaccines for SARS CoV-2. Tools for high-confidence, transcriptome-wide identification of RNA modifications such as ψ have been limited due to a lack of chemical specificity and the absence of proper 'gold-standard’ controls for accurate benchmarking. While chemical labeling methods such as carbodiimide N-cyclohexyl-N′-(2-morpholinoethyl)carbodiimide CMC7–9, bisulfite14,15, and hydrazine16 can identify modification sites when coupled with next-generation sequencing (NGS), these techniques cannot quantify fractional occupancy due to cDNA amplification bias and incomplete chemical labeling, resulting in inconsistency among methods and reduced confidence in calls17. New technologies are being developed that employ novel enzymes and/or sensors. For example, mutational errors during reverse transcription of RNA by Marathon RT has been used to reveal RNA modifications18, although this approach does not reliably detect ψ modifications. Recently, the four canonical ribonucleoside monophosphates and various base modifications were distinguished from one another by reversibly linking them within the lumen of a boronate-modified MspA nanopore19. However, this method must be combined with a controlled digestion-then-detection approach to recover the RNA sequence.

In direct RNA nanopore sequencing (DRS)20 using Oxford Nanopore Technologies’ (ONT) R9 platform, an RNA strand is threaded through a nanopore in a stepwise manner, while changes in the ionic current through the pore provides information about the sequence of nucleotides, or k-mers, located within the most sensitive region of the at any given point. For ONT’s R9 nanopore, the measured signal is primarily influenced by the 5-nucleotide k-mer within the pore’s constriction21,22. Deviations in the expected current signal and/or base identity from the canonical sequence enable the detection of RNA modifications. However, detecting modifications using nanopores is not quantitative because the signals are too “blurry” to clearly identify one modification in a k-mer with high confidence. We and others have recently shown that current signal anomalies produce systematic base-calling errors at or near a ψ site21,23–28 (Supplemental Fig. S1). In our method, Mod-p ID27, we compare the base-calling error against an unmodified human transcriptome to identify putative pseudouridine sites29. These methods are successful in providing a differential analysis30,31, however, are unable to provide absolute quantification of occupancy. Prediction models have been developed to improve ψ identification by leveraging features such as deviations in the expected ionic current, systematic base mismatches, changes in base quality score, and insertion/deletion rates21,24,25,28,32. However, the samples used to train these prediction models contain proximal modifications, which can introduce undesirable noise and reduce accuracies when training a k-mer-specific model with the ψ modification as the central base. Further, these proximal modifications are not a good representation of cellular mRNAs, which contain sparsely-modified RNA nucleobases27. In another approach, RNA constructs prepared using in vitro transcription (IVT) bearing all combinations of ψ-containing k-mers have been used to generate nanopore-based training data for ψ modifications24,33. While this is a practical way to cover a variety of k-mers, this U to ψ substitution IVT method particularly fails to capture situations where there are multiple U sites in the same k-mer (48% of all U-bearing k-mers). Additionally, these controls do not account for the sequences adjacent to the k-mers, which also contribute to the signal at the k-mer of interest (Supplemental Fig. S2–4).

We recently found that the frequency of U-to-C mismatch errors in HeLa mRNA is highest in k-mer sequences that comprise more than one canonical U, consistent with the motifs for TRUB1 and PUS7 which are the dominant ψ synthases acting on human mRNAs27 (GUUCN and UGUAG motifs, respectively). This underscores the need for RNA molecular standards with ψ and U on the same k-mer.

To demonstrate nanopore-based ψ quantification in areas that include U-rich sequence contexts, we recently produced five positive control synthetic mRNA standards by splint ligation of a synthetic ψ-modified RNA with upstream and downstream transcripts, each matching the sequence of a specific native mRNA transcript 34. Each construct comprises a k-mer with canonical U’s adjacent to ψ, which are often found in the transcriptome17. While these controls are expensive and laborious to generate, we sought to answer the question of whether training a classification model that captures the long-range sequence context improves ψ detection via DRS.

Here, we show that DRS of these positive control standards that have a ψ modification and the negative control standards that contain a U at the identical sites has provided us with reads that could be used to train a supervised machine learning (ML) model for each site for site-specific quantification of ψ. Interestingly, we found that features important for ψ classification vary among our constructs. This suggests that for every ψ-bearing transcript within the transcriptome, the synthetic control should replicate the sequence context surrounding ψ within a particular range for accurate transcriptome-wide analysis. We characterized the performance of these ML models and subsequently applied them to quantify the ψ occupancy for these sites across seven human cell lines, demonstrating cell type specific, variable occupancy.

Results

Supervised ML training on ψ-modified synthetic transcripts

We previously designed synthetic RNA standards that are identical in sequence to the native mRNA adjacent to putative ψ-sites27,34. Given the high costs of manufacturing synthetic RNA standards, and as a proof of concept, we chose to focus here on five sites, two of which [MCM5 (Chr22: 35424407, UGUAG) and PSMB2 (Chr1: 35603333, GUUCG)] were validated by CeU-seq9, ψ-seq8, RBS-seq14, BID-seq15, and PRAISE35 while the other three [MRPS14 (Chr1: 175014468, ACUUA), PRPSAP1 (Chr17: 76311411, GAUUG), and RNF7 (Chr3: 141745447, UUUUA)] were believed to bear a putative U modification based on high U-to-C mismatch errors during nanopore sequencing of HeLa mRNA27. To identify the critical features for training accurate ML models for site-specific ψ prediction and quantification in mRNAs, we compiled a comprehensive set of parameters (45 features in total) that included both basecaller and signal information to fit each ML model. We used the “resquiggling” eventalign module in nanopolish22 to align the ionic current to the base calls, and calculated a variety of statistical parameters from the signals of all five k-mers containing a ψ or U passing through the pore reader in our synthetic standards (Fig. 1B). In total, we extracted 30 signal features from each read in the modified and unmodified synthetic datasets that passed the necessary filtration stages (Fig. 1C). These features included dwell time and five statistical parameters (mean, standard deviation, first quartile, second quartile, and third quartile) computed from the recorded signal of all k-mer frames bearing a ψ or U at positions − 2, − 1, 0, + 1, + 2, moving in a 3′ to 5′ direction through the pore constriction (Fig. 1B). The read coverage and mismatch rates for all synthetic standards and native mRNAs used in this study are shown in Supplemental Tables S1–5.

Base calls and deletions, as well as quality scores within a five-nucleotide window spanning base positions − 2 to + 2 relative to the location of ψ or U at index 0 (moving in a 5’ to 3’ direction) served as input features. Insertions were included into the feature space by accounting for the frequency of each inserted base and the total number of insertions observed within the k-mer window. In total, we extracted 15 basecalling features from each filter-passed read. Users can define the size of the feature extraction window, allowing for larger models to be trained with signal and basecalling features located farther upstream and downstream from the modified site.

Selecting a supervised ML classifier for accurate ψ classification

We prepared labeled datasets of features from ψ-modified and unmodified synthetic reads for each synthetic construct and compared the ψ-classification accuracy of five different supervised ML classifiers for each synthetic control: logistic regression (LR), gradient boosting (GBC), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), random forest (RFC), and support vector machine (SVM). We used stratified fivefold cross validation to ensure that all reads in the dataset were equally sampled into the training and testing sets. Therefore, we trained each model using 80% of the data (the training set) and then evaluated its performance using the remaining 20% of the data (the testing set), which was kept hidden from the model during training. To get a general assessment of each classifier, we repeated this process five times, generating a total of 25 unique models (see “Methods”) for each synthetic dataset.

To determine which of the five ML classifiers had the highest sensitivity and specificity for each synthetic construct, we trained each model for each synthetic control and evaluated the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) and its associated AUC using the testing datasets (Fig. 2A–E). The ROC was obtained by sweeping the call threshold on the probabilistic output of the models. For the PSMB2, PRPSAP1, and MCM5 synthetic constructs, an AUC of 0.95 or higher was observed for each classifier (Fig. 2A–C). Similar results were seen with MRPS14, except that the AUC for LR and KNN was 0.92 (Fig. 2D, left). For RNF7, the AUC for all five classifiers was lower, ranging from 0.92 in GBC and RFC to 0.86 in KNN (Fig. 2E, left). Among the five classifiers, RFC and GBC consistently generated the highest AUC results for all five synthetic standards.

To identify the classifier with the highest accuracy, we calculated the average model accuracy (mean ± standard deviation) for each classifier using repeated stratified fivefold cross validation. Our analysis showed that the GBC model consistently achieved the highest accuracy for all five synthetic constructs, with GBC having an overall accuracy of 0.98 ± 0.004 for PSMB2, 0.93 ± 0.01 for PRPSAP1, 0.97 ± 0.01 for MCM5, 0.941 ± 0.02 for MRPS14, and 0.840 ± 0.01 for RNF7 (Supplemental Fig. S5). In comparison, the next best model, RFC, displayed an overall accuracy of 0.97 ± 0.005 for PSMB2, 0.91 ± 0.01 for PRPSAP1, 0.95 ± 0.01 for MCM5, and 0.93 ± 0.02 for MRPS14, 0.82 ± 0.01 for RNF7 (Supplemental Fig. S5). Due to its superior accuracy, we employed the GBC model for the remainder of this work.

Conversely, we found KNN to have the lowest AUC and classification accuracy, which was the supervised algorithm used in a previous study24. However, the reduced performance of KNN in our analysis may be due to the high-dimensional feature space (45 dimensions) of our training data, which is not well-suited for KNN36. The most accurate KNN model reported to date for ψ prediction relies on up to six features, including the signal mean and quality scores at base positions immediately surrounding (− 1 to + 1) the modification site (with ψ at index 0). We employed these six features to test all five ML classifiers used in our analysis in Fig. 2A–E, left panel, with our five synthetic standards. Our analysis of these six features revealed a considerable decline in the accuracy of predicting ψ, with > 10% average decreases in accuracy across all classifiers (Supplemental Fig. S6), emphasizing the need for the comprehensive feature space we compiled for accurate ψ prediction.

Evaluating the sensitivity and specificity of ψ detection using a GBC model

To evaluate how quantitative the GBC models are in detecting ψ at different occupancies, we generated test sets where the ratio of ψ-modified to unmodified transcripts varied. For each gene-specific synthetic standard, we generated twenty different fractions of ψ:U as test sets that ranged from 0:1 to 1:0 in 0.05 increments. Using these test sets, we compared our GBC model with a U-to-C mismatch error classifier. The GBC models trained and tested via five iterations of stratified fivefold cross-validation were invoked on every ψ:U fraction test set, yielding 25 predictions per fraction (see “Methods”). In Fig. 2A–E, we show the true positive (TP) ψ predictions (mean ± st. dev.) as a function of ψ fraction for GBC models (green markers) and U-to-C mismatch models (blue markers). The TP prediction at each ψ fraction reports how many ψ-modified reads were correctly predicted by the model divided by the total number of reads in the test set. For comparison, an ideal (100% accuracy) TP trend is shown as a black dashed line with slope 1. We find the ψ TP rate, i.e., recall, for the GBC models trained with either PSMB2 (GUUCG) or MCM5 (UGUAG) standards have a mean > 0.95 across all fractions of ψ (Fig. 2A,B, black).The mean TP rate of the GBC models trained on either PRPSAP1 (GAUUG) or MRPS14 (ACUUA) standards were between 0.90 and 0.95 (Fig. 2C,D). The mean TP rate of the GBC model trained with RNF7 (UUUUA) standards were between 0.8 and 0.85 across all ψ ratios (Fig. 2E, top right panel), which is lower relative to the other four sites. We consider the lower accuracy observed with the GBC model trained for ψ prediction in RNF7 to be at the limit of detection of our method, as homopolymer regions comprising a canonical base are already prone to a higher basecalling error (Supplemental Fig. S1). Notably, the GBC model greatly outperforms the U-to-C mismatch model in ψ prediction for all five synthetic standards (Fig. 2A–E, bottom right panels). Regardless of the varying U-to-C mismatch rates observed across our five synthetic standards, site-specific GBC models maintain a high TP rate. For example, the U-to-C mismatch rate for PSMB2 classified ~ 40% of ψ-modified reads when the actual ψ fraction was 100%, while the GBC model correctly called ~ 98%.

To determine whether the GBC model was overfitting to ψ-modified reads, we looked at the false positive (FP) trend (red markers), i.e., canonical U sites that the model misclassifies as ψ-modified (Fig. 2A–E, bottom right). As expected, the FP trend had an inverse relationship with ψ fraction, with maximum FP values for 100% U samples of 0.02 (PSMB2), 0.04 (PRPSAP1), 0.04 (MRPS14), 0.04 (MCM5), and 0.15 (RNF7). As a result, we note that for very low ψ occupancies (< 15%), the model trained with RNF7 synthetic reads performs poorly in distinguishing ψ from U. Furthermore, we assessed how the performance of each GBC model varied with the size of the training dataset. (Supplemental Fig. S7–11). For MCM5 and MRPS14, we found that the classification accuracy begins to saturate at a sample size of ~ 600 and ~ 400, respectively, for each class (ψ-modified and unmodified) in the training set. For PRPSAP1 and PSMB2, we observed saturation to start at around a sample size of 2000 of each class in the training set. For RNF7, we see a gradual increase in accuracy that reaches above 0.8 at around sample size of 500.

Investigation of training features important for accurate ψ classification

Our ML analysis has shown that a localized feature space comprising information when ψ is inside the pore constriction (45 features in total) is sufficient for accurate ψ quantification. To further improve the accuracy of GBC models for ψ quantification in homopolymer sequences, such as RNF7, it may be beneficial to include features from a wider range of positions upstream and downstream from the modification site. However, we showed that inclusion of signal and basecalling features spanning from + 5 to − 5 positions relative to the ψ site (as opposed to the + 2 to − 2 range) negligibly improved the overall accuracy of the GBC model for RNF7 from 0.84 to 0.857 (Supplemental Fig. S16), and for PRPSAP1 from 0.927 to 0.934 (Supplemental Fig. S15).

After training site-specific GBC models with localized features extracted from our synthetic standards, we determined the relative importance of each input feature for predicting ψ modifications in mRNAs. To identify which features contributed most to the GBC model accuracy for each synthetic standard, we used scikit-learn’s37feature importance tool to calculate the relative importance of each feature used in the model (Fig. 2F). Differences in the relative importance of individual features among the five synthetic standards corroborates the notion that the features capturing the distinct nature of ψ vary with k-mer sequence24,26. However, for all synthetic standards except PRPSAP1, the feature with the highest average weight across all GBC iterations was the first quartile value from one of the five k-mers that represents the deeper (i.e., lower current) level (25%) of the recorded current (see “Methods”).

To further understand these results, we performed static time-warping and compared the ionic current levels recorded for the five k-mer frames that contained either a ψ (magenta) or U (cyan) at the pore constriction in all five synthetic transcripts (Fig. 3A,C and Supplemental Fig. S12–14, bottom panels). To visualize the distinction in signal blockade depth across the relevant five k-mer frames between ψ-modified and unmodified transcripts, we overlaid the distributions of all first quartile values calculated from the signals of ψ-modified (gold) and unmodified (black) synthetic transcripts within the datasets used for GBC training and testing (Fig. 3A,C and Supplemental Fig. S12–14, top panels). Other than PRPSAP1 (Supplemental Fig. S12), we observed that ψ induces a deeper and more diffuse signal blockade that is appreciable in one of the ψ-bearing k-mer frames (Fig. 3A,C and Supplemental Fig. S13A,14A). In PSMB2, we observed that although there is overlap in all k-mer frames, the first quartile distribution in k-mer frame 0 (k-mer.0) for unmodified transcripts (black) is much narrower than that of ψ-modified reads (gold). Therefore, the first quartile region of the signal at k-mer.0 provides the greatest separation in the ionic current of ψ-modified and unmodified transcripts in PSMB2 (Fig. 3A). For MCM5, we found that the largest separation between ψ-modified and unmodified transcripts to be within the first quartile region of the signal at k-mer. + 2 (Fig. 3C). These findings are consistent with the results shown in the heatmaps displaying the average weighted importance for each GBC input feature (Fig. 3B,D and Supplemental Fig. S13B and S14B).

In contrast to the other four synthetic standards, the GBC model for PRPSAP1 relied heavily on basecalling features. With more than half the total weight allocated to the basecall at the ψ position, this is consistent with the 70% U-to-C mismatch errors observed in the 100% ψ-modified reads (Supplemental Fig. S1). Visually, the k-mer signals in the ψ-modified and unmodified synthetic reads for PRPSAP1 showed no substantial difference, resulting in none of the statistical parameters from the signal being useful for the GBC model’s prediction of ψ (Supplemental Fig. S12). These results suggest that in cases where the sequence context of the k-mers does not produce sufficient separation in the signal between modified and unmodified reads, basecalling features may compensate and aid in accurately predicting ψ. However, the influence of features from the basecaller (i.e., the U-to-C mismatch error pattern) can vary depending on sequence contexts and other factors. To explore this further we trained and tested another set of GBC models for PRPSAP1 while excluding any basecalling information within a + 2/− 2 feature extraction window, which provided an overall accuracy of 0.88 (as illustrated in Supplemental Fig. S18A and B). In contrast to the other four synthetic controls, the 3rd quartile and standard deviation of the signal at k-mer. + 1 and k-mer. + 2 emerged as the most important features (Supplemental Fig. S18C). While this is a slight reduction compared to the 0.93 accuracy achieved when including basecalling features (Supplemental Fig. S5), this confirms that the model can discern subtle distinctions in the ionic current patterns. Importantly, this also suggests that the model’s performance is not inflated due to the presence of basecalling features. While unlikely, it is plausible that certain k-mer sequences do not exhibit significant differences in either basecall or signal when a ψ modification is present.

Further, to determine whether features influenced by the upstream motor protein could enhance the overall model accuracy, we investigated the performance of the GBC model trained for ψ prediction in RNF7 with an increased feature extraction window of ± 10 nucleotides. We observed an overall accuracy of 0.88 (as depicted in Supplemental Fig. S17A,B), which is a 9% improvement compared to the GBC model trained with a ± 2 window (as shown in Fig. 2E) and 2.4% compared to the GBC model trained with a ± 5 window (as shown in Supplemental Fig. S16), respectively. This suggests that the presence of ψ in the motor protein does not significantly impact accurate classification for ψ in the case of the RNF7 site.

Profiling site-specific ψ modifications across human cell line datasets

Prior to using our trained GBC models for site-specific ψ quantification, we tested them on two independent27 IVT libraries from HeLa cells for further validation. Since these IVT mRNAs are unmodified, the accuracy of the GBC model is limited by the FP ψ frequency. Before we applied the trained GBC models to predict the ψ occupancy on the IVT datasets, we accounted for the model error and derived a re-correction factor for each initial ψ prediction using the ψ TP trend and FP trends (Fig. 2A–E, lower right panels) observed after GBC model testing at different ψ:U fractions. As shown in Supplemental Fig. S19, the GBC models had a low rate of false predictions, averaging at 0.03–0.07 for PSMB2, MCM5, PRPSAP1, and MRPS14, across both HeLa IVT sequencing libraries and 0.003 to 0.15 for RNF7.

Using the models derived in Fig. 2 for each synthetic standard, we profiled ψ occupancy at the intended site located on native mRNA transcripts aligned to the gene corresponding to its synthetic standard. Native mRNA reads from seven different human cell lines, as well as a HeLa TRUB1 siRNA knockdown cell line (Supplemental Fig. S20), were prepared with the same read filtration and feature extraction process as the synthetic reads. The quantification results for all five mRNAs were compared across the DRS libraries of seven different cell lines: A549, HepG2, HEK293T, Hct116, HeLa, HeLa with TRUB1 knockdown (KD), NTERA and SH-SY5Y (Fig. 4A and Supplemental Table S7). We found two examples of a significant deviation from the mean across all cell lines: PSMB2 in SH-SY5Y cells and PRPSAP1 in NTERA cells (Fig. 4B) makes the GBC-predicted occupancy results less reliable.

The TRUB1 enzyme is responsible for installing ψ at specific sites with the GUUCNA motif17, which is only present in PSMB2 among the five sites we examined. Upon applying our model to this KD cell line which shows efficient knockdown of TRUB1 (see Supplemental Fig. S21), we observed a substantial reduction in ψ occupancy for the query site on PSMB2 (mean occupancy level of 0.88 and 0.24 in the WT and KD HeLa cells, respectively; Fig. 4A). As expected, we did not detect a reduction in the model’s predicted ψ occupancy for the other four sites within the TRUB1 KD HeLa cells when compared to the WT HeLa cells.

We further benchmarked our model’s quantification results for PSMB2 and MCM5 in HEK293T cells against two orthogonal methods, BID-seq15 and PRAISE35. Both methods use bisulfite treatment of RNA in conjunction with NGS Illumina sequencing to detect ψ sites, which appear in the form of a deletion during reverse transcription. Our ψ occupancy rates for these two genes were more consistent with the deletion rates observed with PRAISE (black squares in Fig. 4A), likely due to the optimized sensitivity in the bisulfite treatment.

To distinguish ψ sites with stoichiometric variability, we identified cell lines with a higher standard deviation in GBC-predicted ψ occupancy, across all replicates, than the standard deviation of the GBC models (n = 25), i.e., the expected model variability. We computed the expected model standard deviation using synthetic ψ-modified samples as a function of read coverage (Supplemental Fig. S20). We observed a large difference between the standard deviation of GBC-predicted ψ occupancy in PSMB2 for SH-SY5Y, NTERA, HEK293T, and HeLa, which was three times greater than the model uncertainty (Supplemental Fig. S20). Similarly, MCM5 exhibited the same trend across all cell lines except for HepG2 (Supplemental Fig. S20). We also found consistent results in HeLa mRNAs aligned to PRPSAP1, MRPS14, and RNF7 (Supplemental Fig. S25). Based on these findings, we believe that our observed variance in GBC-predicted ψ occupancy across biological replicates reflects the state of the cellular RNA from which the mRNAs were derived, rather than noise in our model.

Finally, to evaluate the effectiveness of our method in quantifying ψ on mRNAs with low read coverage, we compared the standard deviations in the ψ TP rate predicted by the re-corrected GBC models (green) and U-to-C mismatch models (blue) using the PSMB2 synthetic standards as a function of ψ-modified read coverage with read counts ranging from n = 7 to n = 200, increasing by n = 2 (Fig. 4C). The ψ-modified transcripts used were sampled from the test sets created for each GBC model iteration (n = 25) shown in Fig. 2A. We found that the standard deviation of the ψ TP rate for the re-corrected GBC model was lower than that of the re-corrected U-to-C mismatch classifier at all read counts, indicating that even with a re-correction factor, a U-to-C mismatch classifier does not achieve the same accuracy as the GBC.

Discussion

We developed here a computational engine that allows quantitative measurement of ψ occupancy in multiple human mRNAs from different cell lines using DRS data. Our method is a two-step process that first uses endogenous transcriptome data to identify putative ψ sites through U-to-C basecalling errors, and then quantifies the ψ occupancy at those sites using ML models trained on sequence-specific synthetic mRNA standards (modified and unmodified)34. While the U-to-C mismatch error observed in DRS data has been previously used for identifying ψ modification sites in the transcriptome de novo 23–27, similar to what was reported by Fleming et al.26, the different U-to-C mismatch error rates (ranging from 25 to 70%) observed with our synthetic standards confirms that this approach is not quantitative due to its sensitivity to sequence context (Supplemental Fig. S1). Our site-specific GBC models show accuracies ranging from of 84–98% (RNF7 and PSMB2) and FP rates within the range of 2–15% (PSMB2 and RNF7) when trained with our synthetic standards (Fig. 2A-E and Supplemental Fig. S5). The lower standard deviation observed in the ψ TP rate of our re-corrected GBC model, when compared to the re-corrected U-to-C mismatch classifier (as shown in Fig. 4C), highlights how the higher accuracy achieved is critical for ψ quantification in transcriptome data with low coverage (< 10 reads), which is the case for most mRNAs sequenced on a MinION flow cell.

We have shown here quantitative profiling of site-specific ψ within and across cell lines using ML models trained by synthetic RNA control datasets. Our approach was further validated using a HeLa TRUB1 KD cell line, as well as benchmarking ψ occupancy against bisulfate/sulfite NGS sequencing methods, showing good agreement. Profiling ψ for five sites across 7 cell lines, we found that ψ occupancies are for the most part conserved, with a few clear significant deviations at specific sites for some cell lines.

Our application of supervised ML models showed that certain signal parameters on different k-mers are required to maximize ψ classification accuracy for each specific site studied. Specifically, long-range sequence context provided by our synthetic MCM5 standards revealed scenarios where features most important for training an accurate model for ψ discrimination derive from signal information at k-mer. + 2 (Fig. 3C,D). This emphasizes the importance of having a synthetic training set that should, at a minimum, comprise a + 4/− 4 nucleotide window when replicating the sequence context of a ψ site in the transcriptome.

While we determined that a feature extraction window of + 2/− 2 was adequate for training accurate GBC models for ψ classification, we were interested in how model performance might vary with a larger feature extraction window. It has been shown that ψ modifications with an adjacent 5’ guanosine (G) can cause the helicase motor to slow down RNA passage through the pore26. Previous work thus used features derived from the signals that correspond to the presence of the modified k-mer while held at the protein motor (~ 10–12 nucleotides upstream from the pore constriction). However, reliance on these signal parameters can complicate the model, requiring consideration of a larger sequence context and possibly resulting in false ψ calls at sites that are near G-rich sequences, which can also stall the motor38. For the RNF7 site, we did not observe a significant improvement in model accuracy when comparing a GBC model trained with a + 10/− 10 feature extraction window (Supplemental Fig. S17A and B) to one trained with a + 2/− 2 window (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, when comparing the overall relative feature importance between the GBC model trained with the + 10/− 10 feature extraction window and the one trained with the + 2/− 2 window, the first quartile region of the signal at k-mer. + 1 (Supplemental Fig. S17C) remained the most significant. Synthesizing and curating a training set that comprehensively accounts for a + 10/− 10 nucleotide window would be impractical.

The observed differences in the expected signal, particularly the deeper blockade of a k-mer when ψ is present, as highlighted by our feature importance analysis (Fig. 3 and Supplemental Figs. S13 and 14), appears to be a reproducible feature of ψ, irrespective of the surrounding sequence context. While our analysis is predicated on a limited dataset of five distinct mRNA synthetic controls, if this pattern is consistent, it might be feasible to train a deep learning model, such as a recurrent neural network like the guppy basecaller, for accurate de novo prediction of ψ in mRNA reads. However, the finding that different features are important for each construct suggests that a large training set of synthetic controls with many combinations of ψ-containing sequences (± 4 nucleotide window) would be required. Moreover, a substantial number of synthetic standards would still be required to produce the necessary training and validation datasets. The high cost of synthetic RNA standards would require significant efforts to synthesize. Fortunately, our investigation of the training window size around the ψ site and its influence on ψ classification accuracy shows marginal improvement when signals beyond ± 2 k-mers ((± 4 nucleotide window) away from the ψ site being at the center of the pore are used to train each model. Hence, synthesizing a training set of with many combinations of ψ-containing sequences within a + 4/− 4 nucleotide window (65,536 different sequences with ψ/U) would be a practical and could be used to train models, both supervised or deep learning, for accurate transcriptome-wide quantification of not only ψ, but other RNA modifications, which is an ultimate goal for the epitranscriptomics field.

Methods

Synthetic mRNA standards bearing Ψ modifications

Synthesis of the 1 kb RNA standards used in this study is fully described in Gamper et al.34 The synthetic standards were designed to replicate the native mRNA sequence of genes (PSMB2, MCM5, PRPSAP1, MRPS14, and RNF7) bearing a putative site-specific ψ, as flagged by a U-to-C mismatch error detection algorithm developed by Tavakoli et al.27 in DRS libraries of HeLa mRNAs. For each gene, unmodified and ψ-modified controls were synthesized. Unmodified transcripts were produced through T7 in vitro transcription from G-block DNAs (synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies), while the ψ-modified reads were produced using a 3-part splint ligation method where two RNA arms (each 500 nts in length) were ligated to a 15-nt RNA fragment that contained ψ in the center position. This method achieved higher yields of full-length ψ-modified synthetic transcripts than the average 2% reported in the literature39. A key feature of the method was the use of a Hepatitis delta virus (HDV) ribozyme cleavage to improve the homogeneity of the 3’ sequence of the left-arm RNA for ligation.

Sequence alignment

We basecalled all DRS data with guppy (version 6.3.2) and used the “rna_r9.4.1_70bps_hac” model (see Supplementary Table S8 for full details). Next,FASTQ files containing the basecalled reads that passed the default ONT filtration stage (> Q7) were aligned to the synthetic reference, or GRCh38.p10 if they were native mRNA reads, using minimap2 (version 2.17)40 with the command line option ‘‘-ax map-ont -un -k14’’. Next, the output alignment file (sam file) was converted into a BAM file using SAMtools (version 1.10). All BAM files were sorted with “samtools sort” and indexed using “samtools index”. Finally, a BAM file was prepared for each gene by slicing out the corresponding reads from the original BAM file using “samtools view -h -Sb”.

Read filtration and feature extraction

After alignment, a second-stage filtration stage was implemented on each gene to remove reads that were truncated near the site of modification. Using Rsamtools (version 2.6.0), a mapping quality filter was implemented to retain reads with a mapping quality score of > = 50. Full-length synthetic reads had its ψ or U base of interest located on base position 511. We scanned all reads for the 511 base position and 7 base positions upstream and downstream, which we refer to as the 15 nt target segment. Reads that passed the second filtration stage were required to have a minimum of 10 base calls (including both correct and mismatch base calls) within its 15 nt target segment.

Next, we set a second window that was 5 nt within the 15-nt target segment centered around the 511-base position as the region for which we extracted basecalling and signal features. This 5 nt window captures the bases and k-mer positions ranging from − 2 to + 2 relative to the ψ or U position. Reads with insertion base calls in the 5 nt feature extraction window surrounding the modified base were also included in our analysis.

To prepare basecalling features, we used Rsamtools to extract base calls (including deletions/insertions) and quality scores within the 5 nt window centered around the ψ or U position. Specifically, we used the cigar string of each passed read to parse the basecall and quality scores at positions − 2 to + 2 relative to ψ or U at index 0, moving in a 5′ to 3′ direction. Additionally, we calculated the frequency of each base insertion (A, G, C, and U) and recorded the total number of insertions within the 5 nt window. Signal features were extracted from the ionic current of every k-mer frame within the 5 nt window, i.e., k-mers − 2 to + 2 relative to the ψ or U position moving in a 3′ to 5′ direction, providing a total of five k-mer frames. From each k-mer, six statistical values were computed from the signal mean, in picoamperes (pA), standard deviation (pA), dwell-time (s), first quartile (pA), second quartile/median (pA), and third quartile (pA). The k-mer signals were prepared using the eventalign module in nanopolish22 (version 0.13.2), which implements a hidden Markov model (HMM) to “resquiggle” the aligned reads to their corresponding ionic current. To do this, nanopolish required a FASTQ file indexed with the FAST5 files storing all the signal data (using the “nanopolish index” command), the aligned BAM file, and the reference FASTA file (GRCh38.p10) as input for the “nanopolish eventalign” command, which used the following input command line options: –scale-events, –summary, –samples, and –signal-index. The nanopolish eventalign process produced two output files: (1) An eventalign text file, which includes the ionic current of all k-mers recorded at every base position for every read in the BAM file and (2) A summary file, which lists the read ID index for all reads included in the eventalign file for extraction of signal information corresponding to each read. Next, our feature extraction software compiled all the basecalling and signal features for every mRNA read that passed the filtration requirements into a dataframe for subsequent model training and testing. Since the feature extraction window was set to 5 nt we extracted 15 basecalling and 30 signal parameters, for a total of 45 features, from every read. After ML training and testing, native mRNA reads were prepared with the same filtering conditions and feature extraction process prior to site-specific ψ quantification. The feature extraction software we developed requires the following input files: (1) an aligned, indexed, and sorted BAM file along with the corresponding (2) eventalign text file produced after “nanopolish eventalign” and 3) summary text file produced after “nanopolish eventalign”.

Data preprocessing

We used the pandas (1.3.5) library for data preprocessing and scikit-learn (1.0.2)41 for model training and testing. For each gene, an equal sample size was prepared from ψ-modified and unmodified synthetic standards and their features were combined into one pandas data frame. The sample size was limited by the number of ψ-modified reads, as this dataset had a lower number of samples than the unmodified dataset after filtering. Next, the training and testing sets were prepared with repeated stratified fivefold cross-validation to ensure that all reads in the data frame were equally sampled into the training and testing sets. For each fold (5 in total), the training set comprised 80% of the dataset, while the remaining 20% was allocated to the testing set. Thus, the combination of data sampled into the training and testing sets was unique for each fold. To get an overall performance of each model, the stratified fivefold cross-validation process was repeated five times, generating a total of 25 unique train-test splits. Prior to model training and testing, the training data was normalized using the StandardScaler function in scikit-learn, where the mean of the data in each feature was recentered around 0 and the first standard deviation was set to ± 1. The normalization parameters for each feature were saved and used to rescale the data in the test set.

Model training/testing/evaluation

Five different supervised ML classifiers were employed in this study and were imported and initialized through the scikit-learn library. The ML algorithms used were logistic regression (LR), gradient boosting (GB), K-nearest neighbors (KNN), random forest (RF), and support vector machine (SVM). Each ML classifier was trained and tested with the 25 unique train-test splits, generated for every gene-specific synthetic standard, through the five repeats of fivefold cross-validation. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) were calculated using the plot_roc_curve function in scikit-learn. For every GB classifier (GBC) trained in this study, the model parameters n_estimators (the number of decision trees in the forest) and max depth (the depth of each decision tree) were set to 200 and 5, respectively. The rest of the parameters were set to their default values.

The ψ true-positive (TP) and false-positive (FP) GBC analysis at varied fractions of ψ-modified to unmodified reads, ranging from 0:1 to 1:0 in 0.05 increments, was done using the same 25 unique train-test splits prepared with the same five repeats of fivefold cross-validation process, providing a total of 25 unique models for every ratio. Every model training and testing iteration underwent a process where the data in the testing set was resampled to the desired ratio prior to the GBC model’s prediction. In parallel with the GBC model, a U-to-C mismatch classifier was used to predict the fraction of ψ-modified reads in the same resampled test set. The TP and FP results of every model for each fraction were saved by seeding all training and testing iterations, along with their normalization parameters. The re-correction factors for the GBC and U-to-C mismatch models were calculated by using linear regression to fit the sum of the mean TP and FP predictions observed at each ψ fraction.

The relative feature importance for each gene-specific GBC was calculated using the feature_importances attribute provided with the scikit-learn GBC module. Briefly, the weighted importance of each feature in a GBC model is measured by its contribution in the reduction of uncertainty, i.e., how often a sample is correctly labeled when the feature is used in a decision tree. This method of measurement is known as Gini impurity. The heatmaps displaying the relative importance of each feature represent the average of the same 25 GBC models generated for the analysis in Fig. 2, with all weights summing to 100.

Cell culture

Immortalized human cell lines used in this study were purchased from ATCC. HeLa cervical adenocarcinoma cells (ATCC CCL-2), A549 pulmonary carcinoma cells (ATCC CCL-185), NTERA testicular malignant pluripotent embryonal carcinoma cells (ATCC CRL-1973), and HepG2 liver hepatocellular carcinoma cells (ATCC HB-8065) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, 10566024), supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FB12999102, Fisher Scientific) and 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin (Lonza,17602E). SH-SY5Y bone neuroblastoma cells (ATCC CRL-2266) were cultured in EMEM/F12 medium (1:1, v:v) supplemented with 10% FBS.

TRUB1 Knockdown in HeLa cells

The siRNA knockdown assay was carried out using reverse transfection. Stock RNAi duplex (AM51331) was diluted with Opti-MEM® I Reduced Serum Medium (31985062) to a final concentration of 100 nM, then gently mixed with Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX (13778030) and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. HeLa cells were suspended in a complete growth medium without antibiotics, where each well in an 8-well chamber contained 275 uL of suspended HeLa cells combined with 25 uL of 100 nM RNAi duplex-Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX complexes. The number of HeLa cells per well was ~ 18,000. Cell density was 30–50% confluent 24 h after plating and were incubated for 24–72 h at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator for 4 days. During the 4-day incubation period, the growth medium (without antibiotics) was replaced daily.

Quantification of ψ on native mRNAs using GBC models trained with synthetic standards

The HeLa (n = 3) and HeLa IVT (n = 2) DRS data was publicly available from Tavakoli et al.27; while the HEK293T (n = 2) and Hct116 (n = 2) sequencing libraries were obtained from the SG-NEx database42. SH-SY5Y (n = 3), and NTERA (n = 2), A549 (n = 2) and HepG2 (n = 2) sequencing libraries were prepared according to the protocols from by Tavakoli et al.27 and the remaining two A549 libraries and one HepG2 library were prepared from the SG-NEx database. Targeting the previously flagged putative ψ site that the synthetic constructs were designed around, all native reads underwent the same filtering stages and feature extraction process as the synthetic reads, generating the same feature space that was used for GBC model fitting. The 25 gene-specific GBC models trained generated previously with synthetic data were each applied to the dataframe comprised of the features from the native transcripts of the corresponding gene, providing an average ψ occupancy prediction at each specific site. To maintain consistency, the basecalling and signal features extracted from native reads were scaled with the same normalization parameters used for training and testing each GBC model, and the re-correction fit calculated with same 25 models was applied after the initial prediction to account for the model error observed during the training and testing. We used the featureCounts module from Rsubread29 on one of the HeLa DRS libraries to demonstrate that the majority of mRNA transcripts have relatively low read coverage (Fig. 4C, background histogram in gold).

Statistical comparison of ψ quantification in native mRNAs across cell lines

The statistical difference in the predicted ψ occupancy of each cell line relative to the other cell lines for a given gene was determined with an independent two-sample t-test. For each cell line, the corresponding ψ occupancy predictions produced by the 25 unique GBC models for each DRS replicate were combined into one sample set. The other sample set comprised ψ occupancy predictions for all other cell lines. Using the ttest_ind module in SciPy (version 1.10.0), a p-value was calculated for every cell line per gene, which was subsequently rescaled as -10Log(p-value).
