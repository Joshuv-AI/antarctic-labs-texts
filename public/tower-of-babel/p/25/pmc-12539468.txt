Streptococcus pyogenes induced septic arthritis of the wrist secondary to a temporal abscess and trapezoid fracture - A case report
Authors: Muhammed Cetinkaya, Frederik Flensted Andersen, Poul Pedersen, Dennis Karimi, Tazio Maleitzke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Septic arthritis (SA) of the wrist is a rare and challenging condition that requires rapid treatment to prevent irreversible joint damage. We present a unique case of a hematogenously spread SA of the wrist caused by Streptococcus pyogenes, secondary to a temporal abscess. The patient, a previously healthy 36-year-old man without known risk factors aside from occasional alcohol consumption, exhibited wrist pain without fever or overt signs of infection. One week prior, he had suffered a temporal laceration and a non-displaced trapezoid fractur following a minor fall on his head and hand. In the following days, the patient developed fever, signs of infection around the temporal wound, and increasing wrist pain, ultimately leading to the diagnosis of a temporal abscess and SA of the wrist.Multiple surgical debridements were necessary alongside targeted intravenous antibiotic therapy, to successfully eradicate the wrist infection. Despite appropriate treatment, the patient experienced persistent pain and functional limitations of the wrist five months later.This case underscores the importance of early diagnosis and treatment of SA in cases of joint pain in combination with a potential infection elsewhere in the body. The case emphasizes the critical role of timely surgical intervention in managing SA of the wrist.

Background

Septic arthritis (SA) of the wrist, an infection of the radiocarpal joint caused by bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites, is rare [1]. The estimated incidence is 2–10 cases per 100,000 person-years in industrialized countries [2,3]. SA primarily affects the hip, knee, ankle, and shoulder, with the wrist accounting for 3–5 % of all SA cases [[4], [5], [6]]. Approximately 60 % of hand infections result from hand trauma, and SA accounts for 2 % of hand infections [7,8].

Wrist septic arthritis (SA) is rare, but three larger clinical cohort studies have been published: a retrospective case series of 40 patients from 2015, covering an 11-year period [9]; a 2022 retrospective series of 39 patients with a median follow-up of 21.5 months [3]; and a recent retrospective case series with a prospective follow-up of 29 months (median) in 44 patients, published in 2025 [25]. Taken together, these case series indicate that SA of the wrist is a rare condition that is frequently diagnosed with delay. Surgical debridement is performed in nearly all reported cases; however, postoperative functional outcomes are often markedly impaired. Apart from these studies, the literature consists mainly of single case reports [23,26]. We found no previous reports of wrist SA secondary to a closed carpal bone fracture.

If diagnosed with a delay and not treated promptly, SA can lead to irreversible joint destruction within days, severe functional impairment, and increased mortality of 11–15 % [5,9,10]. Cartilage destruction may begin as early as 8 h following infection onset [11].

SA results from pathogens infiltrating the synovium through various mechanisms: hematogenous spread, where pathogens are carried through capillaries to the joint; contiguous spread, where the infection extends from adjacent tissues; or direct inoculation, typically resulting from trauma or surgical and medical procedures (e.g. arthrocentesis) [2].

Hallmark symptoms are pain, swelling, restricted movement, and fever. Pain is the most common symptom observed in SA of the wrist followed by stiffness and fever [9]. In the retrospective case series by Krauss et al. all 39 SA patients exhibited swelling, restricted motion, and pain [3].

Gram-positive bacteria (68–85 %), mainly staphylococcus (24–56 %) and streptococcus species, are the predominant pathogens in SA [4,12,6]. Gram-negative bacteria account for 8–15 % of cases [12,13]. Blood cultures are positive in 24 % of cases, underscoring both their diagnostic relevance and limitation [14].

SA diagnosis remains challenging due to nonspecific symptoms. Synovial fluid samples may appear as yellow/green opaque fluid, with elevated white blood cell (WBC) counts (10–100 × 109/L), and >75 % polymorphonuclear neutrophils [12]. A synovial WBC count ≥50 × 109/L strongly suggests SA [12]. Positive microbiological cultures are observed in 60–80 % of nongonococcal SA cases [12,15,6].

Biochemical markers (C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)) aid in infection detection but lack specificity [13]. In wrist SA cases, CRP and ESR are elevated in only 40 and 20 % of cases, respectively [20]. Peripheral WBC counts are often normal, with 38–42 % of upper extremity SA patients exhibiting normal counts [13].

Imaging (X-ray, computed-tomography (CT), ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)) may reveal soft tissue swelling, joint effusion, or bone changes and calcium deposits in advanced cases [12]. Ultrasound may be used to aid with joint aspiration. Imaging should not delay treatment.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with IV contrast is rated “usually appropriate” for suspected SA of the hand and wrist, according to the 2022 American College of Radiology (ACR) Appropriateness Criteria [24]. MRI is the most sensitive modality for early detection of musculoskeletal infection and is especially valuable for assessing synovial thickening, bone marrow edema, tenosynovitis, joint effusion, and soft tissue involvement [24].

In cases where MRI is not available or possible, nuclear medicine techniques such as SPECT/CT or PET/CT may be considered, particularly in chronic infections or when differentiating infection from sterile inflammatory conditions [24].

According to European Bone and Joint Infection Society (EBJIS) guidelines [16], antibiotic treatment for suspected SA should begin after synovial fluid aspiration and blood cultures. In cases of sepsis or septic shock, empirical antibiotics must be initiated immediately, prior to diagnostics. Once culture results are available, treatment should be adjusted accordingly.

EBJIS [16] further recommends arthroscopic debridement in most cases, while open surgery may only be necessary in severe infections or when small joints are involved. Surgical intervention may be delayed for up to 24 h if the patient is stable, the joint has been irrigated, and antibiotics have been initiated.

Case

A 36-year-old man was admitted to the emergency department under the influence of alcohol and cocaine after tumbling and falling from standing height. He sustained a 7 cm laceration of the right temporal region, which was actively bleeding from a side branch of the temporal artery.

The wound was thoroughly irrigated, closed with a polypropylene suture, and a compressive dressing was applied. A CT scan of the head ruled out intracranial hemorrhage or fractures. The patient was discharged the same day.

On day 4, the patient returned to the emergency department due to persistent bleeding from the temporal wound. The wound showed no signs of infection, and the patient was afebrile. He reported feeling unwell, with nausea, sweating, and pallor. Laboratory tests showed elevated CRP of 120 mg/L, and WBC counts of 11.4 × 109/L, which were interpreted as a post-traumatic inflammatory response. A new sterile compressive dressing was applied, and the patient was discharged with a prescription of ondansetron.

On day 7, the patient revisited the emergency department with severe left wrist pain that disrupted his sleep. He disclosed that his hand had been injured during the initial fall and clinical examination revealed tenderness in the anatomic snuffbox. A dual-energy CT scan confirmed a non-displaced trapezoid fracture with associated bone marrow edema (Fig. 1A, B). On day 8, the patient returned with persistent wrist pain. An ultrasound of the left wrist revealed synovitis. The patient was admitted for further evaluation. Physical examination revealed diffuse tenderness of the wrist without signs of infection. For pain management, a peripheral lateral infraclavicular (LIC) nerve block was administered.

On the night of day 8–9, the patient developed fever (>40 °C) and appeared unwell. Examination of the temporal wound revealed swelling, erythema, warmth, and fluctuation without pain. Sutures were removed, and 3–4 mL of purulent exudate were aspirated (Fig. 1D) and sent for microbiological culturing.

Blood tests revealed a CRP level of 300 mg/L and WBC count of 19.7 × 109/L.

Blood cultures were obtained. Due to suspicion of SA, synovial fluid was aspirated from the left wrist (Fig. 1C). IV antibiotics (ampicillin and gentamicin) were initiated. As the analgesic block wore off, the patient experienced opioid-resistant wrist pain and was taken to surgery. Dorsal and volar incisions of the wrist (Fig. 1E, F) exposed purulent arthritis, prompting carpal tunnel release, and partial synovectomy. Subsequently, the temporal abscess was debrided and irrigated.

Postoperatively, antibiotics were adjusted to dicloxacillin and benzylpenicillin. A contrast-enhanced CT of the head and neck showed no residual abscesses. Neurological examination was normal.

On day 9, results from blood cultures, joint fluid, and synovial tissue samples confirmed the presence of Streptococcus pyogenes. The antibiotic regimen was modified to meropenem and clindamycin.

Later that day, the patient underwent a second look operation of the wrist with further debridement, partial synovectomy, and irrigation. The temporal abscess cavity was thoroughly irrigated and left partially open for drainage.

On day 10, the antibiotic regimen was changed to cloxacillin and benzylpenicillin. IV antibiotics were discontinued on day 13 after a seven-day course, and the patient was continued on oral antibiotics for 35 days. On day 19, the temporal wound showed no erythema, swelling, or tenderness, and remaining sutures were removed.

The patient began rehabilitation-therapy of the hand but continued to experience significant pain and functional limitations five months following trauma.

The patient displayed reduced wrist flexion of 40 degrees, compared to 90 degrees on the right side. Grip strength in the left hand was markedly reduced compared to the right. Prolonged use of the left hand resulted in fatigue and tenderness.

Discussion

This case report covers a hematogenously spread SA of the wrist caused by Streptococcus pyogenes following a skin laceration of the head and a trapezoid fracture. Although SA is rare, early diagnosis and treatment are critical to preserve joint function and prevent complications.

Clinical manifestations, paraclinical findings (elevated CRP and WBC count), and imaging (e.g., X-ray, CT, and ultrasound) are often nonspecific. Additionally, SA can mimic conditions like crystalline arthropathy, aseptic tenosynovitis, and other infections [11,17].

Diagnosis is particularly challenging in the absence of classic signs such as fever, redness, swelling, and restricted movement [5,18]. Our patient initially presented with isolated pain of the wrist, and fever only developing later. Fever alone is an unreliable indicator for SA. In a retrospective study with 40 wrist SA patients only 18 % exhibited fever [9].

The patient consumed alcohol approximately once a month, typically until intoxicated, which likely impaired his immune response as alcohol disrupts inflammatory regulation and increases susceptibility to infections [19,20]. Aside from alcohol consumption, no other risk factors for infection or SA were identified. The patient had no prior history of serious bacterial infections.

A retrospective case series of 52 patients with SA of the shoulder, elbow, or wrist in a relatively young population (mean age 44 years) found that only 54 % had comorbidities [13]. This aligns with our findings, indicating that SA should be considered regardless of age and medical history. Studies done in older populations (mean age 64 years) report a higher prevalence of comorbidities (66.67 %) [3], likely due to age-related health conditions.

Hematogenously spread SA of the wrist due to Streptococcus pyogenes is rare. Bacteria in the bloodstream can advance to injured areas with compromised blood supply and impaired immune defenses. Trauma and fractures disrupt natural tissue barriers, facilitating bacterial colonization. Local inflammation and vascular changes further increase susceptibility, as immune cell infiltration may be insufficient to clear the infection.

This case highlights this mechanism where a non-displaced fracture facilitated hematogenous bacterial dissemination. Some cases have been reported where (non-wrist) SA developed following closed intra-articular fractures [21].

Due to intra-operative purulence in dorsal and volar aspects of the wrist, we performed combined dorsal and volar incisions to allow thorough debridement of the radiocarpal and mid-carpal joints, facilitate carpal tunnel decompression, and ensure adequate drainage.

Steubing et al. reported that in a cohort of 44 patients with SA of the wrist, 59.1 % underwent open wrist arthrotomy with debridement of the soft tissue envelope, and 43.2 % required additional soft tissue reconstruction, including flaps or grafts, indicating extensive soft tissue damage. While the surgical approach (dorsal vs. volar) is not specified in detail, the reported need for aggressive surgical debridement provides a rationale for extended access [25]. Gerardi et al. described a case of acute carpal tunnel syndrome caused by wrist SA, which was successfully treated by carpal tunnel release and drainage, leading to prompt restoration of median nerve function [26]. Based on these previous descriptions and the intra-operative findings in our case, a dual-incision strategy was deemed necessary to control infection and prevent neurovascular damage.

Synovial fluid cultures are essential for targeted antibiotic therapy, but in small joints such as the wrist, the limited synovial fluid volume and the narrow joint space can make aspiration technically challenging and delay diagnosis. This difficulty may further be exacerbated in patients with obesity or significant soft tissue swelling, which can obscure anatomical landmarks. The use of sterile ultrasound guidance can improve success rates and facilitate timely diagnosis.

A retrospective study by Chow, et al. found that initiating antibiotics more than five days after symptom onset worsens functional outcomes in wrist SA patients [4]. Thus, while cultures should be obtained, treatment should not be delayed and may be initiated based on clinical suspicion. Findings such as yellow-green opaque synovial fluid, which is immediately visible upon successful aspiration, and a synovial WBC count of ≥50 × 109/L with >75 % polymorphonuclear neutrophils, typically available within 2 h, support the diagnosis.

According to EBJIS, antibiotic treatment should consist of 1–2 weeks of intravenous therapy, followed by 2–4 weeks of oral therapy. The switch to oral antibiotics is recommended when clinical signs (e.g., fever, redness, swelling, pain) and biomarkers (CRP and WBC count) show satisfactory improvement.

CRP is a sensitive biomarker for active infection and treatment monitoring. In this case, CRP levels correlated with infection progression. Conclusion

This case report outlines a rare septic complication following a seemingly minor head trauma combined with an undisplaced trapezoid fracture. To our knowledge, no prior case of Streptococcus pyogenes induced SA secondary to a temporal abscess has previously been reported. Thus, SA following a closed carpal fracture remains extremely rare, yet this case highlights the importance of considering SA in patients with unexplained joint pain and a recent history of infection and/or trauma, even in the absence of skin lacerations, classical symptoms, typical risk factors and comorbidities. Delayed treatment can lead to severe short- and long-term complications including sepsis, functional impairment and pain. Despite appropriate management, at the five months follow-up, the patient continued to experience significant wrist dysfunction.
