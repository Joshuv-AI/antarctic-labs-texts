Interplay of Aquatic and Riparian Variables in Two Anthropogenically-Influenced Small Stream Catchments
Authors: Alexander Feckler, Sebastian Stehle, Jean-Nicolas Beisel, Etienne Chanez, Tanja J Joschko, Alexis P Roodt, Thomas Schmidt, Verena C Schreiner, Ralf Schulz, Cybill Staentzel, Jens Schirmel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall streams are important ecosystems in many areas globally, playing a crucial role in conserving biodiversity and providing ecosystem functions. However, owing to their high connectivity with surrounding landscapes, small streams are particularly susceptible to drivers of biodiversity decline. We report here the results of a monitoring program conducted between 2018 and 2021 at 18 sites in two stream catchments in southwest Germany, characterized by near-natural to highly anthropogenic land use. The monitoring program included 26 environmental, biological, and anthropogenic stressor variables describing the landscape context, aquatic ecosystem compartments, and riparian areas. We used ordination analysis and factor analysis of mixed data (FAMD) to analyse the relationship between variables and the effects of anthropogenic stressors on ecosystem structure and functioning. Results showed that near-natural upstream sites are substantially less impacted by anthropogenic stressors (e.g., pesticide pollution, morphological alterations) compared with agricultural and urban downstream sites. Biotic parameters, such as abundances and taxa richness of aquatic macroinvertebrates, fish, and carabid beetles, as well as leaf litter decomposition, exhibited overall high variations between sites. FAMD assigned the stream sites into four groups (i.e., forest, grassland, agriculture, viticulture), primarily based on land use and anthropogenic stressor influences and found anthropogenic stressors to often co-occur. Lower aquatic macroinvertebrate abundances and diversity, as well as reduced leaf litter decomposition, were observed at sites with higher anthropogenic disturbances. Overall, our findings suggest that small streams are subjected to multiple anthropogenic stressors, resulting in negative effects on both aquatic and riparian ecosystem health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00244-026-01205-5.Keywords: Anthropogenic stress, Catchment, Land use, Pesticides, Aquatic communities, Riparian habitats

Introduction

Small streams denote important and extended landscape features representing 70–80% of global running waters (Biggs et al. 2017; Wohl 2017). They host rich biodiversity, making them essential for both biodiversity conservation and the ecological state of larger downstream rivers (Reid et al. 2019; Tickner et al. 2020). Small streams also provide important ecosystem services and ecosystem functions such as organic matter breakdown (Voß et al. 2015), which benefit from higher species diversities (Cardinale et al. 2012; Hooper et al. 2005). These ecosystems are also closely coupled to their riparian zones, which represent gradients in environmental factors, ecological processes, and biological communities (Gregory et al. 1991). Riparian zones are, in fact, an extremely important component of small stream ecosystems and they interact strongly with riverine conditions (Naiman et al. 2005; Schulz et al. 2015). For example, terrestrial organic matter constitutes the most important energy source in small, forested streams (Wallace et al. 1997). Conversely, resource fluxes from small streams to the terrestrial ecosystem, such as emerging insects as prey for predators in the riparian zone, transport energy and nutrients back to the terrestrial ecosystem (Paetzold and Tockner 2005; Schulz et al. 2015; Middendorf et al. 2025). Small streams and their riparian areas are thus closely connected by exchange of energy, materials and organisms across ecosystem boundaries (Schulz et al. 2015). For assessing the potential effects of anthropogenic stressors on the structural and functional integrity of stream ecosystems, it is essential to consider both aquatic and riparian compartments and their linkages (Soininen et al. 2015).

Global freshwater biodiversity is on the decline (Albert et al. 2021; Tickner et al. 2020), and small streams, in particular, face threats from drivers of biodiversity decline. This vulnerability stems from their high connectivity with surrounding landscapes and relatively low dilution potentials for chemical stressors (Szöcs et al. 2017). The primary drivers of aquatic biodiversity loss are anthropogenic stressors, including land use change, hydromorphological alterations, pollution, climate change, overexploitation, and biological invasions (Jaureguiberry et al. 2022; Reid et al. 2019). Although often overlooked in freshwater ecological studies, toxic chemicals can be a major contributor to freshwater degradation (Bernhardt et al. 2017). Chemical pollutants may enter streams via point sources (e.g., wastewater treatment plant effluents) or diffuse sources (e.g., runoff from agricultural lands) and can cause lethal and sub-lethal impacts on aquatic species, resulting in the impairment of freshwater ecosystem structure and functioning (Schäfer 2019; Fernández et al. 2015). Pesticides used in agriculture pose an especially high risk for surface waters as they have high ecotoxicity potentials and are applied to agricultural fields in large quantities over the entire growing season (Stehle and Schulz 2015a; Schäfer 2019; Vormeier et al. 2023). Small streams in human-dominated landscapes are also often profoundly altered morphologically, biogeochemically, hydrologically, and biologically due to agricultural or urban impacts (Goertzen et al. 2022; Paul and Meyer 2001). As a result, co-occurring anthropogenic pressures strongly affect and shape many small stream ecosystems, including their structure and functioning (Stubbington et al. 2022). However, disentangling the effects of individual stressors and identifying individual stressor-effect relationships in stream ecosystems is challenging due to the multitude of stressors (Weitere et al. 2021; Liess et al. 2021). This highlights a research gap in understanding how multiple environmental variables, including anthropogenic stressors, interact with and potentially influence biotic characteristics in small stream ecosystems.

Despite their abundance, ecological importance, and vulnerability to anthropogenic stressors, small streams are often not included in comprehensive biological and chemical monitoring programs such as the European Water Framework Directive (Weisner et al. 2022; Kelly-Quinn et al. 2023). Holistic monitoring studies focusing on abiotic parameters as well as in-stream and riparian biological communities, which reflect environmental conditions integrated over time, are thus needed. We present here the results of a monitoring program conducted over four years at 18 sites in two small stream catchments in southern Palatinate, southwest Germany. This study was conducted in a region with highly contrasting land use, that is streams originate in forested areas of a UNESCO biosphere reserve and subsequently flow through an anthropogenic landscape (particularly viticulture and agriculture) with various anthropogenic pressures on stream and riparian habitats. The objectives of this study were to (i) quantify the variety of anthropogenic stressors – including pesticide exposure – acting on both the aquatic and riparian habitats along small streams, (ii) analyse and understand the relationships between multiple environmental variables including anthropogenic stressors and biotic characteristics of small stream ecosystems, and (iii) evaluate potential effects of anthropogenic stressors on the structural and functional integrity of small streams.

Methods

Stream Sites and Land Use Variables

The study area is located in Rhineland-Palatinate (southwestern Germany), encompassing the UNESCO Palatinate Forest-North Vosges Biosphere Reserve and the Upper Rhine valley (Fig. 1). Overall, 26 environmental, biological, and anthropogenic stressor variables were assessed at 18 selected stream sites between September 2018 and July 2021. Of these, four variables referred to the landscape level, 15 were determined within the aquatic ecosystem compartment, and seven were measured in the riparian area. Due to logistical and financial constraints, as well as the complexity inherent in multi-stressor assessments, not all variables could be surveyed simultaneously or consistently across all sites (see Study limitations section in the Discussion). The 18 sampling sites were located in the two catchments “Queich” and “Hainbach” (for main characteristics see Table S1), with nine sites sampled in each catchment (Fig. 1; for all stream site characteristics see Table S2). All streams originate in the Palatinate Forest (except site 4) and are mainly small, fine substrate-dominated siliceous highland streams. Thus, geology and climate are comparable between the streams, with all streams in the Queich system (except site 4) and the Hainbach having near-natural forested upstream catchments. Overall, the 18 stream sites were located within four major land use regions, that is, forest (5 sites), viticulture (6 sites), grassland (3 sites) and agriculture (i.e., vegetable farming; 4 sites) (Fig. 1).

Sites 1–9 are located in the Queich catchment and sites 10–18 are located in the Hainbach catchment

For all 18 stream sites, we classified the land use within a 500 m radial buffer (Aviron et al. 2018; Weibull and Östmann 2003) using geographic information systems (GIS) and Corine Land Cover maps for the reference year 2018 (European Union 2023). The following five land use classifications were considered: urban (including also industrial and recreational areas), agriculture, viticulture and orchards, meadows and grassland, and forest. For each sampling site, the percentage surface area of each land use type was calculated within the buffer. Afterwards, we combined the land use types into a single factor per site called land use index (LUI; Feckler et al. 2014). In brief, the share of each land use type was ranked using a scoring system based on its percentage proportion (not present = 0, 0.1–5.0% = 1, 5.1–12.5% = 2, 12.6–31.3% = 3, 31.4–78.1% = 4 and ≥ 78.2% = 5) and weighted according to its expected impact on habitat quality of the stream sites. Land use types with no or low likelihood to exert negative effects (grassland and forest) were weighted positively, whereas land use types with an expected negative impact (agriculture, viticulture, and urban) were weighted negatively (von der Ohe and Goedkoop 2013; Feckler et al. 2014). Finally, we summed up single land-use type scores to calculate the respective LUIs.

Aquatic Parameters

The structural quality of the stream sites was mapped by on-site surveys in October 2018 (15 sites) and May 2019 (3 sites). We used an index-based evaluation method proposed by the German “Working Group of the Federal States on water issues” (“Länderarbeitsgemeinschaft Wasser”; Zumbroich and Müller 1999), which includes assessments of the water body morphology, routing of the water body, longitudinal and cross profiles, water body bed and bank structure, and surrounding environment. The water bodies were classified into seven structural quality classes from 1 (unmodified) to 7 (heavily modified). We determined the width of all stream sites in September 2019. Physico-chemical water parameters (i.e., water temperature, dissolved oxygen, conductivity and pH) were measured at all stream sites on 08 October 2018, 22 May 2019, 20 May 2020, 03 June 2020 and 19 May 2021 using a portable analytical device (WTW Multi 3630 IDS). We classified the stream sites regarding their drying-out potential based on expert surveys (e.g., foresters, anglers, authorities, scientists) into (i) permanent water bodies (no drying out) and (ii) water bodies subjected to temporary drying-out. We further used another category termed “possible drying out” for sites where drying-out seemed likely or could not be ruled out, as these sites were in proximity and/or downstream of sites with drying-out events and where very low water levels have been observed (particularly in summer 2020). Last, we classified the stream sites regarding their potential influence by wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) effluents (Link et al. 2017). Sites 1–4 in the Queich catchment and sites 10–12 in the Hainbach catchment had no WWTP located upstream, whereas the other stream sites were impacted by WWTP effluents (Supporting Information Table S2). We classified the WWTP impact as moderate for sites with WWTPs located in the upstream catchment but not directly located at a given site and as high in cases where a site was located directly downstream of WWTP discharge (i.e., less than 200 m downstream).

Pesticide Pollution of the Stream Sites

To quantify pesticide pollution at the sites we combined two methods: (i) pesticide analysis in the water phase after run-off events (Supporting Methods S1) and (ii) pesticide analysis in autotrophic biofilms as a time-integrating sampling method (Supporting Methods S2).

For the pesticide analysis in the water phase, water samples were collected on 07 August 2019 at seven stream sites (2, 6, 9, 11, 14, 16, 17; additional sites had to be omitted due to resource constraints and logistical challenges) following a heavy rainfall event using event-triggered bottle samplers according to Liess et al. (2021). In brief, two 1-L brown glass bottles were horizontally fixed to a metal stake in the middle of the stream at a given sampling site with the bottle openings approximately 10 and 20 cm above the base flow water level. Bottle lids prevented precipitation from entering the bottles, while a free inflow of stream water was provided over approximately 2 h. The bottles were filled passively by the rising water level during the rainfall event and retrieved within 12 h after the event. The highest filled bottle was taken for pesticide analysis, assuming that the peak concentration of pesticides coincided with the increasing water level (Halbach et al. 2021; Liess et al. 2021). After field collection, water samples were stored at -20 °C until pesticide extraction. The samples were analysed by LC-HRMS for 22 pesticides (17 fungicides and 5 insecticides) currently being approved for use in viticulture and agriculture in the sampling region and have previously been detected in small streams of the study area (Bereswill et al. 2012; Fernandez et al. 2014). Detailed information on sample treatment and analysis are given in the Supporting Information (Supporting Methods S1).

We used regulatory acceptable concentrations (RACs) defined by the German Environment Agency (UBA; https://webetox.uba.de/webETOX/public/basics/literatur.do? id=24707) for an ecotoxicological evaluation of detected pesticide concentrations. RACs are defined within the regulatory risk assessment for pesticide authorization and denote official threshold levels that must not be exceeded to exclude unacceptable ecological effects (see Stehle and Schulz (2015a) for details). We compared individual pesticide concentrations detected at the seven stream sites to the respective RACs and calculated the number of RAC exceedances per site. Furthermore, we evaluated the mixture toxicity of the pesticides detected at each site. In detail, we compared the water phase concentrations of a given sample to the respective tier-I threshold levels (i.e., ecotoxicity values including assessment factors) for the three taxonomic groups considered in the EU regulatory risk assessment (i.e., fishes, invertebrates, primary producers). We calculated tier-I threshold levels by dividing the lowest acute L(E)C50 values (compiled from Lewis et al. (2016) for fish, macroinvertebrates, as well as green algae/macrophytes by their respective assessment factor (i.e., 100 in the case of fish and invertebrates and ten in the case of primary producers; EFSA 2013). The L(E)C50 denote the concentration of a chemical that is expected to cause a defined effect such as mortality in 50% of the tested population (Lewis et al. 2016). The toxicity of the pesticide mixture present at each site was calculated separately for each respective taxonomic group by summing up the concentration to tier-I threshold level ratios for all the pesticides detected in the water phase sample to obtain the risk quotient of the mixture (RQmix) for a given taxonomic group (Stehle and Schulz 2015b):

where Concentrationi is the measured concentration of the pesticide i in a given sample and TLi is the acute tier-I threshold level for a given taxonomic group (fishes, aquatic invertebrates, green algae/macrophytes) of the pesticide i. RQmix < 1 indicates an acceptable risk for a specific taxonomic group.

Besides water samples, we additionally quantified the pesticide exposure of the 18 stream sites using autotrophic biofilm samples (Mahler et al. 2020). In essence, we deployed six unglazed ceramic tiles (4.6 × 4.6 cm) as biofilm colonization substrate for approximately 7 weeks (02 June 2021–23 July 2021) during the pesticide application period at each stream site. The tiles were mounted in a coarse mesh cage (mesh size = 10 mm) made of stainless steel-wire to prevent them from getting washed away. The cages were fixed with metal poles approximately 5–10 cm below the water surface in the centre of each stream site. Prior to their usage, all tiles and cages were cleaned and burned at 300 °C for 6 h to eliminate microbial or chemical contamination. After collection from the streams, the tiles were carefully rinsed with stream water to remove loose material (e.g., sediment or plant material) and stored at -20 °C before further processing. Tiles could be retrieved from ten out of the 18 sites (2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12, 13, 16); at the other eight sites, the cages with the tiles were either washed away by increased flow during heavy rain events or destroyed by vandalism. Biofilm samples were analysed for 90 pesticides, including predominantly current-use insecticides, fungicides and herbicides using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled to a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer by electrospray ionization (UHPLC-ESI-MS/MS). Detailed information on sample treatment and analysis of biofilm samples is given in the Supporting Information (Supporting Methods S2 and Table S3).

We classified the pesticide exposure of the 14 sites with water phase (n = 7 sites) or biofilm (n = 10 sites) pesticide concentrations in pesticide pollution categories. This was done to unify the different pesticide information available for the individual sites, particularly for statistical analyses (see below). In brief, we defined four pesticide pollution categories for the water-phase concentrations as follows: 1: low (no RAC exceedance and RQmix_invertebrates < 0.1); 2: moderate (max. 1 RAC exceedance and RQmix_invertebrates between 0.1 and 1); 3: high (max. 3 RAC exceedances and RQmix_invertebrates between 1 and 100); 4: very high (> 3 RAC exceedances and RQmix_invertebrates > 100). For biofilms, the definitions of the four categories were as follows: 1: low (sum pesticide concentration in biofilms < 10 ng/g); 2: moderate (sum pesticide concentration in biofilms of 10–100 ng/g); 3: high (sum pesticide concentration in biofilms of 100–500 ng/g); 4: very high (sum pesticide concentration in biofilms of > 500 ng/g). We used RQmix for invertebrates for pollution category classification as the highest risks of pesticide water phase concentrations were found for this group of organisms, and aquatic macroinvertebrates are known to be highly susceptible towards pesticide exposure (Schäfer 2019; Lauper et al. 2021). Also, we found a strong positive relationship between RQmix_invertebrates and sum concentrations in biofilms for the three sites (2, 11, 16) with data on both water phase and biofilm pesticide concentrations (Fig. S1).

Fishes

Fish communities (including lamprey [Petromyzontidae]) were surveyed at 16 out of the 18 sites in September 2019 (site 1 in December 2019). No survey took place at site 3 due to equipment failure. Site 4 was fully dried out and thus no fish were found. Fish were captured using backpack electrofishing. We used two electrofishers (EFGI 650, Bretschneider Spezialelektronik, Chemnitz, Germany) wading side by side in the larger stream sites 5–9, and one electrofisher in the other smaller stream sites. Fish were collected in sufficiently large water containers at each site during electrofishing. Immediately afterwards, the species were identified and the fish released unharmed. We determined the species richness and, as an approximate standardized indicator of abundances, the number of individuals per species and estimated area fished [m2] for each site.

Aquatic Macroinvertebrates

Aquatic macroinvertebrates were sampled between 20 and 30 May 2020 at 15 out of the 18 sites using standardized multi-habitat sampling of 20 subsamples each covering 0.125 m2 along an approx. 30 m stream stretch (Meier et al. 2006). In essence, a Surber sampler was placed at the stream bottom with the opening against the current. Aquatic organisms were dislodged from the substrate by physical agitation within the metal frame of the net, and all invertebrates were transferred into a labelled plastic jar and preserved with 90% vol. ethanol. At each stream site, the collected invertebrates were combined into one sample per site. This sampling procedure was repeated for all stream sites by the same operators, allowing the comparability among samples. Samples were returned to the laboratory for sorting and individuals were subsequently identified to the species level. We determined species richness and abundances for the different stream sites.

Merolimnic Insect Emergence

We sampled the emergence of merolimnic insects using floating pyramidal tents made of nylon mesh with a basal area of 0.25 m2 as emergence traps (Cadmus et al. 2016). Two emergence traps were anchored to the stream bottom at each sampling site except for site 3 due to restricted right of access in 2020. Emergence traps were installed from 27 May–10 June 2020 and 24 June–01 July 2020 and emptied once per week, that is 3 times in total. Emerged insects were collected in plastic bottles that were connected to the top of the traps and filled with ethylene glycol as preservative. Upon collection, aquatic insects were stored in ethanol (70% vol.) and subsequently identified to the order level. We determined the order richness and the abundances for the different stream sites.

Aquatic Leaf Litter Decomposition

We evaluated overall aquatic leaf litter decomposition (microbially- and shredder-mediated) at the 18 stream sites using the litterbag method (Benfield et al. 2017). In brief, coarse-mesh (mesh size = 10 mm) litterbags were filled with approximately 4 g (weighed to the nearest 0.0001 g) of oven-dried (at 60 °C for 24 h) alder leaves (Alnus glutinosa (L.) Gaertn.), which are commonly used to assess leaf litter decomposition in aquatic ecosystem (e.g., Voß et al. 2015). Subsequently, litterbags (1 bag per site) were submerged at all stream sites between 29 April–17 June 2020. After retrieval, the remaining leaf material was gently rinsed under running water to remove mineral particles and macroinvertebrates, oven-dried at 60 °C for 24 h and re-weighed to the nearest 0.0001 g. Riparian Parameters

Biotope and Vegetation Mapping

We mapped the biotope structure of the banks along the stream sites by on-site surveys in September 2018 (15 sites) and May 2019 (3 sites) following the official guidelines of the federal state Rhineland-Palatinate (Cordes and Conze 2018). For each site, we mapped the biotope types (i.e., distinct, homogeneous units with same environmental characteristics) in two 20 m parallel segments along the stream banks characterizing the riparian areas. Using the information from the biotope mapping, we calculated the number of occurring biotope types per site as proxy for the habitat diversity. Tree cover was estimated as the % canopy cover in the two 20 m segments. Vegetation was identified once per site between 13 and 23 May 2020. At each site, we recorded all vascular plants in four 2 × 2 m plots placed around the pitfall traps (see below). All vascular plants were identified to the species level or, when not possible as for example for non-flowering grasses, to the genus level.

Carabid Beetles

Carabid beetles were collected with pitfall traps during five sampling campaigns conducted in May, June, September/October 2018 as well as in May and June 2019. In total, four pitfall traps were installed for 14 days during each sampling campaign at both banks of the 18 stream sites. On both sides, the pitfall traps were placed 20 m apart, with one trap placed directly next to the stream channel and one trap in approximately 2 m away from the stream. The pitfall traps consisted of a plastic cup (6.5 cm diameter) filled with ethylene glycol diluted 1:2 (v: v) with water and a drop of detergent. At the end of each sampling campaign, the pitfall traps’ contents were sieved and the ground beetles were stored in ethanol (70% vol.). Ground beetles were subsequently identified to species level according to Müller-Motzfeld (2004).

Terrestrial Litter Decomposition

The terrestrial litter decomposition was assessed using the tea bag approach (Keuskamp et al. 2013). In brief, one green tea bag and one rooibos tea bag (brand “Lipton”) were buried pairwise at a depth of ten cm at two sites (20 m apart) located at both banks (distance approx. 50 cm to the water) of the streams. The bags were buried for 28 d from 07 May–04 June 2019. After retrieval, adhered soil particles were gently removed, and the tea bags were oven-dried for 72 h at 65 °C and weighed to the nearest 0.0001 g. Statistical Analyses

For statistical analyses, we always used mean values for variables with multiple measurements at different time points (i.e., water temperature, dissolved oxygen, conductivity, pH, merolimnic insect emergence, carabid beetles).

In a first step, we conducted multiple pairwise correlations among all 21 quantitative variables (Table S1), resulting in a total of 210 pairwise comparisons. We calculated Spearman’s product moment correlation coefficients (r) and a pairwise correlation was considered significant if its 90% confidence interval excluded zero. To inspect patterns in the dataset, the correlation matrix was ordered by hierarchical clustering using Ward´s minimum variance method based on sum of squared errors.

In a second step, we used factor analysis of mixed data (FAMD) to explore relationships in our dataset containing both quantitative (numerical) and qualitative (categorical) variables (Pagès 2004) using the package ‘FactoMineR’ version 1.34 (Le et al. 2008). FAMD is a method that combines principal component analyses (PCA) for quantitative variables and multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) for qualitative variables. In a preliminary step before performing FAMD, we imputed missing values because our dataset contained missing entries. Therefore, we used the regularized iterative FAMD algorithm with the command ‘imputeFAMD’ (package missMDA; Josse et al. 2016). All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2 (R Core Team 2021). We considered P < 0.05 as statistically significant results.

Results

Overall Patterns of Anthropogenic Stressors and Abiotic and Biotic Parameters

We observed general patterns for the abiotic parameters and anthropogenic stressors from the upstream sites located in forested areas to the agriculturally-influenced downstream sites in both the Queich and the Hainbach catchment (Supporting Information Table S1 and S2, Supporting Results S4 and S5, Table S4–S6, Fig. S2 and Fig. S3).

In brief, water temperature and conductivity increased from upstream to downstream sites, while dissolved oxygen concentrations decreased. Also, upstream sites of both catchments were permanent, whereas particularly the downstream sites in the Hainbach (i.e., sites 12–18) were subjected to drying out events. Pollution pressure from pesticides and WWTP effluents also generally increased along both stream systems. For pesticides, RAC exceedances (5 out of 7 sites), mixture toxicity evaluations (4 out of 7 sites with RQmix > 1) and biofilm results indicated highest exposure and risks for the agricultural sites of both catchments (Table 1; Supporting Results S4 and S5, Table S4 and S5).

The biotic parameters, that is, abundances and taxa richness of fishes, aquatic invertebrates and carabid beetles and aquatic and riparian litter decomposition, showed overall high variations among sites and catchments (Supporting Information Table S1 and S2, Figs. Correlation Analysis

We found significant correlations (all |r| > 0.48) in 35 of the 210 pairwise comparisons of the quantitative variables (Fig. 2). Of these, 21 correlations were positive, and 14 were negative. Significant correlations were frequent between aquatic variables (n = 19), and less frequent between aquatic-riparian variables (n = 7) and between aquatic variables and LUI (n = 7). For aquatic variables, the strongest correlations were found between stream width and fish species richness (r = 0.75, P < 0.001), dissolved oxygen concentrations and specific conductivity (r = − 0.94, P < 0.001), as well as specific conductivity and both water temperature (r = 0.67, P < 0.001) and pH values (r = 0.74, P < 0.001). There was only one significant correlation between a riparian variable, namely decomposition of green tea, and the LUI (r = 0.57, P = 0.007). Regarding correlations between riparian variables, we found only one positive pairwise comparison between carabid species richness and abundances. Frequent significant correlations were found with the LUI (n = 8 correlations), dissolved oxygen concentration (n = 7), aquatic macroinvertebrate abundances (n = 6), specific conductivity (n = 6) and water pH (n = 6).

The five dimensions of the FAMD explained together 74% of the dataset variation, with the first and second dimension explaining 27.3 and 15.9%, respectively, of the observed variation (Fig. 3). Variables that contribute most to the first dimension were region, risk for drying out, LUI, dissolved oxygen concentration, pesticide pollution, conductivity and water temperature (Fig. 3). For the second dimension, the variables contributing most were stream width, region, species richness of fish, abundance of emerging insects, pesticide pollution, catchment size and WWTP influence (Fig. 3).

Relationships between the quantitative variables and their correlations with the first two dimensions can be seen in Fig. 4a. Mainly associated with dimension one, we found positive correlations between LUI, dissolved oxygen concentrations, abundance of aquatic macroinvertebrates and aquatic leaf litter decomposition. These variables were negatively correlated with water temperature, conductivity, pH, and decreasing structural quality of the stream. For dimension two, we mainly found positive correlations between the species richness of fish, stream width and abundance of emerging insects.

The FAMD clustered the stream sites into four groups based on land use, anthropogenic stressor influences and biotic and abiotic parameters (Figs. 4a, b and 5a). The first group (“forest”) consists of near-natural Queich and Hainbach forest sites (i.e., sites 1, 2, 3, 10, 11) associated with low pesticide pollution and no WWTP influences, high dissolved oxygen concentrations, low water temperatures, pH and conductivity, high tree cover and high invertebrate abundance and diversity (i.e., high species richness and abundances of aquatic macroinvertebrates and high taxa richness of merolimnic insect emergence) (Figs. 4a, b and 5a). The second group (“grassland”; sites 7–9) consists of wide and permanent Queich stream sites located in the grassland region. These sites are characterized by moderate pesticide pollution and WWTP pollution influences, a high LUI score, high fish species richness and high litter decomposition in the water phase and the riparian areas (Figs. 4a, b and 5a). The third group includes sites located in the viticulture region (“viticulture”; sites 4, 5, 6, 14, 12) and are characterized by high pesticide pollution, moderate to high WWTP influences, a low LUI score, high abundance and taxa richness of carabid beetles and a high litter decomposition in the riparian area considering Rooibos tea samples (Figs. 4a, b and 5a). Finally, the fourth group consists of sites located in the agricultural vegetable farming region (“agriculture”; sites 15–18) which are associated with very high pesticide pollution, a potential for drying out, high water temperatures, conductivity and pH, low morphological structural quality, a low LUI score, as well as overall low leaf litter decomposition and aquatic invertebrate taxa richness and abundance (Figs. 4a, b and 5a). Overall, stream sites of the different regions (forest, viticulture, grassland, agriculture) and for the two catchments (Queich, Hainbach) showed very similar profiles as indicated in the factor maps (Fig. 5a, b).

Discussion

General Patterns of Anthropogenic Stressors Along the Streams

Although our study area spans just 30 km east–west and 12 km north–south (Fig. 1), the stream sites varied markedly in land use, hydromorphology, and pesticide contamination. Upstream sites in the Queich and Hainbach catchments lie within near-natural forested areas of the UNESCO Palatinate Forest–North Vosges biosphere reserve and generally exhibit high structural quality (Landesamt für Umwelt Rheinland-Pfalz 2025). In contrast, downstream sites in the Upper Rhine Valley are heavily impacted by anthropogenic pressures, including intensive agriculture (low LUI scores) and physical modifications. Additionally, small Hainbach streams in agricultural and viticulture zones were uniquely prone to summer drying, mainly due to irrigation-related water extraction (Struktur- und Genehmigungsdirektion Süd 2022).

Pesticides were found at all stream sites, including those within the Palatinate Forest. As expected, pesticide concentrations – and consequently risks (i.e., no RAC exceedances and RQmix < 1) – were substantially lower at near-natural upstream sites compared to agriculturally influenced downstream sites, which are subject to direct runoff and spray drift (Bereswill et al. 2012). Given the absence of pesticide use in the upstream forested catchments, the contamination of forest sites is likely to result from the aerial transport of pesticides from the downstream viticulture areas (2–10 km from the forest sites; Fig. 1), as also suggested by Schneeweiss et al. (2022). Exceedances of the RACs and the pesticide mixture toxicity evaluations indicate a high risk of adverse effects on stream biodiversity (Liess et al. 2021) and ecosystem functioning (Schäfer et al. 2012), particularly at the agricultural sites. Biofilm samples, which provide an integrated measure of pesticide exposure over longer time periods (Mahler et al. 2020), revealed that Queich stream sites generally experienced exposure to a higher number of pesticide compounds at lower concentrations than Hainbach sites. This is due to the larger catchment of the Queich, where there is potentially more diverse pesticide application, resulting in a higher number of accumulated pesticides in biofilms. At the same time, the smaller water volume of the Hainbach sites, coupled with agricultural areas bordering the banks of the Hainbach directly, results in a lower dilution potential in the water phase and consequently higher concentrations of pesticides in biofilm found at these sites. However, these observations contradict previous studies, which found no correlation between catchment size and the number of detected pesticides (Schreiner et al. 2016), but with the toxicity (Schreiner et al. 2021). Although it is difficult to link these directly to ecotoxicological risks, increased pesticide concentrations in biofilms have been used as a useful proxy for ecological risks in explaining benthic community structure metrics in agricultural streams (Mahler et al. 2020).

In addition to agricultural pollution, the downstream sites in the Queich catchment and the Hainbach face pollution from WWTP discharges, which often contain mixtures of numerous chemicals such as pharmaceuticals, disinfectants and personal care products (Kümmerer 2009; Munz et al. 2017). Importantly, WWTP discharges lead to a continuous chemical exposure in downstream stretches, highlighting the potential for chronic effects already at low concentrations (Fabbri 2015).

Relationships Between Aquatic and Riparian Variables of Small Streams and Anthropogenic Stressors

Relevance of Land Use Region, Environmental Variables, and Anthropogenic Influences for Ecosystem Structure and Functioning

Our results show marked differences in abiotic conditions and aquatic/riparian ecosystem structure and function across stream sites in the four land-use regions. FAMD ordination primarily reflected site geography and the type and extent of anthropogenic impact – near-natural, uncontaminated upstream sites in the Palatinate Forest contrasted with agriculturally and viticulturally impacted downstream sites in the Rhine valley. Land use emerged as a key factor influencing both abiotic and biotic stream variables. Physico-chemical parameters were strongly interrelated and indicative of anthropogenic influence, with, for example, dissolved oxygen decreasing and conductivity and temperature increasing along the Queich and Hainbach due to rising agricultural, urban land use, and WWTP effluents.

In terms of biological responses, aquatic macroinvertebrate abundance increased with higher LUI scores and dissolved oxygen concentrations but declined with increasing anthropogenic pressure, reflected by elevated conductivity, water temperature and pH. Natural land-use and associated favourable physico-chemical conditions thus supported aquatic macroinvertebrates. We also found a positive correlation between LUI and aquatic and riparian litter decomposition, suggesting enhanced aquatic and riparian ecosystem functioning under more natural conditions. These results align with previous studies (Miserendino et al. 2011; Weijters et al. 2009), which report improved stream ecosystem structure and functioning with natural land use and reduced leaf litter decomposition under anthropogenic influence (Schreiner et al. 2023). Lower structural quality of stream sites was associated with reduced aquatic macroinvertebrate richness, highlighting the importance of morphological habitat quality for stream biodiversity (Zerega et al. 2021). However, cross-regional studies suggest that agricultural land-use and diffuse pollution have even stronger impacts than hydro-morphological alterations (Hering et al. 2006; Turunen et al. 2016; Liess et al. 2021). In contrast, carabid abundance and richness showed no clear link to environmental variables or anthropogenic stressors, suggesting that local factors such as temperature and soil moisture – unmeasured here – may play a greater role (Schirmel and Buchholz 2012).

Effects of pesticide pollution on biological endpoints

Pesticide pollution contributed strongly to the explained variance in biological endpoints. Sites with high pesticide-related toxicity generally showed low fish abundance or absence. This supports findings from Switzerland linking pesticides to fish declines (Wermelinger 2019) and a reported threefold increase in fish-toxic pesticide applications in German agriculture from 1995 to 2019 (Bub et al. 2022). Similarly, low aquatic macroinvertebrate abundance at agricultural Hainbach sites correlated with high pesticide levels and RQmix (invertebrates) values. Our results align with broader studies (Stehle and Schulz 2015a; Liess et al. 2021), confirming pesticide impacts on aquatic communities. We also observed reduced leaf litter decomposition at agricultural and viticultural sites with elevated pesticide pollution, consistent with studies showing pesticide-related declines in decomposition (Rasmussen et al. 2012; Schäfer et al. 2012). Frequent detection of fungicides – known to harm fungi and microbes critical for decomposition (Artigas et al. 2012)— – ikely contributed to this effect.

Implication of stream width and flow conditions for aquatic communities

Stream size and risk for drying out were key drivers of biological responses. Temporary drying-out events cause major habitat disruption and strongly affect aquatic communities, particularly on less mobile species (Tolonen et al. 2019). For instance, low aquatic macroinvertebrate abundances were observed at Hainbach sites that experienced drying. In line with that, fish species richness was highest in the larger, permanently flowing sections of the Queich and absent at sites that dried out or likely did. Larger, permanent streams offer greater habitat complexity (e.g., substrate variation) and connectivity to tributary streams, supporting higher fish diversity (Hugueny et al. 2010; Oberdorff et al. 2011). Similarly, merolimnic insect abundance was positively correlated with stream width, highlighting the importance of stable flow regimes for aquatic insect communities.

Study limitations

Although ecological field monitoring is essential for understanding and managing ecosystems, the limitations and uncertainties of our study must be acknowledged. First, our assessment of biological endpoints and stressor variables relied partly on single or annual measurements, and some environmental variables were measured at different time points and not consistently across all sites. Consequently, short-term temporal variability or episodic events may not have been fully captured. However, several of the investigated stressor variables (e.g., land use, WWTP pollution) are comparatively stable over time and therefore likely reflect prevailing site conditions. Moreover, biological communities in small streams integrate environmental conditions over extended periods and may thus be less sensitive to short-term temporal mismatches between measurements. Second, due to logistical and financial constraints, we focused on selected biological and environmental parameters, potentially overlooking other relevant stressors or ecological responses. Third, our study was conducted in only two catchments in southwestern Germany, and the study sites were thus not fully independent. While the findings of our study offer valuable insights, they may not be fully generalizable to streams in other regions with different environmental conditions, land-use patterns, or stressor dynamics. Future studies with higher temporal resolution, broader parameter coverage, and multi-regional comparisons would help to address these limitations and strengthen the applicability of such assessments.

Conclusion

We monitored a range of aquatic and riparian variables in two stream catchments with contrasting land use and anthropogenic stressor impacts. Downstream sections were heavily affected by land use changes, hydro-morphological alterations, and pollution from agriculture and WWTPs, negatively impacting ecosystem structure and function. In contrast, upstream sites within the Palatinate Forest–North Vosges Biosphere Reserve were far less impacted, highlighting the role of protected areas in mitigating anthropogenic pressures and preserving ecological integrity. Our findings show that multiple stressors – such as pesticides, WWTP effluents, drying-out events, and morphological degradation – often co-occur. Consequently, both future stream monitoring programs and restoration efforts should adopt an integrated, multi-stressor approach to effectively address the complex pressures affecting small stream ecosystems and to support biodiversity.
