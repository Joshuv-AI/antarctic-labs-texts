Lyme disease presenting with erythema migrans: First reported case in a returning traveler to the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia
Authors: Mazin Barry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lyme disease is a tick-borne disease caused by Borrelia burgdorferi that often presents with a solitary rash at the bite site, which may have central clearing, giving a “Bull’s-eye” appearance, otherwise known as erythema migrans (EM). The disease is endemic in North America and Europe; however, cases have been reported in non-endemic areas in returning travelers. In the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), one case of EM was reported based on clinical suspicion with no laboratory confirmation; however, there have been no previous reported cases of Lyme disease in returning travelers. Here we present the first case in KSA in a healthy woman presenting with EM after returning from travel to Scotland, where the disease is endemic.

Introduction

Lyme disease (LD) is an anthropozoonosis, a multisystem infection transmitted by ticks, usually belonging to the genus Ixodes, and is caused by a spirochete, Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato (Bbsl). The most common symptom of infection is a red-expanding rash that appears at the bite site, which may clear centrally. The rash does not appear immediately, but after an incubation period of 4–30 days, it is known as erythema migrans (EM) and represents the most common presentation of the disease [1]. The rash, which appears immediately after the tick bite, is a chemical reaction to the tick's saliva. EM is the early, localized phase of Lyme disease, present in 75 % of cases [2]. Lyme disease is the most common tick-borne disease in the Northern Hemisphere [3], which is endemic in North America and Europe [4]. Travel-associated Lyme disease has rarely been reported and is often linked to travel to the United States of America (USA) or Europe [5]. No previous cases of Lyme disease have been reported in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) in returning travelers. Here we present a young woman presenting with EM after travelling to Scotland.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 33-year-old woman presented to the Infectious Diseases outpatient clinic in Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), with a single red skin lesion in her leg behind her left knee for 21 days. The lesion was neither itchy nor painful. She had no other rashes in her body, nor did she have any fever, headache, joint pain, or other systemic symptoms. Two months before her illness, in July and August, she traveled to Scotland, where she had hiked in the Scottish Highlands in tall grassy areas while wearing shorts without tall socks, boots, or any use of arthropod repellent, including diethyltoluamide (DEET), picaridin, or permethrin. One month later, while still in Scotland, she noticed an engorged tick embedded in her posterior left leg, which she self-removed, which she described as a tiny wingless insect. At the time, the rash was only at the bite site but slowly expanded over the three weeks till her presentation.

On physical exam, she appeared generally well, with normal vital signs. The posterior left leg showed a well-demarcated, erythematous, large oval-shaped rash, 15 cm× 10 cm, with slight clearing in the upper edge (Fig. 1). The lesion was warm but was not tender. No other rashes were detected, and no joint swelling was observed. Given the rash with some characteristics of EM, the known tick bite, and travel to an endemic area, Lyme disease with erythema migrans was clinically suspected. An Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) IgM and Western blot were positive for Borrelia burgdorferi IgM, and negative for IgG. She was started on doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for a planned 14-day course and was advised about possible photosensitivity and to use sun protection. Discussion

The case highlights the presentation of Lyme disease in non-endemic regions after travel. The current case was hiking in high-risk areas in the Scottish Highlands with exposed skin. She identified a tick, which she managed to self-remove, but developed a slowly expanding rash at the bite site. It is recommended to promptly remove attached ticks by mechanical means using clean, fine-tipped tweezers or a similar device inserted between the tick body and the skin, as ticks need to be attached for 36–48 h to transmit the infection; however, she did not know for how long the tick was attached to the back of her leg before removing it. Upon presentation, the characteristic rash behind her knee was suspicious for erythema migrans (EM), in addition to the known tick bite and travel to an endemic area. Although a clinical diagnosis is usually sufficient, as seropositivity may be difficult to determine whether it is from a past infection versus a current one for individuals living in an endemic area, or due to cross-reactivity or other autoimmune diseases; serological tests were sent, which helped confirm the diagnosis in a returning traveler. There have been no previous confirmed reports of Lyme disease, EM, or Borrelia burgdorferi infection in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), suggesting the acquisition of the infection during her travel. A case report from Dammam, in KSA, of a man presenting with a clinical suspicion EM and no travel, did not have any laboratory confirmation, and biopsy of the lesion examined by dark field smears showed 0–3 per high power fields of motile spirochetes with lashing movements, but the microscope set used was not equipped with micro-photography facilities [6].

The number of cases of Lyme disease in the United Kingdom (UK) has been on the rise [7]. A recent retrospective analysis of data from the National Health Service (NHS) Highland (North) found that the estimated annual incidence of suspected or confirmed Lyme disease in Scotland was 362 per 100,000 population, suggesting a high burden of disease [8]. Other countries in Europe, like France and Poland, have 40–80 per 100,000 population per year [9]. Surveillance data on ticks attached to dogs in the UK found that the incidence of Borrelia burgdorferi infection was 481 per 100,000 dogs [10].

Case reports of Lyme disease in non-endemic countries linked to travel have been reported, including a case in Japan of a healthy woman presenting with EM after traveling to the USA, in which three days before presentation, she was camping in the forest of Milwaukee, Wisconsin, and was bitten by a tick two weeks before [11]. Another case related to traveling to the USA was recently reported in Peru [12]. As well as a case in Turkey, in an elderly woman who presented with myalgia and EM after traveling to the USA [13]. Lyme disease has also been reported in a Brazilian traveler who returned from Germany [14].

In the USA and Western Europe, the estimated annual number of cases exceeds 475,000 and 200,000, respectively [15], [16], [17]. However, there are differences between the disease in the two regions. In the USA, the tick vectors are Ixodes scapularis and I. pacificus. In Europe, the vectors are I. ricinus and I. persulcatus. In addition, the Lyme borrelia in the USA are mostly Borrelia burgdorferi sensu stricto, but B. mayonii may occur in the upper midwestern USA, while Lyme borrelia in Europe are mostly B. afzelii and B. garinii, as well as several other less common species that may induce human disease, including B. burgdorferi sensu stricto, B. bavariensis, B. spielmanii, and B. lusitaniae [3]. Hence, due to the difference in vector and causative spirochete, clinical presentation may differ if the infection is acquired in the USA compared with Europe, and serology is more complex in Europe [18]. EM in patients acquiring the infection in the USA has a shorter incubation period from tick bite to EM development and is less likely to have central clearing at the time of diagnosis, the frequency of central clearing may depend on the duration of the EM lesion before the diagnosis, and the duration on average is longer in Europe than in the USA. In addition, within Europe, the percentage of multiple EM lesions is lower for adult patients than in the USA, and those infected with B. mayonii can exhibit multiple and very small EM lesions. Borrelial lymphocytoma is unique to Europe that appears as a small skin induration which slowly enlarge to a few centimeters of a solitary bluish-red nodule and is usually located on the breast, and is often accompanied by an EM lesion [19]. Acrodermatitis chronica atrophicans is also unique to Europe and is a late cutaneous manifestation of Lyme disease affecting the extensor parts of the distal extremities. It starts with a reddish-blue discoloration with swelling of the skin, that slowly enlarges, followed by atrophic changes which can occur several months to years later, and EM often precedes this manifestation [20]. The absence of these manifestations in the USA is because these skin infections are usually caused by B. afzelii. The identification of a tick bite at the skin-lesion site is reported to be 60–64 % in Europe, compared to only 25 % in the USA, central clearing is reported in 62–69 % in Europe, compared to 35 % in the USA, and systemic symptoms are reported in 38 % in Europe compared to 69 % in the USA [21], [22], [23]. The current case acquired her infection while traveling in Europe. She identified the tick bite and presented to the clinic with EM with partial clearing and no systemic symptoms.

In summary, the current case reports the first case of Lyme disease in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia after returning from travel to Scotland. Clinicians in non-endemic areas should have a high index of suspicion in returning travelers from Lyme-endemic areas presenting with a solitary rash resembling erythema migrans.
