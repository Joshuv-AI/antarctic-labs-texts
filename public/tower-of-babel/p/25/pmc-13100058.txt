Preparation of motion sensor using AgNWs material and performance analysis in sports activities
Authors: Huayi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-performance motion sensors are crucial for scientific training and health management. However, existing flexible strain sensors often face limitations in signal stability, response speed, and signal-to-noise ratio during dynamic monitoring, which hinders reliable high-precision data acquisition. To address these challenges, this study aimed to develop a novel strain sensor by focusing on optimizing the composite process of conductive materials and flexible substrates, thereby enhancing performance for practical human motion monitoring applications. This study fabricated a resistive flexible strain sensor using Silver Nanowires (AgNWs) as the conductive material and Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) as the flexible substrate. The sensor operates on the principle that strain induces changes in the conductive network’s connectivity. A key innovation in fabrication lies in using dimethyl silicone oil as a pre-dispersion medium, combined with an ultrasonic-assisted process to construct a uniform and robust three-dimensional AgNWs network within the PDMS matrix, ensuring strong interfacial adhesion and structural integrity. The experimental results demonstrated superior performance of the PDMS-based sensor. Compared to sensors based on Cellulose Nanofiber (CNF) and Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET) substrates, it exhibited a resistance fluctuation of less than 5% over 3,000 tensile cycles. At 60% strain, its response time was only 5,000 ms, approximately 50% faster than that of the CNF-based sensor. Furthermore, in the 0.5–1.5 Hz frequency range typical of human motion, the sensor achieved an excellent average signal-to-noise ratio of up to 26.27 dB. This research successfully developed an AgNWs/PDMS flexible strain sensor with outstanding dynamic stability, fast response, and high signal quality, providing a reliable sensing solution for high-precision data collection in sports training and health monitoring. The main innovation points include: (1) Adopting the interface engineering strategy of dimethyl silicone oil pre dispersion to construct a uniform and stable 3D AgNWs network; (2) Integrating spin coating and screen printing to achieve integrated manufacturing of sensors; (3) Achieving breakthroughs in comprehensive performance by linking sensor design and performance, and integrating signal processing.

Introduction

The development of digitization and intelligence has promoted motion monitoring technology, and high-performance motion sensors have become important tools for scientific training and health management. Silver Nanowires (AgNWs) provide an ideal material for preparing highly sensitive and stable motion sensors due to their excellent conductivity and flexibility1,2. However, the uniform distribution and stable adhesion of AgNWs in the sensor substrate remain key challenges in practical applications, directly affecting the sensitivity and repeatability of the sensor. Therefore, this study focuses on optimizing the composite process of AgNWs and flexible substrates, aiming to develop a high-performance strain sensor suitable for human motion monitoring3,4.

In recent years, the research on flexible sensor materials has been continuously advancing. Yao et al. used thermoplastic elastomer SEBS as the matrix and combined AgNWs with MXene to construct a composite conductive network. The prepared sensor achieved a sensitivity of 176.25 at 100% strain and achieved high linear detection in the 0.5–5.5% small strain and 5–50% large strain. It showed good stability in cyclic testing and preliminarily verified the potential application in human motion monitoring5. Wang, R et al. and Castillo-López, D et al. developed a sensor with a maximum strain sensitivity of 236 and stretchability up to 118% by combining AgNWs with graphene on a Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrate. The sensor exhibited fast response, good repeatability, and stability, demonstrating promising applications in wearable devices and intelligent robots6,7. Wang D et al. prepared deep eutectic ion conductive elastomers through photo-induced copolymerization, which had good stretchability, conductivity, and sensing properties, enriching the types of polymerizable deep eutectic solvent sensors8. Yan K et al. combined self-healing polyurethane with lithium salt to develop an ion conductive elastomer sensor that can simultaneously monitor pressure and temperature, distinguish different breathing modes, and provide a new strategy for real-time monitoring of human signs9. Although these studies have improved sensor performance, there is still room for further exploration in monitoring motion state signals. The existing flexible strain sensors have insufficient signal stability, limited response speed, and low Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) in dynamic monitoring. Although the AgNWs/MXene/SEBS sensor developed by Yao et al. has high sensitivity, its stability under long-term cyclic loading still needs to be verified. Although the AgNWs graphene PDMS sensor reported by Wang, R et al. and Castillo-López et al. responds quickly, the suppression of signal noise under complex motion has not been deeply explored yet. However, the research of Wang D et al. and Yan K et al. focuses on material functionalization and environmental adaptability, and there is still room for improvement in the accuracy and stability of motion state signal matching. The current research proposes a material and structural system centered around AgNWs/PDMS to address the aforementioned challenges, using PDMS substrate and optimizing its interface binding and network structure with AgNWs.

To sum up, a novel resistive flexible strain sensor is proposed by optimizing the dispersion process and conductive network structure of AgNWs in PDMS substrate. The innovations of this study are: (1) A novel interface engineering strategy utilizes dimethyl silicone oil as a pre-dispersion medium, combined with ultrasonic-assisted and stepwise drying processes, to construct a uniform and robust three-dimensional AgNWs conductive network within the PDMS matrix, effectively addressing nanowire aggregation and weak interfacial adhesion; (2) An integrated manufacturing route that combines spin-coating for homogeneous sensitive layer formation with screen printing of electrodes, optimized based on Computer-aided Design (CAD) curvature and pattern design to achieve single-chip manufacturing of high-performance sensing elements and skin conformal electrodes; (3) An enhanced sensing mechanism and system integration approach that quantitatively correlates sensor width and curvature with resistance response, and incorporates real-time signal processing algorithms with multi-joint synchronous monitoring, thereby achieving a breakthrough in comprehensive performance including high sensitivity, outstanding dynamic stability, and high SNR over a wide strain range. The research contribution lies not only in providing reliable sensing solutions for high-precision data collection in sports training and health monitoring, but also in providing new material and structural design ideas for the development of wearable sensor technology.

Preparation of a sensor based on agnws

To prepare sensors with better sensing performance, AgNWs with better conductivity are selected as the main material for the sensor. Compared to other metals, AgNWs have easier conductivity and a better aspect ratio. Therefore, choosing AgNWs as the material for motion sensors has better sensing performance10,11. Figure 1 shows the preparation process of the sensor conductive film substrate.

Fig. 1Preparation process of sensor conductive film substrate (Image source: self drawn by the author).

Preparation process of sensor conductive film substrate (Image source: self drawn by the author).

From Fig. 1, there are usually three ways to prepare conductive films. Droplet coating method forms a film by controlling the natural flow of droplets on the substrate surface. Spin coating method uses the centrifugal force of high-speed rotation to evenly spread the solution and form a thin film. Screen printing method uses prefabricated screen printing to pattern and transfer conductive paste onto the substrate. Among these three methods, spin coating can achieve the most uniform film thickness, screen printing is suitable for complex graphic preparation, and droplet coating has simple equipment. The spin coating method introduced in Fig. 1 is particularly suitable for preparing flexible sensors in this study, as it can meet the stringent requirements of conductive layer uniformity, structural integrity, and repeatability for flexible sensors. The spin coating method can form AgNWs/PVA composite films with uniform thickness and smooth surface on flexible substrates by precisely controlling the speed and time, which fundamentally avoids the “coffee ring effect” caused by uneven solvent evaporation in droplet coatings and the nanowire aggregation. The substrate fabrication process is shown in Fig. 1. First, PVA with a molecular weight of 98 kDa and an alcoholysis degree of 98% is dissolved in a 90℃ water bath to prepare a homogeneous solution of 5% (w/v). Subsequently, AgNWs with a diameter of 60 nm and a length of 20–50 μm are dispersed in anhydrous ethanol at a mass ratio of 1%, and uniformly dispersed with ultrasound assistance. The dispersion is mixed with PVA solution and stirred at 600 rpm for 1 h to obtain AgNWs/PVA composite slurry. Then, the slurry is dropped onto a temporary Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET) substrate and rotated at a speed of 2,000 rpm for 60 s in a spin coater. Centrifugal force is used to evenly spread the slurry and form a wet film with a thickness of approximately 50 μm. Finally, the solvent is gradually removed through a step drying process to solidify the film and obtain a dense structure12–14. Then, the mixed object is subjected to degassing operation. The mixed object is poured into the mold for solidification treatment, serving as the substrate for the sensor. The preparation process of the sensor is shown in Fig. 2.

Fig. 2Preparation process of sensor.

Preparation process of sensor.

As shown in Fig. 2, dimethyl silicone oil is a low-viscosity and high compatibility dispersing medium, which is first thoroughly mixed with AgNWs. Its function is to uniformly disperse the nanowires and avoid agglomeration before pouring into the mixed system of PDMS prepolymer and curing agent. Subsequently, the AgNWs/silicone oil dispersion is introduced into the uncured PDMS matrix, and the nanowires are further uniformly distributed by stirring. During the subsequent heating and curing process, PDMS undergoes cross-linking to form an elastic network. Some dimethyl silicone oil remains in the system as a plasticizer due to its compatibility with PDMS and lower molecular weight, while others may migrate or evaporate during the curing process, ultimately leaving a uniformly dispersed and overlapping three-dimensional conductive network of AgNWs within the cured PDMS. Therefore, the core of this process is to use dimethyl silicone oil to achieve initial uniform dispersion of AgNWs, and to “lock” them into a structurally intact sensitive layer through PDMS curing.

Take Polyvinyl Alcohol (PVA) with a molecular weight of 98 kDa and an alcoholysis degree of 98% as the base material. Prepare a homogeneous solution of 5% (w/v) by dissolving in a 90℃ water bath. Ultrasonic assisted dispersion method (40 kHz, 15 min) uniformly disperses AgNWs with a diameter of 60 nm, a length of 20–50 μm, and a mass ratio of 1% in anhydrous ethanol15,16. Then, mix it with the PVA solution and stir at a speed of 600 rpm for 1 h. Perform spin coating to form a film. The spin coating speed is 2,000 rpm and the spin coating time is 60 s, forming a thin film of about 50 μm thickness on a PET substrate. The AgNWs/PVA composite material with dense structure is obtained through a stepwise drying process. The process is set at room temperature of 24 h + 60 ℃ and dried for 2 h. A laser cutting machine is used to cut into 6 cm×1 cm strip-shaped devices and print silver paste electrodes. The characterization shows that the film has a three-dimensional conductive network structure with a square resistance of less than 50 Ω/sq. Optimizing the ultrasonic dispersion parameters throughout the preparation process requires avoiding nanowire damage, suppressing film deformation through step drying, and maintaining PET substrate stability through low-temperature sintering of silver paste at 120 ℃. Preparation process.

The study adopts a systematic process to prepare and validate the performance of AgNWs/PDMS flexible strain sensors. Firstly, flexible electrodes are prepared using CAD design and inkjet printing technology. After drying, curing, and cutting, the sensor is tested for electrical resistivity using the probe 4 method to evaluate its conductive network quality. Subsequently, the sensor is attached to the skin surface and connected to a bioelectric acquisition system to synchronously collect muscle electrical signals in both resting and exercise states. Finally, through signal processing and SNR analysis, the actual performance of the sensor in dynamic physiological signal monitoring is comprehensively verified.

Figure 4 illustrates how to use CAD to design flexible electrodes for collecting electromyographic signals and prepare them using inkjet printing technology. The process supplement explains the manufacturing process of the signal acquisition end in the sensor system. Optimizing electrode patterns (such as curvature) and printing processes can improve the stability and comfort of electrode skin contact, obtaining high-quality physiological electrical signals from the source. Figure 4 illustrates the specific operational procedure for measuring the resistivity (ρ) of a sensor film using the probe 4 test method. This technique involves injecting a known current through the outer two probes while simultaneously measuring the voltage between the inner two probes. The resistivity is then calculated using a correction factor. Its core advantage lies in eliminating the influence of contact resistance, thereby yielding more authentic and accurate conductivity data for the material.

All sensor samples (as shown in Table 2) in the study are fabricated and tested under identical preparation parameters and structural design: The thickness of the sensitive layer film is controlled at 50 ± 2 μm through the same spin coating process. The silver nanowire loading is uniformly set at 1.0wt% of the solid content. The electrode design adopts the same cross finger pattern and the silver paste electrode is prepared through the same screen printing process. Therefore, the observed performance differences can be clearly attributed to the differences in the properties of the substrate material itself, including elastic modulus, viscoelasticity, interfacial bonding strength with AgNWs, and structural recovery ability under high strain. Although CNF is environmentally friendly and has high mechanical strength, it has strong hydrophilicity and is easily affected by humidity, resulting in weak interface bonding with AgNWs and poor cycling stability. PET has stable size and low moisture absorption, but lacks elasticity recovery ability and poor adhesion to the skin, making it prone to plastic deformation under high strain. By comparing with the two, the comprehensive advantages of PDMS-based sensors in dynamic stability, rapid response, and wearable adaptability are highlighted.(Table 2)

Results

Analysis of sensor preparation effect

To analyze the performance changes of the prepared sensor, a comparative analysis is conducted on the substrate properties, including CNF, PET, and PDMS material used in the study. The power ratio method is used to calculate the SNR, which is defined as the ratio of signal power to noise power and expressed in decibels (dB). The calculation is shown in Eq. (8).8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SNR=10\cdot {\log _{10}}(\frac{{{P_s}}}{{{P_n}}})$$\end{document}

In Eq. (8), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P_s}$$\end{document} represents the average power within the signal window. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P_n}$$\end{document} represents the average power within the noise window. The signal window selects the interval during which the amplitude of the electromyographic signal is stable and lasts for no less than 1 s while the subject performs standardized actions. The noise window selects a stationary state segment at least 2 s before the start of the action. When there is no visible electromyographic activation, it is considered as background noise. The calculation step first applies bandpass filtering to the collected raw voltage signal. According to the definition, the signal window and noise window data are separated. The root mean square of voltage values within each window is calculated as the power estimation. The sensor is fixed on the surface of the target muscle group skin using a low-allergenic medical conductive tape according to surface electromyography measurement standards. The main placement positions include: the extensor muscle belly of the forearm and the center of the biceps muscle belly of the upper arm. The signal acquisition uses the Biopac MP160 bioelectric acquisition system, with a fixed sampling frequency of 2,000 Hz. The raw signal is bandpass filtered by the system’s built-in hardware, and then a zero phase digital filter (fourth-order Butterworth, bandpass 20–450 Hz) is used for software side secondary filtering. Each participant completes three tests under standardized motions, with a 10-minute break between each test. The peak SNR and waveform cross-correlation coefficient of the signal amplitude obtained from three tests are used as repeatability indicators. The intra-group correlation coefficients are all greater than 0.85, indicating that this sensor has good stability in short-term repeated measurements. The results are shown in Fig. 6.

From Figs. 12 (a), (b), and (c), under low-density conditions, AgNWs are isolated from each other and cannot form a continuous conductive path, resulting in a high initial resistance of the device. As the density of AgNWs increases, nanowires gradually overlap with each other and form a permeable network, leading to a significant decrease in resistance. When the density of AgNWs is further increased to form a highly interwoven dense network, the decrease in resistance tends to be gentle, indicating that the conductive channels are approaching saturation. The conductive mechanism based on AgNWs/PDMS composite material originates from the permeable three-dimensional network constructed by silver nanowires within the PDMS elastic matrix. The network utilizes interface engineering with ultrasound assisted dispersion and dimethyl silicone oil pretreatment to uniformly distribute AgNWs and form a large number of stable overlap points, forming a continuous electronic transmission path. As a result, the initial square resistance is lower than 50 Ω/q. When the sensor is stretched, the PDMS matrix undergoes deformation, forcing the overlap points between AgNWs to slip, separate, or reduce the contact area, resulting in a significant increase in tunneling resistance and contact resistance, and leading to an overall increase in resistance. This change is highly correlated with the strain. Due to the high elastic recovery of PDMS and the uniformity of AgNWs network, the conductive path can be reversibly destroyed and reconstructed during cyclic stretching, thereby achieving high stability and good repeatability of resistance response. Conclusion

This study successfully developed a resistive strain sensor using silver nanowires as the conductive material and PDMS as the flexible substrate, aiming to improve the stability and reliability of motion signal monitoring. The experimental results showed that the resistance fluctuation rate of the sensor was less than 5% in 3,000 stretching cycles, the response time was 5,000 ms at 60% strain, and the average SNR was 26.27 dB within the human motion characteristic frequency range of 0.5–1.5 Hz. Compared to sensors based on PET and CNF, the prepared AgNWs/PDMS sensor exhibits significant advantages in cycle stability, dynamic response adaptability, and signal quality. Its superiority lies in the high elasticity and fast segment recovery ability of the PDMS substrate, as well as effectively maintaining the dynamic integrity of the AgNWs conductive network. The research not only provides a reliable sensing solution for high-precision data collection in sports training and health monitoring, but also offers new material and structural design ideas for the development of wearable sensor technology.

Limitations and future work

The research still has certain limitations: It mainly focuses on the tensile strain, cyclic stability, and electromyographic signal capture performance of the sensor, without systematically evaluating the temperature stability, long-term wearing comfort, and biocompatibility. Application validation is currently limited to physical activity scenarios such as basketball, and its performance in higher frequency movements, fine motor skills, or complex environments is still unclear. In addition, the performance evolution mechanism of material interfaces under long-term loads and environmental factors also needs further exploration.
