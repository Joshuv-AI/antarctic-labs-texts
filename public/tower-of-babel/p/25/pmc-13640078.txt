Promoting palliative care for people with heart failure: the P3HF study protocol
Authors: Yutong Men, Nathan Wright, Giselle O’Connor, Ling Han, Hargun Kaur, Ella Suh, Jenny Chen, Bashar Kadhim, Tariq Ahmad, Michael Beasley, Nora Segar, Nancy Kim, Jonathan Quang, Mona Sharifi, Shelli Feder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure is often a chronic, life-limiting illness associated with high symptom burden, frequent hospitalizations, and substantial suffering near the end of life. Although palliative care improves quality of life and is strongly recommended by clinical practice guidelines, fewer than 20% of people with heart failure receive it. We developed the Promoting Palliative Care for People with Heart Failure (P3HF) Clinical Decision Support (CDS) tool—an evidence-based, end-user informed tool integrating a validated prognostic-risk model, tiered functionality, and behavioral nudges in the electronic health record (EHR). This manuscript describes the development of the P3HF CDS and protocol for a clinical trial evaluating its effects.MethodsWe will conduct a pragmatic randomized clinical trial at two large urban hospitals, within a single health system, using an embedded, convergent mixed-methods design to evaluate the P3HF CDS. Eligible patients are adults (≥ 18 years) hospitalized with heart failure, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) ≥ 500 pg/mL, and receipt of intravenous diuretics within 24 h of admission. We will analyze data from the EHR and surveys and interviews with end-users (attending physicians, residents, fellows, advanced practice providers) of the CDS tool and palliative care clinicians to assess CDS usability, acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility (implementation outcomes), and efficacy in increasing inpatient palliative care (Primary clinical outcome) consultations. Secondary exploratory outcomes include increasing palliative care outpatient referrals, improved advance care planning documentation, higher hospice enrollment, and reductions in hospital length of stay and 30-day readmissions compared to usual care.DiscussionThe P3HF CDS is an evidence-based, theory and end-user informed tool designed to promote timely palliative care for people with heart failure. The P3HF trial will examine the acceptability and effects of the CDS in real-world clinical settings.Trial registrationNCT06933875 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06933875, Registration date: 2025-04-18).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12904-026-02206-w.

Background

Heart failure is often a chronic, life-limiting illness projected to affect more than eight million Americans by 2030 [1]. Significant symptom burden and recurrent hospital presentations are common among this populaiton, causing significant suffering, particularly near the end of life (EOL) [2–4]. Conversations about prognosis, the benefits and harms of invasive treatments, and patients’ care goals and preferences are essential to promote goal-concordant care among this population. However, these conversations are challenging for healthcare providers to complete and occur infrequently [5, 6]. To address these critical needs, the American Heart Association, the American College of Cardiology, and the Heart Failure Society of America recommend palliative care as a Class I (strong) recommendation “for all patients with heart failure to improve quality of life and relieve suffering [1].” However, less than 20% of people with advanced heart failure receive this vital service [7, 8]. There remains a critical need for system-level interventions that promote guideline-concordant care among this population.

Patient prognosis is a common criterion for consideration of referral to palliative care among people with heart failure [9, 10]. In a previous study, our team developed and validated an automated electronic health record (EHR)-based 1-year mortality risk model for patients hospitalized with heart failure and embedded it into an interruptive best practice alert triggered during order entry to support decision-making and promote guideline-concordant care [11]. In a multi-site trial, we found the prognostic alert to be highly accurate in predicting 1-year mortality and acceptable and feasible among healthcare providers; however, it did not significantly increase referrals or consults to palliative care compared to usual care [12].

To address the pressing need for interventions to promote timely palliative care engagement, our team developed the Promoting Palliative Care for People with Heart Failure (P3HF) Clinical Decision Support (CDS) tool. The P3HF CDS is a theoretically and end-user informed tool embedded in the EHR that incorporates our accurate 1-year mortality risk model [11, 12] with evidence-based CDS design and behavioral economics employed through behavioral nudges, defined as positive reinforcement and indirect suggestions that influence decision-making without restricting choice [13–15]. We hypothesized that through user-centered design, implementation of P3HF CDS can increase palliative care engagement, helping achieve guideline-concordance. Here, we describe the systematic development of the P3HF CDS and the P3HF clinical trial protocol.

Methods

Conceptual framework

The design and evaluation of the P3HF CDS is informed by the User-Centered Framework for Implementation Technology (UFIT), a framework developed to guide CDS development and adaptation (Fig. 1) [16]. UFIT is comprised of principles from User Centered Design (UCD), Human Factors/Ergonomics models (HFE), and the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) and its Outcomes Addendum. UCD ensures stakeholder engagement throughout the design process by grounding the CDS in end-user needs, thereby promoting usability and adoption [17]. HFE principles are applied through the Systems Engineering Initiative for Patient Safety (SEIPS) and Situation Awareness models [18], which guide the alignment of CDS functionality with clinical workflows and decision-making demands. CFIR is a determinant framework used to increase and improve the implementation of evidence-based interventions [19]. Through the incorporation of CFIR, we purposively capture elements that influence individual and system-level decisions to adopt CDS tools into routine practice. The CFIR outcomes addendum extends the framework to include the evaluation of innovation (e.g., intervention) and implementation outcomes [20]. In addition, CDS design elements are guided by the MINDSPACE framework, a behavioral framework encompassing nine key influences on largely automatic (rather than deliberative) decision-making: Messenger, Incentives, Norms, Defaults, Salience, Priming, Affect, Commitments, and Ego [15, 21, 22].

P3HF CDS design overview

We designed the P3HF CDS tool through an iterative, theoretically informed, end-user-engaged process. We began with a formative assessment involving nine interviews and five focus groups (engaging 25 individual key stakeholders), which included referring clinicians (CDS end-users) and palliative care clinicians (those impacted by the CDS), to identify clinicians’ cognitive and affective needs, understand contextual barriers and facilitators to palliative care referral, define requirements for the CDS, and formulate implementation strategies (Fig. 2, steps 1–3). Interviews with palliative care clinicians also explored potential unintended consequences and workflow impacts. Qualitative data were analyzed using rapid qualitative analysis (RQA), a cost-effective and time-efficient approach that produces results consistent and comparable with those of traditional qualitative methods [23–25]. Results were mapped to design elements within the CDS (Fig. 2, steps 4–5) and to the MINDSPACE framework.

After initial data collection and analysis to inform the design of the tool, a clinical informaticist (B.K.) built the first iteration of the CDS tool within the health system’s electronic health record (EHR) Epic (Epic Systems, Verona, WI), which is the EHR that the health system uses. The tool was activated in “silent mode” in the EHR to evaluate its scope and functionality without formal clinician involvement over a period of two months. An additional two rounds of interviews with end-users were conducted to support the evaluation of the CDS tool and the final design in an iterative process, gathering feedback and making updates to the CDS tool’s design and functionality. Initially, potential end-users provided feedback on the tool’s initial design and integration into the workflow and electronic health system based on screenshots of the tool within the EHR. In the latter cycle of evaluation, clinicians reviewed the revised CDS and interacted with test-case patient scenarios in real-time in the EHR to provide final feedback.

P3HF CDS tool description and illustration

The P3HF CDS supplements clinical judgment by identifying hospitalized patients with heart failure who may benefit from palliative care, providing tiered recommendations to clinicians (Fig. 3). The tool deploys both interruptive and passive alerts and applies several behavioral nudges based on the MINDSPACE framework, including “Defaults” (pre-populated orders), “Messenger” (information framing), “Salience” (prominence of display), and “Social Incentives” (actions influenced by social expectations) [22].

P3HF CDS tool illustration

P3HF CDS tier thresholds are derived from previous research on palliative care triggers in heart failure and our clinician interviews (Stages 1–3) [10, 26]. The mortality risk model incorporated into the CDS uses routinely collected demographic, vital sign, laboratory, and illness severity variables available during hospitalization. The CDS architecture separates the background risk calculation engine from alert presentation logic, such that risk is continuously recalculated using real-time EHR data for all eligible patients, while alert presentation occurs only when predefined workflow conditions are met. In prior validation, the model demonstrated good discrimination (area under the curve = 0.74) with excellent calibration [11, 12]. High-tier patients (i.e., predicted one-year mortality ≥ 30% and at least two hospitalizations in the prior year, inclusive of the current one) trigger an interruptive alert during order entry that employs Defaults (pre-populated order sets for single-click ordering for palliative care referral), Messenger (displaying mortality risk, hospitalization history, recommendations, and clinical practice guidelines to contextualize recommendations), and Salience (use of bolding and text color) nudges. The tool is also available within the Epic Admissions Navigator to support decision-making at the time of hospital admission. To reduce duplicative referrals and alert fatigue, alerts are suppressed if there is an active referral or care plan for palliative care, geriatrics, or home-based primary care in the EHR, and clinicians may silence the CDS for 24 h. Additional suppression logic includes temporary snooze functionality and precedence rules whereby higher-tier alerts supersede lower-tier recommendations (Fig. 4).

Medium-tiered patients (predicted one-year mortality 15–29% and two hospitalizations in the prior year, inclusive of the current one) generate a similar interruptive alert at hospital discharge within the Epic Discharge Navigator (a discharge workflow tool), recommending referral to outpatient palliative care. For reproducibility, alert presentation is tied to specific EHR workflow events: high-tier alerts are triggered when clinicians access the Epic Orders activity, medium-tier alerts when the Epic Discharge Navigator is opened, and low-tier recommendations are displayed passively in the navigator bar without interrupting workflow.

For both high- and medium-tiered patients, clinicians are informed that the patient’s primary care provider will receive an Epic “InBasket” message notifying them of the alert and whether the patient was referred to palliative care (Social Incentive). Patients in the lowest tier (i.e., low mortality < 15% and/or no prior hospitalizations) encounter a passive (non-interruptive) alert displaying mortality risk, utilization, and recommendations to assess for other palliative needs. This format aligns with evidence that less intrusive modalities can be effective for lower-acuity scenarios while reducing alert fatigue [27–29].

Across alert tiers, the CDS is intended to support routine clinical decision-making and encourage timely identification of patients who may benefit from palliative care services while preserving clinician autonomy. Clinicians may choose not to act on CDS recommendations at their discretion. They may also submit structured feedback directly within the CDS, indicating whether they agree or disagree with the risk estimate or recommendation; these data will be used to refine the tool and understand clinical usability, acceptability, and appropriateness. The CDS is adaptive to changes in clinical status, with alert tier assignment dynamically updated as risk changes over time. If recalculated risk crosses a higher threshold, alert intensity escalates accordingly (e.g., passive to interruptive alerts), whereas improving risk estimates result in de-escalation to less intrusive alert formats.

Clinician training

To promote the use of the P3HF CDS, clinicians with ordering privileges will receive short training sessions held during regularly scheduled meetings ahead of the clinical trial. The training implementation was derived from the CFIR domains for implementation, utilizing the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) implementation strategy matching tool [20, 30]. We identified facilitators and barriers to implementation from the qualitative data and, as a team, coded each barrier using the CFIR implementation domains. For each identified barrier, we then used the ERIC matching tool (a verified tool that assigns potential implementation strategies and relevance percentages to previously defined, individual CFIR implementation barriers) to generate possible strategies for implementation. For all ERIC-proposed strategies assigned to each individual implementation barrier, we kept only strategies that had greater than 50% relevance. For those barriers that had no generated strategies above 50% relevance, we included all strategies identified. Using counting statistics, we organized our barrier-specific ERIC strategies by most to least frequent, focusing on the strategies that appeared more than 5 times, and matched these with implementation facilitators identified in clinician interviews. Clinician education and training were the most prominent strategies identified using this process. We subsequently used the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation – Behavior System Model (COM-B) [31] and the Behavior Change Technique taxonomy v1 (BCTTv1) [32] to determine approaches to clinician education and training that would be most effective and lead to behavior change.

Clinicians with ordering privileges in participating specialties (e.g., hospital medicine, cardiology, and critical care) will receive brief training sessions delivered during regularly scheduled departmental meetings in advance of trial launch. Initial training will occur during the week preceding CDS activation, with periodic refresher sessions throughout the trial to reinforce workflow familiarity and address implementation questions. Training will include the rationale for early palliative care referral in hospitalized patients with heart failure, an overview of the CDS tool and its integration within the EHR, eligibility criteria for alert activation, interpretation of risk-stratified alerts, recommended referral pathways and documentation expectations, and demonstration of how clinicians should respond to alerts within the EHR. Training incorporates behavior change techniques including instruction, demonstration, and information on consequences [32]. A standardized one-page reference guide summarizing alert triggers, eligibility criteria, recommended actions, and study contact information will be distributed electronically and made available at point-of-care locations as permitted by site policy. Clinicians unable to attend scheduled sessions will receive these materials electronically.

Study design

We will conduct a pragmatic, randomized clinical trial with an embedded, convergent mixed-methods study design to examine the usability, acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of the P3HF CDS and its efficacy, including measurement of the intended effects and effect size as described in the following sections (Fig. 4). We will assess clinician behavior change as a primary outcome (i.e., consultation, referral to and receipt of palliative care) and secondary outcomes including new or revised Advance Care Plan (ACP) documented during hospitalization, discharge to hospice, 30-day hospital readmission, hospital length of stay, and change in code status during hospitalization. This study protocol was developed in accordance with the SPIRIT (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials) 2025 guidelines [33].

Study setting

We will conduct this study at two hospitals within a large academic health system in the Northeast United States. These two hospitals are located within urban settings, and are comprised of 1030 and 511 beds, respectively. The health system serves a diverse patient population across urban, suburban, and surrounding communities, with a broad range of sociodemographic characteristics and clinical complexity.

Eligibility criteria

Patients hospitalized with heart failure are eligible for the study if they are 18 years of age or older, are not pregnant at the time of admission, have a recorded N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) level greater than 500 pg/mL, and have received intravenous diuretics within 24 h of hospital admission. Eligible clinicians include attending physicians, fellows, residents, advanced practice nurses, or physician associates who have hospital ordering privileges.

Recruitment

Patients will be automatically enrolled and randomized when electronically identified as meeting enrollment criteria using an established Epic build used in prior studies [12]. We will purposefully enroll patients with a range of estimated 1-year prognoses who meet inclusion criteria. This approach ensures the trial includes all hospitalized patients with heart failure who meet inclusion criteria, rather than restricting enrollment to those with higher mortality risk.

We will select clinicians caring for heart failure patients who have high engagement (i.e., encountered the tool frequently) with the P3HF CDS tool, as identified through a retrospective review, for post-study feedback, including surveys and interviews. Clinicians will be recruited via email until we reach our anticipated sample size.

Randomization

Randomization will occur at the level of the patient. Eligible patients will be randomized to either the intervention (P3HF CDS) or control arms (usual care) via a 1:1 randomization scheme to ensure equal numbers of patients in each study arm. The random allocation sequence will be generated automatically within the EHR system using a computer-generated randomization algorithm. In the intervention arm, clinicians who care for enrolled patients will interact with the P3HF CDS. Clinicians will continue to deliver usual care for patients randomized to the control arm without access to the CDS. Patients in both arms will be censored after their first hospitalization during the study period. To reduce bias, we will implement triple masking of the patient, investigator, and outcomes assessor. Allocation is concealed through the EHR-based randomization process, which assigns participants automatically without investigator input, preventing foreknowledge of group assignment. As the intervention poses minimal risk and allocation is not directly disclosed, no formal unblinding procedures are planned.

CDS validation, implementation antecedents, and clinical outcomes

We will validate the CDS mortality risk prediction model using death data from the EHR and National Death Index. Implementation antecedents

We will collect data on usability, acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility from CDS tool data, EHR records, provider interviews, and provider surveys. Near trial end, we will conduct interviews with approximately 15 end-users (hospitalists, cardiology clinicians) at each site and up to 15 palliative care providers, focusing on perceptions of the P3HF CDS, workflow fit, and barriers and facilitators to palliative care involvement. We will employ a qualitative descriptive approach and directed content analysis informed by the domains of CFIR to analyze transcripts [20, 34].

We will assess use, acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility using objective user actions (alert engagement rates, recommendation adherence rates), embedded CDS questions (clinician ratings of risk estimates and recommendation appropriateness), and validated surveys including the System Usability Scale (SUS), Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM), and the Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) [35]. The AIM, IAM, and FIM are four-item instruments with established psychometric strength, with scores ≥ 4 indicating agreement that the intervention is acceptable, appropriate, or feasible [35]. Survey data will be analyzed using descriptive statistics and established scoring procedures, including those for the SUS [36]. Following a convergent mixed-methods approach, interviews, CDS/EHR data, and survey-derived outcomes will be analyzed separately, then integrated using joint displays to identify convergent and divergent findings.

Clinical outcomes

Primary and Secondary (i.e., exploratory) outcomes will be assessed with data collected via the EHR. Primary outcomes include referrals to palliative care, completed consultations by palliative care, and the average days from admission to referral and consult. The primary comparison will be the main effects of the P3HF CDS vs. usual care.

Secondary outcomes include the proportion of patients with a new or revised ACP documented during hospitalization, the proportion discharged to hospice, the proportion readmitted within 30 days of discharge, the average hospital length of stay, and change in code status from admission to discharge. ACP notes are templated documents that capture patient and family preferences for advance care, including code status.

As outcomes are obtained from the electronic health record, loss to follow-up is expected to be minimal. All randomized participants will be included in outcome assessment regardless of adherence to the intervention.

Statistical analysis

We will follow intention-to-treat principles for analysis. We will use hierarchical logistic or linear regression with random intercepts for clinicians to account for potential contamination of intervention effects due to clinicians’ switching roles between the intervention and control arms. In the model, palliative care consult or referral, which can occur more than once over the entire 15-month follow-up period, will be modeled as a time-dependent binary event. The effect of the intervention (vs. control) will be modeled as a fixed-effect parameter, and the odds ratio (OR) of palliative care referral and 95% confidence interval (CI) will be estimated using a robust variance estimator. To avoid undue model complexity while facilitating clinical implications, we will add hospital site as an explicit fixed-effects parameter, instead of as a level-3 random intercept, to account for global differences in hospital infrastructure and patient populations, conditional on the clinician-specific random intercept. This will also allow us to quantify and compare the intervention effects between the two hospitals via a hospital by intervention interaction term.

We will conduct secondary analyses for additional clinical outcomes using similar hierarchical modeling approaches. For binary outcomes (new or revised ACP documentation, discharge to hospice, 30-day readmission), we will use hierarchical logistic regression models. For continuous outcomes (hospital length of stay), we will use hierarchical linear regression models with log-transformation if distributions are skewed. All secondary analyses will be adjusted for the same baseline covariates as the primary analysis. Prespecified subgroup analyses explore potential heterogeneity of treatment effects across clinically relevant patient characteristics and include predicted 1-year mortality risk category (low [< 15%], medium [15–29%], high [≥ 30%]), alert tier received (high-interruptive alert, medium-passive alert, or no alert [low-tiered patients]), age group (< 65 years, 65–79 years, ≥ 80 years), sex, race and ethnicity, left ventricular ejection fraction category (preserved, mid-range, reduced), and hospital site. Subgroup analyses are exploratory and will be interpreted cautiously, with primary focus on estimating effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than statistical significance.

We will conduct sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our primary findings including alternative specifications of clustering, per-protocol analysis (i.e., limiting intervention arms patients to those whose clinicians engaged with the tool), and among complete cases. To assess potential temporal trends or learning effects, we will compare intervention effects in the first half vs. the second half of the enrollment period.

Missing data

We anticipate minimal missing data for the primary outcome (palliative care consult or referral) and key covariates, as these will be extracted from structured EHR fields. However, if missing data exceed 5% for any covariate, we will use multiple imputation with chained equations to impute missing values, generating 20 imputed datasets.

Sample size and power

We assume enrollment of approximately 2260 patients (1,130 per arm) based on the original REVEAL-HF trial, accounting for clustering by clinician (estimated intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.05–0.20), and an approximate baseline rate of palliative care referral or 10.5% [12], of which we estimate that 8.5% will receive a palliative care consultation. An increase in these proportions by 10% in the intervention arm would be considered clinically meaningful. Power calculations were conducted using PASS 2025. Controlling for Type I error p < 0.05, we will have 80% power to detect a Minimum Detectable Effect Size (MDES) ranging from ORs of 1.42 to 1.46 (risk difference, 3.6% to 4.0%). Among medium to high-tiered patients only (anticipated n = 817), we estimate a primary outcome baseline rate of 19.2% for the medium group and 27.8% for the high-tiered group. Under the same assumptions as the whole sample, we will have 80% power to detect a MDES ranging from ORs of 1.50 to 1.63 (risk difference, 7.9% to 9.6%).

Potential risks

No significant risks or harms are anticipated, as the intervention is a clinical decision support tool. Potential unintended consequences, such as alert fatigue or inappropriate referrals, will be monitored through clinician feedback and usage data.

Data monitoring

Given the minimal risk nature of this study and the use of an electronic health record–embedded clinical decision support tool, a formal Data Monitoring Committee has not been established. No interim analyses or formal stopping guidelines are planned. Trial conduct will be monitored by the study team through regular review of data quality, recruitment, and intervention implementation within the electronic health record, with any issues addressed by the investigative team.

Discussion

Palliative care is an evidence-based, guideline-recommended [1] intervention that improves the quality of life and reduces suffering among people with heart failure. Despite its benefits, more than 80% of patients with heart failure do not receive palliative care [10]. This persistent knowledge-to-practice gap is driven in part by prognostic uncertainty and wide variation in clinical awareness and attitudes toward palliative care engagement [37, 38].

To address this gap, we developed a theoretically informed CDS tool to promote timely palliative care for people with heart failure. Our approach is innovative in several ways. It integrates a validated, novel prognostic-risk model [11, 12]; employs tiered alert modalities (interruptive and passive); incorporates multiple behavioral nudges, including Defaults, Information Framing, Salience, and Social Incentives [21, 39, 40]; is guided by an established implementation science framework [16]; and systematically evaluates implementation antecedents using validated measures, which are known predictors of downstream implementation success.

The use of prognostic algorithms to identify patients for palliative care is grounded in previous recommendations and endorsed by our team’s prior research [9, 12]. Prognosis emerged as a primary feature identified by end-users during our formative assessment as a key criterion for flagging patients with potential palliative care needs, reflecting current clinical practice patterns and balancing inpatient and outpatient palliative care workload capacity. Nevertheless, tools that identify patients for palliative care solely based on mortality estimates may inflate the importance of prognosis and may fail to account for patient suffering, potentially introducing bias as to who does or does not receive palliative care [41]. Our CDS frames prognosis as but one criterion to consider for palliative care referral, not the sole determinant. The tool preserves clinician autonomy to consider the full spectrum of palliative care indications—including uncontrolled symptoms, psychosocial distress, caregiver burden, and patient preferences for care—beyond mortality risk alone. Moreover, by enrolling all hospitalized patients with heart failure meeting basic inclusion criteria, we encourage clinicians to assess for palliative needs even among patients with lower predicted mortality.

The P3HF CDS employs a tiered approach to recommendations and alert intensity, representing an important design consideration informed by evidence on alert fatigue and CDS effectiveness. High-firing interruptive alerts contribute significantly to clinician burnout and alert override behavior [29]. By reserving interruptive alerts for select patients, the P3HF CDS aims to balance the need for timely palliative care identification with preservation of clinician well-being and alert receptiveness. This approach aligns with recommendations from CDS stewardship initiatives to use interruptive alerts judiciously and consider less intrusive alternatives when appropriate [27–29].

The ongoing P3HF trial will evaluate the effects of this CDS intervention on palliative care referral and consultation outcomes. If successful, findings from this work may inform future multi-site effectiveness-implementation studies, as well as the development of CDS tools incorporating risk-stratified alerts and behavioral nudges for other clinical populations.
