MACH: A Multi-Attribute Catchment Hydrometeorological dataset
Authors: Katharine Sink, Tom Brikowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
MACH is a comprehensive hydrometeorological dataset spanning 1,014 watersheds across the United States. It unifies, extends, and enhances existing large-sample hydrology resources by integrating daily meteorological forcings and streamflow observations at the basin scale over a 44-year period (1980–2023). For 395 catchments, MACH also includes records dating back to 1948, extending the hydrologic analyses period to 75-years. MACH provides a rich set of watershed attributes, encompassing topography, soils, geology, land cover, anthropogenic influences, and a wide range of hydroclimatic indices. This comprehensive structure supports investigations of climate-runoff interactions, hydrologic sensitivity, model calibration, and long-term watershed change across diverse spatial and temporal gradients. MACH serves as a flexible, high-resolution dataset for advancing large-sample hydrology, hydroclimatic assessment, and data-driven water science.

Background & Summary

Hydrology is an ever-evolving science, continually seeking to answer the unsolved questions surrounding Earth’s complex water systems1. Developing models that provide detailed and reliable results for varying hydrologic environments is paramount for advancing hydrologic science2. Large sample hydrology (LSH) represents a “big data” approach to hydrological research3. When evaluating surface water processes, a data-intensive approach involving hundreds to thousands of catchments enables the generalization of hydrologic principles and processes. This broad-scale perspective supports applications to ungauged basins, provides insight into their behavior, and complements regional and site-specific studies with more robust conclusions2,4,5. LSH allows identification of broad patterns and connections between catchments, along with statistical comparisons and classifications, providing a better understanding of hydrological behaviors within dynamic, non-linear systems6.

The International Association of Hydrological Sciences (IAHS) initiated the Decade on Predictions in Ungauged Basins (PUB) in 2003, aimed at making reliable predictions while reducing uncertainty in ungauged basins7. This initiative aspired to significantly improve process-based understanding and characterize causal processes2,8. Climatic variables and catchment characteristics can be used to determine primary streamflow generation mechanisms and hydrological responses, enhancing our understanding of hydrological function across varying spatial and temporal scales1,2,9. The PUB effort led to the development of the first open source LSH dataset, Model Parameter Estimate Experiment (MOPEX), which included data from 431 basins within the United States10,11. The general benefits of LSH include improved understanding through statistical robustness, catchment classification and regionalization, and comparative hydrology2. The Catchment Attributes and Meteorology for Large-sample Studies (CAMELS) dataset was later introduced to address gaps in MOPEX’s spatial coverage and included detailed catchment attributes for 671 basins12,13.

Addor et al.6 identified several key LSH hydrological studies and datasets while noting persistent shortcomings in available data, including the lack of common standards, comparability, uncertainty estimates, and characterization of human impact. More recent LSH datasets have been developed, focusing on standardization at the catchment scale. The Hydrometeorological Sandbox – École de technologie supérieure (HYSETS)14 contains data for 14,425 basins in North America from five sources including Livneh, ERA5, and ERA-5 Land14. A global dataset called Caravan is comprised of 6,830 basins including 482 from CAMELS (US) and 4,621 from HYSETS datasets15. While these two datasets are more expansive, they still require careful vetting by users. For example, HYSETS includes all stations with at least one year of recorded streamflow data. According to research, between 5 and 10 years of data is recommended for model calibration16,17. Also, the watershed identification number is unique to HYSETS and does not provide the corresponding USGS site number for those stations. Caravan attributes were obtained from HydroATLAS, and because those shapefiles do not always align with the source data catchment boundary polygons, basins less than 100 km2 were excluded15. Additionally, HYSETS and Caravan both utilize ERA-5 Land for surface variables. Several studies have indicated decreased model performance due to biases in ERA-Interim and ERA5 precipitation data18–20. A recent study conducted by Clerc-Schwarzenbach et al.21 highlighted the potential impacts to modelling results based on Caravan precipitation and potential evapotranspiration values and they suggested “extending the Caravan dataset with the forcing data included in the original CAMELS datasets … or at least indicating clearly that the forcing data in Caravan come with a data quality loss.”

The MACH (Multi-Attribute Catchment Hydrology) dataset22 presented in this paper derives its name from a combination of the MOPEX and CAMELS acronyms, paying tribute to the foundational datasets on which it is based. MACH is a standardized and consolidated dataset for 1,014 watersheds located within the contiguous United States (CONUS). MOPEX and CAMELS are two of the most widely used LSH datasets for the CONUS, and their extensive proliferation into hydrological research underscores the value of maintaining consistency between these datasets and in future datasets23. MACH combines 395 basins from MOPEX and 671 basins from CAMELS, covering the period from 1980 to 2023, using consistent climate variables from the most recent Daymet Version 424 and GLEAM425.

MACH simultaneously incorporates new consistent forcing data, while maintaining the same USGS stream gage station identifiers used in the MOPEX and CAMELS datasets, ensuring continuity and comparability with previous research that utilized those datasets. While the MACH dataset covers 1980–2023, earlier MOPEX data are also included for the 395 MOPEX basins, encompassing daily precipitation, temperature, and streamflow from 1948 to 1979. Sink et al.23 demonstrated that MOPEX and CAMELS data can be reliably merged for precipitation and temperature at aggregations beyond a daily timestep without requiring bias correction. The original CAMELS catchment averaged forcing dataset used in that study was derived from Daymet V2; comparison of MOPEX with Daymet V4 (used in MACH) demonstrates improved similarity between the datasets.

Methods

Watershed selection

The MACH dataset consists of daily meteorological forcing data and catchment attributes for 1,014 basins across the CONUS. MACH integrates basins from MOPEX (1948–2003, 431 catchments) and CAMELS (1980–2014, 671 catchments), with 52 watersheds common to both datasets12,13. In total, 1,050 unique basins were identified prior to quality control filtering. Of these, 36 MOPEX basins were excluded due to insufficient streamflow data coverage, resulting in a final dataset of 1,014 watersheds (Fig. 1). Metadata for all watersheds were obtained from the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) National Water Information System (NWIS)26 using the dataRetrieval27 package in R. Both the MOPEX and CAMELS datasets were originally designed to represent basins with minimal anthropogenic influence at the time of their compilation, and MACH retains this emphasis on relatively natural flow regimes while extending temporal coverage and harmonizing data sources. All data in MACH were processed and aggregated at the catchment scale using a combination of the R programming language and QGIS. Detailed descriptions of MACH contents, including data sources, units, and temporal coverage, are documented in the accompanying README.csv file22.

Hydrological data

The dataRetrieval package was used to download daily streamflow observations from the USGS NWIS26 for each site using the 8-digit site identification number (SITENO). To ensure consistency with available gridded meteorological forcing data, only stations with daily streamflow observations extending to at least January 1, 1980 were considered, corresponding to the start of the Daymet Version 424 dataset. Furthermore, sites were required to have a minimum of ten years of daily discharge data between January 1, 1980 and December 31, 2023. Applying these criteria resulted in a final dataset of 1,014 watersheds (Fig. 1).

Daily streamflow data provided by the USGS in cubic feet per second were normalized to units of millimeters per day using the total upstream drainage area associated with each watershed, as derived in QGIS from NHDPlusV228 basin polygons. This ensured spatial consistency between hydrologic response variables and basin-averaged climate and land surface datasets.

Because some MOPEX and CAMELS basins lack continuous discharge data over the full 44-year analysis period, the temporal coverage and total number of daily streamflow observations for each station are summarized in the discharge_mach.csv file. This file also reports the number of daily observations available for each calendar year, enabling users to assess interannual data availability and completeness on a site-specific basis. A complete daily discharge record from January 1, 1980 to December 31, 2023 consists of 16,071 observations, accounting for leap years. Of the 1,014 watersheds, 686 watersheds have complete streamflow records (100% data availability). An additional 183 watersheds have 90–99% completeness, 60 have 80–89%, 30 have 70–79%, 17 have 60–69%, 14 have 50–59%, 5 have 40–49%, 15 have 30–39%, and 4 have 25–29%. Record completeness for each site is indicated by point color in Fig. 1. For historical context, the discharge_mopex.csv file summarizes streamflow data availability for the 395 MOPEX watersheds. A complete daily record from January 1, 1948 to December 31, 1979 consists of 11,688 observations, accounting for leap years.

In addition to record completeness, the operational status of each USGS gage was determined based on metadata available through the USGS NWIS Mapper (https://apps.usgs.gov/nwismapper/). Gage status (active or inactive) reflects whether a station was actively reporting discharge data as of access date December 31, 2025. Status information is provided (discharge_mach.csv) to assist users in identifying sites that are currently operational versus those that are discontinued or inactive, which may be relevant for studies requiring ongoing data availability. Active and inactive gages are distinguished by symbol type in Fig. 1.

Watershed delineation

Each USGS monitoring location has a dedicated NWIS webpage26 that reports drainage area information derived from hydrologic delineations based on the National Hydrography Dataset (NHD) and the Watershed Boundary Dataset (WBD), available through the USGS National Hydrography portal (https://www.usgs.gov/national-hydrography). In this context, a drainage area is defined by the USGS as “the area enclosed by a topographic divide from which direct surface runoff from precipitation normally drains by gravity into the stream above that point.”

Watersheds boundaries used in the development of this dataset were obtained from the National Hydrography Dataset Plus, Version 2.1 (NHDPlusV2)29, a nationally consistent hydrographic framework for the United States that integrates the medium-resolution (1:100,000 or better) NHD stream network, 30-meter National Elevation Dataset (NED) surfaces, and hydrologic unit boundaries from the WBD. NHDPlusV2 is jointly maintained by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and USGS and was accessed from https://www.epa.gov/waterdata/nhdplus-national-hydrography-dataset-plus (last updated June 11, 2025).

Watershed boundaries for each USGS station were retrieved using the nhdplusTools30 package in R by querying the NHDPlusV2 dataset with the 8-digit NWIS gage identifier. Basin delineation was performed using the get_nldi_basin() function, which accesses the USGS/EPA Hydro Network-Linked Data Index (NLDI) service. The NLDI links monitoring locations to the NHDPlusV2 stream network by spatially associating each gage with the nearest NHDFlowline, a stream reach identified by a unique common identifier (COMID). For each site, basin polygons represent the total upstream contributing area draining to the downstream end of the indexed NHDFlowline. As a result, the gage location does not necessarily coincide with the geometric outlet of the basin polygon or with the downstream terminus of a visually apparent watershed boundary. The longitudinal position of a gage along a flowline does not alter the upstream contributing area unless the flowline is explicitly segmented at the gage location. Basin delineations therefore reflect network-based flow accumulation rather than local point-based watershed boundaries. These basin polygons were used to clip gridded climate and land cover datasets and to define the contributing area for converting discharge observations to basin-average runoff depth.

Drainage areas reported in NWIS were compared against areas derived from the corresponding NHDPlusV2 basin polygons. Differences between NWIS and NHDPlusV2 drainage areas arise from variations in historical delineation methods, source datasets, and spatial resolution used in NWIS, whereas NHDPlusV2 areas are derived using a consistent DEM-based flow accumulation framework. For quality control, absolute differences exceeding 1% were manually evaluated. Basin areas used in all analyses were calculated directly from the NHDPlusV2 basin polygons using geodesic area calculations in QGIS; QGIS was used solely for area computation and attribute population and not for watershed re-delineation.

Watershed polygons were converted from simple feature (sf) dataframes into individual shapefiles for each basin using R. All MACH boundaries are provided in a WGS84-projected (EPSG:4326) ESRI shapefile. The shapefile attribute tables were then populated in QGIS, using the integrated Python console to add attributes for site number and basin drainage area, which range from 4.7 km2 to 25,546 km2. Several smaller watersheds are nested within larger watersheds, effectively classified as subwatersheds. The MACH dataset provides additional information in the site_info.csv file, which identifies all subwatersheds contained within a larger basin (upstream_site), along with the number of additional gages located upstream (no_upstream). Figure 2 illustrates this concept with four catchments located in Delaware County, New York. The smaller drainage basins, represented by upstream gages 01415000, 01414500, and 01413500, are tributaries contributing to the larger East Branch Delaware River basin, represented by site number 01421000. Meteorological Data

The MACH dataset utilizes the most recent and accurate climate variables from Daymet. Daymet V2 was selected as one of the daily forcing datasets in CAMELS12,13 for its high spatial resolution (1 km × 1 km gridded surface). Daymet Version 4 was released in 2022, addressing inconsistencies in station observation times and sensor biases present in earlier versions24. In Daymet V4, daily station observations from GHCNd V3.2631 are gridded using a method that applies a pre-determined search radius. This radius is designed to encompass an average number of stations based on distance arrays, while station interpolation is performed through the spatial convolution of a truncated Gaussian kernel24. Daymet offers a gridded dataset with finer spatial resolution (1 km × 1 km) compared to other gridded data products like Livneh (1/16 degree)32 and ERA5 (31 km).

For the MACH dataset, daily forcing data were obtained from Daymet V4 for the period spanning January 1, 1980 to December 31, 2023. The extracted variables include minimum air temperature (degrees C), maximum air temperature (degrees C), precipitation (mm/day), snow water equivalent (mm/day), shortwave radiation (W/m2), day length (sec/day), and water vapor pressure (Pa). Mean air temperature (degrees C) represents the average of daily minimum and maximum air temperature. Data retrieval was performed using the FedData package33 in R, with datasets accessible at https://daymet.ornl.gov/getdata. To ensure spatial consistency, NHDPlusV2 basin shapefiles were used as extraction templates. The extracted data were then spatially averaged over each basin for each daily timestep using area-weighted aggregation implemented in the terra package34, in which raster cells partially intersecting basin boundaries were weighted by the proportion of cell area within each polygon. Notably, Daymet annual datasets contain 365 daily records per standard calendar year, meaning that leap years do not include a record for December 31, as documented in the Daymet data overview (https://daymet.ornl.gov/overview). To address this, linear interpolation was applied to fill the missing values. This approach is consistent with prior studies using Daymet data35 and preserves annual totals while ensuring temporal continuity.

Actual evapotranspiration (mm) and potential evapotranspiration (mm) were sourced from the openly available GLEAM4.2a (Global Land surface Evaporation: the Amsterdam Methodology) dataset, covering the period from January 1, 1980 to December 31, 202325. Potential evapotranspiration (PET) in GLEAM was computed using the Penman equation, with actual evapotranspiration determined by applying a stress factor to PET. These values were derived from a combination of various forcing and observational datasets. Annual netCDF files, with a global spatial resolution of 0.1°, were downloaded directly from the SFTP-site, available through https://www.gleam.eu/. The netCDF files were read into R as SpatRaster objects, a spatially referenced surface divided into three-dimensional cells, using the terra package34. The rasters were first cropped to basin boundaries to avoid processing each entire 2.5GB file. Basin SpatRaster objects were then downscaled employing cubic interpolation from terra to increase the spatial resolution to 1 km × 1 km. Data were then spatially averaged over the basin area, using area-weighted aggregation, for each daily timestep. It should be noted that site 10336740 does not contain PET or AET data due to insufficient raster coverage in that basin.

MOPEX time series data

In MACH, the earlier MOPEX data10 are included as daily time series for the 395 catchments from January 1, 1948 to December 31, 1979. The end date was chosen to allow for merger with Daymet V4 data beginning in 1980. The daily MOPEX data can be merged with the MACH data for these 395 catchments, providing a complete time series from January 1, 1948 to December 31, 2023. The original DLY format files were transformed into individual.csv files, featuring a unified date column, SITENO column, and renamed variables aligning with the MACH dataset, including precipitation, minimum temperature, maximum air temperature, and streamflow. The data integrity has been preserved, with no alterations beyond the formatting. As a result, any inherent errors within the original MOPEX dataset will persist. Gaps in the streamflow record were identified, and a complete streamflow record (1948–1979) consists of 11,688 daily observations, including leap years. An attribute table (discharge_mopex.csv) details the number of observations for each calendar year, allowing users to view the annual timespan for each site. MOPEX daily climate data were originally obtained from the National Climate Data Center (NCDC) and Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS) networks10. Missing data indicated by −99 were replaced with NA to be consistent with MACH.

Catchment attributes

MACH catchment attributes were derived from the NHDPlusV2 dataset36, Multi-Resolution Land Characteristics (MRLC) Consortium, National Inventory of Dams (NID), NRCS Soil Survey Geographic Database (SSURGO) and calculated from the daily hydrometeorological time series data included in MACH using R and QGIS.

NHDPlusV2 attributes

In addition to catchment delineations and streamlines, the NHDPlusV2 dataset (ver. 4.0, August 2023) contains a comprehensive series of attributes pertaining to climate, geology, soil, hydrology, hydrologic modifications, landscapes, and topography36. Developed by the USGS National Water-Quality Assessment Project with the EPA as steward, these datasets are available at https://www.epa.gov/waterdata/get-nhdplus-national-hydrography-dataset-plus-data. Attribute values are compiled from multiple authoritative sources, including the Natural Resources Conservation Service soil database and National Water Census Data, and are maintained by federal agencies such as the USGS, EPA, and USDA.

Within NHDPlusV2, each stream reach, catchment, or waterbody is identified by a unique COMID. For each NWIS monitoring site included in MACH, a COMID corresponding to the flowline associated with the site was identified and validated through comparison with basin delineations returned via NLDI and with basin boundaries shown on the USGS NWIS station pages. This validation ensured that the selected COMID represents the same upstream drainage basin used for polygon-based delineations and spatial data extraction.

NHDPlusV2 attributes were extracted and aggregated using the validated flowline COMID for each drainage basin. Many NHDPlusV2 attributes, including hydrologic and landscape summaries, are defined relative to the cumulative upstream drainage area of the flowline, represented by the attribute TotDASqKm (available in the NHDPlusV2 NHDPlusAttributesVAA.dbf). For most sites, TotDASqKm closely matches the drainage area calculated independently from basin polygons in QGIS. Differences between flowline-based cumulative drainage areas and polygon-derived areas were less than 1% for over 99% of basins, indicating strong consistency between attribute areas and basin geometries used for averaging.

A small subset of basins (n = 10) exhibited differences greater than 1% between TotDASqKm and QGIS-calculated basin areas (see Technical Validation). For these sites, basin polygons and spatial delineations remain hydrologically consistent, and attribute values were retained using the validated COMID. Users conducting analyses that are highly sensitive to basin area may wish to exercise additional caution for these basins.

Detailed descriptions of all NHDPlusV2 attributes and their original data sources are included in the MACH README.csv file. Attributes are grouped by thematic category, including geology, hydrology, soils, regional characteristics, and anthropogenic data.

SSURGO attributes

Additional soil attributes were acquired directly from SSURGO37, available at https://websoilsurvey.nrcs.usda.gov/app/WebSoilSurvey.aspx. The general soils survey shapefile, along with the accompanying access database, were downloaded from SSURGO. The SSURGO tables were integrated in QGIS by joining the spatial and tabular components according to the documented parent-child relationships defined in the SSURGO metadata, provided in the README.csv file under the Notes column. Soil map units were intersected with each watershed boundary, and the area of each unique map unit within a basin was calculated using vector overlay operations. These area-weighted summaries were subsequently used to derive basin-scale soil attributes in soil_ssurgo.csv.

Annual land cover

The USGS recently released the Annual National Land Cover Database (ANLCD), based on methodologies from NLCD and Land Change Monitoring, Assessment, and Projection (LCMAP) projects. The Annual NLDC Collection 1 database provides annual land cover from 1985 to 2023, utilizing a modified Anderson Level II classification system38 with 16 land cover classes. All 39 annual landcover GeoTIFF raster files were downloaded from https://www.mrlc.gov/data, and then clipped to each basin shapefile in QGIS using the integrated Python console. Each 30-meter by 30-meter pixel corresponds to a land cover class, such as water, developed, forest, or wetlands. Area-weighted pixel values were grouped by land cover class for each basin, totalled, and the corresponding percent spatial coverages were calculated using R. The annual percentages of each land cover class for all 1,014 basins are provided in separate.csv files.

Vegetation index

Normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) data were obtained using the NASA Earthdata Download application (https://github.com/nasa/earthdata-download). We used the Global Inventory Modeling and Mapping Studies-3rd Generation, version 1.2 (GIMMS-3G+) NDVI product39, which is derived from observations collected by the Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) instruments aboard NOAA satellites. Maximum NDVI values are composited at a bimonthly temporal resolution to reduce cloud contamination and other atmospheric effects40,41.

The GIMMS-3G + dataset is provided in NetCDF format and spans the period from 1982 to 2022. Data have a native spatial resolution of 0.083° (~8 km) and are provided in a geographic coordinate system (WGS84). Two NetCDF files are available per year, each representing a six-month interval and containing two NDVI composites per month. All files were imported into R using the terra package. NDVI values were extracted for each catchment polygon feature using area-weighted averaging to account for partial grid cell overlap. Raw NDVI values were rescaled by applying the provided scale factor (0.0001), yielding dimensionless NDVI values ranging from −1 to +1. The bimonthly NDVI are available for each basin in individual.csv files.

NDVI was selected instead of leaf area index (LAI) due to its substantially longer and more temporally consistent record. While LAI provides a more direct biophysical representation of canopy structure, global LAI products are generally limited to the MODIS era or later, precluding their use in analyses spanning the early 1980s42. The use of NDVI therefore allows for consistent long-term assessment of vegetation dynamics across most of the MACH time period.

MACH climate attributes

Mean annual maximum temperature, mean annual minimum temperature, mean annual temperature, total annual precipitation, total annual potential evapotranspiration, and total annual actual evapotranspiration were determined for two 30-year periods using MACH data (1981–2010, 1991–2020). Climate change indices suggested by the CLIVAR Expert Team on Climate Change Detection and Indices (ETCCDI), part of the World Climate Research Programme, were calculated in R according to definitions listed at https://etccdi.pacificclimate.org/list_27_indices.shtml using daily precipitation and daily temperature. These indices are measured over various temporal scales and are separated into monthly (monthly_climate.csv), annual (annual_climate.csv), and overall values (overall_climate.csv).

Anthropogenic attributes

All watersheds initially selected for inclusion by MOPEX and CAMELS represented as close to natural conditions as possible. The original MOPEX basins were selected from the USGS hydroclimatic data network (HCDN) which was a subset of 1,659 stream gages from NWIS determined to have minimal anthropogenic influence43. The Geospatial Attributes of Gages for Evaluating Streamflow, version II (GAGES II) dataset44 evaluated 9,322 USGS stream gages and identified 2,057 reference sites, which represented near-natural flow conditions as of 2009. The original CAMELS basins were selected from HCDN-2009, a subset of 743 USGS sites that were identified independently of GAGES II site, but still fulfilled all criteria established in GAGES II classifications45. Watersheds were listed as reference or non-reference in the GAGES II dataset and the classifications are included for the MACH gages along with the Annual Data Report (ADR) remarks and screening comments from National Water Quality Assessment (NAWQA) evaluations in the anthropogenic.csv file. ADR information is also available as “Water Year Summary” on the NWIS site. Only 78 of the 395 MOPEX basins are considered reference, however, they may still be acceptable for climate-related hydrologic studies.

To obtain additional information regarding stream alteration and modifications, dam locations and attributes are included in MACH. The National Inventory of Dams (NID)46 contains information for over 92,000 dams located within the United States and is maintained by the US Army Corp of Engineers (USACE), available at https://nid.sec.usace.army.mil/#/. All dam locations were plotted in QGIS based on the provided latitude and longitude and then the NHDPlusV2 shapefile boundaries were used to identify the specific dams contained within each watershed. The integrated Python console was used to create a.csv file that listed all dams within each watershed and linked their corresponding attributes obtained from NID, along with the associated basin in dam_info.csv. The total number of dams in each catchment were determined in addition to the maximum and minimum storage amounts and are included in anthropogenic.csv.

Hydrological signatures

The Indicators of Hydrologic Alteration (IHA) were developed by the Nature Conservancy to characterize hydrologic regimes and alteration attributed to human influence47. The five major components of a natural flow regime include magnitude, timing, frequency, duration, and rate of change in water conditions. A total of 171 hydrologic indices are available to evaluate flow regimes but Olden & Poff48 demonstrated that many are redundant and intercorrelated and that the 33 IHA indices adequately characterize the major components. Archfield et al.49 developed 7 fundamental daily streamflow statistics (FDSS), which include overall mean, coefficient of variation, skewness, kurtosis, autoregressive lag one correlation coefficient, amplitude of the seasonal signal, and the phase of the seasonal signal, and provided evidence that these statistics outperform the 33 IHA in streamflow regime classification. The 7 FDSS were calculated for each basin using the USGS daily streamflow time series (cubic feet per second), summarized by water year (October 1, 1980 to September 30, 2023) where applicable, and the R package EflowStats50. Discharge data in cfs for each basin is included in MACH.

While the FDSS provide valuable information about flow regimes, they do not fully capture several important aspects including seasonality of flow within a year, flow magnitude extremes, baseflow contribution and recession characteristics. To ensure a more comprehensive representation of catchment hydrology, specific IHA signatures capturing baseline and extreme hydrological responses for low/high flows, were computed using USGS daily streamflow data in R48. Including these signatures (e.g. slope of flow duration curve, mean half-flow date) in hydrology.csv gives a more holistic view of catchment behaviour, offering critical insights that are not fully captured by the FDSS alone.

Data Records

The MACH dataset22 is available via Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.18686475). Metadata are provided in README.csv, which contains detailed descriptions of all data incorporated into MACH. The archive includes daily hydroclimatic forcing data, catchment attributes, and spatial basin delineations for 1,014 watersheds across the CONUS. A project homepage with updates and supplementary materials is maintained at https://github.com/k-sink/MACH.

All validated drainage basin polygons are consolidated into a single GeoPackage (GPKG) file, MACH_basins_all.gpkg. Individual basin shapefiles were merged into a unified vector dataset using QGIS Python utilities, preserving a consistent coordinate reference system (EPSG:4326) and attribute schema across all features. Each polygon is uniquely identified by its USGS site number (SITENO) and includes a basin area attribute (area_sqkm) derived from geodesic polygon calculations. The GeoPackage format was selected to ensure portability, open access, and compatibility with standard GIS and hydrologic analysis workflows.

Technical Validation

Drainage basin area and aggregation

Drainage basin boundaries and areas were validated to ensure consistency across MACH attributes, NHDPlusV2 flowline attributes, and independent GIS-based area calculations. Streamflow observations provided by the USGS are recorded at fixed monitoring stations and are representative of drainage basins delineated within the NHDPlusV2 hydrographic framework. Basin polygons derived from NHDPlusV2 catchments were used to extract gridded climate and land cover data from Daymet, GLEAM, and MRLC.

For each site, basin boundaries were obtained using the nhdplusTools37 package in R, which queries the NHDPlusV2 through the Network Linked Data Index (NLDI) (https://waterdata.usgs.gov/blog/nldi-intro/). These polygons were visually compared against delineations displayed on the corresponding USGS NWIS station pages to confirm spatial agreement.

Three independent drainage areas were evaluated for each site: (1) the NWIS-reported drainage area (converted from square miles to square kilometers), (2) the cumulative upstream drainage area associated with the NHDPlusV2 flowline COMID (TotDASqKm from the NHDPlusV2 PlusFlowlineVAA.dbf), and (3) the geodesic area calculated directly from the NHDPlusV2 basin polygon in QGIS. Absolute percent differences were computed for all pairwise comparisons among these three estimates, hereafter referred to as NWIS, NHDPlusV2, and QGIS, respectively.

Comparison between NWIS-reported drainage areas and NHDPlusV2 cumulative drainage areas revealed generally close agreement, with a limited number of notable outliers. Of the 1,014 basins evaluated, 432 exhibited absolute percent differences ≥1%, with a maximum difference of 200%. However, only 56 basins showed differences exceeding 10%. These larger discrepancies were not systematically associated with basin size, occurring in both small and large drainage areas. A 1% threshold therefore represents a conservative and rigorous criterion given known uncertainties in historical drainage area reporting and variability among basin delineation methodologies51.

In cases where drainage area disagreement was identified, basin boundaries were independently delineated using both the USGS StreamStats application (https://streamstats.usgs.gov) and the EPA Waters GeoViewer 2.0 (https://www.epa.gov/waterdata/waters-geoviewer), and the resulting boundaries were visually compared with NWIS station delineations and quantitatively evaluated using QGIS-derived geodesic areas. Manual inspection indicated that discrepancies most commonly reflect outdated or inconsistent drainage area values reported in NWIS metadata, rather than errors in basin geometry.

For example, the Narraguagus River at Cherryfield, Maine (site 01022500) lists a drainage area of 227 square miles (587.92 km2) in NWIS metadata. The corresponding NHDPlusV2 basin polygon, consistent with the mapped delineation displayed by NWIS, reports a cumulative drainage area of 840.84 km2. Independent geodesic area calculation of the same polygon in QGIS yielded 840.57 km2, differing from the NHDPlusV2 value by only 0.03%. This confirms the geometric consistency of the NHDPlusV2 delineation and indicates that the NWIS-reported drainage area is inconsistent with the mapped watershed boundary.

Reported discrepancies can be efficiently illustrated using visual scale approximation rather than exhaustive comparisons of individual basin delineations. For example, a Massachusetts catchment (site 01162500) lists a NWIS drainage area of 19.4 mi2 (50.2 km2). When the basin boundary is overlaid with a 5 km × 5 km reference grid (Fig. 3), the watershed extent clearly exceeds the area represented by two adjacent grid cells (50 km2). The cumulative drainage area reported by NHDPlusV2 for this basin is 125.41 km2, which closely matches the independently calculated QGIS area of 125.55 km2. This comparison demonstrates that, in this case, the NWIS-reported value substantially underestimates the true basin area (absolute percent difference between NWIS and NHDPlusV2 exceeds 85%), whereas the NHDPlusV2 and QGIS-derived estimates are mutually consistent (absolute difference <0.12%).Fig. 3Area discrepancy from USGS NWIS. NHDPlusV2 data are distributed by Vector Processing Units (VPUs), representing hydrologic regions. Data for all 21 VPUs in the CONUS were examined, including flowlines, catchments, and attribute tables. For basins with elevated area discrepancies (≥1%), the spatial position of each gage relative to the NHDPlusV2 flowline network was manually inspected in QGIS.

These individual investigations indicate that the majority of large discrepancies arise from gage to flowline associations rather than errors in basin delineations. As noted in previous studies, when gages are located near flowline junctions or at upstream/downstream endpoints of flowlines, discrete catchment boundaries and network topology can introduce step changes in upstream connectivity52. In such cases, flowline-based cumulative drainage area attributes (e.g., TotDASqKm) may reflect a downstream or adjacent network segment, while basin polygons derived via NLDI remain hydrologically consistent with NWIS delineations.

Two representative examples illustrate this behavior. At site 02108000 (Chinuapin, North Carolina), the gage is located near the junction of adjacent flowlines. Although the basin polygon aligns with NWIS and NLDI-derived delineations, the original flowline-based cumulative drainage area (TotDASqKm) reflected an adjacent network segment, resulting in a 200% area discrepancy. Reassignment to the appropriate flowline reduced the difference to 0.03%. Similarly, at site 06883000 (Deweese, Nebraska), the gage lies on the correctly mapped flowline, but the basin corresponds to an upstream control point represented by a different COMID within the NHDPlusV2 network. In both cases, basin polygons remain consistent across data sources, whereas flowline-based drainage attributes are sensitive to gage snapping near network junctions. Reassignment of COMIDs to the appropriate flowlines eliminated the largest discrepancies (differences >10%) and substantially improved agreement between NHDPlusV2 and QGIS-derived areas.

Additional boundary differences were identified between the original CAMELS dataset and MACH. CAMELS boundaries were delineated using a geospatial fabric that includes inherent uncertainties associated with watershed boundary identification12. As a result, some CAMELS basin boundaries and drainage areas do not match those used in MACH, even when the same USGS NWIS station identifier is referenced. These delineation differences contribute to reduced correlations between CAMELS (Daymet V2) and MACH (Daymet V4) basin-average values for some sites.

An example is shown in Fig. 5 for USGS site 08079600 located near Justiceburg, TX (33.04°N, −101.20°W). The CAMELS basin delineation (white polygon) has an area of 3512.25 km2. For the same station, the USGS NWIS metadata reports a drainage area of 1466 mi2 (3796.91 km2), which approximates the CAMELS area. In contrast, the basin returned from the NHDPlusV2 database for this station corresponds to a smaller delineation (gray polygon) with a drainage area (TotDASqKm) of 1130.44 km2. When the geodesic area of this NHDPlusV2 basin is computed independently in QGIS, the resulting area is 1130.52 km2, yielding an absolute difference of less than 0.01% between NHDPlusV2 and QGIS. This differs substantially from the NWIS-reported drainage area, resulting in an absolute difference of approximately 236% between NWIS and NHDPlusV2 for this site. Consequently, basin-scale variables derived from CAMELS and MACH should not be assumed to be directly comparable on a site-specific basis, even when the same USGS NWIS station identifier is used, due to fundamental differences in basin delineation methodology. Validating meteorological time series

The available Daymet V4 data was subjected to rigorous quality control checks during development and prior to its release24. Sink et al.23 compared CAMELS (catchment averaged Daymet V2 time series) and MOPEX daily precipitation and temperature for 47 shared watersheds beginning on October 1, 1980, and ending on September 30, 2000 and demonstrated that the datasets can be successfully merged despite biases present in the individual datasets.

For comparison purposes, CAMELS represents catchment averaged Daymet V2 data, MOPEX represents the original dataset (NRCS, NCDC), and MACH represents catchment averaged Daymet V4. When MOPEX data ending in 1979 is extended using MACH, beginning in 1980, slight shifts in statistics are expected. To determine if any statistically significant changes are observed when the datasets are merged, a non-parametric homogeneity test was applied to the time series. A Pettitt test53 identifies the time at which a shift occurs and describes the null hypothesis as being that there is no change in distribution. Usage Notes

The MACH dataset harmonizes basins originally defined in the MOPEX and CAMELS datasets using a consistent watershed delineation framework based on NHDPlus Version 2. As a result, basin boundaries and drainage areas in MACH may differ from those reported in the original MOPEX or CAMELS releases, which employed alternative delineation approaches and data vintages. Differences primarily affect area-dependent quantities and may be most pronounced for a small subset of basins.

To support cross-dataset comparisons, MACH preserves the original USGS site number (SITENO) for all basins, enabling alignment with legacy CAMELS and MOPEX records. Drainage area values derived from MACH (NHDPlusV2/QGIS), together with corresponding drainage areas reported in NWIS and the original CAMELS and MOPEX datasets, are provided in area_differences.xlsx, along with absolute percent differences. These metrics allow users to evaluate and account for basin definition discrepancies when conducting area-sensitive analyses.

For applications requiring strict consistency in basin area, users may normalize area-dependent variables using the drainage metrics provided or restrict analyses to basins exhibiting minimal delineation differences. The inclusion of harmonized identifiers, documented area comparisons, and consistent aggregation procedures supports transparent comparison across datasets while maintaining compatibility with prior studies.
