Development and verification of a comprehensive diagnostic model for osteosarcoma using random forest and artificial neural network
Authors: Huishuang Zou, Xiaoning Feng, Huaqi Yu, Runtian Jiang, Guangwei Sun, Lili Huang, Jie Lu, Bin Li, Weihan Shi, Yaning Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteosarcoma (OS) is a malignant tumor originating in bones with high morbidity rates among adolescents. Current approaches for early diagnosis of OS face significant challenges. This study aimed to construct a comprehensive diagnostic model for OS.MethodsIn this study, we used the GSE16088 and GSE14359 datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database and the OS dataset from the TARGET database as the training set. GSE12865 and The Cancer Genome Atlas ‌(TCGA)-Sarcoma (SARC) dataset were used as validation. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between normal and OS tissues were screened. Enrichment analysis was performed to uncover the common functions and pathways of these biomarkers. Random forest models were constructed to screen out key OS-signature genes. The diagnostic model was constructed and verified by Artificial Neural Network (ANN). Finally, quantitative real time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was used to detect the expression of 7 OS signature genes in tissues and cells.ResultsIn this study, 2128 DEGs were screened. A random forest classifier identified seven representative gene. The OS diagnostic model we constructed has good performance with areas under the curves (AUCs) of 0.826 and 0.766 in the training and validation groups. The experimental results show that in OS tumor tissues and cells, the expression levels of CIC, SLC16A2 and GTF2A2 were significantly downregulated, while the expression levels of DCAF11, DLGAP5, PRKD3 and C5orf22 were significantly upregulated.ConclusionsOur findings identified the potential biomarkers for the diagnosis of OS, which may provide novel diagnostic strategies for OS patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04662-5.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) is the most common primary bone malignancy with a high propensity for local invasion and metastasis, among which the lungs are the most commonly invaded site [1]. The large tumor heterogeneity in OS has brought some difficulties to early diagnosis and therapy, such as identifying reliable biomarkers, recurrence mechanisms, and which cell type causes OS [2]. 10%−20% of OS patients present with metastasis at the first diagnosis. Compared with OS patients without metastasis, the 5-year survival rate for patients with early detection of metastasis dropped from 70% to less than 20% [3]. Therefore, it is urgent to search for new diagnostic markers in order to enhance the diagnostic efficiency of OS.

The early symptoms of OS are not obvious. Common symptoms include pain, lumps, functional impairment and systemic symptoms [4]. The diagnosis mainly relies on imaging examinations, pathological examinations and laboratory tests [4]. Furthermore, unlike other types of tumors, OS does not have traditional tumor diagnostic markers. Therefore, some research reports indicate that OS has a relatively high rate of misdiagnosis and underdiagnosis [5]. With the application of various machine learning algorithms such as random forests and artificial neural networks (ANNs) in the fields of disease diagnosis and prognosis, many researchers have also developed OS-related diagnostic methods and models through these deep learning models [6]. Some researchers, based on CT imaging biomarkers, constructed a lung metastasis risk prediction model for OS patients using the random forest algorithm, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.79 [7]. Other researchers have constructed a more efficient OS pathological diagnosis model using ANNs and other deep learning models [8]. Machine learning models such as ANNs have significantly enhanced the efficiency of early diagnosis of OS through their application in imaging and pathological examinations. On the other hand, compared with traditional diagnostic methods, molecular diagnosis has significant advantages in terms of diagnostic efficacy, sample acquisition, and diagnostic costs. However, there are relatively few reports on the use of machine learning models such as ANNs to build early molecular diagnostic models for OS.

In this study, we used the OS-related datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and the TARGET database to screen for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) related to OS. Random Forest and ANNs were used to construct and validate the early diagnosis model for OS. Finally, we also compared the expression differences of the 7 genes in the model between normal and tumor tissues or cells using tissue and cell samples. Figure 1 depicts the entire research flow.

Materials and methods

Data download and processing

We retrieved datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) using ‘Osteosarcoma’ and ‘Homo sapiens’ as keywords. We excluded datasets from human cell lines and mouse models. As demonstrated in Table 1, we searched three sets containing OS and normal samples. The data of To establish a comprehensive training cohort GSE16088,GSE14359,TARGET-OS database (https://xenabrowser.net), we applied the SVA package’s combat functions to remove batch effects. For genes with multiple probes mapping to the same ID, we represented the gene’s expression level by the total expression value of all probes. We converted probe IDs to gene symbols according to microarray annotation. The final training dataset included 24 OS patients and 8 normal samples. We used GSE12865 and The Cancer Genome Atlas ‌(TCGA)-Sarcoma (SARC) (https://xenabrowser.net) for validation.

Screening for differentially expressed genes

In the training set, we identified DEGs utilizing the limma package in R software, setting an adjusted P value < 0.05 and a |log2 fold-change (log2FC)| ≥ 1. Visualizations of the DEGs were created with R packages “heatmap” and “ggplot2”.

Functional enrichment analysis and construction of Protein-Protein interaction network

In order to gain insights into the biological implications of the DEGs, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses using the R package ClusterProfiler. Significant pathways exhibited a P < 0.05 and a corrected P < 0.05. In addition, differentially expressed genes were subjected to Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to identify significantly enriched biological pathways. Briefly, the ranked gene list was prepared based on differential expression results. GSEA was performed for GO terms and KEGG pathways using the cluster Profiler R package. For GO analysis, the gseGO function was applied with org.Hs.eg.db as the annotation database and all ontology categories (ont = “all”). For KEGG analysis, the gseKEGG function was applied with hsa as the organism code. And the results were subsequently visualized using the enrichplot package. We conducted Protein-protein interactions (PPIs) of DEGs analyses using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) and Cytoscape v3.7.1 was used to achieve the visualization of the network.

Screening for signature genes by random forest

We constructed random-forest models using the package random Forest in R. Genes with the lowest cross-validation error were selected as signature genes. Ntree was set to 500 and the seed was 12,345,678. A Gini index was used to determine the importance of features in a random forest model. Scores greater than 1 were selected as important features. The Heatmap R package was used to perform bidirectional clustering of the signature genes on the basis of their expression profiles.

Construction and validation of the diagnostic model by ANN

To mitigate batch effects between OS and normal groups, the expression data of signature genes were converted into “Gene Score” using the min-max method. In each sample, the researcher calculated the median expression of the genes expressed. If an upregulated gene’s expression is greater than its median expression value, it is marked as 1, and otherwise as 0. Similarly, if expression of a downregulated gene in a sample exceeds the median expression value, it is marked as 0, otherwise 1. Gene Score sheets were constructed using the above method.

ANN model was constructed by using the R package “neuralnet”. The random seed was set at 12,345,678. An ANN consists of input, hidden, and output layers. This layer is comprised of the “Gene Score” of signature genes. The output layer contains two nodes representing two classes: OS and healthy volunteers (HCs). Our model was constructed by integrating “GeneExpression” and “NeuralNetworkWeight” to diagnose OS disease. The pROC package was used to generate Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves to evaluate the diagnostic value of OS. The diagnostic value was expressed as the area under the ROC curve.

Identification of immune cell infiltration

By using CIBERSORT (a gene expression profiling-based algorithm) with the LM22 signature as a reference, the immune tissue cell composition can be accurately calculated. R package corrplot was used to distinguish tumor-infiltrating immune cells in OS and normal groups, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated between immune cell ratios.

Patients and samples

This study strictly adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki and has obtained approval from the Ethics Committee of Linfen People’s Hospital (Ethics Approval Number: E20250505034). Forty-five paired OS tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues were collected from OS patient.

Cell culture

OS cells (SAOS-2, MG-63 and U2OS) and hFOB1.19 cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA). These cells were cultured with DMEM medium (Gibco, Grand Island, New York, USA) under 5% CO₂ atmosphere to maintain cellular homeostasis. The medium was enriched with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), along with 100 µg/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Cells were passaged every 3–4 days to maintain sub-confluence. Mycoplasma contamination detection was performed every three months using a mycoplasma detection kit (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) to ensure cell integrity.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from three sample types-OS tumor tissues (Tumor), adjacent normal tissues (Normal) and cultured cells using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Following isolation, reverse transcription was conducted with the PrimeScript RT Master Mix Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China), strictly following the kit instructions. The mRNA expression was detected by SYBR Green fluorescence and we computed the expression of the mRNA by means of the 2− ΔΔCT method. The expression of mRNA was corrected with Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as an internal control. The primers were listed below: Capicua Transcriptional Repressor (CIC): F 5’-GTACAGAAGCCGAGGACGTG-3’, R 5’-AGCCATGGTCCTGAAAGAGC-3’; Solute Carrier Family 16 Member 2 (SLC16A2): F 5’-ACAGGCAGCAGTTGCACATA-3’, R 5’-CCCAGCATGGGAGACACATT-3’; General Transcription Factor IIA Subunit 2 (GTF2A2): F 5’-GAAGTAGGAACCTCCTGCCG-3’, R 5’-TCAAGCTTGCATTGATGTCCTG-3’; DLG Associated Protein 5 (DLGAP5): F 5’-GGTTGTGAGGGTTCCTGCTT-3’, R 5’-TGAAGACATCCTGAGCCACC-3’; DNA damage binding protein 1 (DDB1) and Cullin 4 (CUL4) Associated Factor 11 (DCAF11): F 5’-GTAAGGGGGCGCTCTGATTG-3’, R 5’-ATCCCTCTCGGAAGAAAGCG-3’; Protein Kinase D3 (PRKD3): F 5’-TGCGAGGCGCGTGAAG-3’, R 5’-TAGTTAGCAGTGGCAGGCTC-3’; Chromosome 5 Open Reading Frame 22 (C5orf22): F 5’-GCCACCGCTTTACTGCAAAA-3’, R 5’-GATGATCCTCCACCACCCAC-3’; GAPDH: F 5’-GCACCGTCAAGGCTGAGAAC-3’, R 5’-TGGTGAAGACGCCAGTGGA-3’.

Statistical analysis

The quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. SPSS 25.0 software was conducted for statistical analysis. While paired t-test was employed for two-group comparisons between Normal and Tumor tissues of OS. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the post hoc Tukey test was used for multiple-group comparisons. Spearman’s correlation analysis was conducted to determine the correlation between the two groups. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Differentially expressed genes screening

In the training cohort, we identified a total of 2128 DEGs between OS and normal samples. Among these, 955 were up-regulated (e.g., CKS2, SMC1A, and HSPA13) and 1173 were down-regulated (e.g., GAS6, SORBS3, and PNPLA2). The volcano plots (Fig. 2A) and gene heatmaps (Fig. 2B) illustrate the distributions of DEGs. To obtain a distinct PPI network, corrected P < 0.05 and logFC > 2 were used as the cutoff criterion. we obtained 600 pairs (181 proteins) of proteins. Combined scores of more than 0.7 pairs were visualized using Cytospace software (Fig. 2C). The color indicates the degree, with a brighter color indicating a higher neuron degree.

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs

GO functional classification shows that the DEGs are significantly enriched in neutrophil degranulation, neutrophil activation involved in immune response, focal adhesion, and single-stranded DNA binding (Fig. 3A). A KEGG functional analysis demonstrated that DEGs were associated with Human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection and Central carbon metabolism in cancer (Fig. 3B). The GSEA enrichment analysis also revealed that these DEGs were enriched in immune-related functions or pathways such as T cell activation, T17 cell differentiation, and leukocyte migration (Fig. 3C-D).These pathways were mainly involved in the immune system, including the regulation of neutrophil migration.

Random forest screening for signature genes

A random forest classifier was used to obtain more reliable OS-signature genes from 2128 DEGs. We used a random survival forest approach to assess the relative importance of variables and the relationship between error rate and tree number. In the end, the model with 7 trees was chosen as the final parameter (Fig. 4A). Based on mean decreasing accuracy as well as the Gini coefficient, we ranked each gene according to its relative importance in the predictive model (Fig. 4B). The ANN analysis was conducted using 7 DEGs with MeanDecreaseGini greater than 1. Using the heatmap, we further confirmed the reliability of the seven driver genes’ classification effectiveness (Fig. 4C). Three of these genes (CIC, SLC16A2 and GTF2A2) were upregulated in HCs and four genes (DLGAP5, DCAF11, PRKD3, and C5orf22) were upregulated in OS patients.

Construction and validation of the ANN model

Our ANN analysis relied on the expression levels of seven OS-signature genes. The input layer comprises seven nodes. The output layer comprises two classes (Fig. 5). To evaluate its performance, we measured the area under the ROC curve. The training dataset AUC was 0.826, while the validation dataset AUC was 0.766 (Fig. 6A-B). This indicates good discrimination of this model for OS and normal samples.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

By performing immune infiltration analysis,, we analyzed 22 immune cell phenotypes in OS and HCs. The main objectives were to determine their association with the two groups and the immune infiltrate. A violin plot showing the results of the analysis of immune cell differences between the two groups (Fig. 7A-B) showed lower level macrophages in normal HCs than in OS group, while the levels of B cells, Plasma cells and Monocytes were higher than in OS group. Immune cell correlation heatmap analysis (Fig. 7C) showed that Dendritic cells had the most significant positive correlation with CD4 T cells (r = 0.51). Resting mast cells were negatively correlated with activated mast cells (r = − 0.57). The above results suggest that the genes we selected are closely related to the infiltration of immune cells.

Biomarkers expression validation

In order to further verify the expression levels of the genes in the diagnostic model in OS, we examined the expression of these 7 genes in the cancer tissues and adjacent tissues of OS patients. Compared with normal tissues, the transcriptional levels of three genes (CIC, SLC16A2 and GTF2A2) were downregulated while those of four genes (DCAF11, DLGAP5, PRKD3 and C5orf22) were upregulated in tumor tissues (Fig. 8A-G). This result is consistent with the expression trend shown in Fig. 4C. Furthermore, we also examined the expression of these 7 genes in hFOB1.19 cells and three types of OS cells, and compared the differences in expression. The results also showed that, compared with hFOB1.19 cells, the expressions of CIC, SLC16A2 and GTF2A2 were significantly downregulated in OS cells, while the levels of DCAF11, DLGAP5, PRKD3 and C5orf22 were significantly upregulated in OS cells (Fig. 9A-G). These results further suggest that the diagnostic model we have constructed is feasible for application in clinical testing.

Discussion

OS is a malignant tumor originating in bones with high morbidity among adolescents [3]. With the development of surgical resection and neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the survival rate and life expectancy of OS patients have been improved to some extent, but significant breakthroughs remain elusive [9]. Therefore, early diagnosis of OS is crucial for effective treatment and prognosis. In this study, we identified seven potential markers, including CIC, SLC16A2, DCAF11, DLGAP5, GTF2A2, PRKD3 and C5orf22, which are closely related to and significantly upregulated in OS patients.

With the widespread application of machine learning algorithms in the diagnosis and treatment of tumors, previous researchers have also developed various machine learning-based models for the diagnosis and prognosis of OS. Some researchers have combined the support vector machine and random forest algorithms to build a prediction model for OS recurrence. The accuracy of this model was very high (with an AUC value greater than 0.9) [10]. Other researchers integrated 10 machine learning methods, including random forest, to build a programmed cell death-based OS prognostic risk model. This model has also been proven to provide an accurate assessment of the prognosis for patients with OS [11]. Compared with prognostic models, there are relatively fewer studies that use Random forest or ANN to construct early diagnosis prediction models for OS. Some researchers have developed a diagnostic model for Bone-Related Malignancies (OS, Ewing’s sarcoma, multiple myeloma) using random forests. the diagnostic accuracy of the model was higher than 90% [12]. Shi and his colleagues screened 30 characteristic genes related to immune cell infiltration using ANN and applied them for the early diagnosis of OS [13]. Meanwhile, some researchers, based on the OS-related datasets in GEO, for the first time combined Random Forest and Artificial Neural Network to construct an early diagnosis model for OS and verified the diagnostic efficacy of the model [14]. The aforementioned report demonstrates that random forests and ANN play a crucial role in developing multi-gene early diagnostic models for OS.

On the other hand, the early research models for the early stage of OS were mainly based on the GEO database. However, the sample size of OS in the GEO database is relatively small, which can lead to overfitting of the model [13, 14]. Furthermore, the OS-related diagnostic models that were initially constructed have not undergone biological validation. Therefore, the clinical translational efficacy of these models needs to be evaluated. To address these shortcomings, this study was the first to integrate the OS-related data from the GEO, TARGET and TCGA databases. The OS diagnostic model constructed with a larger sample size has to some extent prevented the occurrence of model overfitting. Furthermore, we also examined the expression of the characteristic genes in the model in OS tissues and cells. The biological-level verification further demonstrated the accuracy of the early diagnosis model for OS that we constructed.

In our research, Seven genes were identified as characteristic genes for the early diagnosis of OS. CIC belongs to the high mobility group (HMG)-box superfamily of transcriptional repressors. CIC fusion with Double-Homeobox (CIC-DUX4) or Forkhead Box O4 (CIC-FOXO4) has been associated with a high prevalence of round cell sarcomas, among which CIC-DUX4 gene fusion holds the position of being the most prevalent genetic abnormality [15–17]. DCAF11 is a protein-coding gene, which is associated with Loeys-Dietz Syndrome 1. DCAF11 has been reported to be involved in the regulation of the cell cycle of OS cells [18]. DLGAP5 was originally recognized as a cell-cycle-regulated protein [19]. Researchers have found that Bisphenol A interacts with DLGAP5 to promote the malignant progression of OS [20]. GTF2A2 was reported to be down-regulated in OS patients, which is consistent with our results [21]. Combined with our findings, it is suggested that GTF2A2 may be potential biomarkers for diagnosis or molecules for understanding of OS tumorigenesis, however, the molecular mechanism still needs further study.

SLC16A2/PRKD3/C5orf22 has not yet been found to be associated with osteosarcoma. SLC16A2 is associated with Allan-Herndon-Dudley Syndrome (AHDS) and Spastic Quadriplegia [22, 23]. Different mutations of SLC16A2 may affect the severity of the clinical phenotype in AHDS [24]. As for tumors, SLC16A2 has been reported to be closely associated with the overall survival time in breast cancer [25]. PRKD3 is found to be related to cell survival, migration, differentiation, and proliferation. PRKD3 is remarkably overexpressed in oral squamous cell carcinoma patients, which is associated with metastasis incidence and poor prognosis [26]. Another study confirmed that high expression of PRKD3 could promote invasion and migration of prostate cancer [27]. C5orf22 was reported to be overexpressed in breast cancer cells, which predicted decreased survival rates in breast cancer patients [28]. Furthermore, C5orf22 depletion will cause aberrant splicing and gene expression related to cell growth and immunomodulation in non-small cell lung cancer [29]. This study utilized tissue and cell samples to detect and compare the expression differences of these 7 genes in the OS group and the normal group. The experimental results show that in OS tumor tissues and cells, the expression levels of CIC, SLC16A2 and GTF2A2 were significantly downregulated, while the expression levels of DCAF11, DLGAP5, PRKD3 and C5orf22 were significantly upregulated. Previous reports and our research findings have further demonstrated that these genes play a crucial role in the occurrence and progression of OS.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the limited number of normal control samples may not fully capture healthy heterogeneity, potentially affecting model robustness and generalizability, and reflects the scarcity of publicly available OS datasets with matched controls. Second, the binary “Gene Score” transformation used for normalization helps mitigate batch effects and outliers but inevitably loses quantitative information, potentially reducing sensitivity to subtle expression changes; alternative normalization methods could be explored in future studies with larger, more homogeneous datasets. Finally, although we applied commonly accepted thresholds (P < 0.05, |log2FC| ≥ 1) with FDR adjustment for differential expression analysis, the small sample size may reduce statistical power and increase variability.

Conclusion

In summary, we screened out seven hub genes by integrated bioinformatics analysis, and a random forest combined with an ANN model for OS diagnosis was successfully constructed and validated. Our findings identified potential biomarkers for the early diagnosis of OS, which may offer novel approaches for diagnosing and treating of OS patients.
