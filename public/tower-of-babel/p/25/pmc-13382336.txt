Bayesian modelling of spatio-temporal dynamics for early-warning and control of severe malaria in Cameroon
Authors: Akindeh Mbuh Nji, Taiwo M Adegoke, Sheetal P Silal, Ali Innocent, Pascal F Madeleine, Masso T Sylvanie, Calvino F Tah, Andrillene LD Wondeu, Christophe Antinio-Nkondjio, Jude D Bigoga, Rose FG Leke, Gillian Stresman, Liwang Cui, Wilfred F Mbacham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria is a significant public health problem in Cameroon where spatial and temporal heterogeneities in transmission make early-warning and control complicated. The traditional forecasting methods have the limitation that they are not always able to model both the spatial dependency and the uncertainty; therefore, restricting their functionality.MethodsWe used a spatio-temporal bayesian model that incorporates a Stochastic Partial Differential Equation (SPDE) that models the spatial correlations and an autoregressive time prior AR(1), solved by Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA). The analysis of severe malaria cases in 2015–2024 was done separately for children <5 years, adults and pregnant women, and rainfall as a climatic covariate and population adjusted offsets for at-risk individuals. Exceedance probabilities were calculated considering historical risk to identify hotspots. Model performance was evaluated through DIC, WAIC and calibration diagnostics.ResultsSevere malaria risk was positively related to rainfall and this relationship was observed for all demographic groups. The Far North had long-term high risk foci; secondary risks were noted in the North, Centre and Littoral. The most intense hotspots were observed in pregnant women and children.ConclusionsThe SPDE-AR(1) model offers a strong and uncertainty-based early warning system for severe malaria in Cameroon, which allows interventions to be targeted in areas and populations at high risk.Keywords: Malaria forecasting, Early-warning systems, Bayesian geostatistics, SPDE–AR(1), INLA

Introduction

Malaria is among the most frequently caused diseases in the absence of a cure and has caused significant morbidity and mortality in sub-Saharan Africa, and Cameroon is no exception (World Health Organization, 2023; Hay et al., 2004). Although prevention and treatment have advanced, the disease still presents a disproportionate burden on vulnerable populations, including children under five years and pregnant women (Bhatt et al., 2015; Cameroon Ministry of Public Health, 2022). Malaria distribution is very heterogeneous spatio-temporally and is associated with climate variability, ecological factors, and socio-demographic conditions (Gething et al., 2011; Weiss et al., 2019). The ability to predict outbreaks and quantify epidemic risk at subnational scales is thus key to enhancing malaria early-warning systems and informing specific responses.

Traditional surveillance systems within endemic environments are largely retrospective and hardly capable of delivering timely indicators on decision-making (World Health Organization, 2018). Machine learning and statistical models have been utilized more in malaria forecasting; however, most models have major limitations. ARIMA Autoregressive and seasonal ARIMA models are used to capture the dynamics of time series but overlook the dependence between regions (Kim et al., 2019). Other spatial models like the Besag- York-Mollie (BYM) formulation uses regional correlation but simplistically models time (Besag et al., 1991; Blangiardo and Cameletti, 2015). Bayesian structural time series (BSTS) and variants can take into account trends, seasonality, but do not explicitly propagate spatially (Scott and Varian, 2014). Additionally, most of them give emphasis on point forecasts and not the decision-relevant quantification of uncertainty, which is restrictive in application in operational early-warning systems (Held et al., 2017).

The current developments in the Bayesian geostatistics offer means to address these issues. A computationally efficient model of Matern Gaussian random fields is provided by the stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) framework, which has the capacity to represent the finescale modelling of spatial correlation on irregular domains (Lindgren et al., 2011). This can be used to coherently smooth and persist across spatio-temporal scales when used in combination with autoregressive (AR(1)) temporal priors. Penalised Complexity (PC) priors further guarantee strong regularisation, which shrands towards parsimonious models but can be complex where the information supports it (Simpson et al., 2017). The technique of Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) enables inference where computational bottlenecks of Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) would otherwise be encountered in high-dimensional Bayesian contexts (Rue et al., 2009). These developments present possibilities to create early-warning models that are not merely truthful, but also interpretable and uncertain.

This paper suggests a probabilistic early-warning system of severe malaria in Cameroon, which combines SP DE-based spatial model and autoregressive time dynamics, rainfall forecasting and population-adjusted over-compensations. The method produces exceedance probabilities on a relative basis to adaptive thresholds and identifies areas with a high likelihood of exceeding that threshold as hotspots. This gives a direct and interpretable connection of the statistical forecasting to operational decision-making.

Threefold are the contributions of this work. First, we show that SPDE-AR(1)-based models can generate strong malaria risk forecasts with an explicit measure of uncertainty. Second, we suggest an exceedance probability model in order to convert predictions into binary hotspot alerts, in line with early-warning use. Thirdly, we present one of the first end-to-end probabilistic early-warning analyses of malaria in Cameroon, in which a 12-month forecast was carried out using the proposed SPDE–AR(1) model. In a broader sense, the framework shows how contemporary Bayesian geostatistical frameworks can support noise-tolerant, uncertainty-conscious forecasting tools of epidemic-prone diseases under resource-constrained conditions.

Methods

Study area

Cameroon, a Central African country situated between latitudes 2∘ to 13∘ N and longitudes 8∘ to 16∘ E, encompasses a variety of climatic zones ranging from humid tropical forests in the south to arid regions in the north (World Health Organization, 2023; Paaijmans et al., 2009). These diverse environmental conditions shape the spatial and temporal dynamics of malaria transmission.

The country is divided into ten regions: Adamawa, Centre, East, Far North, Littoral, North, North-West, South, South-West, and West each exhibiting distinct topographical features, climate patterns, and population structures that influence malaria spread. The southern and eastern regions receive high annual rainfall and maintain dense forest cover, creating ideal breeding conditions for Anopheles mosquitoes (World Health Organization, 2023; Gething et al., 2011). In contrast, the northern regions experience lower rainfall and drier conditions, which contribute to regional differences in monthly transmission rates (World Health Organization, 2023; Gething et al., 2011).

Cameroon's population of over 27 million includes vulnerable groups such as children under five, pregnant women, and rural or semi-urban adults. A decentralized health system supports disease monitoring through regional reporting into the national health information system (World Health Organization, 2023).

Data source

This study conducted a spatio-temporal analysis of severe malaria risks in Cameroon using monthly data from 2015 to 2024. Case data were obtained through the National Health Information System (NHIS) provided by the Ministry of Public Health, Cameroon. These data included annual counts of severe malaria cases reported across the country's ten administrative regions and were disaggregated into three demographic groups: children under five years old, adults aged 15 years and above, and pregnant women.

Environmental data, specifically rainfall, were collected from the Cameroon Meteorological Unit. Annual averages were computed for each region and aligned with the corresponding malaria data to assess the influence of environmental factors on transmission. These environmental variables were incorporated into the statistical models to explore their role in malaria dynamics.

Geospatial data were sourced from the Global Administrative Areas (GADM) database, with administrative boundary shapefiles obtained in GeoPackage format from GADM version 4.1 (GADM, 2024). These spatial boundaries enabled accurate integration of health and environmental datasets by providing precise geographical coordinates for each region.

Fig. 1 shows a series of choropleth maps of the spatial patterns of severe malaria incidence in Cameroon according to the 10 regions of the country, broken down by three of the main population groups: Pregnant Women (left), Children Under 5 (center) and Adults (right). In the three maps, Far North region is always having the most cases of severe malaria, representing its bright yellow coloring. This tendency is especially prominent in children below 5 and the adult population, which indicates the high susceptibility of the region, and perhaps higher exposure to transmission sources. The North, North-West, and the Centre regions also exhibit a significant load in all groups, the majority of which are in orange shade and red, indicating a moderately high number of cases. On the other hand, the South and East regions are always dark purple throughout the groups with comparatively fewer numbers of severe malaria cases. Interestingly, Littoral and West regions exhibit moderate intensity of cases among adults and children but a lesser number of cases among pregnant women.

Model fit diagnostics showed that the SPDE – AR(1) models performed well for all demographic groups. The pregnant women had a Deviance Information Criterion (DIC) value of 12,770, while the children under five and adults had 13,937 and 14,650, respectively. Patterns similar to those in the WAIC were seen for the log conditional predictive ordinate (log-CPO) diagnostics. The measures were employed to assess model fit and predictive consistency within each demographic group.

Strong spatial heterogeneity in the risk of malaria was found through forecast exceedance probability maps (Fig. 2). The Far North had near-certain exceedance probabilities (>0.9) in the short term (forecast horizons) and then higher risks in the North, Centre, and Littoral. There was a more scattered pattern of risk in adults with moderate exceedance in the Far North and Centre, and Littoral and consistently lower scores in the South and East.

The analysis of hotspots intensity (Fig. 3) validated the continuity of the Far North as the most common high-risk area of all strata. Among children, the Far North was a hot spot in almost all forecast months, and the North and Centre and Littoral shared intermediate shares of hotspots (0.25–0.50). The strongest spatial gradient was found in pregnant women, with heavy hotspots in the Far North and scattered warnings in Centre and Littoral. Overall adult hotspots persistence was less strong, but the Far North stayed above threshold during a number of forecast months.

On the whole, the findings indicate that the SPDE-AR(1) model is effective in reflecting spatial clustering of malaria severe risk, emphasizing vulnerable groups, and making probabilistic predictions of hotspots in accordance with observed epidemiological trends in Cameroon.

Discussion

This paper shows that Bayesian SPDE-AR(1) is an effective model to explain the spatial and temporal pattern of severe malaria risk in Cameroon based on routinely reported surveillance. With the combination of spatial random fields, autoregressive dynamics, covariates of rainfall, and population-adjusted offsets, the model provides well-calibrated forecasts, as well as stable posterior estimates across demographic layers. The results show that there are significant and enduring spatial heterogeneity with the Far North having the largest probabilities of surpassing risk thresholds across the forecast horizon. The risk is found in the North, Centre and Littoral zones as secondary zones of high risk, and in the South and East as always below the adaptive thresholds. By these internal consistency patterns, it is suggested that the model can describe specific spatial gradients of malaria severity without the use of external information.

The exceedance probability framework is an efficient linkage between statistical predictive models and operational requirements of early-warning systems since it transforms continuous risk estimates into accurate actionable signals. This methodology is continually found to pinpoint regions of long-term relative high-risk profiles and highlights more distinct hotspots of children under five and among pregnant women. The fact that the model showed a significantly higher result in the pregnant-women stratum implies a resultant difference in data stability or reporting patterns among the demographic groups. The probabilistic outputs give a deeper basis to decision-making than the traditional point forecasts because by measuring uncertainty directly the probabilistic outputs facilitate more efficient operational responses in situations where the variability has to be accommodated in operational responses. Taken together, these findings suggest that the SPDE-AR(1) system provides an effective, interpretable and cost-efficient platform on early-warning surveillance in Cameroon.

Conclusion

This paper used a Bayesian SPDE-AR(1) modelling structure to predict the occurrence of severe malaria in Cameroon, considering both rainfall covaria, population offsets, and exceedance probability levels. The method produced consistent spatio-temporal forecasts, measures of uncertainty, and hot spot flags. Findings repeatedly placed the Far North as the centre of high risk among children, adults, and pregnant women with secondary hotspots in the North, Centre, and Littoral. Stratified risk modelling was shown to be valuable by predictive performance measures indicating that the models were well-calibrated, especially in the case of pregnant women.

The framework intersects statistical inference and operational decision-making, providing an interpretable and uncertainty-sensitive data miner required by malaria early-warn systems operating under resource constraints to translate forecasts into exceedance probabilities.

Recommendation

Based on these results, we present major recommendations to improve malaria surveillance, early-warning, and control in Cameroon:
