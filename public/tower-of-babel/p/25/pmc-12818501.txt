Effect of White Noise Therapy Combined with Cognitive Behavioural Therapy on Clinical Symptoms, Psychological Stress and Sleep Quality in Depressed Patients
Authors: Shuai Wang, Yan Chen, JieFeng Liao, LiHui Dong, Tao Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to investigate the effects of white noise therapy combined with cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) on clinical manifestations, psychological stress and sleep quality in patients with depression.Methods:This study retrospectively analysed 136 patients with mild to moderate depression treated between January 2022 and December 2024. On the basis of their treatment timeline, the patients were divided into conventional group (n = 47, conventional therapy, January–December 2022), CBT group (n = 45, conventional therapy + personalised CBT, January–December 2023) and white noise group (n = 44, conventional therapy + personalised CBT + white noise therapy, January–December 2024). The clinical symptoms [24-item Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD-24), Stroop test], psychological stress [Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), Gross Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ)], sleep quality [Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)], cognitive fusion level [Cognitive Fusion Questionnaire (CFQ-F)], hope level [Herth Hope Index (HHI)] and experiential avoidance degree [Acceptance and Action Questionnaire-II (AAQ-II)] were compared before and 12 weeks post-treatment.Results:Compared with pre-therapy levels, statistically significant improvements at 12 weeks were observed for all three groups (P < 0.05). These included reductions in HAMD-24 scores, Stroop mean reaction time, PSS-10 scores, ERQ suppression subscale scores, PSQI scores and AAQ-II scores, along with increases in Stroop correct responses, ERQ reappraisal subscale scores, CFQ-F scores and HHI scores. Post-therapy between-group comparisons revealed that the white noise group exhibited superior outcomes versus both the conventional and CBT groups across all metrics, while the CBT group outperformed the conventional group on all assessment parameters (P < 0.05).Conclusion:The findings of this retrospective study suggest that white noise therapy combined with CBT may have positive effects on patients with mild to moderate depression, including improvements in clinical symptoms, psychological stress response and sleep quality. These benefits were demonstrated through reduced depression severity, increased use of cognitive reappraisal strategies, enhanced cognitive fusion, reduced experiential avoidance and elevated hope levels.

INTRODUCTION

Depression stands as one of the most devastating mental disorders worldwide, characterised by persistent low mood, impaired cognitive function, sleep disturbances and reduced motivation. As highlighted in Herrman et al.’s[1] 2022 Lancet World Psychiatric Association Commission Initiative on Depression, approximately 280 million people (5.0% of adults) suffer from depression globally. Their study further emphasised that depression is the leading cause of disability among non-fatal global illnesses. Shorey et al.’s[2] meta-analysis of 72 global studies (2001–2020) confirmed a 37% increase in depression prevalence between 2011 and 2020, significantly higher than the 24% recorded from 2001 to 2010. Mannion et al.[3] revealed that approximately 30% of patients show no response to antidepressants, and approximately 20% relapse even after sustained treatment. Side effects such as weight gain and sexual dysfunction also compromise treatment adherence. Their study underscores how family support enhances patients’ social functioning, indirectly validating the necessity of combining psychotherapy with medication. Conventional psychotherapy often lacks specificity, focusing primarily on symptom relief rather than addressing root causes. Its limited capacity to account for individual variations—such as symptom patterns, living environments and personal needs—further constrains clinical effectiveness.

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) constitutes a psychotherapeutic approach grounded in Beck’s cognitive–behavioural model (cognition–emotion–behaviour cycle). This modality facilitates the reconstruction of adaptive cognitive–behavioural patterns by identifying and modifying maladaptive cognitions (e.g., ‘I am worthless’) and disrupting depressive behavioural cycles (e.g., excessive rumination, avoidance and sedentary behaviour), thereby enhancing stress-coping competencies.[4] Although the Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments (CANMAT) guidelines[5] and the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE)[6] endorse pharmacotherapy combined with CBT as first-line therapy, its clinical implementation encounters substantial challenges. As CBT relies on face-to-face therapy by professional therapists, its accessibility is insufficient in areas with scarce medical resources. Some patients may drop out because of the time-consuming nature of the treatment or the lack of targeted content.[78] Notably, >70% of patients with depression exhibit sleep disturbances, including sleep initiation and maintenance difficulties. Sleep quality deterioration may induce amygdala hyperactivation and prefrontal cortex hypofunction, exacerbating depressive symptomatology through the biopsychosocial maladaptive interplay.[910] Developing safe, feasible multimodal therapeutic frameworks targeting multidimensional symptoms represents an urgent clinical priority.

White noise constitutes an acoustic signal characterised by uniform spectral energy distribution across the full frequency domain [i.e., constant power spectral density within the audible range (0–20 kHz)]. Common manifestations include natural ambient sounds such as rainfall and ocean waves. This auditory stimulus enhances δ-band neural synchronisation, induces lower global consciousness states and potentiates phasic dopamine release, thereby producing analgesic, sedative and anxiolytic effects.[11] Although no direct clinical evidence has been established to define a standardised protocol for combining white noise therapy with CBT, existing literature suggests their complementary potential. A randomised controlled trial demonstrated that CBT improves occupational and social functioning in patients with depression.[12] Dafsari et al.[13] and Buschner et al.[14] indicated that CBT reduces depressive severity and enhances cognitive flexibility. Ebben et al.[15] showed that white noise therapy significantly improves sleep quality and duration among individuals in high-noise environments such as New York City. Systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Nigg et al.[16] suggested that white noise therapy may increase task-related interest in individuals with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. Theoretically, CBT focuses on cognitive restructuring and symptom reduction, and white noise therapy may improve sleep and stabilise mood by modulating brainwave rhythms. However, mechanistic studies on their combined effects and an established clinical pathway for their integrated implementation are lacking. Therefore, the present study investigates the effects of combined white noise and personalised CBT on clinical symptoms, psychological stress and sleep quality in patients with depression to provide empirical evidence for integrated therapeutic strategies and address this gap in practical application.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

General Information

Between January 2022 and December 2024, 168 patients from the Third People’s Hospital of Tongling underwent eligibility assessment. In accordance with the inclusion/exclusion criteria, 11 cases were initially excluded because of failing to meet the study design requirements [specific reasons: one case due to comorbid chronic renal failure, one case due to age (59 years), two cases due to suicide attempts within the preceding 6 months, two cases due to having received systematic psychotherapy within the previous 6 months, two cases due to comorbid conditions affecting social functioning (severe rheumatoid arthritis and post-stroke hemiplegia) and three cases due to lactation]. Consequently, 157 patients who met the inclusion criteria were initially selected as the pre-propensity score matching (PSM) sample. Informed consent was obtained from all the patients and their families, and the study protocol was approved by the Third People’s Hospital of Tongling’s ethics committee (Approval No. 202504021) in accordance with the ethical principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.[17]

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

(1) The inclusion criteria were as follows: conforming to the diagnostic criteria for depression as stipulated in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (Fifth Edition) (DSM-5) issued by the American Psychiatric Association[18]; firstly diagnosed with depression; symptoms not caused by physical diseases (such as hypothyroidism), other mental disorders (such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and anxiety disorder) or psychoactive substances (such as alcohol and drugs); complete clinical data; age between 18 and 55 years old; and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24) score between 17 and 24 points.

(2) The exclusion criteria were as follows: pregnant or lactating; having severe physical diseases or significant intellectual deficits, accompanied with severe suicidal attempts/behaviours (suicidal attempts or suicide attempts within the last 6 months), catatonic state, incomplete insight or refusal to accept nursing therapy; having infectious diseases or severe heart, liver or kidney function disorders; experiencing a worsening of the condition during the therapy and unable to continue; having diseases that affect social function in the past and received systematic psychological treatment within the last 6 months.

METHODS

All the participants received foundational pharmacotherapy with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, such as escitalopram or sertraline. Concomitant with standard pharmacological treatment, a 12-week ambulatory therapy was delivered across groups.

Conventional Group

The conventional group received conventional therapy and had a weekly outpatient follow-up visit, each lasting approximately 20–30 minutes. The severity of depression and functional level of the patients were evaluated using depression scales (such as HAMD-24). The patients’ feelings were heard, and emotional counselling was provided. Guidance on treatment medications (types, dosages and side effects of antidepressants) was given. The patients were encouraged to develop regular schedules, healthy diets and moderate exercise. They were recommended to join depression support groups, and information on community rehabilitation institutions was provided. Medication compliance was confirmed at each outpatient visit, and the onset time of the medication and common side effects and their management methods were explained. The patients’ medication responses were recorded, and dosages were adjusted or medications changed in a timely manner. The suicide risk of each patient was assessed at each outpatient visit. If a clear suicide plan was identified, then the family was immediately contacted and the crisis therapy process was initiated (such as emergency medical treatment and 24-hour accompaniment).

CBT Group

Building upon conventional therapy, personalised CBT was administered through weekly outpatient sessions lasting 30 minutes each. (1) During sessions 1–2, interview-based assessments were conducted to identify triggers of depressive episodes; core symptoms; characteristic negative thinking patterns and current levels of occupational, social and self-care functioning. The principles of CBT were explained using visual aids and multimedia materials, with personal experiences illustrating the cognitive–emotional–behavioural interconnection. Individualised treatment goals were collaboratively established. (2) Sessions 3–10 followed a progressive structure: initial focus was placed on recognising cognitive distortions and identifying negative thought patterns through Socratic questioning to evaluate supporting/contradicting evidence, followed by substitution with rational alternatives. Tailored measures included the following: ① psychoeducation through graphical materials and mind maps explaining behavioural activation and exposure principles; ② collaboratively developed daily activity schedules with graduated progression from low-demand to achievement-oriented tasks; ③ hierarchical exposure planning targeting specific avoidance behaviours (e.g., progressing from 5-minute phone calls to 10-minute in-person meetings) and ④ prioritised problem-solving using a five-step approach (problem definition, option generation, consequence evaluation, plan implementation and review adjustment). (3) Sessions 11–12 emphasised relapse prevention and stress management, teaching progressive muscle relaxation and mindful breathing techniques (10-minute daily practice). Therapy procedures were reviewed, self-monitoring through mood logging was implemented and relapse warning plans were established (e.g., contacting therapists when experiencing early awakening for 2 consecutive days or appetite loss, followed by reactivation of basic activity schedules). (4) Standardised operational framework and individualisation criteria for personalised CBT: The personalised CBT protocol was based on standardised procedures recommended by the CANMAT and NICE clinical practice guidelines for depression, with individualisation adjustments determined in accordance with the following three core criteria: ① symptom stratification: cognitive restructuring intensity was tailored based on HAMD-24 scores (mild-to-moderate subgroup) and Stroop test results (degree of cognitive control impairment); ② individual characteristics: initial interviews identified triggers of depressive episodes (e.g., work stress/interpersonal issues/medical conditions), typical negative thinking patterns (e.g., catastrophising/overgeneralisation) and social functional impairments (e.g., social avoidance/declined work capacity), all of which informed the design of graded exposure hierarchies and behavioural activation tasks; ③ therapeutic feedback: patient acceptance of treatment content was assessed every 4 weeks using the ‘CBT Compliance and Satisfaction Scale’ (developed by the research team based on clinical practice, Cronbach’s α = 0.86), with task difficulty adjusted accordingly. A standardised operational manual for personalised CBT was established, defining fixed procedures for core modules (cognitive restructuring, behavioural activation, exposure training and relapse prevention). For example, cognitive restructuring included three mandatory steps (‘negative thought identification–evidence examination–alternative thought establishment’), and personalisation was confined to task content, difficulty and frequency to ensure a balance between structural integrity and individualised adaptation.

White Noise Group

The white noise group received CBT combined with white noise therapy conducted twice a week in the outpatient department, with each session lasting 60 minutes. (1) During the initial session, the patients were exposed to natural (rainforest, waves, streams and birdsong) and environmental (café ambience, page-turning sounds, air conditioning and train tracks) white noise varieties. Two to three preferred sounds were selected on the basis of patient reporting, with the subjectively most relaxing/concentration-enhancing sound designated as the primary stimulus. (2) Sessions 2–6 integrated preferred white noise with sensory grounding and negative thought buffering through the following: ① guided ‘5-4-3-2-1 sensory grounding technique’ during noise exposure (e.g., identifying five visible objects, four tactile sensations, three non-noise auditory inputs, two olfactory stimuli and one taste while listening to wave sounds); ② intermediate-intensity noise exposure (65–70 dB) simulating chaotic environments, during which patients performed arithmetic tasks on cards followed by guided reflection on attention allocation and negative thought triggers. The patients then read aloud positive self-affirmations and motivational quotes printed on cards whilst maintaining focus amidst noise, reinforcing cognitive control capabilities. (3) Sessions 7–11 implemented graded noise exposure hierarchies targeting specific avoidance behaviours: baseline noise desensitisation (35–45 dB single-source noise in therapy room) → multi-source exposure (50–60 dB combined noises during simple cognitive tasks such as number sequencing) → semi-naturalistic simulation (fast-food/retail environments with 65–75 dB multi-noise exposure whilst performing independent ordering/payment procedures). All exercises incorporated extended exhalation breathing patterns (4 seconds inhalation − 2–4 seconds breath hold − 6 seconds exhalation). (4) The final session focused on transforming white noise into daily self-regulation tools: 20-minute pre-sleep relaxation listening, high-frequency noise during anxiety episodes and mid-frequency noise upon nighttime awakening paired with positive reality affirmation (e.g., warm blankets and remaining sleep time) until resuming sleep.

Quality Control

(1) Practitioner qualifications and training:

① Qualification requirements: Both lead therapists hold IACBT-certified CBT therapist credentials with over 5 years of depression treatment experience. The three assistant therapists were psychiatry residents who completed 60 hours of CBT theoretical training (covering core techniques such as cognitive restructuring and exposure) and 30 hours of simulated practical sessions.

② Standardised supervision: Weekly group supervision was conducted by a senior clinical psychologist (10+ years of experience) holding advanced CBT supervision certification. Individualised treatment plans (e.g., appropriateness of exposure hierarchy relative to avoidance severity) were reviewed, and 10% of therapy recordings (patient-consented) were evaluated for technical adherence. The inter-therapist consistency Kappa value was 0.89 during the study period, indicating high agreement with standard operating procedure requirements.

(2) Patient compliance management:

① Compliance monitoring: Three methods were used: weekly nurse-led attendance reminders (absenteeism recorded), daily family-supervised home practice logs (e.g., 10-minute mindful breathing) and daily check-ins via patient–nurse communication groups (white noise exposure duration + CBT task completion).

② Compliance outcomes: Among the 136 patients, compliance rates (completing ≥10 sessions + ≥80% home practice adherence) were 91.49% (43/47) in the conventional group, 93.33% (42/45) in the CBT group and 95.45% (42/44) in the white noise group. No significant intergroup difference was observed (χ2 = 0.578, P = 0.749), indicating balanced and satisfactory compliance across groups.

Observation Indicators

(1) Clinical symptoms: Stroop and HAMD-24 tests were used to assess the three groups’ cognitive control and depression severity before and after the 12-week therapy, respectively. Hamilton[19] created the HAMD scale, and Tang et al.[20] updated it in Chinese. It has 24 elements in seven categories, such as weight, anxiety/somatisation, cognitive impairment and diurnal fluctuation. A 5-point Likert scale (0–4, from no to extremely severe) is used to rate each item according to symptom severity. The critical score for depression is 17, and the overall score ranges from 0 to 96. Mild to moderate depression is indicated by a total score between 17 and 24, and severe depression is indicated by a total score higher than 24. The severity of the depressed mood increases with the score. The HAMD scale’s Cronbach’s α value in this study was 0.894. Stroop was the one who created the Stroop test.[21] The Chinese version of the Stroop test, which was validated by Chen et al.,[22] was used in this study. It has 20 questions, including one that asked individuals to identify the colour of the word ‘blue’ whilst it was printed in red. The average reaction time (the sum of reaction times of correct answers/the number of correct answers) and number of correct answers in the Stroop test were compared among the three groups. (2) Psychological stress: the three groups’ perceived stress and emotional regulation were evaluated using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) and gross Emotional Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ) before and after the 12-week therapy. Cohen et al.[23] created the PSS, and Wang et al.[24] translated it into Chinese. It has 10 items, each evaluated from 0 to 4 on a 5-point Likert scale (from ‘never’ to ‘very often’). The total score ranges from 0 to 40. A high felt stress level is indicated by a high score. Gross and John[25] created the ERQ, and Wang et al.[26] translated it into Chinese. It has 10 items divided into two dimensions: expressive suppression (four items, 4–28 points) and cognitive reappraisal (six items, 6–42 points), each of which is assessed on a 7-point Likert scale (from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’; scores range from 1 to 7). A great inclination towards the relevant emotional control approach is indicated by high scores in each dimension. The PSS-10, cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression components of the ERQ in this study had Cronbach’s α values of 0.826, 0.852 and 0.797, respectively. (3) Sleep quality and cognitive fusion level: the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and Cognitive Fusion Questionnaire (CFQ-F) were used to measure the three groups’ levels of cognitive fusion and sleep quality prior to and 12 weeks following the therapy. Buysse et al.[27] created the PSQI, and Liu and Tang[28] translated it into Chinese. Sleep onset delay and the usage of sleeping tablets are two of its seven components. The overall score ranges from 0 to 21, where a high score denotes poor sleep quality. Gillanders et al.[29] created the CFQ-F, and Zhang et al.[30] rewrote it in Chinese. The overall score ranges from 9 to 63, with a high score denoting a high degree of cognitive fusion. It consists of nine items, each rated on a 7-point Likert scale (‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’, scored 1–7). The PSQI and CFQ in this investigation had Cronbach’s α values of 0.857 and 0.921, respectively. (4) Levels of hope and experiential avoidance: the three groups’ levels of hope and experience avoidance were measured using the Herth Hope Index (HHI) and Acceptance and Action Questionnaire-II (AAQ-II) before and 12 weeks after the therapy. Herth[3132] designed and refined the HHI, and Zhao and Wang[33] translated it into Chinese. Having a good outlook on the present and future (items 1, 2, 6 and 11), performing positive acts (items 4, 7, 10 and 12) and keeping strong connections with people (items 3, 5, 8 and 9) are the three aspects covered by its 12 items. Each item is scored in a 4-point Likert scale from 1 to 4 (from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’). The overall score ranges from 12 to 48, with a high score denoting great hope. Bond et al.[34] created the AAQ-II, and Cao et al.[35] rewrote it in Chinese. The seven items in the test are assessed on a 7-point Likert scale from ‘never’ to ‘always’ (1–7). The total score ranges from 7 to 49, with a high score denoting a high level of experiential avoidance. The HHI and AAQ-II in this investigation had Cronbach’s α values of 0.824 and 0.886, respectively.

Statistical Methods

(1) Basic statistical tests: Data organisation and table construction were performed using Microsoft Excel (Version 2206, Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS software (IBM Corporation, USA, Version 27.0). Normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄± s). The normality of data distribution was firstly assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the results indicated conformity to a normal distribution (P > 0.05). When homogeneity of variance (assessed by Levene’s test) was satisfied (P > 0.05), intergroup and intragroup comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests and paired samples t-tests, respectively. Count data were expressed as n (%) and assessed using the chi-square test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among multiple groups, with post-hoc pairwise comparisons conducted using the LSD-t test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

(2) Control of confounding factors: Propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to control for confounding variables using group assignment as a multinomial dependent variable (conventional group = 0, CBT group = 1, white noise group = 2). Multivariate logistic regression models were constructed using age, disease duration, gender and educational attainment as independent variables to calculate the pooled propensity score (PS). A 1:1 nearest neighbour matching method was applied with a matching width of 0.2 times the standard deviation of PS scores across all three groups. Post-matching analysis evaluated covariates using standardised mean difference (SMD) tests, with SMD values < 0.1 indicating statistically significant group balance.

RESULTS

Baseline Data

The initial screening identified 157 eligible patients as the pre-matching sample. On the basis of their treatment timeline, they were divided into conventional group (n = 55, conventional therapy, January–December 2022); CBT group (n = 52, conventional therapy + personalised CBT, January–December 2023) and white noise group (n = 50, conventional therapy + personalised CBT + white noise therapy, January–December 2024). After PSM matching, 21 patients with no suitable matches were excluded (8 from conventional, 7 from CBT and 6 from white noise group), leaving 136 patients for final analysis (conventional: n = 47, CBT: n = 45, white noise: n = 44). After PSM matching, the baseline data of the three groups, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), marital status, household registration type, educational level, depression severity, co-occurrence of anxiety and types of antidepressant drugs, were compared and showed good balance (P > 0.05, all SMD < 0.1; Tables 1 and 2).

Sleep Quality and Cognitive Fusion Level

DISCUSSION

The precise etiopathogenesis of depression remains incompletely elucidated. Affected individuals frequently present with impaired attentional control, sleep architecture disruptions and anxiety symptomatology. Although integrated pharmacotherapy and psychological therapies yield measurable therapeutic benefits, suboptimal treatment responses persist in substantial patient subsets. White noise therapy constitutes an accessible adjunctive modality that may synergistically augment conventional treatments. This non-invasive therapy expands the therapeutic armamentarium, offering multimodal management options. Therefore, a thorough study of its application effect on patients with depression has certain reference value for clinical treatment.

Awada et al.[36] found that white noise can enhance the sustained attention, accuracy and performance speed of people with inattentiveness, reduce stress levels and improve working memory. Furukawa et al.[37] pointed out that CBT is effective in treating insomnia and depression. The results of the present study show that after 12 weeks of therapy, the white noise group had lower HAMD-24 scores, shorter average reaction times in the Stroop test, lower AAQ-II scores, more correct answers in the Stroop test and higher CFQ-F scores compared with the conventional and CBT groups. These findings are consistent with previous research, suggesting that within the retrospective analytical framework of this study, the combination of white noise therapy and CBT is associated with improved clinical symptoms, enhanced cognitive fusion and reduced experiential avoidance in patients with depression. The potential explanations for these findings include the following: firstly, patients with depression often exhibit cognitive biases such as overgeneralisation and catastrophic thinking. CBT employs Socratic questioning to guide patients in evaluating evidence supporting or contradicting their automatic thoughts, enabling them to identify flaws in their thinking patterns and replace negative thoughts with rational and objective alternatives, thereby reducing negative emotions and alleviating depressive symptoms.[38] Experiential avoidance refers to attempts to evade or suppress internal experiences such as distressing thoughts, feelings or memories. CBT addresses specific avoidance behaviours through graded exposure training, emphasising the acceptance of emotions and internal experiences. This approach helps patients recognise that thoughts are not equivalent to reality and that attempts to suppress or avoid negative emotions are often counterproductive. By fostering their acceptance of negative emotions, patients can reduce their disruptive impact, redirect energy previously spent on avoidance and achieve flexible cognitive fusion.[39]

Secondly, white noise modulates amygdalar hyperactivity via auditory pathways, thereby attenuating negative valence-related neural firing and reducing hypervigilance to adverse stimuli. This finding establishes a neurobiological foundation for CBT components—specifically cognitive defusion and restructuring protocols. Amygdalar suppression facilitates metacognitive processing, enabling patients to adopt an ‘observer perspective’ towards thoughts rather than experiencing emotional inundation. Concurrently, sustained auditory input provides external sensory anchoring, redirecting attentional resources from internal ruminations to exteroceptive sound frequency perception. Through repeated attentional reallocation training, cognitive fusion diminishes, promoting insight that ‘thoughts represent transient neural events rather than objective reality’. As a broad-spectrum stimulus, white noise can trigger the stochastic resonance phenomenon in the auditory system through mechanisms such as neural feedback, suppress the slow wave oscillations related to the resting state in the auditory cortex, make neurons easily activated, increase the synchrony and frequency of neuronal firing, enhance the activity level of neurons and their responsiveness to input and improve the cross-sensory information perception and processing abilities such as touch, vision and hearing, thereby alleviating various clinical symptoms. Combining white noise therapy with cognitive restructuring and exposure training is conducive to correcting negative thinking, promoting cognitive restructuring, weakening the catastrophic interpretation of stimuli and helping patients maintain emotional stability when facing stimuli.[40]

In this study, the white noise group had lower PSS scores and ERQ expressive suppression dimension scores and higher ERQ cognitive reappraisal dimension scores compared with the conventional and CBT groups after 12 weeks of therapy. These findings suggest that the combination of white noise therapy and CBT may be linked to reduced psychological stress responses and promoted cognitive restructuring as an emotional regulation strategy in patients with depression. From a theoretical perspective and by integrating findings from Christensen et al.’s[41] animal study (demonstrating white noise modulates neuronal activity in the central auditory system) and Schuler et al.’s[42] basic research (suggesting white noise may influence cortical excitability), the current work hypothesised that the uniform acoustic frequency distribution of white noise (20–20,000 Hz) may generate background signals via the auditory pathway, competitively inhibiting amygdalar hyper reactivity to negative stimuli. However, the present study did not measure neurobiological indicators such as amygdala activity or electroencephalography. Thus, this mechanism remains speculative and requires empirical validation in subsequent research. On the one hand, white noise can mask sounds in the environment that may trigger anxiety and depression; on the other hand, its uniform and stable sound wave characteristics can create a safe auditory environment for patients and help reduce fear associations triggered by environmental noise, thereby affecting the regulation and processing of emotions.[43] The integration of white noise therapy with CBT facilitates the modification of negative thinking patterns, promotes cognitive restructuring and attenuates catastrophic interpretations of stimuli, thereby assisting patients in maintaining emotional stability when confronted with triggering events.

Warjri et al.[44] demonstrated that white noise could improve sleep quality in critically ill patients hospitalised in intensive care units. Hertenstein et al.[45] applied CBT for patients with psychiatric disorders and comorbid insomnia, observing significant improvements in clinical symptoms and sleep quality. Furukawa et al.[46] found that CBT facilitates cognitive restructuring in patients with chronic insomnia disorder receiving psychoeducation and cognitive–behavioural strategies. The results of the present study show that after 12 weeks of therapy, the white noise group had lower PSQI scores and higher HHI scores compared with the conventional and CBT groups. These findings suggest an association between the combined application of white noise therapy and CBT with improvements in sleep quality and hope levels among patients with depression. The speculated reason is that patients with depression often experience an increase in the number of awakenings due to their sensitivity to the sleep environment. White noise is a sound wave that uniformly covers the entire frequency band from 20 to 20,000 Hz. Its repetitive and monotonous frequency and uniform density spectrum characteristics can reduce the brain’s sensitivity to environmental noise, thereby providing a good masking effect on sudden high or low-frequency noises in the environment. It allows patients to rest undisturbed by external noise, reduces the occurrence of alert reactions and minimises the impact of external stimuli on their sleep cycle, facilitating a quick transition into deep sleep.[47] In the early stage of white noise therapy, identifying the patient’s preference for white noise, establishing a conditioned reflex of ‘white noise − calm response’ and the association of ‘white noise − emotion regulation’, can lay a solid foundation for subsequent therapies. Insomnia in patients with depression is often accompanied by ‘rumination’, causing them to repeatedly dwell on negative thoughts. The continuous use of white noise therapy can enhance present awareness, interrupt rumination and create a constant background stimulus. This approach can help divert the patient’s attention from intrusive thoughts, establish a ‘cognitive buffer zone’ and use white noise as a ‘sleep signal’ to form a conditioned reflex, helping patients establish a neural association of ‘hearing white noise means initiating sleep preparation’ and thereby reducing anxious associations before sleep.[48]

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the retrospective data collection may be subject to recording errors or selection bias, potentially leading to incomplete information and affecting the accuracy of the results. Secondly, as a retrospective study, the sample size was determined by historical data rather than pre-specified power calculations based on effect size and statistical power, which may result in insufficient statistical strength. Additionally, all the participants were recruited from a single hospital, limiting the generalisability of the findings. Thirdly, variations in the implementation details of CBT and white noise therapy (e.g., duration and operational procedures) may have reduced standardisation and compromised intergroup comparability. The extended study period (from January 2022 to December 2024) also introduces the possibility of evolving treatment standards or technological updates that could confound the results. Fourthly, retrospective design constraints may have led to the inadequate collection of confounding variables such as social support, comorbidities and life events, potentially influencing causal inferences. Fifthly, the absence of a ‘white noise-only’ control group precludes the isolation of the independent effects of white noise therapy.

Prospective studies should be conducted with pre-designed rigorous data collection protocols to ensure comprehensive and accurate indicator measurement whilst minimising bias. Expanding sample sizes through multi-centre collaborations would enhance case recruitment and improve the generalisability and reliability of results. Standardised operational manuals for measures should be developed to ensure consistency across treatment groups and mitigate external variability. Future prospective research should incorporate additional potential confounding variables (e.g., comorbid diagnoses and social support scale scores) and implement strict inclusion/exclusion criteria to enhance result accuracy. The independent and interactive effects of white noise therapy can be further elucidated through prospective factorial trial designs including white noise-only, CBT-only and combined groups, along with statistical methods such as factorial analysis, providing refined evidence for precision clinical treatment methods.

CONCLUSION

This retrospective study suggests that the combination of white noise therapy and CBT may yield positive effects on patients with mild to moderate depression, including improvements in clinical symptoms (reduced depression severity and improved cognitive control), psychological stress (reduced perceived stress and enhanced cognitive reappraisal) and sleep quality (low PSQI scores). Moreover, the combined treatment method demonstrated superior outcomes to conventional therapy alone or conventional therapy combined solely with CBT. This integrated approach may provide a valuable reference for the clinical management of mild to moderate depression, though its practical application value requires further validation through prospective studies.
