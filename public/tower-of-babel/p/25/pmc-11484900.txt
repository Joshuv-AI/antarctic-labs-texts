Enhancing heart disease classification with M2MASC and CNN-BiLSTM integration for improved accuracy
Authors: Vivek Pandey, Umesh Kumar Lilhore, Ranjan Walia, Roobaea Alroobaea, Majed Alsafyani, Abdullah M. Baqasah, Sultan Algarni
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Heart disease is a leading cause of death globally; therefore, accurate detection and classification are prominent, and several DL and ML methods have been developed over the last decade. However, the classical approaches may be prone to overfitting and under fitting issues, and the model performance may lag due to the unavailability of annotated datasets. To overcome these issues, the research proposed a model for heart disease detection and classification by integrating blockchain technology with a Modified mixed attention-enabled search optimizer-based CNN-Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (M2MASC enabled CNN-BiLSTM) model. The novel model incorporates a pre-trained VGG16 model to enhance feature extraction and improve the overall predictive accuracy. Leveraging the continuous monitoring capabilities of IoT devices, patient data is collected in real-time, providing a dynamic source to the CNN-BiLSTM model. Blockchain integration ensures stored health data’s security, transparency, and immutability, addresses privacy concerns, and promotes trust in the predictive system. The classifier parameters are tuned using the modified mixed attention and search optimization. The M2MASC-enabled CNN-BiLSTM model performs better than traditional methods of accuracy 98.25%, precision 99.57%, and recall 97.53% for TP 80 with the MIT-BIH dataset.

Literature review

Mohammad Ayoub Khan7 designed a Modified DCNN framework for heart disease prediction, the utilization of cuttlefish optimization aids to extract significant features. The MDCNN model effectively classifies heart disease with better accuracy. Nevertheless, with small amounts of data, the CNN model may be vulnerable to overfitting problems, which also increases the computational complexity of the model.

Simanta Shekhar Sarmah3 utilized a DL-modified network for monitoring and predicting heart disease, and the model provides the highest value for accuracy and sensitivity, which shows that the model can predict heart disease effectively. Utilizing the Huffman algorithm encodes the input data and enhances patient data security. However, the small change in the encoded string may affect the whole message, limiting the model’s performance.

A lightweight CNN based on IoT for heart disease detection was designed by Areej A. Malibari1. The model gathers data using IoT sensors, and the utilization of preprocessing eliminates the repetitions and normalizes the input. The equilibrium algorithm optimizes the parameters of the classifier. However, the model requires much time for execution, thus increasing computational complexity and reducing performance.

Neeraj Sharma et al12. initiated a BiLSTM framework for CHD prediction, which computes both forward and backward orientations, leading to robust feature extraction and representation. The GRU model boosted the model’s performance; however, the hybrid approach may increase the computational burden, and thus, the model is unsuitable for complex scenarios.

Marwa Fradi et al14. designed a CNN-FCN model for heart disease detection, which effectively classifies ECG signals. However, the FCN models are computationally expensive; if the CNN layers were increased, the model would provide better performances and increase system complexity.

Sumit Sharma and Mahesh Parmar15 introduced a Deep Neural Network (DNN) model for heart disease prediction. Talos optimization is a newly created optimization technique in DNN which helps to provide better heart disease prediction. However, the model suffers from data availability.

K.Vembandasamy et al16. presented a Navie Bayes algorithm for the prediction of heart disease. The navie bayes algorithm is used to classify the data set because navie bayes provides accurate results, and it helps in the prediction of heart disease. However it suffers from overfitting issues.

MD Shofiqul Islam et al17. introduced a CNN-BiLSTM to detect the arrhythmia. By adjusting the weight parameters for more reliable spatial information, the enhanced convolution network dynamic route optimization algorithm improves the effectiveness of adaptive routing optimization. However it suffers from computational complexity issues.

R. Valarmathi and T. Sheela18 discussed a heart rate monitoring system that aids in predicting heart disease at the early stage. The major contribution utilized in the research is based on the wavelet transform that detects the normal and the abnormal state of the ECG signal. The preprocessing of the signal was underperformed; hence, the detection accuracy remained weak, which affected the testing efficiency.

Sudarshan Nandy et al19. suggested the HD prediction model with the swarm ANN (SANN). The utilized model outperformed certain benchmark research with greater accuracy when working with the standard learning algorithms. The model disregarded the feature selection and fusion methods that might extract the important characteristics from the data.

Proposed model

The Internet of Things has garnered much attention lately as a possible tool for remote health monitoring. Numerous physiological data, including blood oxygen levels, body temperatures, heart rates, and ECG signals, may be gathered and analyzed by IoT-based devices. The research proposes a Secure Blockchain-based IoT to predict heart disease using an M2MASC-enabled CNN-BiLSTM model. Initially, the data from different persons will be gathered from different locations using IoT sensors, and the data will be secured through a Blockchain Network. Here, we will simulate the sensor-like data transfer through datasets like MIT-BIH and the St. Petersburg INCART 12-lead arrhythmia database. To access the data, the user should verify the security process in Blockchain through effective transaction verification. Once the data is accessed from the Blockchain network, collected data will be subjected to the preprocessing stage, reducing noise in the input signal.

After pre-processing, the segmentation process takes place using wavelet decomposition, which involves decomposing an image into multiple frequency bands using Wavelet transforms and then analyzing the resulting wavelet coefficients to identify meaningful image regions. Then, the feature extraction is performed with features such as pre-trained VGG-based features, R point detection (Pan-Tompkins Algorithm), which is used to diagnose heart rhythm irregularities, temporal interval, temporal distance, P interval, PR interval, QRS interval, ST segment length, T interval, and QT interval will be extracted along with the heart rate variability features and statistical features. The extracted feature will then be concatenated and provided to the M2MASC-enabled CNN-BiLSTM model. The classifier’s weights are appropriately optimized by applying Hybrid Search optimization with the improved Mixed Attention Mechanism. Preprocessing using Band Pass Filter

Baseline wander, T-wave interference, muscle noise, and 60 Hz interference are all lessened by the band pass filter (BPF). The ECG signals are sensitive to nature; small noise in the signal can affect the characteristics of the raw signal. To avoid this problem, this research utilizes a band pass filter for preprocessing, which eliminates the high-frequency noise present in the raw signal and enhances the classification accuracy and the model’s generalization ability22.This process outcome in a filter design with integer coefficients. Since only the computation of integers is needed, a real-time filter may be created using simple processors, and there is still enough processing power left over to finish the QRS categorization operation.

Segmentation using Wavelet transforms (WT)

The preprocessed signals are subjected to Wavelet transform segmentation; in this research, the biorthogonal wavelet Transform is used to segment the ECG signals. The biorthogonal WT provides the input signal’s scaling and transformation function, which calculates time and frequency domain features. The functions majorly work on the decomposition and reconstruction of the signals, as well as the analysis and synthesis of the algorithm.

Feature extraction

The feature extraction is used to get the relevant information from the original signal; in this research, the ECG signal features include statistical features, heart rate variability features, and VGG 16 features. The pan-Tompkins algorithm is used to detect R points in the signals.

R point detection using Pan Tompkins (PT)

To determine the heart rate of an individual from the ECG signal, the PT algorithm is used to effectively detect R-points from the complex QRS waves. This approach finds the R-peaks in QRS complexes using an integrated window’s amplitude, slope, and width23. The significance of R peak detection lies in the fact that it defines cardiac beats and is a prerequisite for the accuracy of all subsequent detections. The heart rhythm irregularities, temporal interval, temporal distance, P interval, PR interval, QRS interval, ST segment length, T interval, and QT interval were also determined using this algorithm. From the R-points, the following features are calculated for the signal described below.

Heart rate variability (HRV) features

The process of obtaining different frequency domains and statistical features from the RR interval, the time gap between successive R-wave occurrences in an ECG signal, is known as HRV feature extraction24. Time domain characteristics, including mean RR, STDRR, mean HR, STD HR, min HR, max HR, and RMSSD, are derived from the signals in this research. The STD of the RR interval or the HR segments is the square-rooted difference between the variance and the mean of the RR interval or the HR segments. The HRV parameter reflects the automatic heart modulations crucial for detecting heart disease. The RMSSD is the square root of the mean squared difference between the neighboring RR intervals.

Proposed convolutional neural network and BiLSTM classifier

The convolutional layer is prominent in CNN and possesses multiple kernels to identify hidden features and feature maps from the input data27. It is possible to construct CNN architecture by stacking three primary layers: convolution, pooling, and fully connected (FC). Every convolution layer has a set of learnable filters whose objective is to automatically extract local characteristics from the input matrix using the learned filters. It is possible to minimize the complexity of the computational load and improve model performance by using filters that execute convolution operations based on two essential notions, namely weight sharing and local connection, which may be achieved via filters.

The RNN model, utilized to analyze time-series data, incorporates a return loop, allowing it to effectively use previous knowledge. RNN, on the other hand, has constraints in terms of memory & information storage. It cannot learn long-term dependencies and may result in disappearing gradients28. The purpose of the merge function is to trim the size of the map objects and avoid overfitting. After extracting the features from the CNN layers as the ECG signal input, the features are fed into the BiLSTM layer for classification. The neurons in each layer of the conventional CNN model can only propagate their signals from the lower to the higher layer, and each time they process a sample, they do so independently.

Moreover, CNN emphasizes the spatial correlation of variables more than other factors, making developing a model with time series data more challenging. Because each layer’s neuron output in the BiLSTM model can directly affect itself over the subsequent period, it is better suited for time series analysis tasks and can be employed to characterize the output of a continuous state across time. As shown in Fig. 2, the bi-directional LSTM is a special RNN; simultaneously, both forward and backward sequences allow for capturing temporal dependencies since each character encoding includes both past and future information. BiLSTM output vectors are fed into a SoftMax layer to perform the heart disease classification task. Experimental setup

The model implementation utilized Python and TensorFlow (version 3.0) and was executed on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 GPU to enhance computational speed. The dataset was partitioned into 80% of training data and 20% of validation or testing data subsets. The model’s performance was assessed using classification metrics, including accuracy, precision, and recall. The M2MASC helps to tune the CNN-BiLSTM classifiers.

The study uses three datasets that include ECG recordings: the St. Petersburg INCART 12-lead arrhythmia database31, the MIT-BIH20dataset, and The Large Scale 12-lead Electrocardiogram32 dataset. Performance indicators like recall, accuracy, and precision are employed to gauge how well the suggested model performs. The learning rate is 0.01, the optimizer is Adam, the loss function is Mean Squared Error (MSE), and the Batch Size is 6433.

Dataset description

MIT-BIH dataset

The MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database contains 48 half-hour excerpts of two-channel ambulatory ECG recordings obtained from 47 subjects studied by the BIH Arrhythmia Laboratory between 1975 and 1979. Twenty-three recordings were chosen at random from a set of 4000 24-hour ambulatory ECG recordings collected from a mixed population of inpatients (about 60%) and outpatients (about 40%) at Boston’s Beth Israel Hospital; the remaining 25 recordings were selected from the same set to include less common but clinically significant arrhythmias that would not be well-represented in a small random sample. The recordings were digitized at 360 samples per second per channel with 11-bit resolution over a 10-mV range34.

St.Petersburg INCART 12-lead Arrhythmia Dataset

The original records were collected from patients undergoing tests for coronary artery disease (17 men and 15 women, aged 18–80; mean age: 58). None of the patients had pacemakers; most had ventricular ectopic beats. These diagnoses were confirmed by enzyme assays, coronary angiography, electrophysiological study, and pressure monitoring where necessary. For each record, the .hea file includes the patient’s age, sex, diagnoses, and a summary of features of the ECG. Each .hea file also includes a patient number (1–32) identifying the source recording; all records with a given patient number were obtained from the same Holter recording35.

A large scale 12-lead Electrocardiogram dataset

This database comprises 45,152 patient ECGs. The number of volts per A/D bit is 4.88, and the A/D converter had a 32-bit resolution. The amplitude unit was a microvolt. The upper limit was 32,767, and the lower limit was − 32,768. The institutional review board of Shaoxing People’s Hospital and Ningbo First Hospital approved this study, granted the waiver application to obtain informed consent, and allowed the data to be shared publicly after de-identification36.

Conclusion and future work

The research designed an IoT-based M2MASC-enabled CNN-BiLSTM model with blockchain technology, which emerges as a powerful tool for accurate and timely heart disease prediction and classification. Integrating IoT devices ensures a constant stream of relevant patient data, allowing the model to adapt to real-time changes in health conditions. Utilizing blockchain technology not only safeguards the privacy of patient data but also establishes a secure and transparent ecosystem for sharing and accessing health information. The hybrid architecture combines the strengths of CNN for effective feature extraction and BiLSTM for capturing long-term dependencies in sequential data, resulting in a more robust predictive framework, and the M2MASC algorithm further refines the classifier parameters.

The presented research contributes to the growing body of knowledge in predictive healthcare and opens avenues for further exploration and refinement of hybrid models in addressing complex medical challenges. The model’s performance is compared with the conventional methods, which depict significant improvements in accuracy 98.25%, precision 99.57%, and recall 97.53% for TP 80 with the MIT-BIH dataset. In the future, the research on heart disease classification can be implemented by combining the DL models for better model performance. Furthermore, integrating the model into clinical decision support systems can optimize healthcare workflows, offering helpful knowledge to medical professionals.
