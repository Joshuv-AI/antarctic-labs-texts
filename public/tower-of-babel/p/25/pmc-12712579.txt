Autonomic Symptoms in Post-Treatment Lyme Disease: Insights From the COMPASS-31 and the 10-Minute Active Stand Test
Authors: Brittany L Adler, Alison W Rebman, Tae Chung, John B Miller, Marzieh Keshtkarjahromi, Ting Yang, Chatuthanai Savigamin, Alba Azola, Peter C Rowe, John N Aucott
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine the prevalence of autonomic symptoms in post-treatment Lyme disease (PTLD) and identify clinical factors that associate with abnormal hemodynamics on the 10-minute active stand test.Patients and MethodsWe administered the Composite Autonomic Symptom Score-31 survey to 37 patients with PTLD and compared them with a cohort of patients with postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) without a known history of Lyme disease. We also report the 10-minute active stand test performed in 210 patients with PTLD recruited from July 1, 2016 through October 31, 2024.ResultsPatients with PTLD had higher total Composite Autonomic Symptom Survey 31 scores than healthy controls and reported similar vasomotor, urinary, and pupillomotor symptom burden as patients with POTS. On the 10-minute active stand test, 9 of the 210 (4.29%) patients with PTLD had orthostatic tachycardia. Although the prevalence of orthostatic tachycardia in patients was not significantly different from that of healthy controls, those with orthostatic tachycardia were more likely to be earlier in their disease course and had higher rates of steroid use (P=.009) and antibiotic exposure (P=.007) after Lyme disease.ConclusionAutonomic symptoms are common in PTLD. The 10-minute active stand test identified a subgroup of patients with orthostatic tachycardia that associated with distinct clinical features of Lyme disease.

Lyme disease, the most common vector-borne disease in the United States, is transmitted by ticks carrying Borrelia burgdorferi.1 Postreatment Lyme disease (PTLD) is a condition characterized by persistent fatigue, cognitive impairment, and/or musculoskeletal pain and occurs in 10% to 20% of patients after standard treatment for Lyme disease.2, 3, 4 Despite growing recognition, the pathogenesis of PTLD remains unclear. Other infection-associated chronic illnesses, such as long COVID, share overlapping features with PTLD,5 suggesting shared mechanisms. Although autonomic dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a key driver of long COVID symptoms6,7 and has been reported in PTLD,8,9 its prevalence and impact on PTLD remain poorly defined.

The most common autonomic syndromes in long COVID are postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) and orthostatic hypotension (OH).10 Both are characterized by impaired postural hemodynamic regulation, causing orthostatic symptoms such as light-headedness, fatigue, and cognitive dysfunction. POTS is defined by sustained orthostatic tachycardia (OT), whereas OH is defined by a drop in blood pressure (BP) when upright.11, 12, 13, 14 Although well characterized in long COVID, these autonomic syndromes have never been systematically studied in PTLD, despite marked symptom overlap.

Beyond hemodynamic dysregulation, patients with dysautonomia often have symptoms across multiple organ systems that are regulated by the autonomic nervous system (ANS). Common manifestations include gastrointestinal (GI), urinary, and secretomotor symptoms (sicca, sweating, and dysfunction), among others. By contrast, PTLD research has largely focused on fatigue, pain, and cognition, with little attention to autonomic symptoms. Given the multifaceted role of the ANS, evaluating multisystem involvement in PTLD is essential for a more comprehensive understanding of dysautonomia in this population.

In this study, we assessed autonomic symptom burden in PTLD using the Composite Autonomic Symptom Survey (COMPASS)-31 survey and compared patients with PTLD with a general POTS cohort. We also evaluated hemodynamic responses during the 10-minute active stand test in a large PTLD cohort, focusing on OT and associated clinical factors.

Patients and Methods

Study Participants

PTLD Cohorts

We used 2 distinct patient cohorts from the Johns Hopkins Lyme Disease Research Center. Inclusion for both cohorts was based on operationalizing the Infectious Diseases Society of America’s proposed case definition for PTLD,15 with the exception that patients were not required to have been ill for 6 months. For both cohorts (PTLD-1 and PTLD-2), all patients were required to have evidence in the medical record of: (1) prior CDC-defined confirmed or probable Lyme disease,16 and (2) antibiotic treatment for early or late Lyme disease of a recommended dose and duration,15 and (3) fatigue, pain, or cognitive dysfunction that began within 2 years of initial Lyme disease onset. Differences between PTLD-1 and PTLD-2 are outlined further.

The PTLD-1 cohort was identified retrospectively from new patient evaluations at the Johns Hopkins Lyme Disease Research Center in 2023. Two physicians (B.A. and M.K.) reviewed records to apply criteria and exclude any patients with confounding comorbidities. Patients were also required to have functional impact of symptoms (see Supplemental Appendix for additional details, available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org). Demographic data, Lyme disease history, and COMPASS-31 scores were abstracted from the medical records. COMPASS-31 scores were compared with those from patients attending the Johns Hopkins POTS Clinic, as described further, and published aggregate healthy control data.17

The PTLD-2 cohort was drawn from a clinical case series study, the details of which have been previously published.18 Participants with a history of fibromyalgia or myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome prior to Lyme disease, or chronic neurologic disease, were excluded from enrollment. Functional impact of symptoms was assessed via self-report of limitations in daily functioning during eligibility screening. Detailed symptom assessments, a medical history, and the 10-minute active stand test were conducted during a dedicated study visit between July 2016 and October 2024. Results from the active stand test were compared with those of a control cohort tested using a comparable protocol.

Data from PTLD-1 was used to assess the frequency of autonomic symptoms in PTLD, whereas data from PTLD-2 focused on hemodynamic responses on the active stand test and clinical associations. Notably, patients in PTLD-1 did not undergo the active stand test, and those in PTLD-2 did not complete the COMPASS-31 survey.

POTS Cohort

The Johns Hopkins POTS clinic, a tertiary referral clinic for patients with POTS, was used as a comparison cohort for patients with PTLD-1. The COMPASS-31 survey was administered to consecutive clinic patients. Patients with POTS thought to be secondary to Lyme disease were excluded from the analysis, although not all patients were systematically screened for Lyme disease.

Healthy Controls

Controls for the 10-minute active stand test were recruited from the community and staff between 2023 and 2025. Participants were included if they were 18 to 70 years old and did not have a psychiatric, neurologic, or other medical condition that could affect testing. Controls were not screened for Lyme disease.

Ethical Approval

The Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board approved all study procedures and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Variables of Interest

Active Stand Test

The 10-minute active stand test was conducted for all patients in the PTLD-2 cohort in a standardized manner. Heart rate (HR) and BP were recorded while the participant was supine for several minutes and at 3, 5, and 10 minutes while standing. The OT was defined as a sustained HR increase ≥ 30 bpm (or reaching ≥ 120 bpm) on at least 2 of the 3 standing measurements compared with supine.13 OH was identified by a drop in systolic BP of ≥20 mm Hg or a drop in diastolic BP of ≥10 mm Hg. For those with supine hypertension (defined as systolic ≥ 150 mm Hg and/or diastolic ≥ 90 mm Hg), a drop of ≥30 mm Hg for systolic BP was applied.19 To meet criteria for OH, these changes were observed on at least 1 of the 3 standing measurements.12 For this study, we combined those with classical OH (whose BP fell by 3 minutes of standing) and delayed OH (hypotension after 3 minutes upright).

COMPASS-31 questionnaire

Patients in the PTLD-1 and POTS cohorts completed the COMPASS-31 questionnaire,20 a 31-item self-reported survey of autonomic symptoms that reports 6 symptom domains: orthostatic, vasomotor, secretomotor, GI, bladder, and pupillomotor.21, 22, 23

Other Patient Surveys

All patients in the PTLD-2 completed several symptom surveys. The General Symptom Questionnaire (GSQ)-30 is a validated 30-item measure of symptom burden in PTLD24 in which symptoms are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0=not at all; 1=a little bit; 2=somewhat; 3=quite a bit; 4=very much). Unless otherwise specified, symptoms were dichotomized into present (a little bit, somewhat, quite a bit, or very much) or absent (not at all). The Short Form Healthy Survey, version 2, is a metric of health-related quality of life, which can be summarized into 8 subscale scores: physical functioning, role physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role emotional, and mental health.25 The Life Events Checklist is a 17-item measure of prior exposure to potentially traumatic events.26

Statistical Methods

Two-group comparisons were performed using χ2 or Fisher exact test for categorical variables and t tests or Wilcoxon rank sum tests for continuous variables. Among patients in the PTLD-2 group, we tested the hypothesis that participants with OT have higher autonomic symptoms through logistic regression models with individual symptoms as the outcome, group (OT vs no OT) as the primary predictor of interest, and age and sex included in the models as potential confounders. To examine potential risk factors for OT in the PTLD-2 cohort, we performed logistic regression models with OT as the outcome and various demographic and clinical risk factor variables as predictors. The assumption of linearity was assessed and verified by visually inspecting the scatter plot between each continuous predictor and the logit values. Variables with P<.10 in unadjusted models were included in final adjusted models.

All analyses were conducted using SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc), and all graphs were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.0.2 (GraphPad).

Autonomic Symptoms in PTLD-1

The mean and SDs for the COMPASS-31 score and its subdomains are reported for each cohort in Figure 1. Healthy controls came from published aggregate data (n=30; all women; SD: 10.7).17 We performed pairwise comparisons between PTLD-1 and controls and between PTLD-1 and POTS. Patient in the PTLD-1 cohort had significantly higher scores than controls across all subdomains (P<.005 for each). Patients with PTLD had similar vasomotor, bladder, and pupillomotor scores as those with POTS (P>.05 for each), whereas those with POTS had significantly higher total COMPASS-31 scores (P=.001) and more orthostatic, secretomotor, and GI symptoms than patients with PTLD (P<.05 for each). The subdomain that most significantly differentiated POTS from PTLD was the GI domain (P<.0001).

Among patients in the PTLD-1 cohort, females had significantly higher mean scores than males across most subdomains and the total score (orthostatic: 23.09 vs 16.00; P=.054; vasomotor: 2.05 vs 0.78; P=.027; secretomotor: 6.72 vs 2.71; P=.004; GI: 9.09 vs 5.54; P=.036; bladder: 2.12 vs 1.48; P=.253; pupillomotor: 2.88 vs 1.95; P=.082; total: 45.94 vs 28.46; P=.005). There was no significant relationship between duration of illness and the COMPASS-31 score (Spearman correlation ρ=0.02; P=.907) or subdomain scores (ρ<0.250; P>.130 for each).

Hemodynamic Changes in PTLD-2

Among participants with complete hemodynamic data, 4.29% (9/210) of the PTLD-2 cohort and 2.04% (1/49) of controls met criteria for OT. In age- and sex-adjusted logistic regression, these rates were not significantly different by group (OR, 2.66; 95% CI, 0.32-22.44; P=.369). Of the 9 patients with PTLD and OT, 6 (66.67%) endorsed light-headedness or discomfort on standing on the GSQ-30, whereas the remaining 3 reported fatigue and brain fog but no orthostatic symptoms. To further characterize the OT cohort, we compared patients with OT with those without OT in the PTLD-2 cohort. Participants with OH (n=22, see further) were excluded from these analyses to avoid overlap, for a final sample of 9 with OT and 179 without OT. Notably, none of the patients with PTLD were taking midodrine, fludrocortisone, droxidopa, or pyridostigmine, and there was no difference in the use of β-blockers (0.00% vs 5.03%; P>.99), stimulants (11.11% vs 4.47%; P=.363), or antidepressants (33.33% vs 15.08%; P=.157) between those with and those without OT.

We next examined OH on the active stand test. A drop in BP at any timepoint (3, 5, or 10 minutes) was observed in 10.48% (22/210) of patients with PTLD and 18.37% (9/49) of controls. Among patients with PTLD, those with OH more frequently reported dizziness than those without OH, although this was not statistically significant (54.55% vs 37.77%; P=.128). Because these hemodynamic changes were also common among controls, we did not pursue further analyses of clinical associations in PTLD and focused subsequent analyses on OT. To increase specificity, we additionally applied more stringent definitions requiring sustained BP drops (ie, present at both 3 and 5 minutes for classic OH or both 5 and 10 minutes for delayed OH). By these criteria, only 4 of the 210 (1.90%) patients with PTLD and 5 of the 49 (10.20%) controls met criteria for sustained OH, precluding additional subgroup analysis.

Association of OT and Symptom Surveys in PTLD

We next examined the relationship between OT and symptoms in the PTLD-2 group. We performed logistic regression models with OT as the predictor of interest and symptom presence on the GSQ-30 as the outcome, controlling for age and sex. Although individuals with OT had a higher mean total GSQ-30 score than those without OT (50.33 vs 37.75; β: 9.93; P=.185), this difference did not reach statistical significance after controlling for age and sex. We then conducted individual logistic regression models for each symptom on the GSQ-30. To increase specificity, symptoms were dichotomized into not at all and a little bit, vs. somewhat, quite a bit, and very much as the outcome and OT status as the primary predictor, adjusting for age and sex. Symptoms with P<0.10 in the final model are displayed in Figure 2. Of the 30 symptoms assessed, 4 were significantly associated with OT, including light-headedness or discomfort with standing, headaches, hot or cold sensations in the extremities, and bladder symptoms.

We then assessed whether hypothesized demographic or clinical factors were associated with OT, recognizing the limited sample size for this group (Table 2). In unadjusted logistic regression models, neurologic disease at presentation, systemic steroid use since Lyme onset, duration of illness, and total antibiotic use showed trends toward an association with OT (P<.10) and were evaluated further in multivariate models. We did not detect any nonlinear relationships between continuous predictors and the OT outcome in logit scale.

As expected, we identified significant collinearity between neurologic Lyme disease at presentation and systemic steroid use (Fisher exact test; P=.011). Neurologic Lyme disease was not included in the final multivariate model owing to this collinearity and its less significant association with the outcome. Therefore, in the final adjusted model, we included only systemic steroids, duration of illness, and total antibiotic use, a model that also produced the lower Akaike Information Criterion value. In adjusted models including these factors, all 3 remained independently statistically significant. Notably, the odds of developing OT were nearly 8 times as high in participants treated with steroids than those in patients who had not received steroids. Although borderline significant, each additional year of PTLD duration was associated with a 78% reduction in the odds of having OT.

Discussion

We showed that patients with PTLD experience significant autonomic symptom burden across multiple symptom domains compared with healthy controls. Their symptom profile closely mirrors that of patients with POTS, a syndrome characterized by dysregulated autonomic responses. Although prior PTLD research has focused on fatigue, musculoskeletal symptoms, and cognitive dysfunction, this is the first systematic assessment of a broader range of symptoms in PTLD. Many of these symptoms may be clinically actionable (eg, volume expansion and promotility agents), underscoring the importance of routine screening in PTLD to guide the diagnostic evaluation and management.

The 10-minute active stand test identified a subset of PTLD participants with OT (4.3%), who had lower quality of life and greater symptom burden. Not all participants with OT met strict POTS criteria because some did not report orthostatic symptoms; however, all had symptoms commonly associated with POTS such as fatigue or brain fog, suggesting that hemodynamic dysregulation may contribute to symptoms. Although the prevalence of OT did not differ significantly from controls, the small sample sizes in both groups limited statistical power. The 1 control with OT may have had unrecognized dysautonomia or underreported symptoms during screening. In contrast, the 10-minute stand test was of limited utility for detecting OH in PTLD; BP drops were common in both PTLD and controls, whereas sustained OH was rare.

Although the active stand test is practical clinically, its yield for detecting hemodynamic dysregulation in PTLD was low despite high autonomic symptom burden. Our definition of OT was stringent, requiring a sustained HR increase of ≥30 bpm or ≥120 bpm on at least 2 measurements. Protocols for the active stand test are not standardized, and more frequent measurements while supine and standing may improve sensitivity. Roma et al29 showed that the prevalence of OT increases when the lowest supine HR is recorded either within 5 minutes before standing or in the 2 minutes after returning to supine following the passive standing test, although applicability to active standing is unclear. Tilt table testing can be more sensitive,30,31 particularly when combined with cerebral blood flow assessment, and should be considered in future studies.

The association between OT in PTLD and Lyme disease features suggests a potential biologic link. Patients with OT tended to have shorter disease duration, consistent with studies showing that fewer patients with POTS meet criteria later in their disease course.32 OT was also associated with steroid and antibiotic exposure, treatments often given for neurologic Lyme, suggesting possible ANS involvement. However, the independent association between OT and a prior diagnosis of neurologic Lyme disease was less strong in our models than with steroid and antibiotic exposure. Alternatively, patients with OT may have more severe disease and therefore received more intensive therapy. Steroids could also alter the immune response to Borrelia, and antibiotics may disrupt the gut microbiome, both potentially increasing dysautonomia risk.33

This study is one of the largest to evaluate autonomic symptoms and orthostatic testing in patients with well-defined PTLD, using standardized assessments and including a healthy control group for comparison. Although we carefully reviewed medical records and excluded patients with known confounding comorbidities, unrecognized medical illnesses or effects from medications cannot be fully excluded. Conditions such as Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, which may mimic or predispose to infection-associated chronic illness, were not systematically assessed in this study and should be addressed in future work. Future studies should also include a comparison group that returned to health after Lyme disease.

Conclusion

We showed that autonomic symptoms are common in PTLD. Although the 10-minute active stand test did not distinguish PTLD from controls overall, it identified a clinically meaningful subgroup with OT. These findings support routine assessment of autonomic symptoms in PTLD and highlight the need for more sensitive measures to capture autonomic dysfunction.
