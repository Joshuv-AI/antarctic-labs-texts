Voluntary assisted dying for dementia: international regulatory approaches and practices
Authors: Casey M Haining, Lindy Willmott, Katrine Del Villar, Ben P White
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn increasing number of jurisdictions now permit people to access voluntary assisted dying (VAD) to end their lives. Access to VAD is controlled by eligibility criteria, which vary in each jurisdiction meaning that not everyone who may wish to access VAD can. In this article, we focus on one specific cohort, namely people living with dementia, who may seek to access VAD on the basis of their dementia. We consider whether it may be possible for such individuals to access VAD in jurisdictions where VAD is lawful, and under what circumstances. To do this, we undertake an in-depth comparative analysis of all the relevant legal frameworks (as well as relevant policy and guidelines) to describe and categorize various VAD models where VAD for dementia may be possible. Those models include concurrent request models (where VAD is accessed and administered when a person has decision-making capacity) and advance request models (where VAD is requested when a person has decision-making capacity but accessed at a point when they no longer have such capacity). We conclude by considering the implications these models have for the availability of VAD for dementia based on eligibility, as well as potential practice implications.Keywords: advance directives, comparative analysis, dementia, eligibility, law, voluntary assisted dying

INTRODUCTION

Voluntary assisted dying (VAD) is now lawful in many jurisdictions globally.1 The term VAD2 refers to the assistance provided to facilitate a person’s access to a medication that will end their life based on their voluntary request. VAD has been lawful in some jurisdictions, such as the Netherlands and Belgium, for decades. More recently, VAD has become lawful in other countries, such as Australia,3 Canada,4 New Zealand,5 Spain,6 and Ecuador.7 Other jurisdictions, such as France8, are actively considering VAD reform. Each jurisdiction regulates VAD differently, ranging from broad decriminalization to court decisions (often accompanied by administrative resolutions and regulations) and dedicated VAD legislation.

Depending on the jurisdiction’s law, VAD may occur through self-administration (where the person takes the medication themselves) or practitioner administration (where the medication is administered by a health practitioner). VAD can occur when a person requests and subsequently accesses VAD while they have decision-making capacity (concurrent request). Alternatively, VAD may be accessed at a point when a person no longer has decision-making capacity, based on a valid VAD request made at a time when they did (advance request). Each jurisdiction adopts its own eligibility criteria, and hence which cohorts can access VAD vary across jurisdictions. In this article, our focus is on a specific cohort of individuals, namely people living with dementia, and whether they are eligible to access VAD for their dementia diagnosis alone and under what circumstances.9

VAD for dementia is vexed, even in jurisdictions known to permit this. This issue has received considerable attention within parliamentary submissions10 and the media.11 There has also been considerable scholarly literature offering ethical analysis and empirically exploring attitudes and perspectives on VAD for dementia among a variety of interest-holders.12 Conversely, there has been limited legal analysis exploring different regulatory models that enable people with dementia to access VAD or exclude them from accessing VAD.13 Given the sustained focus on this issue globally, it is inevitable that some jurisdictions will consider law reform in the future (indeed, Belgium and Canada are currently doing so).14 Understanding how other jurisdictions’ regulatory frameworks accommodate (or not) VAD for people living with dementia provides jurisdictions contemplating reform with a range of possible regulatory options to consider. Furthermore, an in-depth analysis of how existing eligibility criteria apply specifically to dementia can identify legal gaps or potential complexities with existing frameworks and highlight areas in need of clarification or reform in those jurisdictions.

In recognition of the importance of an in-depth comparative analysis of this issue, in this article we explore whether VAD may be an option available to people living with dementia (or a subset of this population) in jurisdictions where VAD is lawful. To achieve this, we undertake a comparative analysis of each jurisdiction’s eligibility criteria. The primary focus of our analysis will be on the law; however, we will also draw on supporting guidelines and policies where available. In undertaking this analysis, we have two main objectives. Firstly, we seek to identify which jurisdictions may permit VAD for dementia and under what circumstances. Secondly, we seek to categorize the regulatory models in which VAD for dementia may be possible.

We begin our examination in Section II where we provide an overview of ‘dementia’ to give the necessary context for our analysis. In Section III, we undertake our comparative analysis and consider which jurisdictions where VAD laws are in effect may permit VAD for dementia and categorize the various types of models. In Section IV, we draw on our preceding analysis to consider the implications the various models have for eligibility.

OVERVIEW OF DEMENTIA

Before considering whether VAD may be possible for dementia, it is important to properly define what dementia is for the purposes of this paper.15 According to the World Health Organization, dementia is defined as a ‘syndrome that can be caused by a number of diseases which over time destroy nerve cells and damage the brain, typically leading to deterioration in cognitive function (i.e., the ability to process thought) that is beyond the usual consequences of biological ageing’.16 Dementia is currently the seventh leading cause of death globally, with an estimated 55 million cases worldwide.17 Case numbers are projected to increase to approximately 152.8 million by 2050.18

Diagnosing Dementia and the Nature of its Presentation

Dementia is a progressive condition commonly characterized by long periods of time from diagnosis until death.19 While a definitive diagnosis is only possible at autopsy,20 a clinical diagnosis can be made based on identifiable symptoms. There is often some delay between the onset of symptoms and formal diagnosis,21 and not all cases will be formally diagnosed.22 In many settings, clinical diagnoses are informed by criteria set out in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5)23 (or largely similar criteria). Diagnosis may be supplemented by neuroimaging and the detection of biomarkers. Unlike the previous version of the DSM,24 the DSM-5 uses the term ‘major neurocognitive disorder’ rather than dementia. The DSM-5’s diagnostic criteria require:

evidence of a significant decline in one or more cognitive domains (complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor function, or social cognition); and

evidence of a significant decline in one or more cognitive domains (complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor function, or social cognition); and

that this cognitive decline interferes with a person’s independence in everyday functioning; and

that this cognitive decline interferes with a person’s independence in everyday functioning; and

that such deficits cannot be explained by another mental disorder or occur exclusively in the context of delirium.

that such deficits cannot be explained by another mental disorder or occur exclusively in the context of delirium.

While dementia is characterized by its cognitive decline and functional impact, people living with dementia can also exhibit psychological and behavioral symptoms (eg, agitation, elation, irritability, apathy, delusions, and sleep or appetite changes)25 and physical impairments (eg, parkinsonism symptoms, gait impairment, seizures, and repetitive purposeless movements).26 Pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions can be used to manage symptoms;27 however, they are unable to halt the progressive decline caused by the condition, reverse the damage sustained, or lengthen the prognosis.28 Thus, there is currently no curative treatment for dementia.

There are various types of dementia. Alzheimer’s disease is the most common, comprising 60–70 per cent of cases.29 Other common forms include Lewy body dementia,30 frontotemporal dementia and vascular dementia.31 Mixed forms of dementia are also common.32 Each type has a distinct pathophysiology and clinical manifestations, particularly in the early phases, although this will depend on the regions of the brain affected.33 As dementia progresses, the symptoms tend to converge regardless of their etiology, making variation between the different types less pronounced than in the early stages.34 In the advanced stages of dementia, the person will often experience noticeable memory loss, particularly in their working memory (ie, memory associated with holding information temporarily) and communication difficulties (including marked language and expression difficulties).35 Behavioral and psychological changes associated with the more advanced stages include verbal and physical aggression, hallucinations, delusions, and a tendency to wander.36

Dementia and Decision-Making Capacity

Merely having a dementia diagnosis does not mean that a person will lack decision-making capacity. Indeed, decision-making capacity will be influenced by context and the specific decision being made. Decision-making capacity is a multidimensional construct,37 and while there are nuances in how capacity is assessed across settings, it will often involve a functional assessment.38 Such a test captures the person’s ability to understand information, manipulate information, weigh options, and communicate their decision in some way.39

The inevitable cognitive decline and associated manifestations (such as memory loss and impaired executive function) that result from dementia will ultimately impair a person’s decision-making capacity at some stage in their trajectory. It is not uncommon for decision-making capacity to fluctuate in people living with dementia even in the early stages, particularly in the presence of comorbidities.40 As the dementia progresses, capacity loss is highly probable, especially for complex decisions such as VAD. However, depending on the nature of their dementia, people may lose capacity earlier in their trajectory.41

Dementia and Suffering

Suffering in dementia is multidimensional.42 Various manifestations of dementia described above may cause significant suffering among people living with dementia (as well as their loved ones). People living with dementia may experience physical suffering and pain, particularly among those with greater care requirements.43 They may also endure mental suffering (mental exhaustion), social suffering (loss of connectedness and dignity), and anticipatory suffering44 (suffering caused by the anticipation of future suffering).

However, while suffering is common, it is not inevitable. Some evidence suggests that a person’s suffering dissipates over time as they adapt to their condition,45 and that people can ‘live well’ with dementia.46 Many people living with dementia will experience anosognosia and lack of insight into their condition, particularly as their dementia advances. This means they may become unaware of their functional impairments and associated cognitive deficits, and may lose their sense of self as their condition progresses. In such cases, people may no longer experience or detect their own level of suffering.47

INTERNATIONAL FRAMEWORKS48

In this section, we begin our comparative analysis and consider whether people living with dementia can access VAD because of their dementia alone (ie, not based on another qualifying condition) under the currently operating VAD models. We broadly categorize the various identified models that may permit this. We do not consider jurisdictions where laws have been enacted but are not currently operational.49

In framing our analysis, we consider concurrent and advance request models separately. We confine our analysis to eligibility criteria relating to the person’s medical condition, although we note that a person may also need to satisfy additional eligibility criteria (such as age50 and residency requirements),51 and procedural requirements. Importantly, some jurisdictions prescribe different categories of qualifying conditions, not all of which are relevant to dementia. If there are categories that are unlikely to be of relevance to dementia (or all dementia types),52 such as ‘state of agony’, we do not discuss them.

Concurrent Request Models

We begin our categorization and analysis by first examining concurrent request models for VAD, including those that may permit VAD for dementia, and those that do not. In all these models, a person must both voluntarily request and access VAD while they still have decision-making capacity.53 VAD concurrent models include those that rely on self-administration, practitioner administration, or both. As will be noted in Part III.B, some of these jurisdictions also permit advance requests.

Dementia Ineligible Concurrent Request Models

Most of our discussion in this Part will focus on jurisdictions where VAD for dementia may be possible. However, for completeness, we note there are several concurrent request models where VAD for dementia is not possible (or is highly unlikely to be). This may be because of an express exclusion of VAD for dementia in the legislation, which is the case in New South Wales, Australia.54 In other cases, people living with dementia are unlikely to be able to access VAD for their dementia due to the limited circumstances in which VAD is permitted in the jurisdiction. For example, in Peru, a Supreme Court decision has only permitted a single person to access VAD and has not established a general right to VAD,55 thereby significantly limiting access. In Italy, a person must have decision-making capacity and be dependent on life-sustaining treatment to access VAD.56 Given, in the absence of other comorbidities, a person living with dementia is unlikely to retain decision-making capacity at a point where they are dependent on life-sustaining treatment, VAD for dementia is unlikely to be possible in Italy.

More commonly, people living with dementia are excluded from accessing VAD for their dementia due to the cumulative impact of a jurisdiction’s eligibility criteria.57 Indeed, several jurisdictions require that a person has decision-making capacity at the time of making a VAD request (and accessing VAD), and be in the advanced stages of their condition (sometimes with a limited prognosis timeframe attached). This is the case in all United States (US) and Australian jurisdictions where VAD is lawful,58 New Zealand, and Ecuador.

In New Zealand,59 the US jurisdictions,60 and Ecuador (under current regulations),61 the person must be expected to die within 6 months of making the VAD request. In the Australian jurisdictions where VAD is lawful62 (except the Australian Capital Territory [ACT)]) a person living with dementia must be expected to die within 12 months of making a VAD request to be considered eligible.63 The ACT’s legislation does not have a time-to-death timeframe but does require that the person’s condition is advanced, which, inter alia, requires that the person is ‘approaching the end of their life’.64 While this requirement is not tied to a particular prognosis, ACT guidelines make it clear that ‘a person who is diagnosed with a terminal condition that is expected to deteriorate over the course of years would not be approaching the end of their life’.65

The requirement in all these jurisdictions for a person both to be approaching the end of their life (regardless of whether it is tied to a prognosis) and retain decision-making capacity to access VAD generates a ‘catch-22 situation’66 for people living with dementia. As by the time most people reach the advanced phases of their dementia (and fall within the prognosis timeframes, where applicable), they are very unlikely to have the requisite capacity to be eligible for VAD, despite not being expressly excluded.

We note, however, that in Ecuador, while VAD via a concurrent request for a person living with dementia is unlikely to be possible, a person may be able to access VAD via an advance request (considered in Part III.B). Moreover, Ecuador’s current regulations are temporary, and, therefore, when legislation is enacted, the legislative eligibility criteria may be framed less narrowly, potentially permitting access in the future. This is particularly so given that current regulations appear to be narrower than the Constitutional Court’s ruling, which extends to non-terminal conditions.67

Dementia Eligible Concurrent Request Models

The remainder of our discussion will focus on jurisdictions where VAD for dementia is more likely to be possible under certain circumstances. This is largely because VAD in these jurisdictions is not exclusively restricted to narrow circumstances (as in Peru or Italy) or requires both that a person is at the end of their life (sometimes within a specified timeframe) and have decision-making capacity, as was the case in the other dementia ineligible concurrent request models. This opens the possibility in these jurisdictions for people living with dementia (or a subset of this population) to access VAD at an earlier point in their dementia trajectory, provided they retain decision-making capacity.68

Non-Prescriptive Self-Administration Concurrent Model

In Germany and Switzerland, a person only requires decision-making capacity and to voluntarily request VAD to be eligible. These jurisdictions have the most liberal legal eligibility criteria of the concurrent request models we consider, but they only permit VAD by self-administration. Unlike the other jurisdictions we examine, right-to-die organizations have an extensive role in VAD provision in these jurisdictions.

Germany: Self administration of VAD has long been lawful in Germany and has been facilitated by right-to-die organizations since the 1980s.69 In 2020, the German Constitutional Court overturned a 2015 amendment to its Criminal Code (that made it a crime to provide professional suicide services on a recurring basis),70 recognizing that the constitutional right to self-determination includes a right to seek assistance to die (including using the services of right-to-die organizations), provided the person self-administers the VAD medication.71 In response to this decision, the professional ban on doctors assisting a person with VAD was removed from the German Model Medical Code of Conduct.72 However, not all of the State Medical Associations have removed this from their relevant Codes of Conduct. Thus, in some states, clinicians still face potential fines or professional sanctions for engaging in VAD practices.73 Outside the health profession, right-to-die organizations continue to play a major role in supporting VAD requests.74

There have been several attempts to establish a dedicated VAD legislative framework in Germany, all of which have been unsuccessful.75 As a result, currently, a person can lawfully access VAD if their decision to die is made freely, and they have decision-making capacity,76 without having a specific medical condition.77 Thus, people living with dementia with decision-making capacity, and who freely and voluntarily choose to access VAD can.

It is possible that any future legislation enacted may subject VAD in Germany to greater regulation. However, the Constitutional Court has warned that any legislation should not restrict accessibility to cases where a person has an ‘incurable or fatal disease’, as the right to a self-determined death should extend to everybody.78 Therefore, even if more regulation was introduced, it is unlikely to preclude people living with dementia with decision-making capacity from accessing VAD.

The lack of regulation and the fragmented nature of provision (between right-to-die organizations and the health profession) mean there is no comprehensive data concerning the practice. However, there is evidence that at least a small number of individuals with dementia have accessed VAD in Germany. For example, data from the right-to-die association, Verein Sterbehilfe, indicated that, in 2021, two people with dementia were among the 129 people assisted by the association to die.79 Further, data from a death certificate study in Munich found that of 37 VAD deaths between 2020 and 2022, one was due to dementia.80

Switzerland: As in Germany, there is no specific VAD legislation in Switzerland. Rather, the lawfulness of VAD stems from the Swiss Criminal Code, which provides that assisted suicide is not unlawful if done for unselfish motives.81 As in Germany, a person may access assistance to die if they voluntarily make a decision to access VAD, understand the consequences of that decision and self-administer the substance.82 Like Germany, right-to-die organizations have a major role in provision in Switzerland.83

End-of-life practice guidelines (which cover VAD) for health practitioners have been issued by the Swiss Academy of Medical Sciences (SAMS). These guidelines act as a supportive tool to guide practitioners and have a role in shaping practice, but are not legislative requirements.84 The guidelines, in addition to reinforcing the need for a person to have capacity and make a voluntary request, suggest a person’s VAD request should be enduring and the person should be suffering.85

Like Germany, the absence of the need to have a specific medical condition in Switzerland means people living with dementia can access VAD, provided they have decision-making capacity and voluntarily request VAD. While guidelines suggest that to access VAD a person should also be ‘suffering’, this is unlikely to pose a practical barrier for people living with dementia, especially without a prescribed threshold. Arguably, the mere fact that a person is voluntarily requesting VAD implies that they are experiencing a degree of suffering.

Switzerland does not keep comprehensive records that explicitly set out the proportion of all VAD cases involving dementia. However, one study reported that over a 20-year period (1999–2018), 56 cases out of a total of 8738 were due to dementia.86 This represents only a small portion (0.7 per cent) of VAD cases in that period.87 There is also evidence that people travel to Switzerland from other countries to access VAD for dementia.88

Suffering-Based Concurrent Model

The second concurrent request model we consider requires that a person has decision-making capacity, voluntarily requests VAD, and experiences a level of suffering. The legislative requirement (cf. guidelines) to experience suffering distinguishes this from the previous category.89 The Netherlands is the only jurisdiction that falls within this category.

The Netherlands: VAD in the Netherlands is regulated by dedicated legislation.90 Both self-administration and practitioner administration are available. VAD will only be lawful if practitioners comply with the legislative ‘due care criteria’ (ie, the eligibility criteria).91 To assist practitioners, supporting guidance from the Dutch VAD oversight bodies (Regional Euthanasia Review Committees [RTE])92 and the Dutch Medical Association93 is available. The guidance explicitly recognizes the complexities of dementia VAD cases and the need to exercise great caution,94 and provides specific advice in relation to this. The RTE annual reports also provide case summaries of dementia VAD cases.95

The due care criteria do not require a person to have a particular medical condition, but do require that they are experiencing hopeless and unbearable suffering96 with no prospects of improvement or reasonable alternatives available to alleviate this suffering.97 Guidance does clarify that the person’s suffering must have a medical dimension, whether somatic or psychiatric.98 However, legislation does not put further limitations on the person’s condition, as other concurrent models, which we subsequently consider, do.

The requirement for a person to experience hopeless and unbearable suffering has been broadly interpreted to capture the subjective nature of suffering, recognizing that what may be bearable for one patient may not be for another.99 This interpretation also recognizes the multidimensional nature of suffering, capturing both physical and non-physical suffering, including anticipatory forms of suffering. The Euthanasia Code explicitly notes with respect to suffering in dementia:

Besides the current decline in cognitive ability and functioning, the patient’s suffering is often partly determined by their fear of further decline and negative impact on their autonomy and dignity in particular ... The key factor is the patient’s perception of the progressive loss of personality, function and skills, and the realisation this process is unstoppable. This prospect can cause profound suffering in the present moment.100

Given this broad interpretation, most people living with dementia, who request VAD, would likely be eligible, provided they have decision-making capacity, their suffering is related to their dementia (or otherwise has a medical dimension), and there are no reasonable alternatives to address their suffering. Eligibility, therefore, will depend on a person having sufficient insight into their condition, and being able to adequately communicate their level of suffering to the assessing practitioner. As is the case in Switzerland and Germany, people who are more advanced in their dementia trajectory and have lost decision-making capacity will not be able to access VAD via a concurrent request. However, unlike in Switzerland and Germany, the Netherlands permits access to VAD via an advance request, thus providing a mechanism for those more advanced in their condition to access VAD (discussed subsequently in Part III.B).

Of all the jurisdictions we consider, the Netherlands has the most comprehensive data on the uptake of VAD for dementia. Since 2017, the RTE reports have recorded the number of VAD deaths for dementia (both concurrent and advance requests).101 Concurrent VAD deaths for dementia (which are more common than advance requests) have comprised a small proportion (2.4–4.8 per cent) of all VAD deaths in the Netherlands since 2017.102 In the RTE’s most recent report, there were 492 deaths reported of people with dementia who made a concurrent request in the Netherlands.103

Suffering from a Prescribed Medical Condition Concurrent Model

The next model we consider requires that a person with decision-making capacity makes a voluntary request and experiences a threshold of suffering due to a prescribed medical condition. This category places parameters around the nature of a medical condition a person must have to be eligible, thus departing from the previous model.104 Jurisdictions that fit within this classification include Belgium, Luxembourg, and Colombia.

Belgium and Luxembourg: Both Belgium and Luxembourg regulate VAD through specific legislation, with Luxembourg’s law closely modeled on Belgium’s. Both jurisdictions permit self- and practitioner administration.105 To be eligible for VAD, a person must have decision-making capacity and be in a ‘hopeless medical situation’ that subjects them to ‘constant’ and ‘unbearable’ physical or mental suffering, with no prospect of improvement.106 This applies to any ‘severe, incurable and irreversible disorder’.107 It is not a requirement that a person dies within a specific timeframe.

As noted in Part II, dementia as a condition is incurable and irreversible, and is likely to be considered severe enough to satisfy the threshold. Whether a person is suffering ‘unbearably’ relies on a subjective assessment. Both jurisdictions’ legislation broadly construes suffering and explicitly identifies that suffering extends to both mental and physical suffering.108 Both jurisdictions’ oversight bodies have clarified that suffering can include suffering arising from fear of the future.109

Significantly, these jurisdictions’ models require that the person’s suffering is constant—thus departing from the Dutch model. Both jurisdictions’ legislation explicitly requires that the assessing practitioner, in all cases, engages in several conversations with the person over a reasonable period of time to assess this.110 The requirement of constant suffering prevents a person from accessing VAD for dementia following the initial shock from diagnosis, if the suffering is not sustained. The need to establish constant suffering may be difficult for those with sufficiently advanced dementia who lack insight into their condition or have trouble communicating their level of suffering. Furthermore, in Belgium, a person living with dementia with decision-making capacity will likely need to satisfy additional procedural requirements111 that apply in cases where a person’s death is not expected in the foreseeable future (interpreted to mean a death that is not expected within the next few weeks or months112).

Finally, advance requests are permitted in these jurisdictions, but only in very limited circumstances (discussed further in Part III.B). Thus, people accessing VAD for dementia in these jurisdictions will most likely do so (if not exclusively) via a concurrent request. Data from Belgium’s Federal Control and Evaluation Commission show that VAD for dementia cases comprise only a small proportion of all cases, ranging between 0.6 and 1.7 per cent of all VAD deaths, between 2017 and 2025.113 Luxembourg does not explicitly report on the number of VAD deaths due to dementia, which are likely captured under other broader classifications (eg, neurodegenerative or neurovascular diseases).114

Colombia: VAD in Colombia is lawful following a series of Constitutional Court decisions.115 The practice to be followed is set out in administrative resolutions published by the Ministry of Health and Social Protection.116 There have been attempts to introduce VAD legislation, but all have failed to date.117

Both self- and practitioner- administered VAD are lawful in Colombia.118 To access VAD via a concurrent request, a person must make a voluntary, informed, unequivocal and persistent request.119 According to the Resolution a person must have a qualifying diagnosis —such as a terminal illness or advanced incurable illness— and be experiencing physical or psychological suffering secondary to this condition.120 ‘Terminal illness’ is defined narrowly as a condition that is expected to cause death within 6 months,121 and hence will unlikely be relevant to a person with dementia who still has decision-making capacity. There is more likely to be scope for people living with dementia to satisfy the ‘advanced incurable illness’ criterion. This is defined as a disease whose course is progressive and gradual, leading to death in the ‘medium term’ (which is not defined in the Resolution).122

Importantly, the framing of the eligibility condition set out in the Resolution is narrower than the right established by the Constitutional Court’s decisions. In recognition of this, since Judgment C-233/21 in 2021, it has been acknowledged that VAD should be available to those who suffer from a ‘serious and incurable illness’.123 In 2024, the Court again called on the Ministry of Health and Social Protection to harmonize the Resolution with the Court’s jurisprudence.124

Although dementia is incurable, because the terms ‘medium term’ and ‘advanced’ are not defined, in the absence of further guidance, it is not clear whether a person living with dementia would be able to satisfy the ‘advanced incurable illness’ criterion set out in the Resolution. If people living with dementia could satisfy this definition, it would exclude those with decision-making capacity whose death was expected at a time beyond the ‘medium term’. People living with dementia with decision-making capacity are more likely to satisfy the Constitutional Court’s ‘serious and incurable illness’ criterion which does not attach a prognostic timeframe or require the condition to be advanced.

In addition to having one of the qualifying diagnoses above (regardless of whether it is under the Resolution’s or the Court’s criterion), a person must demonstrate that their qualifying condition causes them suffering (in the case of the Court's criterion this must be ‘intense suffering’).125 Unlike in Belgium and Luxembourg, the need to constantly suffer is not a requirement, so provided a person still has insight into their condition and is perceived to be suffering at the time of request, they will likely be eligible.

As is the case with Belgium, Luxembourg, and the Netherlands, a person who no longer has capacity cannot access VAD by a concurrent request, but could access VAD via an advance request. As we discuss further in Part III.B, the Colombian advance request model is less restrictive than Belgium’s and Luxembourg’s.

It is not known how many concurrent VAD cases for dementia there have been in Colombia (if any) as the government does not uniformly release data. Data have been released when requested by academics and a Colombian non-governmental organization;126 however, these data do not offer a breakdown by condition. Importantly, statistics provided by the Ministry of Health and Social Protection report that people with a ‘serious and incurable illness’ have accessed VAD. This suggests, in practice, that the eligibility criteria being applied are those set out by the Court decisions rather than the Resolution’s narrower criteria.127

Suffering from a Prescribed Medical Condition and Reaching an Advanced State of Irreversible Decline in Capability Concurrent Model

The next concurrent request model of VAD we consider requires a person to have decision-making capacity and make a voluntary VAD request, be suffering from a prescribed medical condition and have reached an advanced state of irreversible decline in capability. This model differs from the previous as it not only requires a person to have a qualifying condition, but also requires a certain degree of functional decline. The Canadian jurisdictions (including Québec) fall within this category.

Canada: In Canada, VAD was decriminalized under federal criminal law and is regulated by the Canadian Criminal Code.128 However, the province of Québec has enacted its own dedicated legislation, which established a framework for integrated end-of-life care (including VAD) prior to the amendments in the federal law.129 Québec, unlike the other Canadian jurisdictions, limits VAD to practitioner administration.130 To assist practitioners, the Canadian Association of MAiD Assessors and Providers (CAMAP)131 offers a series of guidance documents, including some specific to dementia.

Under both Canadian models, to access VAD, a person must have a `serious and incurable illness'.132 The person must also be in an ‘advanced state of irreversible decline in capability’.133 Guidance clarifies that capability in this context refers to a person’s functional ability to carry out things important to them (such as walking, talking, eating, and socializing), rather than the symptoms of their condition.134 The need for a person to be in an ‘advanced state of irreversible decline in capability’ requires that the person’s reduction in function is ‘severe’.135 The criterion is ultimately assessed as a matter of degree, accounting not only for the amount of cognitive decline a person might have from their baseline, but also the absolute degree of the person’s decline and the state they find themselves in.136

Dementia, given the nature of the condition, would be considered a ‘serious and incurable illness’. Whether a person can satisfy the criterion of being in an ‘advanced state of irreversible decline in capability’ while still maintaining decision-making capacity is less straight forward. While diagnostic criteria for dementia require cognitive decline and functional impairment, many of those in the earlier stages of their dementia trajectory whose decline is not considered ‘advanced’ enough will not be able to satisfy this criterion. Guidance recognizes that it is not sufficient that a person’s cognition merely deviates from its baseline. Further, as is the case with all concurrent models, when a person’s dementia advances to a point where they lose decision-making capacity, they can no longer access VAD by a concurrent request. This, in turn, leaves a narrow opportunity for people living with dementia to be eligible for VAD while they still have decision-making capacity.

The guidelines provide an example in which a person living with dementia is likely to fall within this narrow window, namely when a person with dementia is ‘at imminent risk of loss of capacity’.137 This state, sometimes described as ‘10 minutes to midnight’,138 is considered evidence that a person is in an ‘advanced state of irreversible decline in capability’, because at this point the person will soon be so affected by their dementia that they will be prohibited from making a decision about the course of the rest of their life (as they will be unable to choose VAD), and will potentially have different interests from their current self.139 Given this narrow window, guidance recommends that practitioners periodically review the capacity of a person living with dementia until they reach this point and, at this time, check whether the person still wishes to access VAD.140

To be eligible, a person must also experience enduring unbearable suffering as a result of the decline, that cannot be relieved in a manner they deem acceptable.141 As with many of the concurrent models discussed, suffering has been broadly construed in the legislation, with explicit legislative recognition of both physical and psychological suffering.142 Guidelines recognize anticipatory suffering as a valid form of suffering.143 It is therefore possible that a person living with dementia may experience enduring intolerable suffering, but this necessitates that they retain insight into their condition and can adequately communicate their level of suffering. As discussed above, this may prove difficult for some, depending on how advanced their dementia is.

Importantly, despite the narrow window of opportunity to be deemed eligible under a concurrent request, both Canadian models permit access to VAD through a form of advance request (discussed further in Part III.B). This includes a dedicated advance request for VAD document in Québec and a narrower form of advance request, known as a waiver of final consent, available in all Canadian jurisdictions. These models, to varying extents, permit access to VAD in some circumstances, even if a person loses decision-making capacity at the time when VAD is administered.

Finally, like Belgium, Canada distinguishes cases where a person’s natural death is reasonably foreseeable (track one cases) and those cases where it is not (track two cases). ‘Reasonably foreseeable’ is interpreted more broadly than the Belgian criterion, as being ‘reasonably predictable’.144 This requires that a person has a sufficient temporal proximity to death or that the person’s trajectory towards natural death is predictable based on their clinical state, but does not require a specific prognosis.145 Track two cases require a person to satisfy additional procedural requirements to be considered eligible.146 Guidelines suggest that a person living with dementia’s life expectancy will not be ‘too remote’ once they reach an advanced state of irreversible decline in capability, and therefore they will likely be track one cases.147

VAD deaths due to dementia in Canada only represent a small fraction of total VAD deaths. Indeed, data from Health Canada indicated that, between 2020 and 2024, VAD deaths from dementia accounted for between 0.5 and 2.2 per cent of total VAD deaths.148 In Québec, between 2020 and 2023, it was documented that 0.6 per cent of VAD cases were due to a ‘major neurocognitive disorder’, which extends to dementia.149

Suffering from a Prescribed Terminal Medical Illness or Condition with a Prescribed Level of Impairment Concurrent Model

This concurrent request model category permits access to VAD for a person with decision-making capacity and who makes a voluntary request provided they either satisfy a legislatively defined terminal150 illness criterion or have a medical condition with a prescribed level of impairment. The person must also experience suffering because of their qualifying condition. Two jurisdictions fall within this category: Austria and Spain.

Austria: In Austria, a 2020 Constitutional Court decision declared a previous ban that prohibited suicide assistance unconstitutional.151 The decision repealed the relevant section in the Austrian Criminal Code152 on the basis that it infringed the right to self-determination thereby lifting the ban on suicide assistance, enabling VAD by self-administration. Following this, VAD legislation permitting self-administered VAD was introduced and came into effect in January 2022.153 To access VAD, a person must complete a legally binding ‘Sterbeverfügung’ (‘dying decree’) which will be issued to those eligible following a twelve-week waiting period.154 At present,155 this will stay in effect for 1 year.156 During this time the medication can be dispensed from the pharmacy.

The legislation limits access to those with decision-making capacity who voluntarily request VAD and who are living with either an incurable terminal illness or a severe permanent illness with persistent symptoms.157 In the case of an incurable terminal illness, it is unclear how quickly one must progress to death, but the legislative context suggests death need not be imminent.158 With respect to the second potential qualifying condition, severe permanent illness with persistent symptoms,159 this criterion incorporates a level of impairment a person must have, like the Canadian model. However, Austria’s threshold is more circumscribed and appears to have a higher threshold to satisfy, requiring not only that someone experiences decline in function, but that their resultant impairments permanently impair the entirety of the person’s lifestyle. The person must also have persistent symptoms to qualify under this condition. Regardless of the qualifying condition, the person must be experiencing suffering as a result of their condition that cannot be averted.160 No level of suffering is specified, but guidance confirms this is subjectively determined.161

Based on Austria’s eligibility criteria, people living with dementia may be able to access VAD.162 This is because dementia is a severe and permanent condition that is incurable and will cause death (but not likely imminently in cases where a person living with dementia has capacity) and can cause suffering. If there are no limitations as to how quickly one must progress to death, a person with dementia who has decision-making capacity and is suffering could access VAD under the first qualifying condition.

There may also be scope for someone to qualify under the second qualifying condition. As noted in Part II, to be diagnosed with a major neurocognitive disorder (which includes dementia) based on DSM-5 criteria, a person necessarily must have a level of cognitive decline that interferes with a person’s independence in everyday functioning. However, to satisfy the criterion of this second qualifying condition it is necessary both that a person’s decline results in ‘permanent impairments’ that may affect ‘the entirety of a person’s lifestyle’ and that the person has ‘persistent symptoms’. These criteria thus impose a high threshold, and while people living with dementia with decision-making capacity will experience functional impairment, this decline may not affect the entirety of a person’s lifestyle, particularly in the early stages of their condition, although some may reach this threshold. Thus, it is likely to be easier for persons with dementia to access VAD under the first qualifying condition.

There is some data available from Austria that details the number of deaths since the law’s inception but does not provide a further breakdown.163 The authors did make enquiries to the Austrian Palliative Care Society that advised that there has been a case where VAD was accessed solely on the basis of early-stage Alzheimer’s dementia.164

Spain: In Spain, dedicated legislation regulates VAD.165 To be eligible, a person must be capable of providing informed consent and voluntarily request VAD.166 They must also suffer from either a ‘serious and incurable illness’ or a ‘serious, chronic and disabling condition’.167

The first possible qualifying condition ‘serious and incurable illness’ refers to a condition that has a limited prognosis for life, in the context of progressive frailty.168 Although ‘limited prognosis’ is not defined in the Act, clinical guidance from one of the autonomous communities169 suggests death must be expected in the short-medium term (with no specified prognosis period).170 With respect to the need to be in the ‘context of progressive frailty’, the guidelines provide the example of a person who can no longer respond to stressors (eg, infections).171 The guidance notes that conditions satisfying this criterion refer to pathologies that cause a progressive and irreversible loss of a person’s functional capacity, and cites ‘rapidly progressive dementia’ as one example of this.172 The need to have a limited life expectancy, although not quantified, is likely to restrict the ability of a person with dementia to satisfy this criterion while retaining decision-making capacity, particularly among dementia types with long prognoses.

The second possible qualifying condition, ‘serious, chronic and disabling condition’, may provide greater scope for a person living with dementia to access VAD via a concurrent request, because it does not place the same limitations around life expectancy or frailty, but does prescribe that a person needs to reach a level of impairment. As was the case with Austria’s impairment requirement (for its second qualifying condition), the level of impairment is more prescriptive than Canada’s. Spain’s criterion requires that the person’s condition results in ‘limitations that directly affect physical autonomy and activities of daily living, in such a way that it does not allow for self-sufficiency, as well as the capacity for expression and relationships … on occasions, it may imply absolute dependence on technological support’.173 Each of these circumstances is cumulative174 and must persist over time without cure or appreciable improvement.175 Guidance suggests that a ‘severe progressive dementia of slow evolution’ is an example of a serious, chronic, and disabling condition.176

In addition to satisfying the necessary level of impairment described above, a person must also demonstrate unbearable physical or psychological suffering.177 Like in Belgium, Luxembourg and Canada, this suffering must be constant.178 As the qualifying conditions assume that a person has advanced in their condition, concerns around suffering from the shock of diagnosis and possible adaptation that occurs in the early stages are less relevant. However, the need to demonstrate constant suffering may prove difficult for those who might lack insight into their condition or have trouble communicating their level of suffering due to how advanced their dementia is likely to be at this point.

On balance, access to VAD for dementia via a concurrent request in Spain, while possible, appears restrictive. Indeed, the need for a person to either have a limited life expectancy coupled with progressive frailty or be impaired by their dementia enough that it will have an impact on their physical autonomy and daily living activities, in addition to requiring constant intolerable suffering, imposes a high threshold for eligibility. Applying these criteria, a person who is in the early stages of dementia with decision-making capacity, who does not yet have a short life expectancy, and is not considered to be in a state of progressive frailty, or who is physically independent, able to perform the activities of daily living and engage in relationships, will be ineligible for accessing VAD for dementia in Spain. However, it is possible, unlike in Austria, that those ineligible to make a concurrent request in Spain may be eligible to access VAD via an advance request once they reach the requisite levels of impairment and level of suffering (discussed further in Part III.B).

As is the case in Luxembourg, national data reported by the Spanish Ministry of Health do not specify the number of VAD cases due to dementia. Rather, such cases are likely to be captured within the overall figure provided for neurological diseases.179 However, there is evidence from some autonomous communities’ reports that dementia has induced VAD requests.180 However, it is not clear from all these reports whether VAD is being accessed via a concurrent or advance request.

Advance Request Models

The concurrent models considered in Part III.A required a person to have decision-making capacity at the time of requesting and accessing VAD. For the remainder of this Part, we consider jurisdictions that permit what we define as ‘advance requests’. For the purposes of this article, we broadly define181 advance request models as those that allow a person who no longer has decision-making capacity to access VAD, based on previously expressed wishes made at a time when they did.182 Unlike some of the concurrent models, all advance request models rely on practitioner administration. These models will be most relevant to those with advanced dementia, albeit not exclusively. As noted in Part II, some people may lose decision-making capacity at early points in their trajectory.

Permanent Unconsciousness Advance Request Model

The first advance request model is the most restrictive model and is likely to have limited (if any) utility for people living with dementia. Two jurisdictions, Belgium and Luxembourg, fall within this category.

Belgium and Luxembourg: In Belgium and Luxembourg, a person with decision-making capacity can draft an advance directive specifying a preference to access VAD at a future time.183 In Luxembourg, the person is also required to register their advance directive and the National Commission for Control and Evaluation must request a review of the person’s advance directive every 5 years post-registration.184

Neither jurisdiction requires that a person has a qualifying diagnosis when they draft their advance request. However, the advance directive will only come into effect once they satisfy the eligibility criteria. The criteria require that a person no longer has decision-making capacity; has a serious and incurable disorder; and is in a state of irreversible unconsciousness based on current medical science.185 As was established in Part III.A.2.(iii), dementia would satisfy the criteria of being a ‘serious and incurable disorder’ (which reflects the qualifying condition for a concurrent request). However, the additional requirement that a person is ‘permanently unconscious’ significantly limits the number of people living with dementia who can access VAD via an advance directive given that, in the absence of other comorbidities, loss of consciousness is not characteristic of dementia.186 In recognition of the narrow application of the law, there have been attempts to amend current laws; however, none have been successful to date.187

There is evidence in the national data collected that people in Belgium and Luxembourg have accessed VAD via an advance directive, but it is unclear if this is because of dementia.188

Anticipatory Advance Request Models

Under this model, a person does not need to have a qualifying diagnosis at the time they draft their advance request document, but they must have one when they ultimately access VAD. Unlike in Belgium and Luxembourg, access is not limited to cases where a person is permanently unconscious. Jurisdictions that fall within this category include the Netherlands, Spain, Colombia, and Ecuador.

The Netherlands: Under the Dutch model, when a person loses decision-making capacity, they can access VAD based on previously expressed wishes made at the time they had decision-making capacity. Previously expressed wishes in the form of an ‘advance euthanasia directive’ (AED) is permitted in the Netherlands as the due care criteria189 (ie, voluntary and well considered request, unbearable suffering with no prospect of improvement with no reasonable alternatives available and requisite procedural requirements) are said to apply mutatis mutandis.190 This means that such criteria ‘apply to the greatest extent possible in the given situation’,191 and that practitioners must apply the criteria ‘in a way that does justice to the exceptional nature of such cases’.192

While not legislative requirements, professional guidance offers drafting advice, and encourages practitioners to assist patients to draft their AED.193 Guidance suggests, at a minimum, the directive should clearly indicate the person’s desire to access VAD upon losing decision-making capacity, and identify what constitutes unbearable suffering to them.194 Guidance warns against the use of standard statements that are too general and are unable to provide practitioners with sufficient certainty about the specific time when the person may wish to access VAD.195

The onus is on the person drafting the AED to indicate as clearly as possible the circumstances when they wish to access VAD.196 An unclear or unambiguous AED will not necessarily be invalid, but does raise practical difficulties and not all practitioners may be comfortable fulfilling the advance request where an AED is unclear, nor does the law compel them to.197 Ultimately, whether VAD will proceed will be based on a contextual assessment.198 Indeed, guidance is clear that to apply the due care criteria mutatis mutandis, a practitioner is expected to draw on the patient’s AED, medical records, and any previous conversations they had with the patient, as well as other health professionals responsible for the patient's care and the patient’s family.199 The practitioner must be convinced that the AED was made voluntarily after thorough consideration, and that the person was well-informed.200 The practitioner must account for any conduct that may be interpreted as revocation or amendment when the person still had decision-making capacity.201 Guidance suggests that contraindications arising when the patient no longer has capacity cannot be interpreted as revocation or amendment, but may impact the assessment of suffering (discussed below).202 The Dutch Medical Association’s guidelines suggest that the AED should not be followed only when there is consistent and clear behaviors indicating the person without decision-making capacity wishes to withdraw or modify their request (cf. occasional utterances).203

A practitioner must also be satisfied that the patient is suffering unbearably and that there is no prospect of improvement or reasonable alternatives available to the person. This must be assessed based on the patient’s current state based on observing their behavior and any verbal or non-verbal utterances, in addition to consulting with relevant personnel. Merely because the patient is in a situation they described as unbearable suffering in their AED is not sufficient to satisfy this criterion, but must be accounted for in the assessment.204

The guidance provides that suffering is assessed when a person is in a conscious state.205 Therefore, patients in a coma or state of complete unconsciousness cannot be said to be suffering and hence will be ineligible. This directly contrasts with the position in Belgium and Luxembourg, which only permits ‘permanently unconscious’ individuals to access VAD via an advance request.

Practitioners must also carry out VAD with due medical care. Guidance encourages practitioners to attempt to communicate meaningfully with the patient, but does not require the patient to verify their wishes or their level of suffering when they no longer have decision-making capacity, acknowledging this may not be possible.206 Guidance also notes that in cases of advanced dementia there is a risk that the patient may get agitated, upset or aggressive, which may impact the ability of practitioners to communicate meaningfully with the patient and administer the substance. In such cases, the Euthanasia Code,207 Dutch Medical Association guidelines,208 and the Supreme Court decision in Arends209 note that it may be reasonable for the practitioner to administer sedative medication (or premedication) prior to VAD administration. This may be needed to make the patient comfortable, acknowledging that advanced dementia may cause a person to behave irrationally or unpredictably.210 The Dutch Medical Association’s guidance, however, is more conservative in its advice, suggesting this should be reserved for exceptional circumstances and that it is not always justified.211 The guidance continues that ideally the prospect of sedatives should be discussed with the patient when drafting the AED and, where appropriate, the practitioner should make multiple attempts at communication first.212

A person may access VAD for dementia via advance request in the Netherlands, but data show that this is rare in practice, occurring in only 2–8 cases per year between 2017 and 2025, which is between 1.18 and 2.79 per cent of all VAD for dementia cases in the Netherlands during that time period.213

Spain: In Spain, a person can access practitioner-administered VAD in accordance to previously expressed instructions contained in an advance directive (or similar recognized legal document).214 The legislation does not require a person to have a qualifying diagnosis at the time of drafting the document, nor prescribe a particular form.215 VAD can only occur in accordance with conditions specified in the advance directive if the person no longer has decision-making capacity and is suffering from either of the two qualifying conditions discussed in Part III.A.2.(v): ‘serious and incurable illness’ or a ‘serious, chronic and disabling condition’.216

To have a ‘serious and incurable illness’, a person must reach a state where their condition has advanced to a point where they are considered to have a limited prognosis in the context of progressive frailty.217 Alternatively, the person must reach a level of impairment whereby their condition directly affects their physical autonomy and activities of daily living, preventing self-sufficiency and the capacity for expression and relationships.218 Additionally, a person must be experiencing constant and unbearable suffering due to their condition.

Not all people living with dementia, who no longer have decision-making capacity, can satisfy the above criteria. This was well illustrated by a 2023 case from València, where the Court considered an advance directive drafted by a person in 2015, before VAD was legal in Spain, and before the person had Alzheimer’s disease.219 In this case, the person was found to be ineligible on the basis that they did not reach the necessary threshold of suffering.220

Data suggest that there have been a small number of VAD cases on the basis of advance directives but do not specify how many were due to dementia.221 We do know from some autonomous communities’ reports, however, that VAD has been accessed for dementia via an advance directive, but not all autonomous communities provide this level of granular data.222

Colombia: In Colombia, VAD may also be available through an advance directive.223 A person does not have to have a qualifying diagnosis when making the advance directive, but needs to have a qualifying condition when VAD is provided.

As noted in Part III.A.2.(iii), to be eligible to access VAD according to the Resolution, a person must have a terminal illness or have an advanced incurable illness. To satisfy these criteria, a person must be expected to die within 6 months (in the case of the terminal illness) or in the medium term (in the case of an advanced incurable illness).224 They also must be suffering because of their condition.225

It may also be possible for a person who is intensely suffering from a ‘serious and incurable disease’, as defined by the Constitutional Court, to be eligible for VAD.226 As noted in Part III.A.2.(iii), a greater proportion of people living with dementia are likely to qualify based on the Court’s qualifying condition, given that no prognosis is attached. The more permissive Court criterion, however, is likely to be less relevant in the context of advance requests. This is because, in many cases, by the time a person advances sufficiently in their condition to lose decision-making capacity, they are more likely to have a shorter prognosis and thus could satisfy one of the Resolution’s qualifying conditions. However, loss of decision-making capacity does not inevitably mean that someone will be considered to have a limited prognosis. This was demonstrated in an earlier Constitutional Court decision where a person with Alzheimer’s disease (among other comorbidities) who no longer had decision-making capacity was deemed by the Court not to have a terminal illness.227

Between 2015 and 2022, there were 20 reported cases where VAD was provided in Colombia based on an advance directive. No breakdown according to the underlying illness is provided, so it is not known if any of these cases related to dementia.228

Ecuador: As mentioned in Part III.A.1, VAD is currently regulated in Ecuador by interim regulations.229 These regulations allow for VAD through an advance directive.230

Accessing VAD when a person no longer has capacity is procedurally complex. It requires a valid notarized advance directive made by the person; a judicial decision supporting the appointment of a legal representative; and a formal application for VAD signed by this representative.231 For an advance directive to be acted upon, a person must have a definitive diagnosis of a ‘serious and incurable illness’.232 This is defined as a serious and incurable pathological condition that has been diagnosed by a medical specialist and demonstrates a progressive and irreversible character with a near or short-term fatal prognosis (defined as less than 6 months) and for which there is no curative treatment.233

Dementia is considered a serious, incurable, progressive and irreversible condition. However, whether a person has a short-term prognosis will depend on how far advanced the person is in their dementia trajectory. Therefore, while VAD for dementia may be possible via an advance request, this remains limited to those in the last 6 months of their life. Further, while not the focus of our discussion, we note, for completeness, that the onerous procedural requirements (including court authorization in every case) will likely further limit access.

As noted in Part III.A.1, the current regulations are only intended to be temporary, and hence future legislation may expand eligibility. However, unlike concurrent requests, it is not possible to say that advance requests are consistent with the Court’s original intention.234 Any decision to expand eligibility by permitting advance requests rests with Parliament.

At the time of writing, there have only been two people approved for VAD in Ecuador, neither on the basis of dementia.235

After-Diagnosis Advance Request Model

This model requires a person to have a qualifying diagnosis when the advance request document is drafted. The person’s advance request can be acted upon when the person no longer has decision-making capacity, satisfies eligibility criteria, and exhibits specific clinical manifestations. The only jurisdiction that adopts this model is Québec. In the rest of Canada, this form of advance request is currently prohibited,236 but is being actively considered.237

Québec: As noted in Part III.A.2.(iv), Québec has dedicated VAD legislation. The legislation is prescriptive with respect to advance requests, detailing eligibility requirements that must be satisfied at the time of drafting the advance request, requirements for initiating the VAD assessment process when a person loses decision-making capacity, and requirements that need to be satisfied prior to administration. Practitioners are also supported by CAMAP guidance (including specific advance request guidance).238

To complete an advance request, a person must have ‘a serious and incurable illness leading to incapacity to give consent to care’.239 This criterion will be unequivocally satisfied in the case of dementia, as it is a condition characterized by cognitive decline and eventual capacity loss. A person must also draft their advance request document with a practitioner, and detail ‘medically recognized’ and ‘observable’ clinical manifestations of their dementia that indicate the point at which they would like to access VAD.240 There is some ambiguity with the term ‘observable’ and whether the assessing practitioner needs to be physically present and observe the manifestations, or they can be convinced of the behavior by reviewing documentation, reports, and consultation.241 As is required in Luxembourg, the document must be recorded in a register.242 However, unlike in Luxembourg, reviewing one’s advance request at regular intervals is possible, but not mandatory.

To access VAD via an advance request, a person must no longer have decision-making capacity and satisfy prescribed eligibility criteria. This requires the person to be suffering from ‘a serious and incurable illness leading to incapacity to give consent to care’. This will be unequivocally satisfied in the case of dementia, as will the criterion requiring a person to be in an ‘advanced state of irreversible decline in capability’ given the person would have lost capacity at the point of accessing VAD. We note, for completeness, that it is not a requirement that the qualifying diagnosis a person had when drafting their advance request document is the same condition that leads to their eventual loss of capacity.243

Prior to administration, a person must exhibit the clinical manifestations related to their condition as documented in their advance request document on a ‘recurring’ basis244 and experience enduring and unbearable suffering that cannot be relieved in an acceptable manner.245 In assessing suffering, the practitioner must consider ‘the information at their disposal’ and ‘their clinical judgement’.246 This requires them to draw on the person’s subjective assessment of suffering (based on their advance request document description) and the practitioner’s own observations. If a person living with dementia is irreversibly unconscious, there is some doubt in the Canadian guidelines (cf. the Dutch guidelines where it is clear) as to whether they could satisfy the eligibility criteria.247 Guidelines suggest legislation is needed to clarify this.248

The Québec legislation also explicitly deals with refusals of VAD at the time of administration.249 If a person exhibits behavioral signs of not wishing to proceed with VAD (such as resistance to care), the practitioner must rule out the possibility that the person is refusing VAD before carrying it out.250 Guidelines acknowledge the difficulty of ascertaining whether a person’s refusal is specific to VAD or an overall expression of refusal of care.251 This challenge is particularly acute in the dementia context, given that signs of resistance to care are not uncommon. Guidelines suggest that presedation may be warranted to avoid potential behaviors (such as distress) prior to administration, but call for greater clarity.252 However, if a practitioner concludes that a person is refusing VAD, they must not proceed with VAD, and make sure the person’s advance request is removed from the register.253 If this occurs, the person will not be eligible at a later date.

Québec’s advance request regime is relatively new (since October 2024). While there have been reports that people have made an advance request document,254 a breakdown of how many VAD deaths have resulted from this has not been included in Québec’s most recent VAD report.

Waiver of Final Consent Model255

The final model we consider is a waiver of final consent model, which represents a narrow form of advance request model, and is currently permitted in all Canadian jurisdictions. Under this model, a person with decision-making capacity who is at risk of losing capacity and whose natural death is reasonably foreseeable can enter into a written arrangement with a VAD provider for a waiver of final consent.256 This permits a person to access VAD on or before a specified date.257 This provides a safety net to a person assessed as eligible who may lose capacity before completing the process.

Canada: Both the federal and Québec laws allow a person to enter into a written arrangement with a VAD practitioner that prescribes a date a person wishes to access VAD. The arrangement may include stipulations that need to be followed (eg, waiting for a particular family member to arrive to say final goodbyes).

In Québec, administration of VAD must occur within 90 days from the date of the waiver.258 The federal Canadian law does not stipulate a time period. Consequently, in all Canadian jurisdictions (except Québec), practitioners have been afforded some discretion, with reports of varying levels of practitioner comfort about how far in advance the date can be.259

For VAD to be provided based on a waiver, a person must have satisfied all the eligibility criteria that are ordinarily required for a concurrent request.260 In practice, it is common for a practitioner to assess the patient as eligible and enter into a written arrangement at that point.261 However, it is possible that this arrangement is entered into when a person has decision-making capacity and where their death is reasonably foreseeable, but before they meet the other criteria (eg, advanced state of irreversible decline in capability or enduring intolerable suffering). In such cases, at some point prior to VAD being administered according to the arrangement, the person needs to be documented as meeting the other eligibility criteria.262

A person must have decision-making capacity to enter a waiver of final consent arrangement. This can pose challenges for people living with dementia as cognitive decline is inevitable. As capacity is task- and context-specific, guidance reinforces that a person might have decision-making capacity to request VAD, but not have capacity to agree to a waiver of final consent, given that it is a concept more cognitively complex to grasp.263 This risk may justify the need for a person to enter a waiver of final consent arrangement prior to being assessed as satisfying the other criteria, when they might have greater cognitive abilities.

With respect to the other eligibility criteria, as we concluded in our Part III.A.2.(iv) analysis, some people living with dementia will be able to satisfy the Canadian criteria for a concurrent request. If a person living with dementia with a valid arrangement satisfies these criteria, they will be able to enter a final consent waiver arrangement and access VAD before or on the date specified in their arrangement (subject to any conditions specified), provided they no longer have decision-making capacity, and do not exhibit any signs of refusal (whether via words, sounds or gestures).264 Guidelines suggest that administration of sedatives may be permitted to reduce fear of the administration experience and avoid others misinterpreting involuntary gestures as refusal.265 However, if the practitioner determines the person is refusing VAD, administration cannot be carried out.

Importantly, contemporaneous suffering is not required at the point of administration.266 The guidelines make it clear that when a practitioner comes across someone in a ‘happily demented state’267 who does not show any objective signs of suffering at the time of administration, the waiver is valid and VAD can proceed, provided there are no signs of refusal.268 Further, the absence of the contemporaneous suffering requirement likely means that VAD could be provided to unconscious patients.

While this model may be useful for some people living with dementia, it is unlikely to be helpful to those wanting to access VAD when they are more progressed in their condition, and their desired timeframe is linked to manifestations as opposed to a date. As noted in Part III.B.(iii), a person in Québec who wishes to access VAD when they are more progressed can make a formal advance request, but this is not possible elsewhere in Canada.

Canadian data show that, in 2024, 785 individuals received VAD via a waiver of final consent.269 It is not clear how many of these cases (if any) were due to dementia.

IMPLICATIONS OF CURRENT VAD LEGAL FRAMEWORKS FOR PEOPLE LIVING WITH DEMENTIA

In this article, we analyzed the law (and other relevant regulatory documents) in every jurisdiction where VAD is lawful, to determine whether VAD for dementia may be possible. In several jurisdictions (ie, Australian jurisdictions, New Zealand, Italy, Peru, US states, and Ecuador’s concurrent VAD model), VAD for dementia is unlikely possible due to express exclusion, restrictive access to VAD generally, or requirements to contemporaneously have decision-making capacity and be at the end of life. In the remaining jurisdictions, we categorized the various frameworks (see Tables 1 and 2) based on common features of the eligibility criteria. Unsurprisingly, the variation in the framing of eligibility criteria has significant implications for the availability of VAD for dementia (Table 3) and determines at what point of a person’s dementia trajectory (if any) they may be able to access VAD and under what circumstances.

Eligibility Implications of Concurrent Request Models

Some of the concurrent models appear to permit people early in their dementia trajectory to access VAD, due to minimal eligibility criteria. Examples include the `non-prescriptive self-administration concurrent models' (Switzerland and Germany). In other jurisdictions, where additional eligibility criteria are imposed, the broad construction and liberal interpretation of such criteria mean that, in practice, people living with dementia could likely access VAD while they still have decision-making capacity. This includes both the ‘suffering-based’ (the Netherlands) and ‘suffering from a prescribed medical condition’ (Belgium, Luxembourg and Colombia) concurrent models. These concurrent models do not require a person to be at the end of their life and have broadly construed suffering (where applicable) to capture physical and non-physical suffering. People could, therefore, access VAD at an earlier stage to avoid undesired decline and associated suffering. The requirement in some of these jurisdictions to prove constant suffering (Belgium and Luxembourg) or fulfill a waiting period (Belgium) could be a safeguard against choosing VAD upon initial diagnosis and account for potential adaptation in the early stages of dementia.

All the jurisdictions described above that permit VAD early in one’s dementia trajectory provide a person with some flexibility about when in their trajectory they access VAD. This is particularly relevant in the dementia context, given that many people have a relatively long prognosis when diagnosed. This, therefore, grants a person the ability to still enjoy a good quality of life before they reach a point of undesired deterioration. However, the disadvantage of concurrent request models is that they may force people to access VAD earlier than they otherwise would, particularly in cases where their undesired state is likely to arise post loss of capacity, and there is no provision to access VAD via an advance request in the relevant jurisdiction.

Additionally, some concurrent models require a person to be more advanced in their dementia prior to accessing VAD, by either imposing a level of impairment threshold (Spain and Austria)270 in cases where a person is not qualifying on the basis of a terminal condition (as defined in these jurisdictions) or an advanced state of irreversible decline in capability threshold (Canada). To varying extents, these additional requirements restrict access. Such requirements may mean a person may be at risk of losing insight into their condition (and their suffering) and decision-making capacity prior to accessing VAD, rendering them ineligible. To avoid this outcome, a practitioner may need to closely monitor a person’s condition to determine the appropriate point to access VAD.

Eligibility Implications of Advance Request Models

Some advance request models may be of limited use to people living with dementia due to the restrictive circumstances in which they take effect. For example, in some cases, a person might need to be significantly advanced in their dementia (beyond the point they desired) to be eligible. This is the case in the `permanent unconsciousness advance request model' (Belgium and Luxembourg) and, in Ecuador, where a person must have a 6-month prognosis. In a different vein, the `waiver of final consent model' (Canadian jurisdictions) might be unsuitable for people living with dementia and result in them accessing VAD at an undesirable time, given that this model’s point of access is linked with time as opposed to undesired manifestations. This model will more likely suit someone with a more somatic-focused condition who is in the final stages of their life and risks losing capacity due to their condition or palliative treatments.

Other advance request models are less restrictive with respect to the time one can access VAD but are not without their challenges. Indeed, other advance request jurisdictions (the Netherlands, Spain, Colombia, and Québec) require a practitioner to be satisfied that a person is suffering for the advance request to be acted upon. The suffering assessment in this context is significantly more complex than for concurrent requests. In the latter, a practitioner largely relies on the person’s subjective assessment of their level of suffering, which provides more scope to accommodate non-physical suffering as articulated by the person themselves. Conversely, assessing suffering in the context of advance requests requires a practitioner to make an objective assessment of whether the person has met their pre-determined undesired state(s) based on a pre-defined conception of suffering. While physical suffering may be detectable by using physical manifestations as a proxy, non-physical suffering can be hard for practitioners to assess.271 Advance request documentation may help guide practitioners particularly with respect to what might constitute suffering for that person. However, such documents are not always determinative, and their utility is contingent on the quality of their drafting. Québec’s advance request model addresses some of the limitations observed in less legally prescriptive models, by mandating that a person details in their advance request document, in consultation with a practitioner, ‘medically recognized’ and ‘observable’ manifestations. This requirement will likely maximize the utility of the document and provide greater clarity regarding when administration can occur. The need to have a diagnosis when drafting such a document may also ensure the manifestations are clinically relevant to the person’s clinical trajectory. Further, a practitioner may have greater confidence that the criteria have been met because the manifestations must be recurring. This contrasts to observations of manifestations in a person who is in a fluctuating clinical state (eg, in delirium) which would be deemed insufficient.

The legal frameworks in most jurisdictions are silent regarding the actual administration process. Only the Canadian jurisdictions’ legislation (including Québec’s) addresses the situation of potential resistance or refusal at the point of administration in both the ‘after-diagnosis advance request’ and ‘waiver of final consent’ models. Practical challenges arise where a person with dementia shows signs of refusal and resistance, because refusal and resistance form part of the clinical state of some people living with dementia. This care resistance may not be specific to VAD and may appear when the person receives other forms of treatment and personal care. Delineating the nature of refusal and resistance is clinically challenging. If a person is refusing and resisting VAD at the point of administration, VAD cannot proceed. In the case of Québec’s advance request model, the person’s document must be removed from the register, so that the person will no longer be eligible to access VAD. Conversely, Dutch guidelines (cf. law) do address this situation and provide scope for practitioners to potentially provide VAD at a later time.

Implications of Ambiguity, Lack of Clarity, and Conflicting Guidance

Our analysis identified a significant lack of clarity and ambiguity with respect to some frameworks when it came to applying the law to the dementia context. This is undesirable for VAD practitioners who, irrespective of good intent, face significant legal, professional and reputational risk for non-compliance, as the Arends case demonstrated. It is also problematic for those living with dementia, who are unable to access their legal entitlement to VAD if they are unaware of whether the law applies to them.

Devising eligibility criteria for VAD that are congruent with the relevant overarching policy objectives and striking the right balance between specificity and flexibility is inherently difficult. Having overly prescriptive eligibility criteria (eg, limiting them to particular clinical presentations) risks being under-inclusive.272 For example, in Spain, to make a concurrent request under the ‘serious, chronic and disabling condition’ limb (which would be necessary if the person’s death is not expected in the short-medium term as required by the other limb), a person would need to demonstrate that their condition directly affects their physical autonomy and activities of daily living, preventing self-sufficiency and capacity for expression and relationships. These cumulative criteria raise a high bar for eligibility.273

In contrast, a broad framing of eligibility criteria such as ‘hopeless and unbearable suffering’ as in the Netherlands does provide clinicians with significant flexibility, with the safeguard of requiring two independent practitioners to verify that a person meets the criteria. The downside of flexibility is that broad criteria are more susceptible to different interpretations by individual practitioners, leading to inconsistent practices and potential variations in patient access.274 For example, in the Netherlands, studies have found that practitioners interpret the suffering criterion variously, and some practitioners do not feel equipped to judge existential suffering or suffering in advanced dementia cases in the absence of communication, thus leading to inconsistent practices.275

While the current framing of the law in some jurisdictions does invite some uncertainty when applying the law to dementia, this can be addressed either through law reform or by other means. Previous research on access to VAD for people with dementia has expressed a preference that the law not be too prescriptive.276 For example, one study among Dutch medical practitioners reported that having ‘grey areas’ in the law provides physicians with the opportunity to ‘remain in control and make case-dependent decisions’.277 Given that the law can operate as a blunt instrument at times, deference to other regulatory tools (such as guidelines) could be advantageous.278 Moreover, further clarity by the way of guidelines may be welcomed by practitioners, especially given that many commentators have identified the need for guidelines for practitioners assessing individuals with dementia for VAD eligibility.279 As documented above, some jurisdictions (the Netherlands and Canada) have already provided extensive guidelines (including dementia-specific guidelines) to assist practitioners. However, not all jurisdictions with VAD laws have issued guidelines.280 For example, as discussed above, in Spain, while there is evidence of an autonomous community expressly discussing cases of dementia within their guidance,281 not all autonomous communities have done this, nor has the federal guidelines282 comprehensively covered this.

Importantly, the mere presence of guidelines does not guarantee clarity and consistency of interpretation in all cases. Indeed, even in the two jurisdictions where extensive guidance exists, areas of uncertainty remain. For example, in Canada, practitioner-led guidelines explicitly called for further guidance on situations such as whether VAD can be provided to a permanently unconscious person, or whether presedation can be provided. An advantage of guidelines is that they can be amended much more easily than law when an area of uncertainty is identified. Law reform requires political support to pass legislation or a citizen bringing a case before the Court to rule on the interpretation of the law. While amending or issuing guidance does not face the same difficulties as law, it nevertheless requires active efforts from relevant government entities or oversight bodies to develop such guidance promptly, in response to the needs of practitioners.

Finally, our analysis identified instances where regulatory instruments within the same jurisdiction conflicted. For example, in Colombia and Ecuador there is discordance between how the qualifying medical condition is framed in administrative resolutions and regulations, and Court rulings, with the former being narrower in scope. Such discordance is undesirable and has significant practical implications. A person may need to seek Court authorization to access VAD, if deemed ineligible under the narrower definitions. A preferable outcome would be for all forms of regulation within a jurisdiction to be consistent.

Analysis Limitations

While we have endeavored to be comprehensive, there are limitations to our analysis. To conduct this analysis, we have relied on official English translations of legislation and policy documents where available. Where translations were not available, we translated primary material using DeepL and Google Translate translation software. Where possible, we relied on secondary literature and sought clarification from international colleagues to corroborate our interpretations.283 We acknowledge that this is not the preferred approach of comparative and foreign law scholarship, but note that it is an accepted approach for comparative scholars who do not have the necessary linguistic skills or in-country experience.284

Another limitation of our analysis is that many of our conclusions are based on an umbrella conception of ‘dementia’. This approach inevitably simplifies our analysis. Hence some observations made may not be relevant to all presentations of dementia. Finally, our analysis was confined to one aspect of eligibility, which is only one of many factors that impact access. Indeed, while a person might theoretically be eligible for VAD in any given framework does not mean they will be able to access it. Procedural (eg, requirements for Court authorization and Court appointed representatives in Ecuador), practical (eg, determining whether a person is at imminent risk of losing capacity as required in Canada) and system barriers (eg, lack of willing practitioners to provide VAD for dementia) all may influence a person’s ability to access VAD.

CONCLUSION

As the number of dementia cases grows globally, VAD for dementia will likely continue to receive wide attention. Notwithstanding the challenges associated with providing VAD for this cohort, it is critical for jurisdictions that have made a policy decision to permit access to this cohort (or, at least, have not intentionally precluded access) to understand how their VAD framework impacts possible eligibility. It is also important that any VAD framework provide, either via law or other regulatory instruments, sufficient clarity and guidance to assist practitioners and patients to determine potential eligibility and navigate potential challenges that arise when accommodating access to VAD for people living with dementia.
