Hemithorax Irradiation for High-Risk Chest Wall Sarcomas: Current Evidence, Clinical Practice and Future Directions
Authors: YuanYou Yang, Xin Sun, Jie Xu, Lu Xie, Gang Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Opinion StatementHemithorax irradiation (HT‑RT) is a cornerstone multimodal treatment for high‑risk chest wall bone and soft tissue sarcomas, particularly Ewing sarcoma in children and young adults. Current non‑randomized evidence consistently shows HT‑RT reduces systemic relapse—especially lung metastases—without compromising local control or causing unmanageable acute toxicities, and modern techniques (IMRT, VMAT, proton therapy) further improve its therapeutic ratio by lowering late toxicity. Major gaps remain: no prospective randomized controlled trials (RCTs) confirm independent efficacy, the biological mechanisms are unclear, and long‑term pulmonary function and survivorship data in children are insufficient. Future research must prioritize prospective RCTs, pediatric‑specific dose constraints, advanced technique validation, and mechanistic studies to refine patient selection and optimize outcomes for this high‑risk population. Introduction

Background of Bone and Soft Tissue Sarcomas of the Chest Wall

Bone and soft tissue sarcomas of the chest wall encompass a heterogeneous group of malignant tumors, with Ewing sarcoma (ES) and primitive neuroectodermal tumors (PNET) being the most common in pediatric and young adult populations [1]. These tumors arise from bony structures (ribs, sternum, clavicle) or soft tissues of the thoracic wall and present unique therapeutic challenges due to their proximity to critical thoracic organs (lung, heart, spinal cord) and inherent potential for pleural dissemination [1]. Without adequate local and systemic therapy, chest wall sarcomas are associated with high rates of local recurrence and distant metastasis, particularly to the lungs [2].

Multimodal therapy combining chemotherapy, surgery, and radiotherapy (RT) has significantly improved survival rates for localized disease over the past four decades. For example, 5-year event-free survival (EFS) rates have increased from less than 50% in the 1980s to 60%--78% in recent trials [3, 4]. However, high-risk subgroups—including patients with large tumor volumes, pleural involvement, or intraoperative pleural contamination—remain at elevated risk of treatment failure, necessitating targeted local therapies such as hemithorax irradiation (HT-RT) [5]. The evidence base supporting HT-RT for these high-risk subgroups is primarily derived from non-randomized retrospective subgroup analyses of prospective cooperative group trials (CESS 86, EICESS 92) and single-center retrospective cohorts. HT-RT was a secondary local treatment modality in these prospective trials, not a prespecified randomization factor, and clinical selection of higher-risk patients for HT-RT has introduced confounding by indication; this limits the ability to draw causal inferences about its therapeutic effect, and no prospective RCTs have validated these findings to date. Notably, the number of dedicated studies on HT-RT for thoracic bone and soft tissue sarcomas is inherently limited due to the rarity of this disease subgroup and the specialized nature of this therapeutic approach, which accounts for the focused reference list in this review.

Rationale and Historical Development of Hemithorax Irradiation

The rationale for HT-RT stems from the need to eradicate subclinical malignant cells in the pleural space, which are often inaccessible via surgery or focal RT [6]. Unlike focal RT targeting the primary tumor bed, HT-RT irradiates the entire ipsilateral thoracic cavity, including the pleura and lung parenchyma, to prevent metastatic spread—particularly to the lungs, the most common site of distant failure in chest wall sarcomas [2]. The proposed therapeutic benefit of HT-RT is thought to act through three potential, yet not fully elucidated, mechanisms: (1) improved local control of macroscopic and microscopic pleural disease, (2) eradication of subclinical micrometastatic foci in the ipsilateral lung parenchyma, and (3) reduction of tumor cell shedding into the systemic circulation from pleural lesions. Direct clinical evidence distinguishing the relative importance of these mechanisms is currently lacking and represents a key knowledge gap in the field.

HT-RT was first applied in the prospective Cooperative Ewing’s Sarcoma Study 86 (CESS 86) and later the European Intergroup Cooperative Ewing’s Sarcoma Study 92 (EICESS 92) [5], two landmark multi-center cooperative group trials whose primary endpoints were chemotherapy regimen optimization, not HT-RT evaluation. These trials prospectively collected data on local treatment modalities and clinical outcomes, and subsequent analyses of their databases identified the high risk of relapse in patients with pleural involvement—a problem that persisted despite advancements in chemotherapy and surgery [5]. Early clinical observations identified that patients with chest wall ES and pleural effusion, infiltration, or intraoperative contamination faced a significantly higher risk of distant metastasis, and HT-RT was developed to target subclinical pleural and ipsilateral lung disease, complementing surgery and chemotherapy to reduce systemic spread [5].

Over the past two decades, HT-RT has evolved in terms of dose optimization, technical delivery, and patient selection. Initial clinical studies established its feasibility and efficacy, and subsequent research has focused on refining treatment parameters to balance efficacy and toxicity [7, 8]. Today, HT-RT remains a cornerstone of therapy for high-risk chest wall sarcomas, with ongoing efforts to adapt it to modern treatment paradigms and technical capabilities [9].

Patient Selection and Indications

HT-RT is primarily indicated for patients with chest wall-based bone and soft tissue sarcomas exhibiting high-risk features associated with pleural involvement or dissemination. The selection criteria have been refined through prospective cooperative group trials and subsequent clinical analyses, with consistent identification of key risk factors that warrant HT-RT [3, 5, 9]. Current selection criteria are based on expert consensus and clinical study findings, with no prospective RCTs to validate optimal patient stratification; this limits the generalizability of these indications to diverse clinical practice settings.

Core Indications for HT-RT

Pleural infiltration or malignant pleural effusion: Pleural involvement, confirmed by cytology or radiologic evidence (e.g., pleural thickening, nodularity), is the strongest indication for HT-RT [3, 5]. Even non-malignant pleural effusion has been identified as an independent poor prognostic factor for progression-free survival (PFS), justifying HT-RT in this subgroup [3]. In the CESS 86 trial, 61.9% of patients who received HT-RT had pleural infiltration, compared to only 19.8% of those who did not [5], with pleural effusion present in 64.3% of HT-RT recipients versus 8.1% of non-recipients [5]. This imbalance reflects clinical selection of patients with more extensive pleural disease for HT-RT, a classic example of selection bias that must be considered when interpreting observed treatment benefits.

Patient Stratification and Contraindications

HT-RT is typically reserved for nonmetastatic disease, as patients with distant metastases at diagnosis have distinct treatment priorities, including systemic therapy intensification and metastatic site irradiation [9]. However, recent data suggest that HT-RT may still benefit selected patients with limited metastatic disease (e.g., isolated lung metastases) by reducing local recurrence and subsequent systemic spread [7]. This emerging indication is based on small institutional series with limited follow-up, and prospective validation is required before widespread clinical adoption.

Contraindications to HT-RT include severe pre-existing pulmonary dysfunction, inability to tolerate anesthesia (for young children requiring immobilization), and extensive mediastinal involvement that would expose critical organs (e.g., heart, spinal cord) to excessive radiation doses [7, 10]. Additionally, patients with small, resectable tumors without pleural involvement or other high-risk features are not candidates for HT-RT, as focal RT or surgery alone is sufficient [9]. Evolving Criteria Based on Contemporary Evidence

Contemporary guidelines, such as those from the National Ewing Sarcoma Tumor Board, emphasize HT-RT for patients with pleural effusion (regardless of cytology) due to its prognostic significance [9]. This shift reflects evidence demonstrating that even radiologically identified pleural effusion, without cytologic confirmation of malignancy, is associated with increased relapse risk [3]. Furthermore, the EURO-E.W.I.N.G. 99 trial has expanded HT-RT indications to include patients with multifocal pleural nodules, as these are associated with a high risk of systemic dissemination [5]. All contemporary guideline recommendations for HT-RT are based on expert consensus and existing clinical study data, with no prospective RCTs to validate these expanded criteria.

Dose Fractionation and Technical Considerations

Dose Recommendations: Historical and Contemporary Standards

Dose selection for HT-RT is age-dependent to balance efficacy and toxicity, as pediatric tissues are more radiosensitive than adult tissues [11]. The landmark CESS 86 and EICESS 92 trials established the foundational dose schema: 15 Gy for patients < 14 years old and 20 Gy for patients ≥ 14 years old [5], a stratification adopted to minimize late pulmonary and musculoskeletal toxicities in younger patients while ensuring adequate tumor cell kill [11]. A subsequent analysis confirmed that this dose range effectively reduces systemic relapse without excessive toxicity [6].

In contemporary practice, HT-RT doses remain consistent with these guidelines, though adjustments may be made based on patient-specific factors (e.g., tumor size, extent of pleural involvement, concurrent chemotherapy). For example:

In a 2021 study of 11 pediatric patients with chest wall ES, the median HT-RT dose was 15 Gy (range 12–18 Gy) delivered in 1.5 Gy fractions, with a median total dose to the primary site of 51 Gy (range 44.4–55.8 Gy) [10]. This dose schema was associated with 0% symptomatic radiation pneumonitis and excellent local control, confirming its short-term safety and efficacy [10]. This observation suggests that pediatric lung tissue may have higher radiation tolerance than adult lung tissue; however, this conclusion is based on a small single-center cohort with short-term follow-up and no long-term pulmonary function testing. Long-term survivorship and objective pulmonary function data are urgently needed to confirm this finding, and until such data are available, this hypothesis should be regarded as preliminary with clinical practice adhering to existing conservative dose guidelines.

Technical Delivery: From Conventional to Advanced Techniques

HT-RT delivery has evolved significantly from 2-dimensional (2D) photon fields to advanced 3-dimensional (3D) and particle therapy techniques, driven by the need to improve dose conformality and reduce exposure to normal tissues [7, 10].

Target Volume Definition

The clinical target volume (CTV) for HT-RT encompasses the entire ipsilateral pleural space, lung parenchyma, and diaphragmatic recesses, while excluding mediastinal structures (heart, great vessels, spinal cord) to minimize toxicity [10]. For patients with residual tumor, a boost volume (CTV-boost) targeting the primary tumor bed is added [5]. The planning target volume (PTV) is created by expanding the CTV by 5–10 mm to account for respiratory motion and setup error [10]. Respiratory management using 4-dimensional (4D) CT simulation is standard to account for lung and diaphragm motion during breathing, ensuring adequate target coverage while sparing normal tissue [10].

Key Technical Modalities

3D Conformal Radiotherapy (3D-CRT): The earliest modern technique for HT-RT, 3D-CRT uses multiple photon beams shaped to conform to the target volume. It remains widely used due to its simplicity and accessibility, though it may result in higher doses to adjacent normal tissues compared to newer techniques [10].

Proton Therapy: Proton therapy delivers charged particles that deposit most of their energy at a specific depth (Bragg peak), minimizing dose to normal tissues beyond the target volume. This is particularly beneficial for pediatric and young adult patients, as it reduces the theoretical risk of late toxicities (e.g., pulmonary fibrosis, cardiac disease, secondary malignancies). A 2022 study comparing proton therapy to IMRT for chest wall sarcomas found that protons reduced mean lung dose by 30–40% and heart dose by 50–60%, potentially lowering long-term toxicity risk [7]. In a single-center experience with 39 pediatric patients treated with proton therapy, the 5-year local control, PFS, and overall survival (OS) rates were 97.2%, 74.4%, and 81.6%, respectively, with HT-RT contributing to reduced metastatic risk [7]. Survival and Local Control

Numerous clinical studies have demonstrated the favorable impact of HT-RT on survival outcomes in high-risk patients with chest wall sarcomas. The landmark analysis by Schuck et al. [5] of 138 nonmetastatic chest wall ES patients from CESS 86/EICESS 92 reported a 7-year EFS rate of 63% in those who received HT-RT, compared to 46% in those who did not (p = 0.13). While the difference did not reach statistical significance, the improvement in EFS was attributed to a significant reduction in systemic relapses (22% vs. 39%, p = 0.02), particularly lung metastases (7.3% vs. 20.9%) [5]. HT-RT patients in this analysis had significantly more high-risk baseline features due to clinical selection, creating confounding by indication; the reduction in systemic relapse may reflect targeted treatment of a high-risk cohort rather than a direct independent causal effect of HT-RT. Notably, local control rates were equivalent between groups (12% vs. 10%), indicating that HT-RT enhances systemic control without compromising local disease management [5].

A 2025 national series of 82 rib ES patients further supported these findings, noting that HT-RT was associated with improved PFS and OS in patients with pleural effusion [3]. In this cohort, the 5-year PFS rate for patients with pleural effusion who received HT-RT was 62%, compared to 45% for those who did not (p = 0.049) [3]. Similarly, a single-center experience with 39 pediatric patients treated with proton therapy (15 of whom received HT-RT) reported 5-year local control, PFS, and OS rates of 97.2%, 74.4%, and 81.6%, respectively, with HT-RT contributing to a 40% reduction in distant metastasis risk [7]. This single-center experience has a small HT-RT subgroup sample size and lacks a photon-based HT-RT control group, limiting the ability to isolate the independent effect of HT-RT from the potential benefits of proton therapy.

For patients with rib primaries specifically, HT-RT has shown consistent benefits. In the CESS 86/EICESS 92 analysis, the 7-year EFS rate for rib ES patients who received HT-RT was 62%, compared to 54% for those who did not [5]. Systemic or combined systemic and local relapses occurred in 24.4% of HT-RT recipients versus 36% of non-recipients, while local relapse rates were identical (12%) [5]. This confirms that HT-RT’s primary benefit lies in reducing systemic spread rather than local recurrence, which is typically addressed by surgery and focal RT boosts [5, 6]. Strong selection bias exists in this comparison, as nearly all HT-RT patients had rib primaries versus a small minority of non-HT-RT patients, making direct unadjusted outcome comparisons challenging.

Relapse Patterns

HT-RT primarily reduces systemic relapse risk, particularly lung metastases, which are the most common distant failure site in chest wall sarcomas [3, 5]. In the CESS 86/EICESS 92 analysis, lung metastases occurred in 7.3% of HT-RT recipients versus 20.9% of non-recipients [5]. Bone metastases were also reduced (17.0% vs. 24.4%), though the difference was not statistically significant [5]. A 2024 single-center study of 24 pediatric ES/PNET patients treated with HT-RT reported a pleural control rate of 67% and a local control rate of 83%, with distant metastasis being the most common pattern of failure (50% of patients) [1]. The high rate of distant metastasis despite HT-RT suggests that HT-RT addresses pleural and ipsilateral lung disease but does not eliminate the risk of systemic spread to distant extra-thoracic sites, highlighting the need for optimized systemic chemotherapy in conjunction with HT-RT.

Notably, pleural recurrences are rare in patients who receive HT-RT. In the 2025 rib ES series, pleural recurrences occurred in only 12% of HT-RT recipients, all of which were associated with concurrent distant metastases [3]. This suggests that HT-RT effectively eradicates subclinical pleural disease, and pleural recurrence in HT-RT patients is typically a manifestation of systemic disease progression [3]. This finding supports the hypothesis that HT-RT’s primary mechanism of action is local control of pleural disease, which may reduce the seeding of systemic metastases from pleural lesions; however, direct clinical evidence for this mechanism is lacking, and alternative explanations (e.g., synergy with chemotherapy) cannot be excluded.

Local relapse rates remain low in both HT-RT and non-HT-RT groups, ranging from 10% to 12% at 7 years [5]. This is attributed to the combined effects of surgery and focal RT boosts, which address gross disease at the primary site [5, 6]. In patients with complete surgical resection (R0) and good histological response to chemotherapy, local relapse rates are even lower (1–3%), regardless of HT-RT use [6].

Radiation dermatitis: The most frequent acute toxicity, occurring in 60–80% of patients. Grade 3 dermatitis (moist desquamation) occurs in approximately 4–26% of patients, primarily in those treated with large target volumes or 2D-RT [1, 11]. Late Toxicities

Late toxicities are a key consideration, particularly in pediatric patients, due to the long-term impact on growing tissues. The most notable late effects include:

Restrictive pulmonary disease: Reported in 15–20% of long-term survivors, particularly those treated with whole pleural surface radiotherapy or high lung doses [7, 11]. Severe restrictive disease is rare but may occur in patients receiving HT-RT with a mean lung dose > 20 Gy [11]. In a 2017 study, 1 of 3 patients treated with whole pleural surface radiotherapy developed severe restrictive pulmonary disease, with a forced vital capacity of 48% predicted [11].

In a 2017 study, 1 of 3 patients treated with whole pleural surface radiotherapy developed severe restrictive pulmonary disease, with a forced vital capacity of 48% predicted [11].

Scoliosis: Associated with surgical resection and RT, with a reported incidence of 13–38% [3, 11]. The risk is higher in patients with posterior rib resections, younger age at treatment (< 10 years), and large radiation fields encompassing the spinal column [11]. However, recent data suggest that RT dose to the vertebral body is not a significant predictor of scoliosis; instead, the extent of surgical resection and number of ribs resected are the dominant factors [11]. In a 2025 study, scoliosis occurred in 38% of patients, with 3 of 25 requiring surgical correction [3].

Comparative Effectiveness and Controversies

HT-RT vs. Focal RT

Focal RT targeting the primary tumor bed is standard for low-risk patients without pleural involvement, but HT-RT is superior for high-risk subgroups. A head-to-head comparison in CESS 86/EICESS 92 showed that HT-RT reduced systemic relapse rates by 44% compared to focal RT alone, without increasing toxicity [5]. For patients with pleural effusion or intraoperative contamination, HT-RT is the preferred approach to address subclinical pleural disease [3, 9]. This comparison is limited by non-randomization; patients receiving HT-RT were a pre-selected high-risk cohort, and the observed difference in systemic relapse may not be solely attributable to the type of radiation therapy.

However, focal RT remains appropriate for patients with small, resectable tumors without high-risk features. In the CESS 86 trial, patients with small tumors (< 100 cm³) and no pleural involvement had equivalent outcomes with focal RT and surgery [6].

Timing of HT-RT (Preoperative vs. Postoperative)

The optimal timing of HT-RT relative to chemotherapy and surgery remains an area of debate. Preoperative HT-RT offers theoretical advantages, including early eradication of subclinical disease, tumor shrinkage to facilitate surgical resection, and smaller radiation fields (due to unresected tumor volume) [6]. However, preoperative HT-RT may be associated with higher systemic failure rates due to selection bias—patients with poor chemotherapy response are more likely to receive preoperative RT [6]. This selection bias confounds outcome comparisons, and no prospective RCTs exist to determine the optimal timing of HT-RT in pediatric and young adult patients.

Postoperative HT-RT is the most common approach, as it allows for pathological assessment of tumor response to chemotherapy and surgical margins [5]. In the CESS 86/EICESS 92 trials, 64.3% of HT-RT patients received postoperative RT, compared to 36.1% of non-HT-RT patients [5]. Postoperative HT-RT is typically delivered after 9–12 weeks of chemotherapy to balance systemic therapy efficacy and local disease control [6].

A recent prospective RCT in adult soft tissue sarcomas demonstrated the superiority of preoperative RT in terms of reduced field sizes and better local control [4]. However, this finding has not been replicated in pediatric sarcoma trials, and definitive conclusions regarding the optimal timing of HT-RT in pediatric patients cannot be drawn [6]. Most centers currently administer HT-RT postoperatively or concurrently with chemotherapy consolidation [3, 7]. Extrapolation of adult soft tissue sarcoma data to pediatric Ewing sarcoma is inappropriate due to fundamental differences in tumor biology, treatment regimens, and normal tissue tolerance; pediatric-specific prospective RCTs are needed to address this knowledge gap.

Dose Constraints: Adult vs. Pediatric

Existing pediatric and adult lung dose constraints (e.g., V20 < 20% for bilateral lungs) may overestimate pneumonitis risk in pediatric patients [10]. A 2021 study found that 64–100% of pediatric patients treated with HT-RT exceeded these constraints, yet none developed symptomatic pneumonitis [10]. This suggests that pediatric lung tissue has higher radiation tolerance, warranting age-specific dose guidelines [10]. This conclusion is based on a small single-center study with no long-term pulmonary function follow-up; the absence of acute pneumonitis does not rule out late restrictive lung disease. Evidence-based pediatric-specific dose constraints require large, multi-center longitudinal studies of pulmonary function, and existing constraints should remain the clinical guide with individualized adjustments until such data are available.

The Children’s Oncology Group (COG) recommends bilateral lung V20 < 20% and mean lung dose < 15 Gy for pediatric sarcoma patients [10]. However, in clinical practice, these constraints are often exceeded to ensure adequate target coverage in HT-RT [10]. A 2022 study of proton therapy for chest wall sarcomas found that protons allowed for adherence to COG constraints in 80% of patients, compared to 40% with IMRT [7]. This highlights the utility of advanced techniques in balancing target coverage and toxicity.

HT-RT vs. Other Local Therapies (Surgery Alone, Chemotherapy Alone)

Surgery alone is associated with excellent local control (90–95%) in patients with small, resectable tumors without pleural involvement [6]. However, for high-risk patients (e.g., large tumors, pleural involvement), surgery alone is insufficient, as systemic relapse rates remain high (36–47%) [3, 5]. Combined surgery and HT-RT reduces systemic relapse rates to 22–24%, making it the standard of care for high-risk patients [5]. This conclusion is based on comparisons of heterogeneous patient cohorts; prospective RCTs are needed to confirm that adding HT-RT to surgery provides a significant independent survival benefit in high-risk patients.

Chemotherapy alone is not curative for local disease, as it fails to eradicate gross or subclinical pleural disease [2]. However, neoadjuvant chemotherapy is critical for reducing tumor volume and improving resectability, and adjuvant chemotherapy complements HT-RT by targeting distant micrometastases [9]. The synergy between HT-RT and chemotherapy is poorly understood; potential mechanisms include chemotherapy-induced radiosensitization and HT-RT-induced tumor burden reduction. Further preclinical and clinical research is needed to elucidate this synergy and optimize treatment sequencing.

Future Directions

Refining Patient Selection

Future studies should focus on identifying biomarkers to better stratify patients who will benefit from HT-RT. For example, molecular profiling of tumor samples (e.g., EWSR1 fusion status, gene expression signatures) may identify subgroups at highest risk of pleural dissemination [3]. Additionally, prospective trials are needed to validate the role of HT-RT in patients with limited metastatic disease (e.g., isolated lung metastases) [9]. Prospective RCTs with HT-RT as a prespecified endpoint are the highest priority, as they are the only way to overcome selection bias and confounding by indication that limit existing study interpretations. These trials should include propensity score matching or randomization to balance baseline characteristics between groups.

Technical Advancements

Proton Therapy and Adaptive RT (ART): Proton therapy will continue to gain prominence for pediatric and young adult patients due to its superior dosimetric profile [7]. ART adjusts treatment plans based on anatomical changes (e.g., tumor shrinkage, weight loss) during therapy, ensuring target coverage while minimizing normal tissue exposure [7]. Future research should focus on prospective multi-center studies to validate the long-term clinical benefit of proton therapy and ART in reducing late toxicities and improving survival.

Toxicity Surveillance and Dose Optimization

Long-term prospective follow-up studies are needed to characterize late effects of HT-RT, particularly in pediatric patients. The Pediatric Normal Tissue Effects in the Clinic (PENTEC) initiative aims to develop pediatric-specific dose-volume-effect models for pulmonary and musculoskeletal toxicities [7]. This will help refine dose constraints and reduce the risk of late toxicities. A key priority is evaluating long-term objective pulmonary function in pediatric HT-RT patients to confirm or refute the pediatric lung radiation tolerance hypothesis and develop evidence-based age-specific dose constraints.

Additionally, prospective dose-finding trials are needed to evaluate the optimal HT-RT dose for different age groups and risk strata. For example, a dose de-escalation trial could assess whether 12 Gy is sufficient for young children (< 6 years) with minimal pleural involvement, while a dose escalation trial could evaluate 20–22 Gy for adults with extensive pleural contamination [10]. These trials should include rigorous prospective toxicity surveillance, including longitudinal pulmonary function testing and musculoskeletal imaging, to balance efficacy and toxicity.

Role of HT-RT in Metastatic Disease

The role of HT-RT in metastatic disease remains unclear, with current evidence limited to small institutional series. Preliminary data suggest that HT-RT may benefit patients with limited metastatic disease (e.g., isolated lung metastases) by reducing local recurrence and improving survival [7]. However, prospective trials are needed to validate this approach and define the optimal patient subgroup [9]. Future research should also elucidate the biological mechanisms by which HT-RT may reduce systemic relapse in metastatic disease, with preclinical models paired with clinical correlative analyses.

Elucidation of HT-RT Therapeutic Mechanisms

A critical unmet need is the elucidation of the biological and clinical mechanisms underlying HT-RT’s therapeutic benefit, which is currently inferred only from clinical study data with no direct validation. Future research should combine preclinical models (e.g., patient-derived xenografts of ES with pleural dissemination) and prospective clinical correlative studies to determine the relative importance of local pleural control, ipsilateral lung micrometastasis eradication, and reduced systemic tumor seeding. This mechanistic understanding will be essential to refine HT-RT techniques, optimize patient selection, and identify biomarkers of treatment response.

Conclusions

Hemithorax irradiation (HT-RT) is a cornerstone of multimodal therapy for high-risk chest wall bone and soft tissue sarcomas, especially Ewing sarcoma, with clinical study data demonstrating its ability to reduce systemic relapse (mainly lung metastases) without compromising local control or increasing acute toxicities. It is indicated for patients with high-risk features including pleural effusion, infiltration, intraoperative contamination, and large tumors, and modern techniques (proton therapy, IMRT) minimize late toxicities and optimize the therapeutic ratio based on dosimetric data and preliminary clinical evidence.

The primary limitations of the current HT-RT evidence base are: (1) most data are from non-randomized retrospective subgroup analyses of prospective trials or single-center retrospective cohorts, with HT-RT not a prespecified endpoint and clinical selection bias leading to confounding by indication; (2) no prospective RCTs have validated the efficacy, optimal patient stratification, or dose constraints for HT-RT; (3) the biological and clinical mechanisms underlying its therapeutic benefit remain unelucidated; (4) pediatric lung radiation tolerance observations are based on small cohorts with no long-term pulmonary function or survivorship data.

Outstanding questions include the optimal timing of HT-RT, evidence-based pediatric-specific dose constraints, its role in metastatic disease, and the biological basis for its therapeutic benefit. Future research should focus on biomarker-based patient stratification in prospective trials, dose fractionation optimization via dose-finding studies, validating innovative radiotherapy technologies with long-term prospective follow-up, and elucidating HT-RT’s anti-tumor mechanisms via preclinical and clinical correlative research. Sustained multidisciplinary collaboration will further advance HT-RT application, and prospective RCTs with HT-RT as a prespecified endpoint are the highest priority to confirm its independent efficacy and safety, address critical knowledge gaps, and ultimately offer long-term curative potential for patients with historically poor prognoses of high-risk chest wall sarcomas.
