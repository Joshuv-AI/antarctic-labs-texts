The Impact of SARS-CoV-2 Infection on Symptom Control and Lung Function in Children with Asthma
Authors: Kristina Gaietto, Nicholas Bergum, Natalia Acevedo-Torres, Oliver Snyder, Leigh Anne DiCicco, Gabriella Butler, Sherry Rauenswinter, Jennifer Iagnemma, David Wolfson, Traci M. Kazmerski, Erick Forno
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
RationaleLittle is known about the long-term impact of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection on children with asthma.ObjectivesTo determine whether SARS-CoV-2 infection affects symptom control and lung function in children with asthma.MethodsUsing data from clinical registries and the electronic health record, we conducted a prospective case-control study of children with asthma aged 6–21 years who had (cases) or did not have (control subjects) SARS-CoV-2 infection, comparing baseline and follow-up asthma symptom control and spirometry within an ∼18-month time frame and, for cases, within 18 months of acute coronavirus disease (COVID-19).ResultsA total of 171 cases had baseline and follow-up asthma symptom data, and 114 cases had baseline and follow-up spirometry measurements. There were no significant differences in asthma symptom control (P = 0.50), forced expiratory volume in 1 second (P = 0.47), forced vital capacity (P = 0.43), forced expiratory volume in 1 second/forced vital capacity (P = 0.43), or forced expiratory flow, midexpiratory phase (P = 0.62), after SARS-CoV-2 infection. Compared with control subjects (113 with symptom data and 237 with spirometry data), there were no significant differences in follow-up asthma symptom control or lung function. A similar proportion of cases and control subjects had poorer asthma symptom control (17.5% vs. 9.7%; P = 0.07) or worse lung function (29.0% vs. 32.5%; P = 0.50) at follow-up. Patients whose asthma control worsened after COVID-19 had a shorter time to follow-up (3.5 [1.5–7.5] vs. 6.1 [3.1–9.8] mo; P = 0.007) and were more likely to have presented with an asthma exacerbation during COVID-19 (46% vs. 26%; P = 0.04) than those without worse control.ConclusionsWe found no significant differences in asthma symptom control or lung function in youth with asthma up to 18 months after acute COVID-19, suggesting that COVID-19 does not affect long-term asthma severity or control in the pediatric population.

Early in the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic, there were significant concerns that severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection may severely affect children with asthma, the most common chronic disease of childhood (1). Asthma and other preexisting respiratory conditions were considered a significant risk factor for COVID-19 incidence and severity (2). However, this was based on data mostly from adults, with virtually no studies on asthma and COVID-19 in children at that time (3, 4).

Since then, a growing body of evidence has suggested that children with adequately controlled asthma may not be at higher risk of infection with or more severe illness from SARS-CoV-2. Studies have reported a similar prevalence of asthma among children with COVID-19 and in the general pediatric population (5–7), and three large cohort studies found that children with asthma had either a similar or slightly lower risk of a positive test result for SARS-CoV-2 compared with peers without asthma (8–10). Several reports have also failed to find higher COVID-19 severity among children with asthma compared with children without asthma (5, 6, 11, 12). There are conflicting reports regarding the risk of hospitalization for COVID-19 among children with asthma, with some finding a higher risk of hospital admission, particularly among children with poorly controlled asthma (6, 13–16), whereas others report no difference (11) or even a lower risk (5).

To date, little is known about the long-term outcomes of COVID-19 in children with asthma. Both children and adults can be affected by post–COVID-19 syndrome, also known as “long COVID,” which causes symptoms for weeks to months after an acute SARS-CoV-2 infection (17, 18). Persistent symptoms may be more common after SARS-CoV-2 than with other viral infections in children (19), raising the question of the long-term impact of COVID-19 in the pediatric population.

Recent studies have reported restrictive lung deficits and reduced diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide after COVID-19 in adults (20, 21), but it is unknown whether COVID-19 has similar long-term impacts in children. To assess the longitudinal impact of COVID-19 in children with asthma, we conducted a prospective case-control study comparing change in asthma symptoms and lung function within an 18-month period among children who had (cases) or did not have (control subjects) SARS-CoV-2 infection. We hypothesized that SARS-CoV-2 infection may lead to worse lung function and/or symptom control in children with asthma.

Methods

Study Population and Data Collection

We recruited cases from the Western Pennsylvania COVID-19 Registry (WPACR), which has been described in detail in prior work (6, 22). Briefly, we established the WPACR in March 2020 to track baseline characteristics, acute presentation, and initial outcomes of pediatric patients presenting with a SARS-CoV-2 infection to University of Pittsburgh Medical Center Children’s Hospital of Pittsburgh (CHP), the largest pediatric referral center in the western part of Pennsylvania, and University of Pittsburgh Medical Center Children’s Community Pediatrics, CHP’s affiliated primary care network. We included subjects if they were aged 0–21 years and had a positive SARS-CoV-2 polymerase chain reaction or antigen test result. A multidisciplinary team composed of different pediatric specialties involved in the care of patients with COVID-19 at our institution, including primary care, pulmonary medicine, hospital medicine, adolescent medicine, infectious disease, and critical care medicine, manually extracted relevant clinical data from the electronic health record (EHR). The WPACR is approved by the institutional review board at the University of Pittsburgh (protocol STUDY20110072).

We abstracted patient demographics (age, sex, race, body mass index percentile) and details of acute COVID-19 infection (date of positive test result, symptoms, healthcare use data, laboratory results, and COVID-19 outcomes, including asthma exacerbations). For subjects with asthma, we also directly abstracted available EHR data on baseline asthma severity, asthma controller medications, symptom control (Childhood Asthma Control Test [C-ACT] [23] score [range, 0–27] for youth 4–11 yr old or Asthma Control Test [ACT] [24] score [range, 5–25] for youth ⩾12 yr old), and whether they were seen in follow-up by an asthma specialist (pulmonary medicine or allergy and immunology). We defined asthma severity on the basis of National Asthma Education and Prevention Program guidelines (25).

For this study, we included youth in the WPACR who 1) were aged 6–21 years old, 2) had preexisting asthma, 3) had a positive test result for SARS-CoV-2 infection from March 2020 through August 2022, and 4) had asthma symptom scores (ACT/C-ACT) and/or spirometry before COVID-19 (“baseline” data). We excluded subjects if they had significant pulmonary comorbidities (e.g., cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis). We collected ACT/C-ACT scores and/or spirometry (“follow-up” data) that were completed within 18 months of SARS-CoV-2 infection from the EHR. If no follow-up data were available in the EHR and SARS-CoV-2 infection had occurred within the last 18 months, we contacted subjects (if aged ⩾18 yr) or their parents (if aged <18 yr) via phone call or e-mail and, after obtaining informed consent, invited them to CHP for follow-up testing. We obtained spirometry following ATS/ERS guidelines (26) and expressed results as the percent predicted (%pred) value based on Global Lung Initiative equations (27).

We recruited control subjects from the CHP Asthma Registry, which includes children seen for asthma in the CHP pulmonary clinic, allergy clinic, or emergency department, who are hospitalized for asthma, or who participate in asthma research studies at our center. The visits in the Asthma Registry occurred between October 2016 and November 2022. For this study, we included youth in the CHP Asthma Registry who 1) were aged 6–21 years at time of enrollment, 2) had asthma symptom scores (ACT/C-ACT) and/or spirometry at two separate encounters within 18 months (“baseline” and “follow-up”), and 3) did not have documented COVID-19 infection by the time of the follow-up study visit. Forced expiratory flow, midexpiratory phase (FEF25–75), data were not available in the CHP Asthma Registry, so they were not included in our analyses. The CHP Asthma Registry is approved by the institutional review board at the University of Pittsburgh (STUDY19020359).

Statistical Analyses

Our primary outcomes of interest were 1) change (Δ) in symptoms between baseline and follow-up, measured by ACT or C-ACT scores; and 2) change in spirometry values, including forced expiratory volume in 1 second percent predicted (FEV1%pred), forced vital capacity (FVC%pred), FEV1/FVC ratio, and FEF25–75%pred. Because the ACT and C-ACT have different ranges, we adopted a linear transformation for ACT score before combining it with C-ACT score, as follows: ACT' = c × (ACT − a)/(b − a), where a denotes the ACT minimum score, b denotes the ACT maximum score, and c denotes the C-ACT maximum score (28). Once ACT was transformed, it was combined with C-ACT data, collectively referred to as “ACTS.”

Among cases (i.e., those who had COVID-19), we conducted the following subgroup analyses: children with persistent asthma, children with moderate or severe asthma, children who experienced an asthma exacerbation during their acute COVID-19 illness course, and children seen in follow-up by an asthma specialist (pulmonology or allergy). We separately identified the groups of cases and control subjects whose asthma control or lung function “worsened” at follow-up, defined as a drop in ACTS of 3 or more points (29) or a drop in FEV1%pred of more than 5% from baseline, and compared data for those with and those without worse asthma control.

We conducted bivariate analyses using two-tailed t tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for pairs of categorical and nonpaired continuous variables, McNemar test or paired t tests for pairs of categorical and paired continuous variables, and chi-square test or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. We then evaluated whether the presence of an asthma exacerbation during acute COVID-19 was a risk factor for subsequent worsened asthma symptoms or lung function via logistic regression adjusted for baseline asthma severity (intermittent vs. persistent), age, sex, race (White vs. non-White), and time interval between COVID-19 and follow-up testing. We performed all analyses using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute) or RStudio 2022.07.1 Build 554.

Results

Figure 1 illustrates the inclusion of cases and control subjects in the study. As of August 30, 2022, we had identified 326 children in the WPACR aged 6–21 years with asthma but without other significant pulmonary comorbidities who had ACT/C-ACT (n = 279) and/or spirometry with adequate technique (n = 173) before they contracted COVID-19. Of those, 174 completed ACT/C-ACT and 118 completed spirometry within the 18 months after their SARS-CoV-2 infection. Four subjects were excluded for insufficient details (e.g., COVID-19 mentioned in the EHR without a date of occurrence). Thus, we included 214 cases in the analyses; of those, 171 had baseline and follow-up ACT/C-ACT, and 114 had baseline and follow-up spirometry (71 had both).

As of December 6, 2022, there were 796 children aged 6–21 years in the CHP Asthma Registry. Of these, 129 had two visits within 18 months with ACT or C-ACT scores, and 247 had two visits within 18 months with spirometry, and were thus potentially eligible for inclusion in our study. We excluded 16 individuals with asthma symptom scores and 10 with spirometry who reported SARS-CoV-2 infection before the follow-up visit. In total, we included 287 control subjects in the final cohort; of those, 113 had baseline and follow-up ACT/C-ACT, and 237 had baseline and follow-up spirometry (63 had both).

Baseline demographic characteristics of the case-control cohort are shown in Table 1. For all groups, the median time between baseline and follow-up visit was between 10.0 and 12.5 months. For cases, COVID-19 infection occurred a median of ∼5 months after the baseline visit (5.7 mo for cases with asthma control data and 4.6 mo for cases with spirometry), and follow-up occurred ∼5 months later (median, 5.8 mo for cases with asthma control data and 4.4 mo for cases with spirometry). Figure E1 in the data supplement shows the distribution of cases over time during the study period. There were no differences in sex distribution between cases and control subjects, but cases were slightly older, and those with symptom data were more likely than control subjects to be White (Table 1).

Commonly reported symptoms included cough (48.1%), nasal congestion (44.4%), fever (24.8%), and sore throat (22.0%). Of the 178 cases for whom there was enough information in the EHR, 54 (30.3%) had an exacerbation. Only 10 (4.7%) participants in our study were hospitalized for COVID-19, and no subjects died.

Asthma severity and controller medications for cases before and after SARS-CoV-2 infection are shown in Table E2. There were no significant differences in asthma severity after COVID-19, and most cases (143 cases with asthma symptom data and 86 cases with spirometry) remained in the same controller medication category that they were in at baseline. When compared with control subjects (i.e., patients with asthma seen in our center who did not have COVID-19), baseline asthma control was better in cases (ACTS, 24.0 [20.3–26.0] vs. 22.0 [18.0–25.0]; P = 0.002), although follow-up asthma control did not significantly differ (Table 2). Similarly, baseline FEV1 was higher in cases than in control subjects (96.1 ± 15.3% vs. 92.0 ± 18.7%; P = 0.03), but there were no significant differences in follow-up FEV1 or mean ΔFEV1 between cases and control subjects. There were no significant differences between cases and control subjects in baseline, follow-up, or change in FVC or FEV1/FVC.

We then conducted subgroup analyses of cases. There were no significant differences in asthma symptom control or lung function before and after SARS-CoV-2 infection among the subgroups of children with persistent asthma, with moderate or severe asthma, who experienced an asthma exacerbation during acute COVID-19, those followed by an asthma specialist (i.e., pulmonology or allergy), or those whose controller medication category (ICS or ICS-LABA) did not change between baseline and follow-up visits (Table E3).

Next, we compared cases whose asthma control or lung function worsened after COVID-19 with those who did not exhibit clinically significant worsening (Table 3). After SARS-CoV-2 infection, 30 (17.5%) of 171 subjects had poorer asthma symptom control and 33 (28.9%) of 114 had lower lung function. A similar proportion of control subjects had clinically worse asthma symptom control (11 of 113 [9.7%]; P = 0.07) and lower lung function (77 of 237 [32.5%]; P = 0.50) during a similar follow-up period at our center. Cases with poorer asthma control after COVID-19 were younger (11.8 ± 4.0 yr vs. 13.5 ± 3.7 yr; P = 0.04), had a shorter time to follow-up (3.5 [1.5–7.5] vs. 6.1 [3.1–9.8] mo; P = 0.007), and were more likely to have presented with an asthma exacerbation during COVID-19 (46% vs. 26%; P = 0.04) than those without worse control; there were no significant differences in sex, race, body mass index, asthma severity, type of asthma controller medication, atopic comorbidities, or symptoms at initial presentation for COVID-19. We found no significant differences between individuals with and without worse lung function after COVID-19.

Discussion

In this prospective case-control study of children with asthma who had SARS-CoV-2 infection, we found no significant differences in asthma symptom control or lung function up to 18 months after COVID-19. Moreover, we found no significant differences in follow-up asthma control or lung function between children who had COVID-19 and control subjects with asthma seen in follow-up at our center but without SARS-CoV-2 infection during a comparable follow-up period. Together, these findings suggest that COVID-19 does not affect long-term asthma severity or control in the pediatric population.

Among children without asthma, lung function generally seems to be unaffected after SARS-CoV-2 infection. A small study of 16 children who completed spirometry 1.5–3 months after recovering from acute COVID-19 found no abnormalities in lung function after SARS-CoV-2 infection (30). In a study of 28 children with persistent symptoms for 6 weeks or more after SARS-CoV-2 infection who completed spirometry, the majority had normal spirometry, whereas 3 had obstructive patterns and none had restrictive patterns. Of the 21 patients who performed post-bronchodilator testing, 38.1% demonstrated a significant bronchodilator response (31). In a study of 77 children after acute COVID-19, 69 (89%) participants had normal physical examination findings and normal spirometry. Four of the participants had asthma, and none had a significant change in lung function from baseline (32).

We found no significant differences in FVC, FEV1, and FEV1/FVC in children with asthma after acute COVID-19, including among subgroups with persistent asthma, children with moderate or severe asthma, children who had an asthma exacerbation during acute SARS-CoV-2 infection, and children seen in follow-up by an asthma specialist. This is consistent with previous reports, including a study of 16 children in Italy with COVID-19 and atopy assessed at their first clinic visit after COVID-19 (33) and a retrospective study of 189 children in Turkey with COVID-19 and asthma assessed a median of 72 weeks after COVID-19 (34). However, the Turkish study reported lower FEF25–75 after COVID-19 infection, with obesity and family history of atopy identified as independent risk factors for a ⩾25% decrease in FEF25–75, and concluded that SARS-CoV-2 infection leads to small airway dysfunction in children with asthma (34). In contrast to that study, we did not find spirometric evidence that COVID-19 causes long-term small airway disease in children with asthma, nor did we find an association between obesity and decreased lung function after COVID-19.

We also found no differences in asthma symptom control before and after acute COVID-19. The slightly worse baseline symptom control in our control subjects compared with our cases could be partly explained by the observed improvements in asthma during the early phases of the pandemic (35) and by the fact that some of our control assessments occurred before that period. Importantly, follow-up asthma symptom control did not differ between cases and control subjects. In contrast, the Turkish study reported significant differences in both C-ACT and ACT scores after acute COVID-19, although differences were minimal and likely not clinically significant (34). At least one study has found that children with asthma had more frequent emergency department visits and oral corticosteroid prescriptions in the 6 months after a positive test result for SARS-CoV-2 compared with peers with asthma who had a negative test result for SARS-CoV-2 (36), providing other evidence of worsened asthma control after COVID-19. Although we did not look at acute exacerbations, our findings would suggest that these effects are not sustained in the long term.

Although we found no changes in asthma severity after COVID-19, we did find that five more children with symptom control data and seven more children with spirometry data were prescribed an ICS-LABA after COVID-19 compared with baseline. This raises the possibility that some children may have been prescribed step-up controller therapy before completing post–COVID-19 spirometry (e.g., at a primary care physician visit); however, the increase in ICS-LABA prescriptions also coincides with the 2020 updates to the National Asthma Education and Prevention Program asthma guidelines, which recommended ICS-LABA use earlier in the stepwise approach for management of asthma (25).

Although there were no significant overall changes in asthma severity after acute COVID-19, 17.5% of children with asthma in our study had worse symptom control and 28.9% had lower lung function. We found no differences between those with and those without worse lung function, but children with worse asthma symptom control after COVID-19 had shorter time to follow-up, suggesting that observed differences may be due at least in part to shorter time to follow-up. Although it is beyond the scope or design of our study, it will be important and interesting for future research to assess whether loss of symptom control after COVID-19 prompted earlier follow-up and whether any resulting interventions led to clinical improvement in subsequent months. These children were also more likely to have had an asthma exacerbation during COVID-19; however, the multivariable analysis adjusted for time to follow-up again rendered the acute exacerbation nonsignificant. Additional studies with longer time to follow-up and multiple follow-up points will be needed to answer these outstanding questions. It is important to note that these proportions were very similar to the ones we found over a similar length of follow-up in our control subjects, suggesting that our results could be within the expected variability in a pediatric asthma cohort rather than a consequence of SARS-CoV-2 infection.

It has been hypothesized that allergic asthma may actually be protective against SARS-CoV-2 infection (37). The most predominant asthma endotype in children is type 2–high asthma, characterized by allergic sensitization and eosinophilic airway inflammation (38). This type 2 immune response might counteract the COVID-19 cytokine storm (39) and cause protective downregulation in angiotensin-converting enzyme 2, which is the cellular receptor for SARS-CoV-2 (40, 41). Overproduction of mucus in asthma has also been proposed as a possible protective factor against SARS-CoV-2 because mucus is a physical barrier that prevents the virus from reaching the type 2 pneumocytes, which exist in the alveoli and are the predominant expressors of angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 in the lung (37). In addition, ICS are the first-line treatment for persistent asthma, and ICS have been shown to suppress virus replication and cytokine production (42).

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has several key strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first prospective case-control study of asthma symptom control and lung function after SARS-CoV-2 infection in children with asthma. There have been few studies to date looking at the long-term impact of COVID-19 on children with asthma; of those, we were able to identify only one study with a control group, and that study included data at only one time point for control subjects, was conducted in a different country with a different healthcare system, and arrived at a different conclusion from our study (34). We manually extracted data for this study from the EHR, and thus it provides more detailed, accurate, and complete information than reports based on diagnostic or billing codes. Our relatively large sample size allowed subgroup analyses, which strengthened support for our findings, although our study may have had inadequate power to detect small differences.

The study has several limitations as well. Longer follow-up will be necessary to assess whether children with asthma are at risk of developing impairments in lung function or decreased asthma symptom control further out from acute COVID-19 (i.e., years later), and subsequent studies should compare bronchodilator responsiveness and fractional exhaled nitric oxide before and after COVID-19 infection. Our analysis included a single medical center and a single geographic area; it will be important to conduct multicenter studies to assess if findings vary across settings or regions. Although including control assessments that occurred before March 2020 decreases the likelihood that control subjects unknowingly contracted COVID-19 between the two assessments of asthma control or lung function, some of our results may have been impacted by the different measures adopted early in the pandemic. Because our study used data extracted from an EHR, it is also subject to reporting bias. Finally, because of the nature of our asthma registry, we were unable to include children with asthma managed by a community provider as control subjects.

Conclusions

In summary, during an 18-month case-control study of children with asthma, we found no significant differences in follow-up asthma symptom control or spirometry results between children with and children without SARS-CoV-2 infection, and among children who had COVID-19, we found no significant differences in asthma control or lung function up to 18 months after SARS-CoV-2 infection. Our findings differ from those of a prior study that reported that SARS-CoV-2 infection leads to small airway dysfunction in children with asthma (34). The 17.5% of children with worse asthma symptom control after SARS-CoV-2 infection had a shorter time to follow-up, which may have partially explained the findings, and a similar proportion of control subjects had worse asthma symptom control at follow-up. Our findings suggest that COVID-19 does not affect long-term asthma severity or control in the pediatric population.
