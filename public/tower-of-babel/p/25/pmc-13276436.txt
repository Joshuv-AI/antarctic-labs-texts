Violence Management in Mental Health Nursing: A Conceptual Analysis
Authors: Kenji Takahashi, Koji Tanaka, Kyoko Nagata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTViolence in mental illness settings poses a serious problem, threatening nurses' physical and psychological safety and compromising quality of care. Front line responses tend to rely heavily on nurses' individual capabilities, making the establishment of organisational support systems and management policies an urgent necessity. This study aimed to clarify the essence and components of the concept ‘violence management’ in mental health nursing and to propose a definition that will contribute to future practice and education. We analysed 35 articles, following Rodgers' (2000) conceptual analysis methodology and reporting in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Four antecedent conditions were identified: ‘consumers’ ineffective impulse control’, ‘nurses' affinity for exposure to violence’, ‘difficulty in establishing a shared understanding of safety’, and ‘lack of consistency in violence response’. In addition, five attributes were identified: ‘relationship‐centred non‐invasive care’, ‘ensuring a safe healthcare environment’, ‘adherence to forensic frameworks', ‘early risk detection’, and ‘professional capacity development’. Five consequences were also identified: ‘strengthening support for nurses', ‘strengthening human resource development’, ‘organisational conditions that strengthen safety’, ‘challenges in violence reporting’, and ‘challenges in assessment tools'. Mental health nursing defines the concept of violence management as an effort to provide flexible and comprehensive care across national and cultural differences in response to diverse and uncertain factors that make consumer impulse control difficult, maximising limited resources while establishing an organisational support system. This definition presents a new framework linking nursing management and clinical practice.

Introduction

The International Council of Nurses has identified violence against healthcare workers as a significant occupational risk and emphasised the need for systematic prevention and countermeasures (International Council of Nurses 2017). The International Labour Organization (2020) has recommended organisational risk assessment, reporting and redress mechanisms, ongoing training, and victim support at high‐risk workplaces. Western countries have treated violence in healthcare settings as a core issue in risk management, against a backdrop of heightened awareness of the right to healthcare safety.

Meanwhile, in mental health treatment settings, challenges remain regarding the effectiveness of violence risk assessment tools and their implementation in clinical practice (Higgins et al. 2016). Furthermore, the concept of violence and management frameworks vary significantly between countries and facilities (Higgins et al. 2016; Olsson et al. 2015). These differences stem from variations in individual nurses' risk perception and response capabilities (Renwick et al. 2016) and the inherently ambiguous definition of the term ‘violence management’ (Higgins et al. 2016). Although it has been established that the occurrence of violence is not solely attributable to consumer factors, the nature of nurses' involvement and the quality of the therapeutic relationship also exert influence (Olsson et al. 2015; Renwick et al. 2016). Indeed, instances of violence triggered by the establishment of relationships with consumers or responses during the provision of care are not uncommon (Al‐Azzam et al. 2018; Legesse et al. 2022), with the resulting harm ranging from physical injury to long‐term psychological effects (McKinnon and Cross 2008).

Since the 1970s, Western countries have advanced the systematisation of violence countermeasures and staff safety in mental health treatment settings (Cowman et al. 2017).

In 2002, Japan introduced its Comprehensive Violence Prevention and Program based on the Control and Restraint programme developed in the UK (Paterson 2009) (Kinoshita and Shimozato 2021). However, despite achieving some results, further improvement is needed in training content and intervention programme design (McKinnon and Cross 2008) as well as sufficient evaluation of the effectiveness of these clinical interventions.

Furthermore, ward environments and the presence of institutional support significantly impact nurses' ability to respond and affect consumers' sense of security (Olsson et al. 2015). Nursing managers bear the critical responsibility of ensuring the safety of both consumers and staff. However, the current situation is hindered by ambiguous concepts and inconsistent implementation, making it difficult to establish a comprehensive violence management system. Given these challenges, it is necessary to re‐examine the concept of violence management in mental health treatment settings and clarify a framework for building a safety culture where nursing managers play a central role.

Background

‘Violence management in mental health nursing’ is a dynamic concept that has evolved in response to social contexts, mental health policies, and shifts in clinical practice. Violence from consumers toward healthcare workers has long been recognised as a serious occupational and ethical challenge in mental health care (International Labour Office et al. 2002). Historically, violence was thought to stem from individual pathology, but in recent years it has come to be understood as a phenomenon involving complex interactions between environmental, organisational, and interpersonal factors (Bowers 2014; Duxbury and Whittington 2005).

Thus, the understanding of violence has expanded from individual factors to a multi‐layered perspective encompassing environmental, organisational, and societal factors. Consequently, responses must also be considered not only at the individual level but within organisational and societal frameworks. Addressing workplace violence requires shared responsibility and comprehensive efforts at all levels, including the government, healthcare administrators, healthcare workers, and the community (Lim et al. 2022).

The 2000s saw progress in the development of educational programmes and manuals aimed at preventing and responding to violence (Duxbury 2002). However, a gap between theory and practice has persisted because of diverse consumer characteristics and environmental factors. Mental health nurses, in particular, face the dual challenges of maintaining therapeutic relationships while ensuring their own safety, and they have been reported to experience significant physical and psychological burdens (Renwick et al. 2016).

In recent years, violence management has been redefined as a multifaceted concept encompassing three aspects: risk management, non‐invasive interventions emphasising relationships, and organisational support systems that foster a culture of safety (Price et al. 2024). However, attempts to comprehensively integrate these elements and systematise them into a theoretical and practical framework applicable to clinical settings remain insufficient. A future challenge is to clarify the concept of violence management in mental health care and present a model that contributes to building a culture of safety, with nursing managers playing a central role.

Methods

Framework for Concept Clarification

A concept is an intellectual structure formed by identifying common characteristics of a term, then abstracting and classifying them. Rodgers (1989) noted that concept clarification is an essential process for theory development and the advancement of knowledge in nursing. For terms frequently used in nursing practice but defined ambiguously, we must systematically clarify the structure and meaning of the concept. In her method of conceptual analysis, Rodgers (1989) emphasises the evolutionary perspective as a means of understanding events within dynamic backgrounds, situations, and contexts. Since our research addresses dynamic concepts, we considered this method appropriate.

Data Collection

This study collected data using PubMed, CINAHL (Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature), PsycArticles, and Cochrane. These databases were selected based on their relevance to the research domain. The data analysed in this study were collected between 1991 and 2025, following the PRISMA guideline flowchart and checklist (Page et al. 2021). The data‐collection process was conducted with one co‐researcher. The search criteria ([violence] AND [risk management] AND [(mental health nurse) OR (psychiatric nurse)]) yielded a total of 610 papers: 345 from PubMed, 11 from CINAHL, 234 from PsycArticles, and 20 from Cochrane (March 2025). Articles that did not include violence management, mental health, nurse, or equivalent terms in their titles or abstracts, or were judged to be irrelevant, were excluded (n = 504). In the secondary screening, we excluded non‐English literature (n = 2), literature reviews (n = 19), studies inaccessible to the researchers (n = 16), studies not involving mental health subjects (n = 8), and studies on violence risk scale development (n = 26). Ultimately, 35 research articles were analysed and classified according to the PRISMA 2020 standard format (Figure 1).

Data Analysis

Rodgers' (2000) conceptual analysis examines how target concepts are used, focusing on context and general usage, in order to clarify the concept's antecedents, attributes, and consequences. In the present study, descriptions related to ‘violence management’ were extracted and summarised from the relevant literature. These descriptions were coded based on similarities and differences, abstracted into meaningful units, and classified.

When organising the antecedents, attributes, and consequences of the concept of violence management, we found that the focus of descriptions varied across the literature, and the boundaries between each element were not always clear. We aimed to maintain analytical consistency by defining the classification criteria as follows:

‘Antecedents’ were defined as background factors or preconditions existing prior to the emergence of countermeasures against violent acts, including individual, organisational, and environmental factors that trigger or promote violent behaviour, or situational contexts necessitating countermeasures. In contrast, ‘attributes’ were organised around specific coping actions and practical processes during violence incidents or their prevention, captured as the ‘essential characteristics of actions/practices’ demonstrating how nursing professionals actually respond to violence risks. ‘Consequences’ were positioned to indicate the resulting changes or impacts from violence management practices, organising outcomes and challenges arising from institutional arrangements, shifts in safety culture, and other practical results, institutional development, and shifts in safety culture. In this way, even when conceptual elements overlapped, we clarified the conceptual structure of violence management by systematically organising them into antecedents, attributes, and consequences while prioritising consensus among analysts.

Results

Overview of the Literature on Violence Management in Mental Health Nursing

The concept of ‘violence management’ in mental health nursing is used similarly across various specialties and perspectives within the field, including ‘crisis intervention’. This concept spans multiple disciplines: mental health nursing, mental health treatment settings, clinical psychology, nursing administration, forensic medicine, and risk management. Papers from the perspective of mental health nurses focused primarily on consumer violence risk. The use of assessment tools revealed an overlap between aspects of understanding an individual's mental state and the organisational management perspective. Research methods included quantitative studies, qualitative studies, and mixed methods, such as verifying risk factors and predictive accuracy, analysing nurses' and consumers' experiences and narratives, integrating nurses' and consumers' perspectives, and document analysis. Fifteen articles originated from Europe, nine from Australia, seven from the United States, three from Asia, and one from Ethiopia. Our findings clarify the characteristics of the concept ‘violence management in mental health nursing’ based on Rodgers' approach (see Table 1).

Related Concepts

We also examined the usage of related concepts identified during data collection. The difference between ‘violence management’ and ‘crisis intervention’ lies in the overlapping aspect of ‘temporary nature of the crisis’. Symptoms of mental illness can escalate into crises, necessitating a stepwise approach where preventive measures are implemented based on the gradual onset of symptoms (Borg et al. 2011). However, crisis intervention and violence management differ in their purpose, scope, target population, and implementation methods (Roberts 2000). Therefore, crisis intervention is positioned as a specific, acute‐phase response strategy within violence management. Furthermore, violence management refers to clinical practice within a specific mental illness context, targeting consumer aggression and violent behaviour. Violence management also emphasises maintaining the therapeutic relationship and safeguarding consumer dignity while ensuring safety. This concept is integrated into the care process, including de‐escalation and non‐invasive interventions (Renwick et al. 2016), with a focus on preventing and mitigating violence based on the relationship with the consumer. In contrast, risk management refers to an organisational and institutional framework aimed at minimising the incidence and impact of a wide range of adverse events, including not only violence but also falls, infections, and medical errors (Leape 1994). Although both share the goal of safety, violence management is directly linked to care quality and relationship building whereas risk management emphasises managing the entire system. Furthermore, a ‘violence prevention program’ is a structured intervention framework at the organisational level, incorporating systematic education and training, environmental modifications, and standardised procedures. It also aims to foster consistent staff responses and cultivate a safety culture (Bowers et al. 2015; Price et al. 2015). Violence prevention programmes are specific, packaged programmes for preventing violence that are encompassed within the concept of violence management.

Antecedents

The antecedents for violence management in mental health nursing include characteristic situations or events observed before violence occurs. Conceptual analysis identified four points as follows: (1) consumer's ineffective impulse control, (2) nurses' affinity for exposure to violence, (3) difficulty establishing a shared understanding of safety, and (4) lack of consistency in violence response. These factors are positioned as a background that promotes violence occurrence arising from the mutual interaction of consumer, nurse, and organisational factors.

Consumers' Ineffective Impulse Control

Impulsivity and limited awareness of one's mental health condition are associated with the manifestation of aggression. Such ineffective impulse control is often linked to vulnerability in self‐control and limited awareness of one's mental health condition, and it is associated with the manifestation of aggression (McKinnon and Cross 2008; Yao et al. 2012). Previous research indicates that aggression is partially associated with gender and diagnosis, but its relationship with other factors remains unclear (Lavelle et al. 2016; Olsson et al. 2013). In clinical settings, verbal aggression is most common and is often observed as a precursor to physical violence or property damage (Al‐Azzam et al. 2018; Renwick et al. 2016). Indeed, violence and threats frequently occur in primarily verbal forms (Gunenc et al. 2015; Laake et al. 2025), and recognising these early signs directly contributes to risk reduction. Furthermore, a lack of daytime activity opportunities is recognised as a factor that can promote impulsive behaviour through pent‐up energy and frustration (Meehan et al. 2006; Whittington and Wykes 1996). Studies have also reported differences in the expression patterns and frequency of aggression among adolescents and ethnic minorities based on their attributes and backgrounds (Laake et al. 2025; Renwick et al. 2016). Additionally, the ward environment and type of hospitalisation are important contextual factors influencing violence incidence rates (Delaney et al. 2001; LePage et al. 2005).

Nurses' Affinity for Exposure to Violence

The ‘points of contact’ between nurses and consumers are essential to care but can also trigger violence. Nurses often become primary targets of violence through interactions such as observation, treatment, and verbal communication (Legesse et al. 2022; McKinnon and Cross 2008). Even well‐intentioned approaches can stimulate aggression depending on timing and method (Legesse et al. 2022). Furthermore, violence tends to be concentrated during the initial hospitalisation period. Nurses' pacing, approach, setting of tolerance limits, and early recognition of signs influence subsequent escalation or de‐escalation (Hinsby and Baker 2004; Yao et al. 2012). Additionally, environmental factors such as ward crowding, observation intensity, and admission status have been reported to increase the frequency of violent incidents (Yao et al. 2012). Considering these points, exposure to violence is not an ‘unavoidable risk’ but rather a preventable practice challenge through a combination of interaction methods and environmental adjustments. In fact, it is suggested that nurses who are unaware of workplace training or response policies, or who cannot utilise them, have a higher risk of exposure to violence (Al‐Azzam et al. 2018; Legesse et al. 2022).

Difficulty in Establishing a Shared Understanding of Safety

Difficulty in establishing a shared understanding of safety highlights not a lack of safety measures, but the challenge of who shares the concept of ‘safety’ and how. Without a shared understanding, even well‐established systems and infrastructure may not function effectively. In practice, sharing that incorporates dialogue among staff as well as consumer input is essential. In reality, nurses' risk perception and responses vary (Blando et al. 2013; McKinnon and Cross 2008), and differences in defining safety/risk and relational dynamics influence aggression and safety awareness (Blando et al. 2013; Renwick et al. 2016). Furthermore, a lack of empathy or respectful treatment can provoke consumer aggression (Olsson et al. 2015; Price et al. 2024), underscoring that violence prevention requires the entire team's shared understanding of safety by establishing a shared language through education, training, and clear policies.

Lack of Consistency in Violence Response

Lack of consistency in violence response refers to a situation where the continuous practice of assessing, predicting, preventing risks, and appropriately reviewing incidents to prevent recurrence is not unified within an organisation, resulting in structural difficulties in preventing violence before it occurs.

For example, even when the same situation is judged as ‘dangerous’, responses vary, with one nurse immediately choosing physical restraint and another taking the time to respond while listening to the consumer. Inconsistency also manifests as variations in the use of risk assessment tools. Although tools like DASA (Dynamic Appraisal of Situational Aggression) and V‐RISK‐Y (Violence Risk Screening Tool for Young people) demonstrate good predictive accuracy for assessing aggression and risk levels in hospitalised consumers (Laake et al. 2025), differences in their application and interpretation lead to inconsistent implementation. Consistency is also influenced by environmental and institutional factors such as crowding, observation density, staffing levels, documentation, reporting systems, and the presence of policies (Meehan et al. 2006; Lepping et al. 2009). Few healthcare staff feel confident handling violent incidents, potentially due to underlying response anxiety (LePage et al. 2005). This situation suggests insufficient access to education and training as well as inadequate standardisation of content, which ultimately reinforces inconsistency. Considering the above, establishing consistent responses across the entire organisation requires a combination of standardised assessment tools, clear operational procedures, improvements in environmental and institutional factors, explicit policies, and systematic education and training.

Attributes

The attributes of violence management in mental health nursing are distinctive yet interrelated elements that support practice. Conceptual analysis identified five key attributes: (1) Relationship‐centred non‐invasive care, (2) ensuring a safe healthcare environment, (3) adherence to a forensic framework, (4) early risk detection, and (5) professional competency development. These are core elements for preventing violence and maintaining a safe, therapeutic environment.

Relationship‐Centred Non‐Invasive Care

Violence management must be elevated beyond mere ‘risk avoidance’ or ‘problem behaviour control’ to a practice that engages individuals as people through therapeutic relationships. Central to this is enhancing the quality of dialogue and relationships with consumers, which requires the establishment of therapeutic relationships based on trust. Although mental health treatment settings may employ restrictive measures such as medication or seclusion (Yao et al. 2012), a crucial principle is to limit them to high‐risk situations while prioritising non‐restrictive interventions in daily practice.

Nursing staff manage violence risks through flexible, situation‐appropriate responses while maintaining a therapeutic environment (Reade and Nourse 2012; Trenoweth 2003). They provide reassurance through non‐threatening language, expressions, and nonverbal engagement such as posture, making judgements starting with ‘What is troubling them?’ and ‘How can we support them?’ Furthermore, practices emphasising effective de‐escalation through mutual engagement and emotional regulation are highlighted (Price et al. 2024), with nurses clearly recognising the importance of non‐physical approaches (Price et al. 2015).

Moreover, music has been shown to promote alternative coping through sensory stimulation and choice, whereas music, video games, exercise/snacks, sensory integration, and play therapy can suppress the escalation of potential violent behaviour (Reade and Nourse 2012). These approaches form the core of relationship‐centred, non‐invasive care, thereby supporting practices that prevent violence through building therapeutic relationships independent of coercion.

Ensuring a Safe Healthcare Environment

Ensuring a safe healthcare environment is a critical element that not only deters violence but also enhances care quality, prevents staff burnout, and promotes consumers' therapeutic stability. Violence management must be restructured as a comprehensive approach encompassing not only physical and institutional responses but also the cultivation of a safety culture. Specifically, this involves the establishment of safe care systems through reporting, information sharing, and self‐regulation support (Lepping et al. 2009). Nursing and security personnel should collaborate to intervene promptly and appropriately in consumer altercations (Reade and Nourse 2012). Nurses express concerns about workplace safety, necessitating the strengthening of support systems as well as ongoing supervision (Legesse et al. 2022). Nursing care must be organised flexibly and empathetically, with risk management pursued continuously and collaboratively (Higgins et al. 2016). Furthermore, Price et al. (2024) demonstrated that safety culture, team cohesion, and leadership influence morale. These factors guide the creation of a safe ward environment that respects both staff and consumers while acknowledging inherent risks.

Adherence to a Forensic Framework

This refers not merely to legal compliance but to professional practice aimed at creating ‘places where everyone can be safe’. In forensic mental health settings such as case management of mental incapacity or involuntary hospitalisation, a multidisciplinary collaborative system is established, pre‐determining necessary staffing levels and collaborating professions. The emphasis is on utilising structured risk assessment and management frameworks and prioritising therapeutic interventions rather than relying solely on physical restraints or isolation. Specifically, Pollak and Palmstierna (2024) demonstrated that utilising ‘critical factors’ selected by staff during care plan development can contribute to reducing future violence risks. It is necessary to incorporate assessment tools into care plans and monitor changes in conditions. Through structured risk management, collaborative care planning, and clinical interventions, treatment and safety must be achieved holistically.

Early Risk Detection

The cumulative effect of identifying risks early and acting promptly forms the cornerstone of safeguarding ‘safety’ and ‘dignity’ in mental health treatment. In mental health nursing, patients' mental states fluctuate daily. Within these fluctuations, precursors to potential ‘violence’ may sometimes be concealed. The mechanism for ensuring these signs are detected as early and reliably as possible is ‘early risk detection’. For example, a veteran nurse during morning rounds detects a subtle change in the tone of a usually calm patient's voice. Based on this small sense of unease, the team uses a violence assessment tool to numerically evaluate the patient's risk of violence for that day. Actions considered effective include assessing medium‐to‐long‐term risk using the HCR‐20 (Historical Clinical Risk Management‐20) and coordinating with community transition support, along with ongoing management, including routine screening (Higgins et al. 2016).

Professional Competency Development

Beyond merely acquiring knowledge and skills, each nurse is expected to continually re‐examine their own expertise and adopt a growth mindset through practice. This category focuses on how nurses refine their professional competence while confronting consumers and violent situations with ethical responsibility and professional awareness. Mental health treatment settings offer opportunities for violence response training (Blando et al. 2013; McKinnon and Cross 2008), enhancing response capabilities through education, information gathering, and risk assessment (Delaney et al. 2001; Higgins et al. 2016). However, also noted are insufficient confidence in response and inadequate education (LePage et al. 2005), necessitating standardised policies and continuing education for both individuals and organisations (Lepping et al. 2009). These findings indicate that the development of professional capacity is closely linked not only to individual experience but also to organisational conditions.

Consequences

The consequences of risk management of violence in mental health nursing encompass outcomes and challenges arising in practice and organisational operations. Conceptual analysis yielded the following five points: (1) strengthening support for nurses, (2) strengthening human resource development, (3) organisational conditions that strengthen safety, (4) challenges in violence reporting, and (5) challenges in assessment tools. These findings underscore the necessity of establishing organisational support systems that extend beyond individual efforts to address violence.

Strengthening Support for Nurses

Strengthening support for nurses does not simply mean increasing countermeasures. It means creating a violence‐free, trustworthy, and secure healthcare environment through both a warm, attentive approach rooted in workplace realities and institutional support. Nurses in mental health treatment wards constantly confront the weight of the word ‘safety’, supporting consumers while exposing themselves to danger.

Mental health nurses exhibit higher safety awareness than nurses in other departments (Blando et al. 2013). This stems from constant tension with unpredictable violence. Some nurses actually experience physical and psychological trauma (McKinnon and Cross 2008), with accumulated violence increasing psychological burden and resignation intentions (Al‐Azzam et al. 2018). The rising turnover rate among younger nurses is another organisational challenge.

Despite this, nurses remain on the frontlines, continuously seeking ways to provide better care. Many nurses feel that the current support system is inadequate and call for post‐violence reflection and consultation opportunities, careful follow‐up from managers, and an environment where they can speak up with confidence. This is not only for the individual nurse's peace of mind but also forms the foundation for building a safe organisational culture.

Strengthening Human Resource Development

Strengthening staff development means more than just conducting training; it requires establishing a continuous support system that addresses on‐the‐job anxiety and conflict, comprehensively nurturing knowledge, emotional well‐being, and dialogue. The key to managing violence lies in educational approaches that enable nurses to respond with confidence and reassurance.

However, few healthcare staff feel confident in responding to violent incidents, potentially reflecting underlying anxiety about the handling of such situations (LePage et al. 2005; Trenoweth 2003). This anxiety is linked to existing education and training not always meeting on‐the‐job needs. Many participants point out outdated training content and insufficient discussion (Lepping et al. 2009; McKinnon and Cross 2008). Reports also indicate variations in satisfaction levels across countries (Al‐Azzam et al. 2018; Lepping et al. 2009). Therefore, training must go beyond formal knowledge transfer and incorporate educational approaches tailored to the context of each country and facility. In fact, managing aggression requires stepwise, effective interventions that promote patient aggression control (Delaney et al. 2001; Lavelle et al. 2016), necessitating the inclusion of case‐specific practical techniques in educational content. Thus, to alleviate frontline anxiety and build confidence, it is essential to restructure existing education and introduce flexible, participatory, and practical training.

Organisational Conditions That Strengthen Safety

Organisational conditions that strengthen safety go beyond adequate equipment or manuals. Rather, they foster an entire organisation that prioritises ‘safety’ and develop a support system flexible enough to respond to changes on the frontlines. A space where nurses and consumers can interact with peace of mind emerges where systems, culture, and dialogue intersect.

One nurse stated, ‘No matter how hard you try, safety cannot be maintained if the team is fragmented.’ Indeed, in mental health treatment hospitals, safety culture and morale are influenced by team cohesion and leadership (Price et al. 2024). Violence prevention requires not only empathetic and flexible nursing care but also a continuous, collaborative risk management system. Enhancing responsiveness through education, information sharing, and risk assessment is essential (Higgins et al. 2016). Ward managers in some countries, including the UK, report satisfaction with resources and systems, viewing protocols and training as key effective measures (Lepping et al. 2009). In contrast, some hospitals' reporting procedures and policies are unclear, leading to missed incidents of violence and placing nurses at risk. Satisfaction with protocols for coercive measures and documentation as well as reporting rates also vary by country (LePage et al. 2005; Lepping et al. 2009).

In recent years, strength‐based approaches have been incorporated into user‐centred service planning and emphasised as resources for practitioners (Al‐Azzam et al. 2018; Reade and Nourse 2012). Consequently, there is an expanding movement to provide user‐centred care resources to practitioners, which contributes to staff motivation and continuity of safe care.

Challenges in Violence Reporting

Challenges in violence reporting extend beyond the simple fact of ‘low reporting rates’. They involve a complex interplay of underlying organisational and psychological barriers, deficiencies in documentation and management systems, and anxiety and distrust felt by both consumers and nurses. Violence reporting is not an ‘administrative task’ but a critical process for safeguarding workplace safety and trust. It requires clarification of reporting procedures, standardisation of records, and strengthening of managerial support systems (Higgins et al. 2016). Nurses face organisational and psychological barriers to reporting often due to insufficient managerial support (Al‐Azzam et al. 2018). Support after reporting is often inadequate, and it remains unclear how the reported information is utilised. Furthermore, documentation of reports and risk reassessments is insufficient, and management systems are poorly established (Legesse et al. 2022). On the front lines, there is a psychological resistance to ‘leaving a record’, which inhibits reporting. At the same time, patients who commit violence are also sensitive to the handling of their actions. They may feel they have been ‘labelled as dangerous’ and develop distrust. As Meehan et al. (2006) pointed out, consumers demand prompt and fair investigation and response to aggressive behaviour. Violent incidents should not be reduced to a perpetrator–victim dynamic but instead require transparent and accountable responses.

Challenges in Assessment Tools

Challenges in assessment tools are complex, rooted not only in the tools themselves but also in the lack of operational ingenuity and support for embedding them in practice and integrating them into actual care. Predicting and preventing violence requires not just tool use but also information sharing based on mutual understanding between consumers and staff, combined with appropriate care.

Although tools such as DASA and HCR‐20 demonstrate a certain level of predictive accuracy (Chu et al. 2013; Laake et al. 2025), many practitioners report difficulties such as ‘not really understanding how to use them’ or ‘having manuals but not actually being able to use them’. Nurses may sense changes in consumers but sometimes struggle with how to assess and document these changes. Whereas tools may be effective for predicting specific behaviours, in practice, usage is inconsistent and formal evaluation remains inadequate (Higgins et al. 2016). Additionally, because of heavy workloads, ‘checks become merely formalistic, with unclear relevance to safety’. Nurses recognise the limitations of intuitive care while maintaining a safety consciousness linked to equipment, security systems, and continuous risk assessment.

Violence management in mental health nursing: A conceptual model.

Model Case: Nurse A on an acute mental health setting was assigned to Consumer B, a man in his 20s with a history of schizophrenia. B had experienced insomnia and agitation during the previous night, and by morning, exhibited verbal aggression, including loud shouting. Recognising an increased risk of violence, Nurse A shared a risk assessment during the multidisciplinary conference and discussed non‐invasive strategies. Considering B's past behaviour and psychological state, they agreed to avoid excessive involvement while establishing a reassuring relationship and adjusting the environment. Through dialogue, Nurse A listened to B's anxieties, offered choices rather than restrictions, and provided care sensitive to the consumer's feelings and background. The head nurse informed the entire ward of the situation and worked to ensure safety. Nurse A applied her expertise based on knowledge and experience gained through training, which confirmed legal obligations and the scope of applicable ward restrictions. As a result, Consumer B did not escalate to physical violence, and after stabilising, a therapeutic relationship was maintained. The healthcare team reviewed the case to inform future risk management.

Discussion

This study defined ‘violence management’ in mental health nursing in order to facilitate flexible and comprehensive care that transcends national and cultural differences in response to diverse and uncertain factors that make impulse control difficult for consumers. Violence management involves maximising limited resources while establishing an organisational support system. The extracted attributes revealed a multi‐layered structure extending beyond individual care to include legal responsibilities and workplace environments. Notably, ‘compliance with forensic frameworks’ and ‘ensuring a safe healthcare environment’ highlight the importance of legal accountability and workplace environment improvements alongside clinical judgement (Lepping et al. 2009; Meehan et al. 2006). Furthermore, ‘relationship‐centred non‐invasive care’ substantiates the effectiveness of interventions based on therapeutic relationships rather than coercive restraint (Yao et al. 2012), whereas ‘professional capacity development’ indicates competency enhancement through education, information gathering, and evaluation (Blando et al. 2013; Higgins et al. 2016). In other words, violence management is established through the interaction of individual professionalism and organisational conditions.

As antecedents, ‘difficulty in establishing a shared understanding of safety’ and ‘lack of consistency in violence response’ emerged as factors that hinder standardisation and consensus building. Consumer impulsivity and lack of insight directly impact risk perception (McKinnon and Cross 2008), and unpredictability increases the difficulty of intervention. Nurses are structurally vulnerable to exposure to violence (Blando et al. 2013), and distrust of medication or relationships and authoritarian interactions exacerbate aggression and fragment safety perceptions (Renwick et al. 2016). Furthermore, environmental and institutional factors directly impact safety perceptions and cultural differences, amplifying response inconsistencies (Lepping et al. 2009). Therefore, violence must be understood as a structural challenge involving a complex interplay between the individual (patient characteristics), profession (nursing practice), and organisation (institutions/culture).

Consequently, the antecedents of ‘challenges in violence reporting’ are standardisation and enhanced managerial support (Al‐Azzam et al. 2018; Legesse et al. 2022). ‘Challenges in assessment tools’ indicate effectiveness but insufficient implementation and coordination (Olsson et al. 2015; Price et al. 2024). ‘Strengthening support for nurses’ involves establishing counselling and peer support systems, which are essential to address psychological trauma and reduced job motivation. Furthermore, experiencing violence impacts mental burden and workplace retention (Blando et al. 2013), whereas insufficient education and support hinder safety assurance (Higgins et al. 2016). ‘Strengthening personnel development’ requires systematising simulation training and continuing education. McKinnon and Cross (2008) reported that non‐physical intervention techniques become effective when linked to practical experience, and they contribute to professional growth and organisational culture formation. Finally, ‘fostering organisational culture’ is the foundation supporting all these elements. Lepping et al. (2009) reported that ward environments and institutional arrangements contribute to the formation of safety culture, whereas managerial leadership creates variations in violence response capabilities.

Synthesising these findings, violence management is positioned as a ‘preventive, comprehensive, and continuous effort’ that requires not only individual nurses' efforts but nursing managers& establishment of systems, development of training frameworks, and cultivation of organisational culture. The definition proposed in this study emphasises field applicability while acknowledging the challenges of international diversity and standardisation. As demonstrated by Lepping et al.'s (2009) multinational comparison, common elements exist in safe organisational cultures despite differences in legal systems and cultures. In line with international findings, violence cannot be reduced to individual characteristics but is a phenomenon closely linked to organisational culture and institutional design. Therefore, this study's definition complements existing theoretical foundations and provides an effective perspective for redefining violence management as a universal and practical framework.

Applicability of the Conceptual Model for Violence Management in Mental Health Nursing

Although concepts are important to violence management in mental health nursing, their structure has not been explicitly outlined. This study clarifies the conceptual structure, indicating that nurses require skills and attitudes to recognise and respond to signs of violence, whereas nursing managers need to establish support systems, educational programmes, and a reporting culture. A future challenge is to develop attribute‐based educational models and guidelines. The present study is limited to literature‐based conceptualisation and so a future task is to verify its consistency with and applicability to field practice. Going forward, it will be necessary to conduct empirical research aimed at implementing and evaluating educational programmes and developing management models in order to reflect the extracted attributes and consequences.

Strengths and Limitations

This study is significant for systematically organising the concept of ‘violence management in mental health treatment settings’ through four antecedents, five attributes, and five consequences, thereby presenting a comprehensive definition. This definition provides a theoretical foundation for ambiguous terms and contributes to the formation of a common understanding in research and clinical practice. Furthermore, its originality lies in reconceptualising the issue from a multifaceted perspective that encompasses not only individual‐level care but also organisational culture, institutional factors, and diverse elements transcending national and cultural boundaries. Building on existing research, it bridges theory and practice by linking therapeutic practices where nurses ensure the safety of both consumers and themselves within theoretical frameworks. By identifying managerial perspectives such as ‘cultivating a safe culture’ and ‘establishing organisational support systems’, it demonstrates applicability beyond individual interventions to nursing management and organisational operations. Limitations include an overrepresentation of Western literature in the analysis, resulting in insufficient consideration of cultural diversity; a high level of abstraction that has yet to lead to the presentation of concrete practice models; and a focus primarily on nurses and managers, thereby limiting the perspectives of patients and other professions. Future challenges include both practical application and verification of the effectiveness of the defined framework.

Implications for Future Research

The conceptual model presented in this study provides a foundation for understanding violence management in mental health treatment settings. However, challenges must be overcome in order to effectively apply it to clinical, educational, and managerial practice; this includes verifying cultural diversity, conducting empirical research, integrating multidisciplinary and consumer perspectives, applying it to organisational culture, and incorporating a long‐term perspective. Future research is expected to sequentially resolve these challenges, thereby advancing violence management into a truly safe and therapeutic practice.

Conclusion

Performing a conceptual analysis following Rodgers' (2000) methodology, we identified four antecedent conditions (consumers' ineffective impulse control, nurses' affinity for exposure to violence, difficulty in establishing a shared understanding of safety, and lack of consistency in violence response), five attributes (relationship‐centred non‐invasive care, ensuring a safe healthcare environment, adherence to forensic frameworks, early risk detection, and professional capacity development), and five consequences (strengthening support for nurses, strengthening human resource development, organisational conditions that strengthen safety, challenges in violence reporting, and challenges in assessment tools).

Violence management demonstrates itself as a practice that ensures the safety of both consumers and nurses through therapeutic engagement while also developing healthcare setting culture and organisational systems. In other words, this concept emphasises the importance of a multifaceted approach combining individual‐level responses and organisational‐level development.

Relevance to Clinical Practice

This study re‐examined the framework for safety measures in the mental health field through a conceptual review of violence management. Relationship‐centred non‐invasive care and early risk detection contribute not only to violence avoidance but also to building consumers' trust. Safety culture and organisational support enhance nurses' sense of security and ensure consistency in practice. The results of this study facilitate the assessment of their implementation in clinical settings based on this foundation. Strengthening professionalism through education and training is also essential, and this concept can serve as a new guideline in clinical settings.
