Predictors of a successful glucocorticoid tapering and withdrawal in an inception cohort of patients with lupus nephritis and associations with long-term outcomes and damage accrual
Authors: Ioannis E Michelakis, Alexandros Panagiotopoulos, Eleni Kapsia, John Boletis, Smaragdi Marinaki, Petros P Sfikakis, Maria G Tektonidou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe optimal duration of glucocorticoid (GC) treatment in lupus nephritis (LN) remains unclear. We examined predictors of GC tapering and discontinuation (D/C), flares during tapering and post-D/C and long-term outcomes.MethodsWe analysed inception cohort data (1992–2021) from 136 patients with LN (median follow-up: 121 months) and applied regression models to assess predictors of successful GC D/C and long-term outcomes, including clinical/laboratory, histological and treatment characteristics.ResultsMedian time to 7.5 mg/day, 5 mg/day and GC D/C was 9, 12 and 29 months post-diagnosis, respectively. Composite complete renal response (CR) and Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI)-2K≤4, sustainedly attained until GC D/C (HR: 1.85, p=0.016), membranous LN (HR: 1.81, p=0.01) and persistent use of hydroxychloroquine (HR: 1.49, p=0.04) were associated with a shorter time to GC D/C. Patients diagnosed after 2010 achieved earlier GC D/C.Shorter time to CR (OR: 1.05, p=0.034) and Lupus Low Disease Activity State at tapering from 7.5 mg/day onwards (OR: 0.23, p=0.046) reduced flare risk during tapering. Definition Of Remission In Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (DORIS) complete remission at D/C (OR: 0.20, p=0.005) and persistent hydroxychloroquine use (OR: 0.28, p=0.031) protected against post-D/C renal flares. Neither higher initial GC dose (>40 mg/day) nor slower GC tapering prevented renal flares. Time to GC D/C (OR: 1.02/month, p=0.04) and flares (OR: 2.08, p=0.036) were associated with damage risk at the end of follow-up.ConclusionSustained CR/SLEDAI-2K≤4, membranous LN and persistent hydroxychloroquine use emerged as main predictors of shorter time to D/C, while DORIS complete remission at D/C and persistent hydroxychloroquine use predicted post-DC flares. Time to GC D/C and flares independently contributed to 10-year damage accrual.Keywords: Lupus Nephritis, Glucocorticoids, Hydroxychloroquine

Introduction

Lupus nephritis (LN) is one of the most common and severe manifestations of patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), often presenting management challenges.1,4 Glucocorticoids (GC), combined with immunosuppressive agents (IS), have long been a cornerstone of LN treatment, improving kidney and patient survival.5 Although GC are highly effective in controlling disease activity, their use is accompanied by substantial adverse effects such as infections, cardiovascular morbidity, osteoporosis, avascular bone necrosis, diabetes mellitus and anxiety.6,12 Both high daily GC dosage and prolonged exposure to smaller doses are independent predictors of the harmful effects of GC on patients with SLE.13,19 Concerns regarding GC toxicity are even greater in patients with LN with frequently relapsing disease, who require extended GC treatment.

Minimising GC exposure while maintaining remission is a major goal in LN management. The lowest effective GC dose is recommended unless a dose increase is required to manage extrarenal activity.20,22 The 202420 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) and 2024 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) guidelines recommended GC tapering to ≤5 mg/day by 6 months,20 22 aligning with the 2023 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recommendations to reduce GC to <5 mg/day as early as possible.21,20 KDIGO 2024 states that GC discontinuation (D/C) can be considered after complete renal response (CR) for at least 12 months.20 However, evidence on predictors of safe GC tapering and D/C in LN is limited, and most data have been drawn from SLE studies that include patients with LN. The timing of safe GC tapering and D/C post LN diagnosis, and predictors of pre- and after-DC flares and long-term outcomes remain unclear.

We aimed to investigate predictors of safe GC tapering, D/C achievement, time to D/C, flares during tapering and after D/C and the impact of GC exposure on long-term kidney outcomes and damage accrual.

Methods

Study population

We examined data from an inception cohort of 142 patients diagnosed with LN between 1992 and 2021 at two joint academic units (Rheumatology and Nephrology) at Laiko General Hospital of Athens. Patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) at diagnosis (n=1) or shortly after diagnosis (<6 months, n=2) and those not treated with GC (n=3, all class V) were excluded from the study. All patients met the 2019 SLE classification criteria and had biopsy-proven LN (classes III, IV, V and III/IV+V) according to the 2003 International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society classification system.23 24 All patient data were anonymised.

Data collection

Demographic, clinical (renal and extrarenal manifestations), laboratory (serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), urine sediment, 24-hour proteinuria, C3, C4 levels, anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies), histological (LN class, activity and chronicity index, glomerulosclerosis, crescents, interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy) and treatment (initial and maintenance treatment for LN, GC, persistent hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) use (online supplemental box 1) data were collected for each patient at the time of LN diagnosis and subsequently at 3, 6, 9, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60 and 72 months and at the last follow-up visit. Clinical/laboratory and treatment data were also recorded when each of the three GC dose (prednisone equivalent) goals was achieved: 7.5 mg/day, 5 mg/day and GC D/C. Follow-up was defined as the time from LN diagnosis until last follow-up visit, ESKD or death. Data on the GC D/C achievement and time to GC D/C was available for all patients.

Renal response and flares were defined according to the 2012 EULAR/European Renal Association – European Dialysis and Transplant Association guidelines and the 2012 and 2021 KDIGO recommendations.25,27 SLE activity and remission targets were assessed using the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI)-2K,28 Lupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS) and the Definition Of Remission In SLE (DORIS) criteria.29,31 The sustained attainment of LLDAS and DORIS clinical remission was also evaluated. All the above definitions are presented in the online supplemental box 1.

To evaluate the impact of both renal and extrarenal disease activity on GC tapering and D/C, we assessed a composite variable that combines CR and an SLEDAI-2K≤4, sustained until GC D/C, regardless of which was attained first (online supplemental box 1). We also assessed disease modification based on four criteria (proteinuria, eGFR, renal flares and GC dosage) as previously described.32 To explore potential differences in GC exposure between recent and past decades, patients were divided into two subgroups: those diagnosed between 1992 and 2010 (Period 1, n=66), and those between 2011 and 2021 (Period 2, n=70).

Disease damage was measured using the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics/ACR Damage Index Score (SDI).33 To further explore the impact of GC on various domains of damage accrual, SDI items were classified as certainly GC-related, probably GC-related or GC-unrelated, as previously described.16 34 To streamline analysis, the certainly and probably GC-related categories were combined into a single GC-related category (online supplemental table S1).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as medians with IQRs due to their non-normal distribution, while categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. The Mann-Whitney U test and analysis of variance were used to compare continuous variables among the groups. For categorical or dichotomised variables, the χ2 test was applied, when assumptions were met; otherwise, Fisher’s exact test was used. Logistic, Cox proportional hazard regression and Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were applied to assess the predictors of GC D/C, time to GC D/C, flares during GC tapering and after GC D/C and the association with long-term outcomes. Univariable models were initially conducted, and variables that exhibited significant associations were then included in multivariable models, using a stepwise backward elimination approach to identify independent predictors. Data were analysed using Stata V.18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA), with all tests being two-tailed and significance set at α=0.05.

Results

A total of 136 patients (83.1% women) with LN were included in the study. The baseline demographic, clinical, laboratory and histological data are presented in table 1. The median age at the time of LN diagnosis was 32.5 (IQR 19) years. The median time between SLE and LN diagnosis was 5 (IQR 8) years. 97 patients (71.3%) had proliferative LN (PLN) and 39 (28.7%) had pure membranous LN (MLN). The median follow-up period was 121 (IQR 89) months.

113 patients received GC pulses (0.5–1 g/day intravenous methylprednisolone for 3 days) at treatment initiation, followed by a median oral GC dose of 45 (IQR 10) mg/day prednisone equivalent. 79 (62.7%) patients received cyclophosphamide (CYC) as initial treatment, and 80.2% received mycophenolic acid/mycophenolate mofetil (MPA/MMF) for maintenance. Rituximab was administered to 21 patients (15.4%) (11/21 in combination with CYC, 8/21 with MPA/MMF and 2/11 with GC only), cyclosporine A in 3 (2.1%) and tacrolimus in 1 patient (0.7%). HCQ use was significantly more frequent in the recent period (2011–2021) than the earlier period (1992–2010). The trend for the increased use of HCQ was consistent both at the time of LN diagnosis (11% in 1992–2010 vs 71% in 2011–2021 (p<0.001)) and persistently up to GC D/C (18.7% in period 1 vs 74% in period 2 (p<0.001)) or the end of follow-up (18% in period 1 vs 81% in period 2 (p<0.001)).

The median time to first CR was 6 (IQR 9) months after LN diagnosis; at 12 months, 70.2% of the patients had achieved CR and 75.6% had an SLEDAI-2K≤4.

The median time from LN diagnosis to a GC dose of 7.5, 5 and 0 mg/day (GC D/C) was 9 (IQR 6), 12 (IQR 12) and 29 (IQR 22) months, respectively.

Achievement of GC≤5 mg/day (prednisone equivalent)

75 (55%) patients achieved a GC dose of ≤5 mg/day before 12 months (59/75 before month 12), 28% between months 12 and 24 and 17% after 24 months. Patients diagnosed after 2010 achieved a more rapid reduction in GC dose, with a higher proportion reaching ≤5 mg/day by month 12 (84% vs 55%, p<0.001) compared with those diagnosed before 2010.

Patients on GC doses ≤5 mg/day compared with those on >5 mg/day (n=61) at month 12 had received CYC less frequently as initial treatment (46.4% vs 82.5%, p<0.001) and had at the baseline lower SLEDAI-2K scores (median 11.5 vs 13, p=0.002), lower proteinuria levels (median 2.1 vs 3.5 g/day, p=0.013) and better kidney function (median eGFR 103 vs 95 mL/min/1.73 m², p=0.08). The median initial GC dose was comparable between the two groups (36 vs 32 mg/day, p=0.78) (table 1). Patients on doses ≤5 mg/day at month 12 achieved CR earlier after diagnosis (6 (IQR 9) vs 10 (IQR 12), p=0.001) and the majority of them had CR (82% vs 55%, p<0.001) and SLEDAI-2K≤4 (86.4% vs 60%, p=0.001) by month 12. Among patients with PLN, those with class IV LN were significantly less likely to taper GC to ≤5 mg/day at 12 months (34.5% vs 61.9%, p=0.025) than those with class III or mixed classes.

In multivariate analysis, baseline factors favouring the achievement of ≤5 mg/day GC dose at month 12 were initial treatment with MPA/MMF versus CYC (adjusted OR: 5.1, p<0.001) and lower baseline 24-hour proteinuria (adjusted OR: 0.86, p=0.031) (table 1). These factors remained significant in a multivariable model that initially included all variables with p<0.1 and was refined through a backward selection. Among the patients with PLN, the results of the multivariate model remained consistent.

We also conducted a detailed analysis on GC treatment goals (7.5 mg/day, 5 mg/day and D/C) at 24 months, examining differences in clinical and laboratory characteristics among the above GC dose groups. At 24 months, 26.5% of patients (Group 1) had discontinued GC, 56.6% (Group 2) were on ≤5 mg/day, and 16.9% (Group 3) remained on ≥7.5 mg/day (data not shown). Group 1 achieved significantly higher rates of CR, LLDAS, DORIS remission and disease modification at 12 months (all p≤0.025), despite similar baseline profiles. They were also more likely to have SLEDAI-2K≤4 at months 12 (89%) and 24 (95%) compared with Groups 2 and 3 (p=0.04), persistent HCQ use (61.1% vs 36.4% vs 30.4%, p=0.022) and having been diagnosed during Period 2 (78%, p<0.001).

GC D/C achievement and time to GC D/C

GC D/C was achieved in 117 patients (86%) at a median time of 29 (IQR 22) months post-diagnosis and 23 (IQR 19) months after renal response (CR in all but three with partial response). 36 (31.6%) patients achieved GC D/C by month 24 and 68.4% achieved GC D/C after 24 months.

Kaplan-Meier analysis showed that patients diagnosed after 2010 (Period 2) achieved GC D/C significantly earlier than those diagnosed in Period 1 (median time to D/C 24 vs 37 months, p<0.001, figure 1a). Overall, at every assessed time point, a higher proportion of patients in Period 2 had either discontinued GC or were receiving lower GC doses compared with patients of Period 1 (figure 2). No major differences were observed in histological class or initial treatment, including the use of CYC versus MPA/MMF, initial GC dose or use of intravenous GC pulses. Patients diagnosed after 2010 were slightly older (36 vs 29 years of age, p=0.007) and had higher baseline eGFR (106 vs 93 mL/min, p=0.01).

Among the 19 patients who did not achieve GC D/C by the end of follow-up, 14 had ongoing disease activity [7 experienced renal flares (2 with ≥1 flare), 7 had extrarenal manifestations (central nervous system involvement, gastrointestinal vasculitis, arthritis and rash)] and 5 were in remission, but no decision for D/C was made by the physicians. At GC D/C, the median proteinuria was 0.2 g/day, median eGFR was 105 mL/min/1.73 m² and all patients were on IS treatment. IS tapering began 10 (IQR 12) months before GC D/C in 31 patients (26.5%) and 8 (IQR 29) months after GC D/C in the remaining patients. Most patients met the criteria for LLDAS and DORIS clinical remission at GC D/C (84.6% and 81.2%, respectively), whereas 47% met the DORIS complete remission criteria. Patients with MLN achieved D/C in a shorter median time after diagnosis (25 vs 31 months for PLN, p=0.010, figure 1c).

Combined CR and SLEDAI-2K≤4, sustained until GC D/C, was achieved in 71.3% of patients with LN, who managed to discontinue GC during follow-up. In multivariate analysis, achieving sustained CR/SLEDAI-2K≤4 (HR: 1.85, p=0.016), persistent HCQ use (HR: 1.49, p=0.04) and MLN (HR: 1.81, p=0.01) were associated with a shorter time to GC D/C (figure 1b–d and table 4). In a subgroup analysis focusing on patients with PLN only, the results remained consistent with the overall cohort.

Renal flares during GC tapering

During GC tapering, 22 patients (18.8%) experienced flares, with the majority being renal flares (19/22). Patients who experienced renal flares during GC tapering were younger (26 vs 35 years, p<0.001) and had higher baseline proteinuria levels (6 vs 2.5 g/day, p<0.001). Three patients had renal flares before tapering to 7.5 mg/day, while 16 flared afterwards. Extrarenal flares were infrequent, occurring in only four patients (3.4%). Renal flares significantly delayed GC tapering compared with patients without flares (table 2). All patients with flares received protocol-based treatment and subsequently achieved remission.

Multivariate analysis revealed that shorter time to CR (OR: 1.05, p=0.034) and LLDAS at tapering (7.5 mg/day) (OR: 0.23, p=0.046) were associated with reduced risk of renal flares during GC tapering (online supplemental table S2).

Interestingly, the pattern of GC tapering did not differ significantly between patients with and without renal flares. Neither higher initial GC dose (>40 mg/day), nor slower GC tapering strategies (defined as taking more than 6 months to reduce the initial dose to 7.5 mg/day, or more than 12 or 24 months from 7.5 mg/day to D/C) were associated with flare risk. However, earlier initiation of IS tapering (before GC D/C) was more common among patients who developed flares (68.8% vs 27.8%, p=0.002).

Renal flares after GC D/C

After GC D/C, flares occurred in 29 (24.8%) of 117 patients, including renal flares in 22 (18.8%) patients and extrarenal flares in 7 (6%) patients. Among the renal flares, 18 occurred post-D/C and 4 occurred both pre-D/C and post-D/C. Among the extrarenal flares, 3/7 occurred both pre-D/C and post-D/C. Remission was re-achieved following treatment in all cases.

Renal flares occurred at a median of 25 months (IQR 29) post-GC D/C. Of these, 6 flares occurred during IS treatment, 6 during IS tapering and 10 post-IS D/C. No significant differences were found in the time taken to reach GC tapering goals between patients with and without renal flares (table 2). Baseline and 12-month characteristics were similar across these groups. Higher initial oral GC doses and slower tapering strategies did not reduce the risk of post-D/C flares. Furthermore, achieving LLDAS or DORIS clinical remission at month 12 post-LN diagnosis or maintaining these remission targets did not appear to influence flare risk after GC D/C. In contrast, achieving DORIS complete remission at the time of GC D/C was strongly protective against post-GC D/C flares; this state was more frequently observed in patients without flares compared with those with flares (55.8% vs 18.2%, p=0.018). Persistent HCQ use until GC D/C was also significantly more common in flare-free patients compared with those with flares (51.6% vs 22.7%, p=0.014) (table 5).

We developed a multivariable logistic regression model to identify predictors of renal flares following GC D/C. Persistent HCQ use up to GC D/C (OR: 0.28, p=0.031) and achievement of DORIS complete remission at the time of GC D/C (OR: 0.20, p=0.005) independently reduced the risk of flares.

To further assess the overall risk of disease flares following GC tapering, whether occurring during the tapering period or after GC D/C, we also conducted an analysis on all flares (renal and extrarenal) post GC tapering (from 7.5 mg/day prednisone equivalent onwards). Persistent HCQ use (OR: 0.33, p=0.005), shorter time from diagnosis to 7.5 mg/day (OR: 1.06 per month, p=0.017) and LLDAS at tapering (7.5 mg/day) (OR: 0.38, p=0.018) significantly protected against flares.

Long-term outcomes (damage accrual and progression to ESKD or death)

Finally, to explore the impact of GC tapering and D/C strategies on long-term outcomes, we examined their association with damage accrual and progression to ESKD or death.

By the end of the follow-up, patients who achieved GC D/C exhibited lower SDI score compared with those who did not (median: 0 vs 1, p=0.03). Among patients who discontinued GC, those with no damage (SDI=0) had a shorter median GC treatment duration than those with damage (SDI≥1) (27.5 months vs 31 months, p=0.005), while tapering to ≤5 mg/day by month 12 was more common in the no-damage group (71.6% vs 31%, p<0.001). Patients without damage also showed higher rates of persistent HCQ use until GC D/C (54.1% vs 32.6%, p=0.025) and up to the end of the follow-up (56.8% vs 41.8%, p=0.08) (data not shown). Notably, achieving GC D/C within 24 months was protective against damage accrual at the end of follow-up (75% vs 25%, p<0.001). Additionally, sustained LLDAS and sustained DORIS clinical remission were significantly more frequent in the no-damage group (82.4% vs 62.8%, p=0.018 and 74.3% vs 48.8%, p=0.005, respectively). Renal flares occurred less frequently in the no-damage group (24.7% vs 43.6%, p=0.020), and GC tapering to 7.5 mg/day within 6 months was more common (44.4% vs 18.5%, p=0.002). Additionally, a higher proportion of patients without damage were diagnosed during the more recent period (2011–2021) (66% vs 34%, p=0.002).

In the multivariate analysis, we examined the effect of GC D/C achievement on long-term damage accrual. In the first model, achieving GC D/C was independently associated with a significant reduced risk of damage (OR: 0.23, p=0.012), even after adjusting for several clinical confounders (table 6). Subsequent analysis (model 2) showed that patients with a GC dose ≤5 mg/day at 12 months exhibited a significantly lower risk of long-term damage (OR: 0.18, p<0.001). Model 3 compared tapering to ≤5 mg/day at 12 months to GC D/C ever during follow-up; the former was associated with a significant risk reduction (OR: 0.20, p<0.001), while the latter did not (OR: 0.42, p=0.10). Finally, model 4 examined the time required to achieve GC D/C and showed an increased risk (OR: 1.02) per each additional month on GC (p=0.04) (table 6).

The occurrence of renal flares during follow-up and older age at LN diagnosis were consistently associated with an increased risk of damage accrual across multivariate models. Conversely, persistent HCQ use was associated with a significantly lower risk of damage, particularly in patients who ultimately achieved GC D/C. We also found that tapering to ≤5 mg/day by month 12 was associated with a substantially lower burden of GC-related (33.3% vs 66.7%) and overall damage (18.2% vs 81.8%) compared with those who remained on >5 mg/day (p<0.001) (online supplemental table S3).

No significant associations were observed between GC D/C or GC treatment duration and progression to ESKD (n=13) or death (n=11, of those with ESKD), though low event rates preclude conclusions.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first inception cohort study that examines predictors of GC tapering and D/C, flares during tapering and post D/C and long-term outcomes and assesses the impact of sustained achievement of both renal response and overall low disease activity status. We showed the importance of achieving renal and overall disease remission and maintaining persistent HCQ use for a successful and earlier GC D/C and pre-D/C and post-D/C flares prevention. We also observed that shorter time to GC D/C and absence of incident renal flares were associated with reduced damage accrual.

In this cohort, 86% of patients with LN achieved GC D/C at a median of 23 months post renal response. Patients with MLN achieved GC D/C significantly earlier than patients with PLN (25 vs 31 months, p=0.010), despite similar overall D/C rates between those two groups (86.6% vs 84.6%, p=0.763). This finding supports earlier GC D/C in patients with MLN, consistent with our previous findings showing similar percentages of IS D/C among patients with MLN and PLN, but significantly earlier in patients with MLN.35 Among patients with PLN, class IV versus class III was associated with lower likelihood to achieve GC D/C, further emphasising management challenges related to histological lesions of poor prognosis.4 The sustained CR/SLEDAI-2K≤4, a combination of CR and SLEDAI≤4 was a major predictor of earlier GC D/C, suggesting that both renal response and overall disease control play a significant role in facilitating GC withdrawal in LN. Patients achieving sustained CR/SLEDAI-2K≤4 until GC D/C were more likely to achieve GC D/C earlier and DORIS complete remission at GC D/C provided protection against post-D/C renal flares. These findings align with recent guidelines that recommend remission-based approaches to guide treatment de-escalation.36 37 The importance of integrating these remission targets in LN management was also demonstrated in our recent study on IS D/C in patients with LN, in which we found that initiating IS tapering while in DORIS complete remission significantly reduced the incidence of renal and extrarenal flares during tapering.35

In our cohort, 18.8% of patients with LN experienced flares (primarily renal) during GC tapering and 24.8% developed flares post-GC D/C. A recent study of patients with SLE showed that maintaining a daily GC dose of 5 mg/day may be safer than complete GC D/C;38 however, the abrupt GC withdrawal in that study has been criticised. A meta-analysis of 17 studies involving 738 patients with SLE (58% with LN) reported a 24% global flare incidence post-GC D/C, and a 38% higher risk compared with those continuing GC therapy.39 In the present study, earlier achievement of CR prevented against renal flares during tapering. In previous studies of our group, earlier CR achievement was associated with reduced risk of renal flares during a long-term follow-up and during IS tapering.35 40 We also found that meeting LLDAS criteria when on 7.5 mg/day prednisone equivalent protected against renal flares during tapering, in accordance with Tani et al37 GC pulses in addition to initial treatment did not impact the risk of flares. None of the patients received a standardised protocol of monthly GC pulses (eg, 6 monthly pulses of 125–250 mg methylprednisolone) as add-on treatment to standard of care, as previously described.41 Interestingly, we observed that neither higher initial GC doses (>40 mg/day) nor slower tapering strategies prevented the incidence of flares, supporting earlier tapering efforts.

There is growing evidence supporting an earlier GC tapering in LN mostly via fixed-rate reduction of GCs,20 22 not only for reducing damage, but also for effectively controlling the disease.42 43 Recent multicentre studies have shown that tapering GCs to ≤5–7.5 mg/day within 6–12 months does not compromise renal outcomes or increase the risk of flares compared with slower tapering methods.41 44 The inception Lupus Cruces-Bordeaux cohort study demonstrated that patients receiving 125–250 mg methylprednisolone pulses along with intravenous CYC every 2 weeks, and a fixed reduction of oral GC to 5 mg/day by week 12–14, achieved a significantly earlier renal response (median time to CR: 21 vs 48 weeks, p<0.001), higher overall CR rates (96% vs 74%, p=0.002) and lower risk of GC toxicity and damage accrual compared with standard of care. The analysis was conducted using propensity score analysis, accounting for LN class, baseline kidney function and multiple demographic, clinical and treatment characteristics.41 A multicentre study of 344 patients with PLN from Japan has shown comparable rates of partial response at 12 months [relative risk (RR): 0.92, p=0.758, CR at 12 months (RR: 1.17, p=0.476) and relapse rates over 2 years between patients who tapered GC dose to ≤7.5 mg/day within 6 months and the slower tapering group, adjusting for age, baseline proteinuria levels, eGFR, initial GC dose, methylprednisolone pulses and type of immunosuppressants (mycophenolate mofetil, cyclophosphamide or rituximab).44 Notably, achievement of GC dose ≤5 mg/day at month 12 in our study was independent of prognostic factors of worse outcomes at baseline, such as low eGFR, proliferative LN class and the presence of high activity and chronicity index, crescents and interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy in kidney biopsies (table 1). In contrast, tapering on-demand typically results in greater cumulative exposure to GC, leading to a higher risk of damage. Early low-dose GC achievement may offer greater benefits (lower risk of flare and damage accrual) than delayed D/C when patients are already on low doses.45 46 In our cohort, earlier GC tapering to ≤5 mg and earlier GC D/C were associated with significantly lower SDI scores and GC-related and overall damage, compared with those with prolonged GC exposure. These findings, in line with emerging literature, underscore the importance of timely GC reduction strategies in LN management, not only for damage prevention, but also for maintaining efficacy in disease control and remission induction.

Persistent use of HCQ was independently associated with a shorter time to achieve GC D/C among patients who achieved D/C. In addition, HCQ use was associated with a lower risk of renal and extrarenal flares following tapering to 7.5 mg/day onwards and of renal flares after GC D/C, ultimately contributing to reduced damage accrual at the end of follow-up. These findings align with prior studies examining IS D/C in LN,35 47 48 which demonstrated the protective effect of HCQ against flares following IS withdrawal. Moreover, a recent randomised trial showed a protective role of HCQ in preventing disease relapse after GC withdrawal in patients with sustained clinically inactive SLE.49 Interestingly, our findings highlight a significant temporal shift toward more consistent use of HCQ in LN management over the past decades. The marked increase in HCQ initiation at diagnosis and sustained use throughout follow-up likely reflects evolving treatment paradigms and may partially explain the improved outcomes in contemporary cohorts.

Another interesting observation in our study was that patients diagnosed after 2010 were more likely to achieve lower GC doses earlier during their follow-up and had lower SDI scores at the end of follow-up, reflecting advancements in LN management over recent decades. This finding aligns with previous research from our group on disease modification in LN, which demonstrated higher achievement of its criteria in recent years, attributed to earlier GC tapering and D/C.32 Half of included patients were diagnosed before 2010, prior to formal recommendations on GC tapering, most of which emerged after 2019.20,22 The introduction of biologic therapies likely contributed to more confident and earlier GC withdrawal in recent years. Notably, many patients remained on low-dose GC (5 mg/day) for extended periods (median 18 months) before eventual D/C, possibly reflecting a cautious approach by clinicians due to concerns about relapse risk and a lack of clear tapering strategies in earlier years.

Lastly, it would be of interest to investigate the presence of thrombotic microangiopathy or antiphospholipid syndrome nephropathy lesions and their potential association with renal outcomes. However, this would require a comprehensive re-evaluation of all renal biopsies, aligned with recent international efforts to refine the histopathological classification of antiphospholipid syndrome nephropathy.50 Furthermore, the lack of a significant association between the baseline histological activity index and outcomes in our cohort likely reflects the uniform histological disease severity at diagnosis and consistent use of standardised treatment protocols. These elements may have diminished the prognostic value of initial histological findings seen in more diverse or less intensively treated populations.51 Importantly, emerging data have shown that the activity index correlates with renal outcomes when evaluated in repeat rather than in the initial biopsies,52 and our study assessed the activity index only in the initial biopsy. Achieving a sustained CR/SLEDAI-2K≤4 emerged as a significant predictor of GC D/C, while overall treatment duration and the occurrence of flares were key risk factors for long-term damage accrual. These findings emphasise the importance of disease control over time, rather than baseline histological activity, in determining long-term outcomes.

This study has several strengths: (1) the inclusion of an inception cohort across two academic centres with a common standardised approach and detailed follow-up information data, (2) an extensive follow-up period of a median of 10 years, (3) the assessment of multiple primary outcomes, for example, flares during both GC tapering and post-GC D/C, and several long-term outcomes and (4) the assessment of the prognostic role of a composite variable of renal and extrarenal disease activity (CR/SLEDAI-2K≤4), and its sustained attainment, as well as of LLDAS and DORIS targets. Limitations to consider include the retrospective design and the absence of per-protocol biopsies before GC D/C, which could offer further insights into the histopathological and not only the clinical disease remission.53 54 Limited use of biologics, for example, B-cell depletion and belimumab in this cohort (Period 2 was between 2005 and 2021) limits the assessment of potential associations with a more rapid tapering of GC and better outcomes. Low frequency of HCQ use in earlier decades (1992–2005) should also be taken into consideration, although it reflects real-life data, before the protective effect of HCQ in LN outcomes was established. Furthermore, HCQ adherence was evaluated based on self-report questionnaires and electronic prescription refill monitoring, without using additional measures such as pill counts or HCQ blood levels. Finally, the predominance of white European participants and the public health context with free medical care may limit the generalisability of the findings to other ethnic and healthcare settings.

In conclusion, our findings highlight that achievement of low-dose GC targets, sustained renal and overall disease remission (CR/SLEDAI-2K≤4, LLDAS, DORIS complete remission) and persistent HCQ use are critical components of effective GC D/C strategies, aimed at reducing long-term damage accrual while minimising flare risk during and after GC D/C. Earlier treatment withdrawal in MLN appears feasible and may help reduce the risks of prolonged GC exposure in this group. Future studies should explore whether novel biological therapies20,22 can facilitate earlier tapering strategies while maintaining disease remission across diverse patient populations. Maintenance of both renal response and overall low disease activity status may serve as future targets in LN clinical trials and clinical practice.
