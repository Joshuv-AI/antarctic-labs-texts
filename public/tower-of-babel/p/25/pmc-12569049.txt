Incidence and factor for acute acalculous cholecystitis during prolonged gastrointestinal disuse in external duodenal fistula
Authors: Weiwei Shang, Wuhan Li, Xin Xu, Risheng Zhao, Zheng Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To investigate the incidence, risk factors, and outcomes of acute acalculous cholecystitis (AAC) following prolonged gastrointestinal disuse due to external duodenal fistula. This retrospective cohort study included adult patients with external duodenal fistula who experienced gastrointestinal tract disuse from January 2018 to January 2024. Patients were monitored until discharge or death. The primary outcome was the occurrence of AAC, while secondary outcomes included its treatment and prognosis. Of the enrolled 133 patients, 59 (44.4%) were diagnosed with AAC. The majority (79.7%) of AAC cases occurred during the reintroduction of enteral nutrition (EN). High SOFA score upon admission (Odds Ratio [OR] = 1.59, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 1.13–2.70, p = 0.005) and the vasopressor use upon admission (OR = 4.53, 95% CI: 1.09–19.22, p = 0.04) were associated with increased risk of AAC before EN reintroduction. For AAC occurring after EN reintroduction, the duration of intestinal disuse (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.00-1.13, p = 0.04) was the sole risk factor. The incidence of AAC is notably high in patients undergoing prolonged gastrointestinal tract disuse, particularly after the reintroduction of Enteral Nutrition (EN) in patients with external duodenal fistula.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-15321-0.

Introduction

Acute acalculous cholecystitis (AAC) is a severe gallbladder inflammation affecting 10–15% of patients with acute cholecystitis who lack gallstones1. This condition predominantly affects critically ill individuals and presents diagnose challenges due to its nonspecific presentation1. Mortality rates rates range from 10 to 50%, influenced by the patient’s overall health and the timeliness of medical intervention2. The pathogenesis involves bile stasis, alterations in bile composition, ischemia, and systemic inflammation3. Prolonged gastrointestinal tract disuse promotes bile stasis and sludge formation4–6, potentially leading to gallbladder ischemia and necrosis3.

Managing external duodenal fistula often necessitates fasting and enteral nutrition (EN) via a jejunal tube to prevent abdominal contamination from chyme7. However, reintroducing EN requires careful consideration to avoid exacerbating abdominal infections8. Effective source control, a challenging surgical endeavor, is crucial before safely reinstating EN, often involving puncture, laparotomy, or open abdomen techniques9. This study retrospectively investigates the incidence, risk factors, and outcomes of AAC in patients with external duodenal fistula subjected to prolonged gastrointestinal tract disuse.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in a regional center and approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangning Hospital. We included adult patients referred from external hospitals for external duodenal fistula treatment, requiring gastrointestinal tract disuse from January 2018 to January 2024. Exclusion criteria comprised: diagnosed of cholecystitis or gallstones at admission, previous cholecystectomy, stable EN use before admission, severe acute pancreatitis or other intestinal fistulas, or incomplete data. Follow-up continued until discharge or cessation of care. The primary outcome was AAC occurrence, with secondary outcomes focusing on its treatment and prognosis.

Prolonged disuse of the gastrointestinal tract

There are no established criteria for prolonged gastrointestinal tract disuse. According to previous studies10–12, gallbladder sludge formation begins to form after three weeks of disuse, with significant sludge present in over half of patients by four weeks. Based on these findings, we defined prolonged disuse as more than three weeks.

Reintroducting EN

The treatment followed the SOWATS strategy13, including: sepsis control, optimization of nutritional status, wound care, determination of fistula anatomy, timing of surgery, and formulation of a surgical strategy. Upon admission, sepsis control through puncture and laparotomy was prioritized. Patients fasted throughout the treatment, receiving parenteral nutrition (PN) at 30 kcal/kg/day when EN was not feasible. EN (Peptison Liquid, 1 kcal/ml, Wuxi, China) was administered via nasointestinal tube (NIT) after achieving sufficient drainage and infection control. EN commenced at 20 ml/h on the first day, increasing by 10 ml/h/day until total EN was achieved. Somatostatin was used for patients with high-output fistulas.

Diagnosis and treatment of AAC

When a patient presents with unexplained emesis, SIRS(Systemic inflammatory response syndrome), abdominal pain, bloating, Murphy’s sign, and other clinical manifestations, clinicians should consider the potential occurrence of ACC. AAC was diagnosed using CT, indicated by a distended gallbladder and wall thickness > 3 mm2. Before EN reintroduction, due to uncontrolled infection, CT scans were performed weekly. All AAC patients underwent CT-guided percutaneous cholecystostomy during the routine CT examination process. The drainage tube remained in place until the intestinal fistula resolved, and the patient was able to self-feed. If AAC occurred after EN reintroduction, unexpected symptoms prompted a CT scan. If AAC was diagnosed, EN was suspended, and metronidazole and cefoperazone were administered. Persistent symptoms after 48 h warranted ultrasound or CT-guided percutaneous cholecystostomy.

Data collection, outcomes, and statistical analysis

CT scans at admission assessed abdominal infection. Demographic data, comorbidities, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores, and fistula characteristics were recorded, including pre-transfer medical data. High-output fistulas were defined as ≥ 500 ml/day14. Blood tests were conducted every three days post-admission. For AAC occurring after EN reintroduction, the duration of intestinal disuse included the interval from fistula occurrence to admission and from admission to EN reintroduction. For AAC before EN, it included the interval from fistula occurrence to admission and from admission to AAC occurrence. Antibiotics were guided by fluid cultures, categorized following the World Health Organization (WHO) AWaRe Antibiotic Book15. Hyperglycemia was defined as persistent blood sugar > 10 mmol/L requiring insulin16. Statistical analyses included Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). In addition, multivariate logistic regression and cox regression were used to assess the potential factors based on univariate p-values. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Population and characteristics

A total of 169 eligible adult patients were reviewed for this study. Of these, 36 patients were excluded for the following reasons: 6 patients had gallbladder stones upon admission, 5 had cholecystectomy before admission, 7 had stable use of EN before admission without abdominal infection and were admitted for nutrition support optimization due to high output, 12 had pancreatitis or other intestinal fistulas confounding the diagnosis of cholecystitis, and 6 had incomplete data. Thus, 133 patients were included in the analysis (median age 48 years [interquartile range, IQR: 41–56years]; 77 [57.9%] males). The clinical characteristics are detailed in Table 1. The etiology included: Trauma(n = 80), Upper gastrointestinal tract tumor(39), pancreatitis (n = 8), right hemicolectomy(n = 4), and nephrectomy(n = 2). Among the 133 patients, 59 (44.4%) were diagnosed with AAC. Notably, 79.7% (n = 47) of AAC cases occurred on the 4th day (IQR: 3–6 days) after reintroducting EN. Compared to these 47 patients, the remaining 12 AAC cases occurred before reintroducting EN and presented with higher rates of vasopressor use, higher SOFA scores, and elevated bilirubin levels upon admission (Table 2).

Symptoms and CT scan of AAC

All AAC patients in our study survived. The symptoms of AAC are presented in Table 3. Symptoms in the 12 cases of AAC that occurred before reintroducting EN were not analyzed due to the presence of other abdominal infections, which could have confounded the symptoms. Among the 47 patients who developed AAC after reintroducting EN following source control, the most common symptom was fever (n = 38), followed by upper abdominal pain (n = 33) and emesis (n = 21). Murphy’s sign was not prominently detected in the majority of patients (n = 8). Symptoms resolved, and gallbladder puncture was avoided in 7 (14.9%) of the 47 patients. In the remaining 40 patients who underwent gallbladder puncture, fever resolved within 1 day(IQR: 1–2 days), and white blood cell (WBC) counts normalized to normal after 2 days (IQR: 2–3 days) post-intervention.

Characteristics of patients when diagnosed with AAC.

All suspected patients (n = 59) underwent CT scans to confirm the diagnosis. CT scans revealed a full gallbladder in most cases (n = 54) with a gallbladder size of 58 mL (IQR: 48–64 mL). Gallbladder wall thickening was observed in 39 (66.1%) cases. Among the 52 patients who underwent gallbladder puncture (40 after reintroducting EN and 12 before reintroducting EN), bile cultures identified Escherichia coli (n = 23) as the primary pathogen, followed by Klebsiella (n = 22).

Risk factors for AAC before reintroducting EN

Thirty-five characteristics were examined to identify factors associated with AAC in the overall 133 patients. Three of these characteristics were further analyzed through multivariate analysis (Table 4). Discussion

The present study found that the incidence of AAC was as high as 44.4% following long-term disuse of the gastrointestinal tract in patients with external duodenal fistula. Additionally, most (79.7%) AAC cases occurred during the period of reintroducting EN. Factors associated with AAC before reintroducting EN included higher SOFA scores and the vasopressor use on admission. After EN reintroduction began, the duration of gastrointestinal tract disuse and bilirubin levels on the day of reintroducting EN were risk factors for AAC occurrence.

AAC is a severe inflammatory condition of the gallbladder, distinct from calculous cholecystitis by its lack of gallstones4–6,17. In critically ill patients, the incidence of AAC ranges from 0.5 to 18%5, and its gradual onset can be masked by more prominent symptoms of the underlying illness. The pathophysiology of AAC is complex, involving gallbladder ischemia, bile stasis, and systemic inflammatory responses18,19. Critical illness often leads to abnormal hemodynamics, resulting in decreased perfusion and oxygen delivery to organs, including the gallbladder. Factors such as systemic hypoperfusion, shock states, and the use of vasopressors further compromise gallbladder circulation, creating conditions for ischemia and subsequent inflammation and infection.

Gastrointestinal disuse suppresses normal gallbladder contraction and emptying, leading to bile retention and bile stasis13. Prolonged bile stasis not only exacerbates the inflammatory response in the gallbladder but also creates favorable conditions for bacterial infection, potentially triggering acute cholecystitis20. Under normal circumstances, gastrointestinal motility and feeding stimuli promote gallbladder contraction and bile release. However, in cases of gastrointestinal disuse, the lack of feeding and intestinal activity leads to prolonged gallbladder inactivity. This results in bile accumulation, increased concentration and viscosity of the bile, and further promotion of mucosal inflammation and infection in the gallbladder21.

Our research revealed that patients with AAC before the resumption of EN exhibited higher SOFA scores and greater dependence on vasopressive agents at admission, indicating a more compromised general health status and suboptimal perfusion to vital organs. During prolonged gastrointestinal tract disuse, these factors may potentiate ischemia severity, contributing to AAC development.

A novel finding in this study is that reintroducing EN after extended gastrointestinal tract disuse represents a critical phase for AAC development. Fasting prolongs bile stasis4, increasing bile concentration and viscosity19, both significant risk factors for gallbladder inflammation. When enteral feeding resumes, the gallbladder contracts to release bile22. However, if this contractile response is insufficient to counteract prolonged stasis, it can increase the risk of AAC. Furthermore, during disuse, the gallbladder experiences reduced blood supply23. When refeeding begins, the surge in metabolic demand increases blood flow, potentially causing reperfusion injury characterized by oxidative stress24 and intense inflammatory responses24, which can initiate or aggravate gallbladder inflammation. Additionally, critically ill patients often experience SIRS, which can contribute to AAC. In this systemic response, cytokines and other inflammatory mediators can damage the gallbladder’s endothelium, further promoting ischemia and inflammation.

This study has limitations. First, potential biases may arise from its retrospective design and relatively small sample size. As a result, some variables exhibit exaggerated upper or lower 95% confidence intervals for their odds ratio (OR) values, leading to very broad confidence intervals (e.g., Pars horizontalis duodeni, high output, etiology, methods of surgical infection control, vasopressor use, and chronic hepatitis). This variability contributes to the ambiguity and unreliability of the results, highlighting the need for future research with a larger sample size. However, given the rarity of AAC cases due to external duodenal fistula, the number of patients enrolled may be adequate. Second, all patients with pre-EN AAC underwent puncture and drainage, while some patients with post-EN AAC did not require puncture, possibly affecting outcome data. Nonetheless, the incidence of pre-EN AAC was low, leading to insignificant bias. Third, in our study, somatostatin was administered exclusively to patients with high-output fistulas, resulting in a significant overlap between somatostatin use and high-output fistula status. Somatostatin can indeed reduce blood flow to the digestive tract, and high-output fistulas might affect the hepatobiliary-enteric circulation of bilirubin before the initiation of EN and simultaneous resumption of EN and chyme reinfusion, potentially leading to bile stasis—another risk factor for AAC. However, our multivariate analysis did not identify high-output fistula as a significant risk factor for post-EN AAC occurrence. Therefore, due to the high collinearity between high-output fistulas and somatostatin use, and the lack of association between high-output fistulas and AAC, we did not include somatostatin use as a separate variable in the final analysis. Fourth, similar to other studies, our research cannot provide a specific pathophysiological explanation for AAC. Conclusion

The incidence of AAC is notably high in patients undergoing prolonged gastrointestinal tract disuse, particularly after the reintroduction of Enteral Nutrition (EN) in patients with external duodenal fistula. The majority of AAC cases occur within the first few days of EN reintroduction, with the duration of intestinal disuse identified as a significant risk factor.
