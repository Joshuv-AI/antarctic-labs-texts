Photosensitive small intestinal submucosal hydrogels loaded with the KR-12-a5 peptide promote periodontal osteogenesis and antimicrobial activity
Authors: Shiqing Ma, Qiquan Yan, Le Li, Yazhuo Ni, Keying Chen, Bo Xu, Yaoyang Li, Mengzhen Cui, Fuli Liu, Kexin Chang, Bo Kou, Rui Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodontitis, a chronic inflammatory condition, causes progressive loss of oral soft and hard tissues. Current treatments struggle to balance antimicrobial efficacy with bone regeneration. This study aimed to develop a photoresponsive hydrogel using porcine small intestinal submucosa (SIS) crosslinked with itaconic anhydride (IA) and incorporating the KR-12-a5 peptide to enhance its antibacterial and osteogenic capabilities.ResultsCrosslinking SIS with IA significantly improved the hydrogel’s mechanical and biological properties. Adding KR-12-a5 enhanced biocompatibility, promoted cell proliferation and migration, and demonstrated strong antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Porphyromonas gingivalis, and Streptococcus hemolyticus. The hydrogel boosted mineralization capacity and expression of osteogenic markers (ALP, BMP-2, OPN, RUNX-2) in bone marrow stromal cells in vitro. In vivo, the IA/SIS/KR-12-a5 hydrogel promoted autologous bone formation at defect margins and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, outperforming SIS and IA/SIS groups in both osteogenic and anti-inflammatory effects.ConclusionThe IA/SIS/KR-12-a5 hydrogel exhibited rapid UV-induced gelation, strong mechanical properties, antibacterial efficacy, and significant osteogenic potential. These attributes suggest its suitability as an injectable biomaterial for bone regeneration and repair.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03601-w.Keywords: Periodontitis, Hydrogel, Photosensitive material, Osteogenesis, Antibacterial

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease driven by bacterial infection, marked by the progressive destruction of periodontal tissues, alveolar bone resorption, and the formation of periodontal pockets that significantly undermine tooth stability and function [1–3]. While conventional therapies, such as periodontal scaling and localized antimicrobial treatments, are effective in managing infection, they typically fall short in achieving adequate alveolar bone regeneration [4, 5]. This limitation is primarily attributed to the intricate morphology of periodontal pockets and the hostile local microenvironment, both of which hinder the performance of bone regeneration materials. Moreover, traditional bone regeneration materials, such as the widely used membrane-bone graft combination, lack adequate antibacterial properties, rendering them unsuitable for clinical scenarios with high infection risks and severe bone resorption. In this context, an ideal therapeutic material should exhibit excellent biocompatibility and morphological adaptability to fill complex periodontal bone defects effectively, while simultaneously regulating cellular behavior to promote functional bone tissue regeneration at the tissue levell [6, 7]. Hydrogels, with their three-dimensional network structure, high water content, and capacity to act as bioactive carriers, have emerged as promising candidates. Their ability to conform to the irregular morphology of periodontal defects underscores their potential for clinical application [8, 9]. They can adapt to the irregular morphology of periodontal defects, offering substantial potential for clinical application.

Small intestinal submucosa (SIS) hydrogels, a naturally derived biomimetic matrix, consist primarily of type I collagen, minor amounts of type III collagen, and bioactive factors [10, 11].Quantitative analysis revealed that the decellularized SIS contained 28.09 ± 0.67% type I collagen and 59.5 ± 1.42% type III collagen [12]. Decellularized SIS exhibits excellent biocompatibility and non-immunogenicity, with substantial potential to promote cellular proliferation and osteogenesis. The porous microstructure of SIS facilitates osteoblast adhesion and proliferation, while its ability to induce tissue repair and regeneration within defect regions is well-documented [13, 14]. Degradation of SIS collagen enhances the expression of bone-related factors such as BMP-2 and RUNX-2, activating the BMP/SMAD signaling pathway to promote osteogenesis, bone differentiation, and vascularization. However, as an osteogenic material for periodontal bone defects, SIS is inherently limited under the mechanically dynamic oral environment [15]. Its reliance on van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonds for collagen self-assembly results in a slow gelation process and weak intermolecular interactions, making it prone to collapse under mechanical stress and compromising its functional stability [16, 17].Thus, it is important to explore strategies to improve the gelation rate and mechanical strength of SIS hydrogels to better ensure their mechanical stability after implantation [18].

Compared to other collagen modification approaches, photo-crosslinking offers a simpler and more efficient method for preparing SIS photosensitive hydrogels [16]. While methacrylate-based collagen modification techniques have been widely used for hydrogel preparation, they are associated with irritancy, allergenicity, and cytotoxicity [19–21]. As an endogenous antimicrobial metabolite and immunomodulator, itaconic acid derivatives demonstrate superior biocompatibility, controlled degradability, and anti-inflammatory properties. IA’s dual reactivity (anhydride/C = C bonds) enables efficient photo-polymerization and post-functionalization while degrading into biocompatible itaconic acid [22]. Unlike genipin’s slow kinetics (hours-days) or glutaraldehyde’s uncontrolled crosslinking, IA permits precise mechanical modulation via rapid UV-triggered gelation [23]. As a bio-based alternative to petroleum-derived MA [24], IA uniquely resolves the critical challenge of simultaneously optimizing cell viability and mechanical performance in crosslinked biomaterials [25]. Additionally, IA-modified networks enable sustained drug release while providing inherent antimicrobial activity [26–29]. Under UV irradiation, itaconic acid derivatives undergo photolytic reactions generating acidic intermediates that catalyze photosensitive material curing [30]. The anhydride groups directly react with collagen amino groups to form stable amide bonds, establishing the chemical basis for photo-crosslinking. Using cytocompatible photoinitiators, UV exposure generates radicals that initiate C = C polymerization in IA-modified collagen, forming a mechanically stable 3D network [28]. This dual-crosslinking mechanism not only achieves rapid gelation but also enhances hydrogel stability. Moreover, precise control of crosslinking density enables concurrent regulation of hydrogel degradation kinetics and drug release profiles.

In contrast to antibiotic-based antimicrobial strategies, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have emerged as promising alternatives, owing to their broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, immunomodulatory properties, and capacity to promote tissue repair [31]. Among these, KR-12-a5, a short peptide derived from the antimicrobial peptide LL-37, exhibits potent antibacterial activity and greater ease of synthesis compared to its parent peptide [32–35]. KR-12-a5 has demonstrated efficacy against Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Porphyromonas gingivalis [36, 37]. In periodontitis-associated environments, KR-12-a5 enhances the antioxidant capacity of osteoblasts, alleviating oxidative stress-induced cellular damage [35]. Furthermore, KR-12-a5 promotes osteogenesis by binding to cell surface receptors and activating endogenous signaling pathways, including the BMP/Smad、P38 MAPK pathways, to stimulate osteoblast proliferation, migration, and extracellular matrix remodeling [22, 38–40]. It also regulates osteoblast apoptosis, prolonging cellular survival and promoting new bone formation [22, 33, 41–44]. This multifunctional peptide addresses both bacterial reduction in periodontal pockets and bone regeneration, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for periodontitis.

In this study, we aim to develop a photosensitive hydrogel with enhanced mechanical strength and morphological adaptability using photo-crosslinking technology. A novel antimicrobial system, combining KR-12-a5 osteogenic antimicrobial peptide and a photosensitizer, is designed to suit the structural complexities of periodontal pockets. By introducing itaconic anhydride-modified SIS collagen with carbon-carbon double bonds, we aim to enhance the structural stability and rapid gelation capability of SIS hydrogels, providing a novel material and strategy for the treatment of periodontitis.

Preparation of the SIS hydrogel

Fresh porcine small intestines were dissected to remove the extracellular matrix using mechanical methods, followed by degreasing in absolute ethanol at 4 °C for 72 h. The tissues were then digested in a 0.5% trypsin/EDTA solution (prepared with ddH₂O) at 37 °C for 48 h. After paraffin embedding, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was conducted to evaluate decellularization efficiency(Supplemental Figure S1).The decellularized SIS membrane was subjected to DNA quantification, demonstrating a residual DNA content of 8.3 ng/mg, meeting the established biosafety criteria. The processed SIS membrane has been confirmed to be completely decellularized with DNA content meeting all safety standards. A 1% SDS solution was washed and crushed, and pepsin was added at 100:1 for 48 h, after which the filtrate was filtered. After salting, the white precipitate was centrifuged with 1% acetic acid, and the above mixture was placed in a dialysis bag (18–44 kDa) and dialyzed against 0.5% acetic acid solution for 72 h at room temperature. The dialysate was changed every 4 h. Afterwards, the dialysate was placed in a stainless steel plate, frozen at -20 °C for 24 h, and then lyophilized in a freeze-dryer (FD-10, China) to obtain a lyophilized SIS sponge (Fig. 1a).

Itacononic anhydride-grafted SIS

A total of 0.4 g of frozen-dried SIS powder was dissolved in 20 mL of 1% acetic acid buffer solution. Dissolve 1–5 times the mass of freeze-dried SIS powder in itaconic anhydride, then add the solution to 5 mL of acetone. Mix the resulting suspension with the SIS powder, and stir at 4℃ for 24 h. The mixture was then dialyzed in PBS via dialysis bags with a molecular weight cutoff of 8–14 kDa for 72 h, followed by freeze-drying to obtain photo-crosslinkable collagen(Fig. 2a). The mass ratios of freeze-dried SIS hydrogel to itaconic anhydride were set at 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, 1:4, and 1:5, and the prepared samples were labeled A, B, C, D, and E, respectively.

To prepare the photosensitive hydrogel

1 g of freeze-dried, itaconic anhydride-crosslinked SIS hydrogel was weighed and dissolved in a 0.5% acetic acid solution. Once fully dissolved, the pH was adjusted to neutral pH with 5 mol/L NaOH. The resulting photo-crosslinkable collagen should have a concentration of 20 mg/mL. A 0.2% Irgacure 2959 photoinitiator was added, and the mixture was mixed thoroughly to ensure complete dissolution. The mixture was then centrifuged to remove air bubbles, yielding a prepolymer solution. Two milliliters of the prepolymer mixture for each sample was transferred to a 24-well plate. The samples were exposed to UV light (6 W, 365 nm) for 3 min to obtain photo-crosslinked hydrogels.

Polypeptide KR-12-a5 loaded into photosensitive hydrogel

For experimental evaluation, three separate 1 mL aliquots of the hydrogel formulation were prepared, each containing the KR-12-a5 peptide at the designated concentration. To each aliquot, 500 µg, 1000 µg, or 2000 µg of the KR-12-a5 peptide was added, respectively. The mixtures were gently agitated to ensure complete dissolution, and any air bubbles were removed via centrifugation. The prepared samples were subsequently stored for further experimentation.

Material characterization

Gelation time measurement

One gram of the pregel solution was weighed into a 3 mL sample vial and exposed to a UV light source. The gelation time of photo-crosslinkable collagen samples with different degrees of functionalization was determined and recorded via the tilting method(Fig. 2c).

Hydrogel morphology observation

After freeze-drying, the photo-crosslinked hydrogel was sputter-coated with gold. The morphology of the samples was then observed via electron microscopy at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV.

Dynamic mechanical thermal analysis

The mechanical properties of the hydrogels were measured via a rheometer. The hydrogel sample is placed on the stainless steel parallel plates of the rheometer (diameter of 30 mm), with a gap of 10 mm between the plates. Dynamic frequency sweep tests are performed at 20 °C with a strain of 2%, and the storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G’’) are recorded over a frequency range of 0.1–10 Hz.

Swelling properties of the hydrogel

The swelling ratio of the hydrogel was determined at 37 °C. The freeze-dried hydrogel sponges were weighed and then immersed in 20 mL of phosphate buffer solution (pH = 6.5). The weight of the hydrogel was measured every 2 h until it remained constant. Three parallel experiments were conducted for each sample, and the degree of swelling was calculated via Eq. (1) and averaged.

Hydrogel porosity characterization

Subsequently, samples were fully immersed in anhydrous ethanol and vacuum-degassed at 0.1 MPa for 30 min to ensure complete pore infiltration. Following ethanol saturation, surface-adhered ethanol was carefully blotted with filter paper and the saturated mass (M₂) was immediately recorded. Porosity was then calculated according to Eq. (2).

Peptide release experiment

Peptide standard solutions with concentrations of 12.5 µg/mL, 25 µg/mL, 50 µg/mL, 100 µg/mL, 200 µg/mL, 400 µg/mL, and 800 µg/mL were prepared, with ultrapure water used as the blank control. In vitro deposition experiments

The photosensitive hydrogel was soaked in SBF and allowed to stand at 4 °C for one week. The mixture was then soaked in ddH2O for one day, after which the water was changed every 2 h to remove soluble calcium ion crystals. After freeze-drying and sputter-coating with gold, the gel blocks were observed via electron microscopy (SEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed to evaluate the effect of the hydrogel on calcium ion crystallization.

In vitro antibacterial performance

Based on the in vitro MIC (minimum inhibitory concentration) and MBC (maximum bactericidal concentration) results of KR-12-a5(Supplemental Table 1), the release profile was referenced. Freeze-dried sterilized SIS, SIS/IA, and SIS/IA/KR-12-a5 hydrogels with peptide concentrations of 500 µg/mL, 1000 µg/mL, and 2000 µg/mL, respectively, were co-cultured with bacterial suspensions (Staphylococcus aureus, Porphyromonas gingivalis, Fusobacterium nucleatum, and Streptococcus hemolyticus) at a concentration of 2 × 10⁴ CFU/mL. After 48 h of incubation, the bacterial suspensions were collected for live/dead staining and imaging. The hydrogel blocks were fixed with glutaraldehyde solution, washed with sterile PBS three times (5 min each), and dehydrated with a series of ethanol concentrations (15%, 25%, 40%, 60%, 75%, 90%, 100%). After freeze-drying and sputter-coating with gold, the bacterial morphology and quantity were observed via SEM.

In vitro cytotoxicity and proliferation

Proliferation curves of BMSCs co-cultured with varying concentrations of KR-12-a5 measured by CCK-8 assay (Supplemental Figure S3). The sterilized hydrogel was soaked in DMEM at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v) for 24 h. After centrifugation and filtration, the hydrogel extract was obtained. Rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were used for in vitro biocompatibility and subsequent osteogenesis assays. The BMSCs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% hydrogel extract, 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin, with a control group without extract. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. BMSCs were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁴ in a 24-well plate. Cell proliferation was evaluated on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 via the CCK-8 assay. At the specified time points, the optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm via a Multiskan FC plate reader (Semerce Aerospace Instruments Co., Ltd.). The cytoskeleton was stained with phalloidin and DAPI. Cell viability was evaluated on days 1 and 3 via AO/EB staining and confocal laser scanning microscopy. The number of cells in each field of view was measured via ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA), with three repetitions per experiment.

Hemolysis test

To assess the safety of materials in contact with blood, a hemolysis test was performed. The test hydrogel material was soaked in sterile PBS at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v) for 24 h to obtain the PBS extract. Two milliliters of fresh rat blood was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was removed to obtain a fresh red blood cell suspension. The red blood cell suspension was diluted to 5% with PBS solution. The diluted suspension was mixed with the extract at a ratio of 1:1 and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. After centrifugation, the OD value of the supernatant was measured at 540 nm. Sterile ddH₂O mixed with the diluted cell suspension was used as the positive control, and PBS mixed with the cell suspension was used as the negative control. Osteogenic differentiation

To induce osteogenic differentiation, BMSCs were cultured on the surface of osteogenic differentiation medium supplemented with 10% hydrogel extract (supplemented with 10 mM/L β-glycerophosphate, 0.1 mM/L dexamethasone, and 0.05 mM/L ascorbic acid) for 7 and 14 days. The osteogenic differentiation medium was replaced every 3 days.

Immunostaining

After 14 days of culture, the attached cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min. The cells were then incubated in 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS at room temperature for 15 min and blocked with 1% BSA in PBS at room temperature for 2 h. The fixed cells were incubated at 4 °C with primary antibodies (anti-OPN, anti-RUNX-2, anti-ALP, and anti-BMP-2) in PBS containing 5% BSA. After being washed three times with PBS, the samples were exposed to an Alexa-Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:500. Abcam) at room temperature. Following additional washes, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were captured via a Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and quantification

At 7 and 14 days of osteogenic induction, the ALP activity of co-cultured BMSCs was assessed via an ALP assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 2 h of incubation at room temperature, images were taken under a microscope. The cell lysates obtained from the digested BMSCs on day 7 were reacted with p-nitrophenyl phosphate (p-NPP). The absorbance at 405 nm was measured via a Multiskan FC microplate reader to quantify the ALP content.

Alizarin red S staining and quantification

After being cultured in osteogenic medium for 14 and 21 days, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min and stained with 1% (w/v) Alizarin Red S (pH 4.2, Sigma‒Aldrich) for 1 h. The samples were washed three times with double-distilled water and imaged under a light microscope. The relative stained area was analyzed via ImageJ software for relative quantification of Alizarin Red staining.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT‒PCR)

After 7 days of culture, total RNA was extracted from the cells cultured on each sample via TRIzol reagent. The RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA via a reverse transcription kit (Takara, Japan). RT‒PCR was performed using MaximaTM SYBR Green/ROX qPCR premix (Thermo). All reactions were conducted in triplicate. The sequences of primers used were as follows Supplemental Table 2.

Western blot

BMSCs were seeded at a density of 3 × 10⁴ cells per well in 6-well plates. After 7 days of osteogenic induction, total protein was extracted via protein lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher) and phosphatase inhibitors, and the samples were subsequently lysed on ice. The total protein concentration was determined via a BCA assay kit (Beyotime). Loading buffer was added, and the mixture was heated at 100 °C for 5 min. Proteins were separated by SDS‒PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (anti-OPN, anti-ALP, anti-BMP-2, anti-RUNX-2, and anti-GAPDH). The samples were then incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h. Bands were visualized via an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Thermo Fisher). Band intensities were analyzed via ImageJ.

Animal and surgical procedures

Eight-week-old SD rats were selected for the experiment to establish a periodontitis model. Anesthesia was induced using pentobarbital sodium, and the surgical level of anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of the toe-pinch reflex. Periodontitis was induced in the rats by placing a 0.2 mm ligature wire in the proximal‒distal interdental space of the second maxillary molar and feeding them a high-viscosity, high-sugar diet for 2 weeks. Periodontal bone defects and periodontal abscesses were observed via periodontal probing. The ligature wire was removed, and after basic plaque debridement, the hydrogels were injected into the periodontal pockets. Four weeks after hydrogel implantation, the rats were euthanized, and the maxillae were harvested. Bone regeneration after surgery and material implantation was evaluated via micro-CT and histological analysis.The procedures for handling animals during the experiment adhered to the ethical standards for animal welfare.

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) and histological analysis

Maxillae samples were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The samples were scanned via a micro-CT scanner (SCANCO mCT50, Switzerland) at 70 kV, 200 µA, and a resolution of 20 μm. 3D reconstruction was performed via micro-CT analysis software. The volume of newly regenerated bone was quantified via software (Materialise Mimics Research 19) with a threshold range of 600–1300. After micro-CT analysis, the samples were embedded in 10% neutral buffered formalin, decalcified, and dehydrated. Tissue sections of 10 μm thickness were cut via a rotary microtome (RM2255, Leica, Germany) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome. Images were captured using a Nikon E800 upright microscope.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), with quantitative experiments conducted in at least quadruplicate. Student’s t-test was used to compare two groups, and one-way ANOVA was employed for multiple group comparisons. Statistical significance was denoted as P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), and P < 0.001 (***). Statistical analysis and data processing were performed via GraphPad Prism 8 and Origin 2024.

Results & discussion

Structure and properties

SIS collagen with different grafting densities was prepared by adjusting the ratio of collagen to itaconic anhydride (1:1, 1:2, 1:3, 1:4, and 1:5). FTIR analysis revealed that, compared with that of pure SIS collagen, the functionalized collagen spectrum exhibited a slight redshift and broadening of the absorption peak near 3432 cm⁻¹. This is due to itaconic anhydride reacting with amino groups to form amide bonds, which weakens the NH stretching vibration of primary amines (approximately 3446 cm⁻¹) and enhances the N‒H stretching vibration of secondary amides (approximately 3300 cm⁻¹). Additionally, the peak intensity at 1541 cm⁻¹ increased significantly due to the bending vibrations of C–H bonds in the CH₂=CR₁R₂ structure. These changes confirmed the successful grafting of itaconic anhydride onto the SIS collagen.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that the pure SIS hydrogel had a porous, lamellar honeycomb network structure. After crosslinking, the pore size became denser and more regular, with varying sizes of KR-12-a5 peptide particles visible on the hydrogel surface (Fig. 1b). The porous structure facilitates secretion absorption and water retention. The physical and microstructural images of the photosensitive hydrogel (Fig. 1b) show that the crosslinked gels possess excellent injectability and gel-forming capacity, with all the samples forming gels rapidly within 140 s. The photo-crosslinkable SIS hydrogel demonstrates unique clinical advantages for periodontal applications through its rapid photopolymerization capability. This functional biomaterial not only precisely adapts to the complex anatomy of periodontal pockets for minimally invasive syringe administration, but also provides immediate mechanical support to damaged periodontal tissues while enabling controlled release of encapsulated therapeutic agents.

SEM analysis was performed on freeze-dried scaffolds to quantify pore morphology, with measurements taken from 20 randomly selected pores per sample group. The analysis revealed progressively decreasing average pore diameters across the experimental groups: 145.36 μm (SIS/1IA), 114.47 μm (SIS/2IA), 105.25 μm (SIS/3IA), 75.37 μm (SIS/4IA), and 70.17 μm (SIS/5IA), demonstrating a clear inverse correlation between IA concentration and pore size (Fig. 3h), showing a centralized distribution trend (Supplemental Figure S2). Porosity measurements indicated that as crosslinking increased, the porosity of the hydrogel decreased (Fig. 3d). This is due to the anhydride bonds in itaconic anhydride reacting directly with collagen amino groups, forming amide bonds. The modification of collagen with diacrylic anhydride alters its isoelectric point, increasing its solubility in physiological environments, which results in a denser collagen network structure. The porosity of hydrogels serves as a critical structural characteristic that determines their functionality as biomaterials, directly influencing cellular behavior, mechanical properties, and biological performance. An optimal three-dimensional porous architecture not only provides essential spatial topological cues for cell migration, proliferation and differentiation, while facilitating nutrient exchange and metabolic waste removal, but also enables programmable design of mechanical properties through precise regulation of collagen fiber density.

Dynamic mechanical analysis revealed that itaconic anhydride (IA) crosslinking significantly enhanced both the elastic (storage modulus, G’) and viscous (loss modulus, G”) properties of SIS hydrogels compared to pristine SIS, with the SIS/3IA group showing the most pronounced improvement (6.74-fold and 9.06-fold increases in G’ and G” respectively). All samples demonstrated stable mechanical behavior in the 0.01-1 Hz frequency range, with G’ consistently dominating over G”, confirming their elastic-dominant nature. However, excessive IA incorporation beyond the optimal 3IA ratio led to diminished mechanical properties, attributable to increased free carboxyl groups that enhanced collagen solubility while compromising crosslinking efficiency, ultimately resulting in a net reduction of mechanical strength.

In simulated in vivo environments, the swelling behavior of the photocured gels depends on the carboxyl ratio. An imbalance in the isoelectric point leads to rapid initial water absorption, but the pores eventually reach swelling equilibrium. The results revealed that all crosslinked gels absorbed water rapidly during the early stages, slowed after 2 h, and reached equilibrium after 4 h. As the degree of crosslinking increased, swelling initially increased but then decreased (Fig. 3b).

In the degradation experiments, the pure SIS hydrogel, whose collagen molecules are held together solely by van der Waals forces, exhibited a degradation rate of 94.6% over 4 weeks. The crosslinked groups did not significantly differ in degradation rates. increased amounts of itaconic anhydride increased collagen solubility, but the denser pore size slowed the degradation process. Since SIS/3IA demonstrated optimal mechanical and swelling properties, this ratio was used for subsequent experiments (Fig. 3b.d.e).

KR-12-a5 concentrations of 500 µg/mL, 1000 µg/mL, and 2000 µg/mL were tested to measure peptide release (Fig. 3c). The results indicated that within the first 24 h, peptide release reached 27.4%, 32.1%, and 33.2%, respectively. By day 7, the release levels increased to 48.2%, 67.6%, and 76.5%, respectively (Fig. 3c). Rapid early release is beneficial for early bacterial clearance. Over 14 days, total peptide release reached 53.7%, 75.7%, and 82.1%, respectively. KR-12-a5 binds to collagen via hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds, forming localized high-concentration microdomains that prolong its bioactive duration [45]. Sustained release is advantageous for providing long-term growth factors and antibacterial protection.

After 7 days of degradation in SBF (simulated body fluid), SEM observations revealed mineral deposits on the hydrogel surface, confirmed by EDS analysis to contain calcium and phosphorus components(Fig. 1d and Fig. 3g). The enrichment of biological ions, such as calcium and phosphorus, accelerates the osteogenic process in vivo. The bioaccumulation of osteoinductive ions (e.g., Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻) synergistically enhances in vivo osteogenesis.

Biocompatibility of hydrogels

Cell toxicity is a potential concern for modified collagen-based biomaterials. To assess the biocompatibility of the modified SIS/IA/KR-12-a5 hydrogels, we compared them with the SIS/IA and plain SIS hydrogels via the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 4h). On day 7 of culture, the cell viabilities of the SIS, SIS/IA, SIS/IA with 500 µg/mL KR-12-a5, SIS/IA with 1000 µg/mL KR-12-a5, and SIS/IA with 2000 µg/mL KR-12-a5 groups were 108.8%, 116.3%, 120.5%, 132.9%, and 117.8% that of the control group, respectively (Fig. 4h). These results indicate that both the SIS and SIS/IA light-sensitive hydrogels enhance BMSC proliferation, with the addition of KR-12-a5 peptides further promoting cell growth. However, the cell viability in the SIS/IA with 2000 µg/mL KR-12-a5 group was slightly lower than that in the SIS/IA with 1000 µg/mL KR-12-a5 group, potentially due to the inhibitory effects of the higher peptide concentration.

AO/EB staining experiments revealed good cell viability across all groups, with a higher cell count in the experimental groups than in the blank group (Fig. 4a). Over time, the relative cell proliferation rates increased in all the groups, likely due to the presence of various cell growth factors in the materials that supported cell growth. Cytoskeletal staining provided further insights into the cell morphology and myosin distribution, with the experimental groups exhibiting more pronounced red fluorescence for myosin (Fig. 4b). KR-12-a5 enhances cell-matrix interactions by binding to hydrophobic domains of collagen fibers (e.g., Pro-Hyp-Gly tripeptide sequences), thereby triggering the integrin-FAK-Src signaling cascade to promote cell spreading and proliferation [46–48]. In the cell migration assays, all the experimental groups demonstrated enhanced osteoblast chemotaxis (Fig. 4c), suggesting that the composite scaffold materials facilitate osteoblast growth and provide favorable conditions for bone formation. In the hemolysis assay, all experimental groups demonstrated excellent hemocompatibility, with hemolysis rates consistently below the internationally recognized safety threshold of 5% (Supplemental Figure S3). Notably, across all crosslinking formulations (1IA to 5IA) and peptide concentration gradients, the measured hemolysis rates remained exceptionally low, with no group exceeding 2%. This remarkably low hemolytic potential was observed uniformly regardless of crosslinking density or peptide loading, indicating that the material’s safety profile is robust against compositional variations. The consistently minimal erythrocyte damage (< 2%) suggests that neither the crosslinking chemistry nor the incorporated peptides significantly perturb erythrocyte membrane integrity. These findings strongly support the material’s exceptional blood compatibility, fulfilling a critical requirement for implantable biomaterials where contact with circulating blood components is anticipated.

Antibacterial properties of the hydrogels

To evaluate the antibacterial activity of the hydrogels, we co-cultured them with bacterial suspensions of Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus sanguinis, Fusobacterium nucleatum, and Porphyromonas gingivalis at a 1:100 volume ratio. In the blank and SIS groups, dense and intense green fluorescence was observed, indicating minimal antibacterial activity of the SIS hydrogels. However, in the SIS/IA crosslinked hydrogels without the peptide, an increase in the number of dead bacteria was noted. In contrast, the experimental groups containing the short peptide KR-12-a5 presented varying degrees of red fluorescence (Fig. 5b). Higher concentrations of KR-12-a5 in the hydrogels tended to reduce the number of viable bacteria and increase the number of dead bacteria, with an overall decrease in the total bacterial count as the peptide concentration increased. These results confirm the strong antibacterial effect of KR-12-a5. In the MIC assay conducted with KR-12-a5 solution alone, the peptide demonstrated potent antimicrobial activity. Notably, its inhibitory effect against Gram-positive bacteria was superior to that against Gram-negative bacteria, a difference potentially attributable to variations in cell wall structure and efflux pump activity. Specifically, Gram-negative bacteria (e.g., Porphyromonas gingivalis) express RND family efflux pumps (such as MexAB-OprM) that actively extrude hydrophobic antimicrobial peptides [49], thereby reducing their intracellular concentration and compromising antibacterial efficacy [41, 50, 51].

Antibacterial evaluation of hydrogels with different peptide concentrations. (a) Morphological observation of hydrogels and bacteria after 48 h of co-culture. (b) Live/dead staining of bacteria following 48 h of co-culture, along with quantification of live and dead bacterial cells. (c) Live/dead bacterial counting in co-culture system

We also compared the ratio of viable bacteria to dead bacteria (S. aureus, S. sanguinis, F. nucleatum, and P. gingivalis) in P-nHA-SIS scaffolds with different peptide concentrations. The addition of 1000 µg/mL KR-12-a5 resulted in a significant antibacterial effect. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the bacteria after co-culture with the hydrogels revealed that the bacteria on the surface of the plain SIS hydrogels retained their intact structures (Fig. 5a), with smooth surfaces and undamaged cell membranes. In contrast, in the experimental groups with peptide addition, the bacterial count was markedly reduced, and the bacteria were dispersed on the surface of the scaffold fibers. These findings indicate that KR-12-a5 significantly reduces the adhesion of S. aureus, S. sanguinis, S. gordonii, and F. nucleatum to the scaffold material.

Furthermore, morphological damage was observed in bacteria from the experimental groups, including membrane disruption and cellular flattening. These alterations likely result from KR-12-a5-induced modulation of membrane permeability, attributable to its dual mechanism of action: (1) membrane disruption via hydrophobic residues (e.g., valine, leucine) inserting into lipid bilayers to induce osmotic lysis [52], and (2) quorum sensing interference (e.g., agr system inhibition in S. aureus) suppressing virulence factor expression. These combined effects ultimately lead to bacterial envelope rupture and cytoplasmic content leakage [53].

In vitro osteogenic potential of the hydrogels

To evaluate the osteogenic differentiation effects of the hydrogels on BMSCs, we measured early osteogenic markers, including alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and mineralization, after 7 and 14 days of culture. ALP staining results (Fig. 6a) revealed that the ALP-positive areas in the experimental groups were significantly larger than those in the control group at both time points. Notably, the addition of the KR-12-a5 peptide further increased the proportion of ALP-positive areas.

Osteogenic induction of BMSCs with hydrogels at different peptide concentrations. (a) Alkaline phosphatase staining of BMSCs at 7 and 14 days. (b) Alizarin Red staining of BMSCs at 14 and 21 days. (c, d) Quantitative analysis of alkaline phosphatase activity at 7 and 14 days. (e, f) Quantitative analysis of Alizarin Red staining at 14 and 21 days

Alizarin Red staining, which is used to assess mineralization as a late-stage osteogenic marker, demonstrated that the ability of the hydrogel to promote cell mineralization was significantly enhanced by the addition of KR-12-a5 (Fig. 6b). However, in the SIS/IA/2000 µg/mL KR-12-a5 group, both the number and area of mineralized nodules were lower than those in the other experimental groups, which was consistent with the results of the cell viability assay. KR-12-a5 exhibits a characteristic hormetic effect in osteogenic induction, where its biological activity follows a biphasic concentration-dependent response. At the optimal concentration (1000 µg/mL), the peptide promotes osteogenesis through TLR2/4-MAPK pathway activation and forms a sustained-release system via electrostatic interactions with collagen matrix [54, 55]. However, at higher concentrations (2000 µg/mL), excessive membrane depolarization leads to dysregulated calcium influx that interferes with calcium-dependent osteogenic signaling, while concurrent activation of apoptotic pathways (e.g., caspase-3) partially counteracts the pro-osteogenic effects [56]. This hormetic behavior underscores the importance of precise dosage control in therapeutic applications [57].

Quantitative analysis of ALP and Alizarin Red staining (Fig. 6c-f) revealed higher expression levels in the peptide-treated groups than in the control groups, indicating that hydrogels containing KR-12-a5 have a greater capacity to promote osteogenesis. However, excessively high concentrations of KR-12-a5 may inhibit osteogenesis. Using the SIS/IA/1000 µg/mL KR-12-a5 group as the experimental group, we further evaluated the expression of osteogenic genes, including Alp, Opn, Bmp-2, and Runx-2, to investigate BMSC osteogenic differentiation. RT‒qPCR analysis revealed increased expression of all the selected genes compared with that in the control group, with the KR-12-a5 peptide significantly increasing osteogenic gene expression compared with that in the SIS and SIS/IA groups (Fig. 7a).

Osteogenic induction of BMSCs with photosensitive hydrogels. (a) Quantitative RT-qPCR analysis of osteogenic markers Alp, Bmp-2, Opn, and Runx-2. (b) Western blot analysis of osteogenic protein expression. (c) Densitometric analysis of Western blot results. (d) Immunofluorescence staining of osteogenic markers. (e) Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity

Immunofluorescence staining revealed similar expression patterns, with significantly greater fluorescence intensities for the osteogenic proteins ALP, OPN, BMP-2, and RUNX-2 in the KR-12-a5 group than in the SIS and SIS/IA groups(Fig. 7d). Quantitative analysis of the fluorescence intensity confirmed this trend (Fig. 7e). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that KR-12-a5 enhanced the expression of osteogenesis-related proteins(Fig. 7c).

These findings suggest that the SIS and SIS/IA hydrogels can increase the osteogenic potential of BMSCs and promote the expression of osteogenic genes, with the KR-12-a5 peptide further enhancing the osteogenic activity of the hydrogels. KR-12-a5 exerts significant osteogenic-promoting effects through dual activation of the BMP/Smad and p38 MAPK signaling pathways. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that cationic antimicrobial peptides can regulate osteogenic differentiation via membrane receptor-mediated signal transduction [33, 39, 40, 58]. The observed upregulation of BMP-2 expression and subsequent Smad1/5/8 phosphorylation indicate that KR-12-a5 initiates osteogenic differentiation through the canonical BMP signaling pathway, consistent with the well-established role of this pathway in Runx-2 transcriptional activation. Concurrently, the activation of p38 MAPK (manifested as elevated phosphorylation levels) likely promotes late-stage osteogenic maturation by stabilizing key transcription factors such as ATF4 [40, 58]. The synergistic interaction between these two pathways may account for the enhanced mineralization capacity observed in KR-12-a5-treated groups compared to those with single pathway activation. Notably, the concentration-dependent effects observed in this study suggest the existence of a therapeutic window for KR-12-a5, where optimal osteogenic activity is achieved at intermediate concentrations (1000 µg/mL), while higher concentrations exhibit diminished efficacy, potentially due to activation of negative feedback mechanisms such as apoptotic pathways [40, 56, 59]). These findings position KR-12-a5 as a promising candidate for bone regeneration strategies, particularly for treating infected bone defects, where its dual antimicrobial and osteogenic properties can provide combined therapeutic benefits. Future studies should further investigate the precise receptor interactions and crosstalk mechanisms between signaling pathways to optimize therapeutic applications.

In vivo osteogenesis promotion study

To evaluate the osteogenic potential of the hydrogels, we treated rats with periodontal disease. After periodontal disease was induced by placing the ligatures for 2 weeks, the ligatures were removed, and the bacterial plaque was mechanically removed from the periodontal pockets(Fig. 8a). The hydrogels were then injected into the periodontal pockets and defects, followed by UV curing for 90 s (Fig. 8b). Four weeks after hydrogel implantation, CT scans of the maxillae of the rats were performed. To ensure consistency in measurement, the position of the maxilla was adjusted so that the occlusal plane of the three molars was parallel to the scanning direction.

Application of photosensitive hydrogels in rat periodontitis model. (a) Schematic diagram illustrating periodontal ligation and hydrogel injection. (b) Establishment of the periodontitis model and treatment with hydrogel injection. (c) Micro-CT imaging of small animal models. (d) H&E staining of the alveolar bone across different groups. (e) Masson’s staining of the alveolar bone in each group. (f) Immunohistochemical staining for BMP-2 protein expression. (g) Immunohistochemical staining for RUNX-2 protein expression

The results revealed that the SIS/IA/KR-12-a5 group exhibited new autologous bone formation around the edges of critical-sized defects, whereas the new bone formation in the SIS and SIS/IA groups was limited. Sagittal cross-sectional images confirmed that, compared with the blank group, the SIS/IA/KR-12-a5 group formed continuous regenerative bone at the defect site (Fig. 8c).

Quantitative micro-CT analysis (Supplemental Figure S3)revealed that the volume of regenerated bone in the SIS/IA/KR-12-a5 group were significantly greater than those in the SIS and SIS/IA groups, which was in line with our expectations. Histological analysis via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson’s trichrome staining further confirmed that the SIS/IA/KR-12-a5 hydrogel effectively promoted new bone regeneration(Fig. 8d.e). The experimental group demonstrated well-organized collagen fibers (blue staining) that formed an integrated bone-collagen composite structure with newly formed bone tissue (red staining). Histomorphometric analysis revealed that the neobone primarily localized at the defect margins, exhibiting continuous regeneration patterns in sagittal sections. Notably, the trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) in these regions increased to 1.8-fold of the control group (p < 0.001), indicating significant improvement in structural mechanical properties. This spatial distribution pattern of mineralized tissue suggests enhanced biomechanical competence at the critical bone-defect interface. Additionally, the number of inflammatory cells was significantly lower in the SIS/IA/KR-12-a5 group than in the control, SIS, and SIS/IA groups. Furthermore, fewer inflammatory cells were observed in the SIS/IA group than in the SIS group, suggesting that the SIS hydrogels crosslinked with itaconic anhydride may possess some anti-inflammatory properties. The results of the detection of the osteogenic factors BMP-2 and RUNX-2 revealed that the expression in the positive area of the experimental group was greater than that of the blank group (Fig. 8f.g). BMP-2 positive expression area increased, activating the downstream SMAD signaling pathway. The proportion of RUNX-2 nuclear-positive cells increased, driving pre-osteoblast differentiation. These findings further verified that the photosensitive hydrogel loaded with polypeptide could increase the expression of osteogenic factors in vivo and possessed relatively high osteogenic potential.

Conclusion

In this study, the incorporation of hydrogels with photosensitive properties and greater morphological plasticity enabled them to exhibit more diverse and adaptable structural characteristics, facilitating their potential applications in various biomedical fields. The photosensitive hydrogel demonstrated excellent injectability and rapid gelation under 365 nm UV light. Compared with the pure SIS hydrogel, the crosslinked hydrogel exhibited significantly improved mechanical properties and good biocompatibility. The addition of the KR-12-a5 peptide further enhanced antibacterial activity, significantly promoted BMSC proliferation and osteogenic differentiation in vitro, and facilitated effective bone regeneration in vivo. Owing to its tunable degradation and gelation behavior, as well as excellent mechanical and osteogenic properties, this composite hydrogel shows great potential as an injectable biomaterial or scaffold for bone regeneration and repair applications.
