Enhancing student engagement with introductory microbiology and immunology topics using a novel board game
Authors: Rebecca S. Rivard
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn introductory microbiology courses for non-biology majors, it can be difficult to ensure that students gain a functional understanding of the interplay between the host immune system and an invading infectious agent. Immune Battle is a board game that allows students to explore pathogen evolution and the immune system’s response to incursions in a captivating way, leveraging battleship and worker placement styles of gameplay. Students play in teams as either a pathogenic microbe attempting to reproduce and transmit itself to new hosts or as the immune system trying to defend the host from infection. Immune Battle seeks to mimic real-world interactions between immune cells and microbes using board game mechanics. For example, students will quickly find that adaptive immune system actions are better at fighting off microbial incursions than innate immune actions but require more time to activate. In this way, Immune Battle reinforces student’s understanding of the immune system and pathogenic virulence in an exciting and interactive way that keeps them thinking about host defenses long after the end of the class period or exam. This board game is well suited for advanced high school courses and introductory college-level courses that have only limited time to cover the immune system and microbiology. Documents containing the necessary components of the board game (not including a six-sided die) are included in the supplemental materials, so educators can print out their own copies and use this board game in their classes with no cost to themselves or their students.

INTRODUCTION

The use of game elements to improve student communication with peers, engagement with material, organization of acquired information, and mastery of difficult concepts is becoming increasingly common (1–4). When used in the context of education, board games can offer many benefits that can be difficult to foster in a traditional lecture class, such as the development of student communication and problem-solving skills, enhancing a learner’s metacognitive awareness, and emphasizing the importance of collaborative work (5, 6).

It is important for nursing students to gain a general understanding of the immune system, and how it defends the host against pathogenic microbes; however, nursing students are rarely able to take the introductory biology classes that lay the groundwork for a deep understanding of these topics. The Immune Battle board game was designed to demonstrate the interplay between the immune system and microbial infections in an engaging way that can fit into a single class or lab period. This board game is well suited for advanced high school courses and introductory college-level courses in microbiology that have only limited time to discuss the immune system. The game board, tokens, and rules can be printed out and assembled to use by interested instructors (Appendix S1 to S7).

How to effectively use the board game

This board game is designed to be played in two teams of two to three students. One team plays as the host’s immune system, and the other team plays as a pathogen. The pathogen team has a small version of the main game board that is hidden from the immune system team by screen. In this way, the pathogen team can keep track of the location of their pathogenic cells within the host body without the immune team knowing where the pathogens are, reminiscent of the game Battleship (Fig. 1). The pathogen team can take two actions each turn that they keep track of on their action board (in the style of worker placement games). Worker placement games allow the player to use tokens to mark different actions they use each turn. Worker placement games force players to strategically prioritize certain actions from a wide array of possibilities. Actions the pathogen team can use include reproduction, evolution, gathering energy, and moving around the body. The pathogen team gets points for each microbe that is transmitted, incentivizing them to replicate and evolve to avoid the immune response.

The immune team uses their own action board and action tokens to control their cells in the hopes of locating the pathogens. If a pathogen is discovered, the pathogen marker is moved from the hidden board to the main board. Once the pathogens are on the main board, they can be targeted by phagocytic cells from the immune team. The immune team can cause inflammation of the host to make organs less hospitable to the microbe; however, this action also causes damage to the host health, which will inevitably decrease the final score of the immune team. While the immune team initially has limited action economy, once the immune cells successfully phagocytose pathogens, they can begin to activate the adaptive immune response, making more options available for targeting of pathogenic microbes, including creation of antibodies and CD8+ T cells.

Providing students with the rules (Appendix S2) and a brief instructional video (https://vums-web.villanova.edu/Mediasite/Play/2945fa728e5b4afa8e346c79cfdb24b91d) prior to actual gameplay helps to streamline class time. Playtime varies depending on how quickly the immune system team is able to locate the initial pathogenic infection as well as students’ familiarity with the game mechanics, microbial adaptability, and immune function. The average time for one round of this board game is around 50 min. If a group does end quickly, I suggest that the teams switch roles and play again to get a sense of how the capabilities of the immune response and pathogenic incursion may differ. Playing this board game in a lab setting is a wonderful way to allow students time to experiment with different roles and see how the evolution of different pathogenic traits can impact the outcomes.

At the end of the instructions (Appendix S2), there is a glossary of terms the students can refer to throughout the board game. Additionally, there are a series of reflection questions to help students articulate the patterns they observed in the board game and relate them to the course content. I often assign students some of these questions to complete as a low-stake graded assignment following gameplay. While this board game can be completed without any previous knowledge of microbiology or the immune system, the students seem to find the board game more helpful when it is used to review topics already covered in class.

CONCLUSION

Students consistently reported enjoying playing the board game, with one student saying, “Not only was it educational, but also a good break from the typical lecturing learning style.” Many students have requested the inclusion of more such activities over the course of the class. Overall, the Immune Battle board game seems to improve student experience and engagement with difficult material. I hope that others will be able to use the materials provided here to bring more excitement into the classroom.
