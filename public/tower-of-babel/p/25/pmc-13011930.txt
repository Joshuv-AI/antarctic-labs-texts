A nematode-built conduit for cross kingdom biotrophic interaction
Authors: Valerie M. Williamson, Shahid Siddique
Published: 2026

Plant parasitism has evolved independently multiple times within the phylum Nematoda. These parasitic lineages engage in diverse interactions with their hosts ranging from migrating worms that destructively withdraw nutrients from plant cells to extended interactions in which the nematode becomes sedentary and feeds from elaborately modified feeding cells for an extended period (1, 2). Microscopic examination of feeding cells has long revealed prominent, elaborate structures, called feeding tubes (FTs), in the cytoplasm of feeding cells developed by different lineages of plant parasitic nematodes (PPN) (3). It has long been postulated that these structures are central to the function and maintenance of the various feeding cells, but identification of their molecular composition has been elusive. Using a combination of techniques, Hussey et al. (4) move these structures from a long-standing ultrastructural curiosity to a molecularly defined, nematode-derived interface central to sustained plant–nematode biotrophic interactions (4).

Root-knot nematodes (RKN; Meloidogyne spp.) are obligate parasites that form extended, biotrophic interactions with thousands of plant species. They infect roots as motile second-stage juveniles (J2) and migrate to a site inside the root vasculature where they initiate characteristic galls (root-knots; Fig. 1A) and modifications to host cells to produce feeding sites. Central to the feeding site are approximately five host cells referred to as giant cells that are multinucleate and highly modified to provide nutrients for the nematode (Fig. 1A). The J2 become sedentary and undergo several molts over the course of about a month and develop into pear-shaped females. The adult female will produce up to 1,000 eggs in a gelatinous mass on the root surface (Fig. 1A). During this process, the nematode feeds sequentially from the same strategically positioned giant cells ingesting cytoplasmic nutrients through an anterior hollow spear called a stylet (Fig. 1B). The stylet is used both to introduce nematode molecules into plant tissues (apoplast and symplast) and to ingest cytoplasm.

Because feeding occurs deep within intact plant tissue, direct high-resolution observation of the dynamic feeding process has not been possible. Based on microscopic studies, it appears that during feeding, the stylet pierces the cell wall of the giant cell but does not directly rupture the plant cell membrane (Fig. 1B). Instead, the stylet aperture forms a pore in the membrane that is associated with the end of a FT, a prominent electron-dense, tubular structure that extends into the giant cell cytoplasm. The adult female feeds sequentially from its set of giant cells, and the presence of FT fragments within giant cells indicates that a new FT is produced during each feeding cycle. Electron microscopy studies have shown that these FTs are dense, crystalline structures surrounded by a membrane matrix with a central lumen [(Fig. 1C; (4, 5)]. Based mainly on ultrastructural observations, FTs have been proposed to function as molecular sieves that prevent ingestion of large molecules or organelles, provide hydraulic resistance to prevent leakage of cytoplasm and cell collapse during the intense feeding by the egg-laying female, and/or serve as a conduit delivering nutrients potentially via the endoplasmic reticulum (3, 6). The distal tip of the FT is closed, suggesting that molecules may filter into lumen along its length. However, lack of information regarding the identity of FT composition has limited investigation of their origin and source.

Extensive genomic and transcriptomic studies of RKN have led to identification of proteins that function in parasitism (7, 8). Attention has focused on genes encoding proteins secreted from cells, referred to as effector proteins. Several of these proteins have been demonstrated to have roles in parasitism such as suppression of host defense or to otherwise contribute to parasitism. Despite these advances, little progress had been made in identifying the molecular building blocks of FT proteins. To address this gap, Hussey et al. (4) used labor-intensive and elegant strategy that combined manual collection of giant cell cytoplasm coupled with FT enrichment using an assay based on microscopic observation (4). This was followed by solubilization and LC–MS/MS proteomic analysis and a multifaceted filtering pipeline that identified 11 strong candidates representing 7 gene families (4).

One candidate, Minc03784, was expressed as a recombinant protein and used to generate specific antibody. This antibody immunolocalized to FTs isolated from giant cells as well as in the dorsal gland and dorsal gland ampulla of egg-laying females. This localization pattern provided direct evidence that the Minc03784-encoded protein was a major component of the FT and confirms the nematode origin of FT constituents. Components appear to partially assemble in dorsal gland ampulla, whereas full assembly, possibly with other candidate components likely occurs later (4). Consistent with this model, the dorsal gland activity is highest during later stages of parasitism when there is intense feeding by the egg-laying females.

This identification of molecular components of FTs provides powerful new tools for dissecting the function of this specialized interface. These findings provide a molecular handle to determine the structure of FT and to identify other nematode and plant molecular components that interact with this structure. Additional candidate components of FTs or associated molecules were identified and remain to be confirmed. Host RNAi (HIGs) has been an effective tool for assessing the function of nematode effectors. Perturbing expression levels of this and other components of the FT by expressing RNAi in feeding sites, for example, may provide insight into the function of FTs. Antibodies to Minc03784 and other FT components may facilitate isolation of organelles and membranes associated with FTs. Such approaches may help resolve questions regarding the contributions of organelles and additional molecular components to FT function.

Proteins with similarity to Minc03784 are conserved throughout Meloidogyne species but are not found in other sedentary species for which molecular resources are available (4). While electron-dense structures resembling FTs have been observed in several plant-parasitic nematode interactions, there are differences in FT structure and the timing of presence in feeding cells (3). For example, in cyst nematodes (Heterodera and Globodera spp), which are in the same phylogenetic clade as RKN, FTs are not associated with membrane complexes and do not have crystallin structure (3). Although cyst nematodes produce elaborate feeding sites, molecular and microscopy evidence indicate that these feeding sites have different ontology, structure, and function than those produced by RKN (1). These observations suggest that FTs may have evolved independently in different nematode lineages and may perform distinct roles in supporting prolonged biotrophic interactions. RKN are widely recognized as the most damaging PPN, causing billions of dollars in crop losses worldwide each year. The identification of nematode-derived components of FTs opens avenues for nematode management. Because FTs are essential for sustained nutrient uptake, even partial disruption of their components, whether by genetic interference or by compounds that destabilize FT structure or secretion, is predicted to arrest feeding without directly damaging host cells. Targeting this interface therefore shifts control strategies from broadly toxic interventions to precise interference with a nematode-built structure that is spatially restricted to the feeding site.
