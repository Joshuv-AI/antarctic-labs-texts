Exploration of the genetic diversity of Avena Fatua L. (wild oat) through genotyping-by-sequencing and SDS-PAGE
Authors: Hyemin Seo, Hari Won, Shin Ae Lee, Jun Seong Jeong, Jihoon Jo, Seahee Han, Bo-Keun Ha, Kyung Jun Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe consumption of oats has rapidly increased due to their exceptional nutritional value. However, concerns over genetic erosion have emerged as oat breeding programs rely on a highly limited genetic pool. This study aimed to expand the genetic diversity pool of oats by collecting wild oat (Avena fatua L.) populations in South Korea and assessing their genetic diversity and seed storage protein patterns.ResultsA total of 237 A. fatua individuals were collected in 2022 from eight regions in the southwestern coastal areas of South Korea. Genetic diversity and seed storage protein patterns were analyzed using genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS) and sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). The GBS analysis identified 20,836 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). An analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) based on regional populations revealed that 40.9% of the genetic variation was attributed to differences among populations, while 59.1% was within populations, indicating high genetic differentiation within regional populations. Subsequent population structure analysis and discriminant analysis of principal components (DAPC) both stated the formation of two distinct genetic groups, with an AMOVA value of 70.9% between the groups, suggesting a high level of genetic variation. Pairwise FST analysis was conducted to compare the genetic differentiation between two populations, revealing that Jindo and Jangheung exhibited the highest level of genetic differentiation (FST = 0.795) among the geographic groups. Seed storage proteins were analyzed using SDS-PAGE, and the patterns were grouped using k-means clustering. A comparison between the groups based on protein patterns and those based on genetic variation revealed no significant correlation.ConclusionThis study provides data on the genetic diversity of A. fatua, a wild relative of cultivated oats, aimed at expanding the genetic pool of oats for future breeding programs. These findings are expected to be a foundational resource for oat breeding and genetic improvement efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07315-x.Keywords: Avena Fatua, GBS, Genetic diversity, SDS-PAGE, Seed storage protein

Background

Genetic diversity refers to the degree of genetic variation within a population or species, which arises from mutations and is influenced by gene flow. Moreover, genetic diversity is essential for the evolution and adaptation of species, as the degree of genetic variation determines the ability of each species to adapt to changing environments [1]. However, climate change has resulted in reduced genetic diversity. For example, increasing temperatures have driven animals and plants to migrate to higher altitudes or latitudes, with many species moving toward the polar regions [2]. These environmental changes have led to habitat fragmentation and range contraction, reducing population sizes; thus, genetic diversity ultimately increases the risk of species extinction [3]. In particular, crop genetic diversity has become increasingly threatened by shifts in agricultural production. Advances in technology, such as fertilizer use and irrigation, and the growing trend of market-oriented agriculture have led to a decline in the selection of diverse crop varieties, resulting in genetic erosion. Therefore, preserving and utilizing genetic diversity in crops is crucial for agricultural sustainability and modern breeding [4].

Crop wild relatives (CWRs), which often possess beneficial traits absent in cultivated varieties, play a vital role in crop breeding and food security [5]. CWRs encompass both the immediate progenitors of crop species and other plant species closely related through near evolutionary history [6]. Although taxonomically associated with cultivated crops, these species may occur as wild populations in their natural environments. These species harbor a rich reservoir of genes conferring tolerance to various abiotic stresses [7] and exhibit greater genetic and phenotypic variability than cultivated crops, making them invaluable genetic resources [8]. Avena fatua L. is a hexaploid (AACCDD, 2n = 6x = 42) wild oat species [9], and is an annual species that appears in April or May in temperate regions of the Northern hemisphere. Due to its high seed production and unique germination ecology, A. fatua can readily adapt to diverse environmental conditions [10]. Furthermore, with their abundant genetic diversity, wild oats serve as valuable genetic resources for oat improvement [11]. Moreover, A. fatua genotypes can readily hybridize with cultivated oat varieties, making them useful genetic resources, especially since this species can be sourced directly from natural populations [12]. According to Loskutov and Rines [13], most wild oat species contain high protein, starch, soluble fiber, and lipid concentrations. The seed storage proteins derived from wild oat cultivars have also been utilized as genetic diversity indicators [14, 15].

Molecular markers have been widely employed in genetic diversity and population structure studies in various plant species, including oat. Marker systems such as random amplified polymorphic DNA (RAPD), amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP), sequence-related amplified polymorphism (SRAP), simple sequence repeat (SSR), inter-simple sequence repeat (ISSR), and single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) have been effectively applied in oat germplasm characterization and diversity analysis [16, 17]. More recently, next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have emerged as powerful tools for high-throughput and cost-effective genotyping, overcoming the limitations of conventional markers [18, 19]. Among them, genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS)-an NGS-based technique that utilizes genome libraries generated by restriction enzymes-has been widely adopted in oat genetic studies for developing genome-wide SNP markers and assessing genetic diversity [20].

Additionally, seed storage proteins serve as valuable indicators of genetic diversity and are widely used to understand the evolutionary potential of various crops. Indeed, one of the most economical and widely adopted biochemical methods for analyzing genetic relationships in germplasm is sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Seed storage proteins serve multiple purposes in plant science and breeding, including: (a) assessing genetic diversity within and between species, (b) conserving and improving genetic resources, (c) clarifying genetic relationships, and (d) enhancing agronomic and economic traits in crops. Electrophoretic techniques for separating seed storage proteins are also widely used to detect genomic variation and distinguish among plant varieties. Notably, because seed storage protein banding patterns are relatively stable across different environmental conditions, SDS-PAGE provides a reliable and consistent method for cultivar identification [21]. According to Gepts [22], seed storage proteins are largely unaffected by environmental changes, and any polymorphism in these proteins directly reflects genomic diversity among different species [15].

This study aimed to analyze genetic diversity in A. fatua using SNPs and protein phenotypes through GBS and SDS-PAGE analyses. By analyzing genetic diversity among regional wild relatives, this study aimed to secure the gene pool of cultivated oats for breeding superior individuals and establish a theoretical foundation for the conservation and utilization of A. fatua.

Methods

Plant materials

A total of 237 Avena fatua individuals were used in this study (Table 1; Supplementary Table 1). Seeds were directly collected from eight different locations between May and July 2022 (Fig. 1). Collection of A. fatua from the wild did not require specific permits, as the species is not protected under national or local regulations. All field studies were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines. The species identification and sample collection were performed by Seahee Han. In accordance with institutional guidelines, three voucher specimens were prepared for each collection site and deposited in the herbarium of the Honam National Institute of Biological Resources (HNIBR). In this manuscript, only the accession number of the representative voucher specimen (HNIBRVP42798) is cited for reference. The samples were collected in three provinces in the southwestern region of South Korea: Jeonnam, Jeonbuk, and Gyeongnam. In Jeonnam, a total of 143 individuals were collected from Mokpo (61), Yeongam (14), Jindo (15), Suncheon (25), and Jangheung (28). In Jeonbuk, 75 individuals were collected from Gunsan (49) and Buan (26). In Gyeongnam, 19 individuals were collected from Namhae.

DNA extraction

A. fatua seeds were pulverized using a Tissuelyser II (Qiagen, Germany), and genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Plant Mini kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The DNA quality and concentration were tested using a nanodrop (Denovix, USA) and 1% agarose gel.

GBS analysis

Following the methodology outlined in Elshire et al. 2011, GBS libraries were constructed using the restriction enzymes PstI (CTGCAG) and MspI (CCGG). The library preparation and sequencing were conducted utilizing the Illumina TrueSeq Ver 3.0 protocol for paired-end sequencing with 151 bp read lengths on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform. A total of 96 samples were sequenced in a single lane. De-multiplexing was initially conducted using the barcode sequences from the raw data, followed by adapter trimming using the Cutadapt program (version 1.8.3). Subsequently, sequence quality was verified using the SolexaQA++ (version 3.1.7.2) program, and reads failing the quality threshold were subjected to read cleaning. Clean reads were aligned to the Avena sativa reference genome (OT3098 v2, GrainGenes) using the BWA-MEM algorithm implemented in the BWA program [23]. The alignment results were processed using the SAMtools program (version 0.1.16) to explore raw SNPs for each sample, and the identified SNPs were aggregated to generate a total SNP matrix for analysis.

SNP data analysis

Using the R package poppr (v 2.9.3), an analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) was conducted to compare individuals by region. A discriminant analysis of principal component (DAPC) was performed using the “adegenet” package. Genetic distances and pairwise Weir and Cockerham FST values for regional comparisons were calculated using the hierfstat package. Population structure analysis was assessed using the STRUCTURE 2.3.4 software package. A Bayesian model-based clustering approach was employed to determine the number of subpopulations (K) while considering an admixture model and correlated allele frequencies. Each estimated K value was subjected to 10 independent runs. The Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) burn-in period was set to 10,000 iterations, followed by an additional 10,000 MCMC iterations. The most appropriate K value was determined using the delta K statistic proposed by Evanno et al. [24], which was estimated using STRUCTURE HARVESTER [25]. Individuals in this study were assigned to a cluster if at least 75% of their genome was estimated to belong to that cluster.

Protein extraction

The seeds were stored under refrigeration and placed in a 2 mL tube. Two 5 mm steel beads were added, and the sample was ground using a Tissuelyser II (Qiagen, Germany) with 30 s of vibration per minute for 2 min. This powder was dissolved in 1 mL of urea extraction buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl buffer, pH 8, and 20 mM CaCl2) at room temperature on a shaker for 30 min. After centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 30 min, the supernatant was separated using a pipette, and the concentration was measured using a nanodrop (Denovix, USA).

SDS-PAGE and cluster analyses

SDS gels were prepared using 5 x SDS sample buffer (SDW, 30% acrylamide, 1.5 M Tris pH 8.8, 10% ammonium persulfate, TEMED). Protein samples were incubated at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by electrophoresis on a 12% SDS-PAGE 1 mm gel using Tris–glycine electrophoresis buffer. Electrophoresis was conducted at 100 V for 120 min. After SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, Coomassie Brilliant Blue R250 staining was carried out for 5 h, followed by immersion in a destaining buffer composed of a mix of H2O, ethanol, and acetic acid in a 5:4:1 ratio overnight. Bands were scored as either present (1) or absent (0). The analysis of protein patterns was conducted using the R packages “cluster” and “factoextra”. The optimal K value was determined using the average silhouette method validation index to estimate the number of clusters. After selecting the optimal number of clusters, K-means clustering analysis was conducted, and the clustering results were subsequently visualized.

Results

SNP identification and genotyping

A total of 284 A. fatua individuals were sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq X platform. After the primary quality filtering step, 2,230,696,802 reads were generated, with 2,021,401,404 clean reads. These clean reads were aligned to the reference genome sequence (A. sativa OT3098 v2, GrainGenes), achieving a mapping rate of 95.64%. A total of 5,060,907 SNPs were obtained with a minimal set of initial quality filters. Additional filtering to remove loci with more than 5% missing data and samples with more than 5% missing values was performed, along with excluding groups with fewer than 10 individuals, resulted in a final dataset of 237 individuals and 20,836 SNPs. Various genetic diversity measures were subsequently calculated.

The genetic diversity indices of 237 A. fatua individuals by region are presented in Table 2. The expected heterozygosity (He) ranged from 0.039 (S03) to 0.415 (S01), with an average of 0.188, while the observed heterozygosity (Ho) ranged from 0.061 (S02) to 0.088 (S06), with an average of 0.077. As A. fatua is predominantly self-pollinated and highly inbred, the observed heterozygosity (Ho) was consistently low across all regions, which is in line with its mating system. The expected heterozygosity (He) presented here should be interpreted as a gene diversity index derived from allele frequencies rather than as a true expectation under random mating. Notably, in S03, S06, and S07, Ho values were slightly higher than He values; however, this pattern is most likely attributable to the presence of low-frequency alleles within these populations rather than to a genuine excess of heterozygosity. The chromosome distribution of SNPs derived from the GBS analysis was confirmed to be evenly spread across the 21 chromosomes (Fig. 2). A total of 20,836 SNPs were identified in the A, C, and D genomes. The C genome contained the highest number of SNPs (8484, ~ 41%), followed by the D genome (7092, ~ 34%), and the A genome, which had the fewest polymorphic markers with 5110 (~ 25%). Additionally, 150 SNPs were located in scaffolds not anchored to any chromosome. In the A genome, the highest number of SNPs (1534, ~ 7.4%) was found on chromosome 4 A, while the fewest SNPs (332, ~ 1.6%) were found on chromosome 3 A. In the C genome, the highest number of SNPs (2014, ~ 9.7%) was observed on chromosome 6 C, and the fewest (620, ~ 3%) on chromosome 1 C. The highest and lowest frequencies in the D genome were observed in 4D and 6D, respectively, with 1748 (~ 8.4%) and 322 (~ 1.5%) markers. The maximum number of SNPs observed on a single chromosome was 2014 on chromosome 6 C. The genetic diversity indices for each A. fatua genome are presented in Table 3. The He for each chromosome ranged from 0.153 to 0.276, with an average of 0.19, while the Ho ranged from 0.017 to 0.22, with an average of 0.087. Excluding ChrUn, the highest He value was observed on chromosome 4 A, while the highest Ho value was found on chromosome 4D. The AMOVA of samples collected from the eight regions showed that 59.1% of the genetic variation was within populations, while 40.9% was observed among populations (Table 4). The Phi value, which indicates the degree of genetic differentiation among populations, was 0.41.

Population structure, genetic differentiation, and geographic distance

Selecting the appropriate number of groups to represent the genetic variation of the collected individuals is a critical step, and the DAPC method was used to infer this by assigning individuals to clusters and visually assessing the genetic structure among populations. In total, 200 principal components (PCs) were included in the data preprocessing in the DAPC analysis, which accounted for 99% of the total genetic variation. Generally, many studies have employed PCs that preserve over 80% of the genetic variance; hence, finding a balance between the ability of the DAPC analysis to distinguish clusters and the stability of genotypes assigned to each cluster is essential. Therefore, using 200 PCs in this analysis ensures high statistical power when evaluating the genetic structure of A. fatua individuals [26]. According to the DAPC results, the 237 A. fatua individuals were clustered into two groups (Fig. 3a). In the STRUCTURE analysis, the optimal number of clusters was determined to be K = 2 based on the delta K method (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Fig. 2). At K = 2, individuals were clustered similarly to the DAPC groups. The AMOVA conducted on the 237 A. fatua individuals, divided into two DAPC groups (K = 2), revealed that 70.9% of the genetic variation was between populations, while 29.1% was within (Table 4). The degree of genetic differentiation among populations, indicated by the FST value, was 0.71. This result was consistent with the findings obtained from the STRUCTURE clustering analysis. The distribution ratios of STRUCTURE clusters were calculated to investigate the clustering relationships by region further, and the geographic distribution was mapped onto the national map (Fig. 4). Additionally, admixture individuals were grouped separately from the two subgroups. Cluster 1 was assigned to the S01, S02, and S04 populations, while the remaining populations were assigned to Cluster 2. Admixture individuals were particularly prevalent in the S01 population. The genetic differentiation coefficient is used to measure population structure. In this study, the genetic differentiation coefficient across the eight regions ranged from 0.122 to 0.795 (Fig. 5). The level of genetic differentiation between regions varied significantly, with the highest genetic divergence (FST = 0.795) observed between the S03 and S07 regions; meanwhile, the lowest FST value (FST = 0.122) was found between the S01 and S02 regions. A Mantel test was performed to understand the effect of geographic distance on genetic differentiation. The results revealed a significant correlation between geographic distance and genetic differentiation, with a correlation coefficient of R = 0.54 (P < 0.001) as geographic distance increased (Fig. 6).

Seed storage proteins cluster analysis

Seed storage protein (SSP) bands were confirmed through SDS-PAGE analysis (Fig. 7). A total of 26 bands were detected, with molecular weights ranging from 15 kDa to 120 kDa. The 50 kDa globulin band exhibited variation among individuals, whereas the 70 kDa albumin band was present in all individuals. K-means clustering was performed to partition the 237 individuals into K clusters, assigning each individual to the cluster with the nearest mean value. The optimal number of clusters (K) was determined to be two based on the silhouette method [21, 27]. Each individual was assigned to the cluster center with the shortest distance, and the clustering explained 32.5% of the total data variance. No consistent trend was observed in the distribution of individuals across clusters when comparing the clusters based on protein patterns with those based on genetic variation (Fig. 8).

Discussion

Distribution of A. fatua habitats in South Korea

Genetic diversity plays a crucial role in species survival and adaptation, as genetic variation is necessary for organisms to modify their phenotypes and thrive under unfavorable conditions. Thus, species with higher genetic diversity have more variations available for selection, enhancing their adaptability. Therefore, identifying diverse parental lines is essential to broaden the genetic basis of breeding programs [28]. This study collected 237 wild oat A. fatua individuals from eight regions along the southwestern coast of South Korea to assess regional genetic diversity using GBS and SDS-PAGE analyses. Several studies have reported that key factors influencing the genetic structure of plant populations include mating and reproductive systems, selection or adaptation, and geographical range [29]. Field observations during collection revealed that populations in Yamido and Gunsan were found in a large open area adjacent to the seaside. In contrast, in Seonyudo, populations were located in crevices of barren rocks. In Jindo, populations were growing within a windbreak forest in front of Gagye Beach, while in Yeosu, populations were found along the riverside (Supplementary Fig. 1). These findings indicate that populations can thrive in both dry and wet environments, as well as in areas influenced by salinity. Moreover, growth in such diverse habitats suggests these species can survive well even in environments beyond their previously known ecological range [12]. This supports the findings of the present study, which indicate that the genetic diversity of A. fatua varies across different regions. Furthermore, these findings infer that this species exhibits high adaptability to diverse environments, as A. fatua seeds have been reported to maintain similar growth rates regardless of nutrient availability or soil pH, whether alkaline or acidic [12].

Genetic diversity, differentiation, and population structure

We identified 20,836 SNP markers using GBS, with the highest polymorphic marker frequency observed in the 4D and 6 C genomes. Population clustering analysis revealed that A. fatua genotypes largely clustered into a single group, except for Mokpo (S01), Yeongam (S02), and Buan (S04). DAPC analysis exhibited similar clustering trends. Internationally, A. fatua is considered a weedy oat that negatively impacts cultivated crops. According to Coffman [30], hexaploid A. fatua originated in Asia. Meanwhile, agricultural activities in Korea began in the early Neolithic period, primarily centered around rice cultivation.

The spread of rice agriculture in Korea supports the hypothesis of a northern China route, suggesting that it was introduced to the Korean Peninsula around 4300 BP via coastal regions of the Shandong or Liaodong Peninsulas. This indicates that agricultural practices in the region began during the early Neolithic period. Pollen analysis further suggests that rice cultivation may have first emerged along the southern coast of Korea, initially introduced to the southwestern coastal areas and subsequently spreading throughout the southern coastline [31]. Along this route, A. fatua is presumed to have been introduced as a weedy oat species in association with the spread of agriculture, gradually dispersing from the southwestern coast.

Pairwise FST analysis presented values ranging from 0.122 to 0.795. An FST value between populations between 0 and 0.05 indicates no genetic discrimination among populations [32]. Conversely, if the FST value is between 0.05 and 0.15, it suggests moderate differentiation; values above 0.15 indicate significant differentiation [33]. The lowest differentiation was observed between Mokpo (S01) and Yeongam (S02), as well as between Buan (S04) and Gunsan (S05), likely due to their geographical proximity. The isolation by distance analysis results confirmed a significant correlation between genetic divergence and geographical distance. Prior studies on wild wheat have shown that despite originating from different countries, genetically close populations exist, highlighting that relative proximity facilitates gene flow and dispersal [34]. Additionally, A. fatua seeds are known for their ecological ability to disperse long distances after dehiscence [10], which may explain genetic similarity among neighboring regions.

Jindo (S03) exhibited the highest genetic differentiation. A. sativa, a grass crop native to Central Asia, is believed to have been introduced to South Korea during the Goryeo Dynasty [35]. Due to its poor winter hardiness, oat cultivation in South Korea has been primarily limited to Jeju and southern regions [36, 37]. Jindo, located at the southernmost point among the study sites, was relatively isolated from inland areas until the construction of the Jindo Bridge in 1984, suggesting that the local genotype may have been preserved and adapted to this specific environment. Genetic resources from small, isolated wild populations are critical for conservation and breeding [38]. Most domestic oat genetic resources have been lost, leading to approximately 40 accessions being reintroduced from the United States and Japan for preservation efforts [39]. Furthermore, genetic pools of A. sativa in countries such as Canada and China are reported to be relatively narrow [40, 41]. Since hexaploid species within the Avena genus lack crossing barriers, these species can be readily hybridized [12], making A. fatua a valuable genetic resource for future breeding efforts.

Seed storage protein pattern analysis

Seed storage proteins are relatively stable and are used to classify plant varieties based on protein banding patterns [42, 43]. Moreover, these proteins are employed to assess genetic diversity [44, 45]. As storage proteins are direct gene products and largely independent of environmental influences [46], these proteins are reliable markers for evaluating genetic variation among wild populations [47, 48]. SDS-PAGE analysis revealed a broad range of high-molecular-weight protein profiles in A. fatua from 15 kDa to 120 kDa. This was consistent with previous findings, which reported oat protein electrophoresis bands ranging from 12 to 98 kDa [49]. The 50 kDa globulin band varied among individuals, while Nieto-Nieto et al. [50] identified 7 S globulin as a polypeptide in the 55 kDa range, with minor fractions at 65 kDa. Although faint, the 70 kDa albumin band was present in all individuals, corroborating findings that it is one of the major protein groups in various oat genotypes [51–53]. Clustering analysis was conducted to assess individual diversity using seed storage protein patterns as a trait. An evaluation of the relationships with genotype-based variation groups revealed an overall admixture, and no significant correlation was observed between the GBS analysis results and protein patterns. However, Briggle and Youngs [54] reported that wild oats exhibit high seed protein content. While the reasons for population differences related to habitat remain largely unknown, previous studies have suggested that variations in seed maturation conditions across different regions may contribute to differences in seed storage protein expression [55].

Conclusions

This study provides one of the few insights into the genetic diversity of A. fatua in South Korea. Here, the genetic diversity of A. fatua was assessed using 237 individuals collected from eight regions in the southwestern coastal area. Clustering analysis based on genotypic variation classified the sampled populations into two groups, and clustering based on seed storage protein patterns also resulted in two distinct groups. However, no significant association was observed between the groups derived from genetic analysis and those based on phenotypic characteristics. Previous studies have reported that seed storage protein variation mainly reflects interspecific differences and shows limited intraspecific diversity, often with weak or inconsistent correlations to DNA-based markers [15]. This supports our observation that no significant correlation was found between GBS and protein-based clustering in A. fatua. The genetic diversity of plant populations is influenced by various factors, including life history traits and population development history [56]. Additionally, reproductive mode and biological characteristics can contribute to variations in genetic diversity, alongside distribution range [57–59]. This study provides fundamental data for exploring the genetic diversity of A. fatua, the wild relative of cultivated oats, with implications for genetic resource conservation. However, to further investigate the population structure and regional genetic diversity of A. fatua, additional analyses on morphological traits, seed production, seed germination, and other phenotypic characteristics are required. Therefore, the findings of this study are expected to contribute to the expansion of the genetic resource pool of cultivated oats in Korea and serve as a basis for future breeding improvements.
