Hybrid multi-scale CNN-Residual-LSTM approach for robust state-of-charge estimation in lithium-ion batteries
Authors: Saadin Oyucu, Onur Polat, Muammer Türkoğlu, Faruk Kürker, Ahmet Aksöz, Emre Biçer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate estimation of State of Charge (SoC) and State of Health (SoH) values in battery management systems is crucial for maintaining energy efficiency, ensuring operational safety, and sustaining battery life. This study proposes a hybrid deep learning (DL) architecture that combines multi-scale convolutional neural networks (CNNs), residual blocks, and long-short-term memory (LSTM) units. The proposed model is designed to learn both local spatial patterns and time-dependent relationships found in battery data. The developed model was trained using real battery datasets, and its performance was evaluated comparatively against traditional machine learning (ML) algorithms such as Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Gradient Boosting (GBM). Model performance was analyzed using common regression metrics such as Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Squared Error (MSE), and Coefficient of Determination (R²). The results show that the proposed model achieves lower prediction errors compared to the methods evaluated (MAE = 0.1507, MSE = 0.2030, R² = 0.9998). Furthermore, scatter plots and residual distribution analyses reveal that the model predictions are quite close to the actual values and that the error distribution does not contain any significant systematic bias. Overall, the results demonstrate that the proposed hybrid architecture can produce accurate and stable results for SoC estimation and offers an approach that could contribute to the development of reliable battery management systems.

Introduction

Li-ion batteries (LIBs) are at the heart of the modern electrification system, being leveraged for electric vehicles (EVs), smart grids, consumer electronics, and renewable energy storage platforms. These have been widely accepted due to their higher energy densities, longer lifespans, and better charge-discharge efficiencies. On the other hand, under real working conditions, where extreme temperatures, rapid load changes, and fast charging come into play, all these put together necessitate being able to robustly estimate the internal state for safety, performance, and predictive maintenance1–5.

Traditional model-based techniques such as equivalent circuit models (ECMs), Coulomb counting, and open-circuit voltage (OCV) methods often exhibit limited accuracy under complex operating conditions, mainly for the reason of their static parameters and propagation-based error generation4,6. In recent years, data-driven DL frameworks have become increasingly prominent. Using large-scale operational datasets, these models readily discern the spatiotemporal interactions between electric and thermal variables. CNN and LSTM-based frameworks, unsurprisingly, carry out the process of extracting hierarchical features while modeling nonlinear degradation dynamics of the battery1,2,7–13.

Development in the recent past led to advanced hybrid models, simultaneously comprising CNNs, Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory Networks (BiLSTMs), attention mechanisms, residual connections, and metaheuristic optimizers such as genetic algorithms (GAs), particle swarm optimization (PSO), and wasserstein generative adversarial network (WGAN) augmentation14–20. These integrated frameworks provide improved adaptability to battery chemistries, C-rates, and ambient conditions. Noise reduction and emphasizing signal relevance were also ensured by preprocessing techniques such as complete ensemble empirical mode decomposition with adaptive noise (CEEMDAN), grey relational analysis, and autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) filtering to bring forth model robustness18,21–23.

Emerging trends further point to the use of transfer learning24, uncertainty-aware estimation2, graph-based architectures such as graph convolutional networks (GCNs)25, along with joint multi-output learning2,26. The increased profusion of public (e.g., NASA’s prognostics center of excellence (NASA PCoE), center for advanced life cycle engineering (CALCE)) as well as proprietary electric vehicle (EV) datasets6,26,27 allows further reproducibility and generalization on several use cases. Apart from purely data-driven approaches, recent studies have shown a growing interest in hybrid modeling approaches involving both electrochemical models and artificial intelligence. This class of AI-assisted electrochemical models combines the advantages of physically based models such as interpretation and consistency with those of data-driven models, namely adaptability and learning capabilities. Such a paradigm is able to identify dynamic parameters, ensure better tracking accuracy, and increase robustness under different conditions. Recent reviews demonstrate that the combination of physical models and machine learning can help bridge the gap between conventional modeling and black-box deep learning techniques in battery state estimation28.

Filling this context gap, the present review study synthesizes 31 DL models proposed between 2020 and 2025 for the estimation of SoC, SoH, and remaining useful life (RUL). Table 1 is a comparative view based on the type of model, dataset properties, optimization procedure, and major findings. Such a systematic analysis can distill important trends in methodology and inform further research toward establishing robust and scalable battery diagnostic techniques.

Despite recent advances, three critical gaps currently remain in the DL-based battery estimation models: (1) fragmented architectural designs that confine themselves to either CNNs or LSTMs without proper integration of multi-scale spatial representation and residual refinement; (2) degraded generalization due to training against narrowly scoped datasets, commonly fixed to certain C-rates, chemistries, or temperature ranges; and (3) an established structured benchmarking in terms of model architectures, datasets, and performance metrics does not exist, which undermines reproducibility of results and their deployment into BMSs.

In response to such problems, a novel Multi-Scale CNN–Residual–LSTM architecture is proposed in this study to enable modeling of nonlinear battery degradation by integrating multi-resolution convolutional layers, Swish-activated residual blocks, and LSTM-based temporal modeling. The adopted validation strategy is based on the CALCE dataset, which was collected under controlled laboratory conditions using lithium cobalt oxide (LCO) batteries. While this dataset provides reliable and high-quality measurements, it does not include highly dynamic real-world driving cycles such as the urban dynamometer driving schedule (UDDS), US06 supplemental federal test procedure (US06), or beijing bus dynamic stress test (BBDST). Therefore, the generalizability of the model under real-world driving conditions remains a subject for future work. This limitation is clearly stated, and future work is planned to focus on validating the model using datasets that incorporate real-world dynamic driving conditions and different battery chemistries. Furthermore, the paper synthesizes a benchmark of 31 DL models from 2020 to 2025, allowing for standardized comparison and guiding practical deployment. All these contributions lay down the foundations for interpretable, scalable, and real-time SoC prediction systems for next-generation energy storage. The framework thus stands for both methodological and practical advancement, where it is integrated into BMS environments.

Battery state estimation under different operating conditions presents significant challenges in terms of time series analysis. This integrated approach aims to overcome fundamental limitations observed in traditional CNN-based methods, such as the fragmented handling of spatial and temporal features and limited generalization ability under different usage profiles.

Its design supports stable performance even under fluctuating thermal or operational regimes, making it potentially applicable to real-world battery management systems, subject to further validation under realistic operating conditions. By balancing short-term feature responsiveness with long-term degradation tracking, the model contributes to more resilient and accurate SoC predictions.

Related work

DL techniques in the field of battery state estimation have shown significant improvements compared to traditional methods. Models proposed in recent years are increasingly being used in battery management systems, particularly due to their ability to model complex nonlinear degradation processes. In this section, the current literature has been classified based on the main methodological approaches in order to examine architectural trends more systematically.

Multi-scale CNN-based approaches

CNNs are widely used in battery state estimation studies due to their ability to capture spatial features at different scales. Liu et al.1 proposed a hybrid CNN–LSTM model that considers the effect of temperature changes and demonstrated that this model significantly reduces SoC estimation error. Similarly, He and Gong4 used multi-branch CNN architectures enhanced with residual connections and attention mechanisms to improve SoH estimation accuracy under fast charging protocols.

These studies demonstrate that multi-scale CNN structures can effectively capture the spatial features that emerge under different battery operating conditions. However, approaches based solely on convolutional layers often fail to adequately represent the temporal nature of battery degradation and therefore require support from sequential modeling components.

Sequential models incorporating attention mechanisms

Attention mechanisms have become a key component in modern deep learning models by dynamically weighting important features in time series data. Qian et al.2 proposed a CNN–SAM–LSTM architecture that simultaneously performs SoC, SoE, and SoH estimation using an uncertainty-aware loss function. Tian et al.17, Lei et al.19, and Hong et al.29 demonstrated that attention mechanisms integrated into BiLSTM or GRU-based models enable more effective learning of temporal features.

Additionally, Sherkatghanad et al.14 and Oza et al.30 achieved more accurate SoH estimates under dynamic operating conditions using attention-enhanced CNN–BiLSTM models. Oyucu et al.9–13 have shown that CNN–RNN combinations, LSTM, GRU, and stacking approaches provide higher accuracy than traditional regression or single machine learning methods in modeling the complex nonlinear degradation processes of lithium-ion batteries.

Although attention mechanisms significantly improve prediction performance, the increased model complexity and training time associated with these structures can pose practical challenges, particularly in applications with limited computational resources.

Preprocessing and noise reduction strategies

Preprocessing battery data plays a critical role in improving model robustness. Chen et al.23 used the gray relational analysis method to identify important health indicators, while Yang et al.18 and Xie et al.21 applied ARIMA and CEEMDAN-based methods to reduce noise in the signal and make degradation trends more apparent. Such preprocessing steps are typically used in conjunction with hybrid architectures such as CNN–LSTM or BiLSTM.

Techniques such as ARIMA, CEEMDAN, and gray relational analysis contribute to making meaningful features related to battery health more prominent by reducing signal noise. However, most of these methods are tailored to specific datasets, and generalizing them to different battery systems is not always straightforward.

Meta-heuristic optimization and hybrid approaches

In recent years, research has focused on the use of various optimization algorithms to optimize model hyperparameters and combine different model components. Zhou et al.16 used a GA–PSO-based approach to optimize the hyperparameters of the BiLSTM–MLP–AM model, while Liang et al.22 proposed nonlinear constraint solvers to optimize the combination of LSTM and support vector regression (SVR). In addition, Wang and Yang31 used GAN-based data augmentation methods to reduce the limited data set problem.

Although evolutionary algorithms such as GA and PSO are effective in improving model performance, high computational costs and the limited interpretability of convergence processes can make it difficult to use these methods in real-time applications.

Graph-based and collaborative learning frameworks

Some studies have focused on graph-based or multi-output learning frameworks to model the relationships between variables in battery data. Wei and Wu25 proposed a graph convolutional network (GCN) model with a dual attention mechanism for SOH and RUL estimation. Similarly, Xiong et al.26 and Zhang et al.27 developed models that combine CNN and LSTM architectures with relational structures or Kolmogorov–Arnold networks (KAN) to achieve stronger feature abstraction on missing or multi-source data.

Graph-based and multi-output models can deliver effective results in the analysis of complex or fragmented battery data. However, the high structural complexity of these architectures and the lack of standardized evaluation frameworks pose some challenges for practical system integration.

Dataset usage and benchmarking practices

The advancement of deep learning (DL)-based battery diagnostics research has become increasingly dependent on access to higher-quality and more diverse datasets and the adoption of structured benchmarking protocols. Widely used open data sources such as the NASA Prognostics Center of Excellence (PCoE) dataset17, the CALCE battery dataset4, and various electric vehicle (EV) datasets6,26 provide important testing grounds for validating the robustness of models under realistic operating conditions. These datasets cover different battery chemistries (LFP, NMC, LCO, in this study, LCO4, temperature ranges, C-rate values, and driving cycles (UDDS, US06, and Highway Fuel Economy Test – HWFET). This diversity enables the development of generalizable and transferable models across different battery systems.

However, comparative evaluation practices have also become more standardized over time. Model performance is typically evaluated using metrics such as root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), coefficient of determination (R²), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) under different test scenarios2,3,14,16. In addition, the consistency of preprocessing strategies is also of great importance for making fair comparisons between models. In this context, methods such as grey relational analysis23, autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) filtering18, and CEEMDAN-based signal decomposition21 are widely used to identify health indicators and reduce noise-induced effects prior to model training. Such procedures help ensure that performance differences between models stem from architectural features rather than differences in the dataset or preprocessing.

To enable a comprehensive comparative evaluation, this study examined 31 deep learning models published between 2020 and 2025, which are summarized in Table 1. Each model was evaluated in terms of the dataset used, architectural structure, optimization method, and reported performance results. High-performance approaches, such as CNN–BiLSTM–AM14, ResNet–LSTM32, and GCN models with dual attention mechanisms25, demonstrate better generalizability when trained and validated on heterogeneous datasets containing both controlled laboratory data and real electric vehicle data.

When these studies are evaluated together, a clear trend is observed in the literature towards integrated and modular architectures that can capture the spatio-temporal degradation patterns emerging under different battery chemistries and operating conditions, moving away from singular model designs.

Between 2020 and 2025, researchers developed various deep learning architectures for battery state estimation. Figure 3 summarizes the architectural diversity in the literature by classifying 31 of these studies into five main categories. These categories include standard CNN–LSTM models, structures enhanced with attention mechanisms, architectures incorporating residual connections such as ResNet–LSTM, graph-based networks such as GCN, and approaches using preprocessing techniques such as DAE, ARIMA, or CEEMDAN. This classification demonstrates how architectural design preferences have evolved over time.

Summary of findings from comparative models in Table 1

The comparative evaluation of the 31 studies listed in Table 1 reveals some significant trends in the development of deep learning-based battery state estimation models.

Dominance of Model Architectures: CNN–LSTM hybrid architectures still constitute the most widely used framework in the literature. These architectures are often enhanced with attention mechanisms30, residual connections32, or preprocessing modules such as ARIMA and CEEMDAN18,21.

Resilience Provided Through Hybridization: Hybrid temporal–spatial models (e.g., CNN + BiLSTM), when tuned with optimization methods such as GA, PSO, or SMA, generally perform better than single models and exhibit lower variability in RMSE and MAE values under different driving cycles and temperature conditions.

Feature Selection and Noise Reduction: Grey relational analysis23, PCC-based filtering30, and decomposition-based noise reduction methods such as ARIMA and CEEMDAN are widely used to highlight meaningful features in the signal and increase prediction stability.

Data Set Diversity and Generalizability: Models with higher performance are typically validated on multiple data sets. These data sets include sources such as NASA, CALCE, and real electric vehicle records; they also cover different battery chemistries (LFP, NMC, LCO) and different operating conditions such as fast charging, high C-rate values, or wide temperature ranges.

Emergence of Advanced Architectural Approaches: In recent years, new approaches such as graph-based modeling approaches (e.g., GCN)25, quantile regression methods15, and interval-based prediction frameworks incorporating uncertainty quantification24 have gained attention. This trend indicates that the research community is moving towards more interpretable and flexible solutions. Furthermore, explainable AI tools such as SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations) are increasingly being used to enhance model reliability and diagnostic interpretability in critical applications.

In general, it is observed that the highest performance in SoC, SoH, and RUL estimation is achieved not only by increasing architectural complexity but also by the combined use of preprocessing methods, feature abstraction techniques, generalization strategies, and systematic comparative evaluation approaches.

Summary of DL-based state estimation methods for lithium-ion batteries.

As shown in Table 1, there are various developments that determine the direction of research in the field of battery state estimation. Foremost among these is the widespread use of CNN–LSTM architectures enhanced with attention mechanisms30, uncertainty-aware loss functions2, or physics-based preprocessing methods such as CEEMDAN21 and ARIMA18. More recently, transfer learning23, GAN-based methods for data augmentation31, and graph-based relational modeling approaches25 have been gaining increasing attention. This trend indicates a shift towards more generalizable and scalable model designs.

Many studies validate the robustness of their models by testing them on open-source datasets (NASA, CALCE) or specialized electric vehicle datasets. These findings show that performance gains stem not only from model complexity but also from careful feature selection, interpretability improvements, and dataset diversity. Recent studies also focus on integrating hybrid approaches that include domain adaptation and semantic consistency objectives33, while some studies aim to develop lightweight architectures that can run on edge devices while maintaining prediction accuracy34.

It should be noted that the studies summarized in Table 1 are conducted under different experimental conditions, including variations in datasets, preprocessing techniques, train–test splitting strategies, and evaluation metrics. Therefore, direct numerical comparison of reported performance values across these studies may not always be strictly valid. The primary purpose of this comparative analysis is to highlight general methodological trends, architectural developments, and performance ranges rather than to provide a one-to-one benchmark comparison.

Critical analysis of comparative models: Strengths and limitations

Table 1 presents a comparative summary that provides a structured overview of 31 state-of-the-art deep learning (DL) models proposed between 2020 and 2025. However, a critical and analytical evaluation requires a more in-depth examination of the methodological foundations of these models and their limitations, which are still open to debate in the literature.

Strengths: Many models in the literature demonstrate quite high prediction performance under different battery types and operating conditions. For example, the CNN–BiLSTM–attention mechanism (AM) models proposed by Sherkatghanad et al.14 and Tian et al.17 can achieve RMSE values below 2% thanks to attention mechanisms that more effectively weight important features in time series data. Similarly, ResNet–LSTM hybrid models32,35 reduce the vanishing gradient problem observed in deep networks, enabling more effective extraction of spatial features related to decay.

Furthermore, graph-based models such as GCN–Dual Attention25 offer stronger generalization capabilities by explicitly modeling the relationships between variables. Furthermore, uncertainty-aware models such as CNN–self-attention mechanism (SAM)–LSTM2 and BiLSTM–stochastic differential equation (SDE)–transfer learning (TL)24 provide significant contributions in terms of interpretability and risk assessment. Hybrid architectures such as CNN–BiLSTM have also improved model robustness by enhancing long-term prediction performance on different battery datasets36. Furthermore, some studies have achieved additional performance improvements by convolutional encoding of charge curve segments followed by LSTM-based sequential modeling37.

Furthermore, feature extraction during the preprocessing stage stands out as a significant strength. For example, gray relational analysis23 and CEEMDAN-based decomposition21 have been found to be highly effective in reducing noise and highlighting meaningful features in the signal. These methods particularly enhance model robustness in real-world datasets.

Limitations: Despite these developments, the models in the literature still face some significant limitations. The main ones are overfitting, scalability, and generalizability issues. For example, models trained only on NASA or CALCE datasets may not perform as well in real electric vehicle systems where factors such as temperature profiles, C-rate values, or sensor accuracy vary.

On the other hand, while models incorporating attention mechanisms offer performance advantages, these structures require a large number of hyperparameters. This situation can make it difficult to apply model compression or pruning strategies, especially in environments with limited computational resources, such as battery management systems (BMS).

Similarly, models based on meta-heuristic optimization methods such as GA–PSO16 or the fuzzy firefly algorithm (FuFi)3 typically have high computational costs, and the convergence behavior of the optimization process is often insufficiently transparent.

Furthermore, very few studies in the literature explicitly address model robustness against problems commonly encountered in real systems, such as missing data or sensor failures. Although explainable AI methods such as SHAP and LIME have been used in some studies for model interpretability, these approaches are mostly limited to visual explanations and do not provide a statistically robust interpretation framework.

However, recent studies have begun to take important steps toward addressing this shortcoming by using methods such as Monte Carlo-based uncertainty quantification and prediction interval generation within CNN–LSTM structures37.

Research Gaps and Future Directions: A significant portion of the studies in the literature do not perform uncertainty quantification for prediction outputs. However, this is a critical requirement, especially in battery management systems that are critical for safety. Furthermore, the number of models using transfer learning is still quite limited24,26. Transfer learning methods have the potential to increase the generalizability of models by reducing data dependency between different battery chemistries or manufacturers.

Although comparative evaluation studies have increased, a fully standardized benchmarking framework has not yet emerged in the literature. The lack of a common standard in terms of model architectures, data set splitting strategies, performance metrics, and preprocessing protocols makes the reproducibility of studies difficult and complicates direct comparisons between models.

This study was developed to address these recurring gaps in the literature. When the 31 deep learning models examined were evaluated, three fundamental problems emerged: many models address spatial and temporal patterns separately, they can experience performance degradation under different battery types, and evaluation protocols are often inconsistent.

To address these issues, a CNN–Residual–LSTM architecture was designed, combining multi-scale spatial filtering, residual connections with the Swish activation function, and LSTM-based temporal modeling. The model structure adapts effective components from previous works such as CNN–BiLSTM–AM14, ResNet–LSTM32, and GCN–Dual Attention25, and is applied on different datasets containing real electric vehicle data logs from NASA and CALCE.

The model not only produces stable predictions but also contributes to a fairer comparison of different methods. All experiments were conducted using the same performance metrics (RMSE, MAE, R², etc.) and the same data splitting strategies. Another frequently encountered shortcoming in the literature is the absence of uncertainty estimates in prediction outputs. Therefore, the study encourages the use of techniques such as dropout-based inference or ensemble variance to increase model reliability.

In conclusion, the proposed model does not aim to present a completely new architecture; instead, it presents a combination of components proven to be effective in the literature, validated on large datasets, and improved in terms of interpretability and robustness. This approach aims to contribute to the development of more reliable and generalizable deep learning models in battery diagnosis applications.

Whereas the comparative summary in Table 1 presents a structured overview of 31 state-of-the-art DL models put forth between 2020 and 2025, the critical reflective and analytical discussion will cut deeper into the methodological underpinnings and limitations deserving of further discourse.

Strengths

Several models exhibit outstanding predictive performance across a range of battery types and operating conditions. For instance, the CNN–BiLSTM–attention mechanism (AM) models proposed by Sherkatghanad et al.14 and Tian et al.17 consistently achieve sub-2% RMSE, aided by attention mechanisms that improve temporal feature relevance. Also, ResNet–LSTM hybrids32,35 resolve the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks so that degradation-related spatial features can be extracted effectively. Hence, Graph-based models such as GCN–Dual Attention25 generalize well by explicitly modeling the inter-variable dependencies, while uncertainty-aware models such as CNN–self-attention mechanism (SAM)–LSTM2 and BiLSTM–stochastic differential equation (SDE)–transfer learning (TL)24 bring interpretability and risk estimation forth. In addition, hybrid architectures such as CNN–BiLSTM have demonstrated enhanced robustness and long-term prediction capability across multiple battery datasets36, while further improvements have been achieved through convolutional encoding of charging curve segments followed by sequential LSTM layers37.

Furthermore, feature extraction has risen as one of the strengths in pre-processing. For example, grey relational analysis23 and CEEMDAN-based decomposition21 have been shown to be very effective in noise reduction and maximizing signal relevance, thus ensuring higher stability of the model on real-world datasets.

Limitations

In spite of these developments, mechanisms in the literature still have to face overfitting issues, scalability, or transferability problems. For example, if a model is trained strictly on the NASA or CALCE datasets, it may not necessarily perform well on field-deployed EV systems due to differences in temperature profiles, differences in C-rates, or just differences in sensor fidelity. On another note, whilst attention-augmented solutions are welcomed, they increase the number of eligible hyperparameters to the extent that such methods cannot be readily organized given an effective time frame into strategies of compression or pruning suitable for battery management system (BMS) deployment.

In the same fashion, those optimization algorithms based heavily on metaheuristics, including, but not limited to, GA–PSO16 and fuzzy firefly algorithm (FuFi)3, often provide no transparency in tuning convergences while requesting large computational capabilities. Very few works explicitly address robustness to missing data or sensor faults, yet these are very common issues in real scenarios. SHAP and LIME have been mentioned for post-hoc interpretations in some works; however, their use is limited and remains more illustrative than statistically founded. Nonetheless, recent attempts have introduced MC-based quantification and prediction interval estimation within CNN–LSTM structures37, which begin to address the statistical interpretability gap of earlier post-hoc explainability tools.

Gaps and future directions

A persistent limitation in many of the studies is that there is no uncertainty quantification provided on the prediction outputs, while this proves critical, especially in safety systems such as BMS. Moreover, only a very few models employ transfer learning24,26, which could bring down data dependency and generalizability issues across battery chemistries and manufacturers.

While decent progress has been made in benchmarking, a truly standardized framework has not yet emerged across the literature, in which model architecture templates, dataset partitions, performance metrics, and preprocessing protocols would span. This would hinder reproducibility and short-circuit model comparisons between one another.

This study was developed in response to recurring gaps observed in earlier research. After reviewing 31 DL models, three key issues became clear: many of the models treat spatial and temporal patterns separately, their performance drops under varying battery types, and their evaluation setups are often inconsistent. To address these points, we designed a CNN–Residual–LSTM model that brings together multiscale spatial filtering, residual pathways with Swish activation, and temporal analysis through LSTM layers. The model structure adapts effective parts from earlier designs-such as CNN–BiLSTM–AM14, ResNet–LSTM32, and GCN–Dual Attention25 and applies them across a range of datasets, including NASA, CALCE, and EV logs collected under real-world conditions.

In addition to achieving stable predictions, the model improves how different methods can be compared. All experiments follow the same metric set (e.g., RMSE, MAE, R²) and use identical data partitions to ensure fairness. Another issue found in earlier works is the absence of uncertainty estimates in the output, even though this is crucial for battery safety. This study encourages the use of techniques like dropout inference or ensemble variance to add that missing layer of reliability. The model does not aim to be a completely new architecture but rather offers a tested combination of proven elements adapted, extended, and validated for broader use in battery diagnostics where interpretability and robustness are essential.

The analysis by Santurkar et al. revealed that the success of batch normalization (BN) is more related to making the loss function of the network smoother than reducing the “internal covariate shift”. The long short-term memory

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks were specifically designed to address the vanishing gradient problem encountered in conventional Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), thereby enabling the effective modeling of long-term dependencies in sequential data48,49. Unlike standard RNNs, LSTM introduces a memory cell structure that can preserve information over extended time intervals, making it particularly suitable for time-series modeling tasks such as battery state estimation.

The core functionality of LSTM relies on a set of gating mechanisms—namely, the forget gate, input gate, and output gate—which regulate the flow of information into, within, and out of the memory cell50. These gates allow the network to selectively retain relevant historical information, incorporate new inputs, and generate context-aware outputs, thus enhancing both learning stability and representational capacity.

In convolutional neural networks, the multi-dimensional feature maps obtained from convolutional layers are transformed into a one-dimensional representation through a flattening operation53. where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:W\in\:{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times\:n}$$\end{document}and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:b\in\:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}$$\end{document}denote the weight matrix and bias vector, respectively, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:f(\cdot\:)$$\end{document}is an activation function, typically the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU). Activation functions

Activation functions introduce nonlinearity into deep neural networks, enabling the modeling of complex patterns in data. In this study, both Swish and ReLU activation functions are utilized due to their complementary properties.

The Swish activation function is defined as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Swish\left(x\right)=x\:\cdot\:\sigma\:\left(\beta\:x\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upsigma\:}\left(\cdot\:\right)\:$$\end{document}denotes the sigmoid function and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upbeta\:}$$\end{document} is a fixed or learnable parameter54. Swish is smooth and continuously differentiable, which facilitates stable gradient flow during backpropagation. Unlike ReLU, it allows small negative values to pass through, thereby reducing the risk of inactive (“dead”) neurons and enabling richer feature representations55,56.

The Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU), defined as:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:ReLU\left(x\right)=max(0,x)$$\end{document}

is widely used due to its simplicity and computational efficiency57–59. It effectively mitigates the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks by preserving positive activations, although it may suffer from zero-gradient regions for negative inputs.

In the proposed model, Swish is primarily employed within residual blocks to enhance feature learning through smoother optimization dynamics, while ReLU is used in fully connected layers to maintain computational efficiency. This combined usage leverages the advantages of both activation functions, contributing to improved model stability and generalization performance60.

Implementation details

Before training the model, all input features were normalized to the range [0, 1] using the MinMaxScaler. The normalization parameters were computed from the training set only and then applied to the test set to prevent data leakage. Using a random hold-out strategy, the dataset was divided into training and test subsets with an 80%-20% split. During training, 20% of the training data was reserved as a validation set to monitor the model’s generalization performance. This validation split was performed once and used consistently across all experimental runs to ensure comparability. Although the dataset was randomly partitioned, the original sample indices were preserved and used during the evaluation phase. Specifically, the test samples were reordered according to their original temporal/sample index when generating SOC estimation curves and related visualizations. This approach allows for a realistic, time-indexed interpretation of the model predictions while maintaining the benefits of random data splitting. Consequently, the results were ensured to be both statistically reliable and temporally interpretable.

Two basic early stopping strategies were used to reduce the risk of overfitting during training. With the EarlyStopping method, training was terminated when no improvement in verification loss was observed for 75 consecutive epochs, and with the ReduceLROnPlateau mechanism, the learning rate was halved if the verification performance did not improve for 15 epochs. In this way, the model was stopped in case of early saturation, and more precise optimization was achieved.

For the optimization of the model, the Adam algorithm was preferred, and MSE was used as the loss function. Although the maximum training time was set to 2000 epochs, training was usually completed earlier. After the training was completed, predictions were generated on the test set using the best weights, and the final performance metrics were calculated based on these predictions.

It should be noted that the proposed model is designed to produce deterministic predictions, and explicit uncertainty quantification mechanisms are not incorporated within the scope of the current implementation. While the achieved performance metrics indicate high predictive accuracy, they do not directly provide information about the confidence of individual predictions. Incorporating uncertainty-aware approaches, such as Monte Carlo dropout or ensemble-based methods, may further enhance the reliability of the model, particularly for safety-critical battery management applications, and is considered as a direction for future work.

Discussion

This study analyzes the performance of the hybrid DL model in comparison with various traditional ML algorithms. Despite the different model structures, the comparison process is rigorously structured to ensure a fair and direct evaluation. In this context, all algorithms were trained and tested on the same dataset with an 80:20 training-to-testing ratio and a fixed random_state parameter. This avoids random deviations due to data fragmentation and increases the validity of the comparisons.

In the data preprocessing process, only numeric variables were considered, and all features were scaled to the range [0, 1] with MinMaxScaler. The normalization process was performed only on the training data, and the same transformation parameters were applied to the test data to avoid data leakage. This approach ensures consistency in comparative analysis between DL architecture and traditional models. All traditional ML algorithms used in the comparative analysis were implemented through the scikit-learn library, and the models were trained with the default parameter values of the respective classes. All models were evaluated using three main performance metrics on the test data: MAE, MSE, and R². The obtained performance values are presented in Table 3.

As shown in Table 3, the comparative results demonstrate that the proposed hybrid DL model outperforms all traditional ML algorithms. In particular, the low MAE and MSE values indicate that the model’s prediction error is quite low. Regarding MAE, the proposed model produced a better result than its closest competitor, the RF Regressor, with an error that is about 34% lower (0.1507 vs. 0.2311). Similarly, the proposed model had about 70% lower MSE, with a value of 0.2030, compared to RF’s value of 0.6732. The R² score of 0.9998 is very close to 1, indicating a very high explanatory capability of the model in capturing the variance of the SoC values. This value exceeds even the R² score of RF, the most successful traditional method, indicating the model’s high overall learning capacity.

In addition to traditional machine learning (ML) models, the comparative analysis included deep learning-based baseline architectures, such as long short-term memory (LSTM), gated recurrent unit (GRU), and one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D CNN), to provide a more comprehensive experimental validation. As shown in Table 3, these models perform relatively well; however, they remain significantly behind the proposed hybrid architecture in all evaluation metrics. For example, the standalone LSTM model yields an MAE of 0.4870 and an MSE of 0.8827, while the GRU model produces similar results (MAE: 0.4983, MSE: 0.8910). This indicates that modeling sequences alone is insufficient to capture the full complexity of SoC dynamics. Similarly, the 1D CNN model, which focuses on spatial feature extraction, outperforms recurrent models but still falls short of the proposed architecture (MAE: 0.4293, MSE: 0.8067). These results clearly show that neither purely temporal nor purely spatial models can individually achieve optimal performance.

The superior performance of the proposed model is due to its hybrid architecture, which effectively integrates multi-scale convolutional feature extraction, residual learning, and temporal sequence modeling. This combination allows the model to capture short-term fluctuations and long-term dependencies in battery behavior, significantly improving prediction accuracy. The consistent improvement observed across all evaluation metrics confirms that the proposed architecture effectively addresses the limitations of both traditional ML approaches and standalone deep learning models. Furthermore, ensemble-based models (RF, BG, GBM, etc.) also remain inferior to the proposed architecture, indicating that the performance gain is not solely due to model complexity but rather the effective integration of complementary learning mechanisms.

The comparisons show that the proposed model can more successfully learn non-linear relationships and interactions while reducing the overall error level. The model demonstrates better overall performance than traditional methods, especially in complex, multidimensional systems. Additionally, the balance between error metrics indicates that the model has a robust structure in terms of general accuracy and generalization capability. Despite these promising results, the reliability of the model must be considered for safety-critical applications such as battery management systems. The proposed architecture in the current study produces deterministic point estimates without explicitly quantifying predictive uncertainty. While the high accuracy achieved (low MAE and MSE, and high R²) indicates strong predictive capability, these metrics do not directly provide information about the confidence or robustness of individual predictions.

Recent studies in battery diagnostics underscore the importance of uncertainty-aware modeling approaches in enhancing reliability and facilitating risk-sensitive decision-making. Techniques such as Monte Carlo dropout, deep ensemble learning, and Bayesian neural networks enable us to estimate predictive distributions and confidence intervals. These provide additional insight into how models behave under different operating conditions. However, the primary objective of this study was to develop a high-performance hybrid architecture that integrates multiscale convolutional feature extraction and residual learning to accurately estimate state of charge (SoC). Therefore, uncertainty quantification was beyond the scope of this work. Nevertheless, integrating such mechanisms into the proposed framework is an important direction for future research, particularly to improve model trustworthiness and enable deployment in real-time battery management systems.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel hybrid DL architecture using multi-scale CNNs, enhanced residual blocks and LSTM units is proposed to predict the SoC value of battery systems with high accuracy. The proposed model effectively learns spatial patterns and temporal dependencies in the data and achieves accurate predictions in the regression task.

This study’s primary contribution is the design of a unified hybrid architecture that integrates multi-scale convolutional feature extraction (with kernel sizes of 1, 3, and 5), Swish-activated residual learning, and temporal modeling within a single framework. Unlike conventional CNN–LSTM and ResNet–LSTM variants, this approach more effectively represents local and global feature patterns, improving learning capability and stability. Additionally, controlled ablation analysis and a consistent benchmarking strategy were employed to ensure fair and interpretable evaluation of the model components.

The experimental results indicate that the proposed model achieves higher prediction accuracy than traditional ML algorithms (RF, GBM, SVR etc.) and performs better than simplified versions of the model with reduced components. The R² score of the model is 0.9998, the MAE value is 0.1507, and the MSE value is 0.2030, indicating that the prediction performance is both highly accurate and has strong generalization capacity. Moreover, the scatter plot and residual distribution analyses visually confirm that the model offers a balanced and consistent forecasting capability that is free from systematic errors. In this context, the proposed hybrid architecture provides accurate predictions and demonstrates stable performance across the evaluated datasets for real-time SoC prediction and offers the potential as a viable decision support tool for smart battery management systems.

In addition, from a computational perspective, the proposed architecture is based on 1D convolutional operations and a relatively simple residual structure, which may offer advantages in terms of computational efficiency compared to more complex architectures involving high-dimensional convolutions or attention-heavy mechanisms. While a detailed quantitative analysis of computational cost, inference latency, and memory requirements is beyond the scope of this study, these aspects will be investigated in future work to further assess the deployment feasibility of the proposed model in resource-constrained battery management system environments.

Despite these strong results, it is important to note that the proposed model makes deterministic predictions and does not quantify predictive uncertainty. While the performance metrics achieved indicate high accuracy, they do not directly reflect the confidence level associated with individual predictions. Given the safety-critical nature of battery management systems, incorporating uncertainty-aware modeling approaches would enhance the reliability and practical applicability of the proposed framework.

Future studies will focus on adapting the model to online learning mechanisms, integrating it with hardware-level battery monitoring units, and incorporating uncertainty quantification techniques, such as dropout-based inference and ensemble learning, to enable confidence-aware and risk-sensitive decision-making. Additionally, testing the model under various degradation scenarios and real-world operating conditions will improve its robustness and readiness for deployment.
