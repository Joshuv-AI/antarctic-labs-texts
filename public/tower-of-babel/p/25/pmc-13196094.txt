Caregivers as essential members of healthcare teams: lessons learned from enforcement of the “no visitors” policy during the COVID-19 pandemic in Ontario, Canada
Authors: Danielle Jacobson, Arija Birze, Elizabeth Mansfield, Mehdi Ammi, Walter P Wodchis, Kerry Kuluski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic brought to the forefront caregivers’ unmeasured, unpaid work. Despite increasing recognition of caregivers as essential care-partners, during the pandemic, “no visitor” hospital restrictions prevented caregivers from engaging in their pivotal role within the healthcare system. This qualitative research aimed to better understand the wider effects of the pandemic on cancer care in Ontario, Canada with a focus on the experiences of cancer patients, caregivers, healthcare providers, and healthcare decision-makers (leaders in oncology who direct care, manage units, and/or supervise frontline staff) to capture multiple perspectives on caregiving.MethodsOne-on-one, semi-structured interviews and focus groups were conducted with 32 participants across two hospitals. Participants were 18 years of age or older and had received, supported, or provided cancer care services during the pandemic, at one of the two hospital study sites. Interviews and focus groups were audio and video recorded and then transcribed. Inductive, codebook thematic analysis was conducted following Braun and Clarke’s six steps.ResultsCaregivers were found to be central to in-hospital person- and family-centred care. Their absence and the corresponding gaps in care highlighted their work as integral to comprehensive healthcare. Without caregivers present to absorb and communicate complex information, instructions were often missed or misunderstood by patients. Caregivers were also needed to help with advocacy and challenging navigation of the healthcare system. They alleviated some of healthcare providers’ workload through their work as porters, translators, and nursing and mental health support—responsibilities which were then foisted onto healthcare providers who were over-capacity and unable to absorb the extra work. The “no visitors” policy created barriers to caregivers, causing moral distress, negative emotional impacts, and created or redistributed essential care work in harmful ways.ConclusionsThe exclusion of caregivers resulted in an extra burden of work for healthcare providers. This reinforced the idea that caregivers are essential members of the in-hospital healthcare team whose presence facilitates more efficient and quality care. When healthcare providers were unable to absorb this burden of work, patient care quality and experience often declined. Caregiver presence is therefore essential for quality, person- and family-centred care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14500-7.Keywords: Patient- and family-centred care, Caregivers, Cancer care, Healthcare team, COVID-19

Introduction

Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, hospitals across Canada had made unprecedented progress with visitor policies; 73% had open policies in place in early 2020—a large rise from previous years [1]. Different from traditional models of care, which prohibit anyone (e.g., caregivers, family, and friends) from accompanying patients outside of visiting hours, open visitation eliminates this restriction [2]. Children’s hospitals were some of the first to implement more lenient visitation policies (as early as the 1990s) and the Better Together campaign in 2016, launched by the Canadian Foundation for Healthcare Improvement (now Healthcare Excellence Canada), also contributed to this movement by calling for the extension of this leniency across Canadian hospitals more broadly [2].

The growing philosophy that caregivers (i.e., family and friends who provide care and support to patients) are “true partners in care” [3] was influenced by evidence that caregiver presence increases patient satisfaction, safety, advocacy, and communication [4]. The implementation of person- and family-centred open visitation policies was proven to be beneficial to patient outcomes prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, including reduced complications in intensive care contexts [5], with no evidence linking caregiver presence to increased infection risk (pre-pandemic) [5, 6]. Caregivers’ importance has been demonstrated in cancer care [7–9] given their role in treatment decision-making [10], mental health support, personal care, coordinating care (e.g., treatments, check-ups), providing transportation [11], managing relationships with healthcare providers (e.g., garnering care-related information, getting in touch with the appropriate personnel to address particular needs) [12, 13], and advocating for patient preferences to be met (including cultural and religious needs) [12].

Despite the wide acknowledgement of caregivers as essential to care, during the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic (March 2020 – September 2020), “no visitor” policies were introduced across healthcare facilities in Canada, with the intention to protect patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers from the virus. The conceptualization of caregivers quickly shifted from “essential care partners” (i.e., someone who engages in work that is vital to a patient’s well-being) [14, 15] to “visitors” barred from entering hospitals [16, 17]. Of note was the lack of “transparent and evidence-based decision-making” in developing and implementing the policy [18]. The “no visitor” policy also did not align with: (1) the WHO’s [15] interim guidance on infection prevention and control, which acknowledged caregivers as a subset of “health workers” and recommended their entry into hospitals, (2) the Ontario Hospital Association’s (OHA) [14] recommendation for hospital policies to permit “essential care partners” entry and integration into care, and (3) data gathered during the COVID-19 pandemic, which indicated that visitors did not play a significant part in the spread of the disease in hospitals [16, 19]. Based on these recommendations, the “no visitors” policy was slowly amended over time, however still prevented caregivers from engaging fully in their pivotal role within the healthcare system.

Research examining the impacts of the “no visitors” policy highlights difficulty in building patient/caregiver-provider trust [20] and incompatibility of the policy with patient- and family-centred care [21, 22]. When it comes to cancer care specifically, research on the effect of barring caregivers from in-person support has revealed the resulting mental health impact [23] and higher burden of patient-stress in navigating cancer treatment by themselves, leading to a sense of social isolation [24]. While an understanding exists of the importance of caregiver support for patients receiving cancer care, there is scant research on the ways in which the “no visitor” policy had an impact on cancer care for patients, caregivers, and providers and decision-makers. In particular, what is missing is an understanding of the work that the “no visitors” policy generated for patients, caregivers, providers, and decision-makers to navigate the loss of caregiver support. This work encapsulates any additional effort undertaken by any type of participant as a result of caregivers’ prohibition from entering the hospital setting. This identified gap aligns with Lambert et al.’s [25] recommended priority research areas related to caregivers and cancer care including the need for further work on those most at risk of burden and centering caregiver perspectives on how providers can support them.

The current qualitative research study therefore addressed the research question: What are the wider effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on cancer care in Ontario, Canada, considering the experiences of patients, caregivers, healthcare providers, and healthcare decision-makers (leaders in oncology units who direct care, manage units, and/or supervise frontline staff)? Attention was focused on one component of a larger study [26, 27], which was informed by a critical-social, social-justice-oriented approach (i.e., oriented toward identifying social and structural inequities and working toward transformation) [28]. While originally asked about experiences with cancer care and delays or difficulty accessing diagnosis and treatment, participants wanted to delve into the “no visitor” policy and its impact on cancer care. Based on what participants felt was important to convey, probes centred around the impact of caregiver absence during in-hospital appointments and how the exclusion of caregivers shifted the distribution of essential caregiving work (e.g., advocacy, emotional labour, coordination, system navigation). Interviewing patients, caregivers, healthcare providers, and healthcare decision-makers allowed the research team to capture multiple perspectives on caregiving.

Methods

Conceptual framework

This research study was informed by a critical-social, social-justice-oriented approach, which guided conceptualization, interactions with participants, and collection, analysis, and dissemination of data (e.g., asking questions surrounding how experiences of care may be improved, holding analytic discussions regarding areas for change arising from the data) [28]. The aim was to inform current and future policy by listening to participants’ experiences and critiques of the healthcare system and conveying concerns and recommendations to institutional stakeholders for action [28]. Underlying values which have directed the project include the view that social and institutional structures (e.g., policy, training, social narratives) shape people’s experiences [29]. The goal of this inquiry was to help patients, caregivers, healthcare providers, and healthcare decision-makers by bringing current strengths and areas for improvement in cancer care to the fore. Given the understanding that broader social and institutional processes and structures shape individual experiences, this conceptual framework allowed for exploration of the power dynamics involved in decision-making as well as the challenges and distress associated with those decisions (e.g., policy design and implementation).

Inherent in the critical-social approach is reflexivity [30]; the social identity and positionality of researchers have significant influence on the research [31]. The research team was multi-disciplinary and reflexive in their approach with members across different levels of experience, ranging from research associates to senior scientists and professors. There was a wide range of expertise across team members including in applied health-services research, cancer and multi-morbidity research, patient and caregiver experience research, and qualitative health research. Team members brought varying perspectives to the work, with diverse racial and ethnic identities, and some with experience traversing the Canadian healthcare system as caregivers to loved ones with cancer.

Recruitment

Recruitment took place from February 2023-May 2024 across two hospital settings located in northern and southern parts of Ontario, Canada. Given the drastic changes seen in the provision of cancer care as a result of the emerging and ongoing pandemic, the inclusion of several different participant groups (i.e., patients, caregivers, providers, and decision-makers) was used to generate a comprehensive data set from varying perspectives. In doing so, several perspectives on topics of interest were considered in a way that enriched analysis and facilitated triangulation. Patients and caregivers (i.e., anyone who self-identified as family or a friend who provided care in support of patients) were purposively recruited to reflect the diverse populations served by the two hospital study sites. One study site had a larger proportion of immigrant and visible minority populations served while the other saw a higher proportion of individuals from remote communities. Both sites were regional cancer centres and provided comprehensive cancer care (e.g., inpatient/outpatient services, diagnostic and surgical clinics, treatment, psycho-social and other supportive care services). Recruitment flyers were disseminated via mail to randomly selected active patients and caregivers, posted in waiting areas, and handed out at both study sites. Staff at both sites who did not provide direct care to patients supported dissemination of materials. Patient and caregiver participants were recruited to reflect diversity across age, gender, ethnicity, and type of cancer experienced. While the research team was able to recruit participants with a diversity across age, there was less diversity across gender and ethnicity than intended. Overall, the majority of participants were female, white, and educated. To recruit healthcare providers and decision-makers, study partners (across hospital, community care, policy level organizations) dispersed recruitment material (e.g., e-mails, flyers) across their networks. The research team also leveraged their networks to circulate recruitment material. Healthcare provider and decision-maker participants were recruited to reflect diversity across sectors and professions. All participants interested in the study reached out to the research team via phone or email. Response rates were not tracked. Participants’ accounts were retrospective, recalling what their pandemic experiences were after they took place, providing valuable perspective, reflection, and learning.

Participants

Multiple perspectives on caregiving were captured with 32 unique participants comprised of patients (8), caregivers (5), healthcare providers (14), and healthcare decision-makers (5) (see Tables 1 and 2). Four participants had dual roles: one caregiver also had experience as a patient and three caregivers also had experience as a healthcare provider. Two participants with dual roles participated in two separate one-on-one interviews (one for each role that was coded independently). The two other participants with dual roles participated once but their contributions were coded according to the role they were describing in the moment. To be included in the study, participants were required to be 18 years of age or older, had to have received or provided cancer care services during the pandemic at one of the two hospital study sites in Ontario, Canada, and had to have the cognitive capacity to provide informed consent. There were no additional inclusion or exclusion criteria. Although discussions around cancer type or treatment stage did arise, these details were not a primary focus of the qualitative research and as such were not systematically considered.

Consent and compensation

A team member confirmed each participant’s eligibility and explained the study’s purpose and what participation entailed. Participants were told that participation was voluntary, confidential, and anonymous, that consent was ongoing, and that they could end participation at any time without consequence. All participants reviewed and signed an informed consent form and were compensated for their time and efforts with a $25 CAD gift card to a place of their choosing. Participants were informed of the risks (e.g., risk of negative feelings, no medical risk) and benefits (e.g., informing work aiming for systems change with personal experience, an outlet to share experiences) to participating in the study.

Interviews and focus groups

One-on-one, semi-structured interviews (26 with patients, caregivers, healthcare providers, healthcare decision-makers) and focus groups (3 with healthcare providers and leaders) between 60 and 90 min were conducted (in no particular order) and were audio and video recorded. In one case, a preference for a focus group over an interview was determined by participants who signed up to participate in the study together while the other two focus groups were determined by AB who grouped participants according to roles and schedules/availability. Both interviews and focus groups were used to accommodate participant preferences. Each focus group had between two and four participants. All interview and focus group guides were developed for the current study, not previously published elsewhere, and were conducted via Zoom communication software (for participants’ convenience; if an in-person interview were to have been requested, it would have been accommodated, however no such requests were made) [32]. Guides were developed through literature review and team expertise and iteratively modified as areas of interest emerged throughout the progression of data collection. The risks of using Zoom for research were communicated to participants in writing (e.g., others nearby to participants may overhear the conversation, the internet lacks security). To mitigate these risks participants were asked to be in a private space for the duration of the interview and a randomly generated meeting access number, password, and waiting room were used. Prior to beginning each interview and focus group, the interviewer asked if participants had further questions and re-confirmed consent. The interviewer/facilitator (AB) had no prior relationship with participants. AB is a health services research associate with extensive experience in qualitative research (both interview and focus group-based data generation) examining the pandemic-related experiences of patients, caregivers, providers and decision-makers (including the experience of collective moral distress and injury from differing perspectives). Group dynamics were managed through explicit mention of respectful behaviour and support for equitable turn-taking.

Questions asked during interviews and focus groups did not differ by session type but rather by participant type. Patients were asked questions including, “tell me about the type of care you were receiving prior to the pandemic”, “do you have a family member or friends who help you with your care? Did this change during the COVID-19 pandemic? Please explain”, and “what do care providers need to know about your experience that can help them improve how they provide care?” Caregivers were asked questions including, “in what ways (if at all) did care change during the pandemic?”, “were there particular things about care that did or did not work well?”, and “what (if any) delays did [patient] experience in care due to the pandemic?” Healthcare providers and decision-makers were asked questions including, “what was your role throughout the pandemic?”, “in what ways has the pandemic changed your clinical practices and how you provide care for patients?”, and “how did delays in care impact your team functioning?” The completion of data collection emerged through analytic discussions amongst the research team regarding the repetition of preliminary emerging concepts. Finally, a determination of sufficiency was reached as the team progressed through a rigorous analytical process that generated rich and varied themes addressing the research questions.

Analysis

Audio recordings of interviews and focus groups were transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were checked against the audio-recordings to ensure accuracy. All identifying information was removed and pseudonyms assigned. For all transcripts (both interview and focus group), inductive, codebook thematic analysis (with a reflexively evolving codebook) [33] was conducted using NVivo qualitative data analysis software [34]. The reflexively evolving codebook involved a large codebook that was developed to provide a loose guide to theme development; this approach is common in applied health science research [35, 36]. In other words, a fixed codebook was not used. As analysis progressed, the codebook reflexively evolved alongside developing analytic insights.

A collaborative approach to coding was employed. AB and DJ independently coded the first five transcripts before coming together to review codes and discuss emerging trends. Convergences were plentiful and divergences were few. AB and DJ’s language used to denote codes was aligned. Any discrepancies in interpretation were discussed in detail and informed by revisiting relevant sections of the data. AB and DJ discussed their social locations, perspectives, and approaches to understanding the data and the ways in which these shaped their view of emerging codes and language used to express the meaning [31, 37]. Both AB and DJ have advanced education and training in public health and qualitative research. AB and DJ engaged in reflexive conversation about how this overlap in experience was reflected in the convergence of codes and meaning.

After reviewing the fifth independently coded transcript, with a majority of overlapping codes, AB coded the remaining transcripts independently. DJ then checked codes for all remaining transcripts, adding codes sparingly, indicating agreement on the meaning in the data. Once all transcripts were coded and all codes were double checked, initial codes were consolidated by collapsing similar codes together and eliminating those no longer relevant. Disconfirming experiences were discussed and included in themes if salient to the overall topic (e.g., positive impacts of the “no visitors” policy). At this stage, AB and DJ engaged in reflective conversations about the “bigger picture” and what was missing in the emerging themes that was conveyed by participants. Discussions on the prominence, significance and meaning of codes helped to generate connections with other codes, shaping thematic areas. Throughout coding, periodic meetings among the broader team were held to review transcripts and further discuss initial codes and themes, bringing additional perspectives to interpretation or areas of interest (e.g., how caregivers are/are not conceptualized as essential members of the care team and by whom). Finally, initial themes were refined and sub-themes formed before engaging in analytic writing. KK and EM engaged in analytic conversations surrounding theme development with the broader team.

Findings

In discussing the impacts of the pandemic response on cancer care in Ontario, patients, caregivers, providers, and decision-makers laid bare the essential role of caregivers and their contributions to quality cancer care. Participants experienced the “no visitors” policy as a redistribution (and inevitable deterioration) of essential elements of cancer care through jeopardized person- and family-centred approaches, unmanageable increases in workload for providers, and harms that came about because of the absence of caregivers.

The study participants were comprised of 8 patients, 5 caregivers (mostly female and educated, between the ages of 30 and 69, mostly born in Canada), 14 healthcare providers, and 5 healthcare decision-makers (mostly female, between the ages of 20 and 59, with up to 39 years of professional experience, and with a range of clinical roles and organizational perspectives). Participants in this study communicated the impact of the “no visitors” policy on their experience. Overall, caregivers were found to be essential members of the in-hospital healthcare team and facilitated more efficient, quality, safe care. In this section, the following themes are discussed: (1) caregivers were central to person- and family-centred care and pushing hospital care forward, (2) caregiver’s burden of work alleviated some workload from in-hospital healthcare providers, and (3) harm was caused by the “no visitors” policy (see Table 3).

Summary of findings

Before turning to the themes, which largely centre on the negative impacts of the “no visitors” policy, it is important to note that some pointed to its benefits. May (patient) highlighted how without caregivers present, treatment rooms were not packed with people: “I personally liked the restriction of visitors…I just preferred it when the chemo rooms, or the waiting rooms weren’t packed with people”. Ainsley (nurse) noticed less people in healthcare providers’ way: “I know I’ve complained about the one person visiting [restriction], but there are some positives to that, because sometimes you would have families that had 20 visitors, and as the nurse, trying to work around them could be challenging”. Sophia (clinical manager) highlighted how restricting visitors corresponded with a decrease in non-COVID disease outbreaks:

When we restricted visitors throughout the hospital, we used to have VRE [antibiotic-resistant bacterial infection] outbreaks constantly on our units. We had no VRE outbreaks in the hospital. So you could see that there were some positive things with some of the processes that were implemented.

Despite the benefits highlighted by some participants, the “no visitor” policy ultimately was a source of struggle for most in this study.

Caregivers were central to person- and family-centred care and pushing care forward

Caregivers absorbed and communicated complex information missed by patients

Prior to being barred from the hospital, it was caregivers who acted as a patient’s “second eyes and second set of ears” (Elanor, patient). Caregivers ensured patient’s inclusion in their “healthcare journey” by “absorbing” multifaceted information from providers, “filtering through it”, and communicating it slowly and repeatedly with patients over time to ensure their understanding of “what the next step” was in their care and agreement with their care plan (Jules, caregiver/clinical educator). Terrie (oncologist) explained that without caregivers “there for support, it was certainly a much more challenging interaction”. Without caregivers present to absorb and communicate complex care-related information, patients were left overwhelmed, especially during treatment. Bronagh (patient) described how during chemotherapy she was unable to take in the education given as she was preoccupied with processing difficult emotions:

While they’re doing your first infusion, looking for a vein, injecting red nasty stuff into you, they’re also talking 20 miles a minute. And you’re just looking at this red stuff going into your arm trying not to have a panic attack. And you honestly don’t hear anything that they’re saying.

In response to the “no visitor” restrictions, caregivers like Jules (caregiver/clinical educator) tried to interpret care-related information from a distance:

I’m not at the appointments with her [her mother], so more difficult to try to decipher what was communicated to her. … So yeah, calling the team and following up from afar, those kinds of things…[getting] information from the healthcare providers was sometimes challenging.

Caregivers often had to decipher care-related instructions from the patient’s interpretation and sought out providers directly to verify the patient’s account and obtain missed information. Healthcare providers, too, incurred an additional burden of work given the extra time required to “reiterate the information” to patients who “were too overwhelmed”, “because family members were not permitted [in the hospital]” (Lex, nurse). To help caregivers receive information, Lex (nurse) engaged in “virtual consents” and “virtual teaching”, which often occurred “over the phone”. Without sufficient information, family members would also call providers, which meant that a provider needed to “man their [oncologist’s] messages and phones” (Ingrid, nurse). Sophia (clinical manager) described addressing “increased calls to the clinic” by “getting a little bit strict” with a “one spokesperson per family” rule. Other times, providers found workarounds to communicate with caregivers face-to-face. Janet (social worker) described “actually going outside [to] have a conversation with a caregiver there because they couldn’t come inside”.

Another source of added work for healthcare providers was getting caregivers on board with care plans. Cassandra (discharge planner) explained how caregivers played a key role in approving clinical decisions; when they had not witnessed their loved one’s progression first-hand, it was more challenging for caregivers and providers to build trust and for caregivers to approve plans to move care forward:

for us to sometimes move goals of care forward, family needed to see that their patient is changing and declining. … It’s very hard to … get that movement and acceptance to flip goals if they [caregivers] can’t actually see what’s happening … they’re not going to necessarily trust us.

Without caregivers present during in-hospital appointments, often, patients felt overwhelmed, caregivers had to engage in extra work to attain care-related information, and healthcare providers had to take on a significant burden of work to ensure that diagnostic and treatment related information was adequately received and moved forward.

Caregivers as essential navigators and advocates

The COVID-19 pandemic at times “heightened” the existing challenge of “navigating the [healthcare] system”; Jules (caregiver/clinical educator) explained: “that was the most frustrating time, I think, because the pandemic was just hitting, nobody knew what was going on, just accessing the healthcare system during that time was horrendous”. Nidda (clinical manager) clarified how “there was fear and anxiety, confusion, around … how they’re going to navigate their patient journey”. Caregivers assisted health system navigation by keeping track of and monitoring requisitions and sending reminders to avoid lost appointments and paperwork. Eva (caregiver) explained how “in the healthcare system, you have to be an advocate for your family” by being proactive: “You can’t wait for them to call you. You have to call them for an appointment and things like that. And you have to do it for follow-up and everything in the healthcare system.” Without caregivers present to advocate for patients, some believed they received lower quality care. For example, when the “no visitor” policy became more lenient and Beth’s (patient) husband was permitted to once again attend appointments, he was shocked to observe the poor level of care she had been receiving: “I took my husband to my oncology appointment, and he was shocked. He’s like, ‘Oh my goodness, they have no idea who you even are’. … So, to have that other person [caregiver] there to see, like, are you getting care? No, you’re not. That would help.”

At times, caregivers with a dual role as a healthcare provider engaged in additional advocacy by leveraging personal connections with other providers. Having informal consultations with colleagues was helpful to better understand a loved one’s care path, test results, and to order tests not requisitioned by the patient’s direct doctor. Macey (caregiver/nurse) explained: “I sought out the doctors that I work with … an ICU doctor [colleague] ordered a CT scan for her [patient] which showed the mass better than the [original doctor’s] x-ray”. In-depth system knowledge also meant that caregiver-providers knew when to push against recommendations. Macey (caregiver/nurse) pushed for her mom to have chemo and immunotherapy, rather than immunotherapy alone (doctor’s recommendation), which she believed extended her mom’s life: “he [the doctor] was really leaning towards just immunotherapy, and yet, we fought for chemo and immunotherapy and it shrunk her tumour almost 95%, which I think extended her life”.

Jules (caregiver/clinical educator) reflected on the difficulty that people comparatively had without in-depth system expertise: “how does someone do this when they don’t have … someone in the know advocating for them? … if there is someone that has a little bit of knowledge about the healthcare system I think it’s much better for patients”. When caregivers did not have a dual provider role, some sought advice from provider-relatives. May (patient) described having provider-family members who advised on what tools could minimize patient and provider difficulty: “It also might help that I have family members and close friends who are registered nurses … I remember the aunt calling and saying, ‘You’re going to want a port’”.

In this theme we see that caregivers were central to person- and family-centred care through interpreting, absorbing, and repeatedly communicating complex clinical information, advocating for patients, and ensuring patients were supported, informed, and included in decisions when they were overwhelmed or unable to fully engage. Caregiver absence, thus, led to deteriorations in care quality by leaving patients overwhelmed and unsupported, undermining trust in care plans and shared decision-making, and making it more difficult to advance treatment decisions when caregivers could not directly witness patients’ condition or advocate effectively.

Caregiver’s burden of work alleviated healthcare providers’ workload

Caregivers as health-service workers

Prior to the “no visitor” restrictions, caregivers engaged in a high burden of patient-care work, which alleviated health-service workload (e.g., portering, translation, nursing support). Nidda (clinical manager) explained how the hospital “relied historically on family members to help take patients through in wheelchairs”; when the pandemic hit, “portering was pulled in a million different directions … losing that support through the family member and not having resources to help the patient just made it hard for everybody”. Further difficulty arose when caregivers could no longer engage as translators. Brook (caregiver/radiation therapist) described how “one of the biggest problems we did encounter [was] having a patient with a language barrier”, since without a trusted translator, they did not “feel 100% comfortable”.Some providers, like Mave (radiation therapist), tried to “fight back”, advocating for caregivers to provide in-person supports. However, as Nidda (clinical manager) clarified, “the hospital pushed so hard not to have extra people in the building” even though many providers viewed caregivers as necessary to patient-centred, ethical care: “[The caregiver] wasn’t able to come [into the hospital]. Yet this gentleman [patient] in his 80s didn’t understand English very well and I thought that is completely wrong … we thought that they needed family there”.

Caregivers’ previous work to support patients often became foisted on nurses, who were already overworked, understaffed, and unable to absorb the burden of additional labour. For example, Bronagh (patient) “suffered a lot” from a harrowing experience resulting from not being “allowed to have visitors or support people for the majority of [her] care”. After a breast-removal surgery, she “needed to use the washroom” but lacked assistance to do so:

I fumbled my way into the bathroom and wishing I had a nurse or somebody to support me … and there’s this giant commode chair on top of the toilet. … So I stumbled to the bed and … I can’t reach the call button.

Bronagh (patient) was unable to urinate independently and had to wait so long for help that her bladder “just stopped functioning”; she described being “in agony” and requiring a catheter to release “two litres” of urine, which her nurse considered to be “unsafe”. Bronagh (patient) had not “had anything to drink for 12 hours”, was “dying of thirst”, and struggled to obtain a glass of water. Though she asked a nurse, “they didn’t come back … and nobody was coming into the room”. Without “a support person or enough nursing care” to fulfil her basic needs, Bronagh (patient) had to overexert herself post-surgery, resulting in complications that required unplanned surgery to remedy:

I had started bleeding through my sutures. … And then I started having a panic attack. … Eventually I got in for my second [emergency] surgery. … It’s just where you really need the one-on-one nursing … that’s where I didn’t have the support. Because everybody was just flat out, they had way too many patients.

The basic necessities that some patients needed assistance with but were unable to obtain support for demonstrates caregivers as essential to care, alleviating patient suffering, easing burden of work from healthcare providers, and preventing complications or further harm from inadequate support.

Caregivers as mental health support

The absence of caregivers in-hospital also highlighted their role in supporting patient mental health, which was particularly important upon diagnosis. Janet (social worker) explained that “it was actually quite devastating to get information about a cancer diagnosis and not be able to have somebody with them”. Danni (patient) described the difficulty of navigating diagnosis alone: “you’re sitting there receiving some of the hardest news of your life and you’re having to do it alone and that was terrifying”. The trauma of experiencing diagnosis alone became re-hashed when Danni (patient) had to return to the original site of diagnosis again without support.

It emotionally broke me every time I had to go back into [original site]. … I relived everything that I did the day I got diagnosed. Emotionally, it was devastating … and I was doing it alone because no one could be with me.

Surgery was another problematic stage to lack caregiver emotional and mental health support. Alice (patient) described being unsure if her entire breast would be removed during surgery:

It’s sad to be there by yourself, if that [removal of the entire breast] was going to happen, but [not] knowing waking up what was going to be the result. And knowing I would still be alone. But the staff was very understanding. Lots of tears that day.

Despite showing an understanding of the protective nature of physical segregation and appreciation for compassionate staff, being alone proved a difficult, isolating experience.

In-person caregiver support was described as vital to “recovery, both physically and mentally”; Mani (discharge planner) described how “such severe visitor restrictions, it really took a toll, you could see, on our patients, on their mental health, on their mood”. When healthcare providers noticed patient’s mental health declining as a result of caregiver absence, they often took on additional work to emulate that missing support. Luiza (coordinator) explained how “it could be challenging at times because we [providers] not only had to be the nurse but we also had to be their [patients’] entire support person”. Sam (nurse) highlighted the importance of stepping in to “provide that emotional support” for “patients [who] were dying” and “couldn’t have families at the bedside”. She described “providing all of the care that was needed”, which was “a really difficult time” because the hospital was not “bringing in anybody extra” to compensate for the loss of caregiver emotional and mental health support.

Healthcare providers were unable to offer the same quality of mental health support as caregivers since they were pressed for time. Once “no visitor” restrictions were lifted, this extra work dissipated, reinforcing the essential work that caregivers contribute to in-hospital care teams. Ingrid (nurse) explained, “now, because we are allowing visitors in … you feel like you’re not needed as much by the patient … it’s a good thing, because we really just unfortunately don’t have the time to sit with them”.

In this theme we see that the exclusion of caregivers transferred substantial care work onto already understaffed providers, requiring them to absorb additional physical tasks and one-to-one assistance including intensified emotional and mental health support roles that caregivers had previously performed. We can also see that these shifts in workload often resulted in delays, unsafe care, and preventable complications for patients, particularly around diagnosis, surgeries, and end-of-life care.

“No visitors” policy caused harm to patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers

Moral distress and negative emotional impact

The “no visitor” policy combined with physical distancing restrictions at times created an environment of moral distress for healthcare providers and a negative emotional impact for patients and caregivers. Providers experienced moral distress when they felt powerless and unable to provide the care they viewed as ethical. Mave (radiation therapist) described being required to deliver bad news to an unsupported patient, which put her in a challenging position as she recognized the human need for comfort held up against institutional policy restricting support persons and physical contact:

We had a patient who … thought she was going to be cured and then afterwards we realized nothing is working, you’re going to die. … That conversation [happened] in my treatment bunker with the patient. She did not have any family member. It was myself, the nurse, the doctor, and one of my other colleagues all standing down looking at this poor woman and then she just started to cry. Social distancing six feet. … We can’t even hug her. We can’t console her.

Some patients, like Krystal (patient/caregiver), were empathetic toward healthcare providers in these difficult circumstances:

… when she told me that I had stage 4 breast cancer, the doctor walks into the room, she goes, “Are you by yourself?” I said, “Yeah”. …. And then she tells me. And I felt so bad … I said, “I’m so sorry that you have to share this news with me. It must be so hard on you”.

Patients therefore not only lacked support in hearing devastating news but also at times felt that they had to accommodate for providers, demonstrating immense compassion in their recognition that delivering devastating news to an unsupported patient may be upsetting for the provider.

Treatment was another time at which a lack of caregiver support caused distress. Gabby (radiation therapist) explained how “a big challenge was just watching them [patients] have to deal with it all [treatment] alone, especially patients that were, you know, the disease itself was causing so many disabilities” including a loss of “understanding of what’s going on”. With “the policies of the hospital at the time [being] just no visitors allowed”, and patient’s cognition affected from treatment, it was “very scary for them” to go “under this [radiation] mask” without support and not “really know what’s happening”. Providers like Gabby were empathetic to patients’ declining mental and physical health and felt distress being obligated to enact a policy that did not allow for patients’ needs to be met. A disjuncture therefore occurred between healthcare provider perspectives and the “no visitors” policy; while providers considered physical closeness and emotional support as a basic human need, the “no visitors” policy failed to recognize this as essential.

Luiza (coordinator) described having to enact the “no visitors” policy, which she believed at times went against medical and personal ethics, particularly when loved ones were prohibited from saying goodbye to dying family members:

… even from an ethical, a personal ethics perspective, there was patients that we knew weren’t going to be with us much longer. And we still had to say, “I’m sorry, you can’t have your family member come with you”. … So that was really hard on staff and continues to be hard on staff.

Kimber (manager) described how preventing caregivers from supporting patients went “against everything you’re taught in nursing”: “Family lives with cancer just as much as the person who has the cancer, right? So it [visitor restrictions] goes against that therapeutic relationship with everybody. She continued to explain how “now I’m not allowed to follow my North Star [gut]. So for me that was where the moral distress came from”.

Mirroring the rapid advancements of information on how to manage the COVID-19 pandemic, the “no visitor” policy, too, often changed, making it difficult for healthcare providers to be up to date and provide clear instructions to patients and caregivers. Mani (discharge planner) explained: “One week, we’re being told this many visitors. By the next week, that’s changed and we’re not allowed any visitors”. Mani continued to describe a situation in which a family had “thought it was two people at a time” allowed and that visitors could switch out for loved ones who were dying. One patient’s husband and son visited first and when the son went to switch with the patient’s daughter, she was barred from entering the hospital; the daughter “begged and pleaded” to say goodbye to her mother and the son said: “If I had known that, I would have actually allowed my sister to come”. The lack of clarity in changing policies was therefore “very emotionally distressing” for all involved. “No visitor” restrictions led to a complex loop in which healthcare providers experienced moral distress enacting a policy with which they often disagreed, which led to complex grief for families, which then ignited further moral distress for providers, witnessing the consequences of the rules they were responsible for enacting. Cassandra (discharge planner) explained:

I often felt conflicted by the visiting policy…we know the research around young kids and their parents dying, and we couldn’t help with that closure for them if that is something they wanted … So, you’re holding that and watching that and feeling for those folks, right? … I think that was a big draining thing for a lot of us.

Patient/caregiver-provider and provider-manager animosity

The lack of consistency in the “no visitor” policy ignited “high emotions” and put care teams “on edge” (Cassandra, discharge planner). Despite not always agreeing with the policy, healthcare providers often bore the brunt of blame from patients and families for these restrictions. Kimber (manager) described “having families saying ‘You are doing this. You. I don’t care about your policies. You are stopping me’”. Ainsley (nurse) explained that nurses were the ones “primarily involved with patient care” and so “they looked like the bad guy having to say ‘no’”. Kimber (manager) highlighted resultant “animosity” between patients/caregivers and providers from “preventing people from visiting their dying loved ones”:

That was I think the hardest thing that we could do … to have patients die with nobody at their bedside. … Families were angry. … they would be either crying at me, or they’d be screaming at me, right? Because I wouldn’t let them in. … A lot of animosity.

Ainsley (nurse) described how the restrictions “not just created tension with staff and families, but [between] frontline and management as well”, especially when providers advocated for patients’ to have visitors and managers denied their appeals with “a hard ‘no’”: “The nurse [was] trying to advocate for that patient and said … ‘they really could use their daughter as well as their son today for this reason’. And then that manager sticking to what that policy consisted of”. In other instances, providers were firm about the rules and managers gave flexibility. West (nurse) experienced distress when his enforcement of the policy was overridden by his superior:

having to say ‘no that patient’s visitors can’t come’ in when you know in your heart you really want them in. And then … being in the position where you’ve made a decision where you’ve said no, they’ve gone to patient relations, and then your superior overrides your decision. So, that was the biggest moral distress for me when I was in that role.

Despite the distress experienced when providers had to enforce controversial rules that in the end were waived, patients like Mateo appreciated their advocacy:

The nurses in the cancer centre, the way that they have each other’s backs in the best interest of the patient, to me, is phenomenal. Despite the challenges that they face … a strong advocate for the patients … There’s this warmth, and I know that I’m in good hands.

The “no visitors” policy prevented caregiver-provider and patient-provider relationships

Not only did animosity permeate clinical and collegial relationships, but meaningful connections were also hard to form between patients/caregivers and providers. With less in-person contact, providers had fewer opportunities to develop relationships with caregivers. Brook (caregiver/radiation therapist) described how she “didn’t really get to make a relationship with their caregiver … because they couldn’t come in”. In particular, one essential component to developing strong clinical relationships was providers’ ability to “read” caregivers’ body-language. Lex (nurse) explained, “you can read people when they’re in front of you. … Over the phone, it was different – you don’t feel like you’ve made those bonds with the family”. The hindrance of caregiver-provider relationships by physical distance and a lack of body-language was confirmed for Lex when she noticed an improvement in relationships when the “no visitor” policy relaxed: “Now it’s a little bit better as one person is allowed to come in with family. So I feel that the relationships are getting better and there’s more of a bond”.

Concealing their disagreement with the “no visitor” policy was another facet that had an impact on fostering patient/caregiver-provider relationships. Mani (discharge planner) described that she was “just the messenger” and had to work to mask her dissenting opinion on the policy. As a result, patients and caregivers thought she “agreed with certain [restrictive] decisions”: “There was lots of things I didn’t agree with, decisions that were being made, but I have to still support the workplace that I work for.” Consequently, patients came into appointments angry—another facet that prevented patient-provider relationship development. Lex (nurse) described:

when you start off right off the bat that they [patients] can’t have anyone with them, they’re angry and they’re frustrated … so it makes it a little bit more challenging. You can’t tell if they’re angry at you or angry at the situation.

Despite the strain on clinical relationships, patients and caregivers expressed the importance of forming strong relationships with providers. Brook (caregiver/radiation therapist) explained how during “the full course of her [Dad’s] cancer care” she “never had the opportunity to meet any of the physicians. Only talking to them over the phone. And that was very hard”. Ingrid (nurse) explained how a family she was helping, who were unable to support their loved one in person, “felt very disconnected from the [patient’s] entire [healthcare] experience”. She continued,

the family was upset that, during one of the longest periods of his [patient’s] life where he really needed his family there, and he made new connections, new relationships with people in the cancer centre, including the nurses, that they didn’t get to be a part of that … the family never really got to know them. So that was hard for them.

Some patients and caregivers did still experience positive relationships with their providers, were grateful for the care received, and recognized and were understanding of the strain on providers.

In this theme we see that the “no visitors” policy caused cascading harms where patients faced devastating news, treatment, and end-of-life alone. Caregivers simultaneously experienced complex grief and exclusion and providers suffered moral distress, emotional exhaustion, and conflict when enforcing policies they viewed as unethical. These harms were sometimes compounded by inconsistent policy changes that fuelled anger, animosity, and fractured relationships among patients, caregivers, frontline staff, and managers, undermining trust and therapeutic care for all involved.

Discussion

This study aimed to gain a better understanding of the wider impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on cancer care from multiple perspectives, by asking participants (patients, caregivers, healthcare providers, and healthcare decision-makers) about their cancer care experiences during the pandemic in Ontario, Canada. The main contribution of this work is the demonstration that caregiver exclusion results in the redistribution of essential care work, often leading to harms to patients, caregivers, and providers by creating gaps in care, moral distress, and diminished care quality. Participants emphasized the impact of the “no visitors” policy, which prohibited caregivers from entering the hospital and therefore implied that caregivers were not necessarily essential to the healthcare team. In their absence, caregivers’ dynamic burden of work became clear. Caregivers had contributed to patients’ healthcare navigation (e.g., understanding instructions, advocacy) as well as health-service work (e.g., portering, translating, supporting nursing and mental health). These responsibilities became a gap in patient care which then needed to be fulfilled by providers who were often unable to absorb these duties. Without caregivers present and adequate support for patients, providers experienced moral distress, patients and caregivers experienced a negative emotional impact, and patient care quality and experience often failed—all of which generated more work for all involved in cancer care.

Caregivers are essential members of the healthcare team and imperative to patient safety and person- and family-centred care

This study underscores caregivers as integral to the in-hospital cancer care team and to efficient, effective, person- and family-centred care. Consideration of caregivers as essential aligns with literature published pre-pandemic [2, 38, 39] when caregivers’ integration into care was actioned [3] via open visitation policies adopted by most Canadian hospitals [1]. The culture shift in acknowledging caregivers’ value [2] stemmed from evidence that their inclusion improves patient outcomes (e.g., reduces delirium [40, 41], anxiety [41], medical complications [42]), strengthens communication, increases care satisfaction, and decreases distress [3, 4, 43, 44]. Despite the established importance of caregivers to quality care, early on in the pandemic, caregivers became positioned as outsiders who generated risk of spreading disease and thus were not truly viewed as “essential” [45] in the wake of what was considered a very contagious virus.

At the time, information about COVID-19 was largely unknown, and so, banning caregivers from the hospital was understandably thought of as a key way to mitigate spread of the disease and protect scarce hospital resources (e.g., personal protective equipment). However, there were unexpected costs on the care of individuals. Although the implementation of the “no visitors” policy was aimed at protecting everyone in the hospital in an over-capacity and under-resourced context, in hindsight, as McMillan et al. [21] aptly noted that restricting caregivers from hospitals “reinforced a problematic narrative in contemporary healthcare that devalues” their burden of work [p. 8]. In the current research, this was demonstrated via institutional inaction to re-allocate resources to replace caregiver’s contribution to care. Previous research [22, 45–48] and recommendations [14] similarly report caregivers’ contribution to the same roles reported in this study (e.g., support for communication, translation, diagnosis, prognosis, personal care, decision-making, mobility, physical health, mental health).

By overlooking the importance of caregivers’ work to patient safety and person- and family-centred care, iatrogenic harm ensued [45]. The finding that gaps in care left by caregivers’ absence became foisted onto providers aligns with previous studies which describe providers taking on extra work [49] to act as a surrogate caregiver [22, 50, 51] particularly related to patients’ personal care and comfort [50, 52–54], emotional support [55], and advocacy [56]. However, providers were often unable to fully absorb caregivers’ work since they were overcapacity and under-resourced, resulting in patient suffering. Other work has also reported injuries and complications incurred as a result of lacking physical/mobility assistance, communication support, and advocacy [22]. This may relate to the noted dearth of bedside assistance and decreased care satisfaction for post-operative patients during the pandemic [57]. Particularly for those with language barriers and differential ability to navigate the healthcare system, policy and preparedness planning is required for future crises for which restrictive measures are called in environments which undervalue caregiving work.

Patients additionally experienced post-traumatic stress and declining mental health (e.g., after facing diagnosis and treatment alone) as a result of the devaluation of caregivers’ work. This mental health impact has been conveyed globally: in Canada, Montesanti et al. [22] report patient’s anxiety, depression, and delirium as a result of lacking caregiver support; in Turkey, Er and Delihasanoglu [51] report increased delirium and reduced overall healing; and in Australia, Digby et al. [47] report general decline. Isolation, loneliness [22, 24, 46, 50, 54, 57, 58], heightened distress, and anxiety for patients and caregivers are particularly cited outcomes of insufficient patient support [22, 46, 51, 58, 59]. Caregivers’ assistance in processing and retaining care-related information has also been previously reported; the very same language that participants in the current study used has been used elsewhere to describe caregivers as a “second set of eyes and ears” [54]. Based on the qualitative evidence from the current study, one conceptual contribution that emerged, given the harm caused for all involved, is the argument that quality caregiver support itself is a social determinant of health and patient safety. Caregiver presence or absence directly shaped patients’ ability to understand care, navigate the health system, receive physical and emotional support, and avoid preventable harm, thereby influencing care quality, safety, and outcomes beyond clinical treatment alone. For example, caregivers may pick up on signs of medical concern earlier and more regularly than healthcare providers and may also use their knowledge of the patient and their circumstances to ask questions which alter the treatment pathway. This view aligns with U.S. patient satisfaction work investigating COVID-positive patients’ experiences with the “no visitors” policy in-hospital, in which 98% of patient participants indicated that caregivers increased their well-being [60].

In the current study, healthcare providers, too, experienced harm from the “no visitors” policy—a finding supported by a body of emerging research. Delivering bad news and administering treatment to unsupported patients and prohibiting family from being with dying loved ones weighed heavily on providers’ moral conscience. These actions went against their personal and medical ethics and fostered moral distress, which is known to ensue when providers feel powerless and unable to provide the care they view as ethical [52, 61, 62]. Other studies have similarly found that during the pandemic providers experienced moral distress having to enforce a policy with which they did not agree [21, 53], being unable to provide patient- and family-centred care [21, 22, 52], and witnessing patient suffering [63] including dying alone [62, 64]—these actions have been described as unjust [51], immoral, and inhumane [65]. Providers’ distress in enforcing a policy viewed as unethical demonstrates the need for a better support mechanism for reporting observation of hospital policy as inconsistent with medical and personal ethics. Another layer was the resultant complex grief for families, for which the long-term health effects are currently unknown [45]. The “no visitors” policy has thus been discussed as an institutional betrayal that put all involved at risk of trauma since the healthcare system was meant to protect patients dependent on the institution for care and safety [18, 66–69].

Patient-safety has been described as not only the absence of harm but also the identification and communication of patient needs, advocacy, and helping patients to be informed about their care—all tasks to which caregivers facilitate [70]. Other research, too, describes caregivers as central to cancer-patients’ survival and their importance as a patient’s “safety net”, despite themselves not having such protection [71, 72]. It can be appreciated that during a health crisis, some tenets of care may become compromised in order for the healthcare system to adapt to immensely stressful conditions and mitigate pandemic-related harm as best as possible. However, as demonstrated by the negative impact of caregivers’ absence in-hospital, patient safety becomes hindered when policy implied that caregivers are not essential members of the healthcare team [45]. The unanticipated harm caused by the “no visitors” policy, despite being an effort to address compromised in-hospital safety amidst an enormous amount of uncertainty, presents an important learning opportunity to better prepare for future crises in which alternative measures may be considered for essential care partners who, as demonstrated in the current research, are deeply involved in patient care and whose support improved care quality and safety.

Exemplar models of care and healthcare system strengths

A stronger foundational understanding of caregivers as essential members of the healthcare team, who facilitate patient- and family-centred care and patient-safety, is necessary to be woven into the fabric of the healthcare system through sustained activation and hospital development initiatives. This may require structural changes (e.g., ensuring physical space for caregivers to engage) and better valuing caregivers’ work to facilitate healthcare [73]. The first steps of this needed action are already being taken across Canada and the U.S. For example, the Ontario Caregiver Organization has developed the Essential Caregiver Support Hub, offering resources and training to support caregiver identification, inclusion, and support in the healthcare system [74]. Alberta Health Services together with Calgary Health Foundation (Alberta, Canada) [75] has also implemented a Family Caregiver Centre (e.g., space for caregivers to rest, attain information, and/or access support services). Caregivers Alberta has also made strides in including caregivers in care through their Work and Care Program, which educates workplaces on how to support caregivers [76]. The U.S. provides other examples of caregiver inclusion (e.g., the Caregiver Advise, Record, Enable (CARE) Act requires hospitals to (1) note caregivers on medical records, (2) provide caregivers with discharge information, and (3) educate caregivers on patient care—these requirements are available on a wallet-sized card to present to providers to facilitate the Act [77–79]. Formally incorporating caregivers into care via this policy has reportedly had a positive impact on patient outcomes and experiences [80].

In Canada, Healthcare Excellence Canada [81, 82] launched their Essential Together campaign in response to the visitor restrictions in hospitals to provide guidance on how to identify (e.g., identified by the patient), establish (e.g., provide necessary personal protective equipment, meet providers), and support (e.g., educational supports, address barriers) the reintegration of caregivers into care. In Ontario specifically, actionable pathways identified to better integrate caregivers in care surround their inclusion on Ontario Health Teams (OHT) and inter-professional primary care teams [83]. It has been suggested that to integrate caregivers into cancer care teams specifically, it is necessary for providers to take the time to understand the tasks individual caregivers contribute to care [84], especially considering that strong partnerships with providers are known to support caregivers and contribute to care-sustainability [12]. The current research begins to address the need to understand the array of tasks to which caregivers contribute to cancer care and may be utilized as a basis of understanding for providers in building relationships with caregivers. While the current study draws attention to cancer care, the findings may extend to other areas of patient care. Further research is required to investigate how to support the sustainability of caregivers’ work in the case of future crises and public health emergencies.

Recommendations based on lessons learned from the “no visitors” policy

From this work, three recommendations are made to consider for any future visitor restrictions during times of crisis: (1) caregivers’ essential contributions to the healthcare team must not be overlooked, (2) key concepts must be clearly defined for equitable enforcement of the policy, and (3) exceptions for patients who are dying should be made on humanitarian grounds. Across all three recommendations is the underlying need to better balance standardization with personalization (or in other words, consistency with flexibility) in the policy’s implementation.

Caregivers’ contributions to care, and harm caused by their exclusion should be more heavily considered in the adoption of future restrictive visiting policies. While not necessarily widely known when measures to protect everyone in the hospital and to preserve limited safety resources were implemented [85], some evidence has emerged indicating that caregiver presence may not be associated with a significant increase in infection risk [5, 6, 16, 19, 60]. Creation of restrictive “visitor” policies thus require transparency and a better balance between the risk of harm caused due to caregiver presence (e.g., spread of disease) versus absence (e.g., harm caused due to lacking support). This study demonstrates that when providers are overcapacity, under-resourced, and burning out, caregiver work is at its peak of importance in maintaining quality and person- and family-centred care. It must therefore be considered unacceptable by leaders to implement such policy without consulting the growing body of evidence on the importance of caregiver inclusion. This first recommendation aligns with Montesanti et al.’s [22]. family presence policy recommendations to (A) better balance the risk of spreading disease versus harm from inadequate caregiver support, (B) ensure policies reflect caregivers as essential to functional, comprehensive care, and (C) include safety measures to support caregiver presence (e.g., on the spot rapid testing, personal protective equipment). Considering that caregiver inclusion in policy development will ensure patient- and family-centred values are maintained in times of crisis and may contribute to reduced harm [81, 86, 87], future research may centre on finding optimal integration of caregivers onto healthcare teams prior to any future public health emergencies.

The second recommendation rooted from this work is to define key concepts in restrictive policy to promote equitable enforcement. The “no visitors” policy implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic did not initially distinguish between social visitors and essential caregivers who support patient care and safety [22, 81, 88]. This “one-size fits all approach” [21] hindered care-provision and patient and caregiver engagement [89, 90]. Making this distinction may contribute to more nuanced rules that decrease the number of non-essential individuals traversing the hospital during a crisis while also granting patients their right to be supported [69]. Further, while policy must adapt to changing circumstances, as was the case during the COVID-19 pandemic, rapid policy changes, as demonstrated in this study, often lead to a lack of clarity for providers and inconsistent, inequitable implementation. Future restrictive policy must therefore be thoughtful in the number of changes made within a short time span, which will require a clear, effective dissemination strategy. It may be beneficial for additional research to bring together policy makers with patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers who were affected by the “no visitors” restrictions to better outline optimal plans in preparation for future crises.

The third recommendation is to formalize exceptions for patients who are dying on humanitarian grounds to alleviate moral distress for healthcare providers and complex grief for patients and families. This includes more flexibility to account for patients’ personal context and health status [22]. While there has been noted ethical complexity surrounding the “no visitors” policy (e.g., concern about the potential spread of disease [85] versus the negative impact of caregiver restrictions [49]), there has been moral clarity on the right of dying patients to be supported [21, 85, 91]. Dying alone in the COVID-19 context has been described as a “vile” occurrence [92] with some reporting more fear of dying alone than contracting COVID-19 [93]. Recognizing the failures of the “no visitors” policy and related harm caused, an ethical framework involving three restriction levels (stratified by patients with and without COVID-19) has been posed by Russell et al. [87] to prepare for future crises; centering the patient in their experience of death, conditions are posed to allow for visitation by multiple individuals even for those with COVID-19. However, the OHA and The Change Foundation [2] noted the challenge of ensuring flexibility (personalization) in visiting policies while also keeping them consistent (standardization).

This study captured many perspectives on caregiving, including varied health service roles, but the racial and educational profile of participants lacked diversity (participants were primarily white and highly educated). It is possible that since participants’ accounts were retrospective that there may have been biases in their recall of events that took place. It also may be that the difficulties participants faced in navigating care amidst the “no visitors” restrictions may become exacerbated when considering those in less privileged positions. The study was also localized to Ontario; it is plausible that other provinces or territories may have experienced or interpreted the “no visitor” restrictions with different nuances.

Conclusions

Patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers and decision-makers shared key learnings from the “no visitors” policy implemented in hospitals during the COVID-19 pandemic. Though balancing risk, safety, and quality of life is a difficult health system task during an uncertain, rapidly evolving health crisis, the rich experiential data garnered in this study and lessons learned from the pandemic highlight the importance of being better prepared to consider an alternative balance between controlling the spread of infectious disease and ensuring that caregivers are included as essential members of the healthcare team. Overall, this study demonstrates quality caregiver support as a social determinant of health and patient safety, which becomes hindered when policy does not account for caregivers’ burden of work. The absence of caregivers in-hospital during the COVID-19 pandemic highlighted their work as integral to functional and comprehensive healthcare and their inclusion as central to in-hospital person- and family-centred care, ultimately reinforcing caregivers as essential frontline members of the cancer care team. Future system-level priorities for action may include better preparation for future public health crises by formalizing the integration of caregivers as part of healthcare teams, prioritizing systematization across Canada to ensure equitable implementation, and drafting action plans for caregiver inclusion amidst restrictive measures.
