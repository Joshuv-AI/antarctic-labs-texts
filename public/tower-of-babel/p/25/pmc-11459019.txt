Success of focused transthoracic echocardiography locations for cardiac visualization during cardiac arrest: A video-review analysis
Authors: Daniel M. Rolston, Ghania Haddad, Nicole Sales, Daniel Jafari, Margaret Gorlin, Robert Ellspermann, Mathew Nelson, Timmy Li, Allison Cohen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveOur primary objective was to determine if there was a difference in success of cardiac visualization by focused transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) location (subxiphoid, parasternal, or apical) during chest compression interruptions among cardiac arrest patients. Secondarily, we sought to determine whether there were differences in chest compression interruption times with the focused TTE locations.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of video-recorded, adult, cardiac arrest resuscitations in a quaternary care Emergency Department from 11/2018 to 11/2023. Focused TTE was successful if 1) cardiac visualization was seen on video review, or 2) cardiac visualization was discussed in the recording. A chi-squared test was used to assess differences in success and ANOVA was used to assess differences in interruption times based on TTE locations. Repeated measures multivariable regression models were constructed to control for clinically relevant variables for the primary and secondary objectives.Results136 patients and 365 focused TTE attempts were included in the study (241 subxiphoid, 101 parasternal, and 23 apical). There was no difference in the success rate: subxiphoid 83.4%, parasternal 88.1%, and apical 95.7% (p = 0.190) or in multivariable regression analysis (p = 0.189). There was no difference in the mean chest compression interruption time for each site: subxiphoid 15 sec. (IQR 12–23 sec.), parasternal 17 sec. (IQR 11–22 sec.), and apical 19 sec. (IQR 15–25 sec., p = 0.446) or in multivariable logistic regression analysis (p = 0.803). Sonographers with ≥ 50 quality assured focused TTEs had higher success than those without (94.4% vs. 75.1%; p < 0.001).ConclusionsIn cardiac arrest, the parasternal and apical TTE locations had similar success of cardiac visualization and similar compression interruption times to the more commonly used subxiphoid location.

Introduction

Focused transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is a valuable tool for the management of patients in cardiac arrest, allowing the identification of reversible causes of cardiac arrest (pericardial tamponade or massive pulmonary embolism with right ventricular enlargement and/or clot in transit), and to determine the presence or absence of cardiac activity.1 Unfortunately, several studies have shown that echocardiography is associated with prolonged chest compression interruptions.2, 3, 4 Additional studies have proposed solutions to reduce chest compression interruptions;5, 6 however, no previous studies evaluated which TTE location is associated with the highest success of cardiac visualization or the shortest interruption of chest compressions during a pulse check in cardiac arrest.

Currently, the subxiphoid (subcostal) location is often the initial cardiac window obtained to visualize the heart in patients in cardiac arrest because it avoids interfering with chest compressions and is often easier to access due to defibrillator pads. One prior study of focused TTE in simulated cardiac arrest scenarios found better image quality and shorter image acquisition times with parasternal in comparison to subxiphoid locations.7 We could not identify any studies assessing apical TTE location in cardiac arrest, and there is currently no consensus regarding the ideal location to obtain focused TTE during cardiac arrest.

The primary objective of this study was to determine if there was a difference in success of cardiac visualization by focused TTE location (subxiphoid, parasternal, or apical) during a pulse check in video-recordings of cardiac arrest resuscitations. Secondarily, we sought to determine whether there were differences in chest compression interruption times with the focused TTE locations.

Methods

Study design, setting, participants

This is a retrospective cohort study of video-recorded non-traumatic, adult (≥18-year-old) cardiac arrest patients in a quaternary care Emergency Department (ED) from 11/16/2018 to 11/29/2023. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement was followed in the design and reporting of this study.8 This study was approved by our Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed consent.

In general, a LUCAS-3 (Stryker®, Portage, MI, United States) mechanical chest compression device (MCCD) is used to manage cardiac arrest patients in our ED unless they arrive on a different MCCD, the device does not fit because of body habitus, or the treating team is not obtaining adequate objective measures of chest compression quality (such as end tidal carbon dioxide or arterial line blood pressure) with the MCCD.9 We advocate for attempted TTE cardiac visualization prior to chest compression interruption, utilizing a countdown to pulse check, and recording the TTE during the pause to review after chest compressions are restarted. Video recordings of cardiac arrest patients were reviewed by two Critical Care fellowship-trained Emergency physicians using methods previously published.9, 10 Briefly, we have 3 video cameras in our resuscitation bays: at the foot of the bed is a 12 megapixel fish eye camera (with audio), at the head of the bed is a 3 megapixel camera, and at the right foot of the patient directed at the patient and the monitor is another 3 megapixel camera (also with audio). Videos were deleted after 28 days until January 2022 and then 90 days thereafter per institutional policy so we were unable to re-review videos to obtain any missing data. Videos were reviewed using TruVision software until January 2023 and Avigilon software thereafter. Demographics and outcomes data, as well as the variables extracted from Video review detailed below are entered into a secured Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) data registry and maintained by our institution.

Study outcomes and variables

During each video-recorded interruption in chest compressions where focused TTE was done, we recorded the location of TTE (subxiphoid, parasternal, apical) using all 3 camera positions for accuracy, the associated duration of the interruption in chest compressions, and the credentialing status of the sonographer (credentialed if Emergency Medicine board-eligible and completed at least 50 echocardiography examinations that were reviewed for quality assurance or if completed an Emergency Ultrasound fellowship). Ultrasound attempts during return of spontaneous circulation were not recorded or included in analysis. Cardiac visualization was deemed successful if 1) the heart could be visualized on the ultrasound machine in the video recording (again using all 3 camera positions), or 2) if the treating team commented on the visualization of the heart in the video recording. If neither of these criteria were present, cardiac visualization was deemed unsuccessful. We attempted to confirm quality of cardiac visualization with review of saved images in QPath (Telexy Healthcare, Maple Ridge, BC, Canada); however, the majority of ultrasound clips were either not recorded or we were unable to match time on the video recording software with time on the ultrasound machine as these were not synced.

Video recordings were also reviewed for the initial ED rhythm, whether the patient was defibrillated in the ED, whether they had a MCCD device applied on arrival or in the ED, and whether they were intubated on arrival or in the ED. Patient charts and pre-hospital care reports were also reviewed to obtain demographic data.

Sample size

Since this was a pilot study and the success rate of focused TTE during pulse checks in cardiac arrest was not known prior to this study, we did not perform formal power and sample size calculations. However, we aimed to include ≥ 100 focused TTE attempts in the parasternal location.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the overall study sample. For the primary outcome, a chi-square test for association was used to assess for differences in success associated with the subxiphoid, parasternal, and apical TTE locations. A repeated measures multivariable logistic regression model, accounting for repeated observations within patients, was constructed to control for available clinically relevant variables (sonographer experience [credentialing status]; the duration of chest compression interruption; whether the patient was defibrillated in the ED; and whether a MCCD was in place).

For the secondary outcome, duration of chest compression interruption variable was not normally distributed, therefore, a logarithmic transformation was applied to encourage normality. Analysis of the log-transformed duration of interruption variable by TTE location was performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA). A non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was also performed on the non-log transformed data. A repeated measures multivariable regression model of log-transformed duration of interruption data was constructed to better adhere to regression model assumptions while controlling for clinically relevant variables (patient age; sonographer credentialing; initial ED rhythm; whether the patient was defibrillated in the ED; whether the patient was intubated; and whether a MCCD was in place). The log transformed data was converted back to obtain measurements in seconds.

There was no difference in the median chest compression interruption time for each site: 15 s (IQR: 12–23 s) for subxiphoid, 17 s (IQR: 11–22 s) for parasternal, and 19 s for (IQR: 15–25 s) for apical (p = 0.446). Additionally, the repeated measures multivariable regression analysis found no difference between interruption times for each site: 15.1 sec. (95% CI: 13.2–17.3 sec.) for subxiphoid, 15.9 sec. (95% CI: 13.4–18.8 sec.) for parasternal, and 16.2 sec. (95% CI: 12.1–21.6 sec.) for apical (p = 0.803).

Overall, 49.0 % of focused TTEs were performed by credentialed sonographers, 49.6% by non-credentialed sonographers, and 1.4% had unknown credentialing status. The overall success of focused TTE for cardiac visualization was 94.4% for credentialed versus 75.1% for non-credentialed sonographers (p < 0.001). This was similar in the repeated measures multivariable regression analysis: 96.2% (95% CI: 90.1–98.6%) for credentialed versus 80.9% (95% CI: 65.7–90.3%) for non-credentialed sonographers (p < 0.001). The duration of chest compression interruptions did not differ by credentialing status: 16 sec. (IQR: 12–22 sec.) for credentialed versus 17 sec. (IQR: 13–24 sec.) for non-credentialed sonographers (p = 0.962). This was also similar in the repeated measures multivariable regression analysis: 15.5 sec. (95% CI: 12.1–19.8 sec.) for credentialed versus 16.3 sec. (95% CI: 13.1–20.2 sec.) for non-credentialed sonographers (p = 0.552) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this video review study of cardiac arrest resuscitations, we found similar success rates and interruption times between focused TTE attempts at the subxiphoid, parasternal, and apical locations. Despite its limitations, this study reflects real-world experience of Emergency clinicians' ability to obtain cardiac ultrasound images during time-sensitive, stressful cardiac arrest resuscitations.

Approximately two thirds of focused TTE attempts were at the subxiphoid location during cardiac arrest. This is likely because defibrillator pads rarely cover this area, and sonographers can attempt to visualize the heart during active chest compressions prior to pausing. Further, most of our authors note that they were taught to use the subxiphoid location in cardiac arrest during their Emergency Medicine residency training. However, the subxiphoid location had non-significantly lower success and similar interruption times, suggesting its predominance may not be warranted, and the parasternal and apical locations should be considered more often in certain patients. For example, clinicians may be more successful visualizing the heart in obese patients with protuberant abdomens if the parasternal or apical position is used. Unfortunately, 68.1% of our database is missing height or weight, likely because many patients did not survive, and height and weight were not consistently recorded, so we were unable to assess success rates in obese patients.

While we were not able to assess the quality of the views obtained, the video reviewers and sonographers note that when using the parasternal or apical approach, sonographers actively move the ultrasound probe more than with the subxiphoid, searching for an echocardiographic window around the MCCD or chest compressor during active chest compressions. For the parasternal attempts sonographers often use positions that are more lateral and/or higher on the chest than typical. For the apical they often use positions that are slightly more medial as seen in an apical 5-chamber view.

Additionally, the defibrillator pad location’s impairment of focused TTE should be considered during placement. Since most patients presenting to the ED are in non-shockable rhythms, and focused TTE is most helpful in these patients, right-anterior and left-lateral pad placement would improve access to the parasternal and apical locations for focused TTE in cardiac arrest patients with non-shockable rhythms. Anterior-posterior pad placement should be reserved for shockable rhythms, along with consideration of dual sequential defibrillation.11

In our ED, mechanical chest compressions were used in over 80% of cardiac arrest resuscitations. Since the suction cup and piston of the MCCD is left in place during an interruption in compression, it can interfere with probe placement, which may decrease the likelihood of success and prolong the time required to obtain cardiac visualization in the parasternal location. This may be the reason sonographers used apical TTE locations. Conversely, the more standardized chest compressions delivered by a MCCD may make the parasternal and apical locations more accessible than when hands, arms, and bodies are obstructing access. For these reasons, we analyzed the success and interruption times while controlling for MCCD placement. However, in our cohort, after controlling for MCCD placement alone and with other factors, there appears to be no difference in success rate.

Experienced sonographers are more successful obtaining TTE images,12 and we found that credentialed sonographers (who have performed at least 50 focused TTEs that were reviewed for quality assurance based on institutional credentialing policy) were more successful than non-credentialed sonographers; however, interruption times were similar between credentialed and non-credentialed sonographers. Both the parasternal and subxiphoid locations had similarly improved success rates by credentialed versus non-credentialed sonographers (>20%) while the apical location had similarly high success rates between the two groups, but there were too few apical ultrasounds to make meaningful comparisons. Therefore, in our multivariable regression analyses, we controlled for credentialing status of the sonographer. The results of our study support prior research suggesting more experienced sonographers should perform focused TTE during cardiac arrest as they have higher success rates of cardiac visualization,13 although the duration of interruptions were not significantly different between the two groups.

International guidelines recommend limiting chest compression interruptions to no longer than 10 sec.,14 and suggests against the routine use of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR).15 Despite our institution performing department-wide cardiac arrest video review every other week for quality improvement with a focus on minimizing interruptions in chest compressions and frequent teaching on the risks of focused TTE, as well as providing continuous individual and team feedback to clinicians of all levels based on the video review; the median chest compression interruption time with focused TTE in our study was 16 sec., in comparison to our overall median chest compression interruption time which is 13 sec..16 Our interruption time with focused TTE is shorter than previous observational studies,2, 3, 4 but longer than those involving interventions to decrease interruption time.5, 6 These interventions involve limiting TTE assessments to determine presence of cardiac activity, visualize reversible causes like pericardial effusions and right heart strain,5 and pre-visualizing the heart and recording ultrasound clips to review when chest compressions are restarted.6 While our interruption times may be influenced by other simultaneous procedures being performed (i.e., intubation, pad placement, or MCCD placement); our study further highlights the need to have institutional guidelines in place regarding POCUS during CPR to limit the number and duration of TTE attempts and associated compression interruptions. Our data suggest that experienced sonographers should select the appropriate TTE location to increase the chance of successful cardiac visualization, avoiding further chest compression interruptions to meet guideline recommendations.

Limitations

There are several limitations of this study to acknowledge. First, this is a single centre study with an elderly patient population and a high percentage of MCCD use to provide chest compressions, limiting generalizability. Second, the video reviewers were not blinded to the study hypothesis which may have led to observer bias. Third, because of the time required to review cardiac arrest videos, only one physician reviewed and obtained the focused TTE data for each cardiac arrest; therefore, we could not assess inter-rater reliability. We are currently working on a study to assess inter-rater reliability in cardiac arrest video review. Fourth, we attempted but were unable to assess the quality of focused TTE images/clips because the majority of TTE images/clips were either not stored in QPath or could not be synced with video review data. This is in line with previous research demonstrating 70.5% of cardiac arrest ultrasounds in the ED are not recorded.17 However, we still believe our methods are valid because of the 3 camera angles available to visualize the heart on the ultrasound machine, or if not visualized, the requirement that clinicians discussed the focused TTE findings. Unfortunately, we did not record whether cardiac visualization or clinician discussion was used to define success. Both video reviewers believe the ultrasound screen was visualized in over 50% of focused TTE attempts. On the other hand, our findings are more generalizable as we capture phantom ultrasounds and focused TTEs by less experienced clinicians. Fifth, the small number of patients with multiple observations per patient may reduce the power of the study for detecting a still clinically meaningful difference. Finally, the comparisons of the duration of CPR interruptions could be affected by other procedures performed concomitantly (pulse checks, central line placements, etc.), which we will attempt to assess in another manuscript.

Conclusion

When performing focused TTE during cardiac arrest resuscitations in the ED, the parasternal and apical locations had similar success of cardiac visualization and similar interruption times to the more commonly used subxiphoid location. However, experienced sonographers had higher success irrespective of TTE location. Further studies are needed to verify these findings and determine if there are clinically meaningful differences in success.
