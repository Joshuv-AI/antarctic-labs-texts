Infective Endocarditis and Meningitis in a Healthy Newborn Without Underlying Heart Disease, Due to Streptococcus Agalactiae–A Case Report
Authors: Dalal S Idris, Muath M Al Ghamdi, Maria L Bello Valls, Mohammed Fararjeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStreptococcus Agalactiae endocarditis is a rare clinical entity in newborns with a normal structured heart. It is generally characterized by acute onset, the presence of large vegetations, rapid valvular destruction and frequent complications, particularly embolization. Mortality is high with medical therapy alone. We are reporting a rare case of infective endocarditis (IE) with mitral valve vegetation in a 9 days old healthy newborn following Streptococcus Agalactiae sepsis, complicated by meningitis and micro-abscesses. Mitral valve (MV) vegetation was removed surgically; the patient received intravenous antibiotics for six weeks. Transthoracic echocardiography after surgery showed severe MV regurgitation and severe pulmonary hypertension which was successfully managed by conservative care with cardiac medications.Keywords: Newborn, Infective endocarditis, Mitral valve vegetation, Streptococcus Agalactiae sepsis

Introduction

Mitral valve (MV) vegetation is rarely seen in neonates, especially during the first days of life. There are only few cases reported around the world on MV vegetation in full term healthy neonates right after birth. Several case reports were reviewed in which patients usually presented with MV vegetation after 1 month of life [1,2]. Two other cases of patients with MV vegetation were reported but without mentioning any specific details of these cases, including the age [3]. MV vegetation usually presents in patients who had invasive interventions such as insertion of central lines or umbilical venous catheters. We report a 9 days old infant who was previously healthy with no intracardiac cardiac anomalies except for a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), no insertion of central or peripheral lines, but found to have a large MV vegetation which required surgical intervention.

Case presentation

A 9 days old male neonate, previously healthy and discharged on the seventh day of life from nursery of the Security Forces Hospital Dammam in general good condition after he stayed few days to receive phototherapy for neonatal jaundice. He was born as a full term at 38 weeks of gestation by normal vaginal delivery, appropriate for gestational age, APGAR score was 8 at the first minute and 9 at 5 minutes. Birth weight was 2.28 kg, although infant of a diabetic mother. Mother was a 39 years old lady, G4P2A1, known to have type 2 DM, initially on treatment with Metformin then shifted to Insulin. High vaginal swab was not taken. Examination at birth demonstrated machinery murmur at left infraclavicular area for which the baby was scheduled to be followed up, as an outpatient. He presented again in hospital with a history of continuous fever over 2 days prior to admission, reaching up to 39 degrees Celsius (C) at home, responding to antipyretics. Otherwise history was unremarkable. Initial evaluation showed that he was irritable, continuously crying. Vitally temperature was 39.9 C, heart rate 172 beats/min, RR: 40–45 breath/minute during crying, saturation >93% in room air. Neurological examination was normal, reactive, moving 4 limbs, with anterior fontanel at normal level. Cardiovascular examination showed besides the tachycardia a grade 2/6 continuous murmur. Respiratory examination showed subcostal and intercostal retractions. Examination of ears, nose, throat and abdomen were all normal and unremarkable. The initial working diagnosis was neonatal sepsis, thus intravenous (IV) cefotaxime and vancomycin were started. Blood test results showed: Venous blood gas analysis with pH = 7.32, PCO2 = 52.4 mmHg, HCO3 = 27.3, SPO2 = 90.2 %, Lactate 36 mg/dl, sodium 132, RBS = 139 mg/dl, ALT 7, AST 18, Ammonia 22. Blood and cerebro spinal fluid (CSF) cultures were positive to Streptococcus Agalactiae (group B streptococcus [GBS]). Considering possible cardiac complications of infective endocarditis (IE), transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was requested and performed. TTE showed a vegetation on the anterior leaflet of the MV about 7 × 4 mm in size, mild mitral regurgitation (MR), moderate PDA with left to right shunt. Follow-up TTE was done next day and demonstrated that the vegetation on the anterior MV had increased in size, now about 9 × 5 mm in size, but additionally also abnormal thickening of the posterior mitral leaflet (p2–p3), mild MR with an estimated mean inflow gradient of 3 mmHg, mild turbulence flow seen at the aortic valve with an estimated peak gradient of 13 mmHg, mild to moderate PDA (size 1.5 mm) with left to right shunt, patent foramen ovale (PFO) with left to right shunt. Due to the complexity of patient's case the baby was transferred on the third day of admission to King Abdulaziz Cardiac Center in Riyadh, a higher tertiary cardiac center, for further evaluation, medical and surgical intervention. Patient arrived in hemodynamically stable condition, detailed TTE was repeated confirming above mentioned findings (Fig. 1A–D).

Blood cultures and lumbar puncture (LP) were repeated, infection disease (ID) team recommended to perform brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to rule out the presence of brain abscess. The repeated septic workup showed negative cultures with trending down of the main inflammatory markers while brain MRI showed a micro abscess (Fig. 2A–C).

Brain MRI images (A, B) showing multiple foci of high signal intensity on T2, slightly high T1 with the diffusion resection noted at the bilateral corona radiate, periventricular of bilateral posterior horn of the lateral ventricles and genu of corpus callosum. No blooming artefact. Preserved grey-white matter differentiation according to patient's age. No extra-axial haemorrhage or hydrocephalus.

Immunological screening was done with an unremarkable result. Because of low sensitivity vancomycin was discontinued and treatment was continued with IV cefotaxime (dose 50 mg/kg/day) every 8 hours for 6 weeks and patient then cleared for surgical intervention.

The case was reviewed in a multidisciplinary team meeting, and surgical removal of the vegetation was planned. On the fifth day after diagnosis, the patient underwent surgery. A median sternotomy was performed, followed by aorto-bicaval cannulation, and cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated. The PDA was ligated, and after applying the aortic cross-clamp, cardioplegia was administered. The right atrium was opened, and through the atrial septum, the mitral valve vegetation was excised. The anterior mitral leaflet appeared thickened and inflamed. The ASD was subsequently closed. After deairing the heart, the cross-clamp was removed. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography confirmed an intact repair with no significant residual lesions. Once hemostasis was secured, the sternum was reapproximated with standard sternal wires, and the wound was closed in layers with a sterile adhesive dressing. Although there was a risk of arrhythmia, bleeding, and post-pericardiotomy syndrome. The excised specimen was sent for culture but showed no growth.

On postoperative day 7, transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe mitral regurgitation and pulmonary hypertension. Conservative cardiac management was optimized. However, 48 hours later, the patient developed sudden cardiogenic shock and was transferred to the Pediatric Cardiac Intensive Care Unit (PCICU), where he received inotropic support for two weeks, along with steroids (prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg/day for two weeks), consistent with a possible picture of carditis. At that time, no further surgical options were available, but the patient showed gradual hemodynamic improvement. From an infectious standpoint, he remained stable and was later transferred to the Pediatric Cardiac Step-Down Unit (PCSD), where he completed his treatment without further complications. He was discharged home in stable condition.

The patient has been followed regularly in our hospital. During the first two years, follow-up visits were scheduled every six months. He remained stable with no cardiology-related complications and no neurological deficits. He is now 4 years old, followed by general pediatrics as a normal child and seen annually by cardiology. The most recent echocardiogram, performed in October 2024, showed thickened mitral valve leaflets with mild regurgitation, a mean mitral inflow gradient of 3 mmHg, no significant tricuspid regurgitation, and no evidence of LVOT or RVOT obstruction. Left ventricular systolic function was preserved (EF 74%, FS 36%), with no pericardial effusion.

Discussion

Streptococcus agalactiae (group B streptococcus [GBS]) is the major etiologic agent of invasive infections in newborns, 5–25% of whom develop meningitis [5,6]. In industrialized countries, infection with this agent causes severe disease in form of sepsis, pneumonia, meningitis, osteomyelitis, and soft tissue infections. GBS has also been increasingly recognized as an important pathogen in immunocompromised and elderly patients. GBS emerged also as an important cause of neonatal infections since 1960s [4]. Maternal transmission of S. agalactiae during delivery can be prevented by prenatal screening and peripartal antibiotic prophylaxis. S. agalactiae can also cause bacteraemia in adults, and this is complicated by endocarditis in 2–6% of cases, and S. agalactiae meningitis associated with endocarditis is exceptional in adults. Neonatal endocarditis caused by S. agalactiae is very rare [7]. The patient's case described here appears to be one of the few cases reported on S. agalactiae meningitis associated with endocarditis in a neonate. Surgical intervention was considered; however, alternative non-surgical approaches such as the ANGIOVAC system are not yet established in neonates. Although a few cases have been reported in the pediatric age group, they were primarily performed on the right side [9]. It is noteworthy that most paediatric patients with endocarditis have an underlying heart disease or other risk factors [8], which did not appear to be the case in our patient.

We have shown in this report that, although exceptional, S. agalactiae endocarditis may occur in neonates with no underlying heart disease.

Conclusions

It is important to consider Streptococcus Agalactiae as a cause of neonatal sepsis and to recognize infective endocarditis and meningitis as possible clinical manifestations of this infection. This infection may occur in neonates with no underlying heart disease and, can lead to a fatal complication without the appropriate treatment in a short period. The implementation of CDC guidelines for group B streptococci (GBS) disease prevention is also of high importance, because these recommendations resulted worldwide in a significant decline of invasive neonatal S. agalactiae infections.
