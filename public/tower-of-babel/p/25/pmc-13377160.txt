Antibiotic Prophylaxis for Congenital Cardiac Catheterizations
Authors: Bayan Issa, Kamel Shibbani, Isabella Zaniletti, Matt Hall, Henri Justino, Howaida El-Said, Brent M. Gordon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is significant antibiotic prophylaxis (ABP) variability before congenital cardiac catheterization procedures. The true incidence of procedure-related infective endocarditis (IE) remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate national periprocedural ABP utilization patterns and determine whether ABP dosing strategies are associated with early postprocedural IE.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from the Pediatric Health Information System from 39 pediatric centers. Data were collected on the following procedures: patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) device occlusion, atrial septal defect device occlusion, ventricular septal defect device occlusion, transcatheter pulmonary valve replacement (TPVR), and pulmonary or aortic balloon valvuloplasty. Outcomes assessed were: 1) rate and pattern of periprocedural antibiotic use and 2) incidence of early IE within 30-days postprocedure.ResultsA total of 20,580 procedures were analyzed. Antibiotic dosing varied significantly by procedure, with the proportion of patients receiving ≥2 doses ranging from 38.8% for PDA closure to 85.7% for TPVR. A total of 15 IE cases (0.07%) were identified within 30 days, more than half of which followed TPVR (53%). The incidence of early IE varied significantly by procedure type (P = 0.003): TPVR (0.27%), pulmonary valvuloplasty (0.12%), atrial septal defect closure (0.04%), and PDA closure (0.02%). There was no measurable association between ABP and early IE.ConclusionsProcedure-related IE is exceedingly rare and is significantly related to procedure type. We did not observe a measurable association between early IE and ABP dosing. These findings underscore the need for standardized, evidence-based guidelines to optimize antibiotic protocols for congenital cardiac catheterization.

The risk of infective endocarditis (IE) is significantly increased in patients with congenital heart disease (CHD) compared to the general population. A retrospective national registry from Sweden calculated an incidence rate of IE of 65.5 per 100,000 person-years in patients with CHD as compared to 1.8 per 100,000 person-years in matched controls, and a cumulative incidence of 8.5% by age 87 years in patients with CHD.1 A Canadian population-based study found a cumulative IE incidence of 6.1 per 1,000 children with CHD by age 18, whereas a Norwegian registry reported an incidence of 2.2 per 10,000 person-years in children and adolescents with CHD.2,3 The risk appears to be greatest in those with previous endocarditis, prosthetic valves (surgical or transcatheter), prosthetic material placed within the past 6 months, residual intracardiac shunts adjacent to prosthetic material, uncorrected cyanotic heart disease,4 and in those <3 years of age.2

During cardiac catheterization, trauma to the endocardium can create a substrate for infection. This is especially true in patients with CHD where abnormal cardiac structures and flow patterns can increase susceptibility to endocarditis.5 In clinical practice, the risk of infectious complications from routine diagnostic catheterization is exceedingly low, and sterile technique is considered the primary preventive measure by the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions and the American Heart Association.6 Nevertheless, perioperative antibiotics (commonly a cephalosporin) are recommended to prevent device infection for interventional procedures involving device implantation.5,7

Despite these recommendations, there is significant practice pattern variation in periprocedural antibiotic prophylaxis (ABP) administration and an unclear incidence of early procedure-related IE in congenital cardiac catheterization.8 We sought to evaluate current ABP practice patterns for typical cardiac catheterization procedures and determine if ABP dosing strategies were associated with development of early postprocedural IE.

Methods

The Pediatric Health Information System (PHIS) (Children’s Hospital Association, Lenexa, Kansas) database was reviewed to identify all pediatric patients undergoing congenital cardiac catheterization from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2024. PHIS is a comprehensive administrative database that compiles billing data from 50 children’s hospitals across the United States. PHIS captures patient-level data from a variety of care settings, including inpatient/observation stays, outpatient surgeries, and emergency department visits.

Study population

Our cohort included inpatient, observation, and ambulatory surgery cases undergoing congenital cardiac catheterization to perform patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) device occlusion, atrial septal defect (ASD) device occlusion, ventricular septal defect (VSD) device occlusion, transcatheter pulmonary valve replacement (TPVR), and pulmonary or aortic balloon valvuloplasty. Cases were identified based on International Classification of Diseases version 10 (ICD-10) and Current Procedural Terminology codes (Supplemental Table 1). Age restrictions were applied to each cohort: 6 months to 18 years of age for PDA, ASD, and VSD device occlusion, 3-25 years for TPVR, and 2 months to 18 years for pulmonary and aortic balloon valvuloplasty. PHIS centers were excluded from analysis if they did not consistently contribute data during the study period leaving thirty-nine contributing centers.

Outcome measures

Clinical outcomes of interest were: 1) the rate of periprocedural antibiotic use—defined as the number of cases with ABP within 24 hours of a procedure divided by the total number of cases; 2) the number of doses of ABP; and 3) the rate of early postprocedural endocarditis within 30 days of an index procedure. The time frame of 30 days postprocedure was chosen in consultation with an infectious disease specialist. This timeframe most closely reflects infections plausibly related to procedural bacteremia and therefore potentially modifiable by periprocedural ABP. Patients were determined to have IE based on the presence of ICD-10 diagnosis codes for I330, I339, I38, or I39. IE reflects procedure level-events. Antibiotic administration tracked through PHIS included intravenously administered cefazolin, clindamycin, and vancomycin. Billing data were used to identify cases receiving IV antibiotics within 24 hours of the procedure.

Statistical analyses

Demographic and clinical characteristics were represented as counts and percentages for categorical variables, and median and IQR for continuous variables. Differences between groups were analyzed with chi-square, Kruskal-Wallis, and Wilcoxon rank sum tests, as appropriate. Incidence of 30-day endocarditis was calculated as the proportion of individuals with an event within each cohort. For each cohort, endocarditis incidence and corresponding 95% CIs were estimated using the exact binomial (Clopper-Pearson) method. Each cohort was compared with all remaining cohorts combined; P values were Bonferroni adjusted for multiple testing (family-wise error rate = 0.05). The Bonferroni correction was applied to provide a conservative control of type I error in the context of multiple comparisons, particularly given the observational design and potential for spurious associations. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide (v8.3; SAS Institute). Statistical significance was evaluated at P < 0.05. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained before initiation of the study ensuring compliance with ethical standards for this retrospective review study.

Results

The study cohort comprised 20,580 procedures performed between 2016 and 2024, including ASD device occlusion (n = 7,002), PDA device occlusion (n = 6,592), VSD device occlusion (n = 327), TPVR (n = 3,013), pulmonary valvuloplasty (n = 2,442), and aortic valvuloplasty (n = 1,204) (Figure 1). Rates for the identified procedures were consistent over the study period (Figure 2). The median age at the time of the intervention was 62 months (IQR: 21-142) and 54.9% were females (Table 1). In total, 19.5% of the cohort received no ABP and 80.5% received at least 1 dose of ABP (31.1% received 1 dose, and 49.4% received 2 or more doses). Endocarditis

Across 20,580 procedures performed during the study period, only 15 cases of early postprocedural IE were identified within 30 days, corresponding to an overall incidence of 0.07%. The 15 cases of early IE occurred in 14 patients: 1 patient underwent both ASD device occlusion and TPVR. Procedure-specific incidence of early IE was uniformly low; 0.266% (8/3,013) in TPVR, 0.123% (3/2,442) in pulmonary valvuloplasty, 0.043% (3/7,002) in ASD device occlusion, and 0.015% (1/6,592) in PDA device occlusion. Of the 15 cases of early IE, 2 had a prior history of IE prior to cardiac catheterization: 1 occurred 2 months before pulmonary balloon valvuloplasty and the other 10 months before ASD device occlusion.

Association between endocarditis and periprocedural antibiotics prophylaxis

When comparing procedures with any periprocedural ABP (≥1 dose) to those without, the incidence of early IE was 0.07% (13/16,563) vs 0.08% (2/4,017), respectively. There was no statistically significant association between periprocedural ABP use and early IE (OR: 1.59; 95% CI: 0.36-7.14; P = 0.75). (Table 2). Discussion

In this large, multicenter analysis of more than 20,000 congenital cardiac catheterization interventions, we found that the overall incidence of procedure-related early IE was exceedingly low (0.072%) (Central Illustration). Notably, early IE risk varied significantly by procedure type, with TPVR exhibiting the highest incidence in our cohort. TPVR accounted for more than half of all early IE cases, which underscores its unique vulnerability. This higher incidence of early IE post TPVR is intriguing. The inherent susceptibility of bioprosthetic valves to IE may be an explanation, though the presence of a signal in a timeframe as short as 30 days raises the possibility of other mechanisms, including perioperative bacteremia, as well as contamination at the time of device implantation. Procedures for TPVR generally involve more manual manipulation of the implantable devices as compared to other procedures such as ASD device occlusion, PDA device occlusion, or valvuloplasty. Contamination at the time of the procedure may therefore provide a plausible explanation for this higher incidence.

Another key finding of our analysis is that antibiotic strategy did not demonstrate a relationship with early IE risk. Indeed, we found no association between periprocedural ABP use—either presence or number of doses—and the development of early IE. Given the rarity of early IE in this population, any true association between ABP use and early IE risk may be too subtle for even a large cohort such as ours to reliably detect. Still, the lack of association raises a question about the utility of ABP for all device-related cases. Our findings support reconsideration of routine multidose ABP strategies. Given that early IE was rare regardless of ABP use, and that no protective association was observed, a far less aggressive approach may be appropriate for select cases. For non-TPVR procedures, this could take the form of omission of ABP altogether, especially when procedural contamination risk is low. This is common practice in other subspecialties. Indeed, the guidelines from the Society of Interventional Radiology recommend against the use of routine ABP for intravascular stent placement, use of closure devices, or coil embolization.9 Similarly, routine ABP is not recommended for percutaneous coronary artery stent placement.10 Such a shift would require careful follow-up, but our data raise the question of whether any ABP for cases other than valve replacement exceeds what is necessary for patient safety.

The timeframe selected for outcome assessment warrants careful consideration. We focused on IE occurring within 30 days of the procedure, as this timeframe most closely reflects infections plausibly related to procedural bacteremia and therefore potentially modifiable by periprocedural ABP. Although IE following interventions such as TPVR is known to occur months to years after valve implantation,4,11 these later events are less likely to be preventable by ABP administered in the periprocedural period. Notably, extending our analysis to include events within 60 days yielded similar findings, reinforcing the robustness of our observations with this early postprocedural window. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that our approach does not capture late-onset IE, particularly in high-risk populations such as those undergoing TPVR, in whom long-term susceptibility remains elevated. As such, although our data do not demonstrate a statistically significant association between periprocedural ABP and reduced early IE risk in TPVR, the consistently elevated incidence of IE after TPVR—both in our cohort and in prior literature—supports the continued use of prophylactic antibiotics for this procedure. This aligns with longstanding recommendations supporting antibiotic administration surrounding valve implantation procedures.12, 13, 14

Finally, the substantial practice variability observed across centers highlights an important and actionable need for standardization. Inconsistency in ABP strategies—ranging from no prophylaxis to multidose regimens within the same procedure type—reflects the absence of clear, evidence-based guidance and raises the possibility of both undertreatment and overtreatment. Some of the observed variability may stem from methodological limitation inherent to our data set. For example, TPVR cases that received periprocedural ABP but had to be aborted before valve deployment after coronary compression testing may have been coded as pulmonary valvuloplasty. This could explain the incidence of early IE in the pulmonary valvuloplasty group, a procedure that, like aortic valvuloplasty, would not be expected to increase the risk of IE. Other methodological limitations include instances where antibiotics may have been captured as ABP even when administered for unrelated reasons, such as targeted therapy for a suspected or confirmed infection in critically ill infants who underwent concurrent aortic valvuloplasty for aortic stenosis. Some variability also likely stems from postprocedural triaging. For example, the substantially smaller proportion of PDA device occlusion patients receiving 2 doses of antibiotics compared to their ASD device occlusion counterparts could be explained by the fact that most centers discharge patients after PDA device occlusion but admitted ASD device occlusion patient overnight for observation. Regardless of these nuances, the conclusion remains: developing standardized, procedure-specific ABP protocols informed by actual IE risk, rather than historical habits, would improve quality, reduce unnecessary antibiotic exposure, and better align practice with modern principles of antibiotic stewardship.

Taken together, our findings support a tailored approach to ABP: preserving more intensive prophylaxis for high-risk interventions such as TPVR, whereas considering less intensive prophylactic strategies for lower-risk procedures, and advancing the field toward standardized, evidence-driven ABP practices.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective analysis, its conclusions rely on the accuracy and completeness of clinical documentation and charting within the PHIS database. Cases of early IE or related complications presenting to hospitals outside of PHIS-participating centers may not have been captured. Regarding the higher rate of early IE observed after TPVR, the PHIS data set does not provide key procedural details such as the specific valve type used, the final right ventricular outflow tract gradient, or postimplant antiplatelet strategies—all of which have been shown to influence IE risk following TPVR.11,15,16 In addition, our analysis included only intravenously administered antibiotics, which may have led to undercounting if oral doses were given. However, the impact of this limitation is reduced by our analysis, which compared patients receiving no antibiotics to those receiving at least 1 dose, irrespective of the total number of doses administered. Another limitation was the inability to appropriately classify reason for antibiotic use on a case-by-case basis. ABP was defined using billing data within a 24-hour window, which may misclassify antibiotics administered for nonprophylactic indications; such misclassification is likely nondifferential and may bias the estimate towards the null. Data about adverse events from antibiotic administration were not collected. This limits our ability to weigh risks vs benefits for routine ABP. Furthermore, we selected a 30-day (and 60-day) window to capture early IE events most likely attributable to procedural exposure. However, we acknowledge that this approach does not capture later-onset IE, particularly following precures like TPVR, where ongoing risk persists for years. Another limitation is the inability to determine whether early IE cases were preceded by bloodstream infections or access site infections, limiting insight into potential mechanistic pathways linking catheterization and to early IE. Finally, given the limited number of events of early IE in our cohort, the observed null associations between ABP and early IE should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect limited statistical precision rather than absence of an effect. In the setting of very low event rates, even clinically meaningful protective effects may not be detectible. In addition, because of the small outcome rate, multivariable adjustment was not performed, limiting the ability to account for potential confounding.

Conclusion

In this large, multicenter analysis of ABP in the setting of congenital cardiac catheterization, we found that the risk of early IE following procedures is exceedingly low, and that procedure type—not ABP strategy—was the primary determinant of early IE risk. TPVR stood out as the procedure with the highest incidence of early IE, reinforcing its unique vulnerability and the increased risk associated with prosthetic valve implantation. In this context, maintaining the administration of ABP for TPVR remains a reasonable approach. Although we did not observe a measurable association between ABP and early IE, even for TPVR, we believe that until more robust data are available, continuation of the strategy of providing periprocedural ABP for TPVR is reasonable due to this being the procedure with highest IE risk. Conversely, for most other catheter-based interventions our findings suggest that a far less aggressive prophylactic strategy may be appropriate, including eliminating ABP altogether, but prospective studies are needed.

Finally, the marked variability in ABP practices observed across institutions underscores a pressing need for standardization of prophylaxis protocols. Multicenter studies will shed light on center-level variability and will allow the creation of procedure-specific, evidence-based ABP guidelines that would reduce unwarranted variation, enhance safety, promote antibiotic stewardship, and ensure that prophylaxis intensity is matched to actual early IE risk.
