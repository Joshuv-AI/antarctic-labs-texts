Efficacy and Safety of Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors in the Treatment of Hematologic Malignancies: An Umbrella Review of Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses
Authors: Zhengyu Yu, Zhongwang Wang, Li Xie, Jing Xu, Lang Min, Lingfeng Li, Ting Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThis umbrella review summarizes evidence from systematic reviews and meta‐analyses on the efficacy and safety of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in hematologic malignancies. To our knowledge, this is the first umbrella review to comprehensively evaluate ICIs across multiple hematologic cancers. The goal is to clarify their therapeutic potential, highlight major safety issues, and provide evidence‐based guidance for future clinical and research applications.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews up to May 2025. Systematic reviews and meta‐analyses investigating ICIs in hematologic malignancies were included. The AMSTAR tool was used to assess methodological quality, and the GRADE system was employed to evaluate the quality of evidence for each outcome.ResultsA total of 24 meta‐analyses met the inclusion criteria, covering 39 unique interventions. ICIs demonstrated significant efficacy in multiple myeloma (MM), relapsed/refractory natural killer T‐cell lymphoma (R/R NKT), relapsed/refractory chronic lymphocytic leukemia (R/R CLL), and relapsed/refractory primary mediastinal B‐cell lymphoma (R/R PMBCL). Pembrolizumab combined with lenalidomide and dexamethasone (PLD) significantly improved overall survival (OS) in MM (HR: 0.51, 95% CI: 0.32 to 0.82, high quality). In R/R NKT, sintilimab and other ICIs showed moderate overall response rates (ORR) with favorable safety profiles. Pembrolizumab also demonstrated moderate efficacy in R/R CLL and R/R PMBCL, with low rates of severe adverse events. However, the studies exhibited significant heterogeneity, and publication bias was detected in certain outcomes, highlighting the need for cautious interpretation.ConclusionsICIs show promising therapeutic potential in several hematologic malignancies, particularly R/R NKT, MM, R/R CLL, and R/R PMBCL. While safety profiles are generally favorable, certain regimens are associated with manageable toxicities. Future research should focus on optimizing combination therapies, refining patient selection criteria, and addressing study heterogeneity to enhance the clinical application of ICIs in hematologic cancers.

Introduction

Hematologic malignancies, encompassing cancers of the blood, bone marrow, and lymphatic system, pose significant global health challenges. These cancers include leukemias, lymphomas, and multiple myeloma, each marked by the uncontrolled proliferation of abnormal blood cells [1]. Leukemias, such as acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), arise from the malignant transformation of hematopoietic cells [2]. In CLL, for instance, tumor cells evade immune surveillance through mechanisms such as PD‐1/PD‐L1 interactions [3]. Lymphomas, including Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), include subtypes like diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) and primary mediastinal B‐cell lymphoma (PMBCL), each associated with varying degrees of immune dysregulation [2, 4]. Multiple myeloma, a malignancy of plasma cells, is notorious for its resistance to conventional therapies and frequent relapses [5, 6].

Traditional treatments for hematologic malignancies have historically relied on chemotherapy, which, despite its widespread use, is associated with significant side effects, including toxicity and drug resistance [7]. Targeted therapies, such as monoclonal antibodies like rituximab for NHL and blinatumomab for acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), have become integral components of treatment regimens, often in combination with chemotherapy [8]. However, the relapse or development of refractory disease remains a critical issue in aggressive forms of leukemia and lymphoma, underscoring the urgent need for more effective therapies.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), a class of immunotherapies that block immune‐inhibitory pathways such as PD‐1/PD‐L1 and CTLA‐4, have emerged as promising therapeutic agents. By preventing the inhibition of T‐cell activity, ICIs reinvigorate the immune system, enabling it to target and eliminate cancer cells [9]. ICIs like pembrolizumab, nivolumab, and avelumab have shown encouraging efficacy in a variety of solid tumors, with increasing evidence supporting their use in hematologic malignancies, including multiple myeloma, non‐Hodgkin lymphoma, and chronic lymphocytic leukemia [10]. Notable responses have been observed, but challenges remain in optimizing treatment regimens, addressing mechanisms of resistance, and mitigating immune‐related adverse events (irAEs) [11].

While numerous systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have explored ICIs in individual hematologic malignancies, their conclusions are fragmented and sometimes conflicting. Despite the growing body of literature, substantial variability in patient inclusion criteria, sample sizes, and follow‐up durations has hampered efforts to establish definitive clinical guidelines for ICI therapy in hematologic malignancies [12]. To overcome these limitations, the umbrella review methodology offers a higher level of evidence synthesis by integrating and critically appraising findings across multiple meta‐analyses. This approach allows for a unified evaluation of treatment efficacy and safety, assessment of evidence quality through tools such as AMSTAR and GRADE, and identification of knowledge gaps across disease subtypes.

To our knowledge, this is the first umbrella review to systematically evaluate both the efficacy and safety of ICIs across hematologic malignancies. By consolidating evidence from existing meta‐analyses, we aim to (1) provide a comprehensive overview of ICI performance across different hematologic cancers, (2) identify safety concerns and areas of consistent benefit, and (3) generate evidence‐based insights to guide future research design and clinical practice. This methodological framework not only strengthens the reliability of synthesized conclusions but also sets a precedent for evidence integration in emerging fields of hematologic oncology.

Methods and Analysis

Design and Registration

We systematically reviewed and analyzed data from published systematic reviews and meta‐analyses on the efficacy and safety of ICIs therapy for hematologic malignancies, adhering to PRISMA guidelines [13]. This umbrella review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis of Umbrella Reviews [14] and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews [15]. This umbrella review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251031932, https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/).

Eligibility Criteria

Systematic reviews and meta‐analyses evaluating the efficacy and safety of ICIs therapy for hematologic malignancies in all populations were included. Data for each intervention were extracted separately if a meta‐analysis reported multiple ICIs therapies. For identical ICIs interventions, the latest meta‐analysis was included if published over 24 months apart. Within a 24 month window, the one with the most prospective studies was selected; if tied, the meta‐analysis with the higher AMSTAR score was chosen [16, 17]. If the latest meta‐analysis lacks a dose–response analysis but another includes it, both were considered. Non‐English, animal, and cell culture studies were excluded. When multiple meta‐analyses investigated the same treatment or outcome, the meta‐analysis with the highest AMSTAR methodological quality was prioritized as the primary evidence source for synthesis and discussion.

Population

This umbrella review analyzes systematic reviews and meta‐analyses on ICIs therapy for hematologic malignancies, including ALL, AML, CLL, CML, HL, NHL, multiple myeloma, MPN, and MDS, among others.

Exposure

We included meta‐analyses reporting at least one ICIs intervention, with efficacy assessed using odds ratios (OR), relative risks (RR), or hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Study Designs

Only systematic reviews and meta‐analyses evaluating the efficacy and safety of ICIs in treating hematologic malignancies across diverse ethnicities, sexes, countries, and settings were included. These reviews and meta‐analyses concentrated on ICIs and provided comprehensive methods, including search strategies, inclusion/exclusion criteria, quality assessment, outcome evaluation, analytical procedures, and interpretation criteria. The original studies included in the meta‐analyses comprised randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and non‐randomized interventional clinical trials.

Information Sources

We searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews from inception to May 2025 (2025‐05‐20) for systematic reviews and meta‐analyses of interventional studies and examined the reference lists of included meta‐analyses for further articles.

Study Selection

All literature was screened using Endnote X9. After eliminating duplicates, two authors independently assessed titles, abstracts, and full texts to identify meta‐analyses that met the inclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved by a third author. Additionally, reference lists were manually searched for any potentially missed meta‐analyses (Figure 1).

Assessment of Methodological Quality

The methodological quality of each meta‐analysis was evaluated by two authors using AMSTAR, a validated tool for assessing systematic reviews and meta‐analyses [16, 19]. Health outcome evidence was assessed and classified as “high,” “moderate,” “low,” or “very low” quality using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework to draw conclusions [20]. Epidemiologic evidence for each intervention was classified into four categories: class I (convincing evidence), class II (highly suggestive evidence), class III (suggestive evidence), class IV (weak evidence), and NS (nonsignificant) (Table 1) [21, 22, 23]. To enhance transparency of evidence grading, we evaluated evidence class assignments as an integrated assessment of prespecified criteria—including statistical significance, sample size, between‐study heterogeneity (I2), and prediction interval behavior and reported the key supporting statistics (e.g., pooled estimates, confidence intervals, prediction intervals, heterogeneity metrics) alongside each result to enable independent verification of the assigned class.

Publication Bias Assessment

Tests for small‐study effects and potential publication bias were conducted only when methodologically appropriate, generally when there were ≥ 10 studies and sufficient variation in precision to support asymmetry assessment. In many syntheses in this umbrella review (Table S3), the number of studies or precision variations was limited, under which formal asymmetry tests have low power and limited interpretability. Funnel plot asymmetry may also arise from heterogeneity or other non‐bias sources. Accordingly, these tests were interpreted as indicators of small‐study effects rather than definitive evidence of publication bias and considered alongside other heterogeneity and robustness metrics in the overall evidence assessment.

Major Outcomes

Characteristics of Meta‐Analyses

This systematic search identified 471 records after removing duplicates, from which 24 studies met the inclusion criteria (Figure 1). These meta‐analyses assessed 39 distinct interventions, of which 13 were significantly associated with better outcomes, and 26 did not show significant associations (Table 2). The median AMSTAR score was 8 (range: 7–10), indicating high methodological quality (Table S1). Most results were classified as high or moderate quality based on the GRADE criteria (Table S2). Sensitivity analyses on moderate‐quality outcomes did not change the direction or significance of the results, suggesting robust conclusions.

Multiple Myeloma (MM)

A meta‐analysis comparing various treatment regimens for multiple myeloma found that pembrolizumab combined with lenalidomide and dexamethasone (PLD) significantly improved overall survival (OS) when compared to lenalidomide and dexamethasone alone (LD) (HR: 0.51, 95% CI: 0.32 to 0.82, high quality) [12]. This suggests that the addition of pembrolizumab, a PD‐1 inhibitor, enhances the immune system's ability to target tumor cells by blocking the PD‐1/PD‐L1 signaling pathway. Additionally, elotuzumab combined with pomalidomide and dexamethasone (EPD) showed a superior overall response rate (ORR) (HR: 2.03, 95% CI: 1.24 to 3.32, high quality), as well as partial response (PR) (HR: 0.19, 95% CI: 0.07 to 0.52, high quality) when compared to pomalidomide and dexamethasone alone (PD). However, no significant difference was found for complete response (CR) between EPD and PD (HR: 4.75, 95% CI: 0.57 to 39.42, high quality) (Figure 2).

Relapsed/Refractory Natural Killer T Cell Lymphoma (R/R NKT)

For relapsed/refractory natural killer T cell lymphoma (R/R NKT), sintilimab demonstrated a moderate overall response rate (ORR) (OR: 0.75, 95% CI: 0.59 to 0.91, high quality). Other treatments such as tislelizumab, sugemalimab, and avelumab showed varying degrees of response, with heterogeneity between studies (I2 values ranging from 32% to 63%). The combination of sintilimab and chidamide administered every 3 weeks significantly improved 1 year overall survival (OS) rate (OR: 0.73, 95% CI: 0.58 to 0.87, high quality) [24]. Furthermore, adverse events (AEs) associated with PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibitors combined with HDAC inhibitors were moderate, showing a significant reduction in grade ≥ 3 AEs (OR: 0.20, 95% CI: 0.08 to 0.32, high quality) [24]. This indicated that the combination therapy may offer a safer alternative compared to other regimens, potentially improving long‐term patient compliance (Figure 2).

Relapsed/Refractory Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (R/R CLL)

For relapsed/refractory chronic lymphocytic leukemia (R/R CLL), pembrolizumab at 200 mg every 3 weeks showed a moderate 6 month OS rate (proportion: 0.44, 95% CI: 0.14 to 0.79, high quality) (Figure 2) [25]. This suggested that pembrolizumab may provide survival benefits for patients with relapsed or refractory CLL, although the wide confidence interval reflects some uncertainty. Grade ≥ 3 AEs were moderate (OR: 0.60, 95% CI: 0.39 to 0.79, moderate quality) (Figure 3) [25], indicating that the drug is generally well tolerated in this patient group, with manageable toxicities.

Relapsed/Refractory Primary Mediastinal B‐Cell Lymphoma (R/R PMBCL)

In relapsed/refractory primary mediastinal B‐cell lymphoma (R/R PMBCL), pembrolizumab demonstrated a moderate 6 month progression‐free survival (PFS) (proportion: 0.35, 95% CI: 0.16 to 0.57, high quality) and OS (proportion: 0.35, 95% CI: 0.16 to 0.57, high quality) (Figure 2) [25]. This highlighted pembrolizumab's potential as an effective treatment option for this rare and aggressive lymphoma subtype, especially given its low incidence of grade ≥ 3 AEs (proportion: 0.18, 95% CI: 0.07 to 0.35, high quality) (Figure 2) [25]. The favorable safety profile may facilitate its use in combination with other therapies, potentially enhancing its efficacy without significantly increasing toxicity.

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

We additionally assessed small‐study and publication bias using Egger's test, trim‐and‐fill, and leave‐one‐out analyses where applicable (Table S3). Using random‐effects models (REML), we synthesized each prespecified endpoint and stratified by disease, ICI class, and regimen. To ensure interpretability, effect coding was standardized across all outcomes: for efficacy measures (including ORR, OS, and PFS), all pooled estimates were calculated such that an odds ratio (OR), relative risk (RR), or hazard ratio (HR) of less than 1.0 consistently indicates a clinical benefit favoring the ICI intervention. In R/R NKT, the pooled OR for ORR was 0.58 (95% CI 0.45–0.74; k = 9; I2 = 87.8%; PI 0.29–1.14). By ICI class, PD‐L1 inhibitors showed 0.44 (0.35–0.55; k = 2; I2 ≈0%; PI 0.35–0.55), whereas PD‐1 inhibitors showed 0.65 (0.53–0.80; k = 7; I2 = 85.6%; PI 0.40–1.06). By regimen, monotherapy yielded 0.48 (0.36–0.63; k = 6; I2 = 70.6%; PI 0.26–0.88), while PD‐1/PD‐L1 + HDAC inhibitor combinations yielded 0.76 (0.47–1.22; k = 2; I2 = 90.2%; PI 0.34–1.70). In ND NKT, the pooled ORR was 0.80 (0.63–1.02; k = 2; I2 = 64.0%; PI 0.55–1.17). In MM, OS (HR) was 0.82 (0.34–1.95; k = 2; I2 = 91.2%; PI 0.19–3.52), while DCR (RR) was 0.98 (0.91–1.05; k = 2; I2 ≈0%; PI 0.91–1.05) (Table S3). While a specific high‐quality meta‐analysis of the PLD regimen demonstrated a significant OS improvement (Figure 2), our broader synthesis of MM survival data yielded a more conservative pooled estimate with a wide prediction interval. This discrepancy underscores that the survival benefit of ICIs in MM is highly regimen‐dependent and currently exhibits significant between‐study heterogeneity. Overall, signals of benefit were most consistent for PD‐L1 blockade and monotherapy. In contrast, evidence regarding combination strategies and survival outcomes for R/R NKT and MM remained heterogeneous and uncertain (Table S4).

Discussion

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly those targeting the PD‐1/PD‐L1 and CTLA‐4 pathways, have shown transformative potential in cancer treatment by reversing the immune evasion mechanisms employed by tumors. While these therapies have achieved notable success in solid tumors, their application in hematologic malignancies is still under investigation, with promising results yet significant challenges remaining.

This umbrella review represents the first comprehensive evidence synthesis to evaluate the efficacy and safety of ICIs across hematologic malignancies by integrating data from multiple systematic reviews and meta‐analyses. Compared with traditional systematic reviews, the umbrella review approach provides a broader and more rigorous methodological framework, allowing for cross‐disease comparisons, hierarchical evaluation of evidence quality, and the identification of consistent therapeutic signals across heterogeneous studies. This methodology not only minimizes the bias inherent in single‐disease meta‐analyses but also highlights overarching trends that are clinically relevant yet previously underrecognized.

In multiple myeloma (MM), the combination of pembrolizumab with lenalidomide and dexamethasone (PLD) has been shown to significantly improve overall survival (OS) [12]. This combination works by targeting the PD‐1/PD‐L1 axis, which reinvigorates T‐cells to attack myeloma cells. The clinical benefit is particularly striking in the context of relapsed or refractory MM, where traditional treatments have limited efficacy [26]. The mechanism behind this improvement lies in pembrolizumab's ability to block the inhibitory signals from PD‐L1 on tumor cells, thus restoring T‐cell functionality. Additionally, the combination therapy was associated with increased rates of fatigue and other immune‐related adverse events (irAEs), indicating the need for careful management of these side effects to maximize long‐term benefits [27].

In relapsed/refractory natural killer T‐cell lymphoma (R/R NKT), the combination of sintilimab, a PD‐1 inhibitor, and chidamide, an HDAC inhibitor, resulted in a significant 1 year OS improvement. This combination likely works by modulating the tumor microenvironment, improving immune cell infiltration, and reversing the immunosuppressive effects commonly seen in NKT cells [24]. NKT cell lymphomas present a challenging therapeutic landscape due to their resistance to traditional chemotherapy and their complex immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [28]. While the sintilimab‐chidamide combination shows moderate efficacy, the heterogeneity in responses across patient populations suggests that further studies are needed to refine treatment regimens and identify patient subgroups that would benefit most.

In chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), pembrolizumab demonstrated moderate efficacy, showing a manageable toxicity profile and a 6 month OS rate. CLL is characterized by immune evasion, with tumor cells suppressing T‐cell activity via the PD‐1/PD‐L1 pathway [25]. Pembrolizumab's ability to block this interaction may help to restore immune surveillance and improve patient outcomes, particularly in relapsed or refractory disease [29]. However, the relatively small effect size observed in this cohort highlights a key limitation: monotherapy with pembrolizumab may not be sufficient to achieve durable responses [25]. This suggests that combination therapies, such as combining pembrolizumab with targeted agents or chemotherapy, may offer more effective and durable therapeutic options. Furthermore, the modest benefit observed in the general CLL population emphasizes the need for biomarkers that can predict which subgroups of patients are most likely to respond to ICIs.

In primary mediastinal B‐cell lymphoma (PMBCL), pembrolizumab showed moderate 6 month progression‐free survival (PFS) and OS [25], suggesting its potential role in this aggressive subtype of diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL). PMBCL is highly sensitive to PD‐1 blockade due to the high expression of PD‐L1 on tumor cells [30]. However, despite these promising results, the lack of a significant improvement in complete response (CR) rates points to the need for additional therapeutic strategies. Combination treatments, such as pembrolizumab in conjunction with chemotherapy or other targeted agents, may help overcome resistance mechanisms and improve the likelihood of achieving complete remission. The relatively low incidence of severe adverse events observed in these patients further supports the favorable safety profile of pembrolizumab in PMBCL [25].

Subgroup analyses refined these observations and highlighted where benefit is most likely to generalize. In R/R NKT, the synthesized ORR favored ICIs overall (pooled OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.45–0.74; PI 0.29–1.14), with a more consistent signal for PD‐L1 blockade (OR 0.44, 0.35–0.55; PI 0.35–0.55) than for PD‐1 inhibitors (OR 0.65, 0.53–0.80; PI 0.40–1.06), and for monotherapy (OR 0.48, 0.36–0.63; PI 0.26–0.88) compared with PD‐1/PD‐L1 + HDAC inhibitor combinations (OR 0.76, 0.47–1.22; PI 0.34–1.70). In newly diagnosed NKT, the pooled ORR was borderline (OR 0.80, 0.63–1.02; PI 0.55–1.17), suggesting that average effects may not extend uniformly across settings. In multiple myeloma, the OS estimate was imprecise with wide uncertainty (HR 0.82, 0.34–1.95; PI 0.19–3.52), and DCR was neutral (RR 0.98, 0.91–1.05; PI 0.91–1.05), underscoring the context‐dependence of ICI activity in MM. The observed uncertainty in MM survival outcomes, characterized by the wide prediction interval, suggests that the efficacy of PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibitors may be influenced by the heavy immunosuppressive microenvironment of multiple myeloma. Unlike R/R NKT, where signals are more consistent, the application of ICIs in MM requires more precise biomarker‐guided patient selection to translate individual trial successes into generalized clinical benefit. Notably, non‐response coding of some ORR ORs, together with wide PIs and high heterogeneity, underscores the need for biomarker‐guided selection (PD‐L1/EBV) and standardized endpoints. Overall, these subgroup findings support PD‐L1 blockade and monotherapy as the most reproducible strategies in R/R NKT, whereas HDAC inhibitor combinations and MM outcomes remain uncertain and merit prospective clarification.

Despite it from a methodological perspective, our umbrella review provides a higher level of evidence integration than any single meta‐analysis; several limitations across the studies need to be addressed. Study heterogeneity exists in terms of patient populations, treatment regimens, and clinical endpoints, which may slightly influence the interpretation of results. Despite this, the consistency of the observed therapeutic effects across different studies suggests the robustness of ICI therapy in these settings. Additionally, although long‐term follow‐up data are often limited in the available studies, the current evidence supports the potential durability of ICI treatment in these diseases. Ongoing studies with extended follow‐up will be valuable in providing further insights into the long‐term safety and efficacy of ICIs in hematologic malignancies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this umbrella review not only provides the first high‐level synthesis of ICI efficacy and safety across hematologic malignancies, particularly in R/R NKT, MM, R/R CLL, and R/R PMBCL, but also introduces a novel methodological framework for evidence consolidation in hematologic oncology. Subgroup analyses indicated that benefit was most reproducible with PD‐L1 blockade and monotherapy in R/R NKT, whereas combination strategies (e.g., with HDAC inhibitors) and MM outcomes remained heterogeneous and context‐dependent. While these therapies show promise, challenges such as study heterogeneity, modest effect sizes, lack of long‐term follow‐up, and insufficient biomarkers persist. Given the rarity of certain hematologic malignancies, achieving Class I evidence is challenging. Therefore, while our classification follows established umbrella review protocols, we emphasize the clinical significance of Moderate/High GRADE ratings even when the epidemiologic evidence class is IV. Future research should focus on larger, multicenter studies to reduce heterogeneity, identify predictive biomarkers, and develop combination therapies that improve efficacy and minimize toxicity. The ongoing exploration of ICI therapy in combination with other agents holds great potential for improving outcomes in these difficult‐to‐treat hematologic malignancies.
