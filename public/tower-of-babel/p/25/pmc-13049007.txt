Fracture behavior of rod-in-tube layered configurations under three-point bending: an experimental investigation
Authors: Mohan Kumar, Neelakantha V Londhe, C G Ramachandra, M Srinivas Prabhu, Praveen Kumar Kanti, Chander Prakash, H G Prashantha Kumar, Haiter Lenin Allasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnsuring adequate fracture resistance in structural components is a critical challenge in engineering applications, as sudden crack propagation can lead to catastrophic failure. This study investigates the fracture behavior of novel rod-in-tube layered configuration using EN8 steel, aimed at enhancing fracture resistance and delaying catastrophic failure. Specimens were prepared in accordance with ASTM standards and subjected to three-point bend tests. Stress intensity factor (SIF) values were evaluated over load point displacement and time to assess crack propagation behavior. Compared to plain rod specimens, the layered configurations demonstrated a two-stage crack growth process characterized by initial steady crack propagation and subsequent delayed failure, attributed to the presence of a physical interface that arrests crack growth and redistributes stress. The configuration with an 8 mm diameter inner rod proved as effective, extending the time to failure by approximately 115% when tested and compared with plain rod. The findings affirm the layered design’s effectiveness in enhancing both safe-life and fail-safe performance, offering a promising approach for improving fracture tolerance in structural applications.Keywords: Fracture mechanics, Layered configuration, Rod-In-Tube, EN8 steel, Stress intensity factor (SIF), Crack arrest, Fail-Safe design, Three-Point bend test, Structural integrity, Fracture toughnessSubject terms: Engineering, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Structural failure due to crack propagation remains a critical concern in the design of engineering components, especially when subjected to complex loading. The conventional design of structural components relies on comparing applied stresses to material yield or tensile strength, often incorporating a factor of safety. However, this approach can underestimate the risk of failure in the presence of flaws or cracks, as it does not account for the presence of microcracks that can lead to catastrophic fracture even when stress levels are below the material’s yield strength. Fracture mechanics addresses this limitation by incorporating crack size and material toughness, offering a more realistic framework where failure is predicted when the stress intensity factor (SIF) exceeds the material’s fracture toughness1–3. This approach is crucial because the high-stress concentration at a crack’s tip can cause it to grow; potentially leading to sudden and unexpected structural failure. This shift enables better material utilization and cost-effective, safer designs.

Cracks, especially those with sharp tips, act as potent stress concentrators4. Micron-sized flaws, though initially harmless, can propagate rapidly when local stresses surpass critical thresholds5–7. Several studies have addressed the mechanisms governing crack growth and arrest, including material composition, microstructural constraints, and notch geometry8,9. Metallurgical strategies such as grain boundary strengthening, precipitation hardening, and the inclusion of second-phase particles can enhance a material’s intrinsic fracture resistance10,11. However, these methods can be limited by processing complexities and inconsistent outcomes. A well-known tradeoff exists between strength and toughness; as strength increases, ductility typically diminishes, reducing the ability to absorb fracture energy12. To overcome this trade-off, various techniques have been developed to impede crack growth. Metrics such as crack growth rate, energy release rate, and stress intensity factor (SIF) are thus crucial in assessing fracture behaviour.

An alternative and complementary approach involves the design of structural configurations that extrinsically enhance fracture resistance. The concept of introducing physical interfaces to arrest crack propagation has shown considerable promise. For instance, the use of stop-drill holes, particularly those reinforced with pins, has been demonstrated to effectively inhibit crack propagation by introducing physical interfaces that redistribute stress and delay failure12–14. This principle of crack arrest is central to the development of fail-safe designs, which aim to provide clear indications of impending failure, thereby preventing catastrophic events.

Layered and hybrid configurations offer a promising avenue for improving damage tolerance. For instance, aluminium tubes reinforced with steel rods have demonstrated substantial improvements in Mode I and Mode II fracture toughness compared to monolithic rods15,16. Interfaces in such layered systems can induce crack deflection, branching, or bridging—mechanisms well-documented in composite materials and advanced laminates17–19. Recent experimental and numerical works by Chen et al.20, Wang et al.21, and Gupta & Bhatia22 emphasize the critical role of notch geometry, interfacial bonding, and structural constraints in altering local SIFs and arresting crack growth in layered materials.

Rod-in-tube architectures, while simpler than fiber-metal laminates or 3D-printed composites, also exhibit these interface-driven benefits. Studies by Yusef et al.23, Sarrigiannidis et al.24, and Lee et al.25 show how engineered boundaries can guide crack paths, improve energy dissipation, and delay unstable fracture. Razavi et al.26 and Song et al.27 reported that geometric discontinuities and internal layering significantly alter fracture morphology and improve toughness.

Ariyana et al.28 reported that hybrid SPCC/CFRP laminates exhibited superior Mode I and II fracture toughness when epoxy adhesives were used with the co-bonding method (Option C), achieving GIC = 83.05 J/m2 and GIIC = 254.94 J/m2. This configuration outperformed other manufacturing options by up to ~ 80% and showed failure modes dependent on the bonded surface. Muflikhun and Yokozeki29 developed hybrid CFRP–steel laminates and demonstrated impressive performance, achieving tensile and flexural strengths of 1475.4 MPa and 1516.1 MPa, along with Mode I and Mode II fracture toughness values of 116.4 J/m2 and 330 J/m2, respectively.

Recent investigations in fracture mechanics have highlighted the strong influence of geometric constraint and internal boundaries on crack-tip stress fields and apparent toughness. Studies have demonstrated that variations in constraint can significantly alter the measured fracture toughness in ductile metals30, indicating that geometry-driven constraint acts as an important extrinsic parameter in governing crack propagation. Further, modern crack-arrest approaches in metallic structures show that engineered design features can effectively delay or halt crack growth under service loading31, reinforcing the relevance of structurally induced crack-arrest mechanisms. These findings align with the motivation behind rod-in-tube configurations, where internal geometry is expected to modify the local SIF during bending.

Complementing these experimental insights, several numerical and analytical works have clarified how cracks interact with interfaces. Recent studies show that the penetration or deflection of a crack at an interface depends strongly on the local toughness, constraint level and loading rate32,33. Experimental investigations on layered metallic systems also confirm that interfaces promote crack deflection, branching and additional energy dissipation, leading to delayed or multi-stage fracture behaviour34. Together, these findings highlight that interface-mediated mechanisms play a critical role in enhancing fracture resistance even in metallic materials.

Work on cylindrical and pipe-like geometries further demonstrates that internal or external reinforcement layers can significantly modify stress intensity factors and crack-growth behaviour. Analytical studies have shown that reinforced pipe structures exhibit altered crack-growth kinetics under cyclic loading due to modified constraint fields35. In addition, interface geometry has been shown to influence crack-front shape evolution and propagation stability36, while reviews on small-scale fracture mechanics reaffirm that geometry, thickness and constraint effects are central to fracture response37. These developments collectively underline the need for detailed experimental studies on simple, homogeneous rod-in-tube metallic configurations, where fracture behaviour is governed primarily by internal geometry and interfacial constraint rather than material heterogeneity.

Tubular and layered structural configurations subjected to bending have been shown to exhibit altered stiffness, stress redistribution, and damage evolution compared with monolithic sections due to geometric confinement effects38. Experimental studies on tube-based and strengthened members further demonstrate that cross-sectional configuration strongly influences crack initiation and propagation under flexural loading39,40. Recent advances in fracture characterization and damage analysis methodologies have improved the interpretation of crack evolution in structural components41,42; however, these investigations predominantly focus on heterogeneous material systems or reinforced members. Despite this progress, fracture mechanics studies on homogeneous metallic rod-in-tube configurations under three-point bending remain limited, particularly in terms of crack path stability, load–displacement response, and comparative fracture resistance relative to equivalent solid rod specimens. The present study addresses this gap through an experimental investigation of the fracture behavior of a homogeneous EN8 steel rod-inserted tube configuration under three-point bending, enabling isolation of the effect of geometric confinement alone on crack propagation and fracture resistance.

Existing research gap and significance of the current work

Despite significant advancements in fracture mechanics, most existing studies have primarily focused on improving fracture resistance through either intrinsic material modifications—such as alloying, heat treatment, grain boundary engineering, or composite layering—or through advanced fracture modeling techniques like digital image correlation (DIC), finite element analysis (FEA), and cohesive zone modeling (CZM). While these methods provide valuable insights, they often demand complex manufacturing routes, computational intensity, or lack scalability in real-world applications.

Previous experimental efforts on crack arrest mechanisms have largely relied on composite systems, stop-drill holes, or dissimilar material interfaces to delay fracture. However, such designs involve material heterogeneity or added process complexity. Additionally, while the effects of external notch geometry have been widely studied, the influence of internal geometry in homogeneous metallic layered systems, particularly under Mode I loading, remains underexplored.

Furthermore, most fracture toughness investigations have utilized standard SENB or CT geometries, which do not replicate real-world layered geometrical constraints or enable fail-safe design strategies. Simulation-based studies have predicted the benefits of geometric constraints on local SIF modulation, but there has been a lack of direct experimental validation using monolithic rod-in-tube metallic architectures.

The current study bridges this gap by experimentally investigating a novel rod-in-tube configuration using EN8 steel, which creates a physical interface within a homogeneous material system. This simple, scalable design enables dual benefits: crack arrest and delayed failure without altering material composition. Unlike prior studies limited to composites or simulation domains, this work provides empirical evidence of multi-phase crack growth behaviour, stress intensity factor evolution, and extended fracture life—achieving up to 115% increase in time to failure. The findings validate the effectiveness of internal structural design as an extrinsic fracture resistance strategy, marking a significant step forward in developing robust, fail-safe metallic components. Hence the aim of this study is to experimentally characterize the fracture performance of EN8 rod-in-tube specimens subjected to three-point bending and to establish the effect of inner-rod diameter on crack arrest behavior, failure progression, and improvements over plain rod configurations.

The principal novelty of this work is the experimental demonstration of crack arrest and fail-safe behavior in a fully homogeneous EN8 steel rod-in-tube configuration achieved solely through internal geometry. Unlike existing fracture-toughness enhancement strategies that depend on material heterogeneity, composite interfaces, adhesives, or complex processing routes, the proposed design introduces a press-fitted internal interface within the same metallic material. This configuration enables controlled crack arrest, load transfer, and two-stage crack propagation under three-point bending, providing clear evidence of geometry-induced extrinsic toughening in a monolithic metallic system. To the authors’ knowledge, such a homogeneous rod-in-tube SENB-type configuration has not been previously reported for experimentally enhancing fracture life and fail-safe performance in EN8 steel.

Methodology

Three-point bend test and the specimen

The three-point bend test is used to determine Mode I fracture toughness. This test is conducted according ASTM E399 standards, and the Tada and Paris Eq. (1) is used, to calculate the stress intensity factor (SIF). The Tada and Paris equation offers the flexibility to apply to the test specimens with varying crack depths. All test components are fabricated according to standard specifications, with an initial 1 mm depth of the crack machined on the outer surface. The crack is in the form of V-shaped notch with a 60° included angle and root radius of 1 mm. The testing is performed on a Universal Testing Machine (UTM) with a 400 kN capacity. Figure 1 presents photographs of the test specimens. Standard L/d ratio of 4 for three-point bending specimens is considered.

Specimens used in three-point-bend test.

All specimens were machined from the same batch of EN8 steel with dimensional tolerances within ± 0.02 mm. The V-notch was produced using wire-cut EDM to ensure a precise 1 mm depth, 60° notch angle, and 1 mm root radius. Rod-in-tube samples were assembled using an interference press-fit of 0.02–0.05 mm; no adhesives or welds were used.

Results and discussions

Analysis of stress intensity factor

Samples of each specimen configuration as mentioned in the Table 1 are tested under gradually applied bending load in UTM. For each sample, the load and time are recorded at regular intervals of load point displacements. Three samples of each configuration are tested. Using the recorded values the stress intensity factor (SIF) at various loads are determined using the Eq. (1). The graph of average values of stress intensity factors verses load point displacements (deflection) of three samples for three types of specimens is plotted. Figure 4 shows the combined plots of variation of Mode I stress intensity factor with load point displacement for three types of specimens mentioned in the Table 1.

Average stress intensity factor (SIF)–deflection curves derived from three specimens of each configuration (A, B, and C), showing the differences in fracture behaviour among the three specimen configurations.

Graphs indicate that for the specimens B & C, the nature of the load/stress intensity factor (SIF) verses deflection curves are identical. Here the deflection indicates the load point displacement (deflection) which in other words, is equivalent to increase in the crack length. Both the curves seem to be increasing gradually until a certain point. In this portion the load/SIF varies linearly with the deflection, and slopes of curves for both the specimens are almost the same. Later the curves take horizontal position and the deflection continues to grow at a faster rate with small increase in the load/SIF. Hence the curves indicate two regions of crack propagation, viz., steady state crack growth and rapid crack growth followed by fracture.

In the case of the plain rod specimen (A), the load/SIF curve shows an increasing trend with deflection and ends abruptly upon reaching its maximum value. Hence there is only one clear region of steady state crack growth before failure.

This proves that the rod-in-tube configuration sustains larger deflections compared to plain rod specimen. Figure 5 illustrates the average values of deflection at failure for three types of specimens.

Maximum load-point displacement at failure for plain rod and rod-in-tube configurations, showing significantly higher deflection in rod-in-tube specimens due to crack arrest and delayed failure enabled by the internal rod–tube interface.

The critical SIF is the SIF value at which the local stress at the crack tip crosses the ultimate tensile strength of the material. When SIF reaches the critical value, the crack growth becomes unstable and fracture is imminent. The fracture toughness is the material property and indicates the intensity of stress at the crack tip at failure. Hence the critical SIF is the maximum induced stress intensity at the crack tip before failure which in turn is equal to the fracture toughness. The Fig. 6 shows the percentage variation in SIF values for rod-in-tube type specimens (B & C) with respect to plain rod specimen A.

Percentage variation of critical stress intensity factor (SIF) for rod-in-tube specimens relative to the plain rod, illustrating the trade-off between reduced peak SIF and enhanced fracture life due to interface-induced crack arrest.

It can be observed from the graph that the critical stress intensity factor reduced by 5% for specimen B with reference to specimen A. For specimen C, it decreased by 20%. The average maximum stress intensity factors obtained for specimens A, B, & C are 100 MPa-m1/2, 95 MPa-m1/2, and 80 MPa-m1/2 respectively.

Among the three specimen configurations, the plain rod specimen (A) exhibited the highest strength, followed by specimen B and specimen C. The outer diameter was identical for all specimens. Therefore, the observed reduction in stress intensity factor (SIF) in the rod-in-tube specimens can be attributed to the presence of the internal rod. A further decrease in SIF was observed with a reduction in the thickness of the inner rod. The underlying mechanism responsible for this trend requires additional investigation and is beyond the scope of the present study.

In a general fracture mechanics problem, once the crack is initiated it continues to grow till failure of the member takes place. Such failures are catastrophic in nature and occur suddenly without any prior indication.

In the rod-in-tube configuration, crack growth occurred in two distinct stages, and failure was not sudden. During the first stage, the crack grew steadily until a maximum stress intensity factor (SIF) was reached. This maximum occurred at a load-point displacement of approximately 3.5 mm (about 90 MPa-m1/2) for specimen B and at 2.7 mm (about 78 MPa-m1/2) for specimen C. This was followed by a second stage of crack growth, during which the SIF–displacement curve remained nearly horizontal until final failure. Crack propagation continued without abrupt fracture, indicating delayed failure behaviour. A slight reduction in SIF and load was observed in this stage, suggesting reduced stress concentration at the crack tip. Calculations using Eq. (1) confirmed that the SIF decreased marginally beyond the maximum value attained at the end of the first stage.

During the second stage of crack growth, the decrease in stress intensity factor (SIF) ceased after a small amount of crack propagation, and the SIF subsequently increased gradually. Final failure occurred at an SIF value slightly higher than the SIF at the onset of the second stage.

The reduction in stress intensity factor (SIF) without immediate fracture can be explained as follows. Initially, the notched specimen was loaded gradually, and the crack initiated at the notch tip. The notch located on the outer surface of the tube propagated radially inward, leading to steady-state crack growth within the tube. During this stage, the SIF at the crack tip increased and reached a maximum value at the end of the first stage. Once this maximum was attained, crack growth in the tube was arrested, and load transfer occurred from the tube to the inner rod. In the absence of the inner rod, as in the plain rod specimen (A), failure would have occurred at this stage, albeit at a higher SIF value, as evident from the experimental curves. In the rod-in-tube configuration, however, the load was transferred to the inner rod without immediate failure. Subsequent crack initiation and propagation occurred within the rod. Notably, crack formation and growth in the inner rod delayed sudden failure in specimens B and C beyond the end of the first stage.

The difference between the maximum and minimum stress intensity factor (SIF) values during the second stage was higher for specimen B (15.04 MPa-m1/2) than for specimen C (6.39 MPa-m1/2uperscript>). Since all other parameters were identical for specimens B and C, except for the inner rod diameter, this difference can be attributed to the larger diameter of the inner rod in specimen B.

The reason for the delay in failure is due to the reinforcement of the rod inside the tube. From this stage, the rod bears the further load to some extent. This delays the sudden failure.

Hence it prevents the catastrophic failure of the component and hint about the failure in advance. The design which gives indication of failure in advance leads to the prevention of sudden failures or reduce the effect of failure. Such type of designs is called Fail-safe design. So the present design exhibits two important characteristics known as crack arrest and fail-safe. The crack arrest improves the life of the component and fail-safe design helps in minimizing the after effects of fracture.

Analysis of stress intensity factor and time

From the above discussion, it is evident that the rod-in-tube configuration permits longer crack propagation before failure compared to the plain rod. Greater crack extension prior to failure corresponds to an increased safe life. Accordingly, the time dependence of the stress intensity factor (SIF) was examined to quantify the enhancement in safe life of the rod-in-tube structure. It is well established that the stress intensity factor increases with crack advancement, reaches a maximum, and then drops abruptly, leading to fracture. Since crack growth is time dependent, the SIF also varies with time. Therefore, instead of directly correlating SIF with crack growth, which is time-consuming and experimentally demanding, the variation of SIF with time was analysed to assess the improvement in safe life.

The variation of SIF at different intervals of time during the 3-point bend test is calculated from the experimental data. The graph of SIF verses time is plotted for three types of specimens mentioned in the table which is as shown in the Fig. 7.

Variation of stress intensity factor (SIF) with time under three-point bending, highlighting two-stage crack propagation in rod-in-tube specimens: an initial steady crack growth stage followed by a crack-arrest and delayed failure phase, in contrast to sudden catastrophic failure in the plain rod.

The three curves exhibit distinct behavior for the three specimen types. The curve for specimen A shows a continuous increase in stress intensity factor (SIF) until the end of the curve, at which point failure occurs. In contrast, the rod-in-tube specimens B and C exhibit markedly different behavior. In both cases, the SIF increases linearly until a maximum value is reached, after which the curves do not terminate but instead become nearly horizontal. This indicates a reduction in slope and a decrease in the rate of change of SIF. Subsequently, the SIF increases gradually until a second maximum is reached, followed by final failure. This behavior indicates that sudden failure does not occur in the rod-in-tube specimens. All three curves exhibit an initial linear region, indicating that the SIF is proportional to time. However, the slope of the curve for specimen A is higher than that of the rod-in-tube specimens, indicating faster crack growth and immediate failure at the end of the linear region.

From the Fig. 7 it can be observed that the stress intensity factor increases gradually at the beginning until it reaches a maximum point. This is the first steady state crack growth region found on the cross section of the tube. During this stage the crack propagates and the SIF increases from 20 to 79 MPa-m1/2 in around 13 min for specimen A, from 17 to 82 MPa-m1/2 in around 15 min for sample 2 and from 18 to 82 MPa-m1/2 in around 15 min for sample 3. This portion on the graph is a linear region which indicates that it is a steady state growth. At the end of this stage the crack gets arrested and further growth takes place in the rod. The maximum SIF in this region is 79.3 MPa-m1/2, 82.5 MPa-m1/2, and 82 MPa-m1/2 respectively for the three samples which averages out to be 81 MPa-m1/2.

Beyond the maximum point on the graph, the stress intensity factor (SIF) remains nearly constant over the time interval between 20 and 30 min. This interval corresponds to a slowdown in crack propagation or crack arrest. The absence of an increase in SIF indicates negligible crack growth during this period. Such a region would not be present in a structure without a physical interface or in a specimen with a homogeneous cross section. Therefore, this configuration contributes to an enhancement in the service life of the component.

Beyond the region of constant SIF, the KI again increases until failure. The average stress intensity factor increases from 83 to 97 MPa-m1/2 in nearly 10 min. This indicates the ability of the material to support the load increases due to the presence of the rod. The observation of fractured surface also indicates a steady state crack growth region on the surface of the rod followed by brittle fracture. Hence the life of the component increases due to presence of two steady state regions and a region of constant SIF or crack arrest region. This proves the ability of the current design to improve the fracture life of the component.

Hence the time taken for failure or life before failure is less in specimen A than that for the specimen types B & C. The time taken to reach the breaking point (fracture) in specimen A is 18 min, that for specimen B is 45 min, and for specimen C it is 32 min. Even though same material, same outer diameter, and similar loading conditions are used, the time taken to reach the fracture point is different for three types of specimens. That means, in the plain rod crack propagation is faster and hence it fails early. In the rod-in-tube specimens, the rate of change of SIF seems to be dependent on the wall thickness of the outer tube. Lesser the wall thickness (specimen B, wall thickness 2 mm), lower the slope and hence lower is the crack growth rate. In specimen C with wall thickness of 3 mm the rate of crack growth is moderate. This proves that the rod-in-tube configurations can enhance the safe life of the components and that lower the wall thickness of the outer tube slower is the crack growth.

Comparison of slopes of the linear SIF–time curves for different specimen configurations, indicating reduced crack growth rates in rod-in-tube specimens as a result of interface-induced crack deflection and load redistribution.

The rapid crack growth observed in specimen A can be attributed to its plain cross section. The crack propagates along a direct path across the cross section without any barrier or mechanism to slow its growth. This behaviour may also be associated with the relatively uniform microstructure, with limited grain boundary deflection or interaction with second-phase particles or inclusions. As a result, crack propagation is straightforward and leads to abrupt failure.

In rod-in-tube configuration after initial crack growth along a straight path in the outer tube the crack deflection takes place. The crack deflection is due to the presence of the rod. As the crack path gets diverted more energy is required to drive the crack in the new path. Hence a part of the energy is spent in new crack path generation and the slopes of the curves for specimens B and C are lesser than that of A. Further the specimen B has least slope. The reason for this is discussed in the subsequent section.

In specimen C the outer tube has 3 mm wall thickness and in specimen B it is 2 mm. The faster crack growth rate in Specimen C compared to Specimen B can be attributed to the principles of structural constraint and stress triaxiality. The thicker outer tube of Specimen C imposes a higher degree of constraint on the crack tip, restricting plastic deformation. This leads to an elevated state of stress triaxiality, which raises the localized stress at the crack front and, consequently, reduces the material’s apparent fracture toughness. This phenomenon, where higher constraint accelerates crack growth, is a well-documented principle in fracture mechanics49,50.

While localized compressive stresses may exist on the inner surface of the tube after delamination, the primary mechanism for delaying failure is the crack arrest that occurs at the rod-tube interface. This interface acts as a physical barrier, forcing the crack to deflect and re-initiate in the inner rod. This process dissipates a significant amount of energy, leading to the observed drop in the Stress Intensity Factor (SIF) and the extended life of the component. The critical role of interfaces in enhancing fracture resistance through mechanisms like crack deflection and load redistribution is supported by findings in the literature15,51. Furthermore, simulation-based studies confirm that geometric constraints in layered or sleeved configurations significantly alter local stress intensity factors, which governs the fracture behavior. The experimental results are in strong agreement with these established theoretical and numerical findings.

The fracture behavior of the tested specimens reveals a strong dependency on microstructural configuration. Specimen A, exhibiting a relatively homogeneous structure, undergoes rapid crack propagation along a straight path, characteristic of brittle fracture. In contrast, Specimens B and C incorporate a rod-in-tube architecture that promotes crack deflection, crack bridging, and local toughening mechanisms. These structural features effectively elongate the crack path and enhance energy dissipation during fracture, thereby impeding crack growth. This microstructural induced toughening mechanism explains the reduced crack growth rates and higher resistance to fracture observed in these specimens.

Initial steady-state crack growth in the outer tube, which is a ductile in nature and represented by the dull surface. This fracture pattern confirms the fail-safe nature of the rod-in-tube design. The two-phase crack growth, crack arrest, and extended failure time are visually validated in this specimen. It also illustrates how the physical interface and geometry contribute significantly to improved fracture toughness and component life.

In the current study, the wall thickness of the outer tube was uniform, with only minor manufacturing tolerances considered negligible. However, existing literature confirms that wall thickness can influence fracture toughness and crack propagation behavior through mechanisms such as stress triaxiality and structural constraint effects19,24. In this work, specimens were subjected to three-point bending, where the applied load acts from the top side, and the initial crack (a machined V-notch) lies at the bottom. As the load increases, the bottom region of the specimen experiences tensile stress while the top is subjected to compressive stress1. With continued bending, the compressive force on the upper side intensifies due to the point loading from the test machine. Simultaneously, the inner rod bends and exerts an upward reaction force on the tube wall, creating additional compressive stress on the upper side of the outer tube38. Thus, the top wall of the outer tube undergoes significant compressive loading from both the machine load and the reactive force from the inner rod. These combined forces result in a complex stress field, which has been considered in interpreting the fracture behavior of the layered configuration. This explanation aligns with literature findings on how geometry and internal constraints affect local stress intensity factors and crack propagation22,50,52.

Conclusions

This experimental analysis proves the effectiveness of a rod-in-tube layered configuration in enhancing the fracture behaviour of EN8 steel specimens under three-point bending. The study involved comparison of the fracture behaviour of plain solid rods and rod-in-tube specimens. For all configurations a constant outer diameter of 12 mm is maintained. The major findings of the study are captured in the following paragraphs:

1. Enhancement in life of the component:

The plain solid rod (Specimen A) supported higher loads than the rod-in-tube specimens B & C before fracture. But the advantage of rod-in-tube configuration lies in its capacity to withstand the load for a longer duration without failure. This is proved by the maximum deflection occurring in the specimens. The deflection at failure for Specimens B & C is found to be 113% more than that for the Specimen A. This enhancement is attributed to the structural discontinuity in the form of an interface between the inner rod and the outer tube, which provides additional working life to the rod-in-tube specimens.

2. Enhanced Fracture Resistance:

A notched rod-in-tube specimen with a 1 mm deep V-notch and 60° included angle withstood a peak load of 22.8 kN before failure. Despite the presence of a stress concentrator, the layered design demonstrated higher resistance to crack initiation and propagation than the notched plain rod. This indicates that the rod-in-tube configuration effectively mitigates the detrimental effects of geometrical continuities.

3. Improved Toughness and Energy Absorption:

The load–displacement curves revealed that rod-in-tube specimens absorbed significantly more energy prior to failure compared to plain rods. The presence of two distinct crack propagation phases - initial steady state crack growth followed by a crack retardation phase, was a key indicator of enhanced fracture toughness. Specimen B exhibited a 115% increase in time to fracture, while Specimen C (6 mm inner rod) demonstrated a 50% improvement, relative to the plain specimen.

4. Lower Crack Growth Rates:

The slope of the SIF-time curve, representing crack growth rate, was highest for Specimen A at 6.20 MPa-m1/2/min, followed by Specimen C at 4.36 MPa-m1/2/min, and lowest for Specimen B at 3.72 MPa-m1/2/min. These results confirm a slower crack propagation rate in rod-in-tube configurations, further supporting the observation of enhanced fracture life.

5. Fracture Surface Morphology and Fail-Safe Behaviour:

Fractographic analysis of Specimen B revealed three distinct regions across the fracture surface: an initial ductile fracture zone in the outer tube, a transition zone with crack arrest and load redistribution at the rod–tube interface, and a final fracture zone in the inner rod comprising both ductile and brittle features. This multi-phase failure mode is a hallmark of fail-safe behaviour, allowing for progressive failure and load sharing rather than abrupt fracture.

6. Design Implications:

The results validate that the rod-in-tube architecture provides dual advantages of crack arrest and delayed catastrophic failure. These features are critical for applications demanding high structural integrity and damage tolerance, such as aerospace, marine, and heavy mechanical systems.

In conclusion, the rod-in-tube configuration enhances both safe-life and fail-safe performance through improved fracture resistance, delayed failure, and better energy dissipation. The presence and dimension of the inner rod were shown to significantly affect fracture behaviour, indicating that optimization of internal geometry could yield further improvements. Future work may include parametric studies with varying material combinations and detailed numerical modelling to complement and extend the current experimental findings.
