Preformulated, Shelf-Stable, Dendritic Cell-Targeting Nanogel mRNA Vaccine Delivery Platform
Authors: Amy E. Laturski, Verónica Durán, Bruce T. Schaar, Maria T. Dulay, Shirit Einav, Joseph M. DeSimone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Current mRNA-based vaccine platforms are limited by complex fabrication, stringent cold-chain dependence, and off-target in vivo delivery, restricting their global accessibility and targeting precision. Here we show a modular mRNA delivery platform; our approach integrates inverse microemulsion-derived polymeric nanogels with adsorption-based mRNA loading and the capability for ligand-directed targeting to develop targeted reductively cleavable acrylate-based inverse microemulsion nanogels (TRAINs). By decoupling nanoparticle fabrication from mRNA loading, preformed TRAINs can be stockpiled and later combined with newly designed mRNA, enabling a plug-and-play strategy for rapid adaptation to emerging pathogens, which is particularly advantageous for pandemic preparedness. This architecture supports efficient mRNA association and robust cellular uptake and translation in HEK293T cells. TRAIN also enables postsynthetic surface functionalization; as a proof of concept, CD206-targeted TRAINs promoted selective delivery to antigen-presenting cells (APCs), producing sustained and localized protein expression in BALB/c mice and preferential mRNA delivery to myeloid APCs in heterogeneous human peripheral blood mononuclear cells. Together, these results demonstrate TRAINs as a surface-customizable, adsorption-based mRNA delivery platform with potential for APC-targeted vaccination and rapid, adaptable vaccine deployment.

Introduction

Vaccines are among the most effective public health interventions for preventing infectious disease and reducing global mortality. mRNA vaccines have emerged as a transformative platform, as exemplified by the Pfizer-BioNTech (BNT162) and Moderna (mRNA1273) SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, which have shown high levels of antigen expression and robust immune responses, with clinical efficacy exceeding 90%. − Compared to traditional vaccines, mRNA platforms enable rapid manufacturing and reduced risk of genomic integration and offer the ability to encode various proteins, leading to a growing pipeline of candidates across infectious diseases. − Despite these advances, their broader deployment remains limited by their fabrication constraints and in vivo delivery, including cold-chain dependence and off-target effects. −

Currently, mRNA-based vaccines predominantly rely on encapsulation-based fabrication strategies such as LNPs. − However, during this process, mRNA incorporation is tightly coupled to nanoparticle fabrication, where mRNA must be loaded during particle formation. While effective, this fabrication design restricts independent control over formulation and cargo, limiting modularity, adaptability, and storage flexibility. − In addition, these commercial mRNA-based vaccines offer limited control over biodistribution, often spreading from their injection sites, and accumulation in off-target organs such as the liver and spleen. This off-target biodistribution raises concerns about adverse effects arising from ectopic-antigen expression and highlights the need for delivery strategies that enhance local retention and preferential uptake by immune cells. −

In order to enhance mRNA vaccine delivery, a decoupled approach where mRNA is loaded onto the particle after fabrication is emerging as an attractive clinical alternative strategy, such as an adsorption-based fabrication strategy. By independently synthesizing and optimizing nanoparticle fabrication from mRNA loading, this adsorption-based strategy can enable nanoparticles to be synthesized and stockpiled in advance to serve as ready-to-use delivery scaffolds with RNA adsorption performed immediately prior to administration. This strategy may help streamline manufacturing, improve storage flexibility, and eliminate the need to reformulate the nanoparticle construct for each new mRNA cargo or application. , Moreover, it can enable tunable mRNA association and postsynthetic targeting, providing a more flexible and targeted strategy for mRNA delivery. In 2022, India granted emergency use authorization for an adsorption-based COVID-19 mRNA vaccine, GEMCOVAC-19, which elicited increases in antispike IgG titers in early phase clinical studies, highlighting the capability to induce humoral immune responses in humans. −

Despite their potential, adsorption-based RNA delivery strategies remain comparatively underdeveloped for mRNA delivery, particularly as intentionally designed platforms, and have not been widely developed for targeted immune-cell applications. Here, we present TRAINs (targeted reductively cleavable acrylate-based inverse microemulsion nanogels), an adsorption-based mRNA delivery platform that decouples RNA loading from nanoparticle fabrication. This design enables independent control over mRNA incorporation and surface functionalization, addressing the key limitations of encapsulation-based systems. We systematically evaluate mRNA association, stability, and functional delivery and demonstrate the modularity of the platform through ligand-mediated targeting of antigen-presenting cells (APCs). As a proof of concept, we customize the TRAIN surface with anti-CD206 antibody to demonstrate APC-targeted delivery within heterogeneous human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Together, this work presents a versatile framework for tunable and targeted mRNA delivery.

Results and Discussion

Design and Synthesis of the TRAIN Platform

TRAINs build from prior work in our group and are designed as a modular, polymeric nanogel scaffold for mRNA adsorption and ligand-directed targeting (Figure ). The platform is formulated with three well-established and commercially available monomers, bis­(2-methacryloyl)­oxyethyl disulfide (DSDMA), 2-acryloxyethyl­trimethylammonium chloride (AETMAC), and N-(3-aminopropyl)­methacrylamide hydrochloride (APMA), as shown in Figure A and Figure S1. We chose DSDMA, a redox-responsive cross-linker, whose disulfide bond undergoes cleavage in reducing intracellular environments, such as the cytoplasm, where elevated glutathione (GSH) levels can trigger structural destabilization of the nanogel network necessary for endosomal release and subsequent cytoplasmic mRNA translation. , AETMAC provides a permanent cationic charge to enable electrostatic adsorption of mRNA and promotes interactions with the negatively charged cell membrane, enabling a more efficient entry into cells and downstream translation. , Finally, APMA was incorporated to introduce surface-accessible primary amines, that serve as versatile handles for ligand conjugation via standard bioconjugation chemistries to support receptor-mediated targeting. ,,−

This templated approach produces preformed nanogel scaffolds that decouple nanoparticle fabrication from mRNA loading and surface functionalization, enabling modular RNA cargo incorporation, postsynthetic targeting, and improved storage flexibility.

TRAINs were synthesized via a templated inverse microemulsion (water-in-oil) polymerization to produce nanometer-sized, cross-linked polymeric particles with narrow size distributions. , In this water-in-oil (w/o) system, the aqueous monomer solution is confined within surfactant-stabilized droplets dispersed in a continuous heptane “oil” phase (Figure B). These droplets act as confined reaction vessels or “nanoreactors” for free-radical polymerization to produce spherically shaped, covalently cross-linked nanogels. In prior work from our group, the successful synthesis of polymeric nanogels via inverse microemulsion polymerization was demonstrated and key structure–property relationships were defined. Cross-linking density strongly influenced particle size, with higher cross-linking (>25 wt %) restricting network swelling and yielding smaller nanogels (∼50 nm), while lower cross-linking (<5 wt %) resulted in swelling and larger particle size (∼125 nm). Nanoparticles in the 50–250 nm range are most efficiently internalized by cells via endocytosis, thus, we selected a cross-linking density of 12 mol % to generate particles within the 50–100 nm range. In parallel, incorporation of cationic monomers was required for nucleic acid association and cellular uptake; however, excessive charge increased cytotoxicity. Our previous optimization studies identified 25 wt % cationic monomer as sufficient for efficient nucleic acid binding without compromising cell viability. Therefore, TRAINs were synthesized with 12 mol % cross-linker and 25 mol % cationic monomer to balance particle size, mRNA association, cellular uptake, and biocompatibility.

Characterization of TRAIN Nanoparticles

TRAINs were characterized for their z-average size, polydispersity index (PDI), zeta potential, and colloidal stability to assess their suitability as an adsorption-based mRNA delivery platform (Figure ). In Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffer saline (DPBS), a solution that closely mimics physiological ionic strength, TRAIN’s z-average diameter was around 86.6 ± 1.3 nm (Figure A; P > 0.05) and the PDI of TRAIN remained ∼0.2 across three independent batches, indicating a moderate uniform nanoparticle size distribution. These observations were further supported by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which showed dispersed nanoparticle features with no observable large aggregates or fused structures (Figure B and Figure S2). Notably, DLS measurements yielded a larger hydrodynamic diameter than TEM, which is consistent with the hydrated state of particles in solution and the absence of a hydrodynamic shell in dry-state TEM imaging. The net surface charge of TRAIN, a critical parameter in colloidal stability was measured by electrophoretic light scattering (ELS). TRAIN exhibited a zeta potential of +33.5 ± 1.3 mV in UltraPure water (Figure C), within an optimal range (+15.0 to +35.0 mV) for minimizing aggregation and promoting cellular uptake. Consistent with this, TRAIN maintained colloidal stability with minor change in z-average diameter over one month at 25 °C in DPBS (Figure D). While PDI increased from ∼0.2 to ∼0.25 over 4 weeks, values remained below 0.25, a range generally considered indicative of reasonably uniform nanoparticle size distributions. TRAINs were designed to remain stable extracellularly while undergoing redox-responsive cleavage under intracellular reducing conditions. DSDMA, a well-characterized disulfide cross-linker used in the TRAIN design is known to cleave under reducing conditions. , After incubation in 10 mM reduced glutathione (GSH) at 37 °C, we observed an increase in particle heterogeneity, with PDI values greater than 0.3 (Table S1), suggesting possible disruption of the nanogel structure. , This increase in PDI is likely the result of GSH-mediated disulfide cleavage, which can promote network swelling causing changes in particle heterogeneity, whereas as TRAINs in DPBS without GSH demonstrated a PDI below 0.3 (Figure A). ,

TRAIN-FLuc mRNA Association via N/P Ratio Titration

Firefly luciferase (FLuc) mRNA was used as a model reporter to characterize TRAIN-mRNA association. TRAINs were incubated with FLuc mRNA at 4 °C for 30 min to facilitate electrostatic surface adsorption (Figure A). mRNA association was systematically tuned by varying the nitrogen-to-phosphate (N/P) ratio, defined as the molar ratio of cationic quaternary ammonium groups on TRAIN to anionic phosphate groups along the mRNA backbone. DLS was employed to evaluate changes in z-average diameter and PDI as a function of N/P ratio. As the N/P ratio decreased, we observed a progressive increase in the z-average diameter, consistent with increasing RNA-mediated bridging and aggregation. PDI values remained low and within an acceptable range (below 0.2) until the N/P ratio dropped below 1, at which point the PDI increased by ∼70% (Figure B). At low N/P ratios (<1), excess FLuc mRNA in solution promotes RNA-mediated aggregation, producing larger, heterogeneous particles that lack colloidal stability (Figure B). In contrast, at higher N/P ratios (≥1), the relative excess of cationic TRAINs both reduced free FLuc mRNA in solution and increased net surface charge (Figure B and Figure C). Together, these effects minimized aggregation by limiting RNA-mediated bridging and promoting electrostatic repulsion between particles, resulting in smaller, more compact, and uniform TRAIN-mRNA formulations (Figure B). Zeta potential measurements further supported these trends. At N/P ratios below 1, TRAIN-FLuc mRNA exhibited a net negative charge (Figure C), consistent with excess free mRNA in solution. At an N/P ratio of 1, the zeta-potential approached near-neutral, indicating charge neutralization in solution (Figure C). As the N/P ratio increased above 2, the zeta potential became increasingly positive, approaching the value of TRAINs alone (+33.5 ± 1.3 mV), suggesting the presence of excess TRAINs relative to mRNA in solution (Figure C).

We next quantified FLuc mRNA association with TRAINs across N/P ratios using the QuantiFluor RNA assay to measure the proportion of free FLuc mRNA remaining in solution after 30 min adsorption at 4 °C. Based on a constant input concentration of 3.2 ng/μL FLuc mRNA in each sample (Figure E; see also Equation S1 and Table S2), we observed at low N/P ratios (≤2) incomplete association (∼23% at N/P = 0.5, ∼62% at N/P = 1, and ∼73% at N/P = 2), whereas N/P ≥ 5 resulted in complete association with ∼100% of the input mRNA to TRAINs and no detectable free FLuc mRNA remaining in solution (Figure E). Agarose gel electrophoresis results (Figure D) are supported by the more sensitive QuantiFluor assay. Notably, residual free FLuc mRNA detected at N/P = 2 by QuantiFluor was not readily visible on the gel, consistent with the qualitative nature and higher detection threshold of gel-based analysis. Next, to confirm that mRNA adsorption was reversible, TRAIN-FLuc mRNA was treated with heparin sulfate (50 μg in DPBS, 15 min at 4 °C). Heparin sulfate induced competitive dissociation of surface-adsorbed mRNA (Figure S3A ), as evidenced by the reappearance of free FLuc mRNA at ∼2000 bp on the agarose gel (Figure S3B). This observation was further confirmed by the QuantiFluor RNA assay results, which showed complete release of FLuc mRNA from TRAINs across all N/P ratios following the addition of heparin sulfate (Figure S4).

Protection and Storage Performance of TRAIN-FLuc mRNA

Based on physicochemical characterization (Figure ), an N/P ratio of 10 was selected for all following studies unless specified. We evaluated TRAIN-FLuc mRNA under a controlled degradation challenge using RNase A as a model enzyme, applying increasing enzyme amounts (0.25–20 ng total), consistent with low ng RNase challenge conditions reported to induce rapid degradation of unprotected RNA, from a 0.5 μg/mL stock solution for 15 min at 25 °C. − Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer analysis showed that TRAINs can protect surface-adsorbed FLuc mRNA from RNase-mediated degradation up to 5 ng, while higher enzyme levels resulted in rapid degradation, evidenced by the loss of FLuc mRNA peak and electropherogram smearing (Figure A and Figure B).

Next, we assessed short-term storage performance of TRAIN-FLuc mRNA at −20 °C (freezer), 4 °C (refrigerator), and 25 °C (room temperature) over 1 week. At −20 °C, FLuc mRNA remained well-preserved with minimal degradation (Figure C; see also Figure S5). At 4 °C, gradual degradation was observed, with ∼83% intact mRNA remaining on day 1 and ∼61% by day 7 (Figure C; see also Figure S5), likely due to the intrinsic RNA hydrolysis at elevated temperature, , leading to the slow breakdown of exposed or weakly bound FLuc mRNA. In contrast, storage at 25 °C led to rapid degradation, with only ∼40% intact mRNA remaining by day 3, accompanied by the appearance of a broad peak at 25–35 s on the electropherogram, indicating degraded FLuc mRNA (Figure C; see also Figure S5). By day 5, no detectable FLuc mRNA peak was observed on the electropherogram (Figure C; see also Figure S5), consistent with enhanced intrinsic RNA hydrolysis at 25 °C.

Colloidal stability during storage was monitored by DLS (Figure D). At 4 and 25 °C, the z-average diameter increased over time (Figure D, red dashed line; P > 0.05), likely coincident with mRNA degradation, which can destabilize the surface association of FLuc mRNA with TRAINs (Figure D). , No significant changes in PDI (Figure D; P > 0.05) were observed across any of the tested conditions. Moreover, storage at −20 °C led to an overall increase in z-average diameter relative to the initial sample (Figure D), consistent with literature showing nanoparticle growth following freeze–thaw cycles due to cryoconcentration-induced aggregation. − Increases in hydrodynamic size into the ∼200–300 nm regime may alter cellular uptake pathways, as nanoparticles below ∼200 nm are generally internalized via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, whereas larger particles may exhibit reduced uptake efficiency or shift toward another cellular pathway mechanism. , This size-dependent shift could alter intracellular trafficking, thereby potentially reducing mRNA translation. , Further temperature studies are required to systematically assess storage-induced size changes of TRAIN on cellular uptake, intracellular trafficking and functional protein expression. Together, these initial findings suggest that inverse microemulsion-derived TRAINs could provide short-term protection for surface adsorbed mRNA, maintaining integrity at 4 °C and −20 °C, potentially through electrostatic confinement of the poly­(A) tail to the cationic particle surface, as proposed in other adsorption-based systems.

TRAIN-FLuc mRNA Delivery to Cultured Cells

To benchmark the biological performance of TRAIN-FLuc mRNA delivery, human embryonic kidney (HEK293T) cells, known for their transfectability, robust protein expression, and well-characterized behavior with a variety of nanoparticle platforms, were used. Cytotoxicity was assessed using the PrestoBlue assay, which quantified cell viability through the reduction of a resazurin-based dye by metabolically active cells, producing a detectable color change from blue to red. HEK293T cells were treated with TRAINs at concentrations up to 200 μg/mL for 72 h. Cell viability remained above 80% at concentrations of ≤120 μg/mL after 72 h of exposure (Figure A), indicating low toxicity within this range. However, at concentrations exceeding 120 μg/mL, cell viability decreases below 80%, which is denoted as a healthy cell culture (Figure A). Based on these results, subsequent experiments were performed with TRAINs at 120 μg/mL, with the N/P ratios standardized accordingly.

Cellular uptake of fluorescein (FITC)-labeled TRAINs associated with FLuc mRNA was quantified in HEK293T cells by flow cytometry, using trypan blue (TB) quenching to distinguish membrane-bound from internalized TRAIN (Figure B). TRAIN volume was fixed (50 μL per well) and FLuc mRNA loading was varied to achieve N/P ratios ranging from 0.5 to 15, the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) changed with incubation time (1–12 h) (Figure C). At early time points (1 h), TB quenching led to a substantial reduction in fluorescence across all N/P ratios, demonstrating that the majority of TRAIN-FLuc mRNA were initially membrane-associated due to electrostatic interactions (Figure C). By 4 h, uptake efficiency diverged based on formulation, with N/P ratios <1 showing poor internalization, likely due to excess free mRNA increasing electrostatic repulsion, while N/P ratios >1 exhibited increased internalization (Figure C). Interestingly, by 12 h, TRAIN-FLuc mRNA with N/P ratios ≥ 5 maintained stable MFI post-TB addition, indicating effective and complete internalization (Figure C). A decrease in MFI between 4 and 12 h pretrypan blue was observed in most formulations, especially N/P ratios 1 and 2, which may reflect reduced surface-associated signal, intracellular redistribution, or partial attenuation of FITC fluorescence in the intracellular environment. ,

Next, we investigated in vitro mRNA delivery by measuring FLuc mRNA expression. We compared TRAINs associated with 0.1 μg FLuc mRNA per well to Lipofectamine 2000, a well-established and standardized in vitro gene delivery carrier, containing FLuc mRNA at an N/P ratio of 10, comparable to our TRAIN-FLuc mRNA formulation. Naked FLuc mRNA and TRAIN-FLuc mRNA at N/P ratios less than or equal to 2 demonstrated low transfection efficiency (Figure D; P > 0.05), with protein expression barely above the experimentally determined background, while higher N/P ratios greater than 2 led to efficient translation into functional luciferase (Figure D; P < 0.0001). Moreover, TRAINs were well-tolerated with cell viability remaining above 100% at all tested N/P ratios (Figure S6). We observed the highest expression level at an N/P ratio of 10, demonstrating an approximate 4.5-fold increase in transfection efficiency over Lipofectamine 2000 (Figure D).

Mechanism of Uptake and Release

We next explored the intracellular delivery mechanism of FITC-labeled TRAIN-FLuc mRNA by quantifying cellular uptake via flow cytometry under conditions that either permitted or inhibited endocytosis. At 37 °C, where endocytic activity is intact, TRAIN uptake was highly efficient (∼84 to 100% relative fluorescence) (Figure E). In contrast, uptake was significantly reduced at 4 °C (∼24 to 31%), where ATP depletion and reduced membrane fluidity suppress endocytosis, confirming that TRAIN internalization is predominantly endocytosis-dependent (Figure E; P < 0.0001). To define the specific endocytic pathway involved, we employed small-molecule inhibitors of clathrin-mediated endocytosis (Pitstop 2), macropinocytosis (EIPA), and caveolae-mediated endocytosis (Nystatin). , Uptake was quantified as the percentage of FITC+ cells and normalized to the uninhibited controls. Pitstop 2 caused the largest reduction in TRAIN uptake (∼73%), identifying clathrin-mediated endocytosis as the dominant internalization pathway for TRAIN-FLuc mRNA in HEK293T cells (Figure F, P < 0.0001). EIPA produced modest inhibition (∼24%), indicating a minor secondary contribution from macropinocytosis, while Nystatin had minimal effect (∼3%), suggesting negligible involvement of caveolae-mediated uptake (Figure F).

Next, we proceeded to evaluate the ability of TRAIN-FLuc mRNA to evade endo-lysosomal degradation, with confocal microscopy analysis performed using live staining LysoTracker Red DND-99 for marking lysosomal compartments, and FITC-labeled TRAINs (Figure A). After 6 h of incubation, TRAIN-FLuc mRNA at an N/P ratio of 10 exhibited low colocalization with lysosomes (∼17–22%), indicating that TRAINs do not remain trapped in the lysosomal compartments of HEK293T cells and suggesting efficient endo-lysosomal escape (Figure B). We calculated the Pearson’s correlation coefficient and found that TRAINs exhibited a negative correlation (∼−0.15 to −0.37), indicating an inverse relationship with lysosome staining and supporting the colocalization finding that TRAINs do not preferentially accumulate in the lysosomes (Figure C).

Positive charge is an effective way for intracellular nucleic acid delivery and endosomal escape. On the other hand, positive charge may also hinder RNA release into the cytosol. The positive surface charge of TRAINs (+33.5 ± 1.3 mV) can help to promote endosomal escape through electrostatic interactions with anionic endosomal membranes, leading to membrane destabilization and increased probability of cytosolic access. In addition to these charge-mediated membrane interactions, the presence of a reductively cleavable disulfide cross-linker in our nanogel design may further promote endosomal-to-cytosolic transfer through intracellular cleavage, enabling cytoplasmic RNA release. To further probe the role of the cross-linker, we produced a control nanogel design by replacing DSDMA with a nonresponsive cross-linker, poly­(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA). Nanoparticles cross-linked with PEGDA are known to form more hydrolytically stable networks that degrade slowly over days through erosion, and upon cellular internalization, tend to become entrapped within lysosomal compartments. As anticipated, the replacement of the cross-linker with PEGDA led to a higher lysosomal entrapment, displaying a higher colocalization ratio with the lysosomal compartment (∼27–48%), and a positive Pearson’s correlation coefficient (∼0.38 to 0.60) (Figure B and Figure C; P < 0.01 and P < 0.0001). This comparison study was further validated using the Bright-Glo assay to assess transfection efficiency. Nanogels cross-linked with PEGDA produced negligible FLuc mRNA expression (Figure D; P > 0.05), whereas those formulated with DSDMA generated a strong bioluminescence signal under identical conditions (Figure D; P < 0.0001), highlighting the potential role of DSDMA in nanogel destabilization, endosomal-to-cytosolic transfer, and downstream mRNA translation.

Modulating TRAINs for Preferential APC Targeting

The TRAIN platform decouples particle fabrication from surface modification, enabling postsynthetic, plug-and-play ligand conjugation. This modularity supports cell-specific targeting without reformulation of the underlying nanoparticle construct, ideally suited for targeted mRNA vaccine delivery. As a proof of concept, we functionalized the TRAIN surface with anti-human CD206 antibodies for APC targeting. CD206 (mannose receptor, MR) is highly expressed on myeloid-derived APCs, such as dendritic cells, monocytes, and macrophages, which are central to initiating and directing adaptive immunity. CD206-mediated endocytosis can route TRAINs to early endosomal compartments rather than directly to degradative lysosomes, which may enhance mRNA translation. ,

CD206-conjugation was achieved by reacting surface exposed APMA amines on TRAINs with a succinimidyl-[(N-maleimido­propionamido)-tetraethylene glycol] ester (NHS-PEG4-maleimide) linker, followed by coupling to reduced anti-human CD206 antibodies (Figure A). Successful conjugation was confirmed using anti-human CD206 Alexa Fluor (AF)­647-labeled secondary antibodies and compensation beads, indicating that the CD206 antibody remained structurally intact and accessible following attachment (Figure S7). The z-average diameter of AF488-labeled CD206-TRAINs was ∼110.5 nm and increased further to ∼165.6 nm upon mRNA association, consistent with ligand conjugation and surface-adsorbed mRNA (Figure S8). Functionalization also reduced the surface charge; unmodified TRAINs exhibited a zeta potential of +33.5 mV, which decreased to +16.8 mV for AF488-labeled CD206-TRAINs, and slightly further to +11.3 mV upon mRNA association (Figure S8), agarose gel electrophoresis illustrates signal retained in the well relative to free mRNA, consistent with mRNA association to TRAINs (Figure S9).

Since receptor-mediated targeting requires close proximity to target cells, we first performed preclinical in vivo studies to evaluate CD206-TRAINs using intramuscular (IM) injection, a clinically relevant route known to recruit both tissue-resident and infiltrating APCs. ,, Anti-mouse CD206-TRAINs and isotype controls (1 μg of FLuc mRNA) were administered IM into female BALB/c mice, and luciferase expression was monitored over 72 h (Figure B and Figure S10). CD206-TRAINs exhibited a delayed kinetic profile, with signal gradually increasing from 24 to 48 h, then beginning to decline at 72 h while remaining detectable (Figure B), consistent with timing of CD206+ APC recruitment following IM injection. , In contrast, Isotype-TRAINs showed no detectable signal (Figure B), confirming that expression was not driven by nonspecific uptake. While unfunctionalized TRAINs produced only weak, transient expression (Figure B). These results suggest that CD206-mediated targeting can provide a distinct, and delayed expression profile in vivo, suggesting more selective uptake by recruited APCs. This behavior may be advantageous for mRNA vaccines requiring localized antigen expression and targeted immune activation.

To assess translational relevance, we next evaluated CD206-TRAIN targeting ex vivo in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), which contain both CD206– lymphocytes (T cells and B cells) and CD206+ myeloid subsets (monocytes and dendritic cells). Notably, myeloid cells comprise only ∼5–7% of PBMCs in healthy individuals, providing a stringent model for assessing selective delivery. PBMCs were incubated for 24 h with either unfunctionalized TRAINs (TRAINs), CD206-functionalized TRAINs (CD206-TRAINs), or Isotype-functionalized TRAINs (Isotype-TRAINs), the latter serving as a baseline for nonspecific interactions. Flow cytometry revealed that CD206-TRAIN uptake was restricted to the myeloid population, with minimal uptake by lymphocytes (Figure S11). Cells were gated using forward scatter (FSC) versus side scatter (SSC) to identify PBMCs, followed by singlet discrimination and exclusion of dead cells using Zombie Aqua (Figure S11A). Double-positive (DP) cells, identified by co-staining of TRAIN (AF488-labeled) conjugated to anti-human CD206 antibody (AF647-labeled), were found exclusively within the myeloid population and expressed mCherry (Figure S11B), confirming receptor-specific uptake and functional mRNA delivery.

Further phenotypic analysis demonstrated preferential internalization of CD206-TRAINs by monocytes and dendritic cells, with minimal uptake in T cells and B cells (Figure C and Figure D). Compared to Isotype-TRAINs, CD206-TRAINs showed enhanced uptake in CD206+ APCs, consistent with receptor-mediated endocytosis. CD206 engagement is known to route cargo to early endosomal compartments rather than degradative lysosomes, providing a favorable intracellular pathway for TRAIN. This endosomal localization positions any translated antigen for efficient MHC-II loading and presentation to effectively activate CD4+ T cells. Functional delivery was confirmed by mCherry expression, with CD206-TRAINs performing comparable to the model system Lipofectamine 2000, with expression primarily observed in monocytes and dendritic cells (Figure E). Together, these findings suggest that receptor-mediated targeting could help to direct TRAINs to desirable intracellular trafficking pathways that support functional mRNA delivery and antigen presentation.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed TRAINs, as a surface customizable, polymeric nanogel platform for adsorption-based mRNA loading and the capability for targeted delivery. By decoupling nanoparticle fabrication from mRNA association, TRAINs can be preformulated and stockpiled in advance to serve as ready-to-use scaffolds for mRNA vaccine delivery. With TRAINs we have demonstrated near-complete mRNA loading at N/P ratios ≥ 5, while preserving mRNA integrity and transfection competency without the need for extensive formulation optimization. The surface-associated FLuc mRNA demonstrated protection from RNase-mediated degradation through a RNase degradation challenge assay. In HEK293T cells, TRAINs exhibited efficient cellular uptake predominantly via clathrin-mediated endocytosis with minimal lysosomal sequestration, resulting in a 4.5-fold enhancement in FLuc mRNA translation relative to Lipofectamine 2000. Importantly, the preformulated inverse microemulsion-derived nanogel architecture provides a chemically addressable scaffold that supports postsynthesis ligand conjugation using standard bioconjugation techniques. In preclinical studies, IM administration of CD206-TRAINs in mice produced a gradual and localized FLuc mRNA expression compared to unfunctionalized TRAINs, likely driven by recruitment of CD206+ immune cells and lymphatic trafficking. This delayed kinetic behavior supports selective uptake by infiltrating APC populations at the injection site. As a proof of concept, leveraging the platform’s modularity, CD206-TRAINs selectively delivered adsorbed mCherry mRNA to APCs in human PBMCs, a low-abundance yet critical immune population for DC-targeted vaccination. CD206-TRAINs preferentially transfected CD206+ monocytes and DCs. Together, these results highlight the adaptability of adsorption-loaded, inverse microemulsion-derived TRAINs for mRNA delivery. Future studies will further explore the stability and targeting nature of TRAINs as well as induction of cellular and humoral immunity as a result of efficient APC targeting and mRNA vaccine delivery.
