Classification of mediastinal compartments: is separation of the superior mediastinum crucial?—a clinical practice review
Authors: Takao Nakanishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The division of the mediastinum into specific compartments is essential for identifying, characterizing, and managing mediastinal abnormalities. The current international standard is the three-compartment model proposed by the International Thymic Malignancy Interest Group (ITMIG), which divides the mediastinum into prevascular (anterior), visceral (middle), and paravertebral (posterior) compartments. This model has replaced the traditional four-compartment system, which included the superior mediastinum, due to its simplicity, familiarity, and clearly defined boundaries. However, given the superior mediastinum’s unique anatomy and distinct pathology, it remains clinically reasonable to consider it separately. Continued clinical use of the term “superior mediastinum” has produced numerous conflicting definitions, while the Japanese Association for Research on the Thymus (JART) has recently proposed a computed tomography (CT)-based four-compartment model. Disease distribution in the superior mediastinum is dominated by thyroid goiters and neurogenic tumors, while the incidence of thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) in this region varies among studies. Surgery in the superior mediastinum is challenging due to its narrow space and the presence of major blood vessels and nerves. Operative complications frequently include Horner’s syndrome. Furthermore, studies suggest that superior mediastinal thymomas may demonstrate more aggressive behavior and lower survival rates than thymomas in other locations. Establishing a clear, standardized definition of the superior mediastinum would resolve current inconsistencies and facilitate the accumulation of detailed clinical data on superior mediastinal lesions. This article focuses on the superior mediastinum and discusses its classification, clinical features, surgical approaches, treatment outcomes, and future perspectives.

Introduction

The mediastinum is the central compartment of the thoracic cavity, bordered laterally by the pleural cavities and containing all thoracic organs except the lungs and pleura. It extends from the thoracic inlet to the diaphragm and is bounded anteriorly by the sternum and posteriorly by the vertebral column. The mediastinum houses vital structures, including the heart, great vessels (aorta, superior and inferior vena cava, pulmonary arteries, and veins), trachea, esophagus, thymus, lymph nodes, nerves (vagus nerve, phrenic nerve, and sympathetic trunk), and connective tissue. Dividing the mediastinum into compartments is crucial for accurately identifying, characterizing, and managing various abnormalities. The three-compartment model proposed by the International Thymic Malignancy Interest Group (ITMIG), based on cross-sectional imaging, particularly computed tomography (CT), is the current international standard (1-5). This model divides the mediastinum into the prevascular (anterior), visceral (middle), and paravertebral (posterior) compartments. Although it omits the superior mediastinum as a distinct compartment, other classification systems retain it because of its unique anatomy and specific disease patterns. This article examines the superior mediastinum in detail, focusing on its classification, clinical features, surgical approaches, and treatment outcomes.

Definition of superior mediastinum, recent CT-based divisions, and synonyms

Classifying the mediastinum into compartments is clinically important for describing and categorizing abnormalities. Traditionally, mediastinal classification relied on anatomical landmarks observed on chest radiographs. Although the Shields classification is the most widely used (6), many systems have been proposed, and no universal standard exists. This lack of standardization stems from the mediastinum’s uniform radiographic density and the absence of distinct anatomical landmarks on plain films. The advent of CT, particularly high-resolution multidetector CT, has enabled more precise delineation of mediastinal structures and a more detailed compartmental division. In 2009, the Japanese Association for Research on the Thymus (JART) published the first edition of the general rules for the study of mediastinal tumors, using CT transverse section images to categorize the mediastinum. In 2014, JART proposed this CT-based classification system globally (7). The JART classification divides the mediastinum into four compartments: superior portion, anterior, middle, and posterior. ITMIG later modified this system into a three-compartment model, omitting the superior portion, and introduced it as the new international standard (2). Compartment classification is based on cross-sectional imaging, and large lesions are localized using either the “center method” or the “structure displacement tool”. In the center method, the lesion’s center (defined as the midpoint of its largest axial CT image) determines the compartment involved (7). A structure displacement tool is applied when a lesion spans multiple compartments, assigning the tumor to the compartment adjacent to the displaced organ (2).

In the JART classification, the superior mediastinum, termed the “superior portion of the mediastinum”, is defined as the area between the superior border (thoracic inlet) and a horizontal plane intersecting the caudate margin of the brachiocephalic vein (BCV) with the tracheal midline (7) (Figure 1A,1B). In real-world settings, the only clear anatomic boundary within the mediastinum is the pericardium (8). Thus, JART defined the lower border of the superior mediastinum to align with the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) lymph node map, which uses this line to separate the #2R and #4R lymph nodes. This definition, while consistent, is artificial and lacks natural fascial boundaries. Consequently, neoplastic, infectious, and inflammatory processes can spread freely between the superior and inferior compartments (2,3). For these reasons, and for simplicity and continuity with previous models, ITMIG adopted the three-compartment system (2) (Table 1).

The ITMIG model remains the current international standard, whereas the JART classification has not been adopted outside Japan. Nonetheless, many studies continue to use the term “superior mediastinum” because of its distinct anatomical and pathological features, and it remains clinically reasonable to consider it independently. Limited adoption of the JART system has led to multiple conflicting definitions of the superior mediastinum. The classical definition, above the Ludwig plane [a line from the lower edge of the manubrium to the fourth intervertebral disc (Th4/5)], is still commonly used (9-12) (Figure 1C). In addition, numerous synonymous terms exist for similar regions, including mediastinal roof, top of the mediastinum (13), anterosuperior mediastinum (14), cervicothoracic region (15-17), thoracic outlet (18), and pleural cupula mediastinum (19). Some were differently defined, and others were not defined at all. For instance, Zheng et al. defined the top of the mediastinum as the region above the inferior margin of the aortic arch and tracheal bifurcation (13), whereas Wang et al. defined the cervicothoracic junction as extending from the C7–T4 transverse processes to the first rib and sternum (16).

Distribution of diseases in the superior mediastinum and the diagnostic point of the region

Accurately determining the prevalence of mediastinal lesions and their distribution among mediastinal compartments remains challenging due to the rarity of these lesions and variations in study designs and populations. Differences in imaging modalities, diagnostic criteria, and regional epidemiology must be considered when interpreting findings. Among imaging modalities, CT is most commonly used for evaluating mediastinal lesions, while magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography are applied in selected cases (20). MRI is superior to CT for differentiating cystic from solid lesions (21) and distinguishing thymic hyperplasia from thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) (4). Advances in imaging technology have improved diagnostic accuracy, making comparisons with earlier studies difficult. Study design also affects reported tumor distribution. Whether a study includes only pathologically confirmed cases or those diagnosed radiologically can influence its results. Some benign lesions, such as thymic and pericardial cysts, are not routinely resected or biopsied, and the inclusion of such non-neoplastic lesions varies across studies (20,22). Regional factors influence disease incidence. For example, thymomas are more frequent in China (23), while lymphomas are reported more commonly in Poland (24). The reasons for these differences remain unclear but may involve environmental exposure, genetic variation, and selection bias. There is no published literature on familial clusters of thymoma, nor are there any definitive data showing a link between tobacco, alcohol, diet and thymoma (25). Two large retrospective studies have reported mediastinal tumor distribution using the ITMIG classification (20,23), emphasizing the value of these findings for differential diagnosis and treatment planning. Because the JART classification is less widely recognized, distributions based on it have been published only by JART (7) and in our group’s conference abstract (26). In the JART report, the most common superior mediastinal tumor was thyroid goiter (51.9%), followed by neurogenic tumors (37.0%), mature teratomas (7.4%), and bronchogenic cysts (3.7%); TETs were not identified in this region (7). In contrast, our data showed a higher incidence of TETs (27.2%), cysts (27.2%), goiters (27.2%), and schwannomas (18.1%) (26). To our knowledge, only one other study has evaluated the incidence of superior mediastinal tumors, although it did not use the JART system. That study reported lymphomas (33%) as the most common tumor type, followed by TETs (26%), goiter (13%), and teratomas (13%) (26).

Fujimoto et al. (7) noted that the exclusive presence of intrathoracic goiter and the relative paucity of TETs in the superior mediastinum characterize and support the diagnostic value of the four-compartment model. However, the reported rarity of TETs in this region is not definitive, as several studies have found that 7–44% of TETs occur in the superior mediastinum (10,26,27). Furthermore, although thyroid goiter appears distinctive to this compartment, its diagnosis does not depend on location; CT findings typically allow reliable differentiation (2,3). Higher attenuation relative to soft tissues, calcification, and early, prolonged contrast enhancement are characteristics of mediastinal goiters (Figure 2) (28-30).

Preoperative diagnosis is not essential for resectable thymic tumors (31). However, biopsy for histopathological, immunohistochemical, and molecular analysis is indicated for patients presenting with an irregular, invasive tumor (32). While the relatively large size of the manubrium affects the biopsy route, biopsy of superior mediastinal lesions can be performed with a transsternal or suprasternal approach (33), and the complication rate after biopsy of superior mediastinal tumors is comparable that of other lesions (34).

In summary, classification of the mediastinal compartment is important for the differential diagnosis of mediastinal tumors; however, dividing the superior mediastinum provides little benefit for differentiation.

Surgical approach to superior mediastinal tumors

Because the mediastinum is cone-shaped, its upper portion is the narrowest, making surgical access technically demanding. The superior mediastinum contains several major blood vessels and nerves, further complicating operative management. Surgical approaches to this region are therefore more complex than those to the anterior, middle, or posterior mediastinum. The operative strategy should be individualized based on the tumor’s location, size, and extent of invasion, as well as the surgeon’s experience and preference. Most procedures are performed via median sternotomy, although a hemiclamshell or anterior transclavicular/manubrial approach is also reasonable. Minimally invasive techniques, including video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and robotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS), have been increasingly adopted.

The surgical approach to the superior mediastinum is not standardized, and the literature provides limited guidance, with few studies addressing this issue specifically. Two main factors contribute to this variability. First, the definition and nomenclature of the superior mediastinum differ among reports, and the JART classification is seldom used. Second, heterogeneity in tumor origin, invasiveness, involvement of adjacent structures, and extent of resection has led to diverse non-uniform surgical approaches.

Zheng et al. (13) compared surgical approaches for superior mediastinal tumors, though they defined the superior mediastinum differently as the top of the mediastinum. They reported that VATS and RATS offered superior short-term outcomes, such as reduced intraoperative bleeding, shorter drainage duration, and decreased postoperative hospital stay, compared with conventional open surgery, and emphasized the advantage of RATS for procedures in confined spaces. However, a high incidence of Horner’s syndrome and the substantial cost associated with RATS have also been reported (12). The transcervical approach is another minimally invasive option, typically indicated for tumors located less than 6 cm below the clavicle, measuring under 4 cm, and not invading surrounding structures (14).

Although several studies have compared surgical approaches for thymectomy, most did not specify tumor location (35-37). Malignant tumors, particularly TETs in the superior mediastinum, tend to invade major vessels due to their anatomical position, which significantly influences surgical planning and outcomes. Despite the lack of large clinical trials and formal guidelines (38), the completeness of resection, including extended combined resection of invaded structures, when necessary, remains the most important prognostic factor (39,40). Procedures involving the ascending aorta or aortic arch replacement are technically demanding and have been described only in a few reports (38). The superior vena cava (SVC) and BCVs are the vessels most commonly affected by invasive thymic malignancies; their resection and reconstruction are critical and technically complex aspects of surgery in this region (41) (Figure 3). The operative approach should be tailored to optimize exposure of vital structures. Median sternotomy, hemiclamshell incision, or cervicosternotomy are the primary approaches. Although VATS and robotic techniques have been reported for selected cases (42), indications for a minimally invasive extended resection remain limited, and their long-term outcomes are not yet well established (40).

At the uppermost portion of the superior mediastinum, commonly referred to as the cervicothoracic junction, the surgical approach is especially challenging due to the narrow operative field and proximity to critical neurovascular structures, including the carotid artery, jugular vein, and thoracic duct. Special attention should be paid to the recurrent laryngeal nerve, sympathetic trunk, and brachial plexus, as neurogenic tumors are the most common lesions in this region (53.8%) (16). Although open procedures, such as the anterior cervical approach, median sternotomy, hemiclamshell, trapdoor, and extended lateral thoracotomy, are still commonly used, none have been fully standardized because of poor accessibility and visualization (16,17,43-45). The combined use of the transmanubrial osteomuscular sparing approach (TMA) and VATS is an effective option for cervicothoracic junction tumors, as it provides excellent visualization from an opposing angle (18,46).

Complications following resection of superior mediastinal tumor are distinctive, with a notably high incidence of Horner syndrome, ranging from 5.2% to 41.4% (12,13,17) (Figure 4). Most cases resolve spontaneously within 1 year postoperatively (17,18,44). Other reported complications included hoarseness (2.8–12.5%) (13,16,44) (Figure 5), chylothorax (1.4–8.6%) (12,13,16,17), upper limb paralysis (1.4–12.5%) (12,16,17,44), and upper limb swelling (1.4%) (16).

In summary, there is no single optimal way to approach superior mediastinal tumor; key influencers include their location, size, invasiveness, and the surgeon’s preference. The key factors in selecting approaches are having a clear view and access, and availability of an alternative emergency access (47). Using a combination of different approaches is effective.

Clinicopathological features and prognosis of TETs in the superior mediastinum

When interpreting the prognosis of TETs, their clinicopathological characteristics must be considered. First, many patients, particularly those with stage I and II tumors, die of unrelated causes. These patients may live for years even after recurrence (48). Overall survival (OS) is strongly influenced by patient-related factors, including age, performance status, and comorbidities. In contrast, disease-free survival analysis often excludes patients with incomplete resection, despite reported complete resection rates of 65.5–86.6% (49,50). Second, the inclusion or exclusion of thymic carcinomas, which carry a significantly worse prognosis than thymoma (51), affects survival outcomes. Third, variations in staging systems used across studies also contribute to prognostic differences. Before the release of the 8th tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification (52), the use of the Masaoka-Koga or Masaoka system varied across studies. Finally, due to the rarity of TETs, small sample sizes remain a major limitation, particularly in multivariate analyses (48).

Only one study has specifically examined the prognosis of superior mediastinal thymomas (10), where the superior mediastinum was defined as the area above the Ludwig plane, likely because they included cases that predated the CT era. Superior mediastinal thymomas accounted for 13.9% of all thymomas and were significantly associated with higher rates of myasthenia gravis (55.6% vs. 29.3%), advanced Masaoka-Koga stage (40.7% vs. 18.6%), and postoperative disease progression (44.4% vs. 8.4%) compared with thymomas in other locations. Both OS and progression-free survival were significantly lower in superior mediastinum thymomas (188.5 vs. 349.3 months and 133.7 vs. 316.2 months, respectively). Tumor location and Masaoka-Koga stage were the only independent risk factors for recurrence identified in multivariate analysis.

Numerous prognostic factors for TETs have been reported, including tumor stage (TNM, Masaoka, or Masaoka-Koga) (51,53-56), tumor size (53,55), incomplete resection (54), pleural invasion (57), chest wall invasion (58), lung invasion (59), great vessels invasion (56), number of invaded organs (60), histological subtype (54,55,58-60), age (54,58,59), and male sex (51,58,59). Among these, incomplete resection, pleural invasion, chest wall invasion, great vessel invasion, and multiorgan involvement might be more common in superior mediastinal thymomas because of the narrow space and proximity to vital structures. However, definitive evidence supporting this application is lacking. Further studies are needed to clarify whether tumor location independently affects prognosis, as has been demonstrated in other malignancies such as gastric cancer (61). Therefore, precise definition and consistent reporting of tumor location are essential for future analyses.

Discussion and future perspective

Global standards have limited value unless they are adopted by medical professionals worldwide. To achieve this, a classification system must be simple, easy to understand, and clinically useful. The ITMIG three-compartment model is straightforward and increasingly applied; however, its clinical utility is reduced by the exclusion of the superior mediastinum, which possesses distinct anatomical and pathological features. Consequently, recent studies continue to use the term “superior mediastinum” with varying definitions and nomenclatures.

From a diagnostic imaging standpoint, independent classification of the superior mediastinum offers limited benefit, primarily improving the identification of goiters. Therefore, the ITMIG’s three-compartment model remains appropriate for diagnostic imaging. In contrast, in the surgical management of mediastinal tumors, it is clinically reasonable to consider the superior mediastinum separately because of its unique anatomy, technical difficulty of resection, and impact on surgical outcomes, particularly for TETs and neurogenic tumors.

To address the limitations of the ITMIG’s model and resolve inconsistencies in defining the superior mediastinum, adding an anterosuperior compartment as a subdivision of ITMIG’s prevascular compartment may provide a solution. The lower boundary could be defined by the caudal margin of the left BCV, as proposed in the JART classification. Although this adjustment is sufficient for classifying TETs, it does not adequately include posterosuperior neurogenic tumors. For these, defining the region as the “cervicothoracic junction” is reasonable, although the term is interpreted differently by thoracic surgeons and neurosurgeons (16,62). When multiple tumor types and overlapping specialties are involved, achieving consensus on this issue remains difficult: prioritizing TETs during ITMIG classification refinement would simplify the framework and promote consistency. Establishing a clear definition of the anterosuperior compartment would enable accurate accumulation of clinical outcomes, thereby improving the management of TETs. Although thymoma is the most common lesion in the mediastinum (20), the overall incidence of thymoma is reported to be 0.13 per 100,000 person-years (63). Due to the rarity of these diseases, almost all reports on the distribution of mediastinal lesions or treatment outcomes are retrospective or based on databases (63-66). Thus, the absence of a definitive international classification of the superior mediastinum has resulted in the lack of data on the region in meta-analyses and database analyses (20,67,68). The 9th edition of the TNM classification demonstrates the importance of simplicity and reproducibility by incorporating tumor size to define T1a and T1b (69). Tumor location could similarly serve as a reproducible and clinically meaningful stratification factor if shown to influence TETs prognosis. The modification to the definition has caused confusion (70) and requires time to establish (71), as seen in changes to the lymph node map in lung cancer. However, the lymph node map defined in the 7th edition of the TNM classification for lung cancer (72) greatly improved the uniformity, accuracy, and completeness of data collection, resulting in the subdivision of N2 in the 9th edition (73). To minimize confusion caused by the modification to the definition, we proposed a minor change by adding a subdivision. As the JART defines the border of the superior mediastinum based on the IASLC lymph node map, the time required to establish the refined definition and its effect on the accumulation of evidence regarding the region can be predicted from the IASLC case.

This review could not include a comprehensive literature analysis due to the lack of a standardized definition of the superior mediastinum and the diversity of existing nomenclatures. Nevertheless, it provides sufficient context to understand the current challenges and perspective regarding the region. This review aims to clarify ongoing confusion and contribute to the accumulation of more accurate clinical data on superior mediastinal tumors.

Conclusions

The current international standard for mediastinal compartments classification, the ITMIG’s three-compartment model, omits the superior mediastinum. However, because of its unique anatomical and pathological characteristics, it remains clinically reasonable to consider this region separately, particularly when evaluating surgical approaches and outcomes of TETs and neurogenic tumors. Given the numerous conflicting definitions and terminologies, establishing a global standard for defining the superior mediastinum is essential to unify clinical practice and facilitate meaningful accumulation of data.
