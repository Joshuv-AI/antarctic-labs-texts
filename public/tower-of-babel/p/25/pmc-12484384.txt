Minimal Residual Disease Negativity as the Primary Goal of Multiple Myeloma Therapy
Authors: Jennifer H Cooperrider, Benjamin A Derman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMeasurable (or minimal) residual disease (MRD) testing offers critical prognostic insight in multiple myeloma (MM), surpassing conventional response criteria. While bone-marrow-based assays are most commonly performed, MRD assessment in peripheral blood and advanced imaging may add complementary value. A comprehensive approach, integrating serial MRD testing across compartments, may offer the most accurate appraisal of disease burden. MRD has been validated as a surrogate endpoint for accelerated approval (AA) of MM therapies and is increasingly adopted as a key clinical trial endpoint. Ongoing phase 3 trials are using MRD status to tailor consolidation and maintenance strategies, and emerging evidence supports its role in guiding treatment de-escalation, including discontinuation of maintenance therapy. However, barriers remain to implementing MRD as a treatment goal, including cost, complexity of interpreting results, and uncertainty around the optimal timing for guiding decision-making. Moreover, there is a paucity of data on the use of MRD resurgence to prompt changes in therapy. While MRD negativity represents a compelling endpoint in both clinical practice and research, prospective randomized studies will help to better elucidate how best to incorporate MRD into the MM treatment paradigm.

Key Points

MRD negativity is strongly associated with improved prognosis in multiple myeloma (MM); combining serial assessments across peripheral blood, bone marrow, and imaging modalities enhances disease monitoring and prognostication.Emerging data, including those from ongoing phase 3 clinical trials, have the potential to enable MRD-guided personalization of therapy, particularly with respect to treatment de-escalation.

Introduction

Measurable (or minimal) residual disease (MRD) in multiple myeloma (MM) refers to the measurement of low levels of myeloma cells that cannot be ascertained using conventional methods. MRD in myeloma can be assessed in three different “compartments.” The most familiar is the bone marrow, where a bone marrow aspiration can yield highly precise and informative results. However, myeloma is by nature a patchy disease leading to sampling variability; in addition, extramedullary sites of relapse serve as another frustrating reason for false-negative MRD results from bone marrow aspirate. The second compartment is the extramedullary space, which can be assessed using advanced imaging techniques; while this may capture disease outside of the marrow, it often does not reflect bone marrow status. The last compartment is the peripheral blood, a proverbial “catch-all” for occult sites of disease that other techniques may miss but disadvantaged by the predilection for myeloma cells to reside in the bone marrow. For these reasons, a multimodal assessment of disease burden may be necessary to reach a consensus on the “source of truth.”

Herein, we provide a review of currently available MRD assays and an appraisal of the evidence for MRD as a prognostic tool in MM, as a surrogate endpoint, and as a guide for treatment decisions in MM.

Back to Basics: MRD Assays

Bone-Marrow-Based Assays

The concept of MRD in myeloma is not new, but its significance grows with time owing to increased sophistication and sensitivity of bone marrow MRD assays. The two leading methods for MRD assessment of bone marrow aspirate are multiparametric flow cytometry (MFC) and next-generation sequencing (NGS).

MFC simultaneously identifies cell surface markers characteristic of aberrant plasma cells, and does not require a baseline calibration sample. While this allows for easier access to MFC, MFC analysis strategies vary widely and lack standardization across assays [1, 2]. Most laboratories utilize MFC assays with a sensitivity of 10−4 to 10−5 (able to detect the equivalent of one aberrant myeloma cell per 10,000–100,000 total cells analyzed); some institutions have validated their assay, for example, Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, who used a single ten-color tube with a limit of detection (LoD) of 6 cells per million (6 × 10−6) with at least 3 million cell acquisitions [3]. The most sensitive MFC is next-generation flow (NGF; also known as EuroFlow), which is an eight-color two-tube panel that has an LoD of 2 × 10−6 (20 aberrant plasma cells per 10 million cell acquisitions) and a limit of quantitation (LoQ) of 5 × 10−6 (50 aberrant plasma cells per 10 million cell acquisitions) [4]. Importantly, this means that a highly cellular, large-volume, first-pull bone marrow aspirate is necessary to achieve 10−6 MRD sensitivity. NGF is a standardized and replicable assay and is capable of identifying issues with hemodilution; however, there may be inter-operator variability, and it is not universally available.

MRD testing using NGS works by identifying and quantifying immunoglobulin heavy chain (IgH) variable–diversity–joining (VDJ); diversity–joining (DJ); and kappa–lambda rearrangements that are unique to individual plasma cells [1, 2]. A calibration sample is required to identify the unique clonotypic sequences of malignant plasma cells to enable MRD tracking; 90–95% of patients will have at least one trackable clonotype [5, 6]. The most commonly employed NGS MRD assay is clonoSEQ (Adaptive Biotechnologies, Seattle, Washington), which has an LoD of 6.8 × 10−7 and an LoQ of 1.76 × 10−6 when 20 mg of DNA (approximately 2–3 million cellular equivalents) is input [5]. The LoD of clonoSEQ can also be expressed differently; specifically, it can detect the equivalent of 1.9 aberrant cells in a sample that may occasionally fall below the 10−6 threshold (i.e., MRD < 10−6) depending on the total input. ClonoSEQ is a standardized assay and can be performed on fresh or frozen samples and is capable of achieving 10−6 sensitivity with fewer cells than NGF. While an estimated nucleated cell count analyzed is provided, it is not possible to decipher whether hemodilution has occurred [7].

Evaluating the Extramedullary Compartment: Imaging

Whole-body (WB) positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET/CT) and diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have become the leading imaging methods for detection of both osseous and extramedullary sites of disease.

Prognostically, undetectable disease by PET/CT is complementary to bone marrow MFC at a threshold ranging from 10−4 to 10−5; PET/CT still adds value to these less sensitive bone marrow methods [8, 9]. Other studies that have examined PET/CT and bone marrow MRD methods with more sensitive thresholds ranging from 10−5 (MFC) to 10−6 (NGS) have found that only a limited number of patients (6–11%) have detectable disease by PET/CT despite undetectable disease in the bone marrow following autologous stem cell transplant and/or consolidation [10, 11]. With the improved lifespan of patients living with myeloma, PET/CT still appears to offer a benefit, as the incidence of extramedullary disease at relapse has increased, particularly in patients with high-risk disease features [12, 13]. B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR T) therapy can induce a high rate of MRD negativity and is now approved for earlier use with investigations in the frontline already underway; PET/CT is crucial in monitoring for disease in the post-CAR T setting given the high incidence of EMD in post-CAR T relapse [14, 15]. It is important to note that patients with low expression of the hexokinase 2 enzyme make up 10% of patients with MM, and this may lead to false-negative PET/CT results, though the prognostic implications of this are unclear [16, 17].

WB diffusion-weighted MRI gained popularity following the inclusion of MRI lesions measuring > 5 mm as a myeloma-defining event justifying systemic therapy. One of the key changes that has enabled its use as a tool for detecting MRD is the standardization of the acquisition, interpretation, and reporting of WB-MRI using the Myeloma Response Assessment and Diagnosis System (MY-RADS) [18, 19]. WB-MRI protocols can be completed within an hour. The reported apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values can help distinguish between normal tissues and myeloma disease; for instance, the ADC of normal bone marrow is usually < 600–700 µm2/s, while tumor ADC is between 700 and 1400 µm2/s, and treated or necrotic disease ADC usually exceeds 1400 µm2/s [19]. Nevertheless, ADC values can sometimes show overlap due to biological variability, necessitating clinical correlation for interpretation. In comparison to PET/CT, WB-MRI was found to be more sensitive for the detection of focal and diffuse MM disease [20, 21], raising the question of whether WB-MRI could supplant PET/CT as the de facto imaging tool for MRD assessment.

Peripheral Blood MRD Assays

There are a multitude of assays for MRD in the peripheral blood of patients with MM, but the issue is that MM cells predominantly home to the bone marrow or extramedullary sites rather than circulate in the peripheral blood. This presents a challenge because the low amount of circulating MM cells may limit the sensitivity of cell-based assays compared with bone-marrow-based techniques.

Application of the NGF technology in peripheral blood samples of patients with MM found that detection of MRD in the blood by NGF was associated with worse outcomes [22–25]; however, NGF MRD assessment in the blood is less sensitive than NGF MRD from bone marrow, even when performing CD138+ selection on large-volume blood samples [26, 27]. Additionally, CD38-directed monoclonal antibodies can cause interference with NGF assays, as they bind to epitopes that overlap with monoclonal diagnostic CD38 antibodies [28]. Peripheral blood clonoSEQ NGS is also less sensitive compared with the same method performed on bone marrow samples [29–31]. Other NGS-based assays for MRD detection in the blood in myeloma are under investigation.

Rather than trying to identify circulating tumor cells or DNA, MRD assays that detect the paraprotein produced by aberrant multiple myeloma cells have proven to be highly informative. This should come as no surprise given that serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) and immunofixation (IFIX) have been used for decades for this very purpose. However, SPEP/IFIX carries a maximal sensitivity of 0.01 g/dL, which limits its utility as an MRD assay.

Mass spectrometry (MS) has emerged as a highly sensitive tool for the detection of low levels of monoclonal protein in the blood of patients with MM. Plasma cells acquire unique heavy and light chain genes through somatic recombination and hypermutation, and therefore, each plasma cell produces a unique immunoglobulin with a specific amino acid sequence and molecular mass. MS can detect and quantify these unique monoclonal proteins on the basis of their mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), allowing for precise identification and quantification of residual paraprotein [32].

Despite the umbrella term, not all MS methods are the same. There is the intact immunoglobulin light chain approach (also known as the top-down approach) and the clonotypic peptide sequencing approach (bottom-up approach). Matrix-assisted laser desorption–ionization (MALDI) time of flight MS is a top-down method wherein a laser is used to ionize proteins embedded in a crystalline matrix, allowing for the precise measurement of m/z ratios of the monoclonal immunoglobulin on the basis of their “time of flight” through the flight tube, without extensive sample preparation. MALDI MS has facilitated the development of specific assays such as MASS-FIX (Mayo Clinic) and EXENT (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Whereas the MASS-FIX assay creates five immunoglobulin isolations using camelid-derived nanobodies linked to agarose beads that are directed against IgG, IgA, IgM, kappa, and lambda, EXENT uses polyclonal sheep nanobodies linked to paramagnetic beads to accomplish the same task [33–36]. Both methods can be performed efficiently, can distinguish therapeutic monoclonal antibodies from paraprotein (as in IgG Kappa MM), and are significantly more sensitive than IFIX (Fig. 1) [33–39]. Of note, reported sensitivities of MS assays may differ on the basis of the characteristics of the assay and whether patient plasma or saline were used for dilution experiments. The latter will generate lower sensitivities but may not reflect real-life scenarios, as well, except in cases of low polyclonal background, such as those which occur with anti-myeloma therapies.

MALDI methods can be performed without a baseline diagnostic sample [40–42]. Nevertheless, confidence around the result is superior when a high-tumor burden sample, generally a sample with an M-spike of at least 0.2 g/dL, is available to guide the exact paraprotein m/z ratio(s) to be tracked. Both MASS-FIX and EXENT use antisera for total kappa and total lambda light chains, capable of capturing light chains bound to intact immunoglobulins as well as free light chains. The role of a free light chain (FLC)-MS only assay and whether it offers any advantage over the five-bead MS assays is under investigation [43].

The other intact immunoglobulin light chain approach is liquid chromatography (LC)-MS, where LC separates the proteins by charge and size before MS is performed. LC-MS is ten-fold more sensitive than MALDI and can be “reflexed” off of EXENT-negative samples [36, 44]. It has also been shown to be strongly associated with prognosis [44–46].

The clonotypic peptide approach may be the most sensitive technique of all, approaching 100–1000× superior sensitivity compared with MALDI. First, the clonotypic peptides must be identified from a “high”-tumor-burden sample with an M-spike of at least 0.2 g/dL or FLC of at least 0.04 g/dL (40 mg/dL). The sample is exposed to enzymes that digest the proteins into small peptides before analysis. Using bioinformatics algorithms that analyze and compare the m/z ratios and fragmentation patterns with known peptides, the clonotypic peptides are selected for tracking by MS in subsequent samples [2, 47, 48]. The EasyM (RapidNovor) and M-InSight (Sebia/Corgenix) clonotypic peptide assays have both been shown to detect disease in cases where high-sensitivity bone marrow assays could not [49, 50].

Most peripheral blood assays share several limitations. First, except for NGF, all other assays ideally require or benefit from a baseline sample to enable tracking at the highest sensitivity. This necessitates forethought on the part of the patient and the clinician at the time of diagnosis or another time of higher disease burden. Second, it is unclear when the optimal time(s) to be assessing for MRD in the blood is. Blood NGF and NGS may be better suited for early-response assessments; one study found that blood NGS positivity after four cycles of quadruplet induction therapy was associated with inferior progression-free and overall survival [31]. However, MS methods are better suited for later timepoints, as MS may give the false illusion of detectable disease due to immunoglobulin recycling that is most prominent with IgG antibodies [51, 52]. The time to clearance of IgA antibodies or free light chains will be much shorter. For EXENT, it may take as long as 18 cycles post-transplant for it to be a prognostic marker [40]. For LC-MS and clonotypic peptide assays, the challenge may be that very few patients have undetectable disease, thereby necessitating the definition of a concentration threshold (such as in NGF/NGS) and/or the longitudinal tracking of the miniscule concentrations of M-proteins to inform on prognosis.

Challenges and Limitations of MRD testing

In addition to some of the challenges of specific assays, other pitfalls apply across all existing MRD tests.

Some patients with MM may present with an undetectable M-protein or involved FLC (non-secretory) or with concentrations below the threshold for measurable disease (oligosecretory), which have traditionally made protein-based tracking uninformative. NGS and MFC-based methods do not face this issue. The introduction of MS has reduced the number of cases of true non-secretory disease [53]. Soluble B-cell maturation antigen (sBCMA) is emerging as a potentially useful peripheral blood tool for MRD monitoring and specifically in oligosecretory/non-secretory myeloma [54].

Additionally, MRD testing comes at an added expense, and it may not be available and/or reimbursed in every country [55]. Existing clinical infrastructure must also be adjusted to facilitate the workflow of ordering MRD testing, including sample ordering, collection, processing, and result interpretation. These issues are particularly challenging, as data are still emerging regarding how and when to implement MRD testing to augment treatment.

There may also be additional unseen costs of testing for patients. Anxiety is associated with an MRD-positive status, particularly when some patients who have evidence of disease do not progress over long periods of time. Patients who are found to be MRD-positive report feeling disappointment and anxiety and felt they received insufficient education on the implications of the results [56]. This is especially relevant, as some patients with persistent disease have a very low risk of progression, such as those who develop monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS)-like MM [57]. This highlights the need for clinicians ordering MRD testing to be comfortable with and adept at properly contextualizing MRD results.

All In: The Argument for a Multimodality Approach

In light of these limitations, each compartment—the bone marrow, extramedullary space, and peripheral blood—can give meaningful and complementary information. Achievement of MRD negativity (typically < 10−5 or 10−6) in the bone marrow and MS negativity are each individually associated with better outcomes and appear complementary to each other [40, 42, 45, 58], and cell-based peripheral blood MRD assays such as NGF and NGS appear to be informative as well [31, 58]. In the MRD2STOP study, patients with multimodal MRD negativity by MFC (10−5), NGS (10−6), and PET/CT who discontinued therapy had an estimated 3-year progression-free survival (PFS) of 85% and a cumulative incidence of MRD 10−6 resurgence or disease progression within 3 years of only 30% [59]. These encouraging data suggest that it is feasible to perform these assessments simultaneously and raise the question of whether it can be used to guide decision-making.

Is MRD the Great Prognosticator?

Prognostic Value of MRD Negativity

Depth of response has long been associated with outcomes in MM. Older studies found that achievement of a complete response (CR) was associated with improved survival [60, 61]. However, with improvement in the ability to detect disease using MRD assays, MRD status has emerged as a more powerful prognostic biomarker. Munshi et al. conducted a meta-analysis of 21 studies (n = 496) assessing MRD in myeloma patients who achieved a complete response by International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) criteria, indicating that MRD negativity improves upon the prognostic value of the current response measurements. In this cohort, patients negative for MRD had improved PFS (56 versus 34 months, p < 0.001) and overall survival (OS; 112 versus 82 months, p < 0.001). A later meta-analysis that expanded to 44 studies showed that bone marrow MRD negativity was associated with PFS and OS regardless of the disease setting, assay, depth of clinical response, sensitivity threshold, cytogenetic risk, and timing of assessment [62]. The association between MRD negativity and survival outcomes also holds when using peripheral blood assays, such as MALDI (EXENT) and LC-MS [40, 44].

There is frequently discordance between IMWG response criteria and MRD negativity, and up to 40% of patients can have a persistent M-spike despite achieving MRD negativity in the marrow [63, 64]. Currently, the IMWG consensus criteria for MRD negativity requires achievement of a CR in addition to MRD negativity by either flow, sequencing, and/or imaging [65]. However, persistent M protein in patients otherwise negative for MRD does not appear to impact prognosis. For example, in a pooled analysis of Spanish trials, patients with MRD negativity but a positive M protein had similar PFS and OS outcomes to patients with MRD negativity in at least a CR [66]. MS, which may be seen as an extension of paraprotein-based response evaluation, may be complementary to bone-marrow-based MRD testing and could further inform prognosis.

The More Sensitive the Assay, the Better

While MRD negativity signals a better prognosis in myeloma regardless of sensitivity or method, there is also evidence that the prognostic value of MRD negativity improves with increasing assay sensitivity. A 2018 analysis of the IFM-2009 trial by Perrot and colleagues compared outcomes by depth of response in patients treated with bortezomib–lenalidomide–dexamethasone (VRd) +/− autologous stem cell transplant. They found that patients who were MRD-negative at 10−5 but MRD-positive at 10−6 had a 3-year PFS of 42%, while 3-year PFS was 76% in patients who were MRD negative at both 10−5 and 10−6 [63]. Indeed, LoD appears to be prognostically more important than the type of assay used for detection. MRD was assessed by both NGF and NGS in the FORTE trial, and in subsequent analyses, there was concordance between the two methods in 87% of samples at a 10−5 threshold and 83% of samples at a 10−6 threshold. Additionally, PFS was similar in patients who achieved sustained MRD negativity at 10−5 for 12 months, regardless of whether MRD was assessed by NGS or NGF [7]. It may be possible to achieve an even deeper level of sensitivity with additional prognostic implications. The MRD2STOP trial included an exploratory endpoint assessing MRD detection at a level of 10−7 using CD138+ enrichment of larger-volume bone marrow aspirate. The cumulative incidence of progression was higher for patients, with 10−7 positivity compared with 10−7 negativity at baseline [59].

Trends are More Important Than a Snapshot!

MRD trends over time provide additional prognostic information. In the FORTE trial, the 4-year PFS in the carfilzomib–lenalidomide–dexamethasone (KRd)-with-transplant arm (69%) was superior to the KRd alone (56%) or carfilzomib–cyclophosphamide–dexamethasone (KCd)-with-transplant arms (51%). However, when MRD negativity was sustained for greater than 12 months, PFS was similar across the three arms. Other trials have corroborated the importance of sustained MRD negativity, as well. A longitudinal analysis of MRD negativity in the ATLAS trial showed that patients who achieved sustained MRD negativity over 12 months had superior PFS [67]. Similarly, in the MAIA and ALCYONE trials, which investigated daratumumab (Dara)-based therapies in newly diagnosed MM without transplant intent, MRD negativity sustained over 12 months was associated with superior PFS compared with MRD positivity or non-sustained MRD negativity, regardless of the treatment received [68]. Sustained MRD negativity has also been associated with improved PFS in the relapsed/refractory setting. This was seen, for example, in the CASTOR and POLLUX trials, which randomized patients to receive daratumumab-based triplet regimens or doublet regimens. Daratumumab-containing triplets induced sustained MRD negativity at 6 and 12 months at significantly higher rates than the control regimens, and this was associated with improved PFS [69]. Together, these findings suggest that, while MRD negativity at a single time point is associated with survival, sustained MRD over time carries additional prognostic significance.

Conversely, MRD resurgence following achievement of MRD negativity confers worse survival outcomes. The GIMEMA VEL-03-096 trial, in which patients were treated with bortezomib–thalidomide–dexamethasone (VTd) after autologous stem-cell transplantation (autoSCT), showed that MRD recurrence heralded relapse, and time to next treatment was comparable to that in patients who had MRD persistence (9 months versus 10 months, p = 0.706) [70]. Additionally, in a study of 568 patients with myeloma who achieved sustained BM MRD negativity by NGF for at least 3 months, 39% of patients converted back to MRD-positivity over a period of 9.9 years. Loss of MRD negativity occurred a median of 1-year prior to clinical relapse (range 0–4.9 years) and a median of 6 months before biochemical relapse (range 0–2.7 years). Patients with conversion to MRD positivity had an inferior PFS and OS when compared with patients with sustained MRD negativity (PFS: hazard ratio [HR] 18.9, p < 0.001; OS: HR 1.7, p = 0.01) [71]. MRD resurgence also appears to have prognostic implications in the quadruplet era; a retrospective analysis showed that a log-fold increase in MRD in patients (mostly in active surveillance) who received quadruplet induction therapy and autoSCT was associated with imminent clinically progressive disease, even when restarting treatment [72]. Other factors appear to predict loss of MRD negativity, including having International Staging System (ISS) stage 3 disease, have circulating tumor cells present, and not achieving MRD negativity after induction, which confer a higher risk when combined [73]. These factors may identify patients who are at high risk for resurgence of MRD despite achieving negativity and therefore may be at high risk for relapse in the setting of treatment de-escalation based on single MRD result.

Loss of MRD negativity is associated with worse survival outcomes, but MRD resurgence is not currently considered a criterion of progression in the same way as biochemical and clinical progression [73]. While clinicians may choose not to de-escalate therapy in the case of rising or resurgent MRD [74], there is a paucity of data on whether to change or escalate therapy in patients with MRD recurrence.

Should MRD Negativity Be Used as a Surrogate Endpoint?

Most clinical trials investigating a new treatment regimen in newly diagnosed multiple myeloma use PFS as the endpoint to demonstrate clinical benefit to gain full approval from regulatory bodies such as the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). PFS benefits have been accompanied by OS in several pivotal MM studies [75–77], though this has not uniformly been the case [78–80]. PFS has nevertheless been accepted by the FDA and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) as a valid endpoint for drug approvals in MM, provided that an OS decrement has not been shown.

Comparative studies may not provide a signal for statistically significant superiority in PFS for 5–10 years from completion of patient enrollment. Thus, there has been a growing interest in using MRD as a potential surrogate endpoint for accelerated approval of new (combinations of) drugs.

According to the FDA, for MRD negativity to be accepted as valid intermediate clinical endpoint for accelerated approval, the endpoint must be reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit. The applicant is then required to conduct a postmarketing study to confirm the clinical benefit. For relapsed/refractory MM, overall response rate (ORR) has been the only accepted endpoint to support accelerated approval. This has typically been accomplished through single-arm studies submitted to the FDA; however, the FDA has expressed a preference for accelerated approval trials that have an embedded control arm [81].

To understand how a surrogate endpoint can become established, it is important to review the differences between patient-level surrogacy and trial-level surrogacy. Patient-level surrogacy asks the question “Does the surrogate endpoint carry prognostic value for an individual?” Proving patient-level surrogacy requires a meta-analysis to identify an individual-level association that assesses the impact of the earlier endpoint on the long-term endpoint within an individual patient [82]. With respect to MRD, individual-level associations have already been clearly demonstrated, and MRD negativity is associated with superior survival outcomes, particularly when it is sustained over 12 months [62, 83, 84].

Trial-level surrogacy of a proposed surrogate endpoint must also be demonstrated. Trial-level surrogacy asks the question “Does an intervention that improves the surrogate also improve the final outcome of interest?” Proving a trial-level association requires a meta-analysis to assess the correlation between MRD and either PFS or, ideally, OS. This means showing that a difference in MRD negativity between two interventions is correlated with a difference in a long-term endpoint such as PFS or OS, across multiple trials using individual patient-level data. The output from such analyses is typically an R2 value, with R2 > 0.7 indicating a statistically acceptable correlation [85, 86].

In 2024, the FDA Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee (ODAC) held a meeting to discuss MRD as potential surrogate endpoint for accelerated approval in myeloma. Two groups presented data to support MRD as a surrogate endpoint. The EVIDENCE meta-analysis found that among patients with newly diagnosed MM, MRD < 10−5 was correlated with PFS, (The trial-level correlation was estimated using either a Plackett copula model or weighted least squares [WLS], in which the weights were derived from either the sample size of each trial or the estimated inverse variance [WLSiv]; R2WLSiv 0.67 and R2Copula 0.84), and this remained true for patients ineligible for transplant (R2WLSiv 0.84 and R2Copula 0.85) [83]. However, MRD < 10−5 was not correlated with OS (R2WLSiv 0.33 and R2Copula 0.32) for all patients, though it may have been for patients ineligible for transplant (R2WLSiv 0.83 and R2copula 0.63) [83]. The i2TEAMM also presented similar findings and identified that MRD < 10−5 with complete response at 9 or 12 months was correlated with PFS in newly diagnosed and relapsed/refractory MM populations [84]. The FDA ODAC voted 12–0 in favor of accepting MRD negativity as a clinical valid intermediate endpoint for accelerated approval.

On the one hand, it can be argued that MRD negativity should be celebrated as an endpoint for accelerated approval; it is far more informative than ORR, which has been the standard. Rather than using ORR to usher in marginally effective drugs, MRD negativity would theoretically be used to get highly effective drugs to patients faster. However, there is the concept of a surrogate threshold effect (STE) that must be considered with trials looking to use MRD negativity rates to gain accelerated approval. STE refers to the smallest treatment effect on the surrogate necessary to be observed to predict a treatment effect on the true endpoint [87]. In other words, how much of a difference in MRD negativity rates between two arms must be observed to predict that there will be a meaningful PFS or OS difference?

The FDA provided examples in the ODAC briefing documents based on the data that was presented and found that, for instance, if the control arm has an MRD-negative/CR rate of 30%, the treatment arm would need to have an MRD negativity rate of 48% and 68% to predict a treatment effect on the PFS and OS, respectively. In the case of the IsKia trial comparing isatuximab–carfilzomib–lenalidomide–dexamethasone (Isa-KRd) to KRd in patients eligible for transplant with newly diagnosed MM, the MRD < 10−5 rate was 67% in the KRd control arm and 77% in the Isa-KRd arm [88]. With such a high MRD-negative rate in the control arm, a 10% absolute difference in MRD negativity may be statistically significant, but it would not meet the STE. For this reason, we may see MRD < 10−6 as a more useful endpoint. While the MRD < 10−6 rate was 48% for KRd and 67% for Isa-KRd in that same trial, even this larger difference may not be sufficient to meet the STE [88].

For these reasons, MRD as an AA endpoint may be most helpful with novel immunotherapies, where rapid and deep responses can be obtained compared with conventional chemotherapies. The cautionary tale for MRD as an endpoint is the BELLINI trial, which found venetoclax added to bortezomib and dexamethasone was associated with a superior MRD-negative rate and PFS but an OS decrement [78]. This emphasizes the point that MRD negativity is unlikely to capture toxicity-related deaths and highlights the role of the postmarketing study to verify the clinical benefit.

How Should We Utilize MRD Status to Adapt Therapy?

MRD as a prognostic tool and surrogate endpoint in MM appears to be gaining widespread acceptance, but MRD as a tool to guide decision-making and adapt therapy remains understudied. Nevertheless, there are a number of published and ongoing trials that utilize MRD-adaptive strategies in various stages of MM therapy, a selection of which are highlighted in Table 1.

To Transplant or Not to Transplant?

Recent trials have established the superiority of quadruplet over triplet induction regimens in fit patients under 70 years of age pursuing high-dose melphalan and autologous stem cell rescue [88, 89]. The PERSEUS study compared daratumumab–VRd (Dara-VRd) induction and consolidation followed by daratumumab–lenalidomide (Dara-R) maintenance with VRd induction and consolidation followed by lenalidomide (R) maintenance; 65% of the patients in the Dara arm reached MRD < 10−6 compared with 32.2% in the comparator arm [89]. In the GMMG-HD7 study, 66% of patients reached MRD < 10−5 with Isa-VRd compared with 48% with VRd [90]. Anti-CD38-containing quadruplet regimens have also led to deeper responses and superior outcomes compared with VRd in fit older adults up to the age of 80 years without intent for autoSCT [91, 92]. In the IMROZ trial, the rate of MRD < 10−5 was 58% for Isa-VRd compared with 43.6% for VRd, with a near doubling of sustained MRD negativity of 47% for Isa-VRd versus 24% for VRd [92]. The CEPHEUS trial comparing Dara-VRd with VRd in patients without transplant intent found an MRD negativity (10−6) rate of 46% for Dara-VRd compared with 27% for VRd [93].

The excellent outcomes of quadruplet therapy with or without autoSCT add to an ongoing debate: is autoSCT necessary for all patients eligible for transplant? AutoSCT represents a modality capable of logfold reduction of MRD; one contemporary study found that the median reduction in MRD with autoSCT was 1.10 log10 (range − 1.26–3.41) and appeared to be slightly higher for patients with high-risk cytogenetic abnormalities [94]. However, studies such as IFM-2009 have shown that patients who achieve MRD negativity following maintenance have similarly excellent outcomes regardless of whether they receive autoSCT [63]. If MRD negativity were to serve as a signal to forgo transplant, the major unsettled issue is when the appropriate time to use MRD to guide such a decision is.

Most transplant-intended patients undergo four cycles of induction followed by stem cell mobilization and collection, followed by autoSCT. However, even in the era of quadruplets, most patients will still have MRD positivity after four cycles of therapy. For instance, MRD < 10−6 was achieved following four cycles of induction in only 1% of patients receiving a shorter course (12 weeks) of Dara-VRd in the GRIFFIN trial, 24% in patients receiving Dara-KRd (16 weeks) in the MASTER trial, and 18–27% of patients receiving Isa-KRd in the SKylaRK and IsKia trials. However, it is clear from other quadruplet studies without autoSCT that the rate of MRD negativity increases with additional cycles of therapy; for instance, in patients receiving 24 cycles of Dara-KRd without transplant, MRD < 10−6 increased from 35% following eight cycles to 53% at later timepoints [44]. In both arms of the CEPHEUS trial, MRD negativity rates increased through at least 24 months of therapy [93]. Regardless of depth of response, a patient’s cytogenetic risk may necessitate more intensive treatment. More evidence is needed to help establish whether MRD at any time point can appropriately guide decision-making around frontline autoSCT and, if so, in which patients and at what timepoint.

MRD-adapted transplant decision-making is under further investigation in the ongoing ADVANCE trial, which randomizes patients to eight cycles of DaraKRd versus KRd followed by an MRD assessment [95]. Patients with MRD negativity continue with maintenance therapy, while patients with MRD positivity may optionally proceed with autoSCT. The initial analysis found that the rate of MRD < 10−5 was superior with Dara-KRd compared with KRd (59% versus 36%) [96]. The MIDAS trial assigns patients to received Isa-KRd for six cycles, after which an MRD assessment is performed; patients with MRD positivity at the 105 threshold proceed with a single or tandem autoSCT, while those with MRD negativity are randomized to either additional Isa-KRd or autoSCT with quadruplet consolidation, both followed by lenalidomide maintenance [97]. Among patients with MRD < 10−5 following induction, the rates of MRD < 10−6 did not differ between the auto-SCT (86%) and IsaKRd consolidation (84%) groups (OR 1.17, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.65–2.76, p = 0.65). Moreover, tandem autoSCT did not improve MRD negativity rates over a single autoSCT in the MRD-positive group (32% versus 40%, OR 0.73, 95% CI 0.42–1.25, p = 0.31) [97]. In both the ADVANCE and MIDAS trials, PFS and OS are immature but suggest that MRD status after six to eight cycles of induction may be able to guide decision-making around autoSCT. MASTER-2 trial also employs MRD assessment after six cycles of induction with Dara-VRd; patients with MRD < 10−5 are randomized to receive either Dara-VRd followed by Dara-R or autoSCT followed by Dara-R [98]. These trials will continue to provide much-needed evidence on how MRD status can inform on the utility of autoSCT.

Recent preliminary data on the use of the BCMA × CD3-directed bispecific antibody (BsAb) teclistamab as part of induction therapy in the MajesTEC-5 study showed that all patients were able to achieve MRD negativity at 10−6 threshold within three cycles of therapy [99]. This begs the question whether the deep responses induced by BCMA BsAbs in the frontline may further circumvent the need for autoSCT.

MRD-Adapted Quadruplet Therapy Without Transplant Intent

In patients who are not eligible for or choose to defer autoSCT, MRD status may also provide information on optimal timing of treatment de-escalation (e.g., discontinuation of proteasome inhibition or anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody therapy). Trials utilizing quadruplet therapy without transplant do not have a standard duration of therapy before de-escalation, and there is some evidence that responses continue to deepen with more than eight cycles of quadruplet therapy in the absence of transplant [45, 91–93]. However, there is no randomized evidence to guide duration of therapy without transplant, and more studies utilizing MRD-adapted strategies are needed to inform the optimal timing of quadruplet de-escalation in the absence of autoSCT.

MRD-Adapted Consolidation Therapy

Multi-drug consolidation therapy following autoSCT is often used prior to transitioning to a less intensive maintenance therapy. MRD could also be used during the post-autoSCT phase of treatment to identify patients who may benefit from extended consolidation therapy after autoSCT. Several studies have utilized MRD-adapted strategies to guide therapy de-escalation. The ATLAS trial randomized patients to either MRD-adapted KRd or R monotherapy for at least 3 years following autoSCT. In the KRd arm, patients with standard-risk disease who reached MRD negativity at 10−6 after cycle 6 were transitioned to R alone to complete 36 cycles of protocol therapy. Patients with high-risk disease or MRD positivity at 10−6 after 6 cycles of KRd continued KRd for a total of 36 cycles. Despite some patients de-escalating treatment early, KRd was associated with superior PFS (median 59.1 versus 41.4 months, HR 0.51, p = 0.012). In the MASTER trial, patients could receive multiple phases of treatment including induction with Dara-KRd × four cycles, autoSCT, and Dara-KRd consolidation for up to eight additional cycles followed by lenalidomide maintenance depending on their MRD status. Patients with MRD negativity at 10−5 on two consecutive occasions then discontinued all therapy, which occurred in 71% of patients. The 36-month PFS was 73% in the entire cohort, which was enriched for patients with high-risk chromosomal aberration (HRCAs), though patients with 2+ HRCAs had worse outcomes (36-month PFS 50%) in part due to early progression before discontinuation of therapy. Sustained MRD < 10−6 was associated with statistically superior outcomes, despite these patients stopping treatment [100]. Taken together, these findings confirm that patients who achieve MRD negativity (particularly sustained MRD negativity) may be able to safely de-escalate consolidation therapy.

MRD-Adapted Maintenance Therapy

The ultimate step in de-escalation would be to discontinue all therapy. Current guidelines recommend lenalidomide maintenance after autoSCT, and maintenance is generally continued until the time of disease progression [101]. However, the optimal duration of maintenance therapy has not been fully elucidated to date, and protracted myeloma therapy is associated with important toxicities, including second primary hematologic malignancies [79, 102]. As such, MRD negativity may be an attractive signal to identify patients who can safely discontinue maintenance therapy.

The GEM2014MAIN [103] trial exploring ixazomib + lenalidomide–dexamethasone (Rd) versus Rd as maintenance assigned patients with MRD < 10−6 by NGF after 2 years to discontinue all maintenance; patients with MRD negativity had a 4-year PFS from discontinuation of 83% compared with only 50% for patients with MRD positivity who continued therapy. Other pragmatic prospective trials have focused on patients achieving deep responses regardless of induction strategy, autoSCT receipt, or maintenance.

The MRD2STOP trial allowed for maintenance discontinuation in patients with confirmed MRD < 10−6 with ≥ 1 year of maintenance therapy and found that, with 30 months of follow-up from discontinuation, the 3-year PFS after discontinuation was 85% and the 3-year MRD-free survival was 68% [59]. A Greek study that allowed for maintenance discontinuation only after 3 years of maintenance and three consecutive MRD-negative results showed a 3-year treatment-free survival of 75.8% in those who discontinued maintenance [104]. A Memorial Sloan Kettering study investigating discontinuation of maintenance therapy in patients with 3 years of sustained MRD < 10−5 by flow cytometry has preliminarily shown a 2-year PFS rate of 91% and a 2-year MRD-negative rate of 82% [105, 106]. The ongoing FREEDMM trial allows for maintenance discontinuation in patients who have completed at least 2 years of maintenance therapy and demonstrate sustained MRD negativity [107]. The largest randomized trial investigating an MRD-guided discontinuation strategy in the post-autoSCT maintenance setting is the DRAMMATIC [108] study, where patients are first randomized to receive either Dara-R or R for two years; patients with MRD < 10−6 are then randomized to continue maintenance or stop all therapy. This cooperative group study is posed to provide invaluable information regarding MRD kinetics and its ability to impact decision-making.

With the evolution of highly effective anti-myeloma combination regimens, there may indeed be patients who do not require any maintenance at all. The entirety of data supporting maintenance therapy comes from studies that involved induction regimens most would now consider insufficient. The MASTER trial did not include maintenance for patients with consecutive MRD < 10−5 results and to great effect. In the CASSIOPEIA trial, patients were randomized to Dara-VTd or VTd followed by autoSCT, and then again randomized to either daratumumab alone or active surveillance. In the patients receiving Dara-VTd during induction, only a modest benefit was observed with daratumumab maintenance versus observation (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.58–1.00, p = 0.048). Rates of sustained MRD < 10−6 were slightly higher with daratumumab versus observation (at 12 months, 47.6% versus 36.2%) [109]. While MRD negativity rates were still higher with daratumumab maintenance, these data suggest that a sizable minority of patients will achieve and sustain deep responses without any maintenance.

Early results from the MajesTEC-4 study investigating teclistamab with or without lenalidomide as post-autoSCT maintenance has found the 12-month MRD negativity rate (10−5) to be 100%, once again highlighting the possibility of incorporation of BCMA BsAb into the frontline [110].

MRD Persistence and Resurgence

Nearly all MRD-adapted studies have focused on de-escalation of therapy for patients with MRD negativity. However, it remains unexplored whether patients with persistent MRD positivity may benefit from therapy escalation. The EQUATE study (NCT04566328) is a randomized phase 3 trial that assigns all patients to nine cycles of Dara-Rd, followed by MRD testing by NGS in the bone marrow [111]. Patients with MRD positivity receive bortezomib in addition (Dara-VRd), whereas patients with MRD negativity continue Dara-Rd as part of a consolidation phase. AURIGA is a phase 3 study analyzing MRD negativity rates in patients with MRD positivity following autoSCT receiving Dara-R versus R; Dara-R was associated with higher conversion rates to MRD negativity at 12 months and as best response [112]. In the MASTER-2 trial, patients with persistent MRD positivity after induction are randomized to autoSCT followed by either teclistamab and Dara or Dara-R [98]. These studies may help to elucidate whether intensification of therapy for patients with MRD positivity can lead to better outcomes.

Perhaps even more challenging is what to do for patients with MRD resurgence but without clinical or biochemical progression. Depending on the threshold used for MRD resurgence (i.e., 10−4, 10−5, 10−6), the time to disease progression may differ but the evidence overwhelmingly suggests that progression is inevitable. Similar to what the field has done for biochemical progression criteria, should MRD resurgence be considered a signal to change treatment? One trial aiming to answer this question is the REMNANT study. Patients are followed from diagnosis through their first line of treatment; patients who achieved MRD < 10−5 by NGF are then randomized to start daratumumab–carfilzomib–dexamethasone (Dara-Kd) either at conventional relapse or at the time of MRD resurgence [113]. This ambitious study has the co-primary endpoints of PFS and OS from the time of randomization.

Limitations and Challenges of Existing MRD-Adapted Trials

Most MRD-directed trials in MM to date are nonrandomized single-arm studies. The lack of randomization within MRD groups makes it unclear whether MRD-guided discontinuation or de-escalation of therapy is non-inferior to continuing treatment. The ongoing MIDAS trial may help to address this limitation, as both patients with MRD negativity and MRD positivity undergo separate randomizations to different treatment schema. The DRAMMATIC study also randomizes patients with MRD negativity but does not randomize patients with MRD positivity.

In its guidance to investigators designing MRD-adapted studies, the FDA has advised that the MRD threshold to guide decision-making should be tenfold higher than the lower limit of detection of the assay. For MM, this means that, if an assay can achieve 10−6 sensitivity, then 10−5 sensitivity should be used for decision-making. However, knowing that deeper sensitivity corresponds with better outcomes, is 10−5 a suitable threshold for de-escalation or discontinuation? Patients and clinicians must consider whether a 10−5 threshold could lead to discontinuation of therapy in patients who still have known residual disease and whether this would lead to potentially worse outcomes. In the MASTER trial, MRD < 10−5 was used as the decision-making threshold per FDA guidance; sustained MRD < 10−6—but not sustained MRD < 10−5—was associated with superior outcomes. In the case of an assay such as clonoSEQ, the accuracy around a given result at the 10−6 threshold is 95%, suggesting that less stringent thresholds for decision-making are unnecessary. Multiple trials have illustrated that using MRD < 10−6 to guide discontinuation is feasible [59, 104]. To that effect, the DRAMMATIC study is also utilizing MRD 10−6 as a decision-making threshold. Ultimately, the goal is to identify the least amount of therapy and the lowest number of MRD evaluations to determine when a patient may be able to de-escalate or discontinue therapy.

While MRD-adapted treatment allows for personalization of therapy based on response, the MASTER trial illustrated that therapy de-escalation may not be appropriate for patients with high-risk disease features, such as those multiple high-risk cytogenetic abnormalities. Patients with 2+ HRCAs had significantly worse survival outcomes, even if they achieved sustained MRD negativity (36-month PFS 88% for those with 0 HRCAs versus 60% for 2+ HRCAs). In the MRD2STOP study, patients with at least one HRCA were at a higher risk for MRD resurgence. Thus, discontinuation of therapy in patients with high-risk disease features does not appear to be prudent.

Finally, MRD-adapted treatment has not been investigated in the relapsed/refractory setting. Myeloma patients are receiving novel treatments in later lines of therapy that may lead to MRD negativity—most notably with CAR T-cell therapy [114–116] and bispecific antibodies—which carries prognostic weight [117, 118]. Immunotherapies are also being investigated in combination or in sequence with other therapies—for example, maintenance after CAR T-cell therapy [119] and BCMA-targeting bispecific antibodies combined with CELMODs or anti-CD38 therapies [120]. These are areas of opportunity for treatment de-escalation or intensification based on MRD status in patients with relapsed/refractory disease and merit further investigation.

Should MRD Testing be Part of Standard Clinical Practice?

Using MRD for Clinical Decision-Making

A 2022 case-based survey of myeloma clinicians revealed that 60% changed their treatment recommendation on the basis of an MRD result, despite many participants previously indicating that they felt there was no data to support MRD status as a treatment guide [74]. The evidence base has grown since that time, but the question remains whether MRD testing should be used in the clinic. There are scenarios in which MRD testing can inform standard clinical practice now, which are supported by existing data. Combination therapy in the post-autoSCT setting, such as with Dara-R or KRd, carry at least a PFS benefit over monotherapy and can be de-escalated on the basis of MRD negativity [89, 121]. Multiple studies have also demonstrated the safe de-escalation of maintenance therapy in patients with standard-risk disease and MRD negativity at 10−6. In these cases, MRD testing provides prognostic data that can inform treatment in real-time by identifying which patients might benefit most from additional treatment.

What Might the Future of MRD-Guided Decision-Making Look Like?

Data from ongoing trials will provide a stronger evidence base for MRD-guided decision-making at key points in the treatment algorithm in MM (Fig. 2). For example, the MIDAS, ADVANCE, and MASTER-2 trials will provide additional information on whether autoSCT can be safely omitted in patients who achieve MRD negativity with induction therapy.

MRD status may be used to guide decision-making at other key decision points, for example, to determine the duration of consolidation after autoSCT, the duration and intensity of therapy for patients forgoing autoSCT, and escalation of treatment for suboptimal responders. Randomized approaches within MRD-negative and MRD-positive groups, separately, will be necessary to better elucidate how MRD status can be used to augment treatment at various points in time and may lay the groundwork for more personalized treatment for patients based on response.

Access to MRD testing also needs to be improved to best leverage its capabilities. Patients may not be able or willing to undergo serial bone marrow assessments for MRD assessment, and clinicians may not have enough time to perform them. Despite carrying a low risk for severe complications, bone marrow aspiration does add anxiety and pain for patients [122]. The other piece to access is that high-sensitivity MRD assays such as NGS or NGF are not universally available to clinicians. While MRD assays carry an expense, the potential cost savings associated with treatment de-escalation may more than offset the cost of testing [123].

In the future, peripheral blood assays will also likely add complementary prognostic information without additional invasive testing, though the optimal clinical implementation of these tests remains to be determined. While their non-invasive nature is desirable for patients and clinicians, and they represent the capability to monitor extramedullary disease, there are no data examining their use to guide decision-making.

Lastly, clinicians must also be able to accurately interpret MRD test results and contextualize them in the rapidly evolving landscape of MRD-adapted treatment for multiple myeloma; this may require additional training to understand and implement results from various assays.

Conclusions

MRD in MM is a vital prognostic tool and is poised to be used as an endpoint to support accelerated approval in select settings. The next frontier is incorporating MRD as a decision aid, including serial testing to capture MRD dynamics over time and guide adaptive treatment strategies. Achievement of MRD negativity, especially sustained MRD negativity, can signal opportunities for treatment de-escalation, whereas the clinical implications of MRD resurgence and potential treatment escalation remain uncharted territory. Multimodal approaches that combine the best that bone marrow assays, imaging techniques, and peripheral blood tests have to offer will provide much-needed guidance for patients and clinicians to optimize therapy across all stages of treatment to maximize efficacy and minimize toxicity. Rather than focusing solely on MRD at a single timepoint, the goal should be achieving and maintaining deep responses over time while intervening on MRD resurgence destined to transform to clinical progression. MRD kinetics will be the key to unlocking more precise and individualized care for patients with multiple myeloma.
