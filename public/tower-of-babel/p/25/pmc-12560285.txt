Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1-alpha (HIF1α), Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide (NAD+, NADH), and Nitric Oxide (NO) interplay in critically ill patients, with implications for patient safety and targeted therapies: a review
Authors: David Bar-Or, Kaysie Banton, David Acuna, Jason Williams, Carlos H. Palacio, Charles W. Mains, Raymond Garrett
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCritical illnesses such as sepsis, trauma, and multi-organ failure initiate complex molecular cascades that disrupt cellular homeostasis and contribute to poor outcomes. Key among these mediators are Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide (NAD+/NADH), Nitric Oxide (NO), and Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1-alpha (HIF1α), each playing crucial roles in redox regulation, immune modulation, and tissue adaptation to hypoxia.ReviewThis review examines the individual and interrelated roles of NAD+, NO, and HIF1α in critically ill patients. We explore how imbalances in NAD+/NADH ratio impair mitochondrial metabolism, how dysregulated NO signaling alters vascular tone and immune responses, and how HIF1α stabilization governs transcriptional programs under hypoxia. By mapping the biochemical crosstalk among these molecules, we identify potential feedback loops that influence inflammation, cellular energetics, and patient outcomes. A central theme is the safety and therapeutic relevance of modulating these mediators, with specific focus on the timing, context, and molecular profile of patients in critical care.ConclusionUnderstanding the dynamic interplay among NAD+, NO, and HIF1α offers new opportunities for precision medicine in critical illness. We propose a hypothesis that phase-specific modulation of these pathways—through NAD+ precursors, NO donors/scavengers, or HIF1α stabilizers—could improve outcomes if guided by real-time molecular profiling. Prioritizing patient safety through targeted, biomarker-informed interventions may prevent progression to multi-organ failure and enable more personalized and effective treatments.

Background

A critical illness is a serious health condition where vital organ systems are at risk of failing, requiring intensive medical care and close monitoring to support life. Common causes include trauma, severe infections (such as sepsis), major surgeries, or complications from chronic diseases [1]. These illnesses often lead to an inflammatory response in the body, which can result in systemic issues like acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), kidney failure, or multi-organ dysfunction [2–4]. Patients in critical condition typically require treatments such as mechanical ventilation, medications to support blood pressure, or dialysis, depending on the organs affected. The recovery process is complex and can be prolonged, sometimes leading to long-term physical or cognitive impairments. Immediate, specialized care in an intensive care unit (ICU) significantly increases the chances of survival and recovery, though critical illness remains a challenging medical condition with complex biological responses, a high risk of morbidity and mortality [5, 6]. Among these molecular responses, the roles of Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide, (NADH and NAD+), nitric oxide (NO), and Hypoxia Induced Factor 1 alpha (HIF1α) appear to be important players. NADH is a central cofactor in metabolic redox reactions, critical for ATP production [7–9]. NO is a versatile lipophilic gaseous molecule involved in vasodilatation, immune regulation, neurotransmission, and platelet activation and aggregation [10, 11]. Together these molecules participate in a dynamic network that affects the pathophysiology of critically ill patients.

Designing balanced interventions for critically ill patients is complex and challenging, requiring ongoing research to effectively translate knowledge into clinical practice. The purpose of this review is to explore the individual and interrelated roles of Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide (NAD+/NADH), Nitric Oxide (NO), and Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1-alpha (HIF1α) in the pathophysiology of critical illness [12–16]. We will examine the biochemical crosstalk among these molecules, identify potential feedback loops influencing inflammation and cellular energetics, and outline potential therapeutic interventions aimed at modulating these pathways. By exploring the impact of these molecules on metabolic and adaptive mechanisms, this review serves as a resource for researchers and clinicians, providing insights that may guide future studies toward new diagnostics and therapeutic strategies in critically ill patients.

NAD+ and NADH in critical illness:

The oxidation of NADH is the crucial process of cellular respiration and plays a central role in electron transfer and energy production within cells [8, 9, 17]. Various enzymes have evolved to detect NAD+ levels and translate them into cellular signals, including sirtuin deacylases (SIRT1–SIRT7) [18], C-terminal Binding Proteins (CtBPs) [19], and poly-ADP-ribose polymerases (PARPs) [7, 20, 21]. These NAD+-dependent enzymes irreversibly catabolize NAD+ unlike in glycolysis, where NAD+ is regenerated from NADH when pyruvate is converted to lactate through the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) [22]. Interestingly, NAD+ levels naturally decline with age; by middle age, NAD+ levels have decreased to roughly half of adolescent levels causing diminished sirtuin and PARP activity [16, 18]. In rodent studies NAD+ decline was not limited to just the cytosolic cell fraction, but also equally decreased in nuclear and mitochondrial fractions [23]. There is conflicting evidence about NADH levels during aging, most human studies show NADH level staying the same or increasing with age [24]. Recent studies have demonstrated that treating older mice with PARP inhibitors or NAD+ precursors can significantly improve health outcomes. These benefits include enhanced insulin sensitivity, reversal of mitochondrial dysfunction, decreased stem cell aging, and increased lifespan [19–22].

In critically ill patients, metabolic demand often outpaces supply, and NADH accumulation can indicate impaired mitochondrial function. The disruption of the delicate balance between NAD+ and NADH in critical illness can have deleterious consequences for cellular metabolism from aerobic respiration to anaerobic glycolysis, leading to lactate production and metabolic acidosis. Depletion of NAD+, for example, is associated with inhibition of the glycolytic pathway [25]. Oxidative stress, prevalent in critical illness [26] can further disturb the NAD+/NADH balance. Excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) leads to consumption of NADH in attempts to restore redox homeostasis [27]. Elevated levels of NADH may serve as an indicator of mitochondrial distress, a common occurrence in the pathophysiology of critically ill patients, signaling a shift toward anaerobic metabolism [28–31]. Supplementation with NAD+ precursors have been explored as a therapeutic strategy to boost NAD+ levels, in the context of critical care, such as sepsis, acute lung injury, multi-trauma, and traumatic brain injury. Some NAD+ precursors that have been studied or theorized for use in such conditions. Nicotinamide Riboside (NR) is a highly efficient precursor to NAD+ that can be orally administered [32–34]. In critical illness, the theoretical application of NR would be to support mitochondrial function, enhance ATP production, and reduce oxidative stress, thereby potentially improving outcomes in conditions like sepsis or TBI [35, 36]. Nicotinamide Mononucleotide (NMN) is another precursor to NAD+ that has gained attention for its role in aging and longevity research. It has been shown to improve NAD+ biosynthesis and has potential benefits in cardiovascular health, neurodegeneration, and metabolic disorders [37, 38]. Like NR, NMN could theoretically benefit critically ill patients by boosting NAD+ levels, supporting cellular energy metabolism, and mitigating oxidative damage, though direct evidence in critical care settings is lacking. Nicotinic Acid (Niacin, Vitamin B3) serves as an NAD+ precursor. It has been used to treat dyslipidemia due to its ability to lower triglycerides and raise HDL cholesterol. Its role in increasing NAD+ levels suggests potential applications in critical care, particularly in managing energy metabolism and reducing inflammation. However, its use can be limited by flushing and other side effects. Nicotinamide (NAM), another form of vitamin B3, can be converted to NAD+ without causing the flushing associated with niacin. It has been studied for its protective effects on cells and is involved in DNA repair and sirtuin activation. In critical care, nicotinamide could theoretically help in reducing oxidative stress and supporting cellular repair mechanisms. Its use in sepsis and other critical conditions would aim at enhancing cellular resilience and function. Clinical trials and evidence investigating the efficacy of NAD+ precursors in critical care settings are sparse or in the early stages [33, 39–41]. Most of the evidence comes from animal models, preclinical studies, aging research, and investigations into metabolic and neurodegenerative diseases. However, clinical studies are needed to confirm these effects and determine the optimal dosing, timing, and patient selection criteria. An additional area or consideration is that NAD+ precursor supplementation will likely increase the total pyrimidine nucleotide pool, not only NAD+. While NAD+ precursors hold promise for improving outcomes in critical illness, the clinical evidence in this specific context is still emerging. Future research and clinical trials are essential to fully understand the potential benefits, safety, and practical applications of NAD+ supplementation in critically ill patients.

Nitric oxide: a double-edged sword in systemic inflammation and hemodynamics.

NO is synthesized from L-arginine by nitric oxide synthases (NOS) and plays versatile roles, from vasodilation through the activation of guanylate cyclase and formation of cyclic GMP from GTP as shown in Fig. 1. There are three isoforms of NOS: neuronal NOS (nNOS or NOS1), inducible NOS (iNOS or NOS2), and endothelial NOS (eNOS or NOS3). Each isoform has a slightly different structure and function but shares some common features, such as a heme group, L-arginine binding site, BH4 (tetrahydrobiopterin) binding site, reductase domain, and, in some cases, hemoglobin alpha subunit binding. These shared features are essential for the enzyme catalytic activity and their role in various physiological and pathological processes [44].

NO also modulates inflammatory responses. It is involved in various stages of inflammation, from initiation to resolution, and can have both pro- and anti-inflammatory effects depending on its concentration and the context of its production [45–48]. Additionally, NO is involved in platelet activation and aggregation, a key aspect of the hemostatic process. By regulating platelet function, NO plays a crucial role in preventing thrombosis and maintaining blood flow [49–51].

In conditions such as sepsis and other critical illnesses, NOS expression can become dysregulated, leading to either excessive or insufficient NO production. Both scenarios can have deleterious effects on vascular function and inflammatory responses, contributing to the complexity of managing such conditions [52, 53]. While NO-mediated vasodilation is crucial for tissue perfusion, excessive NO production can contribute to hypotension and impaired tissue oxygenation. Conversely, insufficient NO can lead to microvascular dysfunction and impaired blood flow. Modulating NO pathways offers therapeutic potential but requires a delicate balance to avoid exacerbating the patient’s condition.

NO donors represent a class of compounds that release NO or its analogues, facilitating various physiological effects, particularly vasodilation. These agents have been explored in clinical settings for their potential benefits in treating conditions characterized by impaired NO synthesis or action, such as cardiovascular diseases, erectile dysfunction, and critical illnesses including sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and pulmonary hypertension. NO donors have been studied in the context of sepsis, where both hypo- and hyper-production of NO can occur. In septic shock, NO donors might help to restore vascular tone and improve perfusion [54, 55]. However, their use remains controversial due to concerns about exacerbating hypotension and the potential for increasing mortality. The balance between beneficial vasodilatory and potential hypotensive effects is critical. In ARDS, inhaled NO (iNO) has been the most studied form of NO therapy [54, 56]. iNO can selectively dilate pulmonary vessels in well-ventilated areas of the lung, improving ventilation-perfusion matching and oxygenation. While iNO can improve oxygenation temporarily, its impact on mortality and long-term outcomes in ARDS remains uncertain. NO donors, particularly iNO, are used to manage pulmonary hypertension, including in the context of critical illness [57]. iNO can reduce pulmonary artery pressure and improve right ventricular function without causing systemic hypotension [54]. R Di Fenza et al., reported that in patients with respiratory failure due to SARS-Cov-2 treatment with high-dose of iNO for 48 hours increased arterial oxygenation (PaO/FiO2), and reduced viral load. Although iNO did not reduce mortality, or length of mechanical ventilation [58]. S-Nitrosothiols (e.g., S-Nitrosoglutathione) are a group of NO donors that release NO in a more controlled manner. S-Nitrosoglutathione, for example, has been studied for its potential in various diseases due to its ability to mimic endogenous NO signaling [59–62]. Theoretical and preclinical studies suggest potential applications in conditions like cystic fibrosis [63, 64], hypertension [65], and perhaps critical illnesses although clinical evidence is still emerging [59, 61, 66, 67]. Diazeniumdiolates (NONOates) are a class of NO donors that release NO at physiological pH and temperature, offering potential for more precise NO delivery [68]. They have been studied in various preclinical models of disease. While primarily in the research phase, their controlled NO release characteristics make them of interest for potential future applications in critical care, especially where localized NO delivery is beneficial [69]. L-arginine is the substrate for endogenous NO synthesis by NOS enzymes. Supplementation with L-arginine can enhance NO production in conditions where NO synthesis is impaired [70]. Supplementation has been explored in sepsis, hypertension [71–74], and erectile dysfunction [75]. However, results in critical care settings have been mixed, and its effectiveness remains under investigation [76].

HIF1α is a transcription factor that plays a key role in responding to hypoxia. Under normal oxygen levels (normoxia), HIF1α is continuously synthesized, hydroxylated by prolyl hydroxylase and degraded by the proteasome as shown in Fig. 2. Hypoxia-Response Element (HRE) is a specific DNA sequence found in the promoter region of target genes for HIF1. The consensus sequence for HRE is 5’-RCGTG-3’ (R is a generic purine nucleic acid place holder and can be either Adenine or Guanine). This sequence is recognized and bound by the HIF1 hetero dimer transcription factor. When oxygen levels are low, HIF1α stabilizes and translocate to the nucleus. In the nucleus, HIF1α forms a heterodimer with HIF1β (also known as ARNT, Aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator) to create the active HIF1 transcription factor.

The HIF-1 transcription factor binds to HREs located in the promoters of target genes. This binding enhances the transcription of genes involved in various adaptive responses to hypoxia, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), erythropoietin (EPO), and glycolytic enzymes like glucose transporters (e.g., GLUT1) and pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1) and others as shown in Fig. 3. HIF1α activation allows cells to adapt to hypoxic conditions, which is protective in the short term. However, prolonged activation can have detrimental effects, such as promoting anaerobic metabolism and contributing to acidosis. HIF1α pathway manipulation has implications for modulating immune responses, promoting tissue repair, and improving outcomes in critical illness [79–81].

The role of HIF1α has been increasingly recognized as pivotal, especially in conditions like sepsis, acute lung injury, acute kidney injury and COVID-19. A series of recent studies have shed light on various aspects of HIF1α, ranging from its potential as a biomarker to its implications in metabolic and immune responses in critically ill patients.

The significance of HIF1α as a biomarker in sepsis patients was reported by Tong Z. et al. This study explored the efficacy of serum HIF1α levels as a biomarker in sepsis patients, revealing that HIF1α concentrations were significantly elevated in patients with sepsis compared to ICU controls and showed superior diagnostic accuracy over traditional indicators. It concluded that serum HIF1α levels are valuable for diagnosing sepsis and forecasting patient outcomes [82]. Similarly, Lotsios NS et al. evaluated the prognostic value of HIF1α-induced genes and heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1) in sepsis and septic shock, further emphasizing its role in the progression and outcome of these conditions [83].

The role of HIF1α related to COVID-19 was explored by Yucel K et al., who analyzed HIF1α levels along with ischemia-modified albumin (IMA) in ICU COVID-19 patients. The HIF1α and IMA levels were significantly higher among COVID-19 patients in ICU compared with healthy controls and suggested that they could be used as reliable markers for the prognosis of COVID-19 [84]. Hypoxia also significantly stabilizes HIF1α in tissue-resident alveolar macrophages (TR-AM), leading to increased glycolytic activity. This stabilization results in changes to TR-AM metabolite profiles, cytokine output, and reduced sensitivity to mitochondrial function inhibition. The hypoxia-induced alterations in TR-AMs could be replicated by FG-4592, a stabilizer of HIF1α [85].

It was also shown that the inhibition of glycolysis contributed to neutrophil immunosuppression during sepsis which might be controlled by the PI3K/Akt-HIF1α axis-mediated lactate dehydrogenase A downregulation, thus providing additional evidence into the metabolic and immune responses in septic patients [86]. Evans CE, reviewed HIF dependent mechanisms in lung injury and repair, suggesting the need for targeted therapies considering HIF specificity and the challenges of off-target effects and optimal delivery methods for treating inflammatory lung conditions [87]. Further, Williams NC et al., investigated the role of L-2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG) in activating HIF1α in macrophages and found that the abundance of both enantiomers of 2-HG is increased in LPS-activated macrophages. They demonstrated that L-2HG, but not D-2HG, can promote the expression of the proinflammatory cytokine IL-1β and the adoption of an inflammatory, highly glycolytic metabolic state. These changes were likely mediated through activation of HIF1α by L-2HG, a known inhibitor of the HIF PHD. Their findings highlight the metabolic-immune crosstalk in critical illness scenarios like sepsis [88]. Slingo ME et al. reviewed oxygen sensing, anesthesia, and critical care, offering a comprehensive overview of the relevance of HIF1α in these settings [89]. Hirota K et al. addressed hypoxia-dependent signaling in critical care medicine, emphasizing the significance of HIF1α in response to injury and hypoxia, common in perioperative and critical care environments [90]. Lastly, Li LL et al. explored how monocytes undergo functional reprogramming through the HIF1α signaling pathway to generate immunosuppression in the late phase of sepsis, describing another aspect of HIF1α’s role in immune modulation [91].

These studies collectively point to the multifaceted role of HIF1α in critical care, underlining its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target. They provide new insights into the management of critically ill patients, paving the way for more targeted and effective interventions.

Interplay of NADH, NO, and HIF1α.

The relationship between NADH, NO, and HIF1α is multifaceted, involving direct and indirect feedback loops. For instance, HIF1α can induce the expression of NOS, while NO can influence mitochondrial respiration and hence NADH levels. In response to stress and injury, these molecules can activate compensatory pathways that attempt to restore homeostasis but may lead to pathologic states if dysregulated or excessive.

The interplay among these molecules can significantly influence disease progression, organ dysfunction, and patient outcomes in the critical care setting. Monitoring the levels and activity of NADH, NO, and HIF1α could provide valuable insights into disease states, serving as potential biomarkers for prognosis and treatment responses [92, 93]. NO, for example, can interact/bind with iron [94]. Iron, primarily in the ferrous state (Fe2+) is crucial for many biological processes, including oxygen transport and electron transfer reactions [95]. The availability of iron is important for the proper functioning of HIF1α, as iron is a cofactor for prolyl hydroxylase (PHD) enzyme that hydroxylate HIF1α, targeting it for degradation. Thus, iron availability can influence the stability of HIF1α. Through its requirement for both oxygen and iron, PHD act as a sensor for cellular oxygen and iron levels [96]. When oxygen is abundant, PHD use it to hydroxylate HIF1α. However, in conditions of iron deficiency [97, 98] or when iron is in the ferric (Fe+3) state and thus unavailable for PHD binding, PHD loses its activity. This results in reduced hydroxylation of HIF1α and its accumulation, leading to the activation of hypoxia-responsive genes even in the presence of normal oxygen levels (Warburg effect). Iron chelators, which bind to Fe+2 and remove it from the PHD active site, can inhibit PHD activity. This inhibition can mimic hypoxic conditions by preventing the degradation of HIF1α even under normoxic conditions [99–101]. NO can also directly regulate HIF1α through a reversible post-translational modification in which NO is covalently attached to the thiol group of a cysteine amino acid in HIF1α. [102]. Depending on which cysteine residue is S-nitrosylated, this can either enhance its role as a transcription factor or prevent hydroxylation by PHDs allowing HIF1α to stabilize and accumulate in cells even during normoxia [102–105]. Clinically, elevated HIF1α levels predict poor outcomes in many cancer types and is associated with increased proliferation and metastasis of cancer cells [106–108].

NO can also bind to iron-sulfur (Fe-S), and iron-copper (Fe-Cu) clusters present in a variety of enzymes and proteins. This binding can disrupt the normal function of these proteins, leading to alterations in cellular metabolism and signaling pathways [104]. In mitochondrial respiration, excess levels of NO can reversibly and irreversibly inhibit metalloproteins of the electron transport chain (ETC) through direct binding of NO. Inhibition of any of the metalloproteins in the ETC can lead to build-up of reducing components including NADH which alters the rate of O2 consumption and ATP production [109–112].

NAD+, SIRT1, HIF-1α, and NO crosstalk and the ETC feedback loop. Cellular NAD+ levels tightly control SIRT1 deacetylase activity [113, 114]. Under oxidative/nitrosative stress, NO and RNS/ROS reversibly (and sometimes irreversibly) inhibit mitochondrial complexes (notably I and IV), slowing NADH oxidation, raising the NADH/NAD+ ratio, and reducing SIRT1 activity [114–116]. Diminished SIRT1 favors a more acetylated and transcriptionally permissive HIF-1α, reinforcing glycolytic reprogramming and lactate production [117, 118]. HIF-1α also upregulates NOS isoforms, increasing NO generation and further constraining PHD-dependent HIF-1α hydroxylation [119, 120]. In parallel, NO donors (e.g., S-nitrosoglutathione) inhibit PHDs and stabilize HIF-1α in normoxia, as shown by Metzen et al. [121, 122]. Conversely, augmenting NAD+ (e.g., with NR or NMN) can reactivate SIRT1, deacetylate HIF-1α (and co-regulators such as PGC-1α), promote mitochondrial biogenesis and electron-transport capacity, restore NADH oxidation, and thereby break the NO/ETC/HIF-1α feed-forward loop [123–125].

While this review provides insights into the biochemical and molecular aspects of critical illness, translating these findings from basic research to clinical practice is fraught with significant challenges. The multifaceted roles of NADH, NO, and HIF1α present difficulties in designing interventions that can achieve the desired therapeutic balance in critically ill patients. Furthermore, interventions targeting these fundamental cellular pathways carry a significant risk of unintended side effects. For example, while NAD+ precursor supplementation may support mitochondrial function, its indiscriminate use could potentially worsen oxidative injury or immune dysfunction. Similarly, modulating NO pathways requires a delicate balance; NO donors could improve perfusion but also risk severe hypotension, while L-arginine supplementation has shown mixed results in critical care settings. Agents that stabilize HIF1α might aid tissue adaptation to hypoxia, but prolonged activation can promote harmful anaerobic metabolism and acidosis. The successful clinical application of these interventions is therefore highly dependent on timing, context, and the specific molecular profile of the patient.

Given these complexities, there is a clear and urgent need for high-quality, prospective, randomized clinical trials to validate these therapeutic strategies in critically ill populations. Such trials are essential to determine optimal dosing, timing, and patient selection criteria, and to confirm the safety and efficacy of these novel approaches. However, designing and conducting these trials involves its own risks and uncertainties, including the challenge of identifying reliable biomarkers to guide therapy and monitor patient response. Without robust clinical evidence, the therapeutic potential of modulating NADH, NO, and HIF1α pathways remains largely hypothetical. This review is far from exhaustive, as the details and intricacies involved are beyond the scope of this brief analysis.

Conclusion

The intricate relationships among NADH, NO, and HIF1α contribute significantly to the pathophysiology of critical illness. Understanding these interactions offers potential for the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. From a patient safety perspective, therapeutically modulating NAD+ levels, NO signaling, and HIF1α stabilization in a time-sensitive and context-specific manner may reduce progression to multi-organ dysfunction. This hypothesis supports the design of biomarker-driven protocols that recognize distinct molecular phenotypes and enable precision-targeted interventions.

Redox Imbalance (NAD+/NADH): Targeted NAD+ precursor therapy (e.g., NMN, NR) may support mitochondrial function and reduce reductive stress. However, indiscriminate use could worsen oxidative injury or immune dysfunction. Safety requires phase-specific dosing, likely in early metabolic decompensation phases.

Nitric Oxide Dynamics: While NO donors or inhaled NO can support perfusion and gas exchange, especially in sepsis or ARDS, inappropriate administration risks hypotension, impaired oxygen delivery, or paradoxical immunosuppression. A predictive model of NO bioavailability may be essential for safe use.

HIF1α Stabilization: Agents that stabilize HIF1α (e.g., prolyl hydroxylase inhibitors) could aid tissue adaptation in hypoxic or ischemic states but carry potential risks of promoting anaerobic metabolism, lactate accumulation, or immunoparalysis in prolonged critical illness.

A molecularly guided therapeutic approach prioritizing timing, personalization, and patient safety may unlock new preventive strategies in critical care. Future research should focus on personalized approaches that consider the unique molecular patterns of each patient, potentially allowing for more precise and effective treatments.
