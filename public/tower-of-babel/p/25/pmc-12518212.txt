Identification of biomarkers in gynecologic cancers: a machine learning approach for metabolomics
Authors: SangHun Cha, Sang-Min Lee, Kyeong Eun Lee, Min-Gul Kim, Sun-Young Lee, Kwang-Hee Shin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to identify the clinical metabolic markers associated with gynecologic cancer (GC) by comparing machine learning (ML) algorithms with orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). Untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed on plasma from 42 patients with GC (24 cervical cancer [CC], 9 endometrial cancer [EC], and 9 ovarian cancer [OC]) and 57 healthy female participants. GC and healthy control groups were classified using OPLS-DA and 8 ML algorithms. The ML algorithm with the best classification performance was used to assess CC, EC, and OC with healthy subjects, and metabolite candidates involved in each GC were selected. Upon comparing the classification model performance between the GC and control groups, random forest (RF) model displayed the best performance with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.9999. The multi-classification RF model was established to distinguish all 4 groups and was achieved an AUC of 0.8351. The AUCs of the 3 GC subgroup assessment RF models comparing patients with CC, EC, and OC with healthy subjects were 0.9838, 0.7500, and 0.7321, respectively. Plasma concentrations of 2 identified metabolites significantly increased in patients with GCs. Several ML algorithms were used to distinguish GC, showed better performance than conventional OPLS-DA. Proline betaine and lysophosphatidyl ethanolamine (18:0/0:0) selected in RF models were suggested as metabolite candidates associated with GCs.Keywords: Machine Learning, Metabolomics, Biomarker, Gynecology, Cancer

INTRODUCTION

Gynecologic cancer (GC) refers to cancers that occur in the female reproductive organs, with cervical cancer (CC), endometrial cancer (EC), and ovarian cancer (OC) being the most representative. All of these originate in the Mullerian duct, are commonly found within the female reproductive system and are affected by estrogen. CC is generally caused by the human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, and risk factors include smoking and hormonal drugs. Risk factors of EC include early and late menopause, obesity, exogenous estrogen, and breast cancer history. The exact cause of OC is not yet known; however, risk factors include aging, family history, and female hormones.

While preventive screening methods such as HPV testing and Pap smears have significantly improved early detection rates for CC [1], effective diagnostic tools for EC and OC remain limited, often resulting in late-stage diagnosis and poor prognosis [2,3]. There is a growing need for reliable, non-invasive biomarkers to enable early detection and improve clinical outcomes in GC. Metabolomics, which enables the comprehensive profiling of endogenous small molecules in biological samples, has emerged as a promising approach for biomarker discovery [4]. By reflecting alterations in metabolic pathways associated with cancer development, metabolomic signatures may provide valuable diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic insights for GC.

Multivariate statistical analyses, such as partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) and orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), are widely used in metabolomics [5,6]. The PLS-DA method projects multivariate data into smaller latent variables before regressing them on the dependent variable. OPLS-DA is an improved PLS-DA used to classify 2 or more groups in multivariate data [7,8]. All samples in OPLS-DA model are projected onto a single principal component to estimate predictive ability, and the variable importance in projection (VIP) can be used to select variables [6]. However, since PLS-DA and OPLS-DA are essentially linear algorithms [9,10], accurate classification and evaluation of predictive ability may be difficult in high-dimensional data with many variables and complexes, such as metabolomic data.

Various machine learning (ML) algorithms have recently been applied in metabolomics studies [11,12]. They can be applied to predict accuracy, generalize performance, and even surpass PLS-DA or OPLS-DA. Random forest (RF) and support vector machine (SVM) have achieved better performance than PLS-DA or OPLS-DA in the predictive ability of classification on various metabolomic datasets [9,13]. Previous studies have compared the classification performance of metabolomics data using 6 ML and deep learning (DL) algorithms. Among them, DL models displayed the highest performance with an area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.93, while RF, SVM, and gradient boosting machine models exhibited AUCs of 0.893, 0.891, and 0.89, respectively [14]. However, DL models often require large, well-annotated datasets and lack sufficient interpretability, which remains a significant barrier for clinical biomarker research [15].

In recent years, GC metabolomics studies applying ML techniques have been reported [16,17], but metabolomics studies of simultaneously profiled multiple GCs have rarely been reported using ML. This study aimed to identify the clinical metabolic markers associated with GC by comparing ML algorithms with OPLS-DA.

METHODS

Study participants and sample collection

The overall workflow of the present study is presented in Fig. 1. A total of 99 subjects participated in the study, including 42 patients with GC (24 CC, 9 EC, and 9 OC) and 57 healthy subjects. All plasma samples of patients were collected before receiving chemotherapy. Plasma samples obtained after centrifugation were subjected to protein precipitation for sample preparation. To 50 µL of plasma, 4 volumes of 100% acetonitrile were added, followed by mixing for 5 minutes using a vortex mixer. The mixture was then centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C. Subsequently, 200 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a vial and used for analysis. The clinical study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Jeonbuk National University Hospital (approval no. CHU2017-04-018 and CHU2017-04-022). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Informed consent was obtained from all participants after providing them with sufficient information prior to their inclusion in the study.

Ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HRMS) analysis

The untargeted metabolomic analysis of the plasma samples was performed by UHPLC-HRMS using Ultimate 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) coupled with Q ExactiveTM Focus Hybrid Quadrupole-OrbitrapTM mass spectrometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in electrospray ionization in positive and negative modes located at Mass Spectrometry Convergence Research Center [18].

OPLS-DA and ML algorithms

OPLS-DA

OPLS-DA projects variables into the first principal component that contributes most to classifying groups and then summarizes them into several orthogonal components that explain the variability within the groups [5,6]. The OPLS-DA model was built with the “NIPALS” R package (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and fitted using one principal component and several orthogonal components. Unlike ML models based on AUC, tuning parameter optimization was performed based on “R2Y” and “Q2Y” values. R2Y and Q2Y are indicators that evaluate the model’s suitability and prediction score, respectively. The optimal number of orthogonal components was selected as those with R2Y < 0.01 and Q2Y < 0.05, minus one.

Lasso logistic regression (Lasso LR)

Lasso LR is a feature selection method that applies lasso penalty to the logistic regression algorithm. For high-dimensional data, the training data for model fitting had more input variables than the number of subjects. Therefore, standard logistic regression model (Logπi1−πi=β0+βAAGEi+βBBody Mass Index [BMI]i+β1Z1+β2Z2+...+β643Z643) would overfit the training data, while the Lasso LR is able to perform effectively [19]. Lasso LR performs variable (feature) selection at the same time as estimating the regression coefficients (β0, βA, βB, β1, β2,..., β643) by applying the lasso penalty (λL(∑jβj), j=0, A, B, 1, 2, ...,643) [20,21]. λL in the lasso penalty is called the lasso shrinkage parameter and is used as a tuning parameter in ML. The algorithm analysis was performed using the “glmnet” R package.

Bagging

Bagging is a decision tree-based ensemble method that generates multiple resampled training data by sampling subjects with replacement from training data, creates a decision tree from each resampled training data, and classifies the groups by combining decision trees through majority votes [22]. The tuning parameters used in this model are the minimum number of subjects included in the final node (“minbucket”) and the maximum number of times allowed to overlap input variables used when creating decision trees (“maxdepth”). Bagging algorithm analysis was performed using the R package “adabag” after fitting 50 decision trees embedded in the “rpart” package.

RF

RF is a decision tree-based ensemble method, and the fitting process is the same as bagging. However, RF uses randomly selected input variables when fitting each decision tree and can make predictions based on the results obtained for each tree. This approach can reduce prediction errors, and the fitting speed is relatively higher than bagging and displays stable classification performance [23,24]. The tuning parameters were the same as those of bagging and additionally utilized the number of input variables (“mtry”). RF algorithm analysis was performed using 50 decision trees embedded in the “randomForest” R package.

Boosting

Boosting is an ensemble method that uses decision trees such as bagging and RF algorithms but has a different fitting process. Based on previous model fitting results, performance is continuously improved in the next model fitting [25]. Among diverse boosting algorithms, AdaBoost (for Adaptive Boosting) was used in this study. It updates the decision trees while correcting the misclassification rate by assigning weights to observed values [26]. The tuning parameters and R packages applied to this model were the same as those for bagging.

SVM with 4 kernels

SVM is an algorithm that finds a hyperplane in a high-dimensional or infinite-dimensional space and uses it to perform classification [27]. The hyperplane that classifies data well into clusters has the greatest distance (margin) to the nearest training data. Using the hyperplane with the largest margin results in the lowest misclassification of new data. In addition to linear classification, SVM efficiently performs non-linear classification by utilizing a mapping technique in high-dimensional space called the kernel trick. Four kernels of SVM were utilized in this study. All SVM algorithms were fitted using the “e1071” R package.

Linear kernel SVM (linear SVM) used “cost” as a tuning parameter, which is utilized in all kernel functions of the SVM algorithm. “Cost” specifies how much the SVM model will pay if it draws a line that incorrectly classifies datasets. Then, the SVM model finds another line that minimizes the cost incurred by misclassifying the data. The SVM model can control the overfitting degree using “cost.” Polynomial kernel SVM (polynomial SVM) is often used in image processing. In this study, “cost” and “degree” were utilized as tuning parameters. “Degree” is a parameter of the polynomial kernel, Kxi,xj=xiTxjd, where d is the value of “degree.” Gaussian radial basis function (RBF) kernel SVM (Gaussian RBF SVM) is used when no prior information about the data is available. Here, “cost” and “gamma” were utilized as tuning parameters. “Gamma” is required for the Gaussian RBF kernel, Kxi,xj=e-γxi-xj2, where γ is the value of “gamma,” and the default setting is 1 divided by data dimension. Sigmoid kernel SVM (sigmoid SVM) was mainly used as a proxy for neural networks. The tuning parameters of the sigmoid SVM model were the same as the RBF SVM model. The sigmoid kernel was Kxi,xj=tanhγxiTxj, where γ is the value of “gamma.”

Strategies for developing classification models

ML models were established for identifying metabolic features associated with CC, EC, and OC in 2 major steps: (1) GC and healthy subjects classification and (2) GC subgroup assessments. The first step of the ML model was built using OPLS-DA and 8 ML algorithms to distinguish patients with GC from healthy subjects. The performance of classification models were compared using various indicators, and the best algorithm was selected for the next step. In the second step, a multi-classification ML model based on the best algorithm was constructed by comparing 4 groups: 3 GC subgroups and the healthy subjects. Then, the constructed multi-classification model was applied to distinguish patients with CC, EC, and OC from healthy subjects to establish 3 GC subgroup assessment models. Metabolite candidates related to CC, EC, and OC were selected from the GC subgroup assessment models.

Data preprocessing

For the dataset obtained from UHPLC-HRMS, data normalization was conducted through log transformation [28] and autoscaling [28,29]. Then, 70% of the 99 participants were used to extract training data by sampling without replacement. The training data comprised 33 patients with GC and 37 healthy subjects. The 29 subjects of the remaining 30% constituted the test data and included 9 patients with GC and 20 healthy subjects. Data partitioning was performed using the SPlit() function embedded in the R package “SPlit” [30].

Model training and evaluation

The training and evaluation process of the ML model is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 1. The training data was divided into 5 folds to find the model with the highest AUC value within the tuning parameter ranges set in each algorithm. The AUC value of the receiver operating characteristics (ROCs) curve was obtained using the probability of being predicted as a GC group yielded from one 5-fold cross-validation (CV). One thousand repeated 5-fold CVs were performed, and the average value of the calculated 1,000 AUCs was defined as “Repeated CV AUC.” Finally, the model with the highest “Repeated CV AUC” within the set tuning parameter ranges was determined as optimal for each algorithm.

The ML model performance was assessed with 7 indicators and 95% confidence interval (CI). Evaluating the ML model fitted from the original training data created in one split within the entire dataset by applying it to the original test data was judged unreliable. Therefore, bootstrap resampling was applied to the training and test data.

Feature selection

Feature selection was performed based on each ML model to select input variables that contributed to distinguishing the GC and healthy control compared in the classification models. Features were selected based on each original ML model fitted from the original training data. In Lasso LR model, the Z values were obtained by dividing the regression coefficients of selected features from the original Lasso LR model by the standard deviation of their regression coefficients estimated by 1,000 bootstrap Lasso LR models. Then, the p-values were calculated using the standard normal distribution, and the final selection was made based on p < 0.1. Bagging, RF, and boosting models, decision tree-based ensemble methods, selected the top 20 input variables with high “MeanDecreaseGini” values. The “MeanDecreaseGini” values were measured as scores to indicate the degree to which each input variable contributed to decision tree division in the process of classifying the groups. The SVM model selected the top 20 input variables with high “Importance” values. The “Importance” values (“varImp” function in R package “caret”) were presented by measuring the scores each input variable contributed to calculating the AUC values of SVM models. The feature selection method was the same for all kernels of the SVM model. In OPLS-DA, the VIP values by a principal component and optimal orthogonal components were defined as VIPpred and VIPortho, respectively. The OPLS-DA model calculated the approximated VIP value, VIPtotal, by dividing VIPortho by VIPpred. The top 20 VIPtotal values were finally selected [31]. Endogenous metabolites were finally selected from the provided features.

Using the selected RF algorithm, classification models were constructed to distinguish among GC and healthy control. These included a multiclass classification model as well as binary classification models for each cancer type (i.e., cervical vs. control, endometrial vs. control, ovarian vs. control). From each model, the top 20 most important input variables (metabolite features), based on variable importance scores, were selected as key contributors to classification performance. Among these, endogenous metabolites were further identified through metabolite annotation and verification procedures, and finally selected.

Identification and quantification

Candidate endogenous metabolites were selected based on a match score ≥ 80 between mass spectrometry (MS) spectra from the experimental plasma samples and reference spectra in the MS spectrum library. Metabolite identification was subsequently performed using authentic standards. The standards were diluted to a concentration of 100 µg/mL and analyzed under the same conditions as the study samples using parallel reaction monitoring mode. Fragmentation patterns of the standards were compared with those of the study samples to confirm structural identity. For further verification, the fragmentation patterns were cross-checked against those in mzCloud and the Human Metabolome Database.

Quantitative analysis of the finally selected metabolites was conducted using UHPLC-HRMS. Thioctic acid-d5 was used as the internal standard for electrospray ionization (ESI)− mode, and donepezil-d4 was used for ESI+ mode. Both were added to preprocessed plasma samples to achieve a final concentration of 10 µg/mL, and the analysis was performed under the same conditions as those used for untargeted metabolomics. The plasma concentrations of each metabolite were calculated based on the ratio of the peak area of the metabolite to that of the corresponding internal standard.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate whether the plasma concentrations of the final selected metabolite candidates differed significantly between comparison groups, statistical analyses were performed. Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For each of the 3 GC, comparisons with the healthy control group were conducted using the t-test when the data were normally distributed, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test when the normality assumption was not satisfied. For comparisons among 4 groups, one-way analysis of variance was used for normally distributed data, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed for non-normally distributed data. All graphing and statistical analyses of metabolite candidates were performed using Graphpad Prism 6.0 software (Graphpad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

The final performance of ML models was generally evaluated using various indicators and their 95% CIs. The RF model (AUC, 0.999997) displayed the best performance among the 9 ML models, followed by the linear SVM (AUC, 0.9992), quadratic SVM (AUC, 0.9944), and boosting (AUC, 0.9942) models. In terms of all evaluation indicators, the 4 ML models generally had similar or better performance than the existing method, OPLS-DA (AUC, 0.9819). Therefore, the RF algorithm was selected for GC subgroup assessments. The predictive ability of the ML models to classify patients with GC and healthy subjects is summarized in Table 2, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

GC subgroup assessments

A new multi-classification ML model was constructed to distinguish all 4 groups based on the selected RF algorithm. The range of the tuning parameter “mtry” was set to 1–645, which refers to the total number of input variables, and the multi-classification RF model was optimized by fitting 50 decision trees within that range. The optimal multi-classification RF model was determined when “mtry” = 155 (Supplementary Fig. 3). Applying the corresponding “mtry = 155,” 3 GC subgroup assessment RF models were finally established. The multi-classification RF model presented an AUC of 0.8351, and the 3 GC subgroup (CC, EC, and OC) assessment RF models achieved AUC values of 0.9838, 0.75, and 0.7321, respectively. DISCUSSION

A multi-step ML analysis was conducted to build classification models for metabolic biomarkers of 3 GCs. From the performance evaluation, ML models, such as RF (AUC, 0.999997), linear SVM (AUC, 0.9992), quadratic SVM (AUC, 0.9944), and boosting (AUC, 0.9942), displayed better predictive ability than OPLS-DA (AUC, 0.9819) in overall evaluation indicators. Based on the RF algorithm with the best performance, a multi-classification model was built by comparing 4 groups. Then, a multi-classification RF model was applied to distinguish 3 GCs from healthy subjects, and finally, 3 GC subgroup assessment RF models were established.

Proline betaine is a proline derivative and was significantly increased in the plasma of patients with CC and EC compared to healthy subjects in this study. Proline is the only proteogenic secondary amino acid in the human body, and recent studies have shown that proline metabolism affects tumor formation and metastasis through metabolic reprogramming [32]. Several studies have reported that plasma or tissue proline levels are higher in patients with cervical and EC than in the control group [33,34,35]. Elevated proline levels may be related to collagen synthesis. Prolyl 4-hydroxylases (P4Hs), an enzyme that plays a central role in the biosynthesis of collagen from proline [36], affect the progression of cancer, and abnormal collagen synthesis can lead to tumor invasion and metastasis. P4HA2, the α subunit of P4Hs, promotes glycolysis through upregulation of phosphoglycerate kinase 1 and lactate dehydrogenase A in CC, resulting in increased the proliferation and migration of cancer cells [37].

LPE (18:0/0:0) was identified as a biomarker candidate in the EC assessment RF model, and the plasma concentration was approximately 2.5 times higher in patients with EC than in healthy subjects. LPE is involved in glycerophospholipid (GP) metabolism, and a previous study reported that tissue phosphatidylglycerol (PG) levels were higher in patients with EC than in healthy subjects [38]. PG is a phospholipid belonging to the GP family. A previous study demonstrated that GP and PG levels were increased due to the overexpression of transcription factor EB (TFEB) and estrogen-related receptor α (ERRα) signaling, which regulate lipid metabolism in EC [39]. Moreover, TFEB-ERRα signaling can be a marker to predict the risk of EC metastasis. The overexpression of ERRα is involved in the invasion and metastasis of EC via induction of epithelial–mesenchymal transition [40], and TFEB regulates the overall transcription of genes associated with lipid catabolism and interacts with ERRα [41,42]. A previous study confirmed that phospholipase A2 (PLA2) expression was increased in EC tissue compared with that in normal tissue [43]. PLA2 is an enzyme that hydrolyzes GP, a major component of biological membranes, to produce lysophospholipids such as LPE [44].

Bootstrap resampling was applied to elaborately compare the predictive performance of all models. The clinical studies often involved few subjects or patients. The results may contain potential errors when evaluating the model’s performance utilizing those datasets by simply dividing training data and test data. We utilized 1,000 repeated 5-fold CVs in the model optimization process to build robust models and applied bootstrap resampling in the model evaluation to compensate for uncertainty [9]. The ML model evaluation results indicated that as the number of bootstrap training data increased, the AUC value tended to become relatively less variable and stable (Supplementary Fig. 5).

The strength of this study lies in its use of a ML approach to profile 3 GCs and its application of algorithms from varied origins. ML algorithms were compared with OPLS-DA to explore the applicability of ML techniques to metabolomics research. An appropriate method was selected by classifying patients in the large category of GCs. Then, RF models were established to classify all 4 groups in detailed categories to assess patients with CC, EC, and OC. In all ML models used in our study, age and BMI were added as input variables to correct distinction by considering clinical characteristics. The ML model performance and selected metabolites were obtained under the influence of clinical covariates.

The biased and small number of patients in CC (24), EC (9), and OC (9) is a limitation of this study. The healthy control group comprised 57 subjects; therefore, when compared with each cancer group, differences in subjects may lead to over- or underestimated results. Moreover, the developed model was not validated using an external cohort. Further validation using larger, independent cohorts is warranted to confirm the clinical utility of the identified biomarkers and to enhance the stability and predictive power of the proposed models. In addition, the large age difference between patients with GC (55.1) and healthy subjects (26.8) may explain the very high performance of ML models. To minimize the error in the results, we performed a multi-step ML analysis considering the biased number of subjects and estimated the ML model performance using various indicators.

In conclusion, this study presented a ML approach for the profiling of 3 GCs using ML algorithms from various origins and successfully provided RF models for patients with CC, EC, and OC. The effects of the identified potential biomarkers on specific GC metabolism mechanisms were explored. This approach could be applied to other metabolomics research and contribute to identifying the causes of GCs.
