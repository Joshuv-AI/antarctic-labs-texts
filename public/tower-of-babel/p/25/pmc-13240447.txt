Association between Alzheimer's/Parkinson's diseases and cancers: Modifying effects of alcohol consumption and viral infections revealed by an ecological study
Authors: Wenyu Ye, Ziqi Zhang, Sha Ye, Shifang Li, Wanyi Zheng, Jolaoluwa Grace Oparinde, Guang Ji, Michael Linnebacher, Lili Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAn inverse epidemiological association exists between Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases (AD/PD) and certain cancers. Using a sex‐stratified approach, this global study clarifies shared and distinct mechanisms underlying the complex AD/PD–cancer relationship.METHODSData from two World Health Organization (WHO) databases were analyzed for correlations between AD/PD and 23 major cancers, stratified by sex, region, and etiology.RESULTSAn inverse association was observed between AD/PD and most, especially gastrointestinal (GI), cancers. Conversely, virus‐related cancers including human papilloma virus (HPV)–driven cervical and hepatitis B virus (HBV)/hepatitis C virus (HCV)–related liver cancer (LC) were positively associated solely with PD, but not with AD. Sex‐specific analyses revealed distinct patterns: oesophagus cancer (OC) was positively correlated with AD/PD only in women. LC etiology showed opposing links to PD: HBV/HCV‐related positive and alcohol‐related inverse. Further analysis indicated alcohol exhibited contrasting roles: positive with GI cancers but inverse with AD/PD.DISCUSSIONWe provide novel epidemiological evidence on the complex interplay between neurodegeneration and cancers, suggesting shared biological pathways and modulators.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Related dementias (ADRD) and Parkinson's disease (PD) are leading neurodegenerative disorders. 1 , 2 Concurrently, cancer remains a critical health burden, with gastrointestinal (GI) cancers representing over 35% of all cancer cases. 3 Aging is a common key risk factor for both conditions. Despite shared risk factors like aging, smoking, metabolic conditions, and genetic susceptibility, 4 , 5 inverse epidemiological evidence exists between AD/PD and many cancers, potentially due to opposing pathways in cell death and proliferation. However, this association is not consistent across all cancer types and requires further investigation. 6 , 7 , 8 Notably, disease burden differs by sex; women exhibit a higher burden for AD/PD, whereas men face higher cancer incidence and mortality. 3 , 9 This suggests that sex‐specific biological and sociobehavioral factors may influence the AD/PD–cancer link, necessitating sex‐stratified research. 10 , 11

This ecological study of 183 countries analyzed correlations between AD/PD and 23 cancers, focusing on GI cancers and sex variations. The study aimed to explore the potential associations between AD/PD and various cancers, with a particular focus on GI cancers and potential effect modifiers, including viral infections and alcohol consumption, while also accounting for sex differences across populations.

METHODS

Data were obtained from the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Health Observatory (GHO) and the Global Cancer Observatory (GCO) databases, providing comprehensive health and cancer statistics. We extracted globally aggregated data for 2000, 2010, and 2019. 1 , 8 Detailed information is available in the supplementary materials.

RESULTS

Table S1 summarizes global mortality trends for 23 major cancers and AD/PD from 2000 to 2019. Overall, both diseases exhibited substantial increases in global mortality.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: Existing research on the Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases (AD/PD)‐cancer relationship reveals significant gaps. While an inverse correlation has been established, comprehensive global ecological analyses remain limited. Current literature lacks systematic sex‐stratified analyses and has not adequately explored the opposing associations of specific liver cancer etiologies or the dual role of modifiable risk factors such as alcohol across both diseases. Interpretation: Our findings fill a critical knowledge gap by providing the first global ecological evidence that the AD/PD ‐ 23 major cancers relationship is not uniformly inverse but is fundamentally modified by etiology, viral factors, and sex. This study uniquely demonstrates that alcohol exhibits opposing roles: protective in neurodegeneration but detrimental in GI cancers; while specific viral infections (e.g., HBV, HCV, HPV) emerge as distinct risk factors for PD. Future directions: Subsequent research should (1) investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying alcohol's opposing roles in neurodegeneration and carcinogenesis, particularly via metabolomic and transcriptomic profiling; (2) elucidate why specific viral infections (e.g., HPV, HBV, HCV) increase PD risk but not AD, focusing on viral pathways and host immune interactions; and (3) validate etiological subtyping in prospective cohorts for risk stratification and disease prevention.

Association between disease‐specific mortality in AD/PD and malignant cancers

Overall analysis revealed no significant correlation between total malignant cancers and AD (r = −0.042, p = 0.567) or PD (r = 0.061, p = 0.412; Figure 1A and 1B). However, cancer type–specific analyses demonstrated divergent patterns (Figure 1C and 1D).

Correlation analysis between disease‐specific mortality in AD/PD and malignant cancers. (A) Scatter plot showing the correlation between malignant cancers and AD. (B) Scatter plot showing the correlation between malignant cancers and PD. (C) Forest plot displaying the correlation of various types of malignant cancers with AD. (D) Forest plot displaying the correlation of various types of malignant cancers with PD.

AD exhibited significant inverse correlations with multiple GI cancers including stomach cancer (SC; r = −0.332), colorectal cancer (CRC; r = −0.191), alcohol‐related liver cancer (LC; r = −0.144), gallbladder and biliary tract cancer (GC; r = −0.304), and pancreatic cancer (PC; r = −0.222, all p < 0.05); as well as corpus uteri cancer, testicular cancer, larynx cancer, kidney cancer, and thyroid cancer. In contrast, a positive correlation was observed between AD and melanoma/skin cancer (Figure 1C).

PD was inversely associated with most cancers, particularly GI cancers: CRC (r = −0.391), GC (r = −0.292), and PC (r = −0.400), as well as tracheal/bronchial/lung, corpus uteri, testicular, kidney, brain/nervous system, larynx, mesothelioma, lymphomas and multiple myeloma, and leukemia. Conversely, PD showed positive correlations with LC (r = 0.265, p < 0.001), specifically hepatitis B virus (HBV)– (r = 0.407, p < 0.001) and hepatitis C virus (HCV)–related (r = 0.175, p = 0.018), and human papilloma virus (HPV)–related cervix uteri cancer (r = 0.210, p = 0.004; Figure 1D). To further validate the findings, correlations between AD/PD and GI cancers were analyzed using WHO GCO data on 5‐year prevalence, incidence, and mortality, yielding highly consistent results (Figure S1).

Gender‐stratified association analysis between AD and GI cancers

Given the documented sex disparities in neurodegenerative and GI cancers, female AD mortality was over twice that of males, whereas PD mortality was nearly equal between sexes. Conversely, most GI cancers showed higher male mortality, except GC which was 1.5‐fold higher in females (Figure S2A). Although mortality for both diseases exhibited a consistent age‐dependent increase and peaked in the American, European, and Western Pacific regions (Figure S2B and S2C). Thus, these marked disparities prompted a formal sex‐stratified analysis of AD/PD–GI cancer correlations.

Among males, a significant inverse correlation was observed between AD and total malignant cancers combined (r = −0.215, p = 0.004; Figure 2A). While for females, AD demonstrated no significant correlation with total malignant burden (r = −0.022, p = 0.762; Figure 2B). Notably, AD was negatively associated with specific GI cancers including SC, CRC, PC, and alcohol‐related LC (LC_alcohol, all p < 0.05), but no significant correlation was observed with HBV/HCV‐related LC or oesophagus cancer (OC) among males (Figure 2C, left). Nonetheless, consistent inverse associations were detected with the same spectrum of GI cancers as in males: SC, CRC, PC, GC, and LC_alcohol (all p < 0.05), again with no association with HBV/HCV‐related LC among females. In contrast, a positive correlation was observed with OC across all metrics for females (all p < 0.05). These cancer‐specific correlations were similarly evident across incidence, overall mortality, and prevalence metrics (Figure 2C , right).

Gender‐stratified association analysis between PD and GI cancers

Subsequent sex‐stratified analysis revealed distinct correlations between PD and GI cancers. In males, PD was inversely correlated with all malignant cancers combined (r = −0.161, p = 0.029; Figure 2D). Specifically, negative associations were observed with SC, CRC, PC, and GC (all p < 0.05, except for GC in incidence and 5‐year prevalence). A positive correlation of PD was identified only with LC‐specific mortality, particularly HBV/HCV‐related disease (all p < 0.05), but not with alcohol‐related LC (Figure 2F, left). In females, PD showed no significant association with overall malignancy (r = −0.129, p = 0.082; Figure 2E). Instead, a more complex pattern emerged: PD correlated negatively with CRC, PC, and GC (all p < 0.05, except for GC in overall mortality and prevalence), but positively with OC and LC across all four metrics (all p < 0.05). Further analysis of LC subtypes confirmed positive correlations with both HBV‐ related LC (r = 0.430, p < 0.001) and HCV‐related LC (r = 0.171, p = 0.021), but not with LC_alcohol. These cancer‐specific correlations were consistently observed across all metrics (Figure 2F , right).

Differential role of alcohol consumption in AD/PD and GI cancer mortality

Prompted by the divergent correlations of HBV‐, HCV‐, and alcohol‐related LC with AD/PD, we further evaluated alcohol consumption, a major modifiable factor, in this context. As presented in Figure S3, neurodegenerative diseases were consistently inversely correlated with alcohol exposure. In contrast, most GI cancers demonstrated positive associations with alcohol consumption. Collectively, alcohol consumption appears to play a different role in disease: a risk factor for GI cancers, but a protective factor for AD/PD progression.

DISCUSSION

This ecological study found a general inverse correlation between AD/PD and cancers, most pronounced for GI cancers. Key exceptions were observed: virus‐related cancers (HBV/HCV‐related liver cancer and cervix uteri cancer) showed positive correlations with PD. Liver cancer associations depended on etiology, with viral cases linked positively and alcohol‐related cases inversely to AD/PD. Additional analyses reinforced that alcohol consumption exhibits opposing relationships with these diseases. Significant sex‐specific variations were also noted. These findings provide new epidemiological evidence and point to shared, yet differentially modulated, biological pathways underlying neurodegeneration and cancer. 12

Our data demonstrate positive correlations of cervix uteri cancer and HBV‐/HCV‐related liver cancer with PD. Because over 95% of cervical cancers are caused by high‐risk HPV, viral pathogens may represent a shared risk factor for both cancer and neurodegeneration. Accumulating epidemiological evidence supports this hypothesis, 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 with several large‐scale reports indicating a modestly elevated PD risk following infections such as HBV and HCV. Mechanistically, viruses like HCV can cross the blood–brain barrier, infect brain immune cells, and contribute to dopaminergic neuron loss, which is a key feature of PD. 13 , 17 , 18 Other viruses may similarly promote neurodegeneration through neuroinflammation or direct damage. 19

In addition, our analysis revealed that alcohol consumption was positively associated with GI cancers while negatively correlated with AD/PD. This aligns with established evidence that alcohol consumption is a risk factor for all GI cancers. 20 The relationship with neurodegenerative diseases remains complex, with current evidence on alcohol and AD being conflicting, with studies reporting both protective and detrimental effects. These divergent outcomes appear to be modulated by multiple factors including dosage, drinking patterns, genetic predisposition, metabolic state, and duration of consumption. 21 In our data, total alcohol intake correlated more strongly with AD/PD than alcohol use disorders, supporting the hypothesis that moderate alcohol consumption may have protective effects against neurodegeneration.

As noted previously, 20 despite limitations such as variable data quality and diagnostic standards across countries, this global study provides the ecological evidence of an inverse mortality relationship between PD and most cancers, supporting the hypothesis of opposing biological mechanisms underlying these diseases.

Collectively, our findings establish a significant inverse correlation between AD/PD and GI cancers, moderated by sex‐specific factors. A key observation is the dual role of alcohol, which shows a protective association in neurodegeneration while remaining a risk factor for GI cancers. Notably, virus‐related cancers including HPV‐driven cervical cancer and HBV/HCV‐related LC, displayed a consistent positive association specifically with PD, highlighting a distinct pathogenic pathway not shared with AD. The results emphasize the necessity of disease subtyping by etiology and sex in future studies. From a translational perspective, this work provides a framework for understanding comorbidity risks and supports the development of stratified public health strategies that incorporate lifestyle factors and infection‐related cancer prevention, particularly in aging populations. Future studies are warranted to explore the underlying molecular mechanisms linking viral infections, alcohol exposure, neurodegeneration, and carcinogenesis, and to validate these epidemiological observations in longitudinal and mechanistic studies.
