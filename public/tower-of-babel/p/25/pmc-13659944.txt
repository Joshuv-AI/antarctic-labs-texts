Comparative analysis of the complete mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of Krascheninnikovia arborescens reveals its evolutionary features in Amaranthaceae
Authors: Mingliang Wang, Mengyue Wang, Jialu Liang, Yuying Han, Ruifeng Yang, Meilin Yang, Dongyi Ma, Jinglong Li, Chien-Hsun Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKrascheninnikovia arborescens is a drought-tolerant subshrub of the Amaranthaceae family that is endemic to China and plays an important role in desert ecosystems. However, little is known about the structure and evolutionary dynamics of its organellar genomes. In this study, we assembled and characterized the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of K. arborescens using long-read sequencing data.ResultsThe mitochondrial genome was assembled as a master circular genome representation of 387,891 bp and contains 62 annotated genes, whereas the chloroplast genome exhibits a typical quadripartite structure of 152,039 bp with 90 genes. The mitochondrial genome harbors abundant repetitive sequences and multiple plastid-derived insertions, indicating a dynamic structural organization. In contrast, gene content remains highly conserved, and all core protein-coding genes show signatures of purifying selection, particularly those involved in ATP synthesis and respiratory metabolism. Predicted RNA editing sites differ substantially between the two organelles, suggesting distinct post-transcriptional modification patterns. Phylogenetic analyses based on shared organellar genes consistently place K. arborescens within Amaranthaceae and support its evolutionary relationships within Caryophyllales. These results reveal a combination of structural dynamism and functional conservation in the organellar genomes of K. arborescens.ConclusionThis study provides a foundation for future comparative and evolutionary studies of Amaranthaceae and expands genomic resources for this family.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09545-z.

Introduction

Krascheninnikovia arborescens is a drought-tolerant subshrub of the Amaranthaceae family endemic to China and is widely distributed across arid and semi-arid regions of northern China [1, 2]. Its robust growth form and extensive root system contribute to its tolerance to drought, salinity, and nutrient-poor soils, enabling it to thrive under harsh environmental conditions. These traits have led to its widespread use in grassland restoration, while its high biomass and palatability also make it a valuable forage resource for livestock production [3, 4].

In recent years, studies on K. arborescens have mainly focused on its ecological distribution, as well as physiological and transcriptomic responses to abiotic stresses [4–7]. These studies have shown that stress conditions can affect photosynthesis, antioxidant systems, and hormone signaling pathways, highlighting complex regulatory networks associated with stress responses. However, such studies are largely limited to physiological and gene expression levels, and the underlying genomic basis, particularly at the organelle genome level, remains poorly understood. Organelle genomes, including those of mitochondria and chloroplasts, play essential roles in energy metabolism and cellular homeostasis and are important systems for studying plant genome evolution [8, 9]. Although the chloroplast genome of a related species, Krascheninnikovia ceratoides, has been characterized, organellar genomic resources for K. arborescens, especially its mitochondrial genome, are still lacking [6]. This gap limits our understanding of organelle genome architecture and evolution in this species.

Plant mitochondrial genomes represent a highly dynamic and structurally complex component of plant cells, characterized by frequent recombination, structural rearrangements, and extensive variation in size and organization [10]. Beyond their essential roles in energy production through oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial genes encode key components of the respiratory chain and contribute to cellular metabolism and homeostasis [11–13]. Plant mitochondrial genome organization and stability have also been discussed in relation to stress responses and important agronomic traits such as cytoplasmic male sterility [8, 14–16]. Despite their functional and evolutionary importance, plant mitochondrial genomes remain poorly characterized in many non-model species. To date, no mitochondrial genome has been reported for K. arborescens, limiting our understanding of its organelle genome architecture and evolutionary characteristics. Moreover, although chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes coexist within plant cells and jointly contribute to cellular metabolism, they differ markedly in genome structure, evolutionary rate, RNA editing patterns, and intracellular DNA transfer dynamics [17]. Comparative analyses of chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes can provide insights into how distinct evolutionary processes contribute to variation in organelle genome structure and evolution in Amaranthaceae [18].

Therefore, using PacBio HiFi long-read sequencing data, we assembled and annotated the complete mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of K. arborescens. Beyond generating genomic resources, we compared the structural and evolutionary characteristics of the two organellar genomes, including gene content, repetitive sequences, RNA editing patterns, codon usage bias, and selective constraints. We also investigated intracellular gene transfer through the identification of mitochondrial plastid-derived sequences (MTPTs) and evaluated the phylogenetic position of K. arborescens within Caryophyllales. The comparative analyses presented here provide insights into organelle genome evolution, structural variation, and intracellular DNA transfer in K. arborescens, and contribute valuable genomic resources for future studies in Amaranthaceae.

Results

Characteristics of the K. arborescens organelle genomes

Based on PacBio HiFi sequencing data, the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of K. arborescens were assembled and annotated. The mitochondrial genome was assembled as a master circular genome representation of 387,891 bp with a GC content of 43.99% (Fig. 1). Mapping of PacBio HiFi reads back to the assembled organelle genomes showed complete genome coverage for both the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes. The average sequencing depths were approximately 3,428 × and 28,331 × for the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes, respectively. Continuous read support was observed across the assembled genomes, supporting the reliability of the genome assemblies. The assembly graph visualized using Bandage from the GFA assembly file (Figure S1A) provided additional support for the assembled mitochondrial genome representation and illustrated one possible genomic configuration consistent with the sequencing data. A total of 62 genes were identified, of which 56 were unique, including 36 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 23 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, and 3 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes (Table 1). Nine genes were found to contain introns. Classification of genes in the Krascheninnikovia arborescens mitochondrial genome

The chloroplast genome exhibited a typical circular quadripartite structure, consisting of a pair of inverted repeats (IRs), a large single-copy (LSC) region, and a small single-copy (SSC) region. The lengths of the IR, LSC, and SSC regions were 23,779 bp, 85,433 bp, and 19,048 bp, respectively (Fig. 1). Ninety genes were annotated in the chloroplast genome, including 80 protein-coding genes. Functional classification indicated that these genes comprised 44 photosynthesis-related genes, 28 genes involved in self-replication, 6 genes with other biological functions, including the acetyl-CoA carboxylase subunit (accD), cytochrome c synthesis (ccsA), envelope membrane protein (cemA), protease (clpP), translational initiation factor (infA), and maturase (matK), and 5 genes of unknown function. Intron analysis revealed that, excluding duplicated copies located in the IR regions, 17 unique genes contained a single intron (trnK-UUU, rps16, trnR-UCU, atpF, rpoC1, trnL-UAA, trnV-UAC, petB, petD, rpl16, ndhB(+), trnI-GAU(+), trnA-UGC(+), ndhA, trnA-UGC(−), trnI-GAU(−), and ndhB(−)). In addition, ycf3 and clpP each contained two introns (Table S2).

Repetitive sequences of the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of K. arborescens

Twenty-four simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were identified in the mitochondrial genome of K. arborescens, comprising mononucleotide and dinucleotide repeat types (Fig. 2; Table S3). Mononucleotide repeats were overwhelmingly predominant, accounting for 23 loci (95.83%), whereas only one dinucleotide repeat was detected. Most mononucleotide repeats consisted of A/T motifs, with repeat numbers ranging from 10 to 14, corresponding to sequence lengths of 10–14 bp. In the chloroplast genome, 37 SSRs were detected, including 32 mononucleotide repeats (86.49%) and 5 compound repeats (13.51%). All mononucleotide SSRs were A/T type, with lengths ranging from 10 to 16 bp. All compound SSRs were composed of two flanking A/T mononucleotide repeat regions arranged in tandem, with non-repetitive spacer sequences interspersed between them, and their total lengths ranged from 27 to 69 bp (Fig. 2; Table S4). These results indicated that A/T-type mononucleotide repeats were the dominant SSR type in both organellar genomes of K. arborescens, consistent with the distribution patterns reported for most angiosperms [19, 20]. Sixteen tandem repeats were identified in the mitochondrial genome, with lengths of 15–72 bp, whereas 66 were found in the chloroplast genome, ranging from 11 to 135 bp (Tables S5–S6). All tandem repeats showed clear A/T enrichment, in line with the general A/T bias characteristic of plant organellar genomes.

Additionally, 361 dispersed repeats, including forward and palindromic repeats, were identified in the mitochondrial genome, with short fragments (< 100 bp) representing the predominant category (Tables S7–S8). Notably, three pairs of long repeats (> 1 kb) were detected, the longest reaching 7,434 bp (Table S7). In contrast, dispersed repeats in the chloroplast genome mainly consisted of short to medium-sized fragments ranging from 30 to 679 bp. No ultra-long repeats were identified, and all were non-tandem in type. This relatively simple repeat composition and structurally stable pattern are consistent with the conserved nature of typical angiosperm chloroplast genomes.

Analysis of codon usage bias

A total of 9,792 codons were identified in the mitochondrial genome of K. arborescens, including three stop codons (TGA, TAG, and TAA) (Fig. 3; Table S9). Phenylalanine (Phe) is the most frequently encoded amino acid, predominantly represented by the codon TTT, which occurs 415 times and accounts for 4.24% of all codons. Isoleucine (Ile) and leucine (Leu) are the next most abundant amino acids, with ATT (356 codons, 3.64%) and TTA (295 codons, 3.01%) serving as their major codons. Among all individual codons, TTT (encoding phenylalanine) is the most frequently used, whereas the stop codon TAG appears least often, with only seven occurrences. Methionine (Met) is encoded exclusively by ATG, which is present 258 times in the genome. Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) analysis indicates that 46 codons have RSCU values greater than 1.0, suggesting preferential usage. Among these preferred codons, 26 have A/T at the third codon position, while 10 end with G and 10 end with C. This pattern indicates a clear A/T bias at the third codon position in the mitochondrial genome.Fig. 3Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) analysis of the mitochondrial (A) and chloroplast genomes (B) of K. arborescens. In the chloroplast genome, 25,441 codons were detected (Fig. 3; Table S9). Glutamic acid (Glu) is the most frequently encoded amino acid, represented by 1,109 codons and accounting for 4.36% of the total. Lysine (Lys) and isoleucine (Ile) follow, with 1,102 (4.33%) and 1,074 (4.22%) codons, respectively. RSCU analysis shows that 39 codons have RSCU values greater than 1.0. Among these preferred codons, 29 end with A/T, 7 end with G, and 3 end with C, indicating a pronounced A/T bias at the third codon position in the chloroplast genome as well.

Prediction of RNA editing sites

In the mitochondrial genome of K. arborescens, 301 RNA-editing sites across 27 protein-coding genes were predicted (Fig. 4; Table S10). Among these genes, atp9 and ccmFC had the largest numbers of predicted RNA-editing sites, with 23 sites each, followed by nad2 with 22 sites; rps13 had the fewest predicted editing sites, with only one event detected. The vast majority of predicted editing sites were of the C → U type, accounting for 93.69% of the total (282 sites). The U → C type had a relatively low proportion and was only detected in a small number of genes, such as rps4, ccmFC, and atp8. Among all predicted RNA-editing events, most sites (189, 62.79%) were located at the second codon position, followed by the first position (104, 34.55%) (Table S11). This result indicates a strong preference for predicted RNA-editing sites at the second codon position, where editing may potentially alter amino acid properties; however, the functional significance of these predicted events requires experimental validation. With regard to amino acid substitution types, serine-to-leucine (Ser to Leu) and proline-to-leucine (Pro to Leu) were the two predominant forms. The predicted amino acid substitutions mainly generated hydrophobic residues (Table S12). A total of 266 potential RNA-editing sites were predicted across 56 protein-coding genes in the chloroplast genome (Fig. 4; Table S13). Among these, ycf1 harbored the highest number of predicted editing sites (35 sites), followed by ycf2 with 27 sites. In terms of amino acid substitution types, the main predicted conversions occurred between polar and nonpolar amino acids, suggesting potential changes in amino acid properties; however, these effects remain to be experimentally validated. In general, differences were observed between the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes in terms of the number of predicted RNA-editing sites, editing types, and patterns of amino acid substitution. It should be noted that all RNA-editing sites reported here were identified through bioinformatic prediction using PREPACT and have not been experimentally validated. Sequence similarity between the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes

The assembled mitochondrial genome of K. arborescens is 387,891 bp in length, approximately 2.55 times that of its chloroplast genome. BLASTn alignment analysis of the chloroplast and mitochondrial genome sequences identified 19 homologous fragments (mitochondrial plastid-derived sequences, MTPTs), with lengths ranging from 30 to 5,419 bp and a total length of 17,061 bp, accounting for about 4.40% of the mitochondrial genome (Fig. 5; Table S14). The sequence similarity of these homologous fragments varied from 73.79% to 100%; the majority of fragments showed over 90% similarity, and 5 fragments reached 100% sequence identity, suggesting that they may represent relatively recent interorganellar gene transfer events. Functional annotation results revealed 7 intact genes within these fragments, including 5 tRNA genes (trnV-GAC, trnI-GAU, trnS-GGA, trnW-CCA, trnN-GUU), 1 rRNA gene (rrn16s), and 1 gene encoding the photosystem II D protein (psbD) (Fig. 5; Table S14). Multiple incomplete functional gene fragments of chloroplast origin were identified, such as rps4, rps12, psbA, ndhA, and ycf3. Ka/Ks and phylogenetic analysis

To investigate the selection pressure and evolutionary patterns of mitochondrial genes in K. arborescens, Ka/Ks ratio analysis was performed based on 19 shared mitochondrial protein-coding genes from 9 representative species of Caryophyllales, with S. lycopersicum as the outgroup (Fig. 6; Table S15). The results showed that the Ka/Ks ratios of all 19 shared genes were less than 1, indicating that these genes were mainly subjected to purifying selection during evolution and that their encoded proteins are functionally conserved. Notably, the ATP synthesis-related gene (atp1) and respiratory chain-related genes (cob, cox1, cox3, nad4L, and nad6) had relatively lower Ka/Ks values, suggesting that these genes experienced strong purifying selection. This finding implies that such genes may play important roles in maintaining mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and cellular energy metabolism, with their protein structures and functions being subjected to strong functional constraints during evolution.Fig. 6Ka/Ks analysis of mitochondrial protein-coding genes in K. arborescens and representative Caryophyllales species. To further clarify the phylogenetic position of K. arborescens among angiosperms, two maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees were constructed using S. lycopersicum (Solanaceae) as the outgroup (Fig. 7; Table S16). The trees were built based on the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of 10 species, with 19 shared mitochondrial protein-coding genes and 71 shared chloroplast protein-coding genes used for tree construction, respectively (Table S17). The results revealed that the phylogenetic trees constructed from the two organellar genomes exhibited highly consistent topological structures, which were consistent with the current Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG) classification system. All Caryophyllales species clustered into a monophyletic clade, clearly separated from the outgroup S. lycopersicum. Among them, three species of Caryophyllaceae (Silene latifolia, Agrostemma githago, and Vaccaria hispanica) clustered together, and K. arborescens (Amaranthaceae) and Spinacia oleracea were stably grouped into a single clade, with bootstrap support values of 100% for most nodes. Discussion

The first assembled mitochondrial genome of K. arborescens provides an opportunity to examine organelle genome evolution in a drought-tolerant member of Amaranthaceae. Comparative analyses show that, although mitochondrial genome architecture varies substantially among Amaranthaceae species, coding gene content and GC composition remain conserved (Table S18). Plant mitochondrial genomes exhibit remarkable structural diversity despite their relatively conserved coding capacity, a pattern that is well supported in Amaranthaceae. Within this family, reported mitochondrial genomes generally range from approximately 210 kb to nearly 400 kb, while structural organization varies substantially, from single circular representations to multipartite configurations [21–23]. This decoupling of structural complexity from genome size suggests that recombination-mediated rearrangements may be an important contributor to mitochondrial genome architectural variation in Amaranthaceae. In contrast, GC content remains highly conserved across the reported Amaranthaceae species, typically around 43–45%, as shown in closely related Amaranthaceae species (Table S18–S19) [21–25]. The GC content of K. arborescens falls within this range, consistent with the slow evolutionary rate of plant mitochondrial DNA and indicating limited variation in base composition. Minor variations in GC content among species are more likely attributable to stochastic processes than to adaptive evolution [26]. Our assembly fits these general patterns, further supporting the coexistence of conserved base composition and structural dynamism within the family. Although the current assembly has not yet been experimentally validated by PCR or other molecular approaches, the uniform read-mapping coverage, Bandage assembly graph, and HiFi read support provide evidence for the reliability of the assembled mitochondrial genome sequence and its master circular representation. Nevertheless, alternative mitochondrial conformations cannot be excluded based on the current data.

Codon usage analysis showed that most preferred synonymous codons in both organellar genomes of K. arborescens ended with A or T. A similar pattern has been reported in many plant organellar genomes and is generally associated with their AT-rich nucleotide composition [27, 28]. Previous studies have shown that nucleotide composition is one of the major factors influencing codon usage patterns in chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes, with AT-rich genomes tending to favor codons ending in A or T [27, 29, 30]. Therefore, the codon usage pattern observed in K. arborescens is consistent with the conserved compositional characteristics of plant organellar genomes and reflects the influence of nucleotide composition on synonymous codon preference during organelle genome evolution.

RNA editing is an important post-transcriptional process in plant organelles and can modify organelle-encoded transcripts [31, 32]. In K. arborescens, the predicted RNA-editing events are mostly C-to-U conversions and exhibit a strong preference for the second codon position, consistent with patterns observed in most angiosperms. Notably, genes with high numbers of predicted editing sites, such as atp9, ccmFC, nad2, and nad7, encode key components involved in mitochondrial respiration and cytochrome c maturation [33, 34]. The concentration of predicted editing sites in these genes suggests that RNA editing may alter the amino acid composition of proteins associated with mitochondrial functions. Similar distributions of editing sites have been reported in other angiosperm mitochondrial genomes, indicating that these genes are common targets of organellar RNA editing [35–37]. However, as the identified editing sites are based on computational predictions, they should be regarded as putative RNA-editing events, and their functional significance requires further experimental validation.

Repetitive sequences are another major feature contributing to the dynamic nature of plant mitochondrial genomes [38–40]. A total of 401 repeats were identified in the mitochondrial genome of K. arborescens, with dispersed repeats being the dominant type. The prevalence of these repeats may promote homologous recombination, thereby generating alternative genome conformations and contributing to structural plasticity [39]. Comparative analysis across Amaranthaceae species reveals substantial variation in the abundance and length of long repeats (> 1 kb), suggesting differences in the potential for genome rearrangement [23, 25]. Such variation may influence the rate of structural evolution and contribute to interspecific diversity in mitochondrial genome organization [41]. In contrast to mitochondrial genomes, the relatively low repeat content observed in chloroplast genomes is consistent with their conserved structure and limited recombination activity [17]. The contrasting evolutionary patterns of the two organellar genomes further underscore the potential role of repetitive sequences in shaping mitochondrial genome structure. However, whether these repeats actively participate in genome recombination and their specific contribution to structural variation in K. arborescens remains to be verified through experimental studies.

Intracellular gene transfer from chloroplasts to mitochondria represents an important source of genetic variation in plant organellar genomes and contributes to mitochondrial genome expansion and structural complexity [42]. In K. arborescens, chloroplast-derived sequences accounted for approximately 4.40% of the mitochondrial genome, a proportion comparable to that reported in other Amaranthaceae species [23, 25]. Variation in the abundance and size of mitochondrial plastid DNA (MTPT) among species suggests differences in DNA transfer activity, recombination frequency, and the capacity of mitochondrial genomes to incorporate and retain foreign sequences. Five MTPT fragments in K. arborescens exhibited 100% sequence identity with their corresponding chloroplast sequences. This indicates that at least some transfer events could have occurred relatively recently during evolution. Alternatively, the high sequence similarity may reflect slower sequence divergence after transfer. In addition, tRNA genes account for most of the intact genes retained in MTPT regions. This pattern is widely observed in plants and has been attributed to their high functional compatibility and lower structural constraints compared with protein-coding genes [43–45].

Despite the extensive structural variation observed in plant mitochondrial genomes, Ka/Ks analysis revealed strong evolutionary conservation of mitochondrial protein-coding genes across Caryophyllales [46, 47]. All examined genes exhibited Ka/Ks values well below 1, indicating pervasive purifying selection. Genes associated with core energy metabolism, including atp, cox, cob, and nad families, showed particularly low evolutionary rates, underscoring their essential roles in maintaining mitochondrial function. These genes encode key components of the respiratory chain and are involved in electron transport, ATP production, and redox balance [48, 49]. Their high level of conservation suggests strong functional constraints that preserve mitochondrial integrity and cellular energy homeostasis over evolutionary time [50].

The coexistence of extensive structural variation and highly conserved coding sequences is a common characteristic of plant mitochondrial genomes [51]. While processes such as intracellular DNA transfer and repeat-associated recombination contribute to variation in genome organization, protein-coding genes involved in fundamental mitochondrial functions remain highly conserved. This contrast suggests that structural evolution and sequence evolution may proceed at different rates within plant mitochondrial genomes. Collectively, these findings provide insight into the evolutionary characteristics of the K. arborescens mitochondrial genome and contribute to a broader understanding of mitochondrial genome evolution in angiosperms.

Materials and methods

Plant material and sequencing

Healthy and mature leaves of K. arborescens were collected from plants cultivated in the greenhouse of Inner Mongolia University (South Campus, Hohhot, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, China; 40.758903° N, 111.685566° E; altitude 1037 m). The leaves were thoroughly washed with DEPC-treated water and stored at − 80 °C in the Key Laboratory of the Ministry of Education, School of Life Sciences, Inner Mongolia University. Mitochondrial DNA was extracted and purified using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method [52]. Long-read sequencing was performed on the PacBio Revio platform by constructing a PCR-free SMRTbell library, and the sequencing data were processed using SMRTLink v8.0. Polymerase reads were subsequently filtered to remove reads shorter than 50 bp, reads with a quality value lower than 0.8, self-ligated sequences, and reads containing adapter sequences. High-quality reads were obtained after quality control using SMRTLink v8.0. A total of 126.08 Gb of PacBio HiFi sequencing data, comprising 7,269,330 HiFi reads with a read N50 of 17,105 bp, were generated for organelle genome assembly.

Assembly and annotation of the organelle genome

Based on HiFi reads, the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of K. arborescens were de novo assembled using Oatk v1.0 [53] with default parameters. Assembly graphs and contig connections were visualized using Bandage v0.8.1 [54]. To evaluate assembly reliability, PacBio HiFi reads were mapped back to the assembled mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes using Minimap2 v2.28-r1209 [55]. Assembly quality was assessed based on genome coverage, sequencing depth, and read support across the entire assembly, including all contig junctions and repeat-associated regions. The chloroplast genome was annotated using CPGAVAS2 [56], GeSeq [57], and CPGview [58], whereas the mitochondrial genome was annotated using IPMGA [59], GeSeq, and CPGview, all with default parameters. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and transfer RNA (tRNA) genes were identified using BLASTn, tRNAscan-SE 2.0 [60], and ARAGORN [61], respectively. The final annotation results were generated using OGDRAW v1.3.1 [62], and all annotations were manually inspected and curated to ensure accuracy and completeness.

RNA-editing events

RNA-editing sites in the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of K. arborescens were predicted using the PREPACT online platform [63] with default parameter settings. Reference species were selected based on phylogenetic relatedness and the availability of high-quality organellar genome annotations in the PREPACT database. The mitochondrial genome of Beta vulgaris (NC_002511.2) and the chloroplast genome of Arabidopsis thaliana (NC_000932.1) served as reference sequences for comparative prediction. Given that C-to-U conversion represents the predominant form of RNA editing in chloroplast genomes, whereas other editing types are rare and more prone to artifacts caused by sequencing errors or misalignment, especially within repetitive regions, only C-to-U editing events were considered in the chloroplast analysis to minimize the risk of false-positive results.

Repeat sequence identification

Repetitive sequences in the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of K. arborescens were analyzed, including simple sequence repeats (SSRs), tandem repeats, and dispersed repeats. SSR loci were screened using the MISA web-based tool [64], with thresholds defined as ≥ 10 repeat units for mononucleotide motifs and ≥ 5 repeat units for di- to hexanucleotide motifs. Compound SSRs were only considered when no interruption occurred between adjacent repeat units. Tandem repeats were detected with Tandem Repeats Finder under default settings [65]. Dispersed repeats were identified using REPuter [66], with a minimum repeat length of 30 bp, a Hamming distance of 3, and a maximum repeat number of 5000. Four repeat types, including forward, reverse, complementary, and palindromic repeats, were searched. The abundance and positional distribution of repetitive sequences were analyzed and visualized using TBtools-II [67]. Codon usage bias and relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values were calculated using custom Python scripts, and the results were further visualized in R.

Construction of the phylogenetic tree

To determine the phylogenetic position of K. arborescens, the coding sequences (CDS) of mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes from 9 representative species across 6 families within Caryophyllales were downloaded from NCBI for phylogenetic analyses. These families included Caryophyllaceae, Amaranthaceae, Nyctaginaceae, Cactaceae, Tamaricaceae, and Nepenthaceae. Solanum lycopersicum (Solanaceae, Solanales) was used as the outgroup for phylogenetic tree construction (Table S16). All Amaranthaceae species with both complete mitochondrial and chloroplast genome sequences available in public databases at the time of analysis were included in this study. Therefore, taxon sampling within Amaranthaceae was constrained by the current availability of organellar genome resources. After screening the organellar genomes of the 10 species, 19 shared mitochondrial protein-coding genes and 71 shared chloroplast protein-coding genes were identified (Table S17). Multiple sequence alignments were performed using MUSCLE v3.8.1 [68]. Maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees were then constructed using IQ-TREE2 [69] with the parameters “-m MFP -bb 1000”, allowing the program to automatically select the best-fit substitution model. Finally, the phylogenetic trees were visualized and edited using iTOL v7 [70].

DNA transfer between the chloroplast and the mitochondrion

The assembled chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes were compared using BLASTn v2.14.1 + with an e-value threshold of 1e-5 to identify homologous fragments. A synteny map was subsequently generated using TBtools-II [67]. Based on the BLAST results, collinear sequences were extracted using BEDtools v2.31.1 and re-annotated to further confirm the regions of gene transfer between the two organellar genomes.

Selection pressure analysis

For selection pressure analysis, 9 representative species from 6 families within Caryophyllales were retrieved from the NCBI database, with S. lycopersicum designated as the outgroup. Orthologous genes were identified from shared, annotated protein-coding genes among the ten analyzed organellar genomes. Only genes present in all ten species and showing clear one-to-one correspondence were retained for subsequent analyses. The coding sequences of each orthologous gene were aligned using MUSCLE v3.8.1. Alignment results were manually inspected, and genes showing abnormal sequence lengths or poor alignment quality were excluded from further analysis. Pairwise rates of nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitutions between K. arborescens and each comparison species were estimated using KaKs_Calculator v2.0b [71] under the MLWL model. Selective constraints acting on these genes were assessed by calculating the Ka/Ks ratios, and the resulting data were graphically presented using R.

Conclusion

We successfully assembled and annotated the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of K. arborescens. We further analyzed genome structure, predicted RNA-editing sites, codon usage bias, repetitive sequences, intracellular gene transfer, selection pressure, and phylogenetic relationships.

The mitochondrial genome of K. arborescens was assembled as a master circular genome representation of 387,891 bp with a GC content of 43.99%, whereas the chloroplast genome exhibited a typical quadripartite structure. Comparative analyses showed that mitochondrial genomes within Amaranthaceae vary in genome size, structural organization, and repeat composition, while their core protein-coding gene content and GC content remain relatively conserved. The abundance of repetitive sequences and chloroplast-derived fragments in the mitochondrial genome reflects its dynamic structural characteristics. In addition, Ka/Ks analyses indicated that the shared protein-coding genes were generally subject to purifying selection.

Phylogenetic analyses consistently placed K. arborescens within Amaranthaceae and supported its close relationship with S. oleracea. Overall, this study provides the first organellar genome resources for K. arborescens, expands the genomic resources available for Amaranthaceae, and contributes to a better understanding of organellar genome evolution, structural variation, and phylogenetic relationships in angiosperms.
