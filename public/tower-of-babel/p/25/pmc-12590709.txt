Banana genotype-associated microbiomes play an essential role in suppressing Fusarium wilt
Authors: Wenlong Zhang, Shengtao Xu, Tingting Bai, Bonaventure Omondi Aman, Yongfen Wang, Shu Li, Jielin Wang, Yue Qin, Yun-Yue Wang, Si-Jun Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFusarium wilt of banana (FWB) seriously threatens the banana industry’s sustainable development. Recent evidence indicates that FWB occurrence and development are not only regulated by host resistance genes, but are also significantly influenced by host microbiomes. However, the link between banana genotypes and genotype-associated microbiomes remains unclear.ResultsIn this study, we used the amplification sequencing method to characterize the bacterial and fungal communities in three root compartments (root peripheral, rhizosphere, and endosphere) of six banana genotypes with different levels of FWB resistance. Sequence analysis showed that the banana host’s resistance to FWB significantly correlated with its associated bacterial and fungal communities in the root peripheral and rhizosphere. The FWB highly resistant group (RG) have more stable cross-kingdom microbial networks than FWB moderately resistant group (MG) and susceptible group (SG). The relative abundance of potential beneficial microorganisms differs significantly between RG and SG in the root periphery and rhizosphere. Bacillus, Penicillium, and Trichoderma are enriched in the root periphery of RG, whereas Flavobacterium, Sphingobium, and Pseudomonas are enriched in the root periphery and rhizosphere of SG. Inoculation with the RG biomarker Penicillium sp., which showed high relative abundance in the root periphery, significantly reduced the FWB disease index in the susceptible banana genotypes Brazilian and Guijiao No.1 by 51.67% and 51.72%, respectively.ConclusionsThe outcome of this study provides solid data that banana genotype-linked, root-associated microbiomes can play a highly significant role in suppressing FWB. This provides a promising basis for microbiome-based interventions in FWB management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07565-9.

Background

Holobionts, which are symbiotic associations of plants and their associated microbiota, play a crucial role in maintaining plant productivity and health [1]. As an important component of plant holobionts, root-related microorganisms play important roles in plant availability of nutrients [2, 3], stress tolerance [4, 5], and disease resistance [6–8]. Their assembly is based on species, host genotype, and soil types, and they make corresponding adjustments according to growth period, climate, and stress [9, 10]. These recruited microorganisms are beneficial to plants at this stage of plant nutrition transformation, and have a dynamic and positive impact on plant growth [11, 12].

Increasing evidence shows that plant resistance to soil-borne diseases is related to the associated plant-root microbiomes [13]. For example, higher-abundance Flavobacterium TRM1-10 was enriched in the rhizosphere of bacterial wilt-resistant tomato, and could be used to inoculate susceptible tomato genotypes through isolation and culture to alleviate disease damage [6]. Therefore, analyzing microbiome differences among genotypes with cultivars levels of disease resistance can provide insights into host-microbiome interactions, which are critical for developing resistant cultivars and enhancing disease management strategies.

Currently, banana production is seriously threatened by Fusarium wilt of banana (FWB) a disease caused by the soil-borne fungus Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense (Foc). With the advent of a more virulent strain called the Foc Tropical race 4 (Foc TR4), this disease has become a global threat and is considered one of the most destructive banana diseases in history [14]. There are no comprehensively effective disease control measures, nor any widely available resistant genotypes. Recent studies have shown that the soil microbiomes can affect FWB epidemiology. Soils that suppress FWB had higher alpha diversity and relative abundance of high-potential beneficial bacteria, such as Myxococcales, Pseudomonadales, and Lysobacterales [15]. A lower variability of diversity index and composition was also associated with healthy rhizosphere microbiomes and with those microbial groups that were enriched in healthy plants [16]. Higher species richness and diversity of Gammaproteobacteria and other potentially plant-beneficial species were observed in the rhizosphere of healthy plants compared to susceptible plants [17]. However, these studies mainly focus on comparing healthy and diseased plants, or suppressive and conducive soils. The compositional differences in root-related communities with differential FWB resistance levels in banana genotypes have yet to be explored.

To date, no commercial banana varieties have been identified as being immune to Foc TR4 that would be comparable with the Cavendish group resistance to Foc Race 1 [14]. However, under comparable environmental conditions and pathogen pressure, numerous banana genotypes continue to display milder symptoms compared to susceptible ones [18]. While the primary regulation of plant disease-resistance responses predominantly hinges upon the expression of plant-encoded resistance genes, it is imperative to stress the substantial contribution of microbiome-mediated immune responses in orchestrating the intricate dynamics of disease-resistance processes [19]. In addition, microbiome composition differs across plant compartments, which corresponds to variations in functional capabilities [1]. Evaluating the correlation between FWB resistance levels and the presence of subterranean microbial communities across distinct anatomical compartments is critical for a better understanding of the microbiota’s role in banana’s FWB resistance. This understanding would then serve as a foundation for strategically developing and refining methodologies directed towards safeguarding or modulating the microbiomes within the banana rhizosphere. Therefore, we have explored how banana genotype-associated microbiomes could play important role in suppressing FWB. This study aimed to (1) elucidate the relationship between bacterial and fungal communities in root compartments and FWB resistance; (2) characterize those communities and identify biomarkers linked to different resistance levels; and (3) isolate microorganisms with potential FWB-suppressive activity and evaluate their efficacy.

Results

FWB incidence among different banana genotypes in the field

There were significant differences in FWB resistance levels among the banana genotypes. GCTCV119 (GC) and Pahang (PH) showed no visible FWB symptoms, we defined them as highly resistant group (RG). The FWB incidence of Brazilian (BZ) and Guijiao No.1 (GJ) was 93% and 91%, respectively, which we defined as susceptible group (SG). Yunjiao No.1 (YJ) and Nantianhuang (NT) showed a FWB incidence of 63% and 50%, respectively. As this incidence was significantly lower than in the SG, we defined these as the moderately resistance group (MG) (Fig. 1).

Correlating FWB resistance with bacterial and fungal community composition and diversity

NMDS and PERMANOVA analysis revealed that the greatest effect on the total composition of bacterial and fungal communities, exerted at the compartment (R2 = 0. 39 for bacteria and R2 = 0.24 for fungi, P < 0.001 for both), was far greater than the impact of FWB resistance level (R2 = 0. 03 for bacteria and R2 = 0.05 for fungi, P < 0.001 for both) (Fig. 2a). PERMANOVA also showed that the composition of bacterial and fungal communities was significantly influenced by FWB resistance level in the root periphery (R2 = 0. 12 for bacteria and R2 = 0.15 for fungi, P < 0.001 for both) and rhizosphere (R2 = 0.11 for bacteria and R2 = 0.13 for fungi, P < 0.001 for both). Furthermore, the composition of fungal communities (R2 = 0.10, P < 0.001) was significantly influenced by FWB resistance level in the root endosphere (Fig. 2b). Only in the root periphery and rhizosphere, correlation analysis also showed that the community bacteria composition and fungi significantly correlated with FWB incidence (Fig. S1). In the root periphery, the Shannon index of the SG bacterial and fungal communities was significantly higher than for the RG. There was also positive correlation between the bacterial and fungal community Shannon index and FWB incidence. In the rhizosphere, the Shannon index of the SG fungal communities was significantly higher than that of the RG and MG, and there was positive correlation between the Shannon index of the fungal community and FWB incidence. In the root endosphere, the Shannon index of the MG bacterial community was significantly lower than that of the SG, and the fungal community for the MG was significantly higher than for the SG and the RG (Fig. 2c and Fig. S2).

Trail of Microbiome network in different FWB resistance group

In the three root compartments, the RG network stability was higher than that of the SG and MG (Fig. 3a and c and Table S2). In the root periphery, the MG network complexity was significantly lower than that of RG and SG. In the rhizosphere, RG complexity was significantly higher than that of MG and SG. However, in the root endosphere, the SG complexity was significantly higher than that of MG and RG (Fig. 3b).

Biomarkers for FWB highly resistant or susceptible group

We conducted a comparison between the SG and the RG to identify microbial biomarkers for FWB highly resistance and susceptibility in banana. The RG had a higher relative abundance of main gram-positive bacteria (Bacillota and Actinomycetota) in the root periphery and rhizosphere (Fig. 4a and b). The RG had a higher abundance of Mucilaginibacter, Jatrophihabitans, Bacillus, Humibacter, Penicillium, Trichoderma and Musicilium in the root periphery, as well as Sphingomonas and Musicilium in the rhizosphere. For SG, Sphingobium, Flavobacterium, Mesorhizobium, Pseudomonas, Microdochium, Gibellulopsis, Cosmospora and Fusarium were enriched in the root periphery and Sphingobium, Pseudomonas, Flavobacterium, Mesorhizobium, Pedobacter and Microdochium were enriched in the rhizosphere. Most RG biomarker at genus level negatively correlated with the relative abundance of Fusarium and the FWB incidence in all samples (including MG) in the same compartment, whereas most SG biomarkers showed positive correlation (Fig. 4c). There was no significant difference in the relative abundance of bacterial and fungal genera between SG and RG in the root endosphere. Most biomarkers identified by differential analysis were consistent with those obtained from LEfSe analysis. Interestingly, Flavobacterium was a biomarker of the SG in the root periphery but became a biomarker of the RG in the root endosphere (Fig. S3).

Microbial biomarkers were identified at the OTU level using the DESeq2 package with thresholds of fold change >2, adjusted P < 0.05, and relative abundance >0.1%. These criteria ensure that the selected OTUs represent taxa that are both statistically different and relatively abundant between RG and SG, and are therefore considered biomarkers indicative RG or SG. In the root periphery and rhizosphere, OTUs belonging to the same genus were identified as biomarkers specific to either the RG or SG, but not both. BOTU205151 (Sphingobium), BOTU63718 (Variovorax) and FOTU18480 (Microdochium) was the biomarker for the SG in three compartments, and FOTU14165 (Campylospora) was the biomarker for the RG in three compartments (Fig. S4).

Screening for microorganisms potentially associated with FWB control

Based on our amplicon sequencing data, we attempted to screen for microbes with potential biocontrol activity. We first constructed a phylogenetic tree of all Fusarium FOTUs to identify sequences that are closely related to Fusarium oxysporum (sequences most similar to the pathogen, PS). FOTU24044 was identified as the only sequence most similar to Fusarium oxysporum (Fig. S5), and its relative abundance was significantly positively correlated with FWB incidence and the composition of bacterial and fungal communities in the root periphery and rhizosphere (Fig. S6).

We then analyzed the correlation between OTUs with the high relative abundance (>1%) and FWB incidence, and the relative abundance of Fusarium and PS. In the root periphery, FOTU24822 (Penicillium), FOTU1351 (Penicillium), FOTU25351 (Trichoderma), FOTU20095 (Trichoderma), and BOTU311676 (Actinospica) were negatively correlated with the relative abundance of Fusarium, PS, and FWB incidence across all samples. FOTU18480 (Microdochium), FOTU15067 (Acremonium), FOTU13125 (Cosmospora), and BOTU205151 (Sphingobium), were all positively correlated with the relative abundance of Fusarium, PS, and FWB incidence across all samples. In the rhizosphere, FOTU14165 (Campylospora) was negatively correlated with the relative abundance of Fusarium, PS, and FWB incidence across all samples. FOTU18480 (Microdochium) was also positively correlated with all three metrics in the rhizosphere across all samples (Table 1).

Isolation and validation of potential FWB resistant organisms

The FOTU24822 and FOTU25351 had a very high relative abundance and disease inhibition potential in above analysis. Based on NCBI comparisons, the closest matches for FOTU24822 and FOTU25351 were Penicillium and Trichoderma, respectively. Then we isolated them from the root periphery of Pahang in the experimental site, and then selected 4 Penicillium and Trichoderma strains for subsequent FWB inhibition capacity verification using plate confrontation assays and greenhouse experiments. In plate confrontation assays, Trichoderma sp. (mean colony growth suppression rate: 78.92%) and Penicillium sp. Greenhouse inoculation experiments demonstrated that, compared with the Foc TR4-only treatment, pre-inoculation with Penicillium sp. significantly reduced the FWB disease index, with average control efficacies of 51.67% and 51.72% in Brazilian and Guijiao No.1, respectively (Fig. 5b and c). Compared to the water control, Foc TR4-only treatment significantly reduced plant biomass, while most pre-inoculation treatments with Penicillium or Trichoderma sp. significantly increased biomass relative to the Foc TR4-only treatment (Fig. 5c).

Discussion

The role of microorganisms in expanding plant ecological functions is widely recognized [20]. Analyzing the microbial composition associated with disease resistance in crops offers great potential for manipulating the microbiome to maintain plant health. This study has characterized the bacterial and fungal communities in the three banana root compartments (periphery, rhizosphere, and endosphere) with different FWB resistance genotypes. The outcome of this study has found that the banana host’s FWB resistance level in different genotypes significantly correlated with its associated bacterial and fungal communities in the root periphery and rhizosphere. Different FWB resistance genotypes showed significant differences in the relative abundance of potential beneficial microorganisms, and the microbiomes associated with highly resistant genotypes exhibited more stable networks. In addition, the study has validated the FWB control effect of RG biomarker, Penicillium sp.

The composition of soil microbiomes correlated with the level of genotypes’ FWB resistance

Our results have shown that the influence of root microbiome compartments on bacterial and fungal community composition is higher than that of the host FWB resistance level. This result is predictable, as a large number of research studies corroborate that the difference in bacterial and fungal community composition between different root compartments is higher than genotype or phenotype [21, 22]. This may be mainly related to the functional difference of microbiomes required by the different positions of plant roots [23].

This study also found that genotypes with different FWB resistance levels significantly affected the bacterial and fungal community composition in the root periphery and rhizosphere. Previous studies [24, 25] have demonstrated that soil type and root compartment exert significant influence on the bacterial and fungal communities associated with bananas, while genotype has shown no significant effect. This phenomenon may be attributed to the large variation introduced when samples from different root compartments are analyzed together, rather than comparing genotypes within the same compartment. Although there were significant differences in FWB resistance levels for all samples in this study, the variation is still primarily affected by compartments. Therefore, conducting differential analysis within the same compartment level is more reasonable. This correlation between FWB resistance and the root periphery’s microbiome is slightly higher than that of the rhizosphere. It is generally believed that the rhizosphere microbiome dynamics are of greater significance for plant adaptability than those in more peripheral areas [1]. The root periphery, being more extensive than the rhizosphere, is speculated to provide stronger buffering against Foc TR4 infection. Therefore, shaping a healthier periphery microbiome may be one key to conferring FWB resistance. Subsequent studies should focus on the soil microbiome outside the root, especially the root periphery. In this study, we found that genotypes with different levels of FWB resistance showed no correlation with the composition of endophytic bacterial communities. However, we cannot ignore the disease control function of endophytic microorganisms in bananas. Although controlling root endophytes in the field is more challenging than managing soil microorganisms outside the roots, FWB can also be effectively controlled by inoculating exogenous bacteria or fungi at the tissue-culture seedling stage [26].

Diversity and network of bacterial and fungal communities with different FWB resistance levels

In this study, the diversity of the banana microbiomes significantly positively correlated with FWB incidence in the root periphery, where the lower the resistance, the higher the microbiome diversity. It is partly believed that higher microbial diversity can inhibit soilborne disease [27–29]. For example, Yuan et al. [30] found that fungal communities in healthy soil exhibit higher alpha diversity compared to crops suffering from FWB. However, there were significant differences between different studies [31, 32], with a recent study indicating that healthy banana plants have lower fungal alpha diversity compared to diseased plants [33], as also exemplified in tomato [34]. These contradictory conclusions may be due to differences in soil type, climate, management measures, and plant disease severity. Therefore, alpha diversity as a marker of soil health needs to be more carefully considered.

Network analysis results show that the network of FWB highly resistant bananas is more complex and stable than susceptible genotypes. Common bean resistance to Fusarium oxysporum had a modular topology of the rhizosphere microbiome, which implies a more complex and highly connected bacterial community than in the rhizosphere of the Fusarium-susceptible cultivars [13]. Jiang et al. [35] found that Solanaceous plants with tomato bacterial wilt (Ralstonia solanacearum) resistance had a higher modularity index and an average node degree. A study had shown that when a pathogen infects the host, it will actively inhibit the microorganisms that resist it, by secreting effector proteins, thus creating a micro-environment that is more conducive to its infection [36]. A more stable community relationship may increase the difficulty of pathogen infection taking hold, which may be related to more intense competition within the disease-resistant microbiome [37, 38]. This means that the complex network relationship slows the increase of pathogen abundance, because pathogen has to overcome more pressure from the competition between microbial communities.

Microbiome biomarkers associated with a genotype’s FWB resistance

This study can provide a reference for screening beneficial microorganisms in banana. We identified bacteria and fungi whose relative abundances differed between highly resistant and susceptible genotypes, noting that these differences were observed in a single soil type and may vary under different soil conditions [1]. In this study, Bacterial OTUs within the same genus were not found to serve as biomarkers for both RG and SG in the same compartment. For example, the Pseudomonas OTU identified was a biomarker only for SG in the root periphery and rhizosphere. This may be due to the similar niche and nutrient utilization patterns of microorganisms belonging to the same genus in a community [39]. This again shows that it is feasible to preliminarily understand characteristics of microbial communities through microbial genera [40]. However, it should be noted that microorganisms within the same genus may still have differential effects on the host [41].

We observed differences in microbial compositions between highly resistant and susceptible genotypes in the same soil. We noted that the bacteria present at high relative abundance in highly resistant genotypes are main gram-positive, which is often a sign of healthy soil or of plants at the phylum level [8]. It has been widely proven that Bacillus, Penicillium, and Trichoderma, present at high relative abundance in FWB highly resistant genotypes, can inhibit fungal wilts and have been developed into a variety of biological agents [42–44]. A recent study by Liu et al. [45] shows that the FWB resistance of different banana varieties correlated with the plant’s ability to recruit Penicillium and Trichoderma, which could inhibit the Foc TR4 pathogen. The growth of Penicillium and Trichoderma was promoted by banana root exudates, including shikimic acid, D-(−)-ribofuranose, and propylene glycol. In this study, the relative abundance of beneficial microorganisms differed between susceptible and resistant genotypes. The SG’s biomarkers, Pseudomonas, Sphingobium, and Flavobacterium, are enriched in the root periphery and rhizosphere. Some members of these genera have been reported to control FWB, but their effects can be strain-specific [46, 47]. In the root endosphere, Flavobacterium was the biomarker for the resistant genotype obtained by LEfSe analysis, suggesting that banana resistant genotypes can better resist fungal infection by enrichment of Falvobacterium in the endosphere [7]. Campylospora, an OTU found enriched in the three roots compartments of RG, is an endophytic mycorrhiza reported to inhibit bacterial pathogens [48].

We analyzed the correlation of RG and SG biomarkers with the relative abundance of Fusarium, pathogen-like sequences (FOTU24044), and FWB incidence. It is worth noting that pathogen-like sequences may also include potentially beneficial Fusarium, which could contribute to disease suppression [49]. Genera or OTUs negatively correlated with FWB incidence were consistently enriched in resistant plants, whereas those positively correlated were more abundant in susceptible plants. However, microorganisms that are positively correlated with FWB incidence do not necessarily imply a lack of disease-suppressive potential. For example, Sphingobium, Pseudomonas, and Flavobacterium, were previously identified as key taxa involved in FWB suppression [15, 50]. Susceptible banana genotypes have been shown to recruit disease-suppressive microorganisms, which represents a key mechanism for the formation of disease-suppressive soils [51, 52]. Although distinct bacterial and fungal community assembly patterns appear to confer some degree of disease inhibition, our study did not quantify or compare their relative contributions. Future work should address this question by constructing synthetic communities or performing microbiome transplantation, which would provide important guidance for developing effective biological agents.

Trichoderma and Penicillium suppress FWB infection

It is a common method to preliminarily screen potential biocontrol microorganisms through plate confrontation [53–55]. However, microorganisms without direct inhibitory effects also play an important role in the disease resistance process by activating plant-induced systemic resistance and competition for substrates or colonization sites [56]. Penicillium sp. showed a stronger FWB control effect in greenhouse experiments than Trichoderma sp., which is consistent with the data analysis showing a stronger negative correlation between the relative abundance of Penicillium and FWB incidence. This indicates that the strategy of screening biocontrol microorganisms through high-throughput sequencing is feasible. In this study, the relative abundance of Penicillium and Trichoderma in the soil was very high, indicating that both can be used as effective targets for the managing of field microbiomes. Looking ahead, we recommend studying their preferred environment and carbon sources, and designing corresponding biological regulators to increase their volume in the soils where they occur, so as to maintain a healthy soil environment [57].

Conclusion

The analysis of amplicon data showed that the FWB resistance level in different genotypes of banana significantly correlated with associated bacterial and fungal communities in the root periphery and rhizosphere. The FWB highly resistant genotypes have more stable cross-kingdom networks than FWB highly susceptible group. Most importantly, FWB highly resistant and susceptible genotypes differ in the relative abundance of potential beneficial microorganisms. Furthermore, this study has verified the FWB-inhibition effect of dominant fungi in highly resistant genotypes. This study elucidated the characteristics of the soil microbial community of banana possessing different FWB resistance levels, and improved the understanding of microbially-mediated disease resistance. The feasibility of screening beneficial microorganisms using this study’s methodology was verified, which has provided a basis for manipulating microbial groups to maintain crop health and productivity.

Materials and methods

Field sampling for Microbiome analysis and assessment of FWB incidence in banana

All banana samples for sequencing were collected in the experimental site for FWB resistance evaluation in Xishuangbanna, Jinghong City, Dai Autonomous Prefecture of Xishuangbanna, Yunnan Province, southwest China (E 100°56′13″, N 21°51′43″; altitude 540 m), where banana has been continuously grown since 2008. FWB incidence was assessed following the method of Zuo et al. [58], calculated as: diseased plants/(diseased plants + non-diseased plants) × 100. Plants were classified as diseased based on typical FWB symptoms, including leaf wilting or yellowing, leaf collapse, pseudostem splitting, and vascular discoloration. For resistance identification, each genotype included 3 replicates, with 50 plantlets per replicate. The soil physicochemical properties: pH 6.03; organic matter content, 30.94 g/kg; total nitrogen, 1.52 g/kg; total phosphorus, 1.20 g/kg; total potassium, 2.75 g/kg; hydrolyzable nitrogen, 122.00 mg/kg; available potassium, 465.67 mg/kg; and available phosphorus, 54.60 mg/kg. The banana genotypes used in this study are listed in Table S3. Healthy new shoots were selectively collected for microbiome analysis to minimize the influence of disease on microbial communities. All root and soil samples for sequencing (Fig. 6) were collected from 10 plants at a depth of 15–20 cm around each plant. The sampling method refers to Bulgarelli et al. [59]. Root peripheral samples were collected at 1–3 cm around the roots. The root system was shaken vigorously to remove loose soil. The roots were then placed in a conical flask with sterile water and shaken at 200 rpm for 20 min. The root system was filtered through a 2.5 mm sieve and the soil suspension placed in a 50 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 9000 rpm for 10 min to obtain rhizosphere soil. To obtain root samples for endophytic microbial DNA extraction, the roots were rinsed three times with sterile water using a vortex mixer for vigorous agitation and then immersed in 75% ethanol for 10 min to remove surface microorganism. A total of 180 samples were obtained, consisting of 6 genotypes, 3 compartments (root periphery, rhizosphere and endosphere) and 10 biological replicates per genotype-compartment combination. All samples were stored at minus − 80 °C until DNA extraction was conducted.

DNA extraction and sequencing

The soil and root DNA samples (200 mg) were extracted using the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (D5625-01) (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions, and stored at −20 °C prior to further analysis. The quantity and quality of extracted DNA was determined using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively.

PCR amplification of the bacterial 16 S rRNA genes from the V3–V4 region was performed using the 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’)/806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). PCR amplification of the Fungal ITS rRNA genes ITS1 region was performed using the ITS5F (5’- GGAAGTAAAAGTCGTAACAAGG-3’)/ITS1R (5’- GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3’). The PCR system and program were referenced from our previous work [60]. After the individual quantification step, amplicons were pooled in equal amounts, and paired-end 2 × 300 bp (bp) sequencing was performed using the Illumina MiSeq platform with MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 at the Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Sequence analysis

The Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology (QIIME, v1.8.0) pipeline was employed to process the sequencing data, as previously described [61]. In brief, sequencing raw reads with exact matches to the barcodes were assigned to respective samples and were identified as valid sequences. The low-quality sequences were filtered according to following criteria [62]: sequences that had a length of < 150 bp, sequences with average Phred scores of < 20, sequences that contained ambiguous bases, and sequences that contained mononucleotide repeats of >8 bp. Raw sequencing reads were submitted to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (www.ncbi.NLM.NIH. Gov) Sequence Read Archive database (16 S: PRJNA938956; ITS: PRJNA938961). Paired-end reads were assembled using FLASH [63] After chimera detection, the remaining high-quality sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% sequence identity using the UCLUST algorithm [64]. A representative sequence was selected from each OTU using default parameters. OTU taxonomic classification was conducted using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) to search for regions of local similarity among the representative sequences set against the Greengenes and UNITE Database using the best BLAST hit method [65]. An OTU table was further generated to record the abundance of each OTU in each sample and the OTUs’ taxonomy. Those OTUs containing 0.001% of total sequences across all samples were discarded. To minimize the difference in sequencing depth across samples, an averaged, rounded, rarefied OTU table was generated by averaging 100 evenly resampled OTU subsets under 90% of the minimum sequencing depth (16 S: 15000, ITS: 23834) for further analysis.

Isolating fungal isolates and evaluating disease suppression

Penicillium and Trichoderma were isolated from root peripheral soil of the Pahang banana grown at the experimental site in Xishuangbanna, using a serial dilution method and plating the dilutions on Rose Bengal Agar and Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) media supplemented with 100 mg/L of ampicillin and clarithromycin to inhibit bacterial growth. Individual colonies were then selected to obtain pure isolates. After 3 days of incubation, morphologically distinct and intact fungal colonies were selected and sub-cultured three times. DNA was extracted by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), and the ITS region was amplified using ITS1F(5’-TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG-3’)/ITS4R (5’-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3’) primers, the PCR system and program were referenced from previous research [61]. The resulting ITS sequences were obtained via Sanger sequencing and compared against the NCBI database using BLAST to identify fungal species.

Using Foc TR4 as the target pathogen, four isolates each of Penicillium sp. (PA, PB, PC, and PD) and Trichoderma sp. (TA, TB, TC and TD) were selected for antagonistic assays. Both Foc TR4 and the test isolates were pre-cultured on PDA plates for 7 days, and 4 mm mycelial plugs were then prepared. A dual culture assay was conducted by placing Foc TR4 and test isolate plugs on opposite sides (2 cm from the edge) of the PDA plate. Plates were incubated at 28 °C for 7 days, after which the diameter of Foc TR4 colonies was measured. The inhibition rate was calculated using the formula:

Statistical analysis

Visual plotting was constructed using ggplot2 packages of R version 4.1.2 (https://mirrors.tuna.tsinghua.edu.cn/CRAN/) and GraphPad Prism 10. Alpha diversity indices were calculated by analyzing the Shannon diversity in vegan packages [67]. Differential analysis was conducted using One-Way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Spearman’s correlation coefficient. To assess the bacterial and fungal community’s beta-diversity, Bray-Curtis distance matrices were visualized and calculated using non-metric multi-dimensional scaling (NMDS) ordinations, along with permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) statistical tests in vegan packages. PERMANOVA was also implemented to test the impacts of the different root compartments and FWB resistance on bacterial and fungal community composition. SPSS Statistics 25 was used to analyze the differences in the relative abundance of phyla and genera between different groups obtained using One-Way ANOVA and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Genus-level biomarkers were also determined using Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis with default parameters, via the online interface Galaxy (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/lefse/) with P < 0.05 and LDA score >2. Marked OTUs were screened using the Deseq2 package with P < 0.05 and foldchange >2. The sequences were aligned using the NCBI Sequence Read Archive database and the phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA 9 by Neighbor-Joining. Co-occurrence patterns were reconstructed using Hmisc package and Gephi 0.9.2. A co-occurrence was considered to be robust with a Spearman’s correlation coefficient of >0.70 and P < 0.05 [30]. Biomass and disease indices were tested for differences between groups using SPSS 25 statistics to run the One-Way ANOVA.
