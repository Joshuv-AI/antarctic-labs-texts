Specification of Estimands for Complex Disease Processes Using Multistate Models and Utility Functions
Authors: Alexandra Bühler, Richard J. Cook, Jerald F. Lawless
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn complex diseases, individuals are often at risk of several types of possibly semi‐competing events and may experience recurrent symptomatic episodes. This complex disease course makes it challenging to define target estimands for clinical trials. While composite endpoints are routinely adopted, recent innovations involving the win ratio and other methods based on ranking the disease course have received considerable attention. We emphasize the usefulness of multistate models for addressing challenges arising in complex diseases, along with the simplicity and interpretability that come from defining utilities to synthesize evidence of treatment effects on different aspects of the disease process. Robust variance estimation based on the infinitesimal jackknife means that such methods can be used as the basis of primary analyses of clinical trials. We illustrate the use of utilities for the assessment of bleeding outcomes in a trial of cancer patients with thrombocytopenia.

Introduction

In many chronic disease processes, individuals are at risk of several types of potentially recurrent and terminal events. Individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [1] are at risk of recurrent exacerbations, hospitalization, and death. In cancer research [2], individuals are at risk of tumour progression, metastatic disease, and death, while also experiencing treatment‐related periods of toxicity [3]. Primary endpoints in clinical trials are often chosen based on the intended effect of treatment, but unintended effects on other important outcomes can arise, making it difficult to specify a single event on which to base primary analysis [4, 5]. In orthopedic studies, for example, anticoagulants given to reduce the risk of blood clots can increase the risk of heparin‐induced thrombocytopenia and bleeding [6]. We consider the broad setting involving clinical trials of complex disease processes, motivated in part by recent clinical [7] and statistical [8] work in cardiovascular (CV) clinical trials where patients are at risk of myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, hospitalization, CV‐related death, and death from other causes. In this setting, it is difficult (and perhaps inappropriate) to select a single event type on which to base primary analyses since interest typically lies in assessing the treatment effects on a variety of outcomes.

Use of composite endpoints is common in cardiovascular disease, cancer, and other areas of clinical research [9, 10]. For example, diabetic patients in the LEADER trial [7] may experience non‐fatal MI, non‐fatal stroke, and CV‐related death. An analysis of the time to a first stroke, MI, or CV death treats all components of the composite endpoint as of equal importance, but they clearly differ in severity, clinical relevance, and importance to patients. The main appeal of endpoints such as these is that they can be analyzed using standard methods [11] for failure time analysis. If some non‐fatal clinical events can recur and interest lies in a more comprehensive reflection of the disease burden, composite events involving the cumulative number of non‐fatal and fatal CV events have been proposed [8, 12]. Estimands and treatment effects based on composite outcomes can, however, be difficult to interpret, especially when treatment might affect separate types of events differentially [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. A more recent trend has been to use so‐called win ratio (WR) methods [18, 19], in which potential sample paths for individuals' observed disease processes are ranked from the most to the least favorable. Then all pairs of individuals comprised of persons i in the experimental arm and j in the control arm are examined, and the number of pairs (i,j) for which individual i has a more favorable sample path than individual j is determined. This number divided by the number of cases where the control individual j has the more favorable outcome is called the WR. It can be used as a basis for testing the hypothesis of no difference in the disease processes for treatment and control arms [20, 21, 22]. The desirability of outcome ranking (DOOR) approach [23, 24] is similar in spirit to the WR method in that a set of outcomes is defined based on key features of sample paths, incorporating information on survival, clinical benefit, and adverse events. The goal is to test which arm of a study is associated with better disease paths, defined in terms of a mapping from the (continuous) sample paths to a finite number of ordered categories. Follmann et al. [25] consider an alternative approach based on ordered composite endpoints in which the comparison of paths is based on an ordering score defined in terms of specific event times over the course of follow‐up. The WR, DOOR, and ordered composite endpoint approaches require explicit representation of the disease process, but ranking potential sample paths or defining an ordering score can be challenging. Moreover, merely ranking or ordering sample paths does not reflect the extent to which they differ.

Multistate models provide a powerful framework for studying complex disease processes and understanding treatment effects in randomized control trials (RCTs) [5]. Our objective in this paper is to propose their use for modeling possible sample paths, along with utility functions to synthesize evidence of treatment effects on the occurrence of specific events and times spent in different states. An appealing feature of our approach is the use of established methods for nonparametric estimation and hypothesis testing, which we describe here. In addition, secondary analyses based on multistate models and their intensity functions can give important insights concerning treatment effects [17]. This approach is somewhat similar to that of Mao [26] and Mao and Wang [27], who consider differences in the expected mean sojourn times in different health states, but rather than simply ranking states in terms of preferability (or favorability) we assign utilities that reflect their relative severity. An obvious issue with utility‐based analysis is the need to specify utility functions for the various states and events in the disease process [28]. We argue that when examining treatment effects on complex processes and weighing evidence of benefit or harm, judgments involve implicit utilities. Explicit specification of utilities increases transparency, and sensitivity analyses can be conducted to assess how varying particular utilities affect inferences. We illustrate this approach in an application involving a trial on the prevention of bleeding in patients with thrombocytopenia.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces multistate models for disease processes in RCTs, ways that one might compare their sample paths, and ways that utilities associated with process outcomes might be defined. Pros and cons of ranked sample path and outcome methods are briefly outlined in Section 2.3. Section 3 focuses on utility‐based estimands and develops estimation and testing based on cumulative utility scores. We describe simple nonparametric estimation methods with jackknife variance estimation and demonstrate their accuracy and robustness through numerical studies. An illustration for a platelet transfusion trial in chemotherapy‐induced thrombocytopenia is provided in Section 4. Section 5 concludes with some remarks on the specification of utilities and on the use of secondary analysis to deconstruct the effects of treatment on cumulative utility scores.

Multistate Processes, Ranked Sample Paths and Utility Functions

Multistate Processes and Basic Functionals

We consider disease processes that can be represented by a multistate model having state space 𝒮={0,1,…,K,K+1} with state 0 representing the state occupied at the time of randomization (e.g., often this will correspond to the condition of being alive and event‐free), and state K+1 is entered upon death. The intermediate transient states k=1,…,K represent non‐fatal disease conditions of varying severity. We assume states can be (partially) ordered in terms of “favorability” and let 𝒮k∈𝒮 represent the subset of states that are more favorable than state k, k=0,…,K,K+1. Often, the states are labeled according to their favorability, in which case 𝒮k={0,1,…,k−1}, but this is not necessarily the case.

Some illustrative multistate diagrams are given in Figure 1. Figure 1a is a progressive four‐state model used for the analysis of data from the IBCSG breast cancer trial [3] with state 0 occupied by individuals experiencing toxicity due to treatment, state 1 occupied when individuals are toxicity‐ and symptom‐free, state 2 entered upon relapse, and state 3 entered upon death; see Section 1.6.2 of Cook and Lawless [29]. The illness‐death model [30] in Figure 1b is relevant for oncology trials [31] where state 1 is entered upon cancer progression and the absorbing state 2 is entered upon death. Figure 1c depicts a model useful for neurological studies where states 1 and 2 may distinguish different degrees of functional ability due to the occurrence of mild or severe strokes [32]. Figure 1d characterizes recurrent and terminal event processes [33] where states k=0,…,K represent the cumulative number of recurrent non‐fatal events. In Figure 1a, individuals are always at risk of only a single type of event (progression to the next state), whereas in Figure 1b–d, competing risks arise because individuals may be at risk of transition into two states. We have restricted attention in Figure 1 to progressive processes in which each state can be entered at most once. For simplicity, we consider such processes below, but the methods are easily adapted to deal with more general processes. For example, by adding an arrow from state 2 to state 1 in Figure 1c, these states become recurrent. This multistate model can be used to study processes such as hospital admission and discharge in cardiovascular patients [34], or the onset and resolution of exacerbations in multiple sclerosis [35] and other disease processes [36] with a relapsing and remitting nature.

Overview of Some Common Ranking and Weighting Approaches

A variety of approaches have been proposed for quantifying the benefit of an experimental treatment over a control treatment for a complex disease process. We next discuss and critique some current approaches based on ranking sample paths or states of health in more detail.

Win Ratio

Suppose n individuals are randomized to receive either the experimental treatment or the control treatment. In what follows we relabel individuals and use subscript i for one of the n1 individuals assigned to the experimental treatment (i=1,…,n1) and j for those in the control arm (j=1,…,n0). Pocock et al. [18] proposed the win ratio (WR) method based on pairwise comparisons of treatment and control subjects' sample paths [43, 44]. All possible pairs (i,j) are considered and for each the sample paths (ℋi(τ),ℋj(τ)) over (0,τ] are compared. Using a pre‐specified rule, one member of the pair is declared as having the better outcome (i.e., one individual in the pair is declared the “winner”), or else a tie is declared. In illness‐death processes, for example, individuals are typically first compared based on time of death (later is better) and if no decision can be made on death (typically because both members of the pair survived to τ), individuals i and j are compared with respect to the presence and (if necessary) timing of the non‐fatal event. A classification rule under the WR approach can, in general, be represented as a mapping (2.3) ℛ(ℋi(τ),ℋj(τ))→ℛij(τ),τ>0 where ℛij(τ)=1 if individual i wins over individual j and ℛij(τ)=−1 if individual j wins over individual i. If the two sample paths are ranked equally (e.g., if both persons are in state 0 at time τ), then a tie is declared; in this case, we let ℛij(τ):=0. The WR is defined as the proportion of pairs where individual i wins over individual j divided by the proportion of pairs where j wins over i. In the absence of variable censoring times, this can be estimated by (2.4) θ˜WR(τ)=∑i=1n1∑j=1n01(Rij(τ)=1)∑i=1n1∑j=1n01(Rij(τ)=−1) Oakes [19] and Dong et al. [45] refer to (2.4) as the simple counting estimator for the WR. If all individuals are followed over [0,τ] (i.e., there are no premature dropouts), it can be shown that θ˜WR(τ) is consistent [46, 47] for the WR estimand (2.5) θWR(τ)=πij(1)(τ)πij(−1)(τ) where πij(r)(τ)=P(ℛij(τ)=r), r=−1,1. When follow‐up times are unequal, the ordinary counting approach cannot be implemented, and restriction to pairs (i,j) for which the classification rule ℛ can be applied yields biased estimates of the win probabilities πij(r)(τ) [48]. For the illness‐death model of Figure 1b, Dong et al. [48] developed an inverse probability of censoring weighted WR statistic to account for censoring, but as Parner and Overgaard [49] point out, their adjustment for the second worst outcome (i.e., the non‐fatal event) ignores the fact that no decision could have been made based on the worst outcome (i.e., death). A small simulation study in Section S1.2 of the Online Supporting Information confirms that this can lead to bias in the estimation of πij(r)(τ). Rather than using the counting approach, Oakes [19] noted that πij(r)(τ) can be estimated based on the marginal density function of the overall survival time and the (improper) conditional density for the non‐fatal event time given survival up to time τ. Parner and Overgaard [49] exploit the “integral” formulation of the win probability πij(r)(τ) to estimate it using univariate Kaplan–Meier estimates of survival functions and nonparametric estimates of bivariate survival functions [50].

Desirability of Outcome Ranking (DOOR)

Evans et al. [23] proposed an approach in which a pre‐specified number, say M, of mutually exclusive categories are defined based on possible outcomes of the disease process. These are arranged in order of increasing desirability and labeled here as 1,2,…,M, giving an ordinal score. Evans et al. [23] refer to this process as the desirability of outcome ranking (DOOR). In the context of trials of antibiotic therapies, categories have been defined based on survival status, “clinical benefit”, and the occurrence of adverse events [23]. In another infectious disease setting, Evans and Follmann [24] use three categories including survival with a minor adverse event, survival with a major adverse event, and death. Howard‐Andersen et al. [51] define categories based on the number of adverse events (e.g., alive with no events, alive with 1 event, alive with 2 events, alive with more than 3 events, and death). Of course, within this broad framework, the ordinal variable can be defined in a number of ways, with the choice for a particular trial depending on the nature of the disease process and values associated with different disease courses.

We describe the DOOR framework in the context of multistate processes where the ordinal score is defined at τ and denoted by D(τ). Here, the path of each individual i determines which of M categories they are assigned to; this is typically based on the occurrence of certain events or composite events in their respective histories ℋi(t). This can be represented by a mapping 𝒟(ℋi(τ))→Di(τ)∈{1,…,M},τ>0 with Di(τ)=M if individual i has the most favorable path and Di(τ)=1 if individual i has the least favorable path over [0,τ), i=1,…,n; often those observed to die will be assigned to the lowest category. The distribution of the DOOR scores is then compared between the treatment arms to investigate treatment effects. Typically, this is done based on an estimand characterizing a feature of interest. The probability that an individual from the experimental treatment arm has a higher score (i.e., a more favorable outcome) than an individual from the control group is [24] θDOOR(τ)=P(Di(τ)>Dj(τ)) with this estimand near 0.5 if there is no treatment effect on the distribution of the scores.

Follmann et al. [25] consider another type of ordering score in the context of a hypothetical CV trial in which patients may be at risk of bleeding, stroke, MI, and death from any cause. This approach can be adapted to more general settings and aims to rank individuals based on more specific criteria, incorporating the event times of individuals' least favorable outcome. We consider, for illustration, a trial setting where individuals may be at risk of K different types of non‐fatal events and death. To be consistent with Follmann et al.'s notation [25], we here assume states are labeled according to their favorability (i.e., 𝒮k={0,1,…,k−1}) and let the binary function Δik=1(Tik≤τ) indicate whether a type k=1,…,K event was observed for individual i before time τ; likewise we let Δi,K+1=1(Ti,K+1≤τ). Then with all individuals followed over [0,τ], the ordering score Oi(τ)=Ti,K+1Δi,K+1+(1−Δi,K+1)∑k=1K(kτ+Ti,K+1−k)Δi,K+1−k×∏1≤r<k(1−Δi,K+1−r)+(K+1)τ∏k=1K(1−Δi,K+1−k) ranges from 0 to (K+1)τ; this can thus be represented as a mapping 𝒪(ℋi(τ))→Oi(τ)∈[0,(K+1)τ] Individuals who are alive and event‐free at τ are assigned the highest ordering score of (K+1)τ, while individuals with a type k event as their least favorable outcome over [0,τ] receive a score in [(K+1−k)τ,((K+1)−(k−1))τ), k=1,…,K; within a given score category, longer event times are associated with a higher score. If, for example, two individuals i and j experience the same type k event, then Oi(τ)>Oj(τ) when Tki>Tkj. An ordering score in [0,τ) indicates death, with earlier deaths resulting in Oi(τ) being closer to zero. Different estimands can be formulated based on this ordering score, including the WR using ℛij(τ)=1(Oi(τ)>Oj(τ)) as the classification rule. With variable follow‐up times, the ordering score may involve multiple censoring intervals, but a nonparametric Turnbull estimate [52] can facilitate estimation of, for instance, the WR estimand [25].

Remarks on Methods Based on Ranking Paths or States

Composite endpoints implicitly value each component event equally, but in practice, they may vary greatly in importance.There are numerous examples of trials in which more innocuous but frequently occurring events are incorporated into a composite endpoint to “increase power” or make studies feasible, but a clear interpretation of associated estimands is challenging. The methods discussed in Section 2.2 differ in the importance they place on different features of possible sample paths.

The WR and DOOR approaches involve comparisons of individuals' sample paths based on value judgements about their preferability. Different types of events are ordered in a hierarchy, which is used for declaring the relative favorability of realized paths. A feature of the WR and DOOR approaches in their original presentation is that they rely on what Evans et al. [23] call a “longitudinal snapshot of the experience of each individual patient”. A limitation of the original WR approach is that the designation of the winner in pair (i,j) depends on τ and thus varies with τ [45, 53]. Individual i (from the experimental treatment group) may be declared the winner at τ0, for example, but at τ1 (τ0<τ1) this designation could change. This feature of simple counting estimators of the WR, which also applies to other similar “counting” estimators based on ranks (such as the DOOR), is mitigated by alternative approaches, which we discuss next.

Alternative approaches to estimating the WR estimand have been developed for particular processes [19, 49]. We can generalize this work by considering estimation of the WR estimand in terms of general multistate processes, which provides a natural way of dealing with variable censoring times or analysis times τ. In multistate model notation, individual i wins over j over (0,τ] if i and j occupy the same state at τ but j has entered the state earlier than i, or if i occupies a more favorable state at τ than j. That is, Rij(τ)=∑k=1K+11(Zi(τ)=k,Zj(τ)=k,Tjk<Tik)+∑k=0K+1∑l∈Sk1(Zi(τ)=l,Zj(τ)=k) where 𝒮k is the set of all states l∈𝒮∖{k} that are more favorable than state k. In illness‐death settings (i.e., K=1), and also in other processes, a straightforward approach is to use nonparametric Nelson–Aalen (NA) and Aalen–Johansen (AJ) estimates of the cumulative transition intensities and transition probabilities for each treatment group to obtain estimates of πij(r)(τ), r=−1,1, and thus of the win probabilities for a specific pair of individuals. Explicit expressions for P(Zi(τ)=k,Zj(τ)=k,Tjk<Tik), k=1,2, are given in Section S1.1 of the Online Supporting Information for illustration. A small simulation study in Section S1.2 shows that for the illness‐death process, the AJ estimation approach for win probabilities associated with ℛij(τ) provides estimates of the πij(r)(τ) with negligible empirical bias even when the censoring rate is quite substantial, whereas the ordinary “counting” approach based on (2.4) does not. Although the WR estimand based on ℛij(τ) can be estimated consistently for more general processes from right‐censored data [38], expressions such as P(Zi(τ)=k,Zj(τ)=k,Tjk<Tik) can become quite complicated for large |𝒮|, and estimability issues may arise. Progressive multistate models with relatively few states are, however, less problematic, as illustrated in Sections S1.1 and S1.2.

In addition, hierarchical ranking of sample paths becomes more challenging for more complex disease processes [54]. There is, for example, no consensus on how to rank sample paths in the recurrent and terminal event setting of Figure 1d. Mao et al. [21] used the “counting” formulation to construct the first‐event‐assisted and the last‐event‐assisted WR without addressing the censoring issue. Caution is thus warranted when using the R package WR [21]. In their approach, the n0n1 pairs of individuals (i,j) are initially compared with respect to death, followed by the cumulative number of non‐fatal events. If both individual i from the experimental group and individual j from the control group are alive at time τ with the same number of non‐fatal events experienced over [0,τ] (i.e., Ni(τ)=Nj(τ)=k), the first‐event assisted WR breaks that tie by taking the time to the first non‐fatal event as the basis for a third comparison; for the last‐event assisted WR, as the name suggests, the third comparison is with respect to the time of the last non‐fatal event. Parner and Overgaard [22] would declare that there is no definite winner, although individual j, for example, may have reached state k (and thus experienced k non‐fatal events) much earlier than individual i. The rule ℛ(ℋi(τ),ℋj(τ)) presented in this section takes this into account and breaks such ties by comparing the time to the kth non‐fatal event, which is similar to the last‐event assisted WR but the multistate formulation accounts for censoring.

Unlike the WR, which requires ranking of individuals' sample paths, estimation of the RMT‐IF [26] (2.7) (i.e., θMAO(τ)) involves ordering the states of a multistate process in terms of their relative favorability. This approach differs by emphasizing state occupancy rather than event times. This is simple for a two‐state survival process where the estimand corresponds to the difference in the restricted mean survival time [55], or for an illness‐death process with only one non‐fatal intermediate event (i.e., 𝒮0=∅,𝒮1={0} and 𝒮2={0,1}), but it becomes more complicated for other disease processes. In CV trials, for example, strokes and myocardial infarctions vary in their severity, and so it is difficult to declare prevention of one type of event as more favorable than another. Ideally, state spaces are defined in sufficient detail that they provide an adequate reflection and distinction of different individuals' health courses. Although (2.7) makes fuller use of individuals' trajectories than any WR method, it does not always provide a reasonable assessment of two sample paths ℋi(τ) and ℋj(τ). Specifically, the severity of different event types (or states) is only partially addressed in Equation (2.7). To illustrate this, assume individuals i and j experience illness‐death processes over [0,τ] with event times Ti1=7,Ti2>τ and Tj1=2,Tj2=5; this gives Vij(τ)=3+2+(τ−7) and Vji(τ)=0. A time unit [t,t+dt)∈[2,5) over which i is in state 0 and j in state 1 is equally treated as a time unit [t,t+dt)∈[7,τ) over which i is in state 1 and j in state 2; thus there is no distinction between 0 vs. 1, 0 vs. 2 or 1 vs. 2 comparisons. In other words, there is no further refinement of “better” or “worse”, which is a drawback of the RMT‐IF. In the next section, we propose general utility‐based methods as a preferable alternative to WRs and related ranking methods such as this.

Utility Functions

Introduction

Incorporation of utilities goes one step further than simply classifying paths with respect to their desirability or states in terms of their relative favorability; it does so by quantifying the extent to which one state is favored over another. Specifically, a numerical value uk(t)∈[0,1] is set for the utility of state k∈𝒮 at time t>0. If uk(t) is a measure of the quality of life associated with state k, typically the most favorable state would receive the highest utility (i.e., 1) with the absorbing state (K+1) corresponding to death having the lowest utility of zero. More generally, utility scores uk(t;ℋ(t)) that further depend on parts of the process history could be formulated; this is, however, beyond the scope of this paper. The specification of utilities can be challenging, but much work has been done in clinical research and health economics on how best to set these; we discuss this in Section 5.

Often it will be sufficient to consider time‐invariant state‐dependent utilities with uk(t)=uk, where ul>uk if state l is more favorable than state k (i.e., l∈𝒮k), and uk=ul if states l and k are equally desirable. We can then write the utility at time t as u(t)=∑k∈𝒮uk1(Z(t)=k). Empirical Studies of Finite Sample Properties

Simulation studies were conducted to examine the finite sample properties of var^IJK(θ^U(τ)) and associated tests for H0:θU(τ)≤0 vs. H1:θU(τ)>0 based on

(3.9) θ^U(τ)var^IJKθ^U(τ)=μ^(τ|X=1)−μ^(τ|X=0)var^IJK(μ^(τ|X=1))+var^IJK(μ^(τ|X=0)),τ>0 In the simulations that follow, we refer to (3.9) as the infinitesimal jackknife (IJK) test statistic. We consider the three‐state illness‐death model in Figure 1b for illustration.

Design of Simulation Studies

We generated data from Markov and mixed‐Markov illness‐death processes for n=100,250 and 1000 individuals, following the procedure in Section 2.5 of Cook and Lawless [29]. Individuals are randomized to one of the two treatment groups with probability 0.5, that is, X∼Bernoulli(0.5). For the Markov processes, we assume multiplicative transition intensities of the form λkl(t|X)=λkl0exp(γklX),(k,l)∈{(0,1),(0,2),(1,2)}; the intensities are thus time‐homogeneous Markov. Without loss of generality, we set the administrative censoring time to τ=1 and set λ010,λ020 and λ120 to satisfy the following constraints: (1) If T=min(T01,T02), πT=P(T≤τ|X=0)=0.6, so 40% of individuals in the control group are alive and event‐free (in state 0) at time τ, (2) πT1=P(T01<T02|T≤τ,X=0)=0.9, so 54% of individuals with X=0 are expected to experience the non‐fatal event of interest (i.e., pass through state 1) and 6% die event‐free (i.e., 0→2 transition), and (3) πD=p2(τ|X=0)=0.2, so 14% of individuals in the control group are expected to die post‐event (i.e., 1→2 transition) by time τ. We note that conditions (1)‐(3) imply that p1(τ|X=0)=0.40, subject to πD>πT(1−πT1) and πD<πT; if πD=πT(1−πT1), then no 1→2 transitions are observed. If πD=πT, then p1(τ|X=0)=0 and no one is expected to survive.

For the mixed‐Markov processes, we assume that k→l transitions occur according to the intensity

(3.10) λkl(t|H(t),W)=limΔt↓0P(Z(t+Δt−)=l|Z(t−)=k,H(t),W)Δt=Wλkl0exp(γklX) where ℋ(t)={Z(u):0<u<t,X} denotes the process history including X, and W is a random effect that follows a Gamma distribution with mean 𝔼(W)=1 and Var(W)=ϕ; the process is thus Markov given X and W. We let GW(w)=P(W≤w) denote the cumulative distribution function of W, with associated density dGW(w)=gW(w)dw. The resulting “marginal” intensity

(3.11) λkl(t|H(t))=𝔼Wλkl(t|H(t),U)|H(t)=λkl0exp(γklX)𝔼W(W|H(t−))=λkl0exp(γklX)P(Z(s),0<s<t|X)∫0∞wP(Z(s),0<s<t|X,w)dGW(w) does, however, depend on the process history, and so the marginal process characterized by (3.10) is not Markov. Plots of the marginal baseline intensities (3.11) for patients in the control group (X=0) are provided in Section S4 of the Online Supporting Information. We determined λ010,λ020 and λ120 so that (1) πT=P(T≤τ|X=0,W=1)=0.6, (2) πT1=P(T01<T02|T≤τ,X=0,W=1)=0.9, and (3) πD=p2(τ|X=0,W=1)=0.2, and set ϕ=0.5,0.8; thus the values for λ010,λ020 and λ120 are the same for Markov and mixed‐Markov processes.

We consider different treatment effect scenarios, ranging from no effect of treatment on any transition intensity (i.e., exp(γ01)=exp(γ02)=exp(γ12)=1) to treatment effects on all three intensities (e.g., exp(γ01)=0.75,exp(γ02)=0.9,exp(γ12)=0.9). We set the utility scores for states k=0,1 and 2 to u0=1,u1=0.5,u2=0. Figures S3 and S4 of the Online Supporting Information contain plots of pk(t|X=0) (top row), pk(t|X=1) (middle row) and μ(t|X),X=0,1 (bottom row) over [0,τ] under the Markov and mixed‐Markov models for two particular treatment effect scenarios, k=0,1,2. To accommodate early withdrawal from study we set the censoring intensity to λr(t|X)=λrexp(γrX), which corresponds to generating the right‐censoring time Cr|X from an exponential distribution with rate λreγrX; we solve for λr to ensure πr=P(Cr≤τ|X=0)=0.2 or 0.4, and set exp(γr)=1 or 0.85. For each data‐generating process and parameter setting, we considered estimation of θU(τ) by nonparametric AJ methods and conducted the test based on (3.9) with the IJK procedure of Section Appendix A adopted for variance estimation of θ^U(τ); the survfit(…, influence=TRUE) function provides the AJ estimates of p^k(t|X) as well as the influence matrices 𝒥^k=[I^ik(t(s))] for each treatment group [42], based on which var^IJK(μ^(τ|X)) can be calculated using (A8); see also Section S3 of the Online Supporting Information. We report on the difference between the average treatment effect estimates and the true θU(τ) (BIAS), the empirical standard error (ESE) of estimates, the average standard error computed by the infinitesimal jackknife (ASE), the empirical coverage probability (ECP) for θU(τ) of the nominal one‐sided 95% confidence intervals, and the empirical rejection rate (RR) for the right‐tail test based on (3.9) for H0:θU(τ)≤0 of nominal size ω=0.05. The last performance measure was calculated as the proportion of nsim=10000 simulations in which the IJK test rejected H0 at the nominal ω=5% level. With this many simulations, if the test rejects the null hypothesis at the nominal ω level, the empirical rejection rate is expected to lie in [0.0457,0.0543] for 95% of the samples.

Assessing the Burden of Bleeding in Thrombocytopenia

Platelet transfusions are routinely used for the prevention and treatment of bleeding in thrombocytopenic patients, and Pathogen‐inactivated platelet products are designed to reduce the risk of transmitting undesirable viral and bacterial pathogens to recipients. The process of treating the platelets for pathogen inactivation can reduce the effective number of healthy platelets, thereby reducing the effect of a given volume of platelet product. We consider data from the multi‐center randomized non‐inferiority trial of oncology patients with thrombocytopenia designed to assess whether platelets treated for pathogen inactivation by the Mirasol system are noninferior to standard platelets in the prevention of bleeding [56, 57]. Patients were eligible for recruitment if they were expected to require at least 2 transfusions during a follow‐up period of 42 days. Recruitment took place between November 2010 and April 2016 from 10 different hemato‐oncology centers in the Netherlands, Norway, and Canada, with randomization stratified by center. A total of 127 patients were recruited for the Canadian sites and randomized to receive either standard (plasma stored) platelets (arm A) or the (Mirasol‐treated) pathogen‐reduced (PR) platelets (arm B). We excluded 16 individuals who were bleeding at the time of randomization, leaving n=52 individuals in arm A and n=59 in arm B, respectively, in the analyses that follow. Bleeding status was assessed daily using the World Health Organization (WHO) 4‐point bleeding scale [58, 59] in which grade 0 represents no bleeding, grade 1 represents minor complications such as oropharyngeal bleeding or epistaxis lasting less than 30 min within 24 h, and grades 2 to 4 represent more serious bleeding of increasing clinical importance. Although a grade ≥2 is typically considered clinically relevant, grades 3 and 4 represent the most severe complications. A bleed requiring red cell transfusions would receive a WHO grade 3, for example, whereas central nervous system bleeds and fatal bleeds would be designated as WHO grade 4. The primary analysis was based on the proportion of days with ≥2 WHO grade bleeding over [0,42] [56, 57].

A more nuanced utility‐based analysis could be appealing, however, if it can incorporate the timing of bleeding complications and occurrences of other relevant outcomes (e.g., platelet recovery, death), while recognizing that bleeds differ in their severity. Figure 2 depicts a four‐state model that can be used to this end, with state 0 representing at most grade 1 bleeding (i.e., no bleeding or grade 1 bleeding), and states 1 to 3 entered upon the occurrence of grade 2 bleeds, grade 3–4 bleeds, and death, respectively. Because individuals with grade ≥2 bleeds at the time of randomization were excluded from the primary analysis [56], state 0 is the initial state occupied by patients at t=0. We consider the multistate process in continuous time, but bleeding scores are determined from daily clinical assessments. Follow‐up for some patients ended before administrative censoring due to platelet recovery, non‐compliance, or serious adverse transfusion reactions or immunological platelet refractoriness. Patients who recovered from thrombocytopenia before the administrative end of the study were assumed to occupy state 0 (i.e., experience no bleeding complications) for the remainder of the study, while patients who withdraw prematurely from the study for any of the other reasons were censored.

We reanalyse the PREPAReS trial data based on the four‐state model of Figure 2 with the utility score uk∈[0,1] assigned to state k=0,1,2,3. States associated with less severe bleeding are given higher utilities, so based on the 4‐point WHO bleeding scale, we adopt the constraints 1=u0>u1>u2>u3=0. We base the estimand on the following measure of the burden due to bleeding: μ(t)=∑k=03uk∫0tpk(s)ds,0≤t≤42 in which case μ(t)∈(0,t) with higher values of μ(t) more desirable. Note that μ(t) takes its maximum value t only if an individual stays in state 0 on all days over (0,t] (i.e., μ(t)=t for u0=1). We consider a range of utility scores for illustration.

Because pathogen inactivation reduces the risk of bacteria and transfusion‐transmitted infections, some loss of efficacy on bleeding in arm B is tolerated. If X=1 indicates assignment to arm B (pathogen‐reduced platelets) and X=0 for those in arm A (receiving standard platelets), we let μ(t|X=1) and μ(t|X=0) denote the mean cumulative utilities to time t and define θU(τ)=μ(τ|X=1)−μ(τ|X=0) with τ=42 days. We aim to test for non‐inferiority using the hypotheses

That is, under H0, there can be an appreciable loss in the cumulative mean utility from use of pathogen‐reduced platelets compared to standard platelets, but this loss is no greater than δ under the alternative hypothesis where pathogen‐reduced platelets are non‐inferior. Using the confidence interval approach [60] to testing for non‐inferiority, the null hypothesis H0 is rejected if the lower limit of the one‐sided (1−ω)% CI for θU(τ), given by θ^U(τ)−z1−ωvar^(θ^U(τ)),42, is greater than −δ. For this illustrative analysis we define the margin based on 20% of μ(τ|X=0), as often done in non‐inferiority trials [61]. If μ(42|X=0)=40 is the anticipated cumulative mean utility score at τ=42 days in arm A, the non‐inferiority margin is δ=40−32=8 since 32=0.8·40; hence, a pathogen‐reduced platelets will be considered non‐inferior to standard platelets according to H1 provided θU(τ)≥−8.

Discussion

Many approaches have been proposed for analyzing clinical trials involving multiple types of semi‐competing events and disease states. Analyses based on composite outcomes are very common, but they implicitly treat component events as of equal importance, and the interpretation of resultant estimands can be challenging [14]. The win ratio requires investigators to define the relative preferability of different disease paths to identify “winners” in pairwise comparisons [18, 19]. In contrast, the desirability of outcome ranking (DOOR) approach defines an ordinal score based on disease paths and compares the distribution of scores between two arms of a study [23, 24]. Ordering scores, which make more effective use of event times, may also be considered [25]. Mao [27] considers the mean sojourn time in different states of a multistate process and developed tests aiming to detect treatments that lead to a greater proportion of time in preferred states; this, therefore, involves ranking states (rather than disease courses) in terms of their preferability.

Utilities require one to state how much more (or less) preferable each state is to the others, which requires another level of specification. The quantification of the relative value of states enables one to synthesize data across individuals in the same treatment arm, and to estimate the associated estimand characterizing the treatment effect. The underlying multistate formulation also means that supplementary analyses of cumulative transition intensities and state occupancy probabilities can provide much‐needed understanding about how any effects arise; see also Appendix B. An alternative approach to utility‐based analyses is to calculate individual‐specific utility scores and base analyses on these; [40] this requires the use of inverse probability of censoring weights to accommodate variable censoring times. This alternative framework may make it easier to incorporate the impact of “point events” on the total utility, such as instantaneous costs or other kinds of responses that are not easily embedded into a multistate model. This represents an area of ongoing research.

While utilities can simplify scientific questions and analyses, no single method can be best for all situations—the pros and cons of each will depend on the context in which they are used. Moreover challenges can arise when adopting utilities [28]. Utilities are inherently subjective and may be undesirable for assessing the effects of experimental treatments in clinical trials. We note, however, that utilities are often implicit in many analyses that are now routinely conducted. As noted earlier, progression‐free survival and other composite time‐to‐event responses equally weight the component outcomes in terms of importance. Moreover, when examining evidence of treatment effects across multiple endpoints in marginal analyses, one will often have in mind implicit but unstated utilities when weighing the results to make a decision about the clinical importance of an experimental treatment. The specification of utilities can avoid ambiguity and increase transparency in regulatory decision‐making. There is a large literature on methodology for specification of health utilities, with much of it in the quality of life [62] and health economics literature [63, 64, 65, 66, 67, 68]. Many of these methods (e.g., the basic rating scale [64], standard gamble [69, 70] or time‐trade off [70]) can lead to individual‐specific utilities; clinicians may likewise assign different values to different states of health for different individuals. For these reasons, it can be challenging to define state‐specific utilities that will be widely accepted. Sensitivity analyses may be important to conduct to examine how particular utility values can influence study findings; see, for instance, Glasziou et al. [62] and Revicki et al. [66].

Extensive simulation studies suggest that the infinitesimal jackknife (IJK) variance estimation procedure yields remarkably accurate standard errors for samples of n=100 (yielding an average of n=50 individuals per arm) under a variety of data‐generating processes and modest rates of right‐censoring; excellent control of the type I error rate for two‐sample tests is likewise apparent. When censoring rates are higher or sample sizes are even smaller, there may be a slight inflation of the type I error rate—further investigations into the performance of the IJK procedure are warranted in such settings. For very small samples, one may consider the use of permutation tests based on the statistic in (3.4) or (3.5); computation of the IJK variance estimates is fast, so both are viable discrepancy measures.

Utilities will need to be specified at the planning stage of a clinical trial, along with a model for the disease process. To ensure adequate study power, we recommend a simulation‐based approach for sample size estimation when tests are based on (3.9). This requires specification of how the experimental treatment affects transition intensities. Having specified the process through the intensities, one can simulate sample paths for studies of increasing sample size until adequate study power is achieved; historical data can be helpful to guide the specification of some parameters in this exercise. Adaptive sample size calculations based on internal pilot studies offer another approach for dealing with uncertainty at the planning stage and are an area of ongoing research [71].
