Potential role of bile acids as a microbiome-derived mechanism in synovitis of knee osteoarthritis synovitis
Authors: Jessica D. Murillo-Saich, Helena Mannochio-Russo, Marta Sala-Climent, Nathan Argel, Anna Quan, Michal Kalli Hose, Rocio Paz-Gonzalez, Meghana Akkati, Eric Chang, Adnan Cutuk, Emily Gentry, Roxana Coras, Nancy E. Lane, Pieter C. Dorrestein, Monica Guma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:To evaluate the relationship between bile acids (BAs) and synovitis in knee osteoarthritis (KOA).Methods:Radiographic KOA patients with complete datasets were included. WOMAC total and subscores were calculated. Synovitis was assessed by ultrasound or Krenn score. BAs were profiled in plasma (N=28) or synovial fluid (SF, N=29) using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. OA synovial explants, OA fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS), and bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) were used for in vitro experiments. Data analysis was performed using R and MetaboAnalyst.Results:Sixteen KOA participants had low-grade (0–1) and twelve had high-grade synovitis (2–3). Glycohyodeoxycholic acid (1.198 ± 0.983 vs. 1.954 ± 0.686, 0.76[95% CI: 0.04–1.89]) and lithocholic acid (0.19 ± 0.53 vs. 0.825 ± 0.866, 0.63[95% CI: 0.00–1.58]) were elevated in subjects with high-grade synovitis. LPS-binding protein (LBP) (rho = 0.58, p = 0.037, [95% CI: −0.807–0.46]) correlated with synovitis but only in obese participants (BMI ≥ 30). LBP, lithocholic acid, and glycohyodeoxycholic acid predicted high synovitis (92% sensitivity, 75% specificity, AUC = 0.875[95% CI: 0.99–1.05], p < 0.001). In SF, taurodeoxycholic and glycohyodeoxycholic acids correlated positively with WOMAC pain and stiffness subscores and the total WOMAC score. The BA receptors, TGR5 and LXR, were present in synovial tissue. In vitro, BAs reduced cytokine secretion in FLS and BMDM.Conclusion:Detection of BAs and their receptors in synovial tissue, together with their modulatory effects on synovial cells, supports a potential biological role for BAs in KOA.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) involves progressive cartilage degeneration, leading to joint dysfunction and disability [1]. Local and systemic inflammation has emerged as a key factor in understanding KOA [2]. Studies have revealed that synovitis is present in up to 46% of symptomatic KOA cases [3], and can precede radiographic changes, correlating with increased knee pain and disease progression. Obesity is also associated with KOA [4] and contributes to cartilage imbalance [5]. This suggests that both systemic and local inflammatory factors drive KOA progression.

Growing interest in the microbiome’s role in osteoarthritis (OA) stems from its links to diseases like cancer [6,7] and autoimmunity [8,9]. In OA, elevated plasma levels of microbial products such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and LPS-binding protein (LBP) are associated with synovial macrophage infiltration in the synovia and joint capsule, and with osteophyte severity [10]. Emerging evidence also suggests the microbiome influences OA synovitis through immune system modulation [11–14].

Bioactive lipid mediators, including bile acids (BAs), regulate inflammation and the transition from acute to chronic inflammatory states [15]. Synthesized in the liver and modified by the gut microbiome, BAs influence mucosal immune homeostasis[16] and intestinal permeability [17]. Beyond the gut, BAs circulate in blood and act on innate immune receptors, modulating various metabolic pathways [17–20]. This study examined the association between circulating BAs and synovitis in KOA using mass spectrometry and assessed the in vitro effects of BAs on synovial cells.

Methods

Participants

The study was approved by the local institutional review board (IRB: H170083 and H170130) at San Diego Veterans Affairs (VA) Hospital. Twenty-eight consecutive patients diagnosed with radiographic KOA defined by Kellgren and Lawrence (KL) of 2 or greater score were recruited in our study, and a thorough clinical examination was conducted. Exclusion criteria included knee injection or changes in systemic treatment within the past 3 months, have KL < 2, and the presence of other autoimmune diseases or end stage renal or liver disease. Ultrasound examination (US) was performed for effusion, synovial hypertrophy, popliteal cysts, and Power Doppler (PD) signal within the synovium. Synovitis was defined as the presence of synovial hypertrophy and/or effusion and scored from 0 to 3, according to the OMERACT guidelines [21]. Participants were stratified by degree of synovitis in low-grade (scores 0 and 1) and high-grade (scores 2 and 3). Participants completed the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) questionnaire and the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain. The WOMAC includes 24 items divided into three domains: pain (5 items), stiffness (2 items), and physical function (17 items). Each item is scored from 0 (none) to 4 (extreme), and subscores are calculated by summing the items within each domain. The WOMAC total score is obtained by adding the three subscores, yielding a range of 0–96, with higher scores indicating worse symptoms. Additionally, previously collected twenty-nine synovial fluid (SF) samples from OA patients who underwent a total knee arthroscopy (TKA) were included to evaluate the BA profiling. All the patients signed and kept a copy of the informed consent. No missing data were observed. More details are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1 and supplementary data.

Bile acid extraction, detection, quantification and other methods are provided in supplementary data.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis are provided in supplementary data.

Statistical analysis

The study design ensured complete data collection, and quality control procedures minimized the risk of missing data. No missing values were observed; therefore, no imputation was required. All analyses were conducted under the assumption that data were ’Missing Completely At Random’ (MCAR). Sample size estimation was performed using the “MetSizeR” package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/MetSizeR/MetSizeR.pdf) in R Studio [22], which accounts for multiple testing corrections, inter-metabolite correlation and high variability across features The estimation parameters included 1854 bins, a proportion of significant bins of 0.2, one covariate, probabilistic principal component and covariates analysis (PPCCA) as the model, a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.10, and an untargeted analysis framework. To achieve 80% power with a 10% FDR, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 24. The filtered quantification table (.csv) file exported from MZmine3 [23] was annotated with synovitis grade for each blood plasma sample and uploaded to the Metaboanalyst 5.0 platform [24] for downstream analysis. For multivariate analysis, the samples were normalized by sum, log (base 10) transformed, and range scaled (mean-centered and divided by the range of each variable). Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) multivariate analyses were performed for supervised multivariate discrimination and dimensionality reduction, and discriminatory features between high- and low-grade synovitis were identified based on variable importance in projection (VIP) scores. Univariate analyses were conducted in MetaboAnalyst to calculate fold changes in relative ion feature abundances between groups. Normality of metabolite variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated non-normal distributions. Standardized mean differences were calculated for cross-sectional comparisons, and between-group differences were assessed using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test. Group comparisons were reported with corresponding differences and 95% confidence intervals (CI). To adjust for non-normality and potential confounding by age and sex, rank-based inverse normal transformation was applied prior to linear regression analyses. Spearman correlation analyses adjusted for age and sex were conducted, with 95% CIs derived from 1000 bootstrap resamples using RStudio. To identify metabolite predictors of synovitis, feature selection was performed using the Boruta algorithm package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/Boruta/Boruta.pdf) with100 iterations and adjusted by age, BMI, and LBP using scaled data. Statistical analysie of the in vitro experiments assessing the effects of BAs on synovial cells were performed with Prism software (version 5; GraphPad). Results are reported as means with 95% CI. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05. Given the exploratory nature of the study, P-values were reported without adjustment for multiple testing. Based on Tables ST5 and ST9, between approximately 280 and 1800 statistical tests were conducted.

Results

Participant characteristics and clinical differences between high and low synovitis

A total of 28 osteoarthritis (OA) patients were enrolled, with an average age of 65 years old. Among them, 89% were male, and 46.4% were obese, with an average BMI of 30.89 ± 4.94. Comorbidities included diabetes mellitus (14.3%), high blood pressure (71.4%), and dyslipidemia (65.4%). Out of the 28 participants, 17 (60.7%) exhibited synovitis, with 12 (42%) of these cases demonstrating high-grade synovial inflammation. The participants reported an average total Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) score of 50.75 ± 18.34, including subscale scores of 10.52 ± 3.98 for pain, 4.64 ± 1.48 for stiffness, and 35.59 ± 13.70 for physical function. To conduct subsequent analyses, we divided our participants into two groups: low-grade synovitis (n = 16) and high-grade synovitis (n = 12). We initially compared the clinical characteristics between grades of synovitis. No significant differences were observed in BMI, age, or comorbidities between both groups. There was a tendency for higher pain in high-grade synovitis as assessed by the visual analog scale pain questionnaire (5.29 ± 2.40 vs. 6.89 ± 2.12, p = 0.09, 1.60[95% CI: −0.30–3.60]) but WOMAC components showed no significant variation between high and low-grade synovitis (Table 1).

Metabolome profiles between high and low-grade synovitis

We conducted untargeted metabolomics to compare the two inflammation groups resulting in 277 features for further analysis (ST1). The supervised PLS-DA analysis revealed a distinct global metabolome between the high-grade and low-grade synovitis in the plasma of OA participants, with a slight overlap between them, (Supplementary Fig. S2A). VIP (Variable Importance in Projection) scores were computed to examine the ion features that exert the greatest influence on group separation in the PLS-DA. (Supplementary Fig. S2B) and the relative abundance in fold change (Supplementary Fig. S2C).

To further annotate the dataset, we applied Feature-Based Molecular Networking [25] via the GNPS platform [26], enabling visualization and spectral library searches. This resulted in 55 library matches (17.7% of the chemical space), which were manually curated and assigned level 2 annotations per Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) guidelines [27]. Annotations are detailed in ST1. Of these, 22 were bile acid matches, including adducts: 9 glycine-conjugated, 3 taurine-conjugated, 5 unconjugated, and 5 with unknown conjugation status [28].

Annotated library matches revealed conserved metabolomic differences between high- and low-grade synovitis (Fig. 1A–C). Of the 55 matches, 15 features with higher VIP scores were highlighted (Fig. 1B), including six bile acids (BAs) elevated in high-grade synovitis. Other metabolites, such as tyrosine, were more abundant in low-grade synovitis. To confirm BA presence, commercial standards were analyzed using the same LC-MS/MS method (ST2). Retention times and MS/MS spectra confirmed one unconjugated BA (deoxycholic acid) and five conjugated BAs: taurocholic acid, taurohyodeoxycholic acid, glycocholic acid, glycohyodeoxycholic acid, and glycochenodeoxycholic acid (ST3).

MS/MS data were visualized using molecular networking, which groups compounds with similar fragmentation patterns into molecular families. In these networks, nodes represent MS/MS spectra and edges reflect cosine similarity between them [29]. The full network, including pie charts comparing relative abundances in high- vs. low-grade synovitis, is shown in Supplementary Fig. S3.

To compare metabolite abundances between high- and low-grade synovitis, we calculated log2 fold changes and mapped them onto the molecular network (Fig. 1D). The largest network contained numerous matches to conjugated BAs. Taurocholic acid was more abundant in low-grade synovitis, while taurohyodeoxycholic acid and glycohyodeoxycholic acid were higher in high-grade synovitis. Among these, only glycohyodeoxycholic acid showed a statistically significant difference between groups (Fig. 1D). In addition, glycohyodeoxycholic acid (1.198 ± 0.983 vs. 1.954 ± 0.686, p = 0.029, 0.76[95% CI: 0.04–1.89]) and its in-source fragment [M-H2O+H]+ (2.453 ± 0.271 vs. 2.719 ± 0.372, p = 0.07, 0.27[95% CI: −0.02–0.58]) and [M-2-H2O+H]+ (2.998 ± 0.287 VS 3.205 ± 0.329, p=0.14, 0.21[95% CI: −0.06–0.49]) as well as lithocholic acid (0.19 ± 0.53 vs 0.825 ± 0.866, p= 0.021, 0.63[95% CI: 0.00–1.58]) were higher in the high-grade synovitis group (ST4). Other inflammatory biomarkers such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF), interleukine-6 (IL-6) and lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP) were quantified in plasma. LBP showed higher levels in the high-grade synovitis group compared to the low-grade synovitis group (104.84 ± 44.12 vs. 147.83 ± 39.88, p= 0.03, 42.99 [95% CI: 2.35–79.88]) (ST4).

Associations between annotated bile acids and clinical variables

Spearman correlations were assessed between annotated bile acids (BAs) and clinical variables (synovitis, obesity, LBP and pain) (ST5; Fig. 2). Among the compounds with library hits, we can observe that glycohyodeoxycholic acid [M+H]+ (features 3741; rho = 0.431, p = 0.028, [95% CI: 0.01–0.725]), and lithocholic acid (feature 3782; rho= 0.430, p =0.029, [95% CI: −0.002–0.558]) have a positive correlation with synovitis score. Interestingly, LBP showed a positive correlation with stercobilin (feature 2855; rho= 0.451, p= 0.024 [95% CI: 0.06–0.746]). WOMAC components showed a positive trend-level correlation between lithocholic acid (feature 3782; rho= 0.380, p= 0.055, [95% CI: −0.001–0.41]) and candidate dehydroxylated BA(feature 4032; rho= 0.350, p= 0.080, [95% CI: −0.154–0.698]) with WOMAC stiffness, and between glycine dehydroxylated candidate (feature 3199) and WOMAC pain (rho= 0.376, p= 0.058, [95% CI: 0.003–0.669]). (ST5 and Fig. 2).

Given prior evidence linking obesity to low-grade systemic inflammation and the pathogenesis of knee osteoarthritis [4,5], as well as findings that obesity alters the inflammatory landscape of synovial cells [30], we examined obesity’s impact on metabolomic profile. Among 28 participants, 13 were obese (BMI ≥ 30; 35.46 ± 3.03) and 15 non-obese (BMI < 30; 26.93 ± 1.69), including 14 overweight and 1 normal-weight individual. Metabolomic analysis revealed lower levels of taurohyodeoxycholic acid in the obese group (feature 3419; 0.88 ± 1.02 vs. 1.57 ± 1.02, p = 0.057; −0.64[95% CI: −1.92–0.00]) (ST6). When stratified by BMI, glychohyodeoxycholic acid (feature 3742) was lower in the high-grade synovitis (2.46 ± 0.39 vs 2.82 ± 0.37, p=0.09; 0.355[95% CI: −0.11–0.82]) only in the non-obese subgroup (ST7) while none of the BA showed differences in synovitis in the obese group (ST8).

Subsequently, we explored the correlation between clinical variables and annotated BAs in both non-obese and obese groups (Fig. 3A–B and ST9). In the obese group, there was a positive correlation between synovitis grade and glycohyodeoxycholic acid (feature 3741; rho = 0.52, p= 0.067, [95% CI: 0–0.588]) and the candidate glycine-trihydroxylated BA (feature 2810; rho = 0.49, p= 0.086, [95% CI: −0.056–0.604]). Although no correlation between LBP and bile acids was found in either the non-obese or the obese group, synovitis correlated positively with LBP only in the obese group (rho = 0.58, p = 0.04, [95% CI: −0.807–0.46]) (Fig. 3C–D). There was also a tendency of higher LBP and higher BMI (rho = 0.48, p = 0.10, [95% CI: −0.161–0.944]) only in the obese group as well (Fig. 3 and ST10).

LBP and circulating bile acids as predictors of high-grade synovitis

A random forest classifier was constructed to assess whether the matched features of bile acids serve as predictors for high-grade synovitis, adjusted for age and BMI, utilizing the Boruta algorithm. The feature selection outcomes are detailed in ST11. Among the 19 features incorporated into the model, 16 were rejected. LBP and glycohyodeoxycholic acid emerged as a tentative feature for prediction while lithocholic acid was the only confirmed feature of prediction in the model. Moreover, the Norm Hits represent the instances in which LBP and glycohyodeoxycholic acid were deemed more crucial than the mean shadow across the Random Forest runs (Fig. 4A).

To evaluate the predictive capability of plasma levels of glycohyodeoxycholic acid and lithocholic acid for high-grade synovitis, we utilized the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) of the Random Forest model (Fig. 4B–C). The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.82, indicating a sensitivity of 66% and specificity of 88% (Fig. 4B). Given the significant association between LBP and synovitis and considering LBP as a tentative predictor in the obese group (Table ST11), we incorporated LBP into the model. This adjustment led to an improved AUC of 0.96, increasing sensitivity to 92%, although with a decrease in specificity to 82% [95% CI: 0.99–1.05]. Overall, this enhancement contributed to the overall improvement of the model (Fig. 4C).

Bile acids are also identified in synovial fluid and correlate with WOMAC scores

We also analyzed 29 previously collected synovial fluid (SF) samples from a different cohort of osteoarthritis (OA) patients who underwent total knee replacement (ST 12). Using LC-MS/MS, the Bas identified in SF included taurocholic acid, glycocholic acid, glycohyodeoxycholic acid, cholic acid, taurochenodeoxycholic acid, glycochenodeoxycholic acid, and glycodeoxycholic acid.

We used the Krenn score to classify 17 samples as low (0–4) synovitis and 12 as high [4–9] synovitis. Glycocholic acid levels were slightly elevated in the high synovitis group compared to the low synovitis group (4.29 ± 0.579 vs. 4.52 ± 0.45, p = 0.059, 0.237[95% CI: −0.65–0.05]) (ST13).

Interestingly, taurodeoxycholic acid levels were elevated in the high WOMAC pain group, while glycine-conjugated bile acids (BAs) were reduced in this group, except for glycohyodeoxycholic acid. (Fig. 5A and B). Correlation analysis revealed that glycohyodeoxycholic acid correlated positively with WOMAC pain, WOMAC stiffness, and WOMAC total scores, while glycocholic acid show a positive trend-level correlation with Krenn score. (Fig. 5C and ST14). In the obese subgroup (Fig. 5D and E, and ST15–16) WOMAC parameters correlated positively with glycohyodeoxycholic acid, though the correlation with taurodeoxycholic acid was no longer significant (Fig. 5E and ST16). Furthermore, synovitis correlated with glycodeoxycholic acid and glycocholic acid only in the obese group (Fig. 5E and ST16).

BA receptors are present in the OA synovial tissue

Since BAs reach the synovial joint and are present in synovial fluid, we sought to investigate whether BA receptors are expressed in synovial tissue. Using publicly available data from AMP Phase II [31], which included a cohort of 9 OA patients, we evaluated the expression of reported BA receptors in the dataset [32,33]. We found that, that aside from TGR5 (GPBAR1), LXR-α (NR1H3), and LXR-β (NR1H2), FXR (NH1H4), PXR (NR1I2) and CAR(NR1I3) showed minimal expression across cell types (Fig. 6A) and were therefore not further analyzed. Our analysis revealed that TGR5 (GPBAR1) is expressed at low levels in stromal cells and predominantly in myeloid cells, LXR-α (NR1H3) is expressed in both stromal cells, including fibroblasts, and myeloid cells, while LXR-β (NR1H2) is expressed more broadly (Fig. 6A). Since NR1H2 expression is ubiquitous, we chose to focus on GPBAR1 and NR1H3, excluding NR1H2 from further analysis. Given that distinct subtypes of fibroblasts and monocytes exhibit functional differences in human synovial tissue, we utilized the subcluster annotations from the AMP Phase II metadata to examine the expression patterns of GPBAR1and NR1H3 (Supplementary Fig. S4). IHC confirmed their expression in synovial tissue of both LXR and TGR5 (Fig. 6B), though expression varied among donors (Supplementary Fig. 5).

To investigate the potential role of bile acids (BAs) in modulating proinflammatory cytokine expression within the synovium, we measured cytokine and chemokine levels in OA synovial explants stimulated with TNF in the presence of select BAs identified as significant in our earlier analysis—, glycochenodeoxycholic acid (GCDCA), glycohyodeoxycholic acid (GHDCA), or glycodeoxycholic acid (GDCA). Notably, CCL2 levels decreased after BA treatment specially with GHDCA (1 ± 0.0 vs 0.565 ± 0.245, p = 0.048, −0.435[95% CI: 0.176–0.954]), and a similar reduction was observed for CXCL2 following GCDCA treatment (1 ± 0.0 vs.0.424 ± 0.201, p = 0.08, −0.572[95% CI: 0.105–0.743]) (Fig. 6C).

Anti-inflammatory effect of BA in OA fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) and murine bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDM)

Since TGR5 and LXR were expressed in synovial stromal and myeloid cells, we segregated fibroblasts and monocytes into double-negative (0 reads for GPBAR1 and 0 reads for NR1H3), double-positive (> 0 reads for GPBAR1 and > 0 reads for NR1H3), TGR5-positive (> 0 GPBAR1 reads), and LXR-positive (> 0 NR1H3 reads) and verified this segregation by examining gene expression of GPBAR1 and NR1H3 (Fig. 7A). Transcriptionally, we observed that in fibroblasts, several genes including CD55 and MMP3 were mainly associated with double-positive cells. Interestingly, collagen-encoding genes showed higher average expression in the negative cells (Supplementary Fig. S6A, left). In monocytes, double-positive cells show similar expression of CD68, CD163, MMP2 and MMP14 as LXR-positive cells. (Supplementary Fig. S6A, right).

To investigate differences among the four fibroblast and monocyte groups, we performed differential gene expression (DEG) analysis and visualized the results in a heatmap (Fig. 7B). Double-positive cells exhibited a distinct transcriptional signature. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of fibroblast DEGs (Fig. 7C) revealed that TGR5-positive fibroblasts were enriched in pathways related to smooth muscle contraction, contractile fiber formation, and regulation of transmembrane transporter activity. Conversely, LXR-positive fibroblasts showed enrichment in mesenchymal cell differentiation, steroid response element binding, and positive regulation of chemotaxis. In monocytes, double-positive and LXR-positive cells were significantly enriched in lysosomal, granule-related, and inflammatory pathways, while TGR5-positive monocytes were enriched in phagocytosis and modestly in leukocyte migration and chemotaxis (Fig. 7C, Supplementary Fig. S6B).

To explore the potential role of BAs in modulating proinflammatory cytokine expression in OA FLS and murine BMDM, we assessed cytokine levels following TNF and LPS stimulation in the presence of glycochenodeoxycholic acid (GCDCA), glycohyodeoxycholic acid (GHDCA), or glycodeoxycholic acid (GDCA) (Fig. 7D–E and Supplementary Fig. S7). Notably, CCL2 levels decreased after BA treatment in FLS stimulated with LPS (GCDCA: (1 ± 0.0 vs 0.891 ± 0.035, p = 0.03, −0.110[95% CI: 0.835–0.947]); GHDCA: (1 ± 0.0 vs 0.894 ± 0.085., p = 0.03, −0.106[95% CI: 0.759–1.028]); GDCA: (1 ± 0.0 vs 0.870 ± 0.057, p = 0.01, −0.130[95% CI: 0.780–0.960])), and CXCL2 levels decreased with GCCA treatment in BMDM after stimulation with TNF (1 ± 0.0 vs 0.744 ± 0.081, p = 0.02, −0.256[95% CI: 0.616, 0.872]) (Fig. 7D–E).

Discussion

This study analyzed BA profiles in plasma and SF of KOA patients in relation to synovitis and clinical scores. Several BAs were linked to high-grade synovitis, with distinct patterns in obese versus non-obese individuals. Notably, LBP correlated with synovitis only in obese participants. The presence of BAs in SF and their receptors in synovial tissue suggests a potential role in joint inflammation.

Bile acids (BAs) are bioactive molecules abundant in the liver and gut, present at lower levels systemically. Gut bacteria can carry out numerous biotransformations of bile salts during their enterohepatic circulation, such as hydrolysis of conjugated bile acids to free bile acids and glycine or taurine by bile salt hydrolase (BSH). BSH activity is widespread in commensal bacteria inhabiting both the small intestine and large bowel. Gram-positive gut bacteria have the most diverse distribution of BSH [34]. In this study, glycine-conjugated BAs, particularly glycochenodeoxycholic and glycohyodeoxycholic acids, were most prevalent in KOA plasma. While glycine conjugation has long been attributed to host liver activity, recent studies suggest the gut microbiota can also mediate this reaction [28,35]. Although no major microbiome differences have been found between OA patients and controls [36], animal models have support the role of the microbiome in OA progression [37,38]. Notably, the Gram-positive genus Streptococcus, linked to higher WOMAC pain and effusion, also correlated with circulating secondary BAs [39,40].

Research on the role of BA in OA patients and clinical outcomes is currently limited. Recent reports have shown associations between plasma BAs and symptomatic hand OA [41,42]. In our cohort of knee OA (KOA) patients, we observed positive associations between plasma glycohyodeoxycholic acid and taurohyodeoxycholic acid, structural isomers of glycoursodeoxycholic acid and tauroursodeoxycholic acid, respectively, and synovitis.

BAs influence metabolism and inflammation via TGR5, a membrane receptor with anti-inflammatory, appetite-regulating, and thermogenic effects [43]. Lower TGR5 mRNA levels in PBMCs from RA patients have been linked to higher DAS28 scores [44]. Other BA receptors, such as farnesoid X receptor (FXR), also modulate inflammation [45]. Although FXR was not detected in synovial tissue, intestinal FXR and its ligand glycoursodeoxycholic acid were recently shown to influence OA [46]. These systemic effects may explain the discrepancy between bile acids’ positive association with synovitis in vivo and their anti-inflammatory effects in vitro. TGR5 and LXRα/β, mainly perivascular with variable synovial expression, and differing ligand affinities, likely contribute to tissue variability [47]. Chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), deoxycholic acid (DCA), cholic acid (CA), and lithocholic acid (LCA) have different affinities for FXR. This affinity is determined by the specific pattern of axial hydroxyl groups at the 7 and 12 positions [47]. Together with the fact that numerous additional bile acid modifications exist across human tissues as well as microbial cultures [28], this further underscores the complexity of bile acid metabolism and suggests that studying bile acids as functional groups rather than as individual molecules may be more informative.

GO enrichment analysis revealed distinct biological pathways linked to the two bile acid receptors studied. Consistent with prior research, NR1H3 activation regulates fibroblast differentiation and immune interactions in chronic inflammation [48]. TGR5-positive monocytes were enriched in phagocytosis and immune surveillance pathways, supporting TGR5’s role in macrophage polarization and anti-inflammatory responses [49]. TGR5-NR1H3-positive monocytes showed the strongest enrichment in inflammatory regulation, aligning with findings that NR1H3 activation influences monocyte-driven inflammation and metabolic reprogramming [49,50].

Obesity is a well-established risk factor for incident KOA, and increased radiographic progression of KOA mediated by synovitis [4]. Individuals with obesity have higher plasma BA levels than those with a healthy diet and active lifestyle, highlighting the microbiome’s role in their regulation [51,52] Additionally, obesity has been associated with gut microbiome shifts, particularly an increase in Bacteroidetes and a decrease in Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobia, along with shifts in BA composition, elevated lithocholic, cholic, and deoxycholic acids, and reduced conjugated BAs [53,54]. Obesity is also linked to higher LBP and impaired gut permeability [55]. In our OA population, higher LBP was observed in those with high synovitis, though no significant differences in LBP or synovitis scores were found between obese and non-obese groups.

While this study provides novel insights into the mechanisms underlying OA and synovitis, it has several limitations. The predominance of male participants (89.3%) may limit generalizability, and reliance on BMI without waist circumference data may underestimate obesity-related factors. The relatively small sample size limited the scope of additional analyses, and the absence of adjustment for multiple comparisons represents a further limitation. Finally, only 17% of metabolite features were annotated, limiting molecular family identification. These limitations underscore the need for larger, more diverse studies with comprehensive metabolomic profiling to clarify the role of bile acids in the pathogenesis of obesity-associated OA.
