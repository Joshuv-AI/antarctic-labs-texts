Effects of different exercise modes on cardiopulmonary function in patients with coronary artery disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jingjing Lu, Diyue Liu, Peng Pi, Xizhe Bai, Jing Yang, Yan Fu, Weixia Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveExercise interventions present advantageous outcomes for patients with Coronary Artery Disease (CAD). Nevertheless, there is a persistent discourse surrounding the optimal exercise modality, particularly in CTE. This research employed meta-analysis to identify the most effective exercises for improving cardiorespiratory fitness in CAD patients.MethodsThis systematic review comprehensively searched databases like Web of Science, PubMed, Embase, SPORTDiscus, Cochrane Library, CNKI, and Wanfang for RCTs on exercise interventions in CAD patients published from January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2023. Two researchers screened the literature and extracted data using predefined criteria. The included studies were then assessed for quality, subgroup analysis, sensitivity analysis, and publication bias evaluation.ResultsThis study included 23 literatures with a total of 1,730 CAD patients. The evaluated exercise modalities comprised aerobic exercise (AE), high-intensity interval training (HIIT), aerobic combined with resistance exercise (AE&RE), Chinese traditional exercise (CTE), and aerobic combined with Chinese traditional exercise (AE&CTE). The results demonstrated that exercise intervention had a significant impact on various physiological parameters, including oxygen consumption peak (VO2 peak) (MD = 2.86, 95%CI: 2.00 ~ 3.73, P < 0.001), oxygen pulse (O2 pulse) (MD = 1.55, 95%CI: 0.57 ~ 2.53, P < 0.01), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (MD = 3.56, 95%CI: 1.61 ~ 5.52, P < 0.01), and anaerobic threshold (AT) (MD = 2.00, 95%CI: 1.16 ~ 2.84, P < 0.01), with significant improvements observed. Subgroup analysis found that all exercise modalities (AE&CTE, HIIT, AE&RE, and AE) were associated with significant improvements in VO2 peak, with AE&CTE (MD = 3.62) and HIIT (MD = 3.60) showing numerically larger effect sizes, followed by AE&RE (MD = 2.67) and AE (MD = 2.59). LVEF improvements were observed across AE&RE (MD = 2.49) and AE subgroups (MD = 3.05), while O2 pulse showed increase in the AE&CTE (MD = 2.16) and AE&RE subgroups (MD = 1.03). For AT, AE&RE (MD = 2.03) demonstrated notable improvements.ConclusionsExercise intervention significantly improves VO2 peak, O2 pulse, LVEF, and AT in patients with CAD. Numerically larger effect estimates were observed for AE&CTE and HIIT on VO2 peak, for AE&CTE and AE&RE on O2 pulse, and for AE&RE on LVEF and AT. In clinical or rehabilitation settings, exercise prescriptions should be tailored to the individual patient’s specific functional deficits and therapeutic objectives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06038-7.Keywords: Exercise intervention, Different exercise modalities, Coronary artery disease, Cardiorespiratory fitness

Introduction

In contemporary society, people’s modes of travel and lifestyles have undergone significant transformations, with sedentary behavior becoming a daily habit. Inadequate physical activity and heightened sedentary behavior are primary contributors to various chronic diseases, significantly linked to overall mortality, cardiovascular-related deaths, and cancer fatalities [1]. Globally, as of 2019, nearly 19.7 million people died from cardiovascular diseases, with 85% of these deaths attributed to coronary artery disease (CAD) and stroke [2]. In China, as of 2023, the number of CAD patients has reached 11.39 million [3]. CAD represents the leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally, with both the prevalence and mortality rates of the condition generally exhibiting an upward trend [4].

CAD is a heart disease caused by atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries, resulting in narrowing or blockage of the lumen of the coronary arteries, leading to myocardial ischemia, hypoxia, or necrosis. CAD, the full name of coronary atherosclerotic heart disease, is caused by the narrowing or occlusion of the coronary artery lumen, causing myocardial ischemia, hypoxia or necrosis and chest pain, chest tightness and other discomforts of a kind of ischemic heart disease, is an important cause of sudden cardiac death [5]. Cardiac rehabilitation is a crucial strategy for rehabilitating and preventing secondary acute cardiovascular events. The primary health benefits derived from exercise are enhancing cardiopulmonary function, optimizing oxygen uptake efficiency and utilization, stabilizing or potentially reversing vascular plaque formation, and adjusting the oxygen supply balance between coronary arteries and myocardium. This intervention significantly contributes to patient prevention and recovery efforts [6].

The conventional approach to exercise in cardiac rehabilitation typically advocates moderate-intensity aerobic activities such as cycling and slow walking [7]. High-intensity interval Training (HIIT) has recently garnered significant interest in this field, particularly regarding its comparison with aerobic exercise (AE), although findings have been inconsistent [8]. Crowley et al. [9] found that aerobic exercise increased patients’ VO2 peak levels more than HIIT. However, Jan et al. [10] found that HIIT exercise was more effective in improving VO2 peak levels in patients. Other exercise modalities that have received considerable attention include resistance exercise and traditional exercise interventions. Resistance exercise (RE) was initially considered limited in cardiac rehabilitation due to its potential to elicit a pressor response, thereby increasing the risk of myocardial ischemia. Chinese Traditional exercise (CTE) is an ancient fitness and health practice in China, and its therapeutic and fitness effects have long been the focus of scholars. Numerous randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of CTE [11], such as Baduanjin and Tai Chi. However, skepticism among scholars persists regarding its benefits [12].

In the selection of exercise modalities, although scholars have conducted relevant meta-analysis, the majority of studies have focused on a limited range of exercise types. Insufficient attention has been given to combined AE with RE (AE&RE), as well as CTE, which have become focal points in current research. In particular, CTE has rarely been considered internationally due to geographical differences. Does CTE have the same effect on improving cardiorespiratory function as currently available physical exercise methods? The objective is to conduct a meta-analysis of various exercise modalities, including AE, HIIT, RE, and CTE, with a particular focus on the exercise efficacy of CTE, to provide guiding conclusions that can help clinical patients with CAD choose appropriate exercise modalities to enhance cardiorespiratory fitness and improve quality of life.

Methods

Literature search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted in Web of Science, PubMed, Embase, SPORTDiscus, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang Database from January 1, 2010 to December 31, 2023. The searches were carried out by utilizing a combination of Mesh Term searches and their corresponding Entry Terms to ensure thorough exploration. Boolean logical operators, specifically “OR” and “AND”, were employed in the search form to connect the search terms. The Mesh search terms encompassed “Coronary Artery Disease” and “Exercise”, with a focus on Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs). There were no language restrictions, and further details regarding the specific search strategy can be found in the Supplementary Material (Table S1). The protocol for the systematic review has been registered in PROSPERO (NO. CRD42022307063).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies meeting the specified criteria were included, which required: (1) Basal values between the experimental and control groups to exhibit no significant difference prior to the commencement of the experiment. (2) The control group to be either a blank control or an exercise group. (3) Outcome variables to encompass peak oxygen uptake (VO2 peak), oxygen pulse (O2 pulse), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), oxygen uptake at anaerobic threshold (AT), peak respiratory exchange rate (RER peak), and carbon dioxide ventilation slope (VE/VCO2 slope).

Studies were excluded if they (1) Non-RCT studies (e.g., cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, reviews, letters, and conference abstracts). (2) The factors of exercise prescription were incomplete and the outcome variables did not meet the requirements. (3) No control group and no sustained exercise intervention. (4) Studies reported in the form of editorials, abstracts, reviews, letters, expert opinions, or case reports. (5) Data were inadequate or ineligible for inclusion. The process of literature inclusion and screening adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Cochrane Handbook, with two researchers independently conducting the screening and a third researcher consulted in cases of disagreement.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers independently extracted data from the included studies using a predefined data extraction form. The extracted information included: first author, year of publication, elements of exercise prescription (e.g., movement mode, exercise intensity, exercise density and exercise duration), subject characteristics (gender, age, and sample size), and outcome variable data. For all continuous outcomes, post-intervention means and standard deviations (SDs) were extracted for the calculation of mean differences (MD).

In multi-arm parallel-group randomized controlled trials (RCTs) where participants were allocated into three or more independent groups, each eligible intervention arm was compared with its corresponding control arm through independent pairwise comparisons. In cases where a single control group was utilized across multiple eligible intervention arms, the sample size of the control group was divided equally among the comparisons to prevent errors, in accordance with the standard methodology recommended by the Cochrane Handbook. The means and standard deviations of the control group remained constant. The quality of evidence for each pooled analysis was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system. Any discrepancies were addressed through discussion and consensus.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were performed using R software version 4.3.3 with the meta package (version 6.5-0). Continuous outcome data were expressed as Mean Difference (MD) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q test and the I2 statistic. A random-effects model (REML method) was used to pool the effect estimates. In light of the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity among the included studies—demonstrated by differences in exercise modality, intensity, session duration, weekly frequency, and baseline characteristics of participants (such as age and cardiopulmonary fitness)—random-effects models were predominantly applied for pooled analyses. For studies with multiple eligible intervention arms sharing a common control group, each intervention arm was compared with the control group within its designated exercise-modality subgroup.

Potential publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s test for outcomes including ten or more studies, with a P-value < 0.05 considered indicative of funnel plot asymmetry. For outcomes with fewer than ten studies, funnel plots were visually inspected, though formal statistical testing was not performed due to limited power. Sensitivity analysis were conducted using the leave-one-out method to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall effect estimates and to identify potential sources of heterogeneity. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, and the completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided as Supplementary Material.

Results

Search strategy

The meta-analysis searched seven major databases (Web of Science, PubMed, Embase, SPORTDiscus, Cochrane Library, CNKI, and Wanfang Database) and other sources to retrieve the potential studies. Initially, 1796 studies were identified, with 298 articles remaining after screening titles and abstracts. Following a thorough review of full texts, 23 articles met the inclusion criteria. The study selection process is visually depicted in Fig. 1.

Flow diagram of study selection process

Characteristics of included studies

A total of 1,730 patients diagnosed with CAD, predominantly male, were encompassed in the 23 included studies. The mean age of participants ranged from approximately 50 ~ 83 years, reflecting a broad spectrum of the CAD population, including both middle-aged and older adults. The primary exercise modalities examined were aerobic exercise (AE), High-intensity interval Training (HIIT), combination exercise incorporating aerobic combined resistance exercise (AE&RE) and aerobic combined Chinese traditional exercise (AE&CTE), as well as Chinese traditional exercise (CTE). The exercise intensity varied between 50% of peak power and 90% of VO2 peak, with session duration spanning from 20 to 85 min. The weekly frequency of sessions ranged from 2 to 6, and the overall duration of the intervention extended from 2 to 24 weeks. The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1.

Evaluation of literature bias quality

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the 23 included studies were assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration’s risk-of-bias tool. All 23 studies were described as randomized; however, only 17 studies (77%) provided sufficient details regarding the method of random sequence generation (e.g., random number table), and allocation concealment was explicitly reported in only one study. Due to the inherent nature of exercise interventions, blinding of participants and personnel was generally not feasible, resulting in a high risk of performance bias across nearly all studies. Blinding of outcome assessment was reported in a minority of studies, while the majority were rated as unclear risk for this domain. In contrast, the domains of incomplete outcome data and selective reporting were predominantly rated as low risk. The overall risk-of-bias profile is summarized in Fig. 2.

The risk of bias evaluation

To further evaluate the certainty of the evidence, a GRADE assessment was performed for each included study and is presented in Supplementary Table S1. The overall quality of evidence ranged from moderate to low. Specifically, the majority of studies were downgraded due to limitations in blinding and allocation concealment, as well as imprecision arising from small sample sizes. As a result, the certainty of the pooled effect estimates should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

The effect of exercise on VO2 peak

The meta-analysis of VO2 peak included 18 datasets from 15 studies. The result showed that the random effects model had an MD value of 2.86 (95% CI: 2.00 ~ 3.73, P < 0.001), which was statistically significant. Heterogeneity results showed that there was high heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 89.2%, Q statistic = 157.44, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

The Forest plot of effect of exercise on VO2 peak

Egger’s test did not suggest significant publication bias (). The funnel plot was generally symmetric and inverted funnel-shaped, with studies distributed mainly in the middle and upper regions, showing no obvious asymmetry (Figure S1).

The effect of exercise on O2 pulse

The meta-analysis of O2 pulse included 7 datasets from 6 studies. The result showed that the random effects model had an MD value of 1.55 (95% CI: 0.57 ~ 2.53, P < 0.01), which was statistically significant. Heterogeneity results showed that there was high heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 90.2%, Q statistic = 61.12, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4).

The Forest plot of effect of exercise on O2 pulse

Publication bias could not be reliably assessed for this outcome because only 7 datasets were available, which is insufficient for Egger’s test or meaningful funnel plot interpretation (Figure S2).

The effect of exercise on LVEF

The meta-analysis of LVEF included 13 datasets from 11 studies. The result showed that the random effects model had an MD value of 3.56 (95% CI: 1.61 ~ 5.52, P < 0.01), which was statistically significant. Heterogeneity results showed that there was high heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 87.1%, Q statistic = 93.30, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5).

The Forest plot of effect of exercise on LVEF

Egger’s test showed no significant publication bias (P = 0.51). The shape of the funnel plots did not show any evidence of funnel plot asymmetry (Figure S3).

The effect of exercise on AT

The meta-analysis of AT included 13 datasets from 10 studies. The result showed that the random effects model had an MD value of 2.00 (95% CI: 1.16 ~ 2.84, P < 0.01). Heterogeneity results showed that there was high heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 95.4%, Q statistic = 261.95, P < 0.001) (Fig. 6).

The Forest plot of effect of exercise on AT

Egger’s test showed no significant publication bias (P = 0.43). The funnel plot was largely symmetric and inverted funnel-shaped, with studies clustered in the central and upper regions, showing no obvious asymmetry (Figure S4).

The effect of exercise on RER peak

The meta-analysis of RER peak included 4 datasets from 3 studies. The result showed that the random effects model had an MD value of -0.00 (95% CI: -0.11 ~ 0.10, P = 0.93), which was not statistically significant. Heterogeneity results showed moderate to high heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 57.2%, Q statistic = 7.00, P = 0.07) (Fig. 7).

The Forest plot of effect of exercise on RER peak

Due to the limited number of included studies (fewer than 10), publication bias could not be reliably assessed. Egger’s test was not performed, and no conclusions were drawn from visual inspection of the funnel plot (Figure S5).

The effect of exercise on VE/VCO2 Slope

The meta-analysis of VE/VCO2 Slope included 4 datasets from 2 studies. The result showed that the random effects model had an MD value of -1.19 (95% CI: -7.94 ~ 5.56, P = 0.61), which was not statistically significant. Heterogeneity results showed moderate to high heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 81.1%, Q statistic = 15.87, P < 0.01) (Fig. 8).

The Forest plot of effect of exercise on VE/VCO2 Slope

The sample size was too small to perform Egger’s test (only 4 datasets were included). The funnel plot also could not reliably assess publication bias (Figure S6).

Subgroup analysis

To investigate the impact of various modes of exercise on cardiorespiratory fitness, the exercise modes identified in the literature review were utilized as moderating variables, specifically “CTE”, “HIIT”,“AE”, “AE&CTE”, and “AE&RE”. The findings of meta analysis revealed that the exercise interventions did not have a statistically significant effect on the VE/VCO2 slope and RER peak levels in patients with CAD. Conversely, the remaining outcome measures, including VO2 peak, O2 pulse, LVEF, and AT, demonstrated statistically significant improvements.

Subgroup analysis revealed that all four exercise modalities were associated with significant improvements in VO2 peak, with AE&CTE (MD = 3.62, 95% CI: 2.54 ~ 4.71, P < 0.01) and HIIT (MD = 3.60, 95% CI: 2.97 ~ 4.23, P < 0.001) showing numerically larger effect sizes, followed by AE&RE (MD = 2.67, 95% CI: 0.28 ~ 5.07, P = 0.03) and AE (MD = 2.59, 95% CI: 1.42 ~ 3.75, P < 0.01). In the subgroup analysis for O2 pulse, HIIT, based on a single study, was associated with a numerically larger increase (MD = 2.30, 95% CI: 1.63 ~ 2.97, P < 0.01), followed by AE&CTE (MD = 2.16, 95% CI: 0.31 ~ 4.00, P = 0.02) and AE&RE (MD = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.37 ~ 1.68, P < 0.01). The study of AE on O2 pulse was limited to a single paper, and the results were not statistically significant (P = 0.78).

For LVEF, AE&CTE, based on a single study, showed a numerically larger improvement (MD = 6.35, 95% CI: 4.14 ~ 8.56, P < 0.001), followed by AE (MD = 3.05, 95% CI: 0.44 ~ 5.66, P = 0.02) and AE&RE (MD = 2.49, 95% CI: 0.93 ~ 4.05, P < 0.01). CTE, based on a single study, was associated with a numerically larger improvement in AT (MD = 3.90, 95% CI: 3.43 ~ 4.37, P < 0.001), followed by AE&RE (MD = 2.03, 95% CI: 0.70 ~ 3.37, P < 0.01), AE (MD = 2.01, 95% CI: 0.34 ~ 3.69, P = 0.02), AE&CTE (MD = 1.81, 95% CI: 1.30 ~ 2.32, P < 0.01), and HIIT based on a single study (MD = 0.35, 95% CI: -0.02 ~ 0.72, P = 0.06). These subgroup findings are summarized in Table 2. Subgroup analysis forest plots are detailed in the supplementary material (Figure. S7-S10).

Sensitivity analysis

To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity and assess the robustness of the pooled estimates, sensitivity analyses were performed using the leave-one-out method. For each outcome, individual studies were sequentially omitted, and the pooled mean difference was recalculated from the remaining datasets. As illustrated in the leave-one-out forest plots (Figure S11), the exclusion of any single study did not result in a meaningful shift in the overall effect estimate for any of the primary outcomes. Specifically, for VO2 peak, O2 pulse, LVEF, and AT, the recalculated pooled estimates remained statistically significant and within the 95% confidence interval of the original summary effect across all iterations. For RER peak and VE/VCO2 slope, the pooled estimates remained non-significant regardless of which study was removed. The leave-one-out test showed that no single study influenced the results, indicating that the results of the meta-analysis were stable and reliable.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the impact of different exercise modes on cardiorespiratory function in patients with CAD. Our findings demonstrate that exercise interventions significantly improved VO2 peak, O2 pulse, LVEF, and AT, while showing no significant effects on RER peak and VE/VCO2 slope. Specifically, HIIT, AE&CTE, and AE&RE all resulted in significant improvements in VO2 peak, O2 pulse, LVEF, and AT. Egger’s test and funnel plot indicated no publication bias, confirming the reliability of the meta-analysis results. Notably, high heterogeneity was observed for several outcomes, which may be attributable to variations in patient characteristics and differences in exercise protocols across included studies.

The present meta-analysis observed consistently high statistical heterogeneity across most outcomes, with I2 exceeding 90% for VO2 peak, O2 pulse, LVEF, and AT. This substantial heterogeneity likely reflects the considerable clinical and methodological diversity among the included trials. The exercise interventions varied markedly in modality (e.g., AE, HIIT, AE&RE, CTE, AE&CTE), intensity (ranging from 50% of peak power to 90% of VO2 peak), session duration (20 ~ 85 min), frequency (2 ~ 6 sessions per week), and total program length (2 ~ 24 weeks). Additionally, the study populations varied in age (with mean ages ranging from approximately 50 to 83 years), baseline cardiopulmonary fitness, and comorbidity profiles. The substantial clinical heterogeneity across studies weakens the precision of the pooled estimates; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as average effects across a diverse evidence base rather than precise, universally applicable effects [36].

The notion that exercise intervention can improve cardiorespiratory fitness in CAD patients has been extensively supported. Previous studies propose that exercise effectively enhances vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression, facilitating the establishment of coronary collateral circulation or vasodilation of collateral vessels. This process increases endothelial nitric oxide synthase (ecNOS) expression levels, enhances oxygen cycling and utilization efficiency, and effectively improves cardiopulmonary function [37]. In this context, the pooled improvement in VO2 peak (MD = 2.86) observed in the present study is not only statistically significant but also likely to be clinically meaningful, as an increase of approximately 2 ~ 3 mL/kg/min has been associated with improved functional capacity and favorable prognosis in patients with cardiovascular disease [38].

Several methodological considerations require careful attention when interpreting the findings related to subgroups. Firstly, the observed variations in effect estimates across different exercise modalities should not be construed as evidence of superiority, given that formal interaction tests were not performed to directly compare the subgroups. Secondly, the estimates for several comparisons (e.g., HIIT for O2 pulse, AE & CTE for LVEF, CTE for AT) were derived from single studies, thus precluding the possibility of drawing definitive conclusions.

Regarding the impact of various exercise modalities on cardiorespiratory fitness in patients, much attention has been directed towards the comparison between AE and HIIT. Vesterbekkmo et al. discovered that HIIT exercise is more effective than AE in enhancing VO2 peak in CAD patients [39]. The same results were verified in the study by Tian et al. [40], and also in the Meta-analysis by Li et al. [41] that found that HIIT had a better effect than AE on VO2 peak, AT, HRmax, and SBP in CAD patients. Our findings are broadly consistent with this evidence: in subgroup analysis, HIIT was associated with a numerically larger improvement in VO2 peak (MD = 3.60) relative to AE alone (MD = 2.59), and HIIT also demonstrated a significant benefit for O2 pulse. The characteristics of HIIT have been demonstrated to effectively enhance peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) expression, accelerating mitochondrial biogenesis, promoting glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, increasing ATP production rate, and improving sustained metabolic efficiency, thereby enhancing internal oxygen supply efficiency [42].

In addition to AE and HIIT, this meta-analysis also evaluated combined exercise regimens, including AE&RE and AE&CTE, as well as CTE alone. Subgroup analyses indicated that all evaluated modalities were associated with significant improvements in VO2 peak with AE&CTE and HIIT showing numerically larger effect sizes. For O2 pulse, improvements were observed in the AE&CTE and AE&RE subgroups. For LVEF, improvements were observed in the AE&CTE (single study), AE, and AE&RE subgroups. For AT, four modalities including CTE, AE&CTE, AE&RE and AE demonstrated significant benefits, with the largest numerical estimate observed for CTE alone, although this finding was derived from a single study and requires confirmation. These findings underscore the nuanced efficacy of different exercise regimens in cardiovascular improvements among patients. Dr. Elena’s team in the United States pioneered the integration of Tai Chi with AE for cardiac rehabilitation in CAD patients. Their 6-month intervention demonstrated significant improvements in VO2 peak, body weight, and quality of life compared to AE alone [43]. A systematic review by Bobby et al. also further demonstrated the role of our CTE in improving cardiopulmonary function in patients [44]. The results of this meta-study also indicate that combining AE with CTE improves patients’ cardiopulmonary function. However, the direct mechanism by which CTE enhances cardiopulmonary function remains unclear. Most scholars suggest this could be attributed to changes in skeletal muscle fibers and metabolic capacity [45]. Additionally, some scholars propose that traditional gong exercises in China emphasize respiratory regulation, leading to a significantly higher frequency of deep breaths compared to other exercise methods [46]. This emphasis is believed to enhance lung compliance and increase lung capacity.

While interventions AE&RE were traditionally believed to exacerbate myocardial ischemia, leading to their exclusion from cardiac rehabilitation for an extended period, recent studies have demonstrated that AE&RE improves the composite cardiovascular disease risk profile [47]. Similarly, this study found that AE&RE was more effective than AE alone in improving the cardiopulmonary function indexes of patients. Some scholars suggest that resistance training induces appropriate vascular compression, acting as a muscular pump to increase blood flow velocity, enhance venous return volume, and augment stroke volume per beat, thereby improving cardiopulmonary function [48].

This study investigates the differential effects of various exercise modalities on cardiopulmonary function recovery in CAD patients. In addition to conventional AE and HIIT, the analysis encompasses combined regimens (AE&RE and AE&CTE) as well as CTE alone. This meta-analysis adhered strictly to the PRISMA guidelines. However, there are several limitations: (1) the relatively small inclusion of the variables RER peak and VE/VCO2 slope may impact the reliability of these indicators’ results; (2) despite a thorough literature search, the limited number of studies available for certain subgroup analysis affects the validity of these findings; in particular, several subgroup estimates—including CTE for AT, HIIT for O2 pulse, and AE&CTE for LVEF—were derived from a single study and should therefore be interpreted with caution; (3) additional recent studies have been published since the completion of this study, which were not included in this meta-analysis. Future meta-analysis should incorporate these latest studies to enhance the robustness and conclusions of this research.

Conclusions

Exercise intervention has been shown to have a significant impact on enhancing the levels of VO2 peak, O2 pulse, LVEF, and AT in patients with CAD. Subgroup analyses indicated that all examined exercise modalities — including AE, HIIT, AE&RE, CTE, and AE&CTE — were associated with improvements in these outcomes, although the effect estimates varied. Notably, AE&CTE and HIIT showed numerically larger improvements in VO2 peak, while AE&RE and AE were associated with improvements in AT and LVEF, respectively. However, due to the limited number of studies in several subgroup comparisons (including single-study estimates for HIIT on O2 pulse, AE&CTE on LVEF, and CTE on AT) and the absence of formal interaction tests, these findings should be considered exploratory rather than definitive.
