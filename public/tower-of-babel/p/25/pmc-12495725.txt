Malária incubada: a mixed methods analysis on knowledge and experiences on Plasmodium vivax and asymptomatic malaria infections in a hard-to-reach and mobile population in the Amazon
Authors: Lorraine Plessis, Irene Jimeno Maroto, Yann Lambert, Muriel Galindo, Teddy Bardon, Stephen Vreden, Martha Suarez-Mutis, Amanda Figueira, Jane Miller Bordalo, Maylis Douine, Alice Sanna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs the Americas move towards malaria elimination, its circulation is increasingly concentrated in isolated communities with poor access to healthcare, and the silent human reservoir is a challenge (particularly for Plasmodium vivax). The deployment of tafenoquine for radical treatment is an important new asset. The CUREMA project evaluates a complex intervention aiming at malaria elimination in artisanal and small-scale gold mining (ASGM) communities of the Guiana Shield Region. A pre-intervention assessment was conducted to explore ASGM populations’ knowledge and perceptions of asymptomatic Plasmodium carriage and P. vivax malaria.MethodsThe target population was the ASGM community working across Suriname, French Guiana and Amapá (Brazil). Data was collected in 2022 in ASGM cross-border staging areas, during the CUREMA pre-intervention qualitative and quantitative (cross-sectional) surveys. This analysis explores knowledge, experiences and representations on asymptomatic plasmodial carriage, recurrences and P. vivax infections.ResultsThe qualitative survey included 25 semi-structured interviews, 14 group interviews and 38 informal conversations. The quantitative survey included 539 participants, mostly men (73.5%), with a median age of 38 years. Almost all the participants recognized mosquitoes as vectors of malaria, and a fraction recognized a microorganism as the causative agent of the disease. Awareness of asymptomatic forms of the disease (“malária incubada”) and of risk of recurrences was common, and they were generally considered to be the result of inadequate treatment. Most participants could identify different types of malaria, but knowledge about P. vivax characteristics and radical treatment was limited.ConclusionsUnderstanding the interpretation of the disease by affected communities is essential to develop context-specific strategies and for enhancing acceptability and effectiveness of malaria elimination efforts.Trial registration NCT05540470 on clinical trialsSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05566-5.

Background

The countries and territories of the Americas have made significant progress in the fight against malaria over the last decade. Nevertheless, its transmission persists in particular in the Amazon Region, with Brazil, Venezuela and Colombia being the biggest contributors to continental incidence (548,100 cases and 342 deaths from malaria were estimated in 2023), and approximately two thirds of cases are attributable to Plasmodium vivax [1]. In contrast, nine countries and territories of the Region have been identified among candidates for malaria elimination within the World Health Organization (WHO) E-2025 initiative, among which Suriname and French Guiana – two amazonian territories located in the Guiana Shield.

Several studies have shown that, as the intensity of transmission decreases, the proportion of asymptomatic carriage increases [2, 3]. Despite their lower average parasitaemia, the high prevalence of asymptomatic carriers makes them an important source of new vector-borne and, consequently, human infection [4–6]

Moreover, P. vivax presents specific challenges. On the one hand, when it presents as circulating forms, it generally presents lower parasitaemia than Plasmodium falciparum and is, therefore, less easily detectable [7]. On the other hand, its dormant intrahepatic stage, known as hypnozoite, is responsible for secondary malaria episodes in the absence of a new infecting bite. Recurrences of P. vivax can therefore be linked not only to a recrudescence (related to incomplete or ineffective treatment of the blood stage) or to reinfection, but also to relapses linked to the reactivation of hypnozoites. Biologically and clinically, it is very difficult, if not impossible, to distinguish between these three forms of recurrence in endemic settings. It is estimated that in the absence of a specific anti-relapse treatment, up to 80% of P. vivax malaria attacks are caused by relapses [8]. There is currently no available diagnostic for the detection of hypnozoites, which can only be treated with 8-aminoquinoline drugs (8-AQ), primaquine or tafenoquine, delivered in addition to medication against blood-stage parasites [9]. 8-AQ are contraindicated for individuals with G6PD deficiency, because of an important risk of haemolysis [9]. The recent validation and gradual market introduction of tafenoquine, a single-dose 8-AQ, and of a semi-quantitative point-of-care test for G6PD deficiency, promises a major step forward in the fight against malaria in Latin America [9–13].

Beyond the rapid treatment of acute symptomatic malaria attacks, to eliminate the invisible human reservoir of the parasite in contexts where elimination is the goal, specific actions can be taken, ranging from mass drug administration (MDA) to selective treatment of asymptomatic individuals considered to be at risk of carriage on the basis of epidemiological criteria (for example targeted drug administration or TDA, or reactive peri-focal drug administration) [14–18]. Moreover, recently P. vivax serological test and treatment strategy (PvSeroTAT) proposes to select for this treatment individuals who have recently been infected by P. vivax (and therefore potentially carriers of hypnozoites) identified through serological exposure markers (SEM) [19, 20]. The technological innovations mentioned above open interesting prospects on this front, offering a potentially simple and safe combination for population-scale actions [20, 21].

For the successful development and implementation of interventions aiming at eliminating malaria, many elements need to be taken into account. The acceptability of these interventions by targeted communities, a key condition for successful implementation, is closely linked to the knowledge and representations that community members have of malaria [22, 23]. Malaria as a nosological entity is derived from biomedical thinking, and may be integrated by the targeted community and by individuals in variable ways, depending on their own experience of the disease, their culture and its representations, their contact with the healthcare system and its biomedical paradigms [24]. In the literature, there is a limited number of studies focusing on these cultural dimensions in a South-American context, despite their major influence on the fight against malaria.

As malaria transmission declines, the last pockets of transmission are increasingly limited, and the communities most affected are often the most isolated: indigenous, migrants, rural communities, with difficult access to healthcare [25, 26].

The Guiana Shield is a vast region of South America, located between the Orinoco and the Amazon rivers, that includes territories from 5 different countries (Venezuela, Guyana, Suriname, France and Brazil), and is characterized by rainforest coverage, low population density, and difficult access to healthcare. In the South of the Guiana Shield, across Suriname, French Guiana and the neighbouring Brazilian State of Amapá, individuals involved in artisanal and small-scale gold mining (ASGM) represent the community with the most significant active malaria transmission: the interruption of malaria transmission (and specifically of P. vivax circulation) in this population represents the main challenge to malaria elimination in the region [27–29]. Often originating from the northern and northeastern States of Brazil and from very modest social groups, the garimpeiros (Brazilian word defining the persons involved in ASGM) migrate with the aim of improving their economic situation, and have a high degree of regional mobility, moving according to available opportunities [30–32]. Their mobility is extensive and can spread to many other gold-rich areas located in the Guiana Shield and, more generally, in the Amazon. Gold miners work in very isolated rainforest areas, and their activity is often informal or even illegal. Their access to healthcare and prevention is severely compromised by their remoteness, their stigmatization and, particularly in French Guiana, by the police enforcement to which they are exposed [33].

In this context, a research project called CUREMA was implemented, with the objective of evaluating a public health intervention aimed at eliminating malaria in the community involved in ASGM and mobile across Suriname, French Guiana and Amapá. The CUREMA project’s intervention includes (but is not limited to) awareness-raising and health education activities as well as a TDA focusing on P. vivax treatment (PvTDA, with chloroquine and 8-AQ). The protocol of the CUREMA project has been described in detail elsewhere [34]. As part of this project, a cross-sectional quantitative survey and a qualitative study were realized to serve as a baseline for the intervention’s evaluation, and to support the production of the study’s Information, Education and Communication (IEC) tools.

This article presents a mixed-methods analysis of the CUREMA project’s pre-intervention data, specifically aiming to explore the study population's knowledge, experiences and representations on asymptomatic Plasmodium carriage and on P. vivax malaria.

Methods

Mixed methods design

The CUREMA project includes an intervention, which took place from March 2023 to December 2024, and its mixed-methods evaluation based on quantitative and qualitative surveys conducted before (2022) and after the intervention (2024–25) [34].

This article presents the results of i) the pre-intervention cross-sectional survey (quantitative component) and ii) the qualitative descriptive baseline survey. The development of survey tools, data collection and data analyses for both components were carried out in parallel, following a convergent design based on Tariq and Woodman’s [35]. Quantitative and qualitative data were triangulated when the initial analysis was interpreted, and an iterative process led to the identification of emergent categories presented in this article. Study settings

Inclusions were carried out along the rivers Maroni and Oyapock (for both surveys) which are the borders of French Guiana with respectively Suriname and Brazil, as well as in the Surinamese capital Paramaribo (only for the qualitative survey) (Fig. 2). Qualitative component

Study population and sampling

This survey interacted with i) adults actively working in informal gold mines in French Guiana, Suriname and Amapá; ii) other key actors from the community (among which hotels and shops-owners, religious leaders, civil society organizations actors, health care providers). Convenience sampling was used to target variability in relation to age ranges, gender, and state and country of birth. Further informants were selected by snowball sampling [36].

Data collection

This survey is based on an ethnographic, descriptive, and exploratory approach. The field work was realized by a social science researcher (IJM), who was a native Spanish and an intermediate level Portuguese speaker. A facilitator helped to ensure reliable and convenient entry into the community in Brazil. The facilitator and two research assistants (one in Brazil and one in Suriname) collaborated in the fieldwork. The facilitator and the research assistants were native Portuguese speakers, and were related to ASGM community. Data collection was based on i) observation techniques, that is participant observation and informal conversations; ii) semi-structured interviews, individual and group; iii) documental research. Participants gave an oral informed consent for the participation in the study. Interviews were registered with an audio recorder if the participant consented. The fieldwork was conducted in two stages: the first stage between January and February 2022 with 15 days of field work in Suriname; the second stage between August and October 2022, 12 days in Brazil, 13 days in Suriname. Outside the periods formally dedicated to the survey, the researcher was in regular contact with the community in the field from October 2021 to the end of 2023, in the context of the preparation and implementation of the CUREMA project.

Data analysis

Qualitative data analysis began in the field with an inductive and iterative process, data saturation was applied as a criterion to interrupt the data collection [37]. At the end of the fieldwork, a session with the research assistant allowed for the pre-analysis of the data (thematic pre-categorization of the field notes). Audio recordings of the interviews were transcribed in the original language by a Brazilian professional familiar with the context and the community. All the transcripts were reviewed to add comments and clarifications on the informant's nonverbal language. Collected information was systematized with the support of the Dedoose® software. The analytical approach was qualitative, descriptive and thematic.

Quantitative survey

Study population and sampling

A multicentre, cross-sectional survey was conducted between September and December 2022 by two multiprofessional teams (a doctor, a nurse and a facilitator). Potential participants were met by prospecting in the inclusion sites (Fig. 2), or by snowball strategy. Inclusion criteria were to be 18 years or older and to have returned from an ASGM site located in French Guiana since seven days or less. The target number of participants was calculated according to the CUREMA project evaluation design, but the inclusion period was constrained by project’s schedule and, consequently, the number of participants eventually enrolled [34].

Data collection

After collecting the participant’s written informed consent, the inclusion process included a questionnaire administered in Portuguese by the facilitator on socio-demographic data, lifestyle, activities related to ASGM, health status, history of malaria, and knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) on this disease (described in the questionnaire in the Additional file 1). The words used in the Portuguese version of the questionnaire were chosen based on the field experience of Portuguese-speaking researchers in contact with the target population, and on the validation of the questions as having the correct meaning by members of the community. More specifically “malária incubada” was considered the translation of the persistence of the malaria agent in the absence of acute malaria symptoms and associated with a risk of symptomatic malaria recurrence; “recaída” as a malaria recurrence without new infection. Regarding the KAP questions, they were asked as they were formulated. A blood draw and a medical examination were also performed.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using STATA12 and STATA19. Quantitative variables were described using medians and interquartile ranges, or mean and standard deviations, and categorial variables were described using frequencies and proportions.

To build one or more scores reflecting knowledge about asymptomatic malaria and the risk of recurrence, a set of variables of the KAP questionnaire were selected, covering the concepts of asymptomatic carriage, risk of recurrence and P. vivax malaria (listed in Table 1). Participants were asked to answer each item as true, false, or “don’t know”. A false answer was equivalent to zero points, a “don’t know” answer to one point, and a correct answer to two points. The dimensions of this set of variables were studied using an exploratory factor analysis. Based on the distribution of eigenvalues by identified factor, the number of factors (scores) to retain were selected. The variables were assigned to one score, or another based on the factor with which they were most strongly associated (correlation coefficient). The findings of this exploratory factor analysis were confronted with the results of the qualitative analysis. Bivariate analyses of the association of above-mentioned scores with variables of interest were performed by Wilcoxon test for qualitative variables and simple linear regression for quantitative variables. Multivariate analyses were performed by multiple linear regression models, in order to identify the characteristics associated with knowledge after accounting for possible confounding factors. The variables included in the multivariate analyses were chosen according to the results of the bivariate analysis (p < 0.2) and selected to avoid multicollinearity. Variables retained in the final models were selected using a top-down stepwise method.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

During the qualitative survey, 23 individual interviews (18 in Suriname and 5 in Brazil) and 14 group interviews (10 in Suriname and 4 in Brazil) were conducted, as well as 38 informal conversations (25 in Suriname and 13 in Brazil).

The quantitative survey included 539 participants: 328 in Suriname and 211 in Brazil. They had a median age of 38 years (with a mean of 39.4, standard deviation = 12.3) anda male:female ratio of 2.77. Almost all the participants (95.9%, 517) were Brazilians, most of them originating from the states of Maranhão (52.1%, 281), Pará (20.8%, 112), and Amapá (13.9%, 75). The 37.6% (202) of participants were directly involved in gold extraction, the others in the ASGM's multiple support occupations. The median length of experience in ASGM was 8 years. Malaria experience and risk perception

Of all the participants included in the cross-sectional survey, 68.5% (369) declared having had malaria at least once in their life (Table 2). Nearly half of the participants included (47.1%, 254) said that malaria was the main health problem on ASGM sites (70.2% among those included in Brazil, 32.3% among those included in Suriname); only 26% (140) considered themselves to be at high risk of malaria infection while working in the ASGM areas. Although 95.2% (513) knew that malaria can be potentially fatal, 93.3% (503) did not consider it a serious threat (Table 3). Only 16.7% (90) of participants in the quantitative survey reported having received information about malaria in the previous 3 months, in most cases through traditional media (TV, radio) (Table 2). Knowledge on malaria transmission and aetiology

In qualitative data, the vast majority of informants mentioned the fact that malaria is transmitted by the mosquito, identified as carapanã by natives of the states of Pará and Amapá, or as muriçoca by natives of the state of Maranhão, which is (from their point of view) also responsible for transmitting other vector-borne diseases. Nevertheless, some of the people interviewed also suspected that contaminated river water consumed as drinking water could play a role in the transmission of the disease. When the cause of the disease was explored in greater detail, informants presented a heterogeneous vision. A relevant proportion attributed the origin of the symptoms to a microorganism sometimes called virus da malária (the malaria virus), and some of those interviewed detailed how this microorganism is transmitted from person to person via the mosquito (Table 4). Others considered it to be caused by a contaminant present in the water where the mosquitoes breed (Table 4).

In the quantitative data, 91.3% (252) of participants knew that the mosquito is involved in the transmission of the disease, but only 46.7% attributed the cause to a microorganism (Table 3).

Knowledge on symptoms of malaria

The majority of participants to quantitative survey (94.4%, 509) were able to mention malaria symptoms: 88.1% (475) of participants mentioned fever, 78.8% (425) headache, 55.5% (299) a feeling of cold or 12.2% (66) chills, 48.8% (263) body aches and 34.7% (187) vomiting (Table 3).

All the informants gave very similar and consistent description of malaria symptoms in qualitative interviews. Malaria fever was described as always appearing at the same time of the day and with a growing severity: these peculiar characteristics helped them to suspect malaria and differentiate it from other diseases (Table 4). At the onset of malarial episodes, people often experienced uncertainty amidst various symptoms and tended to make their own differential diagnosis also based on whether symptoms resolve after taking various types of drugs.

Knowledge on asymptomatic/pauci-symptomatic infection and recurrences

In qualitative data, beyond the manifestation of specific set of symptoms, malaria was also described as a condition that causes inflammation and enlargement of certain organs such as the liver, spleen, or kidneys. In this context, the recurrence was also referred as a new malaria symptoms episode due to a presumed organ damage: as long as malaria remains in the body, it continues to gradually affect the abovementioned organs which become sensitive to certain external factors, such as “irritating” food or drinks (comida reimosa) that are able to trigger new symptoms episodes (Table 4, and Additional file 2). Informants reported that between two malaria symptoms episodes, malaria remains asymptomatic, stays inside (recolhida) or incubated (incubada). During her fieldwork, the qualitative researcher observed several situations in which members of the community approach the malaria diagnostic points of the local health system to request a rapid malaria test even without presenting specific symptoms or ailments: they perceived this behaviour as a way of taking care of themselves and check their health status. Some respondents thought that there is no cure for malaria, that it persists indefinitely in the human body. Those who thought that malaria can be cured, associated recurrences with inappropriate treatment (i.e. because the patient did not take the right drugs and/or in the right way) (Table 4): it was common to hear among informants that one drug or another does not cure but rather alleviates symptoms and “incubates malaria”.

In the qualitative survey, respondents seemed unaware that malaria can be transmitted when it is asymptomatic, and particularly when it is not detectable by the usual diagnostic tests (Table 4). The use of traditional medicine was frequently mentioned in qualitative interviews. Informants reported that consumption of these remedies occurred in different situations: i) when they did not have access to medications for malaria; ii) as a complementary remedy to heal the liver from damage caused by malaria itself and by anti-malarial drugs; iii) as a cure when anti-malarial drugs were not effective stopping recurrences (Table 4).

The quantitative survey showed that in 94.1% (507) of cases, participants agreed with the existence of “malária incubada”, and the existence of asymptomatic Plasmodium carriers was known by 72.5% (391) of participants (Table 3).

Knowledge of malaria species and of specific characteristics of Plasmodium vivax

In the qualitative survey, many informants were able to mention the two main malaria species: “falci” or “falsifaro” (Plasmodium falciparum) and “vivax” (Plasmodium vivax). In most cases, knowledge of malaria types was associated with experience of the disease and the management by the health system, leading to the identification of a stronger (sometimes identified as P. falciparum) and a weaker malaria, with different treatments given by health professionals (Table 4). It is important to emphasize that, in the experience of the informants, any type of malaria can cause a recurrence and therefore there was no awareness of the risk of recurrences specifically linked to P. vivax hypnozoites (Table 4).

Only few informants were able to name chloroquine and primaquine, and to identify them as treatments against P. vivax (Table 4). Primaquine, according to some informants, can be perceived as a protective drug for the liver, and prescribed to avoid adverse effects, not as an antimalarial. Very few informants reported that they bought chloroquine and primaquine in the gold mining areas, where the most common smuggled antimalarial drug reportedly available was artemether-lumefantrine. In the quantitative survey, 78.5% (423) of participants stated that several types of malaria exist in the Region (Table 2), 94.8% (511) of the participants agreed with the existence of “a type of malaria that can lead to recurrence”, and 83.5% (450) of participants agreed with the existence of a treatment to prevent recurrence (Table 3). Nevertheless, only 19 participants reported having received a treatment by primaquine within the past four years (corresponding to 14.5% of the 131 who reported having had malaria in the same period) (Additional file 3). These treatments had all been administered by health professionals or community health workers. Eight out of nineteen reported adverse effects, including two who reported very dark urine and one who reported pallor/icterus (Additional file 3). Among the participants who reported ever having had malaria, only 12 (3,3%) said they had been informed by a healthcare professional about a contraindication to primaquine, and 14 (3,8%) said they had taken a test to identify a contraindication (Additional file 3). When asked about their knowledge of malaria, 37.8% (204) of participants said that not everyone could receive primaquine treatment, and 27.1% (146) said they did not know (Table 3).

Quantitative analysis of factors associated with knowledge

The factor analysis performed on selected KAP variables (listed in Table 1) revealed a multidimension construct on the scree plot of eigenvalues (Additional file 4). The first two factors had much higher eigenvalues than the subsequent factors. After reviewing them in the light of the qualitative results, the first two factors were selected, grouping variables representing specific aspects of participants' knowledge and behaviour (Table 6). Factor 1 comprises variables 1 to 5, and explores participants' knowledge of asymptomatic malaria carriage and the risk of recurrence (called 'malária incubada' score). Factor 2 comprises variables 6 to 8, and measures knowledge to the appropriateness of anti-malarial treatment (called 'adequate treatment' score). Discussion

This mixed-methods study provides a description of a topic that has been poorly explored in malaria research, yet is a priority for territories aiming to eliminate malaria. The study population is represented by people originating from various states of the Brazil mainly located in the Amazon region, with socio-economic and health vulnerabilities and significant malaria occupational exposure in the rainforest. This is thus an exemplary case of a community affected by residual malaria circulation and target of the latest elimination efforts. This study shows that concepts specifically related to P. vivax infections and their treatment are quite poorly known in the community studied, but that a significant experiential knowledge exists in relation to asymptomatic malaria carriage, the risk of recurrences and the importance of adequate treatment to prevent them.

Main limitations of the study

This study is a response to one of the CUREMA project's secondary objectives: the timetable and data collection strategy were therefore not defined primarily for this purpose, which leads to some limitations in data interpretation. For example, if the qualitative component had been carried out entirely before the quantitative component (QUALI- > quanti), it would have been possible to adapt the categories and wording of the KAP questionnaire, enabling a more refined quantitative analysis. In addition, the quantitative fieldwork, conducted with teams of facilitators and health professionals experienced in malaria-related issues, may introduce reporting bias as participants adjust their responses to meet the interviewers' expectations, and can potentially partially explain the difference in knowledge scores among participants enrolled in Brazil and Suriname. Moreover, the impossibility of meeting the target population directly on gold mining sites leads to a selection bias of the study population, which is not a random sample of the target population. In the case of the qualitative survey, this obstacle leads to an impossibility of carrying out an immersion in the reality of the daily life of gold miners, with an observation of their practices in situ, and an in-depth ethnographic description.

Another limitation is that the researchers involved in data collection (qualitative component) and analysis (both components) are not native Portuguese speakers. However, a growing proficiency in the language of the target population was acquired during the study period, and strategies were put in place to limit the risk of misunderstanding or loss of nuance in the interpretation of the data.

The approach adopted is inductive and exploratory, but the study was not designed or scaled to provide comprehensive insight into the beliefs and behaviours of the target population: further studies will be needed to deepen these findings and develop a robust theoretical model.

Widespread knowledge of malaria and of its asymptomatic and recurrent forms

Both quantitative and qualitative data showed a widespread and robust knowledge of malaria symptoms, its mode of transmission, as well as the existence of asymptomatic/pauci-symptomatic forms of malaria—“malária incubada”—which can be the cause of recurrences of the disease. This knowledge seems to be related to individual and collective experience of the disease: age and previous experience of malaria are associated with better knowledge of the subject. In the study conducted by Murta et al. [38] among gold miners working in the Lourenço district, Amapá (Brazil), the concept of asymptomatic malaria carriage did not seem to be widely recognized by the community members interviewed. Nevertheless, the current results are consistent with those observed by Freitas de Souza [39], who studied the experience of malaria in riverine communities in the Brazilian Amazon (state of Amazonas). In her ethnographic study, she describes how, according to her informants, malaria can remain “incubated” in the liver despite treatment, and can cause recurrences, particularly after the consumption of “comidas reimosas” (foods said to trigger an inflammatory process). The importance of abstaining from these foods, also found among the informants of the qualitative component, is interpreted by Freitas de Souza as a residue of the concepts of medieval Hippocrato-Galenic medicine imported to South America by the European colonizers [39]. The concept of malaria as an infection by a microorganism is partially integrated into the community's representations, as also observed in other studies carried out on the continent [38–40].

Insufficient knowledge of anti-relapse treatment

Although the two main malaria species in the region are often known and cited, the high risk of recurrences specific to P. vivax and the specifics of its drug treatment remain largely unknown—just as in Freitas de Souza work [39]. The participants in this study seem unaware that recurrences can have different causes: in particular, although they often associate the phenomenon of “malária incubada” with persistent liver trouble, they do not seem to know that P. vivax infections are linked to a specific type of recurrence associated with persistent and silent infection by hepatic hypnozoites. This can be interpreted in the context of the experiential nature of their learning, as the distinction between different types of recurrence is theoretical and, in real life, practically impossible in endemic territories even for health professionals and researchers [8]. Nevertheless, it is clear to them that, in most cases, adequate and well-managed treatment helps to prevent recurrences.

Knowledge regarding the adverse effects of 8-AQ and G6PD deficiency was partially explored in this study, that showed a very low level of knowledge on this topic. It is interesting to note, on the other hand, that a significant proportion of participants who reported recent use of primaquine in the quantitative survey, describe symptoms potentially consistent with hemolysis during a last drug intake. Field health professionals in Manaus (Amazonas, Brazil) interviewed in 2017 by Murta and colleagues were unaware of G6PD deficiency and the hemolytic risks of primaquine [41]: this lack of awareness among field healthcare workers likely applies to the context of this study. It is thus not surprising that patients themselves are not aware of the risks, as their understanding on medical treatment is often shaped by the information provided by healthcare professionals. Training professionals and educating patients about G6PD deficiency and 8-AQ treatment safety need to be promoted and has been a major challenge, both in the context of the CUREMA project [42, 43] and in the context of the deployment of tafenoquine in healthcare systems in South America [44, 45].

Health education and behaviour change: a complex link

In light of these considerations, one question nevertheless remains unexplored in the context of the current study: what are the links between knowledge of the disease and adherence to drug interventions targeting the asymptomatic reservoir of Plasmodium with the purpose of eliminating malaria?

These aspects have been investigated in qualitative or mixed-methods studies in sub-Saharan Africa or Asia, and contrasting results have been found in various contexts [22, 46–51]. In an interventional study conducted in the Gambia [23, 52], the study population aknowledged the presence of asymptomatic forms of malaria, such as situations where the symptoms of the disease are very mild, or the pathogen is “hidden” in the body. However, treating these asymptomatic forms was not acceptable to the informants, and the proposed intervention (reactive treatment of household members of a case) involved significant community participation efforts to adapt the message to local cultural beliefs, which led to its success [23].

In Laos, a mixed-methods survey revealed low awareness of asymptomatic infections among inhabitants of malaria-endemic villages in southern Savannakhet Province. However, this lack of awareness was not necessarily directly associated with a lower willingness to participate in a MDA campaigns, as other factors also influenced the decision [53]. In contrast, in Cambodia, participants'awareness of the possibility of being asymptomatic carriers of malaria was associated with more consistent participation across different rounds of MDA [46]. Overall, the familial, social, political, and cultural dimensions, community mobilization strategies and financial compensation, are factors contributing to adherence to these interventions [22, 23, 54]. Moreover, the fear of side effects and the lack of tests confirming the presence of the carriage are often cited as barriers to the acceptance of these strategies [22, 38, 41].

The determinants of human behaviour are indeed complex, determined by numerous factors both at the individual and collective levels, and theoretical efforts are being made to analyse the pathways that lead individuals to take action [55]. The PASS model is an example of a theoretical framework developed to improve health-seeking behaviour and access in the context of malaria [56]. In this model, factors linked to the accessibility and social acceptability/desirability of care come into play, but the illness interpretation (composite of knowledge, perceived severity and perceived susceptibility) also have a decisive role in individual decision-making. This underlines the importance of developing context-specific interventions that are informed by local culture and issues [57].

In this respect, this study highlights the presence of important levers for the implementation of interventions targeting the asymptomatic reservoir of malaria in the population studied, namely the awareness of the issue of "malária incubada": actors involved in malaria elimination efforts with communities socio-culturally similar to the one studied can, therefore, build on this knowledge and develop educational interventions to facilitate adherence to radical treatment for P. vivax and interventions targeting asymptomatic malaria carriers, such as PvTDA or PvSeroTAT. It also indicates that it would be beneficial to improve their knowledge of the specificities of P. vivax and its treatment, and the potential role of asymptomatic carriers in transmitting the disease to the community. The multivariate analysis of quantitative data identified characteristics associated with lower knowledge levels, and helps identifying sub-populations that would require targeted health education interventions. Older people with a history of illness were more knowledgeable about asymptomatic malaria, highlighting the importance of direct experience and peer-learning. Low community exposure to educational messages also emerges from the analysis; moreover messages are usually transmitted by the traditional media, and probably not tailored. As an alternative, targeted use of social media communication and interaction between community members, the system of community solidarity and “trusted persons” (community leaders), as described by Le Tourneau (17), should be a key strategy to improve the effective transmission of educational messages.

This study supported the elaboration of health education contents and tools tailored to the ASGM used during the CUREMA project and to feed into community engagement activities (Additional file 5), in order to increase adherence to the intervention, and in general to empower the community members to face this burden [58]. The final evaluation of the CUREMA intervention – which is currently ongoing – will provide information on the other individual and collective determinants of acceptability and effective participation in the proposed PvTDA in the context of this study.

The high mobility of this population, partly illustrated by their diverse geographical origins, makes this group an even more important target for prevention efforts: upon returning to their territories of origin, they can act as reservoirs for the disease and reintroduce it into areas where its circulation has already been contained.

Conclusions

This mixed-methods study investigated knowledge, perceptions, and behaviours related to malaria among gold miners in the Guiana Shield. Most participants recognized malaria symptoms and mosquito transmission, but had limited understanding of P. vivax, treatment with primaquine, G6PD deficiency and treatment risks. The risk of recurrence and the concept of "malária incubada" were widely known, often linked to local cultural beliefs.

These findings highlight the need for tailored health education and community engagement to support malaria elimination strategies. A better understanding of the interpretation of the disease of the communities the most affected is an essential lever for fostering community participation and providing appropriate health education actions. In this light, this work provides important elements for the implementation of interventions targeting the silent reservoir of malaria and P. vivax malaria in South American communities with similar cultural background. It could also inspire further studies and give food for thought for the development of interventions elsewhere in the world.
