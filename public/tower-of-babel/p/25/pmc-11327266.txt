Vagus nerve stimulation during training fails to improve learning in healthy rats
Authors: Alan M Carroll, David T Pruitt, Jonathan R Riley, Tanya T Danaphongse, Robert L Rennaker, Crystal T Engineer, Seth A Hays, Michael P Kilgard
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLearning new skills requires neuroplasticity. Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) during sensory and motor events can increase neuroplasticity in networks related to these events and might therefore serve to facilitate learning on sensory and motor tasks. We tested if VNS could broadly improve learning on a wide variety of tasks across different skill domains in healthy, female adult rats. VNS was paired with presentation of stimuli or on successful trials during training, strategies known to facilitate plasticity and improve recovery in models of neurological disorders. VNS failed to improve either rate of learning or performance for any of the tested tasks, which included skilled forelimb motor control, speech sound discrimination, and paired-associates learning. These results contrast recent findings from multiple labs which found VNS pairing during training produced learning enhancements across motor, auditory, and cognitive domains. We speculate that these contrasting results may be explained by key differences in task designs, training timelines and animal handling approaches, and that while VNS may be able to facilitate rapid and early learning processes in healthy subjects, it does not broadly enhance learning for difficult tasks.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Learning and memory

Introduction

Neuroplasticity is a key substrate for many forms of learning1–6. Shaping connections between neurons in motor and sensory cortex can lead to the acquisition of new skills and improve behavioral performance. Promoting plasticity in these and related brain areas may thus lead to further performance improvement. It is well known that synaptic plasticity occurs with learning and is a fundamental component of cortical map plasticity7. Although cortical map plasticity is often correlated with learning progress and expert performance, its exact role in driving improvements remains unclear3,6. In particular, questions remain regarding the general utility of externally produced cortical map expansions to alter the rate or degree of learning in healthy adult brains8–10. If such plasticity is a primary determinant of learning, then enhancing map plasticity could accelerate learning across a wide variety of tasks and skill domains.

Neuromodulatory signals related to attention and arousal can alter neural processing and promote plasticity. Neural activity in motor, sensory, or cognitive networks paired with activation of neuromodulatory centers like the cholinergic nucleus basalis and the noradrenergic locus coeruleus can increase the efficiency of information transmission in those networks11–14. Changes driven by this combination of neuromodulators and neural activity can be highly specific and long-lasting. When paired with specific tasks or stimuli, these network modifications and enhancements often correlate with improved performance during motor, sensory, or cognitive learning10,15–17. Disruption of these neuromodulatory and plasticity processes likewise disrupts learning and memory18–21. Facilitation of attention and arousal signals during learning may thus be a general mechanism to improve learning and increase performance on a range of tasks.

Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) triggers bursts of spiking activity in neurons located in the nucleus basalis and the locus coeruleus. Stimulating the vagus nerve thus releases neuromodulators, including acetylcholine and norepinephrine, throughout the central nervous system22–24. Repeatedly pairing brief trains of VNS with neural activity consistently generates plasticity specific to the paired activity across motor, sensory, and cognitive domains25–31. Likewise, VNS driven cortical map plasticity is disrupted if the nucleus basalis or locus coeruleus are lesioned19,20. The similarities between learning and VNS driven plasticity suggest that VNS could enhance learning. VNS has already been shown to facilitate task-relevant plasticity in a wide variety of training paradigms in models of neurological disorders25,28,29,32–34. Additionally, multiple studies have reported learning enhancements from pairing VNS with behavioral performances including motor learning23, auditory reversal learning35, visual discrimination reversal learning36, and cognitive tasks like novel object recognition37.

These reports have demonstrated VNS-enhanced learning only over relatively short training times, showing either same-day or next-day results on simple and limited tasks35,37, or facilitating learning on tasks where expert performance is reached over a few hundred trials23,36. These results leave open the question of whether VNS can function as a general aid to learning, particularly over longer training timelines for difficult tasks. The demonstration that VNS could produce significant, long-lasting performance benefits on difficult to learn tasks would provide greater understanding of the neural mechanisms that limit learning and potentially open the door for potential applications of VNS to enhance learning in humans. Additionally, it remains unknown if VNS can accelerate the acquisition of novel perceptual discriminations, or if enhancements are instead limited to updating knowledge of previously learned stimuli. Neural reorganization can depend on learning phase and task requirements. Initial learning related to gross, overall improvements occurs as a separate neuroplasticity process from late-stage refining of dexterous movements or perceptual discriminations which potentially require cortical map plasticity3,38,39. As VNS may enhance both early and late learning processes, we sought to determine if known, plasticity-inducing VNS pairing strategies can facilitate long-term learning over weeks of training on difficult tasks across a range of skill domains.

In this study we hypothesized that using VNS pairing strategies known to facilitate motor and sensory map plasticity and enhance memory and cognitive networks would also generally enhance the learning process, leading to faster training times and improved behavioral performance. To test if VNS can generally facilitate learning, we paired VNS in healthy, female adult rats during training on motor, auditory, and visual-spatial tasks, including skilled forelimb supination, visual paired-associates learning (PAL), and two auditory discrimination tasks (Fig. 1).

Methods

Subjects

A total of 103 adult female Sprague–Dawley rats (Charles River Laboratories) were used across all experiments, and 75 included in final analyses (Table 1). Rats were housed on a 12:12 reversed light cycle environment, and all behavioral training was performed during the dark cycle to increase daytime activity. Rats were habituated to being handled by researchers and exposed to reward sugar pellets (45 mg dustless precision pellet, BioServ, Frenchtown, NJ) over the course of a week before being food deprived. During training, rats were food deprived to no less than 85% of their normal body weight at the time of deprivation and were supplemented daily and over weekends to maintain a healthy weight. Water was available ad libitum. When necessary, euthanasia was performed through overdose of sodium pentobarbital delivered intraperitoneally. All handling, housing, surgical, and behavioral training were approved by the University of Texas at Dallas Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All experiments described here conform to the ARRIVE guidelines.

Vagus nerve cuff implant surgery

Subjects in VNS and Sham groups in all experiments had a custom, platinum-iridium bipolar cuff electrode implanted around the left cervical vagus nerve, as described previously25,31,40,41. Briefly, rats had the cuff secured around their left cervical vagus nerve and the wire leads from the cuff tunneled subcutaneously to the top of the head. The leads were then plugged into a two-channel connector affixed to the skull with bonescrews and acrylic. A brief period of VNS was then applied to trigger the Hering-Breuer reflex, a predictable momentary cessation of breathing accompanied by a drop in oxygen saturation42. This ensured the cuff was working and the vagus nerve was being stimulated. Incisions were then sutured closed and any exposed wires or components of the headcap were encapsulated in acrylic to protect the electrode connections and provide structural integrity. Rats were given antibiotics and analgesics and monitored for healthy recovery for seven days after surgery before continuing in their respective experiments.

VNS parameters and delivery

The exact timing of VNS delivery varied between experiments, but stimulation parameters were otherwise constant and consisted of a 500 ms train of 100 us biphasic pulses delivered at a frequency of 30 Hz and current intensity of 0.8 mA. These parameters have been consistently shown to drive plasticity and neural activity in multiple contexts in both healthy and disease rodent models22,28–34,40,43–49, as well as in humans50–54. Additionally, these parameters are known to phasically activate key neuromodulatory centers including the locus coeruleus and nucleus basalis and drive release of norepinehprine and acetylcholine in cortical and sub-cortical regions of the brain22,24,55–59. All tasks were conducted in behavioral chambers containing commutators attached to the ceilings and connected to stimulators (A-M Systems, model 2100, Fig. 1a), permitting delivery of VNS to rats during training. All rats were connected to commutators, regardless of whether they received VNS or not. Delivery of VNS was fully automated and programmatically controlled by extensively tested custom MATLAB programs. Events triggering delivery of VNS, such as sound presentation or trial success, were programmatically monitored and controlled by closed-loop systems ensuring rapid pairing of said event with nerve stimulation. A TTL impulse signal would be sent to connected stimulators upon triggering events, resulting in immediate delivery of VNS to the subject. Delivery speed was limited only by the temporal resolution of the monitoring programs, and in all cases was under 50 ms from triggering event. These systems have repeatedly been used in our lab to drive VNS-enhanced plasticity in both behavioral and passive settings and in both healthy and disease models25,26,28,30,31,40,46. The use of these automated systems helps to reduce uncertainty surrounding stimulation pairing events.

Accelerated learning tasks

Motor: Forelimb supination task

Rats were trained on a forelimb supination task which required them to reach, grasp, and rotate a knob using their right forelimb past an adaptively scaled angle threshold, as described previously40,60. Motor cortex may or may not be required for expert execution of some motor skills. For example, it has been demonstrated that rats may continue to perform a difficult, stereotyped movement at high levels of accuracy after motor cortex lesions, even on day one of recovery, if they have already learned to do so prior to injury61. Multiple studies have found that damaging motor cortex in rats results in large performance deficits for the forelimb supination task used here40,46,60,62. These deficits persist over many weeks of testing. Recovery of motor function and task performance has been associated with neuroplasticity in motor cortex46. In agreement with the conclusions of Kawai et al., we consider these results evidence that motor cortex is necessary for rats to perform the supination task at expert levels capable of meeting our training criteria.

Briefly, rats were required to reach for a spherical manipulandum and rotate it clockwise (Fig. 1b, top row). Turn angles were measured using a rotary encoder and custom software. If the rat turned the knob past the threshold angle within 2 s of initiating rotation, then a reward pellet was delivered and the trial was counted as successful. Performance was quantified as the percentage of successful trials per day. Rats performed two 30 min training sessions per day with a minimum 120 min break between sessions, five days per week. Sham rats were always connected to stimulators but never received stimulation, while VNS rats received stimulation paired with every successful trial during every stage of training. Behavior sessions were controlled and data automatically collected by custom MATLAB programs.

Shaping to use the knob

Initial training to perform the forelimb supination task proceeded through several increasingly difficult stages, as described previously60. These shaping stages are categorized as either Unweighted or Weighted. During these stages the rotation threshold for a successful trial was set to 5 degrees. Briefly, during the Unweighted stage the knob was placed inside the cage, 0.5 cm from the inner cage wall. As rats learned to perform the task, the knob was retracted further away, eventually requiring the rats to reach out of the chamber 1.25 cm from the inner cage wall. Rats then moved to the Weighted stage, and counterweights were progressively added to the knob to increase the rotation difficulty. Once rats successfully performed 50 trials during both daily sessions using a 6 g counterweight, they were advanced to the Supination training stage (Fig. 2a).

VNS failed to improve motor learning. VNS paired with successful trials failed to decrease the number of days required to learn the Unweighted, Weighted, or Supination training stages of the task, or the Total number of days spent training overall compared to Sham controls (a, Unweighted: Z = 1.63, p = 0.31; Weighted: Z = 1.21, p = 0.84; Supination: Z = -1.03, p = 1.00; Total: Z = 0.01, p = 1.00). There was no difference between VNS and Sham for the rate of successful trials during the Weighted training stage (b, left, Z = − 1.59, p = 0.25). Both groups improved their rate of success during the first several days of Supination training (b, middle, F(1,115.18) = 73.20, p < 0.001), but no difference was seen between VNS and Sham rats (F(1,29.46) = 0.00, p = 0.97). Success rates at the end of training were also comparable between groups (b, right, Z = − 0.03, p = 1.00). All values are mean ± sem.

Supination training

In addition to the 6 g counterweight, the forelimb supination stage introduced an algorithm to adaptively adjust the rotation threshold necessary to score a successful trial and trigger delivery of a reward pellet. The threshold on each trial was set to the median turn angle from the previous 10 trials, up to a maximum threshold of 60 degrees, with a minimum threshold of 15 degrees. Rats were considered fully trained on the forelimb supination task when at least 70% of trials over 5 consecutive training days exceeded 60 degrees rotation.

Subjects

Twenty-five adult female Sprague–Dawley rats weighing approximately 250 to 300 g were used for the forelimb supination experiment. Four subjects were excluded from all analysis: Three were removed due to VNS cuff failures or loss of the connector affixed to the skull, and one failed to learn the supination task to criteria. Two groups were used for this experiment: A Sham group that received cuff implants but never received VNS, and a VNS group that received VNS paired with every successful trial during all training stages. Rats underwent VNS cuff implant surgery and were randomized into Sham (n = 13) and VNS (n = 8) groups prior to beginning training.

Auditory: Speech discrimination tasks

Two different speech discrimination experiments were conducted in this study: Single-distractor and Multi-distractor (Fig. 1b, middle row). Both used an operant Go/No-go procedure requiring rats to respond to a Target speech sound and withhold responding to Non-target speech sounds, as described previously63–66. The two experiments differed in the number of Non-target distractors presented during a behavior session, and the number of Target speech sounds learned over the course of training. Both experiments began by shaping rats to use the operant training booth by nosepoking through an IR break-beam sensor within 3 s of presentation of a Target sound to receive a reward pellet. Responding during silent catch trials (or to Non-target sounds during later training stages) resulted in extinguishing of an overhead house light and an 8 s timeout before lights were restored and the next trial was presented. Trials were otherwise presented every 8 s. Typically 200 to 300 trials were presented per session. Performance was quantified as the mean percentage of correct responses to Target, Non-target, and silent catch trials. Rats were required to achieve at least 75% correct stimulus detection vs. silence for 10 cumulative sessions before being considered proficient in the basic Go/No-go task paradigm and advancing to the first stage of their respective speech discrimination experiments. All auditory behavior sessions were 60 min long and conducted in double walled sound isolation chambers remotely monitored via webcams and controlled by custom MATLAB programs. Rats performed two sessions per day, with a minimum 90 min break between sessions, five days per week.

Speech stimuli

The speech stimuli used in these experiments have been previously described in other studies from our lab30,64,66,67. Briefly, they were one syllable consonant–vowel-consonant sounds spoken by an adult female and shifted up by one octave using the STRAIGHT vocoder to better match the rat hearing range. They were calibrated such that the intensity of the loudest 100 ms of the vowel component was 60 dB SPL. The eight stimuli pairs (Target / Non-target) used in the Single-distractor experiment were: "Mad" / "Kad", "Gad" / "Lad", "Bad" / "Fad", "Zad" / "Pad", "Wad" / "Jad", "Nad" / "Yad", "Chad" / "Rad", and "Dad" / "Tad" (Fig. 3a). The stimuli used in the Multi-distractor experiment were "Shad" (Target) and "Had", "Sad", "Jad", and "Chad" (Non-targets) (Fig. 4a).

VNS failed to improve auditory learning for a series of eight discrimination tasks between two speech sounds. Rats learned all discrimination pairs but VNS did not decrease the time required to complete each Single-distractor discrimination (b, F(1,24) = 2.56, p = 0.12) or the total number of days to finish training on all 8 discrimination pairs (c, Z = − 1.55, p = 0.12) compared to Sham. All values are mean ± sem.

Rats improved and learned to withhold responding to Distractor sounds over time (b, Week: F(1277) = 200.35, p < 0.001, Sound: F(5277) = 41.38, p < 0.001, Week:Sound: F(5,277) = 27.16, p < 0.001), but VNS did not further improve learning compared to Sham (F(1,16.40) = 0.00, p = 0.98). Overall performance improved over time (F(1,37) = 213.89, p < 0.001), but there was complete overlap between VNS and Sham groups over the 4 week training period (c, F(1,20.39) = 0.00, p = 0.95). Single-distractor task

Rats underwent sound detection training using a 500 ms white noise burst. Training time to detection proficiency typically took 3 to 4 weeks. After training to proficiency, rats underwent an additional training stage to learn to perform Go/No-go sound discrimination. They were required to respond to the same 500 ms white noise burst used in detection training and withhold responding to a novel 500 ms complex sound with irregular temporal and spectral features10. Rats were considered proficient in discrimination after achieving at least 75% correct performance for 5 cumulative sessions. Training time to discrimination proficiency typically took less than a week. Rats then received VNS cuff implants and were randomized into VNS and Sham groups by balancing training time required to reach proficiency. In the Single-distractor task rats were trained to discriminate a Target speech sound from a Non-target distractor which differed only in the initial consonant sound, e.g. "Mad" vs. "Kad". The Target and Non-target speech sounds each had a 40% chance of being pseudorandomly selected for presentation on any given trial. The other 20% of trials were silent catch trials. Responding to the Target sound within 3 s of presentation resulted in delivery of a reward pellet. Responding to the Non-target sound or during a silent catch trial resulted in extinguishing of the house light and an 8 s timeout. VNS rats received stimulation paired with presentation of either Target or Non-target speech sounds. Sham rats were plugged in to the stimulators during behavior, but never received stimulation. After rats achieved at least 75% correct performance for 2 cumulative sessions they stopped training for that consonant pair and started training on a novel Target / Non-target consonant pair, e.g., "Gad" vs. "Lad". In total, rats progressed through 8 unique consonant pair speech discriminations. The order of training on consonant pairs was M/K, G/L, B/F, Z/P, W/J, N/Y, Ch/R, and D/T, and was the same for all rats.

Subjects

Forty adult female Sprague–Dawley rats weighing approximately 300 to 400 g were used for the Single-distractor experiment. Fourteen subjects were excluded from all analysis: Four died during surgery, and ten were removed due to VNS cuff failures or loss of the connector affixed to the skull. Two groups were used for this experiment: A Sham group that received cuff implants but never received VNS and a VNS group that received VNS paired with every speech stimulus presentation. Rats were randomized post-surgery into Sham (n = 10) and VNS (n = 16) groups based on balancing the mean training time to detection and discrimination proficiency before advancing to the Single-distractor task.

Multi-distractor task

Rats underwent sound detection training using the Target speech sound "Shad". Training time to detection proficiency typically took 4 weeks. Unlike the Single-distractor design, rats did not undergo a separate discrimination training stage and instead immediately received VNS cuff implants after completing detection training. In the Multi-distractor task rats were trained to discriminate the Target speech sound "Shad" from multiple, Non-target distractors which differed only in the initial consonant sounds: "Had", "Sad", "Jad", and "Chad". The Non-target speech sounds and silent catch trials each had approximately an 11% chance of being pseudorandomly selected for presentation on any given trial. The Target sound, "Shad", had approximately a 45% chance of being presented. Responding to the Target sound within 3 s of presentation resulted in stimulation in VNS rats concurrent with delivery of a reward pellet. Sham rats were connected to stimulators, but never received stimulation. Responding to presentation of any of the Non-target speech sounds or during silent catch trials resulted in extinguishing of the house light and an 8 s timeout. Rats trained on the Multi-distractor task for 4 weeks.

Subjects

Fifteen adult female Sprague–Dawley rats weighing approximately 300 to 400 g were used for the Multi-distractor experiment. Two subjects were excluded from all analysis: One died during surgery, and one required euthanasia before completing the experiment. Two groups were used for this experiment: A Sham group that received cuff implants but never received VNS and a VNS group that received VNS paired with their correct responses to the Target speech stimulus ("Shad"). Rats were randomized post-surgery into Sham (n = 8) and VNS (n = 5) groups based on balancing the mean training time to detection proficiency.

Visual-spatial: paired-associates learning (PAL) task

The PAL task used in this study is similar to other rodent touchscreen PAL tasks68–70, except we chose to use line patterns as our stimuli instead of the more typical 'flower-plane-spider' shape set as some evidence suggests that line patterns are easier for rats to visually discriminate between on touchscreen devices69,71. In our task, rats had to learn to associate a specific line pattern (five Horizontal, Vertical, or Diagonal white lines against black background) with a specific location on a touchscreen display (Left, Center, Right). Each Stimulus:Location pair filled a 2.5" × 5.3" rectangle on the screen, with 1" spacing between the Left/Right and Center locations. Trials began automatically, and a trial did not end until the rat responded. On every trial, a matched (S +) and a mismatched (S-) Stimulus:Location pair were presented on the touchscreen (see Fig. 1b, bottom row, showing matched S + Horizontal:Right vs. mismatched S- Diagonal:Left). The third location remained blank except for a thin white rectangle demarking the location's perimeter on the screen, and touches in this area were ignored. Rats responded by touching the screen anywhere in either of the presented S + /S- areas. Touching the S + resulted in delivery of a reward pellet at the back of the cage and removal of the stimuli, while touching the S- only removed the stimuli. Correction trials were used to counteract location and stimulus preferences69. After an incorrect response, and following a 15 s intertrial interval, the same S + /S- combination would be presented to the rat again. These corrections trials would continue until the rat selected the S + , which then resulted in delivery of a reward pellet. The number of correction trials presented during behavior is a useful metric for perseverative activity, however only the first incorrect response was counted for overall percentage correct performance. As trials began automatically it was hypothetically possible for rats to make random selection responses by engaging with the touchscreen prematurely. To prevent these errant responses, rats had to withhold touching the screen for at least 2 s before a trial would begin. Additionally, any touch recorded within 500 ms of presentation was ignored, and the rat was required to again withhold touching the screen for at least 500 ms before being allowed to make a selection response. All training and testing stages were 60 min long per session. Rats performed two sessions per day, with a minimum 90 min break between sessions, five days per week. Subjects were individually monitored during behavior sessions via webcams connected to a central behavior server. Custom MATLAB programs on each touchscreen device controlled selection and display of visual stimuli and recording of touch responses.

Behavioral apparatus

Rats were trained in custom built operant chambers (13.5" × 11" × 8.1") made of acrylic and containing a tablet computer with a touchscreen visual display (Dell Venue 11 Pro) embedded on one side. Inside the cage the touchscreen was accessible through a rectangular cutout of the acrylic wall (display area 9.4" × 5.3") and could accept touch responses from the rats across the entire visible area. Touches from a rat's paws or nose registered equally well, and multiple near-simultaneous touches could be processed with sub-millisecond precision and pixel level location accuracy. While it was possible for a rat's tail to trigger touch responses also, the cage was large enough that rats could fully turn around without contacting the screen, the response area accessible to the rats was elevated off the cage floor, and researchers never observed inadvertent tail-touch responses during any stage of training. Small receptacles for sugar pellet delivery and ad libitum water were placed on the wall opposite of the touchscreen.

Training to use the touchscreen for PAL

Rats were shaped to the touchscreen PAL task through two brief training stages. Shaping Stage 1 exposed the rat to the touchscreen environment and introduced the association of the offset of a visual stimulus on the touchscreen with pellet delivery. The session started with 10 pellets being delivered to the pellet tray, and a single S + stimulus appearing on the touchscreen. If the rat touched the stimulus on the screen then it immediately turned off and 3 pellets were delivered. If the rat did not touch the stimulus after 30 s then it turned off automatically and a single pellet was delivered. Another S + would be pseudorandomly selected and displayed after a 15 s intertrial-interval. The session ended after 60 min and the rat was placed back in her home cage. After two Stage 1 sessions rats were advanced to Shaping Stage 2. During Stage 2 training, each trial presented two S + 's on the touchscreen. These stimuli remained displayed until the rat made a selection response to either one. Responses to the blank location on the touchscreen were ignored. Responding to either stimulus triggered offset of the stimuli and delivery of a single reward pellet. After a 15 s intertrial-interval a new trial began and two more S + 's were displayed. As the goal in Shaping Stage 2 was simply to get the rats accustomed to interacting with the touchscreen and task context to trigger pellet delivery, only correct Stimulus:Location matches were presented, and receiving a reward pellet was the only possible consequence for responding on the touchscreen. Rats were required to achieve 60 pellets within a single 60 min session to advance to Initial-PAL training. Touchscreen shaping typically took less than a week.

Training on initial-PAL

Rats were trained on an initial set of Stimulus:Location associations prior to receiving VNS implants. This Initial-PAL training ensured that all rats were capable of performing the task regardless of VNS treatment, allowed us to balance groups based on performance, and permitted testing VNS with a reversal learning component. The Stimulus:Location associations during Initial-PAL training were Horizontal:Left, Vertical:Center, and Diagonal:Right. Trials began automatically and stimuli remained displayed until the rat made a selection response on the touchscreen. A 15 s intertrial-interval occurred after selection, followed by either a new trial or a correction trial depending on whether the rat selected the S + or S-, respectively. Rats were required to achieve two sessions with at least 80% correct performance before receiving VNS implants and advancing to Shift-PAL training.

Novel and reversal learning on shift-PAL with VNS

After receiving cuff implants and being randomized into Sham and VNS groups, rats began training on the PAL task with Shifted Stimulus:Location associations from the Initial-PAL stage. The Stimulus:Location pairs in Shift-PAL were Vertical:Left, Diagonal:Center, and Horizontal:Right. Of the six possible S + /S- combinations that could be presented on any given trial during Shift-PAL training, three S + /S- combinations were novel and three were reversals from Initial-PAL (see Supplementary Fig. S2). Thus, Shift-PAL required rats to learn completely novel Stimulus:Location associations as well as reverse previously learned associations. VNS was delivered during behavior upon correct selection of the matched S + stimulus, concurrent with offset of the touchscreen visual display and reward pellet delivery. Rats were required to achieve two sessions with at least 80% correct performance to be considered proficient on the Shift-PAL stage. After achieving proficiency, they were tested on two sPAL probe sessions.

sPAL probe sessions

After rats achieved performance criteria on Shift-PAL we tested them on two sessions of a probe task called 'same' PAL (sPAL)68. These test sessions verified that they had learned the updated Stimulus:Location associations in Shift-PAL and not simply six individual visual discrimination pairs. The sPAL task used the same S + associations as Shift-PAL, but every trial presented identical patterns in two locations – one in the correct, matched location, and one in the incorrect, mismatched location. Rats had to select the correct, matched location for the presented line pattern. No VNS was delivered during sPAL probe sessions. The task was otherwise identical to Shift-PAL training.

Subjects

Twenty adult female Sprague–Dawley rats weighing approximately 300 to 400 g were used for the PAL experiment. Five subjects were excluded from all analysis: One failed to learn how to use the touchscreen, two died during surgery, and two VNS rats were removed due to broken cuffs. One rat completed the Shift-PAL task but had to be euthanized prior to completing the two sPAL probe test sessions due to headcap loss. Her data is otherwise included in all analyses. Two groups were used for this experiment: A Sham group that received cuff implants but never received VNS and a VNS group that received VNS paired with their correct selection of matched Stimulus:Location pairs. After successfully learning the Initial-PAL Stimulus:Location associations, rats received VNS cuff implants and were randomized into Sham (n = 8) or VNS (n = 7) groups based on balancing the mean number of Initial-PAL training days between groups.

Analysis

Analyses were performed blind using custom Python and R scripts. For most analysis we employed Restricted Maximum Likelihood linear mixed models with individual rats as random effects using the lme4 R package72, as these models tend to be robust to violations of statistical assumptions and work well with unequal group sizes73. The p-values of fixed effects in these models were derived using F-tests with Satterthwaite approximations for the degrees of freedom using the lmerTest R package74, which tend to produce acceptable Type I error rates even for unbalanced datasets75. Simple comparisons were performed using approximative two-sample Fisher-Pitman permutation tests with 10,000 samples using the coin R package76, as these tests do not depend on distribution assumptions like conventional statistical tests77,78. An alpha cutoff of < 0.05 was used everywhere. Significant improvements in performance measures were considered indicative of learning in all cases. Where appropriate, analyses were conducted with both mixed models and permutation tests to ensure our findings were robust for statistical assumptions which vary across analysis methods. No differences in results were found between methods, and so only one set of statistical results is reported for each comparison. Supplementary Table S1 aggregates our reported statistical comparisons, organized by Experiment and Figure panels. A series of power analyses is included in the Supplementary Methods section.

Results

VNS did not improve motor learning on a knob rotation task

VNS paired with movement during training on a skilled task facilitates plasticity in motor cortex19,20,26,45,79–81, a measure which may correspond to the capacity to learn and execute skilled movements3. Delivering VNS with successful attempts in a pellet-reaching task has also previously been shown to rapidly enhance learning and task performance in mice23. To test if this approach can accelerate motor learning generally, we paired delivery of VNS with forelimb movement during training on a multi-stage, skilled forelimb supination task60, a movement which requires intact motor cortex to perform at expert levels40,46,60–62. Rats first trained on a series of shaping stages (Unweighted, Weighted) which consisted of gradually increasing the difficulty of the task (see Methods). Rats in the VNS group received VNS during all training sessions, including shaping stages. After completing the shaping stage, rats began training with an adaptive threshold until they reached task proficiency (Supination).

Rats in both groups learned to successfully rotate the knob and complete training on the shaping stages. VNS did not accelerate learning during this initial training period for either the Unweighted or Weighted shaping stages, with rats requiring comparable number of training days to meet the task criteria (Fig. 2a, Unweighted and Weighted). After completing the shaping stages, rats moved on to the Supination training stage. This stage introduced an adaptive rotation threshold which required the rats to learn to rotate the knob further and further as they improved, up to a maximum of 60 degrees. VNS failed to accelerate learning during this stage as well, with both VNS and Sham rats requiring a comparable number of days to achieve the performance criteria to be considered fully trained on the supination task (Fig. 2a, Supination). Rats took an average of 30 days to complete the entire training protocol, with no difference in training time between VNS and Sham groups (Fig. 2a, Total).

Although VNS did not lead to overall faster training times at any stage of the task, it is possible that it could have facilitated learning and improved trial success rates at different time points. Rats in both VNS and Sham groups achieved similarly high rates of successful trials during the Weighted shaping stage (Fig. 2b, left). We next examined the first several days of Supination training to see if receiving VNS during training improved performance with the introduction of the adaptive rotation threshold. Rats in both groups improved their success rate over the first seven days of Supination training, but no difference was observed between VNS and Sham performance (Fig. 2b, middle). Success rates at the end of Supination training were also comparable between groups (Fig. 2b, right). Overall, receiving VNS at any point throughout task training did not accelerate skill acquisition compared to Sham controls. Additionaly, rats in all groups performed a similar number of trials (Z = 1.3935, p = 0.1674) and achieved a similar number of hits per session (Z = 1.3578, p = 0.1809) suggesting that VNS also did not substantially alter motivation. These results demonstrate that pairing VNS with correct responses does not necessarily enhance motor learning or performance of the forelimb supination task.

VNS did not accelerate learning for multiple speech discrimination pairs

Pairing VNS with presentation of tones or speech sounds can drive plasticity in auditory networks resembling learning-induced plasticity30,31. This plasticity can increase the neural discriminability between different speech stimuli in multiple auditory cortical areas, a metric that correlates with behavioral discrimination ability of the same sounds30,63,64,67. Previous work has shown that pairing VNS during tone presentations can accelerate reversal learning during auditory classical conditioning35. Stimulation of the locus coeruleus, a neuromodulatory center activated by VNS parameters used in this study24, has also been shown to accelerate reversal learning on tone discrimination15. Neuromodulatory release from the VNS-driven locus coerulues and nucleus basalis may generally improve perceptual abilities17,82–86. To test if VNS can accelerate auditory perceptual learning for multiple tasks, we paired VNS with presentation of speech sounds in healthy adult rats during training on eight separate Go/No-go speech discrimination tasks (Fig. 1, middle row, and Fig. 3a).

Both VNS and Sham rats learned all eight speech discrimination pairs (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. S1). Receiving VNS paired with speech sound presentations during training did not reduce the time needed to learn each speech discrimination to criteria compared to Sham (Fig. 3b). Likewise, VNS and Sham required a comparable number of total training days to learn all eight discriminations (Fig. 3c). The number of presentations for either the Target or Distractor speech sounds was not different between VNS and Sham groups for any of the eight discrimination pairs (F(1,24) = 0.37, p = 0.55), indicating that rats in both groups were equally motivated to complete trials during training. It is possible that receiving VNS during presentation of the speech sounds could prime rats to be more alert and responsive through activation of the attention and arousal neuromodulatory systems. However, there was not a substantial difference in reaction times to either the Target or Distractor speech sounds between VNS and Sham groups in any of the eight discrimination tasks (F(1,24) = 2.71, p = 0.11). These results indicate that VNS paired with active listening did not further enhance perception and attention, improve signal discriminability through facilitation of auditory plasticity, or generally accelerate the overall auditory learning process.

VNS did not enhance learning on a difficult speech discrimination task

Although VNS paired with speech sound presentation did not accelerate learning to discriminate between multiple pairs of speech syllables, it is possible that a more focused application over time could increase signal discriminability in a challenging task. A recent study shows that VNS can augment reinforcement signals to improve both rate of learning and overall behavioral performance23. To test if VNS paired with reinforcement can accelerate learning or enhance performance on a perceptually challenging discrimination, we delivered VNS concurrently with reward during training on a Go/No-go task with multiple spectrotemporally similar speech syllables (Fig. 1, middle row, and Fig. 4a). Whereas the Single-distractor design focused on the speed with which subjects could learn novel discriminations, this Multi-distractor design sought to test the utility of VNS pairing in facilitating learning of difficult discriminations over longer periods of time. Therefore rats simply trained for four weeks on this task, with no pre-determined performance criteria as a training goal.

Discrimination by both VNS and Sham groups improved over the course of training (Fig. 4b and 4c). Rats learned to withhold responses to the distractor speech sounds and continue responding to the target. However, pairing VNS with reward delivery following correct responses to the target sound did not facilitate an increase in the rate of responding to the target or decrease the rates of responding to the distractors (Fig. 4b). Overall performance and learning across four weeks of training was nearly identical regardless of VNS treatment (Fig. 4c). Similar to results from the Single-distractor auditory experiment, there were no group differences in motivation or reaction times between VNS and Sham rats. Rats in both groups completed a comparable number of Target and Distractor trials (F(1,11) = 0.46, p = 0.51). Likewise, pairing VNS with correct responses during 4 weeks of training did not improve reaction times to either the Target or Distractor speech sounds (F(1,11) = 0.63, p = 0.44).

VNS did not facilitate novel or reversal learning on a visual-spatial paired-associates task

VNS has repeatedly been shown to drive plasticity in the hippocampus and cognitive networks, and to enhance memory processes32,37,87,88. Additionally, norepinephrine release in cognitive neural networks driven by VNS may accelerate reversal learning by improving cognitive flexibility36,37. Previous reports have shown that VNS can improve performance in multiple cognitive tasks, such as novel object recognition, passive avoidance, reversal learning, and cognitive flexibility35–37,87,89. However, with the exception of reversal learning on a touchscreen visual discrimination36, these results have focused on same-day or next-day effects from VNS treatments. It remains unknown if VNS can be utilized to generally facilitate learning for challenging cognitive tasks requiring weeks, rather than minutes, of training. To test this hypothesis, we delivered VNS during training on a visual-spatial paired-associates learning (PAL) task containing both novel and reversal learning components.

All rats trained for at least 10 days before any achieved the performance criteria of two sessions with at least 80% correct performance required to be considered proficient in the Shift-PAL task (Fig. 5b). Therefore, we compared initial performances between VNS and Sham groups over the first 10 days of training, as well as how long each group took on average to complete the Shift-PAL task. Rats initially performed worse than chance performance (50% correct) on Shift-PAL, specifically on trials with reversed associations from Initial-PAL (Fig. 5d), but improved above chance over 10 days of training. During this time, VNS did not enhance performance or accelerate learning compared to the Sham controls (Fig. 5a and d). The total number of training days required to meet proficiency criteria for Shift-PAL was also not reduced by delivering VNS during training (Fig. 5b). As in our previous results, no difference was observed between VNS and Sham rats in the number of trials completed per session, indicating that pairing VNS with correct responses did not impact motivation to perform the task. Although no difference in performance or learning was observed between VNS and Sham groups, it was possible that VNS treatment throughout training could have altered the speed with which rats made decisions and selected either the S + or S- stimuli. However, we did not see a difference in time to selection of the matched S + stimulus (Z = 0.29, p = 0.79) or incorrect selection of the mismatched S- stimulus (Z = 0.07, p = 0.95) between groups.

VNS failed to improve visual-spatial novel and reversal learning. Rats had to learn a shifted set of visual Stimulus:Location associations on a touchscreen (Shift-PAL task) after training on an initial set of associations (Initial-PAL, see Methods; no VNS was delivered during this initial training). Rats improved over the first 10 days of training on Shift-PAL (F(1,134) = 182.71, p < 0.001), but there was no difference between VNS and Sham performance (a, F(1,13) = 0.00, p = 0.99) or on the total number of days required to pass Shift-PAL training criteria (b, Z = 0.39, p = 0.69). No difference in performance was found between the last two training sessions for the Shift-PAL task and two sPAL probe test sessions (c, F(1,41.45) = 2.72, p = 0.13), indicating that rats correctly learned the Stimulus:Location associations. Additionally, VNS and Sham rats learned the associations equally well (c, F(1,11.77) = 3.08, p = 0.12). The Shift-PAL task was composed of trials with completely novel S + /S- stimuli and reversed S + /S- stimuli relative to Initial-PAL training (see Methods). Rats initially performed worse on (d, F(1,282) = 88.28, p < 0.001) and had elevated perseveration for (e, F(1,282) = 140.30, p < 0.001) reversed trials compared to novel trials. However there was no difference between VNS and Sham rats on either performance (d, F(1,13) = 0.00, p = 0.96) or perseveration (e, F(1,13) = 2.77, p = 0.12). Dashed grey lines represent chance performance. All values are mean ± sem.

To ensure rats had indeed learned the Stimulus:Location associations explicitly, and not simply six pairs of visual discriminations, we tested them on two sessions of a 'same' PAL (sPAL) probe task (see Methods). In sPAL, the S + and S- both show the same visual stimulus in different locations, one matched and the other mismatched. Performance on the two sPAL probe sessions was not significantly different from performance on the last two Shift-PAL training sessions, or between VNS and Sham groups, indicating that rats in both groups correctly learned the updated Stimulus:Location associations (Fig. 5c). Receiving VNS during training did not lead to the formation of stronger associations.

Although VNS did not improve overall performance or rate of learning on Shift-PAL, it could potentially enhance cognitive flexibility through activation of the noradrenergic locus coeruleus24,36,90,91. Perseverative activity in rodent PAL tasks is quantified by the repeated selection of the mismatched S- stimulus in correction trials (CTs) following an initial wrong answer; more CTs indicates more perseveration as the subject continually selects the incorrect response. Perseveration on reversal trials was initially higher than novel trials, but rats improved over the first ten days of training on Shift-PAL (F(1,282) = 65.48, p < 0.001). Application of VNS during training did not decrease perseverative activity for either reversed or novel trial types compared to the Sham controls (Fig. 5e). Pairing VNS with the selection of correct associations in a PAL task thus did not enhance learning of novel associations, facilitate reversal learning, or increase cognitive flexibility following incorrect responses.

Discussion

Summary of work

Neuroplasticity underlies most forms of learning and memory. VNS drives phasic release of plasticity-inducing neuromodulators including acetylcholine and norepinephrine, thus leading to enhanced neuroplasticity. It is possible that VNS paired with specific neural activity could function as a general aid to learning and memory by facilitating plasticity for task-relevant neural circuits. We tested this hypothesis by delivering VNS in healthy, female adult rats during training on motor, auditory, and visual-spatial tasks. VNS failed to accelerate learning on any of these tasks, suggesting that its plasticity-inducing mechanisms may not generally enhance learning in healthy subjects when repeatedly paired throughout training.

Previous studies have shown that plasticity in auditory circuits driven by acetylcholine or norepinephrine release can lead to improved perceptual performances10,15,16. Likewise, pairing VNS in healthy adult rats on successful trials in motor tasks is known to produce plasticity in primary motor cortex19,20,26,45,79–81. VNS has also repeatedly been shown capable of generating plasticity in cognitive networks and facilitating memory processes37,56,87–89,92,93. Nevertheless, delivering VNS during training on tasks in each of these modalities failed to facilitate learning or lead to any improvements in overall performance.

While it is feasible that our tasks were not sensitive enough to observe potential latent enhancements in, e.g., auditory processing brought about by plasticity or neuromodulatory activity, the consistency of results across distinct task designs and cognitive domains suggests that learning rate in the healthy brain is primarily limited by other processes. Learning-related plasticity may thus generally be an outcome of the entire learning process rather than a bottleneck to it. Indeed, improving the fidelity of neural encoding for an incoming auditory signal through neuromodulatory enhancement of cortical auditory networks may yield little improvement in behavioral performance if a rat is utilizing a sub-optimal task strategy94–97. It may yet be possible to utilize VNS-driven acetylcholine and norepinephrine release to augment top-down operations like strategy selection and refinement. These neuromodulators are suspected to signal expected and unexpected uncertainty during trial-and-error learning, working in concert with dopamine to balance an explore-exploit trade-off that permits the execution of reward maximizing behaviors while still balancing search for better alternatives15,98–101. However, we did not observe any evidence in our tasks that routine delivery of bursts of VNS during learning altered strategy or decision-making processes. Future work should continue to examine the mechanistic and neurophysiological effects of VNS pairing with behavior during learning to verify whether VNS-driven neuromodulator release drives additional plasticity without concomitant improvements in learning, as reported here.

Contrasts with other studies

Other recent studies have reported VNS-driven enhancement of learning and performance by employing VNS pairing paradigms during task training similar to the experiments in this study23,35–37. VNS paired with skilled reaching training in mice led to an increase in performance compared to controls over a two week period23, but only when paired with successful attempts and not when delivered randomly or paired with the initial reaching motion. Two other studies found a VNS enhancement of reversal learning. Rats learned a touchscreen visual discrimination reversal quicker when VNS was paired with presentation of the stimuli36, and ferrets showed improved tone-reward associations during repeated auditory classical conditioning when tones were paired with delivery of VNS35. Both studies found that delivering VNS concurrently with the task stimuli was required for the observed improvements, and that learning was not enhanced if VNS was delivered at other time points during behavior. Finally, VNS was reported to improve cognition and memory performance in healthy adult rats on a variety of tests37. It must be noted that Driskill et al. and Altidor et al. utilized male subjects in their reports, as opposed to our use of female subjects here. Previous VNS studies using both male and female subjects, including clinical trials, have not observed sex-specific differences in treatment outcomes23,102,103. Given that prior published comparions have failed to show a sex-specific effect of VNS, we do not believe the absence of a learning effect in the present study is likely sex-specific. However, this factor cannot be ruled out as a potential explanation for the divergent learning results observed in our experiments.

Our results stand in contrast to earlier reports which suggest that VNS may function as a general learning aid across sensory, motor, and cognitive domains. A common thread from these studies is the relatively short timelines for either training or observation of the primary VNS benefit to performance. Driskill et al. examined same-day effects on cognitive performance37, Lai and David observed VNS enhancements on the 2nd day of pairing (and subsequently switched tone-reward associations every 2 days)35, and both Altidor et al. and Bowles et al. show a primary benefit of VNS within the first 2–3 training sessions23,36. By comparison, our tasks examined the influence of VNS on learning for tasks spanning multiple weeks and requiring thousands of trials to master. Thus the results from our series of experiments should not be purported to be refutations of earlier reports of VNS learning enhancements across different modalities, but rather a demonstration of possible constraints on when VNS may be capable of facilitating learning in healthy subjects.

VNS may facilitate rapid early learning

It is possible that VNS-driven learning enhancements in healthy subjects is largely limited to acute and short-term scenarios, where facilitation of early learning may provide an initial advantage leading to intermediate performance gains. Rapid early learning in motor tasks, such as the VNS-enhanced learning reported by Bowles et al., has been connected with relatively quick reassociations of existing neural activity patterns, while late phase learning of novel or highly skilled movements requires formation of new neural activity patterns over longer periods of time39,104–106. For example, Sadtler et al. show that Rhesus macaques trained to use a brain-computer interface by modulating neural activity in M1 learn novel re-mappings of controller function better and faster if they fall within existing neural population activity structures compared to re-mappings that fall outside of them. VNS-driven release of neuromodulators may facilitate this rapid reassociation of synaptic weights107,108, permitting a short-term boost to flexible learning within existing networks while otherwise failing to accelerate slower learning processes required for novel or difficult tasks109,110. Under this paradigm, we would expect VNS paired with training to facilitate rapid learning of "within" re-mappings, e.g. of brain-computer interfaces106, and potentially fail to facilitate longer term learning of the more difficult "outside" re-mappings105. The forelimb supination task used here has multiple stages and requires weeks of training with thousands of trials before rats reach a plateau in performance62. In contrast, mice in the pellet-reaching task reported by Bowles et al. on average plateau and enter late-phase learning in as few as 100 successful trials. Additionally, Bowles et al. observed that pairing VNS with successful pellet-reaching trials, which led to enhanced motor learning and performance, tended to only modulate the activity of neurons that were already activated by trial success23.

Likewise, straightforward reversals of rewarded stimuli may only require a quick update of simple target/reward associations within existing neural activity patterns109,111. Lai and David share this speculation that VNS may enhance learning through rapid updating of existing neural connections, noting that their positive VNS results may reflect a benefit to learning reward associations within known categories rather than the learning of novel auditory discriminations 35. This pattern also fits a similar report of accelerated tone reversal learning following stimulation of the VNS-associated locus coeruleus15. In comparison, our single-distractor and multi-distractor speech tasks depend on fine discrimination of novel stimuli requiring weeks to master rather than a simple shift in reward association.

Rats in the report from Altidor et al. were fully trained on a two-stimulus visual discrimination task before receiving VNS paired with reversal learning, again potentially requiring only an update of existing reward contingencies to achieve high levels of performance, as in Lai and David or Glennon et al., rather than learning of novel categories or stimuli36. Our visual-spatial Shift-PAL task contained a reversal component, allowing for the possibility of producing a similar VNS enhancement as Altidor et al. VNS paired with training could have accelerated acquisition of reversed trials compared to Sham controls (Fig. 5d and e), independent of possible learning enhancements for novel trial discriminations. We found that VNS failed to facilitate learning of both novel and reversed associations, enhance cognitive flexibility, or increase overall performance. A potential explanation for these contrasting results may be that rats in our task learned specific associations of stimulus patterns and locations rather than the alternative possibility of mastering six separate two-stimulus visual discriminations. This latter case would have permitted simple updating of reversed reward contingencies between a given pair of stimuli, as may have happened in Altidor et al. However, under this outcome we would have expected chance performance (50%) on our sPAL probe test sessions (Fig. 5c) which showed identical visual stimuli in one correct and one incorrect location, as rats that only learned simple discriminations and failed to form specific Stimulus:Location associations during training would be unable to determine which selection should be correct68,69. Instead we found that rats maintained high performance levels, indicating learned assocations of specific patterns with specific locations, regardless of the alternative, incorrect stimulus. It is possible that such a strong association is not as readily reversible as switching reward contingencies between two stimuli in isolation, as evidenced by the significant perseverative behavior initially observed during Shift-PAL training (Fig. 5e), instead requiring the formation of completely novel associations that VNS treatment was unable to facilitate.

Altogether, evidence suggests that a majority of the observed VNS learning benefits to date may occur during a period of reassociation of existing neural activity patterns, rather than accelerating slower neural processes necessary for longer term learning.

Importance of task design

For motor learning specifically, it is also possible that Bowles et al. observed a VNS facilitation of learning and performance for pellet reaching due to a richer set of success and failure signals which VNS could then augment. Failure to reach a pellet, reaching but failing to grasp, knocking a pellet off its pedestal, dropping a pellet in transit, and successfully grabbing and retrieving a pellet all contain a plethora of sensorimotor feedback signals which may be missing in comparison to the binary failure/success outcome separated by a few degrees of knob rotation in our supination task. Explicit cues for success during motor learning have been shown to increase learning and performance in humans, possibly by driving stronger engagement of cognitive and sensorimotor association networks early in training112,113. As VNS has consistently been shown to acutely enhance cognitive network activity37,56,87,89,92,93, pairing VNS with pellet reaching may have augmented association and consolidation of sensorimotor feedback signals during initial training sessions, leading to a lasting benefit over the remaining days of training114. Providing a stronger differentiation of success and failure, or else including additional visual, tactile, or auditory cues corresponding to degree of success (such as a sound normalized to the target rotation threshold which indicates proximity to exceeding it and scoring a successful trial) may have resulted in enhanced learning in our forelimb supination VNS group. Alternatively, rats in our supination task may have indeed experienced a similar hypothetical sensorimotor and cognitive benefit from VNS pairing during the initial training sessions also, but failed to meaningfully manifest such a benefit into increased performance due to the inherent difficulty in learning the task. Such a result would suggest that training and task designs are a critical component for determining whether VNS may or may not be able to enhance motor learning in healthy subjects, and may be significantly limiting factors to the general utility of VNS accelerated learning. In comparison, identical training and VNS pairing procedures during the supination task have consistently increased behavioral recovery after injury28,49,60, indicating that these are not inherently limiting factors of VNS treatment overall.

It is important to evaluate the implication of these mixed results. While exploratory testing of hypotheses can be a very effective strategy for finding conditions under which a given manipulation has a reliable effect, it often remains unknown if it is robust and broad-reaching or else conditional on the particularities of an experiment. We sought to determine whether VNS could act as an overall enhancer for the learning process across tasks and modalities, particularly for difficult tasks requiring weeks of training to master. Given the comprehensive failure to facilitate learning across a range of tasks and skill domains reported here using VNS pairing strategies known to drive neuroplasticity, we consider it unlikely that VNS can be utilized to broadly enhance learning in healthy brains. However, VNS may facilitate some forms of learning through its ability to enhance distributed memory processes generally.

Alternative VNS parameters

VNS-driven plasticity follows an inverted-U dose–response curve, failing to generate plasticity when stimulations are either too intense or not intense enough43,45, or if various VNS parameters such as frequency or train duration are too low or too high115. Interleaving high-intensity stimulations with plasticity-promoting ones is enough to disrupt all VNS-driven plasticity, suggesting that even brief overactivation of cortical receptors by neuromodulators could interfere with plasticity processes81. Several studies reporting a learning enhancement from VNS used a slightly lower intensity setting than we utilized in our experiments36,37, or else individually calibrated intensity settings for each subject23,35. The VNS parameters used in this study are known to be effective in promoting cortical plasticity for multiple modalities in both healthy rats26,30,31,43,44,80 and in disease and injury models25,28,29,32–34,40,46–49, as well as in humans50–54. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that VNS may generally improve learning under a more limited range of stimulation intensities or other alternative parameter settings. For example, a recent review of VNS studies shows that on aggregrate the VNS parameters used in this study are optimal for driving cortical plasticity in healthy rodent models, and behavioral recovery in injury models, but slightly lower intensity (0.8 mA vs. 0.4 mA) may be preferable for enhancing memory specifically115. Additionally, we did not measure neuromodulator release at cortical sites during VNS pairings and thus cannot report the degree to which VNS drove neuromodulatory activity while subjects trained.

VNS in rehabilitation

VNS has repeatedly been shown to improve motor and sensory rehabilitative outcomes in both pre-clinical models and in clinical populations following a variety of injuries to the central nervous system. VNS paired with rehabilitative training is an FDA approved therapy for people with chronic hand and arm deficits due to ischemic stroke102. In these cases, enhanced neuromodulatory signaling through VNS pairing appears to relieve a plasticity constraint derived from the injury and resulting maladaptive plasticity. Facilitation of beneficial neuroplasticity thus leads to faster and better behavioral recovery. The healthy brain, by comparison, may already be optimally interpreting learning signals, and the addition of more feedback through VNS pairing is likely unhelpful. This differential outcome suggests that the process of learning a new skill has fundamental distinctions from the process of re-learning following injury, particularly with respect to the utility of key neuromodulatory signals responsible for driving changes in functional behaviors116,117. It is unclear why intact neuromodulatory signaling can be insufficient to generate recovery after injury without adjunctive VNS therapy, yet similar utility may not be seen for learning generally. Locally maladaptive plasticity constraints which restrict recovery following injury may benefit from the global release of neuromodulators and corresponding global increase in cortical excitation that VNS is known to drive22. Without such maladaptive plasticity constraints, learning in the healthy brain may not benefit from adjunctive VNS signaling. Future work should examine the mechanisms and neural network states that result in these differential outcomes and further clarify the exact role of neuromodulatory signaling in cases where VNS is reported to drive learning enhancements.
