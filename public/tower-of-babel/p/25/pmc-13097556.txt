The relationship between harsh parenting and adolescent cyberbullying: a network analysis
Authors: Jingtao Wu, Yanhong Shao, Jun Hu, Xinjuan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite harsh parenting has been found to increase the risk of developing cyberbullying, there is still a gap in understanding the complex interplay between harsh parenting and cyberbullying in the general population. MethodsThis cross-sectional study aims to examine such a complex relationship between harsh parenting and cyberbullying in adolescents with a network analysis. A total of 1,409 secondary school students successfully completed a set of self-report questionnaires measuring harsh parenting and cyberbullying. To enhance stability, subscales of a questionnaire were used as nodes in the network, which was constructed with edges representing partial correlations between nodes. Network Comparison Tests were used to investigate the differences between participants with high cyberbullying and low cyberbullying.Results(1) Harsh parenting was positively correlated with adolescent cyberbullying, after controlling for the inter-relationships between all nodes in the network; (2) Verbal cyberbullying showed the highest centrality strength, while anonymous identity exhibited the highest intermediary and closeness centrality. The network structure demonstrated sufficient stability; (3) Comparison between high and low cyberbullying subgroups indicated that the high cyberbullying subgroup exhibited higher global strength in the network (strength difference = 0.47, p = 0 0.48). No significant difference was found between the network structures of the two subgroups (maximum difference in edge weights = 0.18, P = 0.43), indicating a small inter-group difference and a highly similar overall connectivity pattern; (4) Regarding geographical regions, no difference was observed in global connectivity strength between Jiangsu and Sichuan (difference = 0.37, P = 0.62), suggesting broad comparability across provinces.ConclusionThese findings underscore the importance of addressing harsh parenting as a potential risk factor for cyberbullying in adolescents and emphasize the need for targeted interventions to mitigate these negative outcomes.

Introduction

Cyberbullying is a form of deliberate and repeated aggression that utilizes electronic devices such as computers and cell phones to humiliate, harass, and abuse individuals who are unable to defend themselves [1]. With the widespread availability of the internet and the emergence of new media, cyberbullying has become increasingly prominent in recent years. According to an online survey conducted by UNICEF, 24% of students aged 10–18 in China have experienced cyberbullying [2]. Studies have also indicated that cyberbullying victimization among adolescents and children ranges from 14% to 57.5% [3], with nearly 12.3% of teenagers experiencing or witnessing online harassment [4].

Cyberbullying is a multidimensional construct, primarily encompassing verbal cyberbullying, anonymous cyberbullying, and false information cyberbullying [5]. Different dimensions exert varying mechanisms of influence on adolescents’ mental health and social behaviors, demonstrating distinct effects [6]. Verbal cyberbullying tends to involve direct linguistic aggression, causing emotional distress to victims [7]. Anonymous cyberbullying, by concealing identities, reduces self-restraint and exacerbates bullying behaviors [8]. False information cyberbullying damages others’ reputations through fabricated claims [9]. Cyberbullying has detrimental effects on adolescents’ physical and mental well-being. Research has highlighted the negative impact of cyberbullying on victims’ self-esteem, leading to psychological issues such as depression, anxiety, and even potential engagement in extreme behaviors [10]. Furthermore, cyberbullying can strain victims’ family and interpersonal relationships, ultimately affecting their social adaptation abilities [1].

However, despite the systemic harms of cyberbullying documented in prior research, investigations into the impact of family-level factors on cyberbullying remain insufficient, with limited in-depth discussion in this area. In particular, research on harsh parenting—an established antecedent of adolescent cyberbullying—is scarce. Moreover, the relationships between different forms of cyberbullying and their underlying mechanisms have yet to be systematically delineated. Clarifying these interrelated mechanisms is essential for informing targeted intervention strategies.

Harsh parenting, characterized by hostility, aggression, and strict discipline toward children [11], has been identified as a key factor in the development of cyberbullying behaviors [12]. Several studies have provided compelling evidence of the link between harsh parenting and subsequent engagement in cyberbullying behaviors [4, 13]. Harsh parenting is characterized by core features such as hostility, coercive control, and emotional rejection, and has been shown to be closely associated with adolescents’ aggression and bullying behaviors. Moreover, paternal and maternal harsh parenting exert differential effects: fathers’ harsh parenting tends to predict externalizing behaviors, whereas mothers’ harsh parenting is more closely linked to emotional dysregulation and maladaptive coping styles. These distinctions suggest that harsh parenting is not a unidimensional construct; rather, multiple pathways may underlie its relationship with bullying.

Moreover, regional and socioeconomic disparities may influence both the prevalence of cyberbullying and the way in which harsh parenting is connected to it. Scholars suggest that economic development and internet accessibility are key determinants of cyberbullying behaviors. Adolescents in eastern coastal cities, where internet usage is more frequent, exhibit higher rates of cyberbullying [14], while their counterparts in western regions, with comparatively limited internet access, experience lower incidence [15]. Modecki et al.‘s meta-analysis highlights that cross-regional variations in cyberbullying are primarily shaped by parenting styles [16]. Further studies confirm that in areas with significant economic and educational disparities, the association between harsh parenting and cyberbullying tends to be more pronounced [17].

Jiangsu, one of China’s most economically advanced eastern provinces, boasts high internet penetration and digital literacy, with adolescents actively engaged in social media platforms [18]. In contrast, Sichuan, located in the mountainous western region, has lower internet accessibility and digital competency, resulting in fewer opportunities for youth to engage with digital media [19]. In regions with low internet penetration, the aforementioned disparities tend to attenuate the association between harsh parenting and cyberbullying. Limited exposure to the internet corresponds to a lower incidence of issues such as online harassment. Conversely, in areas with high internet accessibility, cumulative family risks and harsh parenting are linked to increased cyber aggression. Existing research confirms that individuals who have experienced harsh parenting in family contexts exhibit more pronounced cyberbullying behaviors during internet use [20]. Other scholars have further noted that adolescents subjected to harsh parenting may resort to online platforms to gain a sense of control and power, thereby venting their frustrations [21]. Additionally, studies indicate that adolescents from harsh parenting backgrounds often seek external emotional support, which can exacerbate internet dependency and heighten their vulnerability to cyberbullying [22]. Building on these insights, this study conducts a cross-regional comparison between Jiangsu and Sichuan in central and western China. The aim is to examine whether adolescents’ susceptibility to cyberbullying differs significantly under varying conditions of internet penetration and family socioeconomic environments, and to assess the consistency of such differences.

Previous studies have predominantly employed regression models that treat harsh parenting as a single predictor of cyberbullying. Although these models yield interpretable findings, their underlying assumptions of linearity and unidirectionality are insufficient to capture the interrelationships among different forms of cyberbullying or the transmission of family-related risks. Theoretically, cyberbullying arises from a confluence of multiple interrelated factors rather than being triggered by a single variable. In this regard, network analysis offers a more theoretically congruent perspective, conceptualizing harsh parenting and cyberbullying as dynamically interconnected within a network system. This approach identifies key nodes and bridge connections, thereby elucidating the specific pathways through which harsh parenting is linked to cyberbullying. Consequently, network analytic techniques are particularly well-suited for capturing the complexity, heterogeneity, and interdependence inherent in adolescent cyberbullying.

The mechanisms and theoretical foundations linking harsh parenting to adolescent cyberbullying

Harsh parenting is a highly controlling and punitive educational management style characterized by physical punishment, verbal abuse, and excessive emotional detachment [23]. In such family environments, fathers and mothers often neglect the psychological and emotional needs of their children [24], exhibiting strong desires for control, excessive interference in daily behaviors, and frequent negative emotional feedback [25]. Emotional neglect is particularly common, where parents fail to provide the necessary emotional support and validation, reflecting low empathic capacity [26]. This stems from an excessive need for dominance, as parents attempt to mold “obedient” children, ultimately damaging parent-child relationships and impairing mental health.

Harsh parenting has significant adverse effects on the psychological well-being and social behaviors of children and adolescents. Research indicates that this parenting style undermines children’s sense of social security, exacerbates psychological distress (e.g., depression and anxiety), and impairs social adaptability [27]. Over time, it disrupts emotional regulation, leading to emotional dysregulation and extreme behavioral responses [28]. At the behavioral level, harsh parenting is associated with increased aggression, violence, antisocial tendencies, and academic decline [29]. Longitudinal studies further confirm its intergenerational effects, where individuals subjected to harsh parenting in childhood replicate similar behaviors in adulthood, perpetuating a vicious cycle within families [30]. Cultural context also plays a critical role—while harsh parenting may be normalized in collectivist cultures, its negative consequences are more pronounced in individualistic societies [31].

Recent studies have begun exploring the internal mechanisms linking harsh parenting to adolescent cyberbullying. One key mediator is impaired emotional regulation: punitive and emotionally detached parenting fosters maladaptive coping strategies, increasing the likelihood of resorting to online aggression when facing conflict or frustration [27]. Additionally, adolescents under harsh parenting may internalize stress, expressing it through problematic online behaviors, with the anonymity of digital spaces further facilitating cyberbullying [32]. Furthermore, the erosion of parent-child bonds due to harsh parenting can lead to social isolation, prompting adolescents to seek emotional solace online—a context where cyberbullying frequently occurs [33].

In summary, harsh parenting demonstrates robust associations with multiple dimensions of adolescent cyberbullying, yet further research is needed to disentangle their intricate relationships. Grounded in social network theory [34], harsh parenting may exacerbate adolescents’ cyberbullying behaviors by fostering hostility, which can then spill over into the digital realm, manifesting specifically in verbal attacks online. Network analysis offers a novel methodological lens to examine the interplay between these variables, enabling the identification of key pathways, weight distributions, and underlying mechanisms. Based on the theoretical framework outlined above, this study proposes confirmatory hypotheses and tests them by examining edge-level associations within a partial correlation network.

H1: In the partial correlation network, harsh parenting nodes are positively associated with various cyberbullying nodes—namely, verbal bullying, anonymous identity, and false information bullying.

The linking mechanism between harsh parenting and cyberbullying in social network structures

The prevalence of digital and online information is influenced by socioeconomic and cultural factors, leading to regional variations in the relationship between harsh parenting and adolescent cyberbullying. Specifically, in regions with lower digital media penetration, adolescents have limited internet access, resulting in reduced cyberbullying incidence [35]. Digital media environments, to some extent, constrain real-time cyberbullying scenarios, demonstrating high contextual dependency [36]. However, research indicates that harsh parenting undermines adolescents’ social support systems and emotional regulation capacities, thereby increasing risks of both traditional bullying and cyberbullying [37]. The moderating role of regional internet accessibility is evident in the link between harsh parenting and cyberbullying. Specifically, greater accessibility exacerbates the effect, while limited accessibility reduces it.

In areas with higher digital media penetration, the virtual and anonymous nature of online environments provides adolescents with more opportunities for cyberbullying. For instance, adolescents raised in harsh parenting households often experience emotional deprivation, driving them to seek validation and social support in cyberspace—frequently through aggressive behaviors or participation in online harassment [38]. The virtual setting facilitates verbal aggression and abusive language, as perpetrators can target victims without face-to-face confrontation, deriving psychological gratification from such acts. This phenomenon is particularly prevalent in regions with advanced digital infrastructure [39].

Building upon the contextual framework, this study further examines the differential effects of maternal versus paternal parenting on adolescent cyberbullying. Within harsh parenting families, fathers typically adopt more punitive and controlling roles, which significantly impacts adolescents’ mental health [27]. Compared to mothers, fathers are more likely to employ violent disciplinary measures, including verbal reprimands, physical punishment, and severe sanctions [31]. Martínez-Ferrer et al. found that paternal and maternal parenting behaviors differentially affect adolescents’ school adaptation and online aggression, with fathers’ harshness being more strongly associated with externalizing behaviors (e.g., verbal attacks) in digital spaces [40]. Prolonged exposure to such parenting fosters emotional alienation and psychological distress, exacerbating negative affect.

The combined analysis of regional and parental roles revealed that paternal harsh parenting showed a particularly strong and identifiable association with externalized cyberbullying in high internet-penetration areas, as opposed to maternal parenting or areas with lower penetration. Studies suggest that fathers’ authoritarian discipline and intense harsh parenting exhibit a high “centrality index” with cyberbullying prevalence. This correlation reflects adolescents’ tendency to weaponize online aggression as an outlet for emotional venting and psychological compensation [41]. Among high-frequency cyberbullying groups, adolescents demonstrate broader and more intensive social media interactions, which serve as conduits for negative affect discharge—effectively amplifying the adverse effects of paternal harsh parenting [42].

Despite existing research elucidating the paternal harsh parenting-cyberbullying linkage, critical gaps remain. First, the underlying mechanisms—particularly the operationalization of the “centrality index” of paternal discipline in cyberbullying networks—lack empirical validation. Second, the amplifying role of high-intensity online social networks in facilitating cyberbullying requires granular examination. To address the aforementioned gaps, grounded in the theory of paternal harsh parenting, this study proposes the following confirmatory hypotheses regarding network nodes.

H2: Within the network structure, paternal harsh parenting exhibits higher centrality compared to other parenting nodes.

H3: Significant differences exist in overall cyberbullying intensity between the high and low cyberbullying groups, which will be tested through network comparison.

Examining regional differences in the association between harsh parenting and cyberbullying

The internal associations between harsh parenting and cyberbullying may vary across regions, manifesting in differences in cultural background, socioeconomic status, digital environment, and other resource-related factors. Regional variations—primarily shaped by economic development, cultural values, and digital media penetration—serve as key moderating variables in this relationship [43].

In economically disadvantaged regions, heightened financial pressures on parents often exacerbate the frequency and severity of harsh parenting practices [29]. León-Moreno identify social adaptation as a critical mediator in understanding adolescents’ online aggressive behaviors [44]. Within resource-constrained environments, limited access to educational and emotional support systems impairs adolescents’ ability to regulate emotions, driving them toward virtual spaces where linguistic aggression and conflict emerge as outlets for frustration—ultimately increasing their likelihood of becoming cyberbullying perpetrators [45]. Conversely, in economically developed areas, families benefit from superior educational resources and social capital, which mitigate parental anxiety and buffer the adverse effects of harsh parenting [46]. However, higher digital connectivity in these regions also expands opportunities for cyberbullying, rendering such behaviors more covert and complex [26].

Cultural values fundamentally shape parenting norms and adolescent outcomes. Despite the prevailing characterization of China as a collectivistic society, substantial regional heterogeneity exists, evidenced by disparities between urban and rural areas, socioeconomic status, and cultural contexts. In collectivist-oriented families, harsh parenting may be normalized as a societal expectation, with parents employing strict behavioral standards to foster conformity and collective well-being [47]. Adolescents in such contexts exhibit greater acceptance of parental authority, stronger psychological resilience, and robust social support networks, resulting in lower negative affect [48]. In contrast, individualist cultures often perceive harsh parenting as emotional neglect or power abuse, leading to physical punishment, verbal hostility, and emotional deprivation. These practices fuel adolescent distress, prompting them to seek emotional release through digital platforms [49]. As Romero-Abrio et al. demonstrate, self-centric ideologies mediate the link between harsh parenting and cyberbullying victimization [50]. It should be noted that cultural factors and the level of internet penetration not only interact with each other but also indirectly shape the mechanisms behind behaviors like cyberbullying. This interplay can lead adolescents to use online platforms as an outlet for emotional expression. Furthermore, cultural values influence family power dynamics: collectivist settings may see cyberbullying weaponized to uphold group interests, whereas individualist contexts correlate with personal anger, emotional dysregulation, and psychological imbalance [51].

Digital infrastructure further moderates the harsh parenting-cyberbullying nexus. Regions with low digital penetration exhibit reduced cyberbullying incidence due to limited online access, creating a form of physical insulation [16]. In contrast, high-connectivity areas leverage the anonymity and invisibility of digital platforms to facilitate cyberbullying. Here, harsh parenting exacerbates adolescents’ psychological distress, driving them to exploit online “freedom” and elevating cyberbullying risks [52]. Excessive digital engagement also equips adolescents with sophisticated tools to execute or evade cyberbullying, indirectly intensifying harsh parenting’s role in perpetuating victimization [53].

In summary, regional differences modulate the harsh parenting–cyberbullying relationship through three pathways: (1) Economic development determines resource availability and parenting stress levels; (2) Cultural values shape adolescents’ interpretations of and responses to harsh parenting; (3) Digital penetration alters the online environment’s accessibility and anonymity. Future research should employ cross-cultural and cross-regional comparisons to decipher the mechanistic interplay of these moderators and develop context-specific interventions to mitigate harsh parenting’s impact on cyberbullying.

Drawing upon comparative observations across different regions of China, and considering the socioeconomic and cultural disparities as well as varying levels of internet penetration between the central-western regions, this study builds upon prior research. Existing literature has established that regions with higher internet accessibility tend to exhibit a higher incidence of cyberbullying [26, 35–39], and that socioeconomic status influences exposure to cyberbullying, which is often correlated with the frequency and intensity of internet use [14, 18]. Given the paucity of prior research employing network analysis for regional comparisons, the following hypothesis is proposed as exploratory in nature.

H4: Differences exist between Jiangsu and Sichuan provinces in network structure—specifically in closeness, strength, and betweenness—with respect to the association between harsh parenting and cyberbullying.

Methods

Participants

This study employed a non-probability convenience sampling method, recruiting participants from secondary schools in Leshan City (Sichuan Province) and Yancheng City (Jiangsu Province) based on the institutional affiliations of research team members. A total of 1,573 secondary school students were recruited for this study, with 608 participants from Leshan City, Sichuan Province, and 965 participants from Yancheng City, Jiangsu Province. Prior to the survey, informed consent was obtained from the school principals, students, and their parents. Additionally, the class teachers were provided with a clear understanding of the purpose and procedures of the questionnaire administration to mitigate any potential confusion or cognitive biases that could impact the validity of the results. During the survey, a qualified psychologist was present to oversee and interpret the process, ensuring the well-being of the participants. Participants were given the opportunity to withdraw from the study if they experienced any psychological discomfort. Following completion of the questionnaire within a designated time frame of 10 min, the class teachers collected the questionnaires. Importantly, to minimize potential systematic biases associated with the questionnaire, reverse items were included. If a participant failed to correctly answer more than three reverse items, their data were excluded from the analysis [54].

After the survey, data exclusion procedures were implemented. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) individuals with excessively homogeneous responses; (2) individuals with a family history of mental illness; (3) individuals with recent mental illness; (4) individuals with missing or incomplete data; (5) individuals failing to correctly answer more than three reverse items. Eventually, 1409 people were recruited, including 891 people from Jiangsu Province with an average age of 15. years (SD = 1.7), 48.8% (n = 435) of whom were male and 51.2% (n = 56) of whom were female, and 518 people from Sichuan Province with an average age of 15.3 years, 47.5% (n = 246) of whom were male and 52.5% (n = 272) of whom were female. The study ultimately recruited 1,409 valid participants, comprising 681 males (48.3%) and 728 females (51.7%). The sample included 518 students from Leshan City, Sichuan Province and 891 students from Yancheng City, Jiangsu Province. Participants ranged in age from 12 to 18 years, with a mean age of 15.2 years (SD = 1.7). This study was approved by the Academic Committee of Leshan Normal University (LNU20240108). All participants and their parents provided written informed consent.

Measures

Harsh parenting scale

The measurement of harsh parenting was conducted using the Harsh Parenting Scale, translated by Wang [11] based on Simons et al.‘s scale [55]. Participants were asked to provide self-reports on their experiences of parental treatment in their daily lives. The questionnaire assessed harsh parenting behaviors using 8 items (4 for fathers and 4 for mothers), rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“very often”). An example item is: “When I make mistakes, my father exhibits anger toward me, including shouting.” Composite scores were calculated separately for paternal and maternal harsh parenting. In the network analysis, the two subscales were treated as distinct nodes to reflect their theoretical distinction and further enhance interpretability. Additionally, the use of subscale-level nodes, rather than item-level nodes, reduced the number of parameters to be estimated given the sample size, thereby improving the robustness and reliability of the regularized network and centrality estimates [56]. The scale demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s α value of 0.878. Additionally, the fit indices, including χ²/df = 2.872, IFI = 0.937, CFI = 0.927, TLI = 0.912, GFI = 0.927, AGFI = 0.907, and RMSEA = 0.062, further supported the adequacy of the measurement model.

Cyberbullying scale

The measurement of adolescents’ cyberbullying was carried out using the revised Chinese version of the Cyberbullying Scale by You [57]. This scale comprises two subscales: the Cyberbullying Victimization Scale (12 items, e.g., “Someone has shared or used my personal photos or videos online without my consent”) and the Cyberbullying Perpetration Scale (8 items, e.g., “When conversing with someone online and things don’t go my way, I may use offensive language to insult them”). Items were rated on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (Never happened) to 4 (Frequently happened), with higher scores indicating greater involvement in cyberbullying. The scale demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s α value of 0.952. Formal tests of measurement invariance across regions were not conducted; however, identical instruments and scoring procedures were applied to all participants, and the scale has been previously validated in Chinese adolescent samples. Additionally, the fit indices, including χ²/df = 2.018, IFI = 0.906, CFI = 0.942, TLI = 0.934, GFI = 0.925, AGFI = 0.937, and RMSEA = 0.062, further supported the adequacy of the measurement model.

Data analysis

Descriptive analysis

The survey data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0. The mean and standard deviation were calculated for all variables. Gender differences in these variables were examined using independent sample t-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA). Additionally, group differences in these variables between high levels of cyberbullying and low levels of cyberbullying were examined. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Network construction and centrality estimation

In the first step, we conducted partial correlation network estimation to analyze the relationship between harsh parenting and cyberbullying variables. Specifically, we calculated partial correlation coefficients while retaining direct connections between nodes. To determine the number and range of edges, we employed the EBICglasso procedure recommended by Epskamp and Fried [56], which effectively reduced estimation errors in parameter estimation by setting other parameters to zero. Furthermore, we utilized a tuning parameter (γ) of 0.5 in the EBICglasso procedure [58], which represents the commonly recommended value balancing model sparsity and sensitivity in psychological network estimation. Finally, we employed the Fruchterman and Reingold [59] algorithm to enhance the centrality and compactness of nodes, distinguishing between virtual and actual connections using dashed lines, and placing core nodes in the center of the graph, as depicted in Fig. 1.

In the second step, we calculated centrality indices, including node strength, closeness centrality, and betweenness centrality [60–62] for the variables. Additionally, we examined the predictability and expected influence (EI) of each node [63]. Potential confounding variables—such as gender, age, parental education level, and income—were not included in the node-level network analysis, as the primary focus was on the relationships among the main variables rather than on demographic predictors.

In the third step, we analyzed the association between the variables. Firstly, we assessed the presence of multicollinearity within each group of variables, and no significant multicollinearity issues were found. Subsequently, following the guidelines proposed by Tonidandel and LeBreton [64] for partitioning explained variance, we examined the correlations between different variables, as well as their relative contributions and explained variances.

Network structure measurement

In the Network Structure Measurement, a two-tailed test with 1,000 iterations was employed to examine the differences in network structure, globality, and edge invariance between the two groups [65]. Data collected from Jiangsu and Sichuan provinces were pooled and analyzed. Based on the composite cyberbullying scores, participants were categorized into quantile groups. Scores were calculated according to the scale’s scoring manual, with items summed and averaged in a consistent direction. Using the overall distribution from the full sample, individuals scoring at or above the 75th percentile were classified as the High Cyberbullying Group, while those at or below the 25th percentile were classified as the Low Cyberbullying Group. The intermediate group was excluded from subsequent comparative analyses [66]. The analysis and testing procedures were primarily conducted using JASP software, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. By comparing the observed network measures to the null distribution generated through random permutations, we assessed whether the observed differences in network structure between the groups were statistically significant. In this study, H1 to H3 are confirmatory hypotheses, while H4 is an exploratory hypothesis.

Results

Sample characteristics

Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the study sample. Compared to the female participants, the male participants exhibited a higher prevalence of being only children and reported higher monthly family incomes (p < 0.05). Furthermore, certain items pertaining to harsh parenting practices by both mothers and fathers were significantly higher among males in comparison to females (p < 0.05). However, no statistically significant gender differences were observed in terms of cyberbullying (p > 0.05). Moreover, the results of network structure analysis revealed no significant differences between male and female participants (i.e., the strength difference was 0.47, p = 0.48) and overall network invariance (i.e., the maximum difference in edge-weights was 0.18, p = 0.43). The results indicated that gender did not significantly alter the network structure. However, no significant difference was observed in the level of cyberbullying (P > 0.05). Furthermore, network comparison tests revealed no significant differences between males and females in either global strength (strength difference = 0.47, P = 0.48) or network structure (maximum edge weight difference = 0.18, P = 0.43), indicating a small magnitude of difference and suggesting that the overall network patterns were largely consistent across genders. Furthermore, the findings confirmed H1, which posited a positive association between harsh parenting and cyberbullying within the network structure.

Network structure of the main dataset

Figure 1 presents the resultant regularized network of the main dataset, which primarily encompasses 14 nodes in the aspects of mother’s harsh parenting, father’s harsh parenting, verbal cyberbullying, anonymous identity, and fabricated cyberbullying. Figure 1 reveals that verbal cyberbullying exhibits the highest centrality strength, indicating it maintains the most robust connections with other nodes in the network and serves as the core node linking harsh parenting to adolescent cyberbullying behaviors. The anonymous identity node demonstrates both high centrality and closeness centrality, suggesting its pivotal bridging role within the network structure—a pattern likely attributable to the concealed and complex nature of anonymous interactions. Notably, false-information cyberbullying displays relatively lower centrality and weaker network connectivity, reflecting its comparatively lower prevalence among adolescent cyberbullying manifestations. After controlling for the associations between nodes, it was found that there are positive correlations between mother’s harsh parenting, father’s harsh parenting, fabricated cyberbullying and verbal cyberbullying, anonymous identity. Additionally, there is a negative correlation between Item 8 of fabricated cyberbullying victimization and Item 1 of verbal cyberbullying. The network analysis in this study validates Hypothesis H2.

Network inference

Figure 2 presents the centrality indices of the 14 nodes. Remarkably, CB2 and CB3, representing verbal cyberbullying, exhibited the highest strength centrality, followed by CB6, representing anonymous identity, and CB4, representing verbal cyberbullying. These findings suggest strong connections with other nodes in the network. Moreover, CB6, representing anonymous identity, demonstrated the highest betweenness centrality, signifying its potential role as a critical bridge connecting different nodes within the network structure. Additionally, CB6 also displayed the highest closeness centrality, indicating its close proximity to other nodes in the network.

Network stability and accuracy

In the main dataset, based on 1,000 bootstrap iterations, both the stability and accuracy of the network indicated reliable edge weights. The CS-coefficients for node strength and expected influence were 0.68 and 0.71, respectively, both exceeding the threshold of 0.50, indicating that the centrality estimates demonstrated good stability in the current study sample. Additionally, the CS-coefficients for closeness and betweenness were 0.73 and 0.67, also reflecting satisfactory stability. It is important to emphasize that this stability reflects the robustness of the current dataset and does not imply that the same centrality ordering would be fully replicable across different populations or contexts.

Discussion

This study examined the association between harsh parenting and cyberbullying among adolescents using network analysis. By treating variables as nodes and partial correlations as edges, the estimated regularized network identified conditional associations between harsh parenting and different forms of cyberbullying. Importantly, these findings reflect statistical interrelations within a cross-sectional network framework and should not be interpreted as evidence of directional or causal effects.

The relationship between harsh parenting and adolescent cyberbullying

The study identified positive associations between harsh parenting and multiple cyberbullying dimensions. Specifically, we found that harsh parenting is positively connected with verbal cyberbullying, anonymous identity, and fabricated cyberbullying, which is consistent with the literature [17, 20, 67–69]. According to the study on 665 students, significant associations exist between harsh parenting and cyberbullying [70]. Surprisingly, we found stronger associations between harsh parenting and verbal cyberbullying than anonymous identity and fabricated cyberbullying. Zurcher et al. indicated that harsh and aggressive parenting styles can significantly predict higher levels of adolescent cyberbullying, particularly in the form of verbal harassment, with this association being even more pronounced in instances of anonymous online attacks [71].

Guided by the social learning theory [72], it can be observed that parents who employ harsh parenting practices fail to provide children with appropriate emotional and behavioral regulation strategies. Consequently, individuals may develop heightened emotional sensitivity and maintain a state of heightened vigilance towards anger signals and negative emotions in their surroundings, leading them to overreact to others’ behaviors in a self-defensive mindset [73]. Due to the inability to employ effective emotional strategies and problem-solving approaches, they are more susceptible to experiencing isolation and even bullying, particularly in the form of verbal aggression [74]. The findings of the study underscore the importance of cyberbullying on online aggression and victimization.

Beyond these findings, several additional factors warrant consideration. Both harsh parenting and cyberbullying were assessed using self-reported questionnaire data, which may have inflated the observed associations. Moreover, the authoritative role of parents and the broader Chinese cultural context may have influenced adolescents’ willingness or ability to accurately report their experiences, potentially introducing reporting bias.

Overall, these findings are consistent with Hypothesis 1, which proposed positive associations between harsh parenting and multiple forms of adolescent cyberbullying within the network structure.

Centrality metrics in network analysis

The study indicated that cyberbullying through verbal aggression exhibited the highest strength centrality, while anonymous identity demonstrated the highest betweenness centrality and closeness centrality in the network structure of harsh parenting and cyberbullying, which is consistent with previous studies [75, 76].

In the context of verbal aggression, the factors contribute to its high centrality. Firstly, the ease of dissemination and amplification of verbal aggression through online platforms allows it to reach a larger audience compared to other forms of cyberbullying, which may increase its potential impact on victims [77, 78]. Secondly, the use of derogatory language and abusive speech in cyberbullying through verbal aggression can deeply affect individuals, causing significant emotional distress and psychological harm, including feelings of humiliation, anxiety, and depression. In the context of anonymous identity, there are explanations leading to its betweenness centrality and closeness as follows. On the other hand, anonymous individuals play a crucial role in controlling the flow of information and interactions among other participants by acting as intermediaries or conduits, connecting different individuals or groups involved in cyberbullying and increasing their betweenness centrality. Furthermore, due to their active participation in cyberbullying activities, anonymous individuals may have a higher closeness centrality, forming a tightly connected subgroup within the network and having closer ties with other individuals involved in cyberbullying [9, 79].

Kardefelt-Winther also found that individuals who experience cyberbullying, based on the theory of compensatory internet use, tend to immerse themselves further in the online world [80, 81]. They seek emotional comfort and exhibit higher closeness and betweenness centrality, consistent with the centrality results of this study. Overall, these findings once highlight the prominence of cyberbullying through verbal aggression and the role of anonymity in the network structure of harsh parenting and cyberbullying. It should be noted that centrality estimates may be influenced by sample composition and measurement context. Although the CS-coefficients for centrality in this dataset fell within an acceptable range, caution is warranted when interpreting these indicators, as the ranking of centrality may vary across different populations and may fluctuate depending on factors such as geographic region, age, and risk level. Extrapolation of these findings should therefore be undertaken with careful consideration of the specific context. As such, the centrality results reported in this study should be understood as reflecting the relative importance of nodes within the current cross-sectional network, rather than serving as causal indicators or as universal characteristics applicable across populations. With respect to H2, paternal harsh parenting was found to be interconnected with cyberbullying nodes, with elevated centrality observed specifically for verbal bullying and anonymous identity bullying. Accordingly, Hypothesis 2 received partial support.

Network structural differences between high- and low-cyberbullying groups

The study revealed no significant differences between adolescents who experienced high levels of cyberbullying and those who experienced low levels of cyberbullying, which is consistent with previous research [9–84]. First, adolescents reporting higher levels of harsh parenting also tended to report greater internalizing difficulties and emotional inhibition. When confronted with online verbal aggression, they may be more inclined to seek comfort and support in digital environments. This patterned response appears consistent across different exposure subgroups, which may attenuate the measured intensity of cyberbullying and diminish observable between-group differences [85, 86]. This represents one possible interpretative framework rather than a confirmed developmental pathway. Furthermore, overall sample heterogeneity and the methodological distinctions inherent in quantile-based grouping may further reduce the statistical power to detect such differences [16]. This issue also relates to the relative stability of network structures and varying degrees of exposure intensity, as articulated in Hypothesis H4 of this study. In summary, the lack of significant between-group differences likely reflects both the structural consistency of network measures and a convergence in coping strategies across groups, rather than merely indicating an absence of meaningful variation.

Second, most prior research has focused on narrowly defined or small samples, where group differences are easier to detect. By using broad, epidemiological screening and a diverse sample, the present study diluted the magnitude of such differences [16]. Although we did not observe significant between-group differences, our network-level metrics confirmed the robustness of these null findings: the structural strength of cyberbullying ties remained equivalent across high- and low-victimization groups, underscoring the universal reach of harsh parenting’s influence rather than its confinement to particular subpopulations [42, 82].

Contrary to Hypothesis 3, the global strength of the high-cyberbullying group did not significantly exceed that of the low-cyberbullying group. This suggests structural similarity rather than intensified connectivity among adolescents with higher cyberbullying involvement.

The moderating role of regional context in the link between harsh parenting and cyberbullying

Comparison between the two regions revealed that overall connectivity levels were largely comparable (with a non-significant difference in global strength), while differences in network structure were observed, though these differences were relatively localized, which aligns with previous research findings [27, 87–90]. Factors such as regional economic conditions, cultural influences, social cognition, and parenting styles are associated with the network structure of harsh parenting and cyberbullying. However, there were no significant differences in the network structure across different regions within the participant groups [91–93].

The lack of significant differences in the network structure across different regions within the participant groups, including Jiangsu and Sichuan provinces, can be attributed to several underlying reasons [14, 18, 39]. Firstly, it may be attributed to the prevailing cultural and economic similarities that outweigh any potential variations, indicating the influence of shared societal norms and values that transcend regional boundaries. Secondly, it may also be attributed to the inclusion of a diverse and representative sample from both Jiangsu and Sichuan provinces, suggesting that participants’ experiences of harsh parenting and cyberbullying are influenced by similar factors irrespective of their regional location. The finding highlights the universal nature of the relationship between harsh parenting, cyberbullying, and their network structures across different regions [16, 94].

In relation to Hypothesis 4, although regional socioeconomic differences were evident, the overall network structure and global strength did not significantly differ between Jiangsu and Sichuan. Thus, Hypothesis 4 was not supported at the structural network level.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be noted. Firstly, reliance on self-report questionnaires may introduce cognitive and social-desirability biases; mixed-method designs (e.g., behavioral observations, peer nominations, or digital trace data) are needed to triangulate findings and enhance methodological rigor. Second, the use of non-probability convenience sampling in this study limits the generalizability of the findings, particularly with regard to the network structure. Although bootstrap sampling was employed to verify the stability of the network, this approach remains insufficient. Future research should consider adopting probability sampling and multi-stage sampling strategies, and conduct systematic sensitivity analyses to further assess the stability and replicability of the network structure. Thirdly, geographical restriction to two provinces limits generalizability; future work should expand the sample to additional regions and sociodemographic strata to test the universality of the observed patterns.

Fourthly, although network analysis elucidates relational structures, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; longitudinal and multi-wave designs are required to disentangle temporal ordering and causal pathways. Fifth, current network metrics did not incorporate potentially confounding contextual factors—such as family dynamics, peer relationships, and school climates—that could moderate observed associations. Integrating these layers into future network models will yield a more holistic and nuanced understanding of cyberbullying processes. Alternatively, it is possible that the observed cross-provincial invariance in network structures reflects similarities in participants’ response styles rather than true structural equivalence. This distinction warrants further investigation in future research.

Conclusion

The findings of the study can be summarized as follows: (1) Harsh parenting is positively correlated with cyberbullying dimensions—verbal cyberbullying, anonymous identity, and fabricated cyberbullying; (2) In the network structure, verbal cyberbullying (CB2 and CB3) show the highest centrality strength, while anonymous identity (CB6) exhibits the highest intermediary and closeness centrality, and the network structure demonstrates sufficient stability; (3) There were no significant differences between adolescents who experienced high levels of cyberbullying and those who experienced low levels of cyberbullying; (4) There are no significant regional differences in the network structure of harsh parenting and cyberbullying.
