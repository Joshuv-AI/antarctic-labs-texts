Cyberlegs X-Leg at CYBATHLON 2024: insights on the control development of an active transfemoral prosthesis
Authors: Stefano Nuzzo, Sophia Taddei, Eligia Alfio, Rossana Lovecchio, Menthy Denayer, Stijn Hamelryckx, Elias Thiery, Stijn Kindt, María Alejandra Díaz, Louis Flynn, Kevin De Pauw, Tom Verstraten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Powered lower-limb prostheses aim to enhance the mobility of people with amputations in daily life. The CYBATHLON competition offers an opportunity to evaluate these devices’ performance across 10 tasks inspired by everyday activities, such as sitting and standing, avoiding obstacles, walking on uneven terrain and slopes, and climbing stairs. This study reports on the development and deployment of the CYBERLEGs X-Leg, a powered knee–ankle prosthesis equipped with a hierarchical control architecture and task-specific finite state machines (FSMs) during the CYBATHLON 2024 leg prosthesis race. The pilot, an individual with a transfemoral amputation, was trained for approximately 46 h, attempted eight tasks during the competition, and completed three. Overall, the pilot successfully controlled the prosthesis’s behavior to execute multiple tasks. The prosthesis delivered stable knee-swing flexion during walking (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 65^\circ $$\end{document}), obstacle-clearance trajectories driven by thigh kinematics, and knee-extension assistance during sit-to-stand (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx -120$$\end{document} Nm). The control design and competition experience underlined the importance of initiating control co-design at earlier development stages and revealed the extensive tuning effort required for a multitask FSM-based system. Performance analysis identified reliance on manual locomotion mode selection, sensitivity in threshold-based FSM transitions, and user discomfort at higher torque levels as key areas for improvement. The competition highlighted both the feasibility and the limitations of FSM-based control when deployed across diverse functional activities. These findings motivate future work on autonomous task recognition, smoother inter-state control blending, the integration of enriched sensing modalities, and human-in-the-loop optimization for user-specific personalization.

Introduction

Worldwide, it is estimated that there are forty million people with amputation, with 90% of these having lower-limb amputations [1]. Amputation can result from several causes, such as traumatic events, vascular problems (i.e., necrosis due to progression of atherosclerosis or diabetic complications), cancer, infection, or congenital causes [2]. In addition to physical symptoms, people with lower limb amputation are also affected by sleep deprivation, anxiety, and social dysfunction [3, 4]. The latter can be mitigated by using prosthetic devices, which are associated with an increase in quality of life and satisfaction [4]. However, due to their limitations and functionality, these devices have not yet fully met the expectations and needs of the target population. For example, people with lower-limb amputations are a group at high risk of falling [5], with studies reporting that 50% of the population experiences at least one fall per year, often leading to injuries and requiring medical attention [6, 7], reducing their confidence and social participation. Among the areas of improvement, functional, ergonomic (e.g., less pain), and psycho-cognitive (e.g., social integration) aspects can be listed [2]. These aspects are intrinsically related to each other, and solutions cannot be provided solely by prosthetic device functionality but must also consider the prosthetic interface (e.g., socket), design, and appearance. [2].

Most individuals use passive prostheses that allow the storage and return of energy, but with reduced capacity compared to human limbs [8]. Alternatively, microprocessor-controlled knees (MPKs) are designed to actively modulate joint damping/resistance via sensors and algorithms for improved adaptability across terrains or speeds [9]. However, neither device type generates net positive power or biological-level torque. This strongly limits the actions that can be attempted, especially in individuals with weakness in their contralateral limb, such as climbing stairs with a step-over-step strategy or standing from a seat, while equally loading weight on both limbs. The reduced balance and gait symmetry, observed with passive prostheses and MPKs, represent a key problem, leading to increased energy consumption and long-term consequences such as low-back pain, osteoarthritis, or osteoporosis [10].

Today, the development of advanced hardware, such as powered prostheses, aims to address gait impairments and long-term consequences [8] by providing positive net work to the user [11–13]. Commonly used controllers include trajectory-tracking controllers (e.g., position or torque-profile), impedance controllers [14, 15], myoelectric controllers [16, 17], and model-based controllers [18], which rely on mechanical or neuromuscular representations [7, 19]. Myoelectric and neuromuscular model-based strategies aim to exploit physiological signals to provide more intuitive and adaptive control, but they often require complex calibration and signal processing, and struggle to maintain consistent performance in different scenarios [7, 20]. Currently, powered prosthetic legs are typically controlled with impedance and trajectory tracking controllers with finite state machines governing transitions between tasks and within tasks [21]. However, this type of control architecture becomes increasingly complex as additional locomotion modes and actuated joints are considered [22], and typically requires a time-consuming tuning process.

The CYBATHLON is intended to address these challenges by pushing technology advances through competition. Founded as a nonprofit project at ETH Zurich more than ten years ago, CYBATHLON challenges teams from around the world to compete every four years [23].

The Vrije Universiteit Brussel (VUB) participated in the leg prosthesis discipline with an ankle-knee powered prosthesis, as in the previous CYBATHLON 2016 edition. The CYBATHLON 2024 race included ten different tasks that required skills such as ascending and descending stairs, obstacle navigation, balancing on a beam or uneven terrain, sit-to-stand, and incline/decline walking.

Here, we describe the development process of the control systems of the VUB CYBERLEGs X-Leg active prosthesis for CYBATHLON 2024, including the rationale for the specific control structure, the shortcomings of that control structure, and our perspectives on improving future active prostheses.

Methods

Prosthetic hardware

During the CYBATHLON 2024 and subsequent tests, one subject used the CYBERLEGs X-Leg prosthesis designed at the Vrije Universiteit Brussel. Compared to the CYBERLEGs Beta-Prosthesis [24] used during CYBATHLON 2016, the latest transfemoral prosthesis adopts a revised mechanical architecture. Although both systems feature two active sagittal-plane joints, the previous knee design relied primarily on series elastic actuation and a dedicated weight-acceptance mechanism to provide passive stiffness during stance. In contrast, the present prototype replaces this dual-path architecture with a ballscrew-driven knee joint with a 4-bar linkage to create an angle-dependent gear ratio. At the ankle, the variable stiffness MACCEPA joint was changed to incorporate a beamspring that can be engaged in parallel or in series with the joint, depending on the ankle angle and actuator position. These design changes improved torque consistency across the joints, reduced mechanical complexity, and lowered the overall system mass.

The current device version includes two active joints with one degree of freedom in the sagittal plane. A picture of the hardware highlighting the different components is presented in the first section of the supplementary materials (Supplementary Material, Fig. 1). The ankle module is composed of a footplate connected to the shank through an axle (single-axis joint) and two fiberglass beam springs that run between these components. The shank houses the actuation system, which is composed of a brushless motor (EC 4-Pole 200W, Maxon, Sachseln, Switzerland) and a ballscrew actuator (Misumi, Tokyo, Japan). Based on the ankle angle and motor position, measured respectively using a magnetic absolute encoder and a motor-mounted incremental encoder, the contact points between the springs and the other components shift accordingly.

The knee module can be modeled as a 4-bar linkage mechanism. Torque is again delivered through a ballscrew (Misumi, Tokyo, Japan) actuated by a motor (EC 4-Pole 200W, Maxon, Sachseln, Switzerland). A test bench has been used to estimate the nonlinear gear ratio of the knee over its full range of motion (between 0 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$110^{\circ }$$\end{document}). As for the ankle, the knee angle and velocity are measured by an absolute encoder at the joint axis and an incremental encoder at the motor.

The connection between the two joints is made through double female pyramid adapters to match the prosthetic knee axis joint with that of the sound leg. Overall, the full setup, including a footplate cover and shoe, weighs around 5.3 kg. Other components, such as the computing units (sbRIO-9651, National Instruments, Austin, TX), the batteries (8 x ICR18650, Samsung, Seoul, South Korea), and the communication modules (Wi-Fi and Bluetooth), are housed in the knee case. Prosthesis control

The controller of the CYBERLEGs X-Leg is a standard hierarchical structure, with high, mid, and low-level layers. Each layer serves a distinct role: the high level estimates the user’s intent and selects the appropriate task mode, the mid level specifies prosthesis parameters and joint trajectories for that mode, and the low level directly controls joint torques and the resulting physical interaction with the environment. Maintaining consistency across all layers is fundamental for meeting an output that aligns with user needs [20].

This type of structure allowed for the independent development of mid level controllers, which enabled the division of the programming tasks between small groups within the team. This approach removed the need to immediately develop a unified solution across all tasks, as required in more advanced control architectures [25]. Accordingly, we implemented separate finite state machines for each of the CYBATHLON tasks.

Able-bodied data collection

We conducted a trial with 13 able-bodied participants performing the CYBATHLON tasks to create a dataset that would allow us to determine transitions and events for the state machines we would build for the prosthesis. This was necessary because there were no existing data sets for the required motions of the tasks. The subjects wore a full-body IMU set (Xsens Awinda, Xsens Technologies B.V., Enschede, Netherlands), placed according to Xsens guidelines before calibration. The sensors were synchronized with two Vicon Vue cameras for labeling purposes. The recorded data included raw accelerations along the three axes from the IMU sensors, as well as post-processed joints and segments accelerations, velocities, and orientations. The latter were computed using the MT Software Suite.

High level control: task selection

The use of individual FSMs for each task poses a problem for automatic high level detection of the task being performed. To address this problem, a dedicated interface was implemented to communicate with the prosthesis over a Wi-Fi connection using Transmission Control Protocol. This allowed the pilot to directly select the active state machine without the need to create an automatic detection system.

The interface transmits control commands and receives information on the hardware state. Color-coded outputs were used to indicate when a command was sent and when the controller confirmed the switch to a new state machine. In addition to the task selection, the user can also enable or disable the motors, reset the knee encoder count (thereby redefining the current joint position as the reference angle), and perform the IMU initialization (by collecting orientations while the user maintains a static posture). Finally, if communication is lost, a notification is displayed so the user can re-establish the connection manually. Figure 2 presents a schematic overview of the interface layout. Mid level control: finite state machine

Each task was broken down into substates representing different user motions. While the transition between tasks was triggered by manual input, transitions between states within a task were triggered by one or more conditions. These conditions were threshold-based and mostly relied on the joint angles and angular velocities in the sagittal plane. Below, we present the control logic for the CYBATHLON tasks used in the leg race. Some tasks are presented in pairs when similarities occur. One task (High-Step) was excluded because it was not attempted during the race, and no data were collected.

Sit to stand: bench&table task

This task mimics a picnic table structure and requires the user to climb over a bench with two lateral obstacles before sitting. Accordingly, two distinct movement pathways have been implemented, depending on the user’s intended action. The first allows the user to climb over a bench with the sound side (as requested by the CYBATHLON rules) and then with the prosthetic knee. The second enables the athlete to perform the sitting and standing cycle. These two pathways are independent and can be executed autonomously. State transitions are defined using kinematic cues. From idle, lifting the sound leg triggers the swing in state, during which the prosthetic knee remains extended. When the sound leg returns to the ground, increasing the angular velocity of the prosthetic-side thigh initiates swing pr, allowing knee flexion. In this state, the knee position target is defined as a function of the prosthetic side thigh angle. Alternatively, from idle, leaning the torso forward triggers the transition into stand to sit. When both thigh angular velocities approach zero, the system transitions to sitting. An increase in angular velocity of both thighs subsequently triggers sit to stand, after which the system returns to idle.

Similar to the walking controller, a phase variable is computed in real time to represent movement progression. The healthy side thigh angle was selected due to its monotonic behavior during the stand to sit and sit to stand movements [27, 28]. This variable is expressed in a percentage, where 0% corresponds to the standing position and 100% to the sitting one. Both the Hurdles and Step Over tasks require the pilot to clear obstacles along the path (such as horizontal rods or boxes). During the Step Over task, the athlete was asked to pick objects placed on the ground. During the data collection on able-bodied subjects, two main strategies have been observed. One is performing a squat movement, and the other is only leaning the trunk forward. The user also adopted the trunk-leaning strategy, which was incorporated into the controller design.

Stairs and ladder

The two FSMs implemented for the Stairs and Ladder tasks employ similar state detection and control strategies for step climbing (Fig. 5). From the stance position, the user approaches the first step with the sound side. Stepping onto the first stair triggers the transition to the push-off state, followed by the swing, which allows the positioning of the prosthetic side on the next step. Once the prosthetic foot is positioned, a constant torque is applied at the knee joint. Finally, the return to stance happens when the prosthesis reaches full extension.

The difference between the two FSMs lies in the strategy adopted for descending. For Stairs, the initial state allows the user to maintain a standing position and bend the knee when force is directed forward. To do so, the user positions the prosthetic foot at the edge of the step, and the load on the prosthesis counteracts the knee extension torque generated by the device. On the other hand, the Ladder descending section is more complex, considering that turning around once on top was not feasible for our participant. For this reason, the pilot will initiate the movement by performing hip extension on the intact side. This will trigger a transition, and the knee prosthesis provides torque to regulate the downward motion.

The Stairs FSM allows the user to climb over multiple steps, whereas the Ladder FSM is limited to two steps. This is because the second FSM has been tailored for the specific CYBATHLON task. Wobbly, balance beam & cross country

Normative data collection revealed substantial intra- and inter-subject variability in movement patterns, with no clearly identifiable segments suitable for state detection. To address this challenge, the controller design was guided by user preferences and hardware limitations. Fixed reference positions were set for both joints. While the knee remains predominantly in an extended configuration during these tasks, the ankle, through its passive components, allows a wider range of motion, contributing to the user’s balance.

Low level control: trajectory tracking

Independent of the task, the output of each sub-state is tracked by one of the following low-level controls. For both joints, when the requirement is a set motor position, the error between the current and the desired configuration is fed to a closed-loop position controller with an inner velocity control loop. The gains of proportional (for the position) and proportional-integral (for the velocity loop) tuned to achieve fast and stable convergence to the target. This has been used for all states that maintain the prosthetic joint in a fixed position, such as knee extension during weight loading. On the other hand, when no load is applied (i.e., during swing), the target position is commanded through an impedance control. The stiffness and damping coefficients have been tuned according to the user preferences during training. This provides compliant and adaptive behavior to the prosthesis, for safer interaction with the user and the environment.

Finally, when the commanded target is a torque profile, this is tracked using a characterization derived from load-sensing test bench experiments over the joints’ range of motion. In the ankle case, given the presence of the beams, the torque output depends on the motor position and the ankle angle. A 3D lookup table has been generated from the characterization. The lookup table maps the current joint angle and the desired torque to a corresponding motor position reference.

Pilot and training

One subject participated in the study. The participant was a 58-year-old male with a K-Level 3 amputation who trained with the device for a period of three months, for a total estimated time of 46 h. The subject participated in 24 test sessions, with a frequency of 2 sessions per week. These sessions included tuning of the FSMs and control law parameters, as well as functional training to perform the tasks in accordance with the competition rules. A new socket interface was specifically designed to allow a wider range of motion, for example during stair climbing. Compared to the socket used in daily life, this interface provided a tighter fit and was positioned more distally with respect to the hip joint. During each session, the subject was asked to perform either all or a subset of the CYBATHLON tasks multiple times. During the initial weeks of testing, these trials were primarily used to tune the different parameters for each task. The tuning of the individual FSM was performed while the subject wore the device and attempted the activity (e.g., stair climbing). Researchers modified the control parameters based on the subject’s feedback, after which the task was repeated. This process was iterated 5–6 times per task, and the resulting parameters were saved for subsequent sessions. Tuning was continued as needed until a configuration was achieved that allowed the subject to perform the task safely and without discomfort. To mitigate fatigue, rest breaks were provided between tasks and, when necessary, between repeated trials of the same task. During the tuning phase, physically demanding tasks (e.g., Stairs and Ladder) were scheduled on different days to prevent excessive user fatigue.

Once tuning of the various FSMs was completed (approximately 22 h), the focus of the test sessions shifted towards the subject gaining sufficient expertise and confidence in the task execution with the prosthesis. During this second phase, variations emerged in the way the pilot executed the tasks across different sessions. These variations occasionally caused state transition errors, hindering task execution and requiring further fine-tuning of selected control parameters. By the end of the training, the user was able to complete the entire parkour under six minutes, corresponding to the duration of the CYBATHLON leg race.

After the competition, the subject was invited to participate in another test session. In these circumstances, no time restriction was imposed, and at least three runs per task were performed. Proper rest was offered between one task and the other. Data collected during this session included measurements from the prosthesis-embedded sensors and from the five-IMU set used for control.

Results

During the competition, the user attempted 8 of the 10 tasks and completed three of them within two rounds of 6 min. The following section reports the results of the test session conducted after the competition, with the participant wearing the prosthetic device. The obstacles were representative of those encountered in the CYBATHLON leg race. The structures used are presented in the second section of the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Material, Fig. 2). All data from the prosthesis were collected at 100 Hz and analyzed in MATLAB. Since the presented tasks include several states and control actions, the data have been segmented and resampled. The kinematic and kinetic outputs are presented as the mean and standard deviation (SD) across at least three trials per task. Discrete peak values were extracted from each gait cycle individually and averaged across cycles.

Discussion

The CYBATHLON 2024 competition was used to demonstrate the capabilities of an FSM control strategy and an in-house prosthesis prototype across multiple functional tasks resembling daily life activities. Rather than proposing a novel control algorithm, this work relied on a standard task-based approach to assess how such strategies perform when confronted with the practical constraints of competition preparation, pilot training, and real-world execution. Therefore, this section not only focuses on control performance but also aims to provide broader insights gained during preparation and the competition itself.

Preparation and training

Initially, the team aimed to identify common behaviors among the FSMs that would enable the combination of different tasks into more generalized state machines, allowing for automatic switching between exercises. However, the availability of only kinematic data from the IMU set and the multitude of tasks made it challenging to identify straightforward solutions. For this reason, the high-level control relied on manual locomotion mode selection. This approach ensured predictable task recognition and reduced unintended high level transitions, thereby simplifying the implementation of multiple FSMs in a single controller. On the other hand, this approach can lead to an increased cognitive load, loss of naturalness, or lower responsiveness during an activity. Moreover, this approach is vulnerable to human error, which is more likely to occur during physically demanding activities or when used by inexperienced users [30].

Overall, the tuning process required an estimated 22 h spent with the participant. In addition, approximately 24 h were dedicated to training the pilot to improve his performance in the tasks. This duration is aligned with previous studies on functional mobility training for multiple locomotion modes [22, 31]. At the end of the training period, the participant was able to complete the entire CYBATHLON parkour in under six minutes. However, it is reasonable to assume that more training would have helped the participant to further improve their proficiency in each task. In competitive settings, users with longer accommodation periods or greater familiarity with the obstacles would be expected to demonstrate improved performance and confidence.

Co-design and personalization

Our experience in the control implementation highlighted the importance of co-design and personalization. As mentioned in Section Able-bodied data collection, the FSM implementation began with the identification of common patterns in the task execution of able-bodied subjects. This approach was motivated by the absence of kinematic data for most of the exercises that took place during the CYBATHLON. However, the identified patterns did not always reflect the strategy that a person with a transfemoral amputation would use. From a broader perspective, the need for personalization in wearable robotics may arise from the intrinsic variability of human motor control. Humans can accomplish the same motor task through multiple coordination strategies and synergies depending on experience, morphology, or environmental constraints [32]. Ting et al. [33] highlighted how movement patterns may vary across individuals and cultures, reflecting learned motor behaviors rather than a single solution to a task. In this context, prosthetic controllers designed around a single reference strategy (i.e., based on able-bodied performance) may fail to capture the diversity of possible motor solutions. This is also the case for complex tasks, such as those presented in Section Wobbly, balance beam & cross country. Here, the differences in motor control were evident not only among the able-bodied subjects but also between trials performed by the same individual. Similarly, the high standard deviation observed in some task states (e.g., Fig. 10) could be attributed to the redundancy in the possible solutions that the participant can adopt to achieve the same action.

Personalization, therefore, is essential not only to account for prosthetic hardware and user-specific biomechanics but also to accommodate the range of motor coordination strategies individuals may use when interacting with assistive devices. During the tuning process, the redesign of some FSMs through iterative feedback from the participant led to substantial modification of the initially implemented logic and assistance. This additional step improved the task execution performance, but also highlighted limitations of the late co-design approach.

Due to the limited availability of the pilot, most of these changes prioritized rapid state machine tuning rather than capturing specific human motor behavior. For example, during level walking (Fig. 6), the prosthetic knee consistently achieved swing-phase flexion magnitudes around \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$60^\circ $$\end{document}, comparable to healthy adults overground or treadmill walking [29, 34, 35]. The swing phase, driven by a constant flexion torque, resulted in a knee flexion dependent on the state duration. Because the applied torque magnitude is fixed, in contrast to the typical biological trend [36, 37], knee flexion decreases as walking speed increases. Here, the simplicity of a fixed-magnitude torque was chosen for expediency over an adaptive speed control law, and proved sufficient as the pilot never reported issues with this particular controller.

These choices result in a relatively inflexible state machine, increasing the risk of overfitting the control to the individual’s preferences, capabilities, or the task-specific setup. In the Bench and Table FSM, the delivered assistance aligns with previous research findings [38]. However, both the participant’s and seat’s height (50 cm) influenced the parameters used to calculate the bench phase and the preferred assistance. This might change when tested under different conditions, for example, with a lower seat height (i.e, 40 cm)[38].

Sensitivity of threshold-based FSMs to user performance

The preparation and training process revealed several limitations when scaling the task-specific FSM approach to a multi-task scenario under time and fatigue constraints. Although thresholds were tailored to a single participant, they proved to be sensitive to variation in performance and were inconsistent across multiple days. In fact, the FSMs were sensitive to motion variability, such that additional fatigue (i.e., at the end of a training session) was sometimes enough to cause missed state transitions. Efficient use of the state machines required the pilot to understand where thresholds occurred and how to trigger them, even though thresholds were intended to be reached during average movement. This required the pilot to learn specific triggers for each action, which were difficult to memorize. The high demand associated with controlling a prosthesis can increase the mental workload of the user, which can then lead to a loss of performance and inefficient use of the device [39]. The mental workload has been linked in the past to dissatisfaction and lower rates of acceptance in prosthetic users [40].

For this reason, when possible, some FSMs were progressively simplified during the tuning process. One example is stair descent (Figs. 5, 9), where the final version only relied on a single state. By reducing the number of states and transitions, the control logic became easier for the pilot to remember and less sensitive to performance variations, resulting in more consistent and robust task execution.

Predictability and user trust

When simulating the competition setting, each task was performed under pressure and time constraints, increasing the physical and mental fatigue. During these test sessions, the user demonstrated higher confidence when performing tasks with limited control actions that required relatively low assistance or simple control logic, and exhibited more predictable behavior. In the Hurdles and Step-over tasks (Fig. 10), the relationship between the thigh roll and the knee flexion angle made the prosthetic behavior easier for the participant to predict, with the transition to stance state when the prosthetic knee was already straight. In contrast, transitions such as those from sitting to sit-to-stand resulted in an abrupt torque increase. These transitions were perceived and reported by the participant as providing discontinuous and unpredictable assistance. For this reason, some control actions, chosen during the tuning, prioritized repeatability over the maximum possible assistance. For example, during stair ascent (Fig. 8), a low and constant extension torque (17 Nm) was selected. This reflected the user’s preference to avoid discomfort at the residual limb interface caused by high torque application during the action. Although this level of assistance was insufficient to replicate biological joint support [41], the participant compensated by using handrails or strategies commonly observed in the population. These include a higher hip extension moment during the pull-up phase and greater hip flexion on the amputated side compared to the contralateral one during the swing phase [42].

These conservative solutions highlight how predictable hardware behavior can enhance user trust: knowing what the prosthesis will do can be more valuable than receiving stronger but less predictable assistance. This observation is consistent with previous studies on user-specific tuning during stair ascent, where a wide range of power at the knee joint is reported [22], and where less experienced users tend to select parameters that inject lower amounts of energy than more trained individuals or healthy references. This may be explained by the fact that, even if the delivered torque is lower from a biomechanical perspective, the consistency of the control output allows the pilot to adapt their movement strategy accordingly. In addition, the reduced assistance likely contributes to a stronger sense of control over the device, as the user still needs to actively contribute to the movement (e.g., by adjusting hip motion or relying on upper-body support), rather than being fully driven by the prosthesis. Similar trends were observed in other teams’ strategies, where control actions were lower than biological requirements for specific tasks (e.g., High Step). Still, the pilot, aware of the device’s limit, would compensate to succeed in the exercise. This was also the case for the winning team’s device, which can provide up to 25% of a biological knee torque [43, 44].

Future work

The challenges encountered during the competition allowed us to identify the current limitations of our powered prosthesis in daily tasks. CYBATHLON was designed to include a variety of tasks that, in theory, could be encountered in daily living, but which are neither common nor cyclic enough to have been extensively studied. This highlighted the limitations of hierarchical FSM methods in both high and mid level control layers. Using this system, high level controllers must be able to distinguish between many different tasks, and as more are added, the manual selection method becomes impractical. Moreover, creating heuristically optimized threshold classifiers becomes more difficult as a higher number of tasks means a larger number of parameters required to classify the action. A higher number of tasks might also mean that the sensor signals defining the initiation of a task become more similar, thereby increasing the complexity of detection. The implemented control structure increased the functionalities of the device, at the cost of ease of use. As discussed earlier, during several stages of training, the participant struggled to fully benefit from the prosthesis. The added complexity, then, limited the performance rather than augmenting it. Our work showed how the user felt more confident when using simpler controls, easy to understand and memorize. Future prostheses should move, in our opinion, towards automatic systems more intuitive for their users and personalized to their individual needs, rather than trying to generalize to the entire target population.

We are in the process of creating AI-based classifiers by conducting a new data collection with able-bodied participants and prosthesis users to address these challenges. This data collection, based on CYBATHLON tasks, includes additional sensing modalities intended to assess their contribution to classification performance. In particular, integrating a load cell sensor to measure the distribution of forces along the different axes of the prosthesis could be beneficial for the differentiation of kinematically similar movements. Increasing the number of sensor modalities is expected to support the development of a robust classifier, even for a large number of task classes.

In addition, the extensive time required for the tuning process of the mid-level controllers could be mitigated by including human-in-the-loop optimization methodologies. Of the estimated 46 h spent with the pilot, approximately 50% were invested in the tuning process. Moreover, this time would then increase by applying personalized torque profiles instead of constant outputs. This new framework offers the opportunity to automatically and continuously adapt wearable device assistance based on a defined cost function, such as user preference or muscle activity [45]. In this context, we are currently exploring Bayesian optimization approaches to tune assistance based on user preference, following approaches similar to those proposed by Tucker et al. [46] for exoskeleton control tuning.

Finally, based on feedback from the participant, greater continuity in the provided assistance would enhance the overall user experience. The current FSM controllers are based on piecewise-defined control strategies. The discontinuity between states could be mitigated by employing better methods of blending control actions across transitions or by shifting towards phase variable or oscillator-based controls. For instance, Bartlett et al. [47] implemented an assist-as-needed controller that minimizes the influence of the controller on the user’s natural dynamics, while ensuring the achievement of target kinematics by linearly increasing the stiffness gain as a function of the phase variable.

Conclusion

The CYBATHLON competition provided a concrete opportunity to evaluate the current capabilities and limitations of our prosthetic device by applying baseline control strategies to everyday tasks. Since the device is designed to support non-trained users in daily life activities, we selected a pilot representative of this target population. The hierarchical control relied on manual input for the locomotion mode selection and finite state machines tuned to the user preference.

The main tasks addressed can be summarized as walking, stairs ascent/descent, sit-to-stand, and obstacle navigation. While the participant felt confident with the assistance provided during walking or obstacle navigation, issues were reported during Sit-to-stand and Stairs. In these tasks, the torque delivered in some of the states led to discomfort at the residual limb and, eventually, resulted in compensatory strategies on the contralateral side.

Overall, the participation in the CYBATHLON provided hands-on experience in developing controllers tailored for the competition, but that can be applied to a more general context. Besides being an excellent learning opportunity for everyone involved in the project, it laid the groundwork for future studies. Future improvements will focus on high level control for task recognition, a shift towards more personalized assistance profiles, and automation of the tuning process.
