Machine learning-derived biomarker cutoffs for Alzheimer’s disease: Validation and application in preclinical and prodromal phases
Authors: Wei Wang, Shuangshuang Hou, Bo Wang, Di Sun, Hong Liu, Tan Zhao, Qi Wang, Shuo Xu, Tingting Li, Meina Quan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study aims to derive biomarker cutoffs in Alzheimer’s disease dementia (ADD) and validate their concordance in preclinical and prodromal stages. 341 sporadic and 103 familial participants were selected from the China Cognition and Aging Study (COAST) and the Chinese Familial Alzheimer’s Disease Network (CFAN) cohorts, separately. Machine learning was used to generate prediction models and biomarker cutoffs for identifying ADD. Agreement test was used in cognitive normal and mild cognitive impairment (CN + MCI) participants. For COAST, the optimal regression model was CSF Aβ42/40, ptau and left medial temporal atrophy (MTA-L), with area under the curve (AUC) of 0.841. The optimal decision tree model was CSF Aβ42/ptau, Aβ42/ttau, and MTA-L (AUC = 0.820). For CFAN, the optimal regression model was left precuneus relative volume and MTA-L (AUC = 0.935). The optimal decision tree model was left hippocampal and precuneus relative volume (AUC = 0.806). They showed significant concordance in CN + MCI participants with cutoff-based diagnosis in ADD. Machine learning-enhanced thresholds could improve participant stratification in early Alzheimer’s disease (AD) trials.Subject areas: health sciences, neuroscience, machine learning

Introduction

Increasing evidence has shown that biomarkers change at least 10–20 years before the symptom onset of Alzheimer’s disease (AD).1,2,3,4 This period is called preclinical AD. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) represents an intermediate stage between normal aging and dementia and has been attributed to numerous etiologies, among which MCI due to AD is the major etiologic subtype, also known as prodromal AD.5

Diagnosing preclinical and prodromal AD is becoming increasingly important,2 since an increasing number of prevention and intervention trials targeting this patient population have entered international clinical trials.6,7 The diagnosis of preclinical and prodromal AD heavily relies on identifying evidence of AD neuropathologic changes, namely, β-amyloidosis (A) and tauopathy (T). Since the National Institute on Aging and the Alzheimer’s Association issued a research framework for AD diagnosis in 2018,8 biomarkers of the “ATN” system in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and imaging have been widely reported. In the “ATN” system, A represents Aβ deposition, T represents aggregated tau, and N represents neurodegeneration or neuronal injury.2

While cutoff values of biomarkers in the “ATN” system were reported in AD dementia stage (ADD) in numerous studies, few studies have reported cutoff values for biomarkers required to diagnose preclinical and prodromal AD. In the clinical settings, while PET can be used to suggest AD continuum, we still need CSF and MRI to confirm AD diagnosis when PET is not available. Machine learning methods have been employed in recent studies for AD classification using biomarkers.9,10,11 Machine learning is further divided into supervised learning and unsupervised learning. Supervised learning methods are more widely used in the fields of medicine and biology due to their more interpretable results and the ability to obtain the cutoffs and biological meaning of selected features.12

This study aims to first generate cutoffs for CSF and MRI biomarkers in ADD, and then utilize them in cognitively normal (CN) and MCI subjects to identify preclinical and prodromal AD using supervised machine learning methods.

After matching for demographic variables, a total of 341 participants were included in the China Cognition and Aging Study (COAST) dataset. A total of 103 participants were included in the Chinese Familial Alzheimer’s Disease Network (CFAN) dataset. Figure 1 shows the diagram of inclusion and exclusion procedure. There was no significant group difference (CN + MCI vs. AD) for age, sex, or education in the COAST or CFAN datasets (p > 0.05). Regression-based prediction models and cutoffs

Figure 3 shows the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the CSF and MRI biomarkers and combinations. For single CSF biomarkers in the COAST dataset, the area under curves (AUCs) ranged from 0.739 to 0.765 (Figure 3A). For the CSF biomarker ratios, the AUCs ranged from 0.787 to 0.807 (Figure 3B). The AUCs for CSF biomarker combinations ranged from 0.816 to 0.832 (Figures 3C and 3D). For single N biomarkers in the COAST dataset, the AUCs ranged from 0.647 to 0.748, with ttau demonstrating the highest AUC (Figure 3E). The AUCs for the combinations of N biomarkers with CSF A and T biomarkers ranged from 0.829 to 0.841, with the combination of Aβ42/40, ptau, and left medical temporal atrophy (MTA-L) demonstrating the highest AUC (Figures 3F and 3G). For single MRI biomarkers in the CFAN dataset, the AUCs ranged from 0.840 to 0.884, with MTA-sum demonstrating the highest AUC (Figures 3H and 3I). For MRI biomarker combinations, the AUCs ranged from 0.920 to 0.935, and the combination of MTA-L and precuneus-relative-L demonstrated the highest AUC (Figure 3J).

Correlations of biomarkers with cognitive assessments

The correlations between biomarkers and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores in the COAST dataset are shown in Figure S3. None of the CSF biomarkers except Aβ42 were significantly positively correlated with the total MMSE score in the ADD group, and Aβ42 and Aβ42/40 were significantly positively correlated with the total MMSE score in the CN + MCI group. For the MRI biomarkers, all the MTA scores were significantly negatively correlated with the total MMSE score in both the ADD group and the CN + MCI group. Similar results were found for the correlations between biomarkers and MoCA scores in the COAST dataset (Figure S4). Furthermore, similar significant results were found for the correlations between MTA and cognitive assessments in the CN + MCI group, but not in the ADD group in the CFAN dataset (Figure S5).

Decision tree-based prediction models and cutoffs

The decision tree machine learning results are shown in Figure 4. When the minimum number of objects was set at eight, confidence factor was set at 0.3; the AUC reached the highest level for both COAST and CFAN datasets. The best prediction model for distinguishing ADD from CN + MCI in COAST dataset was the combination of Aβ42/ptau, MTA-L, and Aβ42/ttau, with an overall AUC of 0.820 (Figure 4A). Specifically, in the following circumstances, ADD can be decided: (1) Aβ42/ptau≤8.38 and MTA-L = 0; (2) Aβ42/ptau≤4.33 and MTA-L = 1; (3) Aβ42/ptau≤8.38 and MTA-L>1; and (4) Aβ42/ptau ≤8.38, MTA-L>1, and Aβ42/ttau≤2.82. The best prediction model for distinguishing ADD from CN + MCI for the CFAN dataset was the combination of precuneus-relative-L and hippo-relative-L, with an overall AUC of 0.806 (Figure 4B). When precuneus-relative-L≤0.598, ADD can be determined.

Concordance between different prediction models in identifying preclinical and prodromal AD

The concordance between best prediction models and cutoff-based diagnosis of the AD continuum in CN + MCI participants is shown in Table 3. All three methods (cutoff, logistic regression, and decision tree) exhibited significant concordance (p < 0.05). Specifically, in the COAST dataset, for optimal cutoffs, Aβ42/ptau was used to determine the AD continuum; for logistic regression, the combination of Aβ42/40, ptau, and MTA-L was used to determine the AD continuum; and for the decision tree, the combination of Aβ42/ptau, MTA-L, and Aβ42/ttau was used to determine the AD continuum. In the CFAN dataset, for optimal cutoffs, hippo-relative-L was used to determine the AD continuum; for logistic regression, the combination of MTA-L and precuneus-relative-L was used to determine the AD continuum; and for the decision tree, the combination of hippo-relative-L and precuneus-relative-L was used to determine the AD continuum. The concordance between the cutoff-based diagnosis and various machine learning models showed moderate to strong significance for COAST and CFAN.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to assess the concordance of “ATN” system biomarker cutoffs in pre-dementia Chinese subjects using both sporadic and familial AD cohorts. MTA biomarkers exhibited comparable cutoff values in sporadic and familial AD. The cutoffs for ADD could be utilized in identifying preclinical and prodromal AD.

Firstly, the significant group differences in CSF and MRI biomarkers in sporadic and familial AD were consistent with previous findings of biomarker changes in AD.13,14,15,16 Furthermore, the optimal CSF “A” biomarker was Aβ42/40, and the optimal ratio was Aβ42/ptau, which was also consistent with previous findings in AD.10,11,17 Aβ42/40 is a more practical and reliable marker than Aβ42 alone, as it accounts for variability in amyloid production and pre-analytical issues.

For sporadic AD patients, our results indicate that MTA biomarkers are better than CSF ttau when combined with CSF Aβ42 and ptau, suggesting that N biomarkers cannot be utilized alone when diagnosing sporadic AD, and must be combined with A and T biomarkers. For familial AD patients with known pathogenic genes, MRI N biomarkers are likely sufficient without obtaining CSF biomarkers. A previous study using the AD Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) dataset revealed that Hippo-absolute performed better than CSF ttau in identifying AD from CN.18 Another study measuring both MTA and hippocampal volume reported that MTA was better than hippocampal volume in distinguishing CN and amnestic MCI.19 Though the results in the COAST dataset were partly inconsistent, probably due to different stages of AD, results in the CFAN dataset were consistent with their findings.

The optimal combination was Aβ42/40, ptau, and MTA-L in sporadic AD cohort, suggesting that the left hemisphere plays a more important role than the right hemisphere. The results from the familial AD cohort also indicated that the optimal imaging biomarker was in the left hemisphere. These findings are consistent with a previous study in the US showing that the MTA-L and left hippocampal volume combination was the most predictive measure of diagnostic classification.19 In addition, the added value of left precuneus relative volume to MTA-L in diagnosing ADD, with an increase in AUC of 0.05, suggests the importance of left precuneus. Previous studies in familial AD have indicated that precuneus is the first region to show reduced cortical thickness1 and functional connectivity with other regions in the posterior default mode network in preclinical stage.20 Another study in sporadic AD has identified left precuneus as one of the earliest regions of Aβ accumulation.21 Further research is needed to verify the critical role of precuneus in early stages of AD continuum.

The cutoff values of CSF biomarkers have varied considerably across studies. In studies of the Chinese population, cutoffs for Aβ42, ptau, and ttau in distinguishing ADD range from 500 to 620.8 pg/mL,22,23,24,25 from 45.67 to 56.49 pg/mL,9,22,24 and from 225 to 286.8 pg/mL,22,24 respectively. The cutoffs for Aβ42/40, Aβ42/ptau, and Aβ42/ttau range from 0.0642 to 0.109,24, from 7.14 to 10.49,22,23 and <2.41,22 respectively. Our results of the Aβ42 and Aβ42/ptau cutoffs were in the aforementioned ranges, while the ptau and ttau cutoffs were higher, and the Aβ42/40 and Aβ42/ttau cutoffs were lower than the aforementioned ranges. Further stratification by distinguishing ADD from CN+MCI-O did not change the cutoff values much, except that Aβ42/40 lied in the above range. Such stability of results by further stratification indicates the reproducibility of ADD-derived biomarkers. The differences especially in higher ptau and ttau cutoffs may be attributable to sample size, patient and control population, and diagnostic kits used. For example, in Gao’s study,9 they included participant of dementia without confirmation by amyloid PET, and used Simoa kits for CSF measurements and unsupervised machine learning method to measure cutoffs; in Lei’s study,22 they included only participants not older than 65 years, without confirmation by amyloid PET, and their control group included only CN participants, not including MCI. In studies of Caucasian populations, cutoffs for Aβ42, ptau, and ttau in differentiating ADD range from 880 to 1,100 pg/mL, from 19.2 to 59.50 pg/mL, and from 213 to 461.26 pg/mL, respectively.10,11,26,27,28 The cutoffs for Aβ42/ptau and Aβ42/ttau have been established to range from 35.71 to 54.6 and from 3.03 to 4.74, respectively, based on the findings of previous studies.10,11,26,27 These results indicate significant racial disparity in CSF biomarker cutoffs. Racial disparity in biomarkers has also been reported between African American and non-Hispanic White populations.29,30,31 These findings indicate that for AD international trials with increasing population diversity, patient selection criteria, treatment strategies, and outcome measurements for biomarkers should consider the racial disparity in biomarker cutoffs.

With respect to the cutoff values of the MRI biomarkers, in studies of the Chinese population, the cutoff for MTA was reported to be 1.5.32,33 The same cutoff value was found in the Caucasian population.34 Our results for MTA-L and MTA-R were consistent with them. We further reported the MTA-sum cutoff for sporadic and familial AD, respectively. The cutoff for hippocampal volume has not been reported in the Chinese population. In the Caucasian population, the cutoffs for Hippo-absolute ranged from 5,700 to 6,723 mm3.18,35,36,37 In the ADNI dataset, a previous study identified a Hippo-relative cutoff of <0.369.34 One study in Chinese AD patients reported that the average hippo-relative-L and hippo-relative-R were 0.287 and 0.308, respectively, resulting in an approximate Hippo-relative of more than 0.58 despite the absence of a cutoff value.38 The variation may be attributable to MRI morphometric approaches, encompassing acquisition, neuroanatomic operationalization, software selection, end-user expertise, and decision rules for output evaluation.28 Another possible reason for the inconsistency of our results with previous studies may be due to different participant population; for example, MRI volumetric data in our study were from familial AD cohort rather than sporadic AD cohort and the age range was relatively younger than reported in the literature. Our results of the cutoffs for hippocampal volumes were higher than the above ranges in Caucasian population, and comparable to the latter study. These findings further substantiate the existence of racial disparities in hippocampal volume in relation to brain size.29

The correlation results indicate that CSF biomarkers, especially “A” biomarkers, might be more applicable in the pre-dementia stage, whereas MRI “N” biomarkers might be applicable in both dementia and pre-dementia stages. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that “A” biomarker abnormalities appear early in the disease continuum, whereas “N” biomarker abnormalities appear later.39 A previous study on Chinese subjects revealed a significant correlation between Aβ42/40 and MMSE scores in all subjects with a combination of non-dementia and dementia, but subgroup analyses were not performed.9 Another study of the ADNI dataset revealed no correlations between CSF AD biomarkers and MMSE scores in MCI patients.40 The inconsistency with the latter study is probably due to different criteria for the non-dementia group. Previous studies have also reported a significant association between MRI biomarkers and MMSE scores in all subjects or across the AD continuum.18,32,41 Our results are consistent with these findings and further indicate that the association is more significant in the AD subgroup.

The employment of machine learning enabled the implementation of the most suitable biomarker combination, in conjunction with cutoff values for identifying preclinical and prodromal AD in both datasets. The machine learning methods frequently employed in previous studies for AD classification and cutoffs included logistic regression and Gaussian mixture modeling (GMM).9,10,11 The utilization of GMM was excluded in this study due to its nature as an unsupervised method. We selected supervised method using either PET or pathogenic gene mutation as a reference. The decision tree method is most useful when there is a significant interaction between the features and the target, and it has been used in previous studies of AD.42,43 The performance accuracies of AD classification were 80.46%42 and 83.3%,43 respectively. Our accuracy results were similar to theirs. In addition, the cutoff values of the decision tree-selected biomarkers are very similar to the Youden index-based cutoffs, and the AUCs are also comparable though not as high as the logistic regression outputs of the best biomarker combinations, which is also consistent with a recent machine learning study.44 The significant concordance between these two machine learning methods in both the sporadic and familial AD at preclinical and prodromal stages provided further evidence for applying biomarker cutoffs in the early identification of AD continuum.

Limitations of the study

The present study has several limitations. Firstly, it is a cross-sectional study, with further validation needed in future longitudinal studies. Secondly, the CFAN dataset for this study has a relatively small sample size, and the COAST dataset for this study do not have 3D T1 images for analyzing regional brain volume, thus the cutoffs for the hippocampal volume biomarkers need further validation. Despite these limitations, MTA cutoffs were consistent, and the AUCs in CFAN were notably high, suggesting a potential reduction in sample size in MRI biomarker studies. Thirdly, while utilizing AV45 PET as a reference to supervise machine learning in calculating CSF and MRI biomarker cutoffs, visual inspection could be subjective and imprecise at times, necessitating further study for quantification. Fourthly, most participants that have handedness information were right-handed, thus further comparison of MRI biomarkers regarding handedness difference is not available. Future studies will focus on the following objectives: (1) include follow-up data and other types of dementia, so as to make the results applicable to more general dementia and pre-dementia populations; (2) include multicenter data domestically or internationally to strengthen the reproducibility of cutoff values; and (3) use different assay methods and imaging tools to measure CSF and imaging biomarkers, so as to evaluate whether similar results can be obtained.

Experimental model and study participant details

Participants’ characteristics

Subjects were selected from the China Cognition and Aging Study (COAST, ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT03653156) and the Chinese Familial Alzheimer’s Disease Network study (CFAN, ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT03657732). The participants from COAST in the current study were recruited from January 2021 to March 2024, including CN, MCI and sporadic ADD patients. The subjects from CFAN in the current study were recruited from January 2014 to December 2018, including CN, MCI and familial ADD patients. All the procedures contributing to this work comply with the ethical standards at Xuanwu Hospital on human experimentation and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2016. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants before undergoing any procedure.

The inclusion criterion was 50–80 years of age for COAST, and 40–80 years of age for CFAN. All participants have 7 or more years of education, and underwent cognitive assessments via the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). Participants from the COAST for this study must be able to have CSF samples for biomarker tests. The exclusion criteria were patients with any significant neurological disease, including other types of dementia, Huntington disease, seizure disorders, brain tumors, or multiple sclerosis; any significant systemic illness or unstable medical condition; any psychiatric diseases, namely schizophrenia, depression, or anxiety disorders.

Method details

Diagnosis of MCI and AD continuum

Clinical diagnoses of ADD were based on the 2011 NIA-AA criteria,45 and diagnoses of MCI were based on the 2004 International Working Group criteria.46 Further clinical criterion of AD continuum in this study for COAST was positive amyloid deposition on AV45 PET imaging by two trained radiologists, and that for CFAN was carrying known pathogenic gene mutations. Since all participants in this study had 7 or more years of education, further criteria for general cognitive assessments were as follows: 1) MMSE score ranging from 22 to 27 and MoCA score ranging from 18 to 24 for MCI; 2) MMSE score ranging from 28 to 30, or MMSE score ranging from 24 to 27 and MoCA score ranging from 25 to 30 for the CN.47,48 Age, sex and education were matched between the CN + MCI and ADD group in each dataset. The CN + MCI group in COAST was further divided into CN+MCI-A and CN+MCI-O groups, based on visual inspection of AV45 PET positivity and negativity, respectively. Subjects of AD continuum (AV45 PET positivity) in the CN + MCI were grouped as CN+MCI-A. Subjects other than the AD continuum in the CN + MCI were grouped as CN+MCI-O.

CSF biomarker measurements

For COAST study participants, lumber puncture was performed within one week after cognitive assessment and MRI, following the internationally recognized procedure and protocol.49 Blood samples were obtained under morning fasting conditions for APOE genotyping and routine biochemical tests. The samples were aliquoted and preserved at −80°C until analysis. The concentrations of the biomarkers were measured via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (INNOTEST, Fujirebio) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The samples were anonymized to protect participant confidentiality. CSF biomarkers include Aβ42, Aβ40, total tau (ttau), and phosphorylated tau 181 (ptau). Aβ42/40, Aβ42/ttau, and Aβ42/ptau were further calculated.

MRI biomarker measurements

For the COAST dataset, clinical structural MR images were acquired with 3.0T scanners (Siemens) with a 20-channel phased-array head–neck coil, including T1, T2, DWI, FLAIR sequences, and a coronal scan of T1. For the CFAN dataset, whole-brain T1-weighted three-dimensional (3D) magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) scans were acquired.

The visual rating of medical temporal atrophy (MTA) was assessed by one rater on T1-weighted coronal slices, who was blinded to the subjects’ diagnosis, based on a visual estimation of the medial temporal lobe and surrounding CSF spaces, particularly the temporal horn of the lateral ventricle and the choroid fissure, on the left and right sides separately and together.50 Scores ranged from 0 to 4 (severe atrophy). Finally, three scores were recorded, including the left, right and bilateral MTA (MTA-L, MTA-R and MTA-sum). For the CFAN dataset, T1 images were preprocessed via the fslmaths command and the FreeSurfer software (version 5.3.0) for brain extraction and segmentation. Details of preprocessing can be found in our previously published studies.51,52

The absolute volume of bilateral hippocampus and precuneus was extracted. The relative volume was calculated to correct for differences in brain size. Finally, 9 measures were used, including the absolute volume of bilateral hippocampus (Hippo-absolute-L, Hippo-absolute-R, Hippo-absolute), the relative volume of bilateral hippocampus (Hippo-relative-L, Hippo-relative-R, Hippo-relative) and precuneus (precuneus-relative-L, precuneus-relative-R, precuneus-relative).

Quantification and statistical analysis

For demographic data, continuous variables in each dataset were compared between groups via the independent sample t test. Categorical variables were compared via the chi-square test. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. For CSF and imaging biomarker data, normality was tested via the Shapiro‒Wilk test. UNIANOVA was used for group comparisons, controlling for age, sex and education. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Bonferroni correction, and the significance level was set at 0.05 divided by number of biomarkers. SPSS software (version 22.0, IBM) was used for statistical analysis, and GraphPad Prism (version 9.5) was used to generate figures.

The biomarkers that demonstrated significant between-group differences (ADD vs. CN + MCI) were utilised to generate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, with the implementation of logistic regression to generate predicted probability (Proportional Reduction in Error, PRE) for various biomarker combinations. The area under the curve (AUC) of the ROC curves was analyzed to determine the diagnostic performance in distinguishing ADD from CN + MCI. These ROC curves were then employed to generate cut-off values for each biomarker, determined with the highest Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity – 1). The cut-off values for biomarker combinations were determined using PRE with the highest Youden’s index. The model (either single biomarker or biomarker combinations) that yielded the highest accuracy was identified as the optimal prediction model. For further stratification, Youden’s index-based cut-offs for each biomarker or optimal biomarker combinations in distinguishing ADD from CN+MCI-O were also extracted. Since the groups were matched for age, it was not adjusted for when generating cut-offs. Partial correlation analyses were performed between biomarkers and MMSE/MoCA scores in each group, controlling for age, sex and education. Significance level was set at Bonferroni corrected p < 0.05/2 (number of groups).

Decision tree machine learning was performed in Weka software (version 3.8.3, New Zealand) to search for the optimal prediction model. This machine learning method was additionally selected because its models are more interpretable than other machine learning methods.44 The dataset was trained and tested using 5-fold cross-validation, and parameters of “minimum number of objects” and “confidence factor” were fine-tuned. The performance of each model was evaluated by multiple metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive and negative percent agreement. The model that yielded the highest accuracy was identified as the optimal prediction model.

The classification of the AD continuum by clinical diagnosis, optimal cut-off, logistic regression and decision tree model were evaluated for concordance using Cohen’s kappa coefficient and percentage agreement from the agreement test in the CN + MCI subjects.
