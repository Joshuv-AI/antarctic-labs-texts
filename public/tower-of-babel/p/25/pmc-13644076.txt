When to conceive following metabolic-bariatric surgery to optimize both surgical weight loss and gestational weight gain
Authors: Raheleh Moradi, Maryam Kashanian, Ali Sheidaei, Mohammad Kermansaravi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost patients undergoing metabolic-bariatric surgery are women and half of them are at reproductive age. Although it is recommended delaying pregnancy after surgery, the optimal time to conceive yet remains controversial. This study aimed to define the best conception time following surgery to optimize both surgical weight loss and gestational weight gain.MethodsThis cohort study included all women who gave birth after surgery recorded in the Iranian National Obesity Surgery Database during 2009–2023. First, weight loss pattern from surgery to pregnancy was addressed by curve fitting and univariate segmented relationship and then evaluated by regression model added with maternal and perinatal outcomes.ResultsOverall, 430 women with a mean age of 33.81 ± 4.70 years, entered the analysis. We revealed two change points of 4 and 13 months in weight loss trend from surgery to pregnancy. Accordingly, pregnancy within 4–13 and over 13 months was significantly associated with a higher mean of percent total weight loss (β = 13.35, 95%CI: 9.24, 17.46 and β = 14.32, 95%CI: 10.24, 18.40, respectively) and a lower risk of inadequate gestational weight gain (OR = 0.26, 95%CI: 0.09, 0.75 and OR = 0.12, CI: 0.04, 0.34, respectively) than pregnancy before 4 months following surgery.ConclusionsPregnancy at least 13 months after metabolic-bariatric surgery is associated with greater surgical weight loss and a lower risk of inadequate gestational weight gain. Therefore, we recommend a broad guide of 12–18 months as the earliest time to plan for pregnancy after surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09627-8.Keywords: Pregnancy, Bariatric surgery, Weight loss, Gestational weight gain, Time-to-pregnancy

Background

Obesity as a health-related condition affects physical, mental, social, and spiritual well-being. By 2035, a quarter of the world’s population and nearly a third of all women are estimated with obesity [1]. Metabolic-bariatric surgery (MBS) is the most effective method to induce significant weight loss, and three-quarters of patients undergoing MBS are women, half of whom are at reproductive age [2].

Pregnant women with obesity are predisposed to pregnancy-related complications including cesarean delivery, preterm birth, and macrosomia in fetuses who themselves become adults with obesity in the future [3]. Pregnancy following MBS is associated with a reduction in obesity-related complications, including diabetes and hypertension during pregnancy [4]. While pregnancy after MBS can be associated with an increased risk of fetal growth restriction [5], there may be a protective effect on fetal macrosomia, and pediatric or adulthood obesity [6]. Pregnancy should be avoided following MBS until the weight stabilization and the correction of possible nutritional deficiencies [7].

Although clinical practice guidelines recommend delaying pregnancy after MBS, the optimal time to conceive yet remains controversial [8]. Also, total weight loss (TWL) in early pregnancy as the closest outcome of MBS is affected by surgery-to-conception interval and may be associated with gestational weight gain (GWG). Therefore, this study first aimed to define the best conception time following MBS to optimize both TWL and GWG. Secondly, this timeframe was validated by other surgical and pregnancy outcomes to achieve both optimal surgical response and safe pregnancy.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

In a retrospective cohort study, all women who conceived after MBS between 1 January 2009 and 31 December 2023, were included in the study. In addition, surgery-to-conception interval was calculated in months from surgery date to conception based on the last normal menstrual period estimated by the first trimester ultrasound. The data was extracted from the Iranian National Obesity Surgery Database (INOSD) in the Minimally Invasive Surgery Research Center, Iran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran [9]. Conceptions by assisted reproductive technology (ART) in which egg or embryo was manipulated [10]; twin/multiple pregnancies; and abnormal fetal development consisting of spontaneous abortion, voluntary termination of pregnancy, or intrauterine fetal demise; were exclusion criteria to address sampling bias. Also, the pregnancies lost to follow-up or without birth data were excluded.

Data sources/measurement

Current pregnancy characteristics included maternal age (year), maternal education (≤ diploma or university), maternal job (housekeeper or employee), history of abnormal fetal development (abortion/stillbirth), preconception care, nulliparous (a woman who has never completed a pregnancy beyond 20 weeks of gestation), unintended pregnancy (whether mistimed or unwanted conception), body mass index (BMI) at conception (kg/m2), and multidisciplinary/consultant-led care (prenatal care led with a multidisciplinary approach where possible).

Medical and surgical history consists of maternal surgery to one of the methods of sleeve gastrectomy (SG), one-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB), or Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), BMI at surgery date (kg/m2), MBS morbidity and mortality, percent TWL [(surgical weight – conception weight / surgical weight)×100], percent excess weight loss [(EWL: surgical weight – conception weight / surgical weight – ideal weight)×100], BMI reduction (BMI at surgery date – BMI at conception); as well as diagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypothyroidism, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), and infertility.

Maternal, fetal, and neonatal outcomes were also recorded as GWG characterized according to the institute of medicine (IOM) recommendations [adequate (underweight BMI: 12.5–18 kg, healthy BMI: 11.5–16 kg, overweight BMI: 7-11.5, and obese BMI: 5–9 kg), inadequate (underweight BMI < 12.5 kg, healthy BMI < 11.5 kg, overweight BMI < 7, and obese BMI < 5 kg), or excessive (underweight BMI > 18 kg, healthy BMI > 16 kg, overweight BMI > 11.5, and obese BMI > 9 kg)] [11], and booking hypertension (HTN) during pregnancy, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), anemia, amniotic fluid (AF) disorders including polyhydramnios or oligohydramnios, cesarean section (CS), puerperal complications (hemorrhage, infection, or intensive care required), gestational age (week), preterm birth (before 37 completed weeks), birth weight (g), admission to neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), as well as small for gestational age (SGA) as birth weight less than 10th percentile for gestational age [12].

Study size

In setting the statistics and data software (STATA: College Station, Texas, USA), considering one-sample binomial test, α = 0.05, β = 0.2, and SGA proportion of 0.19 in postoperative pregnancies compared to a reference value of 0.17 [13, 14], as well as 20% drop out to manage missing data, the sample size was calculated as 430.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented in mean with standard deviation (SD) and median with interquartile range (IQR) based on normal and kurtosis/skewed distribution, respectively. Group differences were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and One-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables, as appropriate. Two-tailed P-values less than 0.05 were considered significant and all analyses were performed by the statistical software packages STATA version 17.0. and R version 4.2.2 (https://www.R-project.org).

To identify change points in the maternal weight loss trend from surgery to pregnancy, we first performed scatter plot and curve fitting. Subsequently, we applied univariate regression with multiple change points (MCP) between linear segments, based on the Bayesian inference method. This approach using the random initial points, divides the total trend into segments, aiming to minimize the ratio of inter-segment changes to intra-segment changes. The optimal number of segments was determined by fitting a linear model containing one segment compared with models including 2, 3, and 4 segments. Finally, the models were compared by the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) which resulted in the selection of the most appropriate number of thresholds.

Considering whether statistical assumptions/concerns were met or passed respectively, multivariable linear or logistic regression was run to adjust the effect [95% confidence interval (CI) for beta (β)/odds ratio (OR)] of potential confounders, including age, education, BMI, T2DM, HTN, surgery type, %TWL, nulliparous, and preconception care. The covariates were selected based on the current literature and univariate statistical analyses to assess the impact of surgery-to-conception interval on obstetric and bariatric outcomes.

Finally, as a same woman could be contributed in this study for more than one pregnancy, the analyses were conducted separately among the first postoperative pregnancies to account for potential dependence.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Totally, 430 women with singleton births following MBS, entered the study (Fig. 1). The median interval between MBS and conception was 21 months and half of the women conceived between 11 and 42 months after surgery. The minimal and maximal interval between surgery and conception were 14 days and 110 months. Current pregnancy characteristics revealed that the majority of mothers with a mean age of 33.81 ± 4.70 years, were housekeeper (70.23%) and graduated (53.72%). At least one history of abnormal fetal development was reported by 38.37% of women. Although the preconception care rate was 41.86%, multidisciplinary/consultant-led prenatal care decreased to 36.98%. Multiparous (63.02%) and intended pregnancy (57.68%) were recorded with a higher frequency than nulliparous (36.98%) and unplanned pregnancy (42.32%). Additionally, hypothyroidism (23.02%), PCOS (14.88%), T2DM (6.98%), and infertility (2.32%) were the most reportable comorbidities (Table 1).

Impact of MBS-to-conception interval on the obstetric and bariatric outcomes

In a multivariable regression model, maternal weight loss and inadequate GWG were related to surgery-to-conception interval. That is, the mean value of %TWL [β = 13.35, 95%CI (9.24, 17.46); and β = 14.32, 95%CI (10.24,18.40); respectively], %EWL [β = 29.92, 95%CI (20.46, 39.37); and β = 32.93, 95%CI (23.54, 42.32); respectively], and BMI reduction [β = 5.53, 95%CI (3.70, 7.37); and β = 6.28, 95%CI (4.45, 8.10); respectively] within 4–13 and over 13 months were higher than up to 4 months from surgery to pregnancy. Also, the risk of inadequate GWG was respectively 74% and 88% lower within 4–13 [OR = 0.26, 95%CI (0.09, 0.75)] and over 13 [OR = 0.12, 95%CI (0.04, 0.34)] compared to less than 4 months between surgery and pregnancy (Table 4).

Weight loss outcomes were associated with preconception care and T2DM in addition to MBS-to-conception interval, so that non-diabetic women who received preconception care, more lost total [β=-5.06, 95%CI (-8.56, -1.56) and β = 2.91, 95%CI (0.89, 4.93), respectively], and excessive [β=-9.25, 95%CI (-17.30, -1.20) and β = 7.21, 95%CI (2.57, 11.85), respectively] weight, as well as BMI ([β=-1.99, 95%CI (-3.56, -0.43) and β = 1.62, 95%CI (0.72, 2.52), respectively], Table S1).

There were associations between GDM [OR = 0.92, 95%CI (0.85, 0.99)], AF disorders [OR = 0.92, 95%CI (0.86, 0.99)], and SGA [OR = 1.06, 95%CI (1.02, 1.10)] with %TWL was disclosed. That is, mothers with more weight loss had a lower risk for gestational diabetes and polyhydramnios or oligohydramnios, as well as a higher risk for fetal growth restriction (Table S2).

Impact of surgical procedure on the obstetric and bariatric outcomes

Although there was no association between surgical procedure and MBS-to-conception interval; anemia [OR = 2.56, 95%CI (1.50, 4.37)], %TWL [β = 2.50, 95%CI (0.27, 4.73)], and BMI reduction [β = 1.05, 95%CI (0.05, 2.04)]; were measured higher in gastric bypass than in sleeve (Table S3).

Sensitivity analysis

In a total of 373 women who conceived for the first time following MBS, as in all participants, %TWL (β = 13.13, 95%CI:9.02, 17.23 and β = 14.52, 95%CI:10.43, 18.60, respectively), %EWL (β = 29.38, 95%CI:19.99, 38.77 and β = 33.46, 95%CI:24.11, 42.80, respectively), BMI reduction (β = 6.01, 95%CI:4.14, 7.87 and β = 6.94, 95%CI:5.08, 8.80, respectively), and inadequate GWG (OR = 0.26, 95%CI:0.08,0.77 and OR = 0.12, CI:0.04,0.34, respectively) were associated with surgery-to-conception interval within 4–13 and over 13 compared to less than 4 months, although other obstetric outcomes were not associated. Also, %TWL [β = 2.57, 95%CI (0.35, 4.79)] and anemia [OR = 2.73, 95%CI (1.53, 4.86)], were measured higher in gastric bypass than in sleeve (Table S4).

Discussion

In this study, 4 and 13 months were recognized as change points in the trend of maternal %TWL from MBS to pregnancy. In addition, pregnancy within 4–13 and over 13 months following MBS was significantly associated with a higher mean %TWL and lower inadequate GWG risk than up to 4 months.

In comparison with the current literature, Carreira et al. assessed 42 pregnancies to identify the ideal conception time after MBS for optimizing the maternal and fetal weight gain [15]. Although there was no association between BMI reduction and surgery-to-conception interval, adequate GWG and birth weight were directly associated with pregnancy over 24 months after MBS. In Rottenstreich’s study, higher GWG and lower SGA were revealed in 173 pregnancies over 6 months compared to 23 cases in the first 6 months after SG [16]. From a total of 6388 post-bariatric pregnancies, Xu et al. showed that a shorter surgery-to-conception interval or lower surgery-to-conception weight loss was associated with lower GWG [17]. In the recent research on 200 pregnancies after MBS consists of (1) up to12 months, (2) within 12–24 months, and (3) over 24 months, while inadequate GWG in groups 1 and 3 was more reported, adequate GWG in the group 1 and excessive GWG in the group 3 were respectively lower and higher than in the both other groups [18].

There is a potential relationship among MBS-to-conception interval, maternal weight loss, GWG, and birth weight that has been spectrally revealed in different study protocols. The present study is a comprehensive investigation of surgery-to-conception interval and weight loss, as well as maternal and perinatal outcomes compared to the previous scoping studies which assessed pregnancy after MBS, weight loss, or pregnancy outcomes.

As we discerned preconception care was associated with maternal weight loss at the onset of pregnancy following MBS, postoperative follow-up had a relationship with weight loss in general population studies. Spaniolas et al. found that completed follow-up until 12 months after MBS was partially associated with EWL ≥ 50% and TWL ≥ 30% in 51,081 patients [19]. Also, Lima et al. measured a rate of 96.58% for EWL > 50% among the 117 adherent patients who returned to the postoperative clinic [20].

Women of reproductive age are included a significant proportion of patients undergone MBS, therefore planning for pregnancy should be discussed early and as a part of perioperative care to prevent, control, and manage the obstetric consequences, as well as to optimize surgery outcomes. Citing Schlottmann’s comment [21], perioperative preconception care may be the Achilles’ heel of good clinical/surgical practice in “obstetric and bariatric” patients.

Pregnant women who were diagnosed with T2DM achieved a lower weight loss than peers without T2DM. A multivariable analysis of weight loss up to 12 months after RYGB, predicts that T2DM was negatively associated with EWL validated by an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.830 [22]. Also, in a longitudinal assessment of 35 patients who underwent MBS, there were single correlations between preoperative glycemic indices (fasting glucose and glycosylated hemoglobin) and postoperative BMI, meaning that well-controlled diabetes can be assumed effective in weight loss after surgery [23].

Preoperative euglycemic status as a secondary objective leads to the primary goal of optimal weight loss postoperation, so screening of diabetes in perioperative preconception care using fasting sugar and hemoglobin A1c is recommended. In addition, after gastric bypass, gestational diabetes is diagnosed by capillary blood glucose monitoring instead of a glucose tolerance test, due to a precipitation of dumping syndrome [24].

Gastric bypass with a greater weight loss had a predisposing role for maternal anemia by a poor-controlled hypoabsorption. Because the prior studies investigated single or composite surgical procedures [25, 26], our results address future studies to consider optimal surgery type in women of childbearing age.

Maternal weight loss was associated with lower GDM, AF disorders, and fetal growth. Bourke et al. followed up 54 pregnant women with a history of MBS that accordingly birth weight, independent from surgery-to-conception interval, was related to maternal TWL in early pregnancy [27]. In a case-control study on 158 women matched for age and BMI; GDM and birth weight in MBS group and SGA in control group were significantly less frequent [28]. A prospective study displayed both longer gestational age and heavier birth weight in 108 nonoperative compared with 54 postoperative pregnancies [29]. Moradi and her colleagues with assessment of 56 births, found that not only MBS but also weight loss in mother was associated with lower neonatal weight [14].

Literature review indicates that there was always a relationship among MBS, maternal, and perinatal complications using univariable analysis, while only surgical weight loss remained significantly related to pregnancy outcomes in multivariable models. In conclusion, maternal weight loss is a more reliable predictor than MBS in obstetrics.

Strengths, limitations, and future studies

In this study, the optimal interval from surgery to pregnancy was validated by a variety of weight loss and pregnancy outcomes, but there may be a potential bias related to the naturally low prevalence of maternal and perinatal consequences. In addition, wide confidence intervals observed for such uncommon obstetric outcomes, suggest limited statistical power for these endpoints. So, historical, geographical, methodological, or spectral generalizability with external data is addressed in future studies. Although, our results were adjusted by basic, bariatric, and obstetric characteristics, there are potential residual confounding factors regarding the nutritional status and micronutrient deficiencies, which should be considered in subsequent research. Exclusion of conceptions by ART, twin/multiple pregnancies, and abnormal fetal development, can affect generalizability of results to all pregnancies. Therefore, this gap addresses future studies in high-risk groups. As, no correction was used for multivariable comparisons to maintain the power of study, the potential implications of applying the recommendations should be considered.

Weight gain monitoring in pregnancy is based on preconception BMI [11], while MBS-to-conception interval, active weight loss, weight stability, and fetal growth in addition to maternal BMI, should be considered in pregnant women with a history of MBS to specify GWG. Therefore, further studies are recommended to specialize in GWG after MBS.

Conclusions

Pregnancy at least 13 months following MBS was associated with the greater surgical weight loss and a lower risk of inadequate gestational weight gain. Although a minimum interval of at least 13 months is recommended, individualized care based on nutritional status and weight stability remains essential.
