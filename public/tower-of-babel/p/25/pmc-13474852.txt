Magnitude of multidrug resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis and associated risk factors among pulmonary tuberculosis patients in Tigray region, Ethiopia
Authors: Guesh Fesaha Weldemariam, Medhanie Asmelash Mulaw, Tesfaye Sisay Tessema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Tuberculosis [TB] caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) remains a significant public health problem worldwide. Globally, tuberculosis kills 1.8 million individuals, with 95% fatalities occur in developing nations [1]. It is also the most public health issue in Ethiopia. Among previously treated TB cases, the prevalence of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) is 18% and 3.4% of new cases with an incidence of 484 per 1,000 individuals [2]. Around 500,000 cases of Multidrug resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MDR-TB) and approximately 100,000 deaths occur each year [3].

The burden of Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR‐TB) is high in developing countries [4]. Human immuno-deficiency virus (HIV) co- infection, low socioeconomic status, and limited diagnostic and treatment facilities are among the several factors highly exacerbate the effect of MDR-TB in the developing countries, including Ethiopia [5]. Based on a WHO report, Ethiopia ranked third in Africa and is one of the 30 highest TB, TB/HIV, and multidrug-resistant (MDR) high-burden TB countries in the world [6].

Some studies conducted in different parts of Ethiopia showed variability in the prevalence of MDR-TB. A study conducted in Northern Ethiopia revealed that, MDR-TB was 2.18% in newly diagnosed and 21.07% of previously treated cases [7] and from July 2018 to August 2019, another study conducted in Tigray region, Ethiopia showed that 11.6% of newly diagnosed and 32.7% of previously treated cases had MDR-TB [8]. Similarly, MDR-TB prevalence was detected at the rate of 15.3% in Amhara region [9] and 11.5% in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia [10].

In Tigray region, there was no data on the magnitude and associated factors with the occurrence of MDR-TB in the literature since the war began in November, 2020. Therefore, the current study aimed to examine Magnitude of MDR-TB and associated risk factors among pulmonary tuberculosis patients in Tigray region, Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study site and design

The study was conducted in Tigray Region. Mekelle, the capital of Tigray, is located 783 km from Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia. The area covers 54,573 km2 and is situated in Ethiopia’s Northern highlands at latitude 14.2 and longitude 38.8 degrees, 12o15 to 14o57 N, and 36o27 to 39o59 E. Tigray Health Research Institute (THRI) is the only facility offering drug susceptibility test and TB culture in the region.

A cross-sectional study was carried out in five hospitals of the Tigray region: Mokeni Hospital, Mekelle Hospital, Adigrat Hospital, Aksum Hospital and Sihul Shire Hospital (Fig. 1). More than 80% of health facilities have been damaged and only 3.6% of the remaining healthcare facilities were operating at full capacity, since the war began in November 2020 [11]. The study sites were among the few functioning health settings in Tigray.

Map of the study area in the post-war Tigray Region, Ethiopia, February 2024- January 2025

Population, inclusion and exclusion criteria

All PTB patients who visited the chosen hospitals in the area during the study period were the study populations. PTB patients who were not receiving treatment at the time of the study, ˃ 15 years age, tested positive for GeneXpert, and were able to give written informed consent were all included in the study. Extra-pulmonary TB cases and patients younger than 15 years of age were not included in the study.

The following operational definitions are used in this study.

New case

A patient who has never had treatment for TB, or has been on treatment for less than four weeks.

Relapse

A patient who has previously been treated for TB, was declared cured or whose treatment was completed at the end of their most recent course of treatment and is now diagnosed with a recurrent episode of TB.

Previous treatment

A patient who took TB treatment for one month or more in a previous time.

Failure after treatment patient

A patient who has previously been treated for TB, and whose treatment failed at the end of their most recent course of treatment.

Defaulter (treatment after loss to follow-up)

A patient whose anti-TB treatment was interrupted for two consecutive months or more.

Relapse

A patient who has previously been treated for TB, was declared cured or whose treatment was completed at the end of their most recent course of treatment and is now diagnosed with a recurrent episode of TB.

Primary resistance (resistance among new TB cases)

Resistance in patients who did not have a history of anti-TB treatment.

Secondary resistance (resistance among previously treated cases)

Resistance in patients previously treated with anti-TB drugs.

Treatment success rate refers

To the percentage of notified TB patients who were successfully.

Data collection

Patients were asked for their permission to participate after being fully informed about the purpose of the study. A questionnaire prepared in Tigrigna (the local language) was used to collect clinical data, socio-demographic, co-infection and behavioral characteristics from participants who gave their consent (Supplementary File).

Sputum collection for culturing

(5–10 ml) of sputum sample from every participant who tested positive for the GeneX-pertMTB/RIF assay was collected by laboratory staff using a sterile, coded 50 ml falcon tube, per the Federal Ministry of Health’s recommendation [12]. All sputum samples were transported to the Tigray Health Research Institute (THRI) in an ice bag after being carefully packed and stored at 4˚C.

Sputum specimen digestion and decontamination

NALC/NAOH (N‑acetyl‑L‑cysteine and sodium hydroxide) (1%) was used to prepare the specimens. Phosphate buffer (PH 6.8) were added to neutralize NaOH, and dilutes the homogenate to lessen the viscosity and specific gravity. The homogenate was centrifuged for (15 min by centrifugation at 3000 RPM). Sputum was directly examined under a microscope for acid-fast bacteria (AFB) using the standard Ziehl-Neelsen staining at Tigray health research institute (THRI).

Sputum culture

At the Tigray health research institute (THRI), the decontaminated supernatant decanted sputum sample was cultured on a Lowenstein-Jensen (LJ) egg medium and on a liquid culture Mycobacterium Growth Indicator Tube; BACTEC MGIT 960 culture (Becton Dickinson Microbiology systems, Sparks, MD, USA), following the standard operational procedures. The tubes for the solid culture were in a slant position and incubated at 37˚C to ensure an even distribution of inoculums for one week and thereafter at 37˚C in air for another seven weeks, and then checked once a week for mycobacterial growth. Cultures were considered negative when no colonies were seen after six weeks (42 days) for a liquid culture and eight weeks of incubation for a solid culture.

Drug susceptibility test for first-line and second-line anti-TB drugs

Line Probe Assay (LPA) was used to carry out drug-susceptibility test for first-line anti-TB drugs (isoniazid and rifampicin) using GenoType® MTBDRplus, and second line anti-TB drugs (ofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, amikacin, capromycin, kanamycin and viomycin) using GenoType MTBSL, following the manufacturer’s instruction (GenoType® MTBC; Hain Life Science, Nehren, Germany).

GenoLyse kit was used to extract DNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Hain Lifescience, Nehren, Germany) in a DNA contaminating free working area. After centrifugation, the DNA containing supernatant was transferred to a new cryo-tube. In another room free from contaminating DNA, amplification Mixes A (10 µl) and B (35 µl) were prepared freshly and a total of 45 µl Master Mix was transferred to each polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tubes. 5 µl extracted DNA was added to the master mix. The amplicon was detected with a series of procedures after amplification, by adding different reagents to the strip. The strips formed color bands after the addition of the final substrate reagent [13].

Quality assurance and quality control

Laboratory analyses were carried out following standard operating procedures. Both the solid culture and LPA laboratory were validated. Reference strains of M. tuberculosis H37Rv were used as quality control organisms throughout the LPA test. Both start and end controls were used during each batch of specimen processing and DNA extraction. Additionally, Lot Quality Assurance System was used for verification of new LPA reagents.

Data analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 was used to analyze data. Odds ratios (OR) with a 95% confidence interval and descriptive analysis were computed. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors statistically associated with the outcome variable (MDR-TB). Variables with a p < 0.25 in the univariable analyses were included in the multivariable models. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics and characterization of drug resistant-TB isolates

A total of 3018 presumptive and patients younger than 15 years were excluded from the study because of the exclusion criteria. GeneXpert MTB /RIF assay positive study participants (n = 163), 112 (68.7%) males and 51 (31.3%) females, were included in this study. Of these, 106 (65%), 46 (28.2%), 7 (4.3%) and 4 (2.45%) were MTBC culture-positive, culture-negative, contaminated and non-Tuberculosis mycobacterium (NTM) respectively (Fig. 2). Among 106 MTB culture positive isolates, the proportion of drug susceptible TB cases to INH and/or RIF was 86 (81.1%), and the proportion of drug resistance to RIF and INH were 21 (19.8%) and 25 (23.6%) respectively. As well as, the proportion of mono resistance to INH was 5 (4.7%) and the overall proportion of MDR-TB was 20 (18.9%) (Table 1).

A total of 80 (75.5%) participants were new TB cases, and 26 (24.5%) were previously treated patients .The patients’ ages ranged from 15 to 71, with a median age of 32. Of the patients, 121 (74.2%) were new cases of tuberculosis and 42 (25.8%) had received prior treatment. Majority of patients, 55 (33.7%), were between the ages of 25 and 34; 69 (42.3%) were married; 102 (62.6%) were urban dwellers; 46 (28.2%) had only completed elementary school and 64 (39.3%) were unemployed.

Risk factors of MDR-TB

Five candidate variables (Smoking, Residence, Marital status, History of TB patient in family and HIV co-infection in the bivariable logistic regression analysis were associated with MDR-TB and only three variables (Smoking, HIV status and Single patients) in the multivariable analysis were associated with MDR-TB.

Patients with cigarette smoking habits (AOR = 15.64, 95% CI = 3.055–80.126) were 15.64 times more likely to develop MDR-TB compared to those who did not smoke. HIV positive patients (AOR = 5.311, 95% CI 1.305–21.615) were 5.311 times more likely to develop MDR- TB compared to those who did not have HIV. Single TB patients were less likely to develop MDR-TB than married and divorced (Table 2 and Table 3).

Discussion

The prevalence of MDR-TB in this study was 18.9%. This finding is higher than reports from previous studies conducted in Tigray, Ethiopia [8], Addis Ababa, Ethiopia [10], and Oromia Region, Ethiopia [14], which documented MDR-TB prevalence of 16.7%, 15.3%, 11.5%, and 4.7%, respectively. Similarly, the prevalence observed in the current study was higher than findings from studies conducted in Swaziland [15], Mozambique [16], and Somalia [17], where MDR-TB prevalence rates were reported as 7.7%, 5.8%, and 5.2%, respectively. The prevalence observed in the present study was also substantially higher than the current global estimate, where approximately 3.2% of new TB cases develop MDR-TB according to the World Health Organization Global Tuberculosis Report 2024 [18]. This discrepancy suggests that the burden of MDR-TB in the study area reflects severe local vulnerability and ongoing healthcare challenges rather than the general global trend. Possible explanations may be disruption of tuberculosis diagnostic and treatment services, treatment interruption, delayed case detection, reduced healthcare access, and weakened health system capacity following the armed conflict in Tigray, all of which may have contributed to increased emergence and transmission of MDR-TB. In addition, the post-conflict humanitarian crisis led to massive population displacement, overcrowding in temporary shelters, food insecurity, and poor living conditions, creating favorable conditions for TB transmission within communities [19–20].

In this study, mono resistance to rifampicin was detected in 0.9% of the isolates. This finding is less than Studies carried out in Eastern Ethiopia [21], Myanmar [22] and Cameron [23] which showed an overall prevalence of 1.7%, 2% and 2.1% respectively.

The association between HIV infection and anti-TB drug is still controversial. Studies revealed that North America had more associations than Africa [16]. In this study, HIV infection was associated with the development MDRTB. This finding was consistent to a study done in Amhara National Regional state, Ethiopia [24]. Whereas a study carried out in southwestern Ethiopia, by Gemeda et al., showed lack of association between HIV infection and drug resistance [25]. Similar results were reported in other African countries [15–16]. The possible association between HIV infection and anti-TB drug resistance could be explained from different perspectives. These may include malabsorption of anti-TB drugs among HIV-positive patients, poor treatment adherence, exposure of HIV/ADS persons to MDR-TB patients during hospitalizations, or frequent visits to health facilities compared with HIV negative patients [26].

In this study, patients who had a history of cigarette smoking were at a higher risk of developing MDRTB. This finding was consistent with a study conducted in Tigray, Ethiopia [8], Burundi [27], Nepal [28] and Brazil [29].But, it was inconsistent with a study conducted in Shoa, Ethiopia [30]. Smoking of cigarette causes ciliary dysfunction and diminishes the immunity of individuals, makes them prone to primary MDR-TB [31]. These conditions became more pronounced in Tigray following the armed conflict due to economic collapse, displacement, and reduced public health interventions [19]. HIV infection, in particular, compromises immunity and increases susceptibility to active TB disease, while smoking damages lung defense mechanisms and may worsen treatment response [18]. Together, these social and behavioral determinants may have amplified both TB transmission and the development of drug resistance in the study area.

Limitations of the study

Since all study participants were TB patients seeking health services at the hospital, the reported findings may not accurately reflect the true burden of the issue at the community level. Despite these limitations, the study provides baseline information on MDR-TB and assesses various risk factors associated with MDR-TB cases in the study area.

Conclusion

The prevalence of MDR-TB was found be high. HIV co-infected, cigarette smoking habits and previous history of TB treatment were aggravating factors in the development of MDR-TB. This situation warrants urgent public health intervention, including strengthening diagnostic capacity, ensuring uninterrupted treatment supply and adherence support, and rebuilding health systems to prevent further transmission of MDR-TB in the community.
