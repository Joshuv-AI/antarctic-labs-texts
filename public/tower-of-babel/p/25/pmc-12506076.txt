Italian oncology nurses’ perceptions of empowering leadership styles: a descriptive cross-sectional exploratory study
Authors: Elsa Vitale, Alessandro Rizzo, Rocco Mea, Yun-Chen Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThe study aims to assess the associated influencing oncology nurses’ perceptions of managers’ empowering leadership style and to examine the influence of sex, work experience, shift work, membership of an oncology nursing association or a scientific society, nursing educational level and specialised nursing training in oncology on oncology nurses’ perceptions of managers’ empowering leadership style and its subdimensions.DesignDescriptive cross-sectional study.SettingIn March 2024, oncology nurses employed in oncology settings were invited to participate.ParticipantsAll Italian oncology nurses who were currently employed were eligible to participate.Main outcome measuresThe Italian version of the Empowering Leadership Questionnaire was used to assess nurses’ perceptions of managers’ performance in leading by example, participative decision-making, coaching, informing and demonstrating concern/interacting with the team.ResultsA total of 298 nurses agreed to participate. The associated factors for an empowered leadership style across all subdimensions were sex (p=0.006) and the educational level in nursing (p=0.004). Participative decision-making, coaching, informing and demonstrating concern/interacting with the team subdimensions were associated with shift work. Participation in scientific associations (p=0.005) was also influential.ConclusionsExploring the data according to the nursing specialisation appeared intriguing, since it could be a more indicative suggestion for future interventions to implement an empowering leadership style in clinical nursing practice and better inform health policymakers to achieve the right solution in their health organisations.Keywords: Nursing Care, Nursing research, ONCOLOGY, Health policy, Organisational development

Introduction

The intricacy of healthcare systems and competition in the healthcare profession necessitate structural and managerial changes to ensure flexibility, efficiency and quality. Hence, nurse leaders may improve an empowering leadership model to optimise their management-related outcomes.1 2

The literature highlights the importance of gradually organising healthcare structures and adopting an empowered working philosophy for staff that supports decision-making, management and a shift to empowered teams.1 3 Studies have indicated that empowerment can enhance job satisfaction4 and reduce the incidence of burnout among nurses,5 leading to superior patient-centred care and clinical outcomes.6,8 Hence, nurse leaders must develop empowerment conditions for their working groups and themselves9 to increase job satisfaction and autonomy.10,13 Arnold et al14 proposed an instrument to assess the implementation of an empowering leadership style and its subdimensions—the Empowering Leadership Questionnaire (ELQ). Kanter15 described empowerment in terms of formal and informal power. Formal power involves the recognised right to make decisions in an optimal working climate. Informal power involves relationships among peers and staff that extend beyond the workplace.1 Research has demonstrated that both formal and informal power affect job satisfaction by influencing access to information, support and resources.15

Laschinger et al16 expanded the concept of structural empowerment to include psychological dimensions, work stress and satisfaction.16 Structural empowerment may predict perceptions of work experience that influence staff relationships.17 Hence, nursing managers must facilitate the provision of care, enhancing patient safety and the quality of nurses’ work life through the adoption of an empowering leadership style.18 19 Studies have suggested that nurses perceive their competencies as under-represented in organisational hierarchies19,21 and that this reduced recognition adversely affects decision-making. This lack of recognition of nurse competencies affects organisational processes essential to the nursing role, such as quality of life, patient outcomes, efficiency, productivity, performance and accountability. The association between leadership style and staff experience in promoting empowerment is essential for nursing managers and leaders striving to create a work environment that results in a high level of engagement and a low turnover rate.22 Empowerment is associated with awareness management, capability and outcome internalisation23 and involves four subdimensions: employee values and beliefs, employee ability to perform work tasks, employee awareness of responsibilities and employee perceptions of the value of their work.24 25 Empowerment requires motivation,26 defined as ‘values-driven human lifestyles responding to self, others and the environment’.27

In healthcare, enhancing productivity and improving patient outcomes is crucial,26 and research has indicated that empowerment may increase access to essential details, supplies and support required to complete tasks effectively within an organisation.28 Studies exploring empowering leadership in oncology and how oncology nurses perceive the leadership styles of their nursing managers are lacking.

The present study explored the role of associated factors influencing oncology nurses’ perceptions of empowering leadership styles. Specifically, we investigated the influence of some demographic characteristics of oncology nurses’ perceptions of their managers’ empowering leadership styles and their subdimensions. The demographic characteristics included sex, work experience, shift work, membership of an oncology nursing association or a scientific society, nursing educational level and specialised nursing training in oncology.

Materials and methods

Study design

This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in March 2024 using convenience sampling.

Sample, sampling and sample size

This study adopted the simple random sampling method. In our sample, we included all Italian oncology nurses. Retired oncology nurses were excluded, since the present study only examined empowering leadership perceptions among oncology nurses in active clinical practice.

In Italy, there were nearly 400 000 nurses who were employed in all the Italian clinical specialties.29 30 A representative sample size was estimated at 400 nurses across all specialties. Because the Italian healthcare system comprises approximately 70 clinical specialties, we enrolled at least 100 oncology nurses for our study.

Data collection

The questionnaire was shared through nursing social media pages. A Google Moduli questionnaire was created and disseminated through the Nurseallface website. At the beginning of the questionnaire, a letter explaining the purpose of the study was provided, and only those who gave their written informed consent could proceed further to complete the questionnaire (online supplemental file 1).

The questionnaire began with sociodemographic questions, such as sex, years of work experience in the oncology field, shift work, membership of an oncology nursing association or a scientific society, nursing educational level and specialised nursing training in oncology.

In the second part of the questionnaire, we included the Italian version of the ELQ,31 which assessed the influence of these factors on oncology nurses’ perceptions of their managers’ empowering leadership style. The ELQ comprises 38 items, which were translated into Italian and validated by Bobbio et al,31 and assesses the following five dimensions of the empowering leadership style:

Leading by example (items 1, 6, 11, 16 and 21) describes a set of behaviours that demonstrate a leader’s commitment to their work at a level at least equivalent to that of coworkers.

Leading by example (items 1, 6, 11, 16 and 21) describes a set of behaviours that demonstrate a leader’s commitment to their work at a level at least equivalent to that of coworkers.

Participative decision-making (items 2, 7, 12, 17, 22 and 26) measures a leader’s ability to solicit and use suggestions from a group to make shared decisions.

Participative decision-making (items 2, 7, 12, 17, 22 and 26) measures a leader’s ability to solicit and use suggestions from a group to make shared decisions.

Coaching (items 3, 8, 13, 18, 23, 27, 29, 31, 34, 35 and 38) refers to a set of actions that a leader implements to train employees and help them gain self-confidence.

Coaching (items 3, 8, 13, 18, 23, 27, 29, 31, 34, 35 and 38) refers to a set of actions that a leader implements to train employees and help them gain self-confidence.

Informing (items 4, 9, 14, 19, 24 and 28) refers to a leader’s ability to disseminate critical information, the mission of the organisation, rules and priority objectives.

Demonstrating concern/interacting with the team (items 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 32, 33, 36 and 37) concerns the behaviours of a leader that demonstrate their interest in the well-being of employees and their willingness to remain apprised of the group atmosphere.

The items were preceded by a brief introduction asking the participants to answer each statement by referring to the behaviour of their nursing coordinator.

Each item was scored on a 5-point Likert scale with endpoints ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). The ELQ has a normal distribution and assesses the values for each subdimension as continuous variables.31 By assessing means for each subdimension within each dimension, a value ranging from 1 to 5 was obtained. Higher values indicate greater perceived levels of the investigated subdimension in nursing managers. Evidence suggested a good validity for the ELQ, with a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.967.32

Data analysis

All data were analysed using Excel. Participants’ characteristics were considered categorical variables and assessed as frequencies and percentages. Bobbio et al31 demonstrated the normal distribution of the Italian version of the ELQ and considered the values for each subdimension as continuous variables.31 Means and SD were calculated for each subdimension. To determine associations between the ELQ subdimensions and sample characteristics, linear regressions were conducted, considering all demographic characteristics collected as independent variables at the same time. Additionally, residual assessment for each dependent variable (ELQ subdimensions) was performed to better assess residual analyses and confirm that the differences between the actual (observed) values of the dependent variable and the values predicted by the regression model reached a good fit, highlighting that the adopted linear model represented a good choice for the data displayed in the present study (online supplemental file 2). All p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Oncology nurses were not involved in the development of the research protocol, and data were anonymised and provided under agreements that preclude patient contact.

Ethical considerations

The questionnaire shared contained, at its beginning, a clear statement of the purpose of the study. In accordance with the Committee on Publication Ethics,33 the questionnaire was shared anonymously, respecting the principles of the Italian Data Protection Authority and highlighting the willingness of individuals to provide informed consent. Participants could withdraw from the study at any time. In 2020, the Italian Superior Institute of Health summarised all the competencies and functions of the Italian ethical committee (EC). The EC should express opinions on protocols of clinical drug trials, observational clinical trials, clinical trials with medical devices or protocols for therapeutic use of investigational drugs outside clinical trials; on biomedical, psycho-educational, social or other research involving human subjects; on epidemiological, evaluative and medico-social research projects that require the collection of personal data or have environmental ethics implications; on patient information sheets and informed consent forms; on the ethical-scientific, methodological and economic aspects of experimental research protocols or amendments; and on the qualification of investigators for the purpose of conducting the proposed research as well as the ethical and scientific aspects of the same. Since the present study assessed associated factors influencing oncology nurses’ perceptions of managers’ empowering leadership style, without investigating the abovementioned fields of research, the EC of General Hospital Policlinic of Bari, Italy, with letter id no. 0023278/09/03/2023, waived the permission.

Results

A total of 298 Italian nurses employed in an oncology setting were enrolled in the present study. Of these, 67.1% were female and 32.9% male. Most of them (50%) had oncology work experience of less than 5 years; 64.1% worked night shifts, 75.1% were not associated with oncology nursing associations and 65.4% had no specialised oncology nursing training (table 1).

With regard to the participative decision-making dimension, the associated factors for this dimension were sex (p=0.036), shift work (p=0.014) and educational level in nursing (p<0.001).

In the coaching subdimension, the associated factors were sex (p=0.004), shift work (p=0.007) and educational level in nursing (p=0.002), while in the informing subdimension, the associated factors were shift work (p=0.031) and educational level in nursing (p=0.029).

In the showing concern/interacting with the team dimension, the associated factors were sex (p=0.017), shift work (p=0.033), participation in scientific associations (p=0.005) and educational level in nursing (p=0.005).

As shown in table 3, male nurses working the night shift and not part of any scientific associations, with basic and specialised oncology nursing educational training, recorded higher levels across all five subdimensions of the ELQ. Specifically, the associated factors for the leading by example subdimension were sex (p=0.006) and educational level in nursing (p=0.004), as male nurses and participants with lower educational levels recorded significantly higher values in this subdimension. With regard to the participative decision-making dimension, the associated factors for this subdimension were sex (p=0.036), shift work (p=0.014) and educational level in nursing (p<0.001), as male nurses, participants also working the night shifts and those with a lower educational level recorded significantly higher values in this aspect of the empowering leadership style. With regard to the coaching subdimension, the associated factors were sex (p=0.004), shift work (p=0.007) and educational level in nursing (p=0.002), with the same trend observed as in the participative decision-making dimension. Considering the informing subdimension, the associated factors were shift work (p=0.031) and educational level in nursing (p=0.029), as participants working night shifts and those with a lower educational level recorded significantly higher values in this aspect of the empowering leadership style. Finally, in the showing concern/interacting with the team dimension, the associated factors were sex (p=0.017), shift work (p=0.033), participation in scientific associations (p=0.005) and educational level in nursing (p=0.005), as male nurses, participants working the night shift and with a lower educational level and who were not members of nursing associations recorded significantly higher values in this aspect of the empowering leadership style.

Discussion

We investigated the influence of some demographic characteristics of oncology nurses’ perceptions of their managers’ empowering leadership styles and their subdimensions. The demographic characteristics included sex, work experience, shift work, membership of an oncology nursing association or a scientific society, nursing educational level and specialised nursing training in oncology.

A representative sample of Italian oncology nurses was enrolled, and associated factors for an effective empowered leadership style were sex and the educational level in nursing across all the empowering leadership style subdimensions (p<0.05). We also highlighted that an additional associated factor could be the shift work for the participative decision-making, coaching, informing and showing concern/interacting with the team subdimensions. Only for the showing concern/interacting with the team dimension was the participation in scientific associations (p=0.005) also considered. However, the literature shows that men were more innovative in work behaviours than women, exhibiting better innovative work behaviours and more favourable performance assessments.34 These studies gained importance in promoting innovative behaviours in the workplace, since they could help policymakers to address the phenomenon of ‘think innovation—think male’.35

Similarly, Kuokkanen and colleagues36 demonstrated how this approach promoted better outcomes in terms of moral principle orientation and sociability and competence among nurses.

As shown in the present paper and in several previous studies,37,39 the education of nurses, the type of nursing licence, workload, work independence and work satisfaction were influential relevant factors in nurse empowerment; however, the trend was different since evidence suggested that nurses with higher education felt more empowered at their work,37 probably because of more theoretical knowledge and self-confidence. However, in our study, more empowered nurses had basic nursing degrees. These findings may also depend on nursing specialisation. In any case, Rankinen et al40 suggested improving empowerment education in workplaces, especially in organisational challenges, to make the decision-making processes and related effects of organisational changes for staff members truthful. Moreover, young, graduated nurses seemed to be more empowered than their colleagues.36 These results seemed to agree with the present findings, since we observed that nurses with 6–10 years of work experience recorded higher levels of perceived empowerment. In this regard, another previous study on nurses’ perceptions of empowering leadership, without any specific specialisations, reported that younger nurses perceived empowerment more from their nursing managers than their older colleagues; demographic patterns also influenced nursing managers’ empowerment.1 Conversely, literature suggested that a lack of experience in empowerment management could reduce information, support and perception of empowerment itself,36 41 although the other variables considered in the present study, such as sex and association with any scientific societies, have not yet been explored in previous literature.

Strengths and limitations of this study

The present study represents a novel contribution, since it examined the association between participants’ characteristics and leadership features. Perhaps social desirability bias could be considered a potential limitation of our study. Additionally, the cross-sectional design of the present study could not adequately describe causality since several factors were measured at the same time. The ability to determine the temporal order and, therefore, the causal direction is limited with respect to their related outcome. Additionally, the online questionnaire could not reach all participants, especially those who were not using nursing social media pages. However, considering that nursing social media pages have nearly 8500 nurses, across all clinical specialties, the 298 oncology nurses enrolled could represent a low response rate. However, it could be considered a representative sample size.

Conclusions

Variables such as sex and association with any scientific societies have not yet been explored in the literature, and data regarding other variables are scarce. Exploring data according to the nursing specialisation appears intriguing, as it could be a more indicative suggestion for further interventions to implement in clinical practice.
