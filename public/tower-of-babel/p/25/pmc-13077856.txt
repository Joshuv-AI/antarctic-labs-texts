Mapping educational scholarship in a mixed-financing health system: a scoping review of health professions education research in Singapore
Authors: May May Yeo, Aditee Narayan, Bernard C Y Tan, Melissa S L Ng, Hak Koon Tan, Deborah D Navedo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMore than a decade since the 2010 landmark Lancet Commission called for systems-based reforms, aligning health professions education (HPE) with health system needs remains a global imperative. The aim of this scoping review was to systematically examine the literature on adult learning among Singapore’s healthcare professionals to provide evidence to guide national improvement and inform global adaptation. The study focuses on Singapore as a case of a system characterized by mixed-financing and open access to specialist care.MethodsThis scoping review followed the methodological framework established by Arksey and O’Malley, incorporating enhancements by Peters et al. The review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews checklist. Background

More than a decade ago, the landmark Lancet Commission on the Education of Health Professionals for the 21st Century issued a call for a third generation of reforms, proposing that professional education be transformed to strengthen health systems in an interdependent world [1]. The Commission highlighted a global “mismatch of competencies to patient and population needs" and advocated for educational priorities and research agendas to be directly informed by the service delivery structures, governance mechanisms, and population health goals of the systems they support (1, p. 1923). Internationally, health professions education (HPE) research has highlighted innovations such as simulation, interprofessional education, and technology-enhanced learning as key strategies for capacity building [2–5]. These innovations are most effective when tailored to the specific sociocultural, economic, and policy contexts in which they are applied [6–8]. However, amidst growing calls for scholarship that integrates local contexts and needs [9, 10], there remains a gap in comprehensive analyses addressing how different health system structures shape educational priorities, feasibility of research, and transferability of educational outcomes [6, 9, 11].

To address this gap, this study examines HPE within Singapore’s health system. We hypothesize that the health system structure is linked with HPE research feasibility. We define health systems as similar based on two key criteria: 1) a mixed-financing model that blends public subsidies with significant private insurance and out-of-pocket payments and 2) a governance structure that allows open access to specialist care. We hypothesize that these features foster a competitive and fragmented provider landscape. This environment, in turn, may disincentivize the cross-institutional collaboration and resource-sharing required for complex, multi-site research, while favoring less complex, institution-specific studies. Because peer East Asian economies such as Hong Kong and Taiwan share the structural characteristics of 1) and 2), Singapore serves as a relevant case to explore these dynamics. This model contrasts sharply with the integrated, publicly funded systems in countries such as Canada and the United Kingdom, which create different incentives for research. (Supplementary Table S1).

Therefore, the aim of this review is twofold—to map the characteristics, focus, and evidence gaps in Singapore’s HPE research and to analyze this landscape to generate transferable insights for how health system structures influence educational research. By doing so, this work provides a replicable methodology for other systems to analyze their own contexts and strategically align educational research with national priorities and global healthcare challenges.

A scoping review is a form of evidence synthesis that maps the breadth of the existing literature within or across contexts,; identifies knowledge gaps; and informs practice, policy, and HPE research [12, 13]. Seminal contributions by Arksey and O’Malley (2005), and subsequent enhancements by Levac et al. (2010), Colquhoun et al. (2014), Tricco et al. (2016 and 2018), Peters et al. (2015, 2020, and 2022) demonstrate the value of this methodology for clarifying concepts, refining definitions, and guiding research agendas [13–20]. In HPE, scoping reviews have been employed to synthesize the literature on interprofessional learning, digital education, and continuing professional development, directly informing curriculum design and faculty development [21–25].

Accordingly, we conducted a scoping review between February and July 2025 to systematically identify and synthesize the published literature on adult learning among healthcare professionals in Singapore.

Method

Study design

The review was conducted according to the framework of Arksey and O’Malley (2005), with enhancements proposed by Peters et al. (2015 and 2020) [14, 17, 18], and it is reported in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [20]. The structured yet flexible guidance provided by this framework ensured the comprehensiveness and clarity required for this first scoping review of HPE research in Singapore. The review included publications and abstracts that described, examined, referenced, or commented on adult learning in Singapore. Sources were drawn from four databases: PubMed, ERIC, SCOPUS, and CINAHL.

Identifying the research question

To establish a baseline for subsequent analysis, our foundational mapping question was “What are the characteristics, focus, and evidence gaps of the published literature on adult learning among healthcare professionals in Singapore?” This question aligns with the primary purpose of a scoping review, which is to systematically map the extent and nature of an evidence base. To develop this question, we consulted the university librarian and conducted a preliminary search in PubMed, which confirmed that no prior synthesis of this body of work existed.

Second, building on this evidence map, our second research question was “How does the landscape of HPE research in Singapore reflect the systemic challenges within a mixed-financing health system, particularly regarding the demonstration of educational impact?” This question was designed to use the findings from the first stage to analyze the transferability of our findings and address the gap in understanding how health system structures influence educational research. Both questions were structured using the participants–concept–context framework [20].

Identifying relevant articles

We searched four databases: PubMed (Medline), ERIC, Scopus, and CINAHL (Annexure A). To capture all relevant publications, we combined controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., Medical Subject Headings in PubMed) with subject headings and keywords related to adult learning, continuing education, and professional development, together with the term “Singapore.” The PubMed search string included Medical Subject Headings terms “Learning,” “Curriculum,” “Education, Continuing,” combined with keywords such as “adult learning,” “professional development,” “andragogy,” and “Singapore.”

Search queries were adapted for each database to optimize the retrieval of articles focusing on adult education in Singapore. No publication date limit was applied, and the search spanned from the inception of each database through February 2025 (Annexure A). The inclusion criteria were as follows:

The search was intentionally focused on peer-reviewed literature and did not extend to grey literature, such as government reports and institutional documents. This decision was made to ensure a baseline of scholarly integrity through the peer-review process and to maintain a manageable scope by focusing on the formal academic research landscape.

Article selection

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts to exclude articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria. Full-text screening was then conducted on the remaining studies to confirm their relevance to the research question. Eligible studies were retained for data extraction. The screening process was documented using Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia).

Charting the data

During the extraction phase, we piloted 10 publications using Covidence’s reference template. The team discussed and added three fields to strengthen the ability to answer the research question. The first field, teaching methods, encompassed simulation, online learning, and the clinical learning environment. Items within this field were developed inductively and grouped during extraction to identify common and emerging teaching methods as well as their outcomes.

The second field applied the “educator competencies and activities” framework outlined by the professional standards of the Academy of Medical Educators (AOME) [26]. This framework defines the skills and knowledge required of medical educators and is broadly applicable to both clinical and non-clinical educators. The competencies include designing and planning of learning, teaching and facilitation of learning, assessment of learning, educational scholarship and evidence-based practice, and educational management and leadership. Classifying articles using this framework allowed us to map the most frequently researched educator activities and highlight gaps that would be valuable for future research.

The third field incorporated the Kirkpatrick Model to classify the “learner-related outcomes” of each study [27, 28]. This framework defines four levels of impact: level 1— learner reaction or satisfaction, level 2—learner knowledge and skills gained, level 3—the extent to which learners applied knowledge and skills during the program, and level 4—the degree to which targeted outcomes occurred because of the learning.

Additionally, we replaced “Study Design” in the default Covidence template with “Methodology Used” and “Method of Data Collection.” This modification allowed us to classify studies as quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods and to document the data collection approaches. We piloted 20 additional articles and finalized the template through consensus between two reviewers. The extracted information included the title, year of publication, health profession, undergraduate (UG) or postgraduate (PG) education, study aim, methodology, method of data collection, AOME practice domains, teaching method, sample representation, number of participants, conclusion, limitations, future research directions, and Kirkpatrick level achieved/discussed.

Collating, summarizing, and reporting the results

We used Covidence’s download feature to transfer the extracted information into Microsoft Excel, where descriptive statistics were computed and review characteristics were summarized. During two team calls, we discussed the review findings in relation to the research question and resolved differences through discussion.

Our search identified 762 unique articles. After title and abstract screening, 618 were excluded. Full-text reviews was conducted on 144 articles to confirm their relevance to the research question, and 58 articles met the inclusion criteria for data extraction. Nursing professionals comprised the largest group studied (34%), followed by medical professionals (26%). Quantitative approaches were the most common (50%), with survey-based data collection used in 29% of studies. More than half of the publications (55%) focused on PG or continuing professional development programs. Limited attention was given to assessment—UG (10%) and PG (5%), and to educational scholarship—UG (2%) and PG (0%). The majority of publications evaluated outcomes at Kirkpatrick Level 2 (52%) and Level 3 (31%), with few assessing Level 4 results (5%).

Discussion

This scoping review was designed to achieve a twofold aim. The first was to map the characteristics, focus, and evidence gaps in Singapore’s HPE research. Our mapping revealed a landscape that is concentrated on specific professions; is focused on quantitative methods; and lacks multi-site, Kirkpatrick Level 4 outcome publications. The second, more interpretive aim, was to analyze this landscape to understand how health system structures might influence educational research. This analysis does not demonstrate causality but rather generates a testable hypothesis about this relationship and provides a clear agenda for future inquiry. Our synthesis of 58 articles revealed that over half (55%) were in PG and continuing professional development settings, with relatively few multi-institutional studies and a small proportion of higher-order outcome evaluations (5% at Kirkpatrick Level 4). We interpret these trends as plausibly shaped by the broader characteristics of a mixed, competitive healthcare delivery model, in which multiple payers and providers interact in ways that may prioritize local performance and productivity over collaborative, longitudinal research on organizational and patient-level outcomes.

Predominance of nursing and medical professions

The nursing and medical professions were most frequently studied (34% and 26%, respectively), followed by allied health professionals (9%), pharmacists (5%), and dental professionals (2%). Analyses of global HPE research from 2000 to 2020 similarly revealed a predominant focus on nursing and medicine, with limited coverage of other health professions [9]. Another scoping review evaluating programs for nurses and allied health professionals demonstrated a disproportionate emphasis on nursing despite shared educational needs [88]. These international benchmarks suggest that our findings are consistent with global trends, potentially amplified by resource allocation toward the larger nursing and medical workforces. As of 2024 in Singapore, there were 50,389 nurses (8.3 nurses per 1,000 population), 17,582 doctors (2.9 per 1,000), 4,283 pharmacists (0.7 per 1,000), and 2,812 dentists (0.5 per 1,000) [89]. The distribution of research evidence across healthcare professions was proportionate to national workforce data and reflected Singapore’s emphasis on strengthening the nursing workforce to meet demands of an aging population [90]. Nevertheless, based on Knowles’ principles of andragogy [91], which emphasize relevance to professional life and problem-centered learning, allied health, pharmacy, and dental professions should expand HPE research to address the specific educational needs of their learners. More broadly, our study suggests a shared, global challenge of ensuring the educational needs of all health care professions, not just those with the largest numbers, are met with robust scholarship Therefore, Singapore and countries with similar health systems would benefit from moving beyond proportionate representation towards fostering educational scholarship within and across diverse healthcare professions and strengthening the evidence base for quality education quality and patient care.

Methodological approaches

Quantitative designs (50%), such as surveys and questionnaires, were the most common, followed by qualitative methods (22%), primarily interviews and focus groups, which have gained prominence over the past decade. Mixed- or multi-method studies (14%) were increasingly utilized. A review of 90 HPE papers published in 2018–2019 across 15 HPE journals, with participants from North America (49%) and Europe (20%), found that quantitative designs comprised 51%, qualitative 28%, and mixed methods 20% [92]. Similarly, an analysis of trends in HPE research revealed that over half of 1,126 publications employed quantitative methods, followed by qualitative and mixed approaches [9]. Thus, the methodological distribution in Singapore's HPE research aligns with international patterns. Additionally, descriptive studies and commentaries (10%) typically highlighted program implementation.

The increase in mixed-methods research in Singapore is encouraging, as it enables researchers to generate insights beyond those attainable through quantitative or qualitative approaches alone [93]. Continuing professional development programs could incorporate workshops on mixed-methods design to equip researchers to address HPE challenges specific to professional domains, Singapore’s multicultural landscape, and cross-cultural contexts involving international collaborations. Other health systems with similar structural complexities may similarly consider developing and funding multi-site, mixed-methods research frameworks capable of generating robust, transferable insights to understand educational outcomes across diverse contexts.

Distribution of UG and PG programs

More than half of the studies (55%) examined PG or continuing professional development programs. This may reflect national priorities on lifelong learning, such as the SkillsFuture Singapore scheme, which provides funding for continuing professional development activities [94]. While future studies may assess the long-term impact of such initiatives on healthcare delivery [95], it is equally important to evaluate the continuity between UG and PG training to optimize the professional development of healthcare professionals.

Therefore, the shared challenge is developing research frameworks that can capture and enhance the entire educational continuum, ensuring that investments in lifelong learning build upon—rather than compensate for—robust foundational training. Potential frameworks include longitudinal cohort studies, which follow learners from UG education through early-career practice to examine how educational experiences evolve over time, and transition-focused research that explore critical handoff points between educational phases, particularly in complex health systems where learners move between diverse practice settings.

Sample sizes and populations

Sample sizes varied, ranging from fewer than 20 participants in qualitative studies to more than 100 in quantitative studies. Most studies were single-institution or single-cohort, with few spanning multiple sites or cohorts. Because learning in healthcare professions occurs in authentic workplace contexts, the generalizability of findings was limited. Incorporating mixed-methods, multi-cohort, and multi-institutional designs could better capture the diverse contexts of healthcare professionals and generate insights to inform inclusive global HPE [96].

We speculate that the complexity of coordinating research across public and private institutions represents a systemic barrier, contributing to evidence skewed towards single-institution studies. The shared challenge is to build sustainable collaborative frameworks and infrastructure—such as data-sharing agreements and dedicated streams to overcome structural barriers. By doing so, these systems can collectively generate more robust, generalizable evidence that reflects the complex realities of learning within their unique healthcare contexts.

Teaching methods and educational priorities

The most frequently studied pedagogical methods were simulation-based training (29%) and online learning (12%). The emphasis on simulation, including virtual reality and role-play, aligns with global trends that identify it as a standard for experiential, skills-focused training [97]. Similarly, the rise of online learning, likely accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, reflects a system-wide move toward flexible educational delivery to overcome time and geographical constraints [98, 99].

Beyond the methods used, the research also highlighted key educational content areas. A significant number of publications focused on teaching communication skills and the delivery of effective feedback. These topics were often taught using the methods described above, such as role-play for difficult conversations or structured online modules for feedback frameworks. This emphasis reflects a growing recognition that non-technical skills are essential components of competency in healthcare [100, 101].

In addition, we identified several topics, including needs analysis (9%) and the clinical learning environment (7%). The focus on needs analysis highlights its role in ensuring that professional development is relevant and impactful for patient outcomes [102, 103]. Similarly, studies on the clinical learning environment reflect an understanding that workplace-based factors are critical to learner development and professional satisfaction [104, 105].

AOME practice domains

Figure 2 presents heatmaps of AOME practice domains by program type. The distribution of studies showed concentrations in “teaching and facilitating learning” (16% UG, 19% PG) and “designing and planning learning activities” (14% UG, 17% PG). In contrast, domains such as “assessment of learning” (10% UG, 5% PG) and “educational scholarship and evidence-based practice” (2% UG, 0% PG) were substantially underrepresented. This distribution suggests that while “teaching and facilitating learning” and “designing and planning learning activities” were well represented, the full scope of domains outlined by AOME has not been reflected in Singapore’s HPE research. Underrepresentation in “assessment of learning” and “educational scholarship and evidence-based practice” contrasts with their established importance. Boyer emphasized expanding the concept of “scholarship” beyond a binary of “teaching versus research” while van der Vleuten highlighted assessment practices that achieve “maximal fidelity” [106, 107].

We propose there exists a limitation within mixed-financing health systems where educators face competing demands that prioritize teaching over resource-intensive effort of educational scholarship. Addressing this imbalance requires intentional efforts to cultivate scholarship within these systems—by creating protected time, establishing dedicated funding, and developing academic career pathways for educators engaged in HPE research.

Kirkpatrick-level outcomes

Figure 3 presents the distribution of Kirkpatrick outcomes by program type. The findings showed a predominance of Level 2 (learning), which accounted for 52% of the studies (26% UG, 26% PG). Level 3 (behavior) was also well represented, comprising 31% of the studies (14% UG, 17% PG). In contrast, Level 4 (results) was reported in only 5% of the studies (0% UG, 5% PG), while Level 1 (reaction) was reported in 7% (2% UG and 5% PG). A small subset (3%) of programs involving both UG and PG learners was excluded from the distribution.

The heavy emphasis on Level 2 (learning) outcomes (52%) relative to Level 4 (results) outcomes (5%) may be reflective of systemic challenges identified earlier—single-institution study designs and focus on educational delivery over sustained scholarship. While this limitation is not unique to Singapore, we postulate that it exists in advanced economies with similar mixed-financing and multi-institutional health systems, such as Hong Kong. In these contexts, a competitive provider landscape and fragmented delivery systems may create powerful disincentives against the resource-intensive, cross-institutional collaboration required for Level 4 evaluations. This hypothesis is consistent with broader patterns observed in systematic reviews of faculty development for teaching effectiveness [108, 109], which also find a predominant focus on lower-level outcomes.

Synthesizing gaps in healthcare professionals’ education research in Singapore and similar healthcare systems

Our review revealed limited attention to assessment research (5% at the PG level) and to educational scholarship, with evidence-based practice nearly absent at both UG and PG levels. While most studies evaluated improvements in knowledge, skills, or attitudes (Kirkpatrick Levels 2 and 3), outcomes assessed at Kirkpatrick level 4 (results) were scarce. These gaps restrict understanding of how educational interventions translate into measurable changes in professional practice and healthcare outcomes.

Future studies should therefore prioritize designs capable of demonstrating meaningful impacts on patient care and organizational performance and system-level outcomes, thereby increasing the relevance of HPE research to healthcare professionals’ roles and work goals [110]. Studies in this review frequently emphasized the need for more rigorous evaluation, including longitudinal follow-up, larger and more diverse samples (e.g., multi-institutional groups), and replication across settings and professions to strengthen generalizability of findings.

Study limitations

A major limitation of this study is its restriction to English-language publications, which may have excluded relevant research in other languages. We also did not apply snowball sampling or other supplementary methods to identify additional articles beyond the four databases searched, potentially limiting the breadth of studies and insights on adult education among healthcare professionals in Singapore. Moreover, the exclusion of grey literature may have excluded relevant program evaluations or policy commentaries not published in peer-reviewed journals. Additionally, the full texts of seven studies were unavailable through the library, which may have further constrained the analysis. Furthermore, by treating empirical studies and non-empirical sources (e.g., editorials) as equal data points in our map, this review documents the prevalence of topics but does not formally assess the strength or quality of the underlying evidence for each topic. Finally, while categorization using the Kirkpatrick model provides a useful snapshot of the research landscape, the distribution of studies across the Kirkpatrick levels should be interpreted with an awareness of the model's constraints [111, 112].

Conclusion

By systematically mapping the HPE research within a mixed-financing health system (Singapore), this study offers a replicable methodology for other countries to analyze their contexts. Our review found that Singapore’s HPE research is concentrated in nursing and medicine, predominantly uses quantitative methods, and shows limited evidence of educational scholarship or assessment research in PG programs. The observed patterns—underrepresentation of multi-institutional and higher-level outcome studies—raise important questions about potential systemic influences. While our descriptive findings cannot establish causality, they provide a basis for hypothesizing how health system structures may influence educational priorities and research feasibility. We therefore propose investigating the hypothesis that competitive provider environments in mixed-financing systems may inhibit the collaborative, resource-intensive research needed for higher-order, multi-institutional evaluation. By providing a replicable methodology and clear research agenda for understanding local contexts, this review helps advance the reforms called for by the 2010 Lancet Commission and its 2022 follow-up Health Policy, and to advance the vision of adapting global educational frameworks to local contexts to strengthen health systems [1, 98].
