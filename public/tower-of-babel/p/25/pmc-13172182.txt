Pain Relief and Multidimensional Outcomes of Fast-Acting Subperception Spinal Cord Stimulation Therapy for Chronic Pain: The FAST Prospective, Multicenter Study
Authors: Magdalena Anitescu, Eric Loudermilk, James North, Sayed Wahezi, Ajay Antony, Tim Leier, Drew Trainor, John Noles, Gregory Moore, Jennifer Lee, Julio Paez, Derron Wilson, Virginie Van Belleghem, Edward Goldberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFast-acting subperception therapy in spinal cord stimulation (FAST-SCS) uses low-frequency, subthreshold stimulation to engage the dorsal column-mediated surround inhibition mechanism and thereby relieve pain. Our study was performed to better characterize the multidimensional long-term clinical outcomes of FAST-SCS therapy in patients with chronic low back and/or leg pain.MethodsOur prospective, multicenter, single-arm, open-label trial, conducted at 12 pain management centers across the USA, included male and female patients with chronic low back pain with/without leg pain refractory to conservative therapy. All eligible patients received an SCS implant. Outcomes evaluated included pain relief, disability, mobility, health-related quality of life, sleep quality, depression, pain catastrophizing, and global impression of change.ResultsFifty-six patients received a permanent SCS implant and 55 were activated with FAST-SCS therapy. At 12 months, 89% and 59% of patients achieved ≥ 50% and ≥ 80% pain relief, respectively (p < 0.0001), and pain intensity (verbal numerical rating scale) decreased by 4.9 ± 2.2 points (p < 0.0001). Significant improvements were observed in disability (Oswestry Disability Index − 24 ± 16 points, p < 0.0001) and endurance (6-Minute Walk Test + 73 ± 96 m, p < 0.0001), while mobility and balance improved without reaching statistical significance (Timed Up-and-Go test − 7.5 ± 37.5 s, p = 0.17). All psychosocial measures improved significantly at 12 months compared to baseline, with an increase in the EQ5D-5L index (+ 0.200 ± 0.182, p < 0.0001) and decreases in the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality index (− 3.96 ± 4.31, p < 0.0001), Pain Catastrophizing Score (− 13.3 ± 11.4, p < 0.0001), and Beck Depression Index (− 7.9 ± 10.0, p < 0.0001). At 12 months, 87% of patients reported being “much” or “very much” improved. Responses to FAST-SCS were at or above the minimal clinically important differences across all measured clinical dimensions.ConclusionThis prospective study demonstrated that FAST-SCS provided statistically significant, multidimensional benefits across pain, disability, mobility, and psychosocial outcomes over 12 months in patients with chronic low back and/or leg pain. Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?There is limited prospective evidence characterizing the multidimensional outcomes of spinal cord stimulation (SCS) in patients with chronic low back and/or leg pain, particularly with respect to objective functional and psychosocial domains.This prospective study evaluated the effectiveness of fast-acting subperception therapy (FAST-SCS) on pain relief and also assessed its impact on global patient outcomes, including functional measures, objective mobility tests, and psychosocial endpoints.What was learned from this study?Results over 12 months demonstrate that FAST-SCS provided sustained pain relief, accompanied by significant improvements in mobility, quality of life, and psychosocial outcomes.These findings inform the ongoing management of an often difficult-to-treat patient population, in whom chronic pain represents only one component of a broader multidimensional condition.

Introduction

Since the introduction of the gate control theory [1] and subsequent clinical implementation of spinal cord stimulation (SCS) using low-frequency, paresthesia-based modalities [2, 3], the SCS field has seen continual technological evolution. Notably, higher-frequency, paresthesia-free waveforms have emerged, enabling novel treatment mechanisms targeting both dorsal column fibers and dorsal horn networks [4–9].

As evidence has grown, SCS systems have evolved from monotherapy platforms to multimodal devices capable of delivering customizable treatments based on patient needs [10–14]. Concurrently, mechanistic studies have improved our understanding of subperception stimulation, particularly regarding wide dynamic range (WDR) and lateral pathway involvement. These findings guided further waveform optimization, including advanced subperception modalities [8, 9] and combination therapy [15, 16].

Fast-acting subperception therapy (FAST-SCS) emerged within this landscape. FAST-SCS uses low-frequency, subthreshold stimulation to engage the dorsal column-mediated surround inhibition mechanism [17–22]. Unlike paresthesia-based SCS, which targets the central receptive field, FAST-SCS selectively excites the surrounding neurons to suppress activation of the WDR projection neurons. In the first clinical experience with FAST-SCS, published in 2021, a profound and rapid analgesic response over the 6-month follow-up was reported in 41 subjects, and FAST-SCS required substantially less energy than conventional subperception waveform paradigms [23]. Long-term observational studies using retrospective chart reviews have demonstrated profound pain relief and durable effects over time, with up to 60% of patients reporting minimal pain (numerical rating scale (NRS) ≤ 2) over 2 years [24, 25].

Holistic assessments of SCS have been conducted for different waveforms [26–28] and a guiding framework was recently developed to discuss the implementation of multidimensional clinical measures in clinical practice [29].

We aimed to characterize the multidimensional impact of subperception FAST-SCS therapy by conducting a prospective, multicenter study to assess long-term pain relief as well as functional and psychosocial outcomes. The primary objective was to demonstrate the effectiveness of FAST-SCS, using a non-inferiority hypothesis based on the 3-month responder rate (proportion of patients with ≥ 50% pain relief without an increase in daily opioid use). Secondary outcomes included pain intensity, physical function, health-related quality of life (HRQoL), emotional health, sleep quality, and patient satisfaction. Here, we present the 12-month interim results of this ongoing prospective trial.

Methods

Study Design

The FAST study is a prospective, multicenter, single-arm, open-label study in which patients with chronic pain of the lower back and/or lower limbs were implanted with an SCS system (Boston Scientific, Valencia, CA, USA). It was conducted at 12 sites within the USA (ClinicalTrials.gov; NCT04618471), sponsored by Boston Scientific Neuromodulation Corporation (Valencia, CA, USA). Each site obtained ethical approval (Table S1), and the study was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practices (ISO14155) guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. This ongoing study will continue until all subjects have completed their 5-year follow-up visits. Patients were enrolled between February 10, 2021 and November 16, 2023, and provided signed informed consent before screening.

Patient Selection

Adults (male and female, aged 22 years or older) with chronic, predominantly neuropathic pain of the trunk and/or limbs for at least 6 months, with back pain ≥ leg pain, were screened (Fig. 1a). The average targeted (i.e., area of pain intended to be treated by SCS) pain intensity had to be ≥ 6 (0–10 NRS) at baseline, with significant disability due to low back pain (Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) score ≥ 30 and ≤ 80), and pain refractory to conservative treatments (e.g., pain medications, physical therapy, facet joint/medial branch nerve blocks, etc.). If taking prescription opioids for their primary chronic pain complaint, patients must have been on a stable dose, with an average total daily morphine equivalent ≤ 200 mg during at least 30 days prior to screening.

a Study schematic; b patient disposition

Patients were excluded if they had received back surgery within 6 months prior to screening; radiographic evidence of spinal instability requiring fusion; a body mass index ≥ 45 at screening; leg pain > low back pain; a current systemic infection close to the anticipated surgical field or current condition that might increase the risk of infection during study duration; a high surgical risk (e.g., spine compression, appreciable neurological deficit, etc.); significant cognitive impairment that could impair participation; received a previous SCS trial or had already been implanted with active implantable devices (e.g., pacemaker, drug pump, implantable pulse generator); a condition requiring diathermy and/or MRI not approved per device instructions for use. The full inclusion/exclusion criteria are in Table S2.

The study had an eligibility review committee (comprising physicians and a psychologist experienced in evaluating patients for SCS and clinical research) responsible for reviewing de-identified medical history, screening documentation, and the report from the psychological/psychiatric evaluator; this enabled independent review of subject eligibility and ensured consistency of subjects enrolled across study centers. Reviews were conducted after the baseline visit; subjects deemed “eligible” per the committee’s decision proceeded with SCS implantation. Patients who provided written informed consent but did not meet all study eligibility criteria were not implanted or activated. These patients were deemed “enrolled but not activated” and did not count towards the enrollment cap; their reason for ineligibility was documented.

For patients using opioid medications for their chronic pain prior to enrollment, an “opioid medication lock” visit was conducted within 35 days of informed consent and prior to the baseline assessments. During this visit, opioid prescriptions were locked, i.e., no changes in opioid type, dose, route, or frequency were permitted from that point through the 3-month post-activation visit. The only exception to this restriction was the use of short-term opioids or other analgesic medications during the 14–28-day healing period following permanent implant, to support postsurgical recovery.

SCS Procedure

All patients who met the study eligibility criteria proceeded to an SCS trial, conducted according to standard of care. The trial was deemed successful if the patient reported ≥ 50% reduction in targeted pain intensity compared to baseline. Only patients with a successful trial proceeded to permanent implantation of the SCS system (WaveWriter Alpha™; Boston Scientific, Valencia, CA, USA).

During the permanent implantation procedure, patients received percutaneous or surgical paddle leads, all designed with 16 or 32 tight-spaced contacts. As per standard practice for SCS therapy, the placement of percutaneous leads was done with on-table testing to confirm ≥ 80% pain paresthesia overlap. Appropriate lead placement was confirmed with postoperative imaging. Following the implant, patients underwent a 14–28-day healing period, during which the device remained inactive.

The “activation” visit (day 0) marked the start of FAST-SCS therapy. At this visit, the implanted device was turned on and programmed with the FAST-SCS waveform. FAST-SCS programming utilizes biphasic symmetric pulses with active recharge at 90 Hz frequency and 210 ± 50 μs pulse width. After paresthesia was steered with these settings at high resolution and at the lowest practical amplitude capable of inducing paresthesia to cover all painful areas, a subperception amplitude was personalized to each patient at 30% of the perception threshold. The time required to achieve ≥ 50% pain relief after FAST-SCS activation was documented.

Follow-up visits occurred at 3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 months after activation.

Data Collection

The primary endpoint was the responder rate at the 3-month follow-up visit, defined as the proportion of patients with ≥ 50% reduction in the average targeted pain intensity (based on patient-reported pain relief on a 0–100% scale) at 3 months post-activation compared with baseline, with no increase in baseline average daily opioid medications used to treat pain. Secondary endpoints at 3 months included changes in pain intensity, percent pain relief (PPR), responder rate (proportion of patients with ≥ 50% pain relief), physical functioning (disability and mobility), HRQoL, sleep quality, depression, catastrophizing, and patient global impression of change compared to the baseline visit. These were also evaluated as exploratory endpoints over 12 months, with data over 24 months still being recorded.

Pain relief outcomes were assessed using the 11-point verbal NRS (VNRS; scale 0–10, where 0 was “no pain” and 10 was “worst possible pain” to measure pain intensity of the overall targeted pain (i.e., the pain area intended to be treated with SCS) and low back and/or leg pain; score ≤ 3 (mild pain), 4–6 (moderate pain), ≥ 7 (severe pain) [30]) and the patient-reported PPR questionnaire (scale 0–100%). Responder rates were defined as the proportion of patients with ≥ 50% pain relief and were reported for overall pain as well as low back and leg pain. Physical functioning assessments included a measure of functional disability due to low back pain via the ODI [31]. In addition, objective mobility tests were performed: the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT) [32] and the Timed Up-and-Go test (TUG) [33]. The 6MWT is a submaximal exercise test used to assess aerobic capacity and endurance; the distance covered over a time of 6 min is used as the outcome. The TUG test is used to assess functional mobility, balance, and fall risk; it consists of timing an individual as they stand up from a chair with armrests, walk a 3-m distance at a comfortable pace (with or without their usual walking aid), turn around, walk back, and sit down again. The HRQoL evaluation used the EuroQol 5 Dimension 5 Level questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L index) [34], while sleep quality was evaluated using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Inventory (PSQI) questionnaire [35]. The Beck Depression Index (BDI) [36] and Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) [37] questionnaires were used to describe changes in patients’ emotional functioning. Patient satisfaction was measured with the patient global impression of change questionnaire (P-GIC) [38]. An optional qualitative survey was also sent to all patients after they had completed their 12-month follow-up visit. Finally, adverse events (AEs) were documented during the course of the study. Full details of data collection are outlined in Table S3, with further details about clinical assessments in Table S4 and minimal clinically important difference (MCID) references for multiple clinical endpoints in Table S5.

Statistical Analysis

The required sample size for this study (66 activated subjects) was calculated based on various assumptions (Table S6). Multiple “futility and effectiveness stopping” interim analysis looks for the 3-month primary endpoint were planned.

The statistical analysis was conducted in the intent-to-treat population, defined as all implanted and activated subjects. The primary endpoint was the proportion of subjects achieving ≥ 50% reduction in average daily targeted pain intensity at 3 months post-activation, with no increase in baseline opioid use. The study was designed to assess non-inferiority relative to a pre-specified objective performance criterion (OPC) responder rate of 0.40, using a non-inferiority margin of 0.20. Non-inferiority was concluded if the lower bound of the two-sided 95% confidence interval (CI) for the difference between the observed responder rate and the OPC exceeded − 0.20.

Results

Patient Demographic and Baseline Characteristics

Overall, 177 patients were enrolled based on their signed informed consent (Fig. 1b). Of these, 101 were discontinued due to screening failure (n = 85) or study withdrawal (n = 16). Seventy-six patients had a screening trial procedure, followed by permanent implantation of the SCS system in 56 subjects. Twenty trialed patients did not proceed with permanent implantation due to screen failure (n = 5), unsuccessful trial (n = 5), eligibility review failure (n = 4), study withdrawal (n = 4), and insurance denial (n = 2). Permanent implantation of the SCS system was performed on average 55.3 ± 38.3 days after the trial period (median 45 [14–257] days). The SCS leads used for permanent implantation included 8-contact percutaneous leads (n = 27), 16-contact percutaneous leads (n = 16), and 32-contact surgical paddles (n = 13). Out of the 56 implanted patients, 55 were activated with FAST-SCS (one implanted patient failed screening as they did not achieve 80–100% pain paresthesia overlap during intraoperative testing at permanent placement of the SCS leads) and constituted the study group (Fig. 1b). Enrollment was suspended after an interim analysis confirmed the primary statistical hypothesis.

The most frequent diagnoses for chronic pain were radiculopathy (40/55 patients, 72.7%) and persistent spinal pain syndrome type 2 (PSPS2; 35/55, 63.6%) (Table 1). All patients had low back pain, associated with leg pain in 72.7% of patients (40/55). At the time of the baseline visit, low back and leg pain had lasted for 14.0 ± 10.1 years and 9.9 ± 9.1 years, respectively.

At baseline, the mean pain intensity was 7.4 ± 1.0 for overall and low back pain, and 5.0 ± 1.9 for leg pain. Patients had severe disability (mean baseline ODI score 51.7 ± 8.4), with 78% (43/55) presenting with severe disability (ODI score 41–60) and 15% (8/55) being “crippled” (ODI score 61–80). Mobility and physical capabilities were limited (objective mobility tests: 6MWT distance 231 ± 123 m, n = 51; TUG time 18.7 ± 35.0 s, n = 54). Patients also reported mild depressive symptoms (mean BDI score 14.7 ± 9.4, n = 55) and sleep disturbances (mean PSQI score 11.6 ± 4.3, n = 55).

The number of patients who completed their study visits was 55 at 3 months and 53 at 12 months, with two voluntary study withdrawals.

Primary Endpoint at 3 Months and Pain Relief Outcomes over 12 Months

At the activation visit, the mean time to achieve 50% pain relief with FAST-SCS was 2.6 ± 3.0 min (n = 48) after the therapy was turned on.

At the 3-month visit after the activation visit period, 78.2% of patients (43/55) (95% CI 0.673–0.891) reported ≥ 50% overall pain relief without opioid increase (p < 0.0001).

The change in VNRS for the overall targeted pain was − 5.1 ± 2.2 at 3 months (n = 54, p < 0.0001) and − 4.9 ± 2.2 at 12 months (n = 52, p < 0.0001), with a mean overall VNRS pain score of 2.2 ± 2.0 and 2.5 ± 2.0 at 3 and 12 months, respectively. Similarly, low back and leg pain VNRS scores were significantly reduced to levels that correspond to mild pain. At the 3- and 12-month follow-up visits, low back pain decreased by − 5.3 ± 2.2 and − 5.1 ± 2.2 points, respectively (p < 0.0001), and the VNRS leg pain by − 3.7 ± 2.3 and − 3.3 ± 2.4 (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2a). At 12 months, the patient-reported PPR was 76.8 ± 20.9% for overall pain (n = 53), 76.0 ± 22.6% for low back pain (n = 53), and 73.4 ± 24.5% for leg pain (n = 40). The responder rate for ≥ 50% overall pain relief at 3 and 12 months was 81.8% (45/55) and 88.7% (47/53), respectively (p < 0.0001), with 59% patients reporting > 80% pain relief (p < 0.0001). Similar responder rates were reported for low back pain and leg pain (Fig. 2b). Daily opioid doses were found to be stable over time, with a mean 26.7 ± 37.6 mg/day (median 11.3 [0–160], n = 55) at baseline, 24.9 ± 36.3 mg/day (median 11.3 [0–160], n = 55) at 3 months, and 25.4 ± 42.3 mg/day (median 10.0 [0–200], n = 53) at 12 months.

Physical Functioning over 12 Months

Disability improved significantly at 3 months (change in ODI − 26.1 ± 14.9, p < 0.0001), with sustained outcomes over 12 months (mean ODI score 28.0 ± 14.8; change − 23.7 ± 16.1; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3a).

Disability: a Oswestry Disability (ODI) score; b evolution of patient distribution across ODI stages. Error bars represent standard errors of the mean

The proportion of patients with severe or crippled disability dropped from 93% at baseline to 25% at 12 months, while the percentage of patients with minimal or moderate disability increased from 7% to 76% (Fig. 3b).

Objective mobility tests showed that patients experienced an increase in functional capability. Functional endurance as assessed by the 6MWT distance significantly increased by 74 ± 95 m (n = 47; p < 0.0001) at 3 months. This rapid improvement was sustained with a 304 ± 129 m walking distance measured at 12 months (+ 73 ± 95 m, n = 49; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4a). Mobility and balance, as assessed by the TUG duration, improved by 7.9 ± 35.7 s (from 18.7 ± 35.0 to 11.2 ± 3.9 s, n = 52; p = 0.11) at 3 months, which was sustained over 12 months (change − 7.5 ± 37.5 s, n = 50; p = 0.17) (Fig. 4b). Although not statistically significant, the mean reduction in TUG duration allowed patients to reach average levels that were very close to the normative reference values in healthy adult populations [39, 40]. Further analysis of TUG results tested the median of the TUG change using the signed rank method, which showed statistical significance at both the 3- and 12-month follow-up compared to baseline (p < 0.0001).

HRQoL and Sleep Quality over 12 Months

The HRQoL improved significantly at 3 and 12 months, with the EQ-5D-5L index improving by 0.219 ± 0.161 points (p < 0.0001) at 3 months (n = 55) and by 0.200 ± 0.182 points (p < 0.0001) at 12 months (n = 53) (Fig. 5a). At 12 months, the mean EQ-5D-5L index (0.772 ± 0.136, n = 53) had reached a level similar to the established 0.815 US normative reference value for a 55–64-year-old population [41].

Change in a health-related quality of life (EQ-5D-5L index) and b sleep quality (PSQI score) over 12 months after FAST-SCS activation. Error bars represent standard errors of the mean. FAST-SCS fast-acting subperception therapy spinal cord stimulation, EQ-5D-5L EuroQol 5 Dimension 5 Level, PSQI Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Inventory

There was a significant improvement in sleep quality over 3 months, with the PSQI score reduced from 11.6 ± 4.3 to 7.8 ± 4.0 (− 3.8 ± 4.0 points; p < 0.0001). At 12 months, the average reduction in the PSQI score (− 4.0 ± 4.3 points, n = 53; p < 0.0001) was significant and showed a clear evolution towards the normative value of 6.3 (established in a US community sample [42]) (Fig. 5b).

At 12 months, the proportion of patients with good sleep quality (i.e., PSQI ≤ 5) increased from 9% (5/55) at baseline to 38% (20/53) at 12 months.

Depression and Catastrophizing over 12 Months

In association with pain reduction, significant improvements were reported in the BDI and PCS scores. The BDI was reduced from 14.6 ± 9.4 points at baseline (in the range of mild depression) to 6.9 ± 7.3 at 12 months (− 7.9 ± 10.0, n = 53; p < 0.0001), corresponding to minimal depression (Fig. 6a).

Change in a depression (BDI); b patient catastrophizing (PCS) over 12 months after using FAST-SCS; c evolution of PCS scores in patients who had a PCS > 30 at baseline (n = 10). Error bars represent standard errors of the mean. BDI Beck Depression Inventory, FAST-SCS fast-acting subperception therapy spinal cord stimulation, PCS patient catastrophizing scale

The proportion of patients with moderate (BDI score 20–28) or severe (BDI score 29–63) depressive symptoms decreased from 27% (15/55) at baseline to 9% (5/53) at 12 months, while patients with minimal signs of depression (BDI score 0–13) increased from 53% (29/55) to 81% (43/53).

The average PCS score decreased from 22.0 ± 9.9 at baseline to 8.6 ± 8.8 at 12 months (mean change 13.3 ± 11.4 points, n = 53; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 6b).

At baseline, 18% patients (10/55) reported a clinically significant level of pain catastrophizing at baseline (PCS score > 30); at 12 months, 80% (8/10) were no longer catastrophizing, while two patients continued to meet criteria for clinically significant catastrophizing (Fig. 6c). Catastrophizing patients showed a significant reduction in their PCS score, from 36.8 ± 5.0 at baseline to 13.3 ± 10.6 at 12 months (− 23.5 ± 11.8; p < 0.0001), which allowed patients to shift towards normative PCS values observed in non-clinical populations [43].

Clinical Significance of 12-Month Outcomes

The breadth of treatment response was calculated for multiple domains (pain intensity, functional disability, quality of life, sleep quality, walking endurance, mobility and balance, depression, and pain catastrophizing), reflecting how each assessment reached a validated MCID (Table S5) at the 12-month follow-up when compared to baseline. Results showed that on average, the overall patient population improved by 1.0–2.9 times the MCIDs across all measured domains (Fig. 7).

Error bars represent standard errors of the mean. 6MWT 6-Minute Walking Test, BDI Beck Disability Index, EQ-5D-5L EuroQol 5 Dimension 5 Level questionnaire, FAST-SCS fast-acting subperception therapy spinal cord stimulation, ODI Oswestry Disability Index, PCS patient catastrophizing scale, MCID minimal clinically important difference, PSQI Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Inventory, TUG Timed Up-and-Go, VNRS verbal numerical rating scale

The ODI mean change was more than twice the established 10-point MCID cutoff reference value [44–46]. The 6-MWT distance increase was deemed clinically significant according to the 75-m MCID [47]. Although not statistically significant due to the large standard deviation observed at baseline, the mean reduction in TUG duration at 12 months reached twice the MCID of − 3.4 s (established in patients after surgery for lumbar degenerative disc disease [48]). The increase in EQ5D-5L index observed at the 12-month follow-up corresponded to 2.9 times the MCID [41]. Sleep quality improvement (PSQI) was also higher than the established 3-point MCID [49]. With regards to depressive symptoms, the mean reduction in BDI score exceeded the established 5-point MCID [50, 51]. Recent BDI MCIDs have expressed the minimal change as a percentage of reduction from baseline score; our results exceeded these values (approx. 30%), with a 41% decrease in the BDI score at 12 months [52, 53]. Finally, the significant reduction in PCS exceeded the MCID of 6.8 points established in patients with chronic low back pain [54]. The mean PCS score decreased by 58.3 ± 38.6%, and is considered substantially clinically important according to recommendations [51]. The PCS reduction also exceeded the 1.9–13.6 MCID range defined in patients with chronic pain treated with SCS [55, 56].

Global Impression of Change and Qualitative Surveys

Overall, 86.8% of patients (46/53) reported being “much” or “very much” improved (P-GIC) at the 12-month follow-up (Fig. 8a). Similar results were reported from clinicians’ perspectives (C-GIC) (Fig. 8b).

Global impression of change over 12 months after FAST-SCS activation. a P-GIC; b C-GIC. FAST-SCS fast-acting subperception therapy spinal cord stimulation, P-GIC patient-reported global impression of change, C-GIC clinician-reported global impression of change

The optional qualitative survey was sent to all patients from September 2024 to January 2025, when all subjects had completed their 12-month follow-up visit. Results from 34 patients showed that 97.1% (33 patients) found it easy to control their therapy, 85.3% (29 patients) considered that achieving pain relief while in the physician’s clinic gave them confidence that their SCS therapy was working, and 73.5% (25 patients) rarely used their remote control to adjust their neuromodulation settings. All clinicians agreed that fast-acting pain relief helped their overall patient care and SCS therapy assessment, both during the trial period and when patients come back to the clinic for routine or study evaluations (Table 2).

Safety

Thirty-four serious AEs (SAEs) were reported by 11.3% of patients (20/177) during the study over 12 months of follow-up (Table S7). Of these, only one event (pain in a left lower extremity) was assessed as device-related, occurring 2 months after permanent implant; the SAE resolved within 1 week following medication and a hospital stay. The remaining SAEs were deemed unrelated to device or procedure.

Discussion

This study represents the first prospective evaluation of FAST-SCS for the management of chronic low back and/or leg pain. The primary endpoint was achieved, with 78% of patients reporting ≥ 50% pain relief at the 3-month follow-up without any increase in baseline opioid use, underscoring the analgesic efficacy of FAST-SCS and its potential role in opioid-sparing strategies for chronic pain management. Sustained benefits were observed through 12 months following FAST-SCS activation, extending and reinforcing findings from prior retrospective reports [24, 25]. At 12 months, patients demonstrated a mean 5.1-point reduction in VNRS pain scores, an 89% responder rate, and nearly 60% of patients reporting ≥ 80% pain relief, supporting the durability of treatment response over time.

In association with the substantial reduction in pain, patients experienced marked improvements in disability, with mean ODI decreasing by more than twice the established 10-point MCID at 12 months. The 23.7-point reduction in the ODI score in this study exceeds the average improvement reported in a recent meta-analysis of 22 studies evaluating multiple SCS waveforms, which demonstrated a mean 17-point ODI improvement at 12 months [57]. Clinically significant improvements in sleep quality were also observed in our study [49], reflecting functional benefits that frequently co-occur with reduction in chronic pain. In parallel, HRQoL scores measured on the EQ-5D-5L reached levels comparable to normative values in the US general population aged 55–64 [41], further underscoring the broad health benefits associated with FAST-SCS [29].

To our knowledge, this study is the first to prospectively evaluate the impact of subperception SCS on objective measures of patient mobility and functional capabilities. While prior prospective work has assessed mobility outcomes in non-SCS populations—such as the study by Maldaner et al. [58], which demonstrated short-term improvements in TUG and 6MWT following spine surgery—comparative data in SCS treated patients remains limited. In our study, the 6MWT demonstrated a statistically significant improvement compared to baseline, indicating enhanced functional endurance and sustained ambulation capacity. In contrast, the TUG test showed numerical improvement that did not reach statistical significance, which may reflect greater variability and sensitivity to balance and transfer mechanics rather than sustained walking ability. However, while the mean TUG change compared to baseline did not reach statistical significance, an additional analysis (signed rank test) to test the median of the TUG change led to statistical significance. Taken together, these findings suggest that FAST-SCS improved patients’ capacity for prolonged functional activity, with more modest or heterogeneous effects on short duration mobility tasks. These promising results using objective measures need to be further evaluated to confirm and refine the impact of SCS on physical mobility.

Psychosocial outcomes such as pain-related catastrophizing and depression improved substantially in our study. FAST-SCS led to a 7.9-point improvement in BDI score and a 13.3-point reduction in PCS score—both results exceeding their respective MCIDs and demonstrating consistent and beneficial psychosocial outcomes. Interestingly, 80% of patients who reported a clinically significant level of pain catastrophizing at baseline experienced substantial changes in their emotional functioning 12 months after FAST-SCS activation. This level of improvement is consistent with prior reports using Burst SCS [26] and add new psychosocial evidence to a recent meta-analysis that reported 4.7-point (n = 1127) and 12.1-point (n = 1067) pooled improvements in BDI and PCS scores, respectively, at 12 months [59]. Overall, our study contributes to recent efforts made to evaluate SCS therapy via a holistic set of clinical measures [26–28], which may help with a better determination of treatment expectations as well as patient selection criteria.

Our results also represent an encouraging validation of FAST-SCS. This novel subperception therapy may play a significant role in the future, as it offers low energy settings and reduced charging burden—in contrast to previous paresthesia-free paradigms, which had been developed with higher stimulation frequencies. The fast-acting character of this waveform has also been confirmed in our study, with 50% pain relief being achieved less than 3 min on average after activation. This short wash-in duration could be meaningful in clinical practice during trials or reprogramming sessions, as clinicians and patients can immediately assess the effects of the subperception therapy.

While promising, these results should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. This was a single-arm, open-label study without a control group. The absence of blinding may also introduce bias in patient-reported outcomes. The patients in our study were implanted with various types of leads, which could potentially induce a risk of heterogeneity in the study population. A post hoc analysis of the primary endpoint and 12-month improvement in overall pain scores (VNRS) and disability (ODI) did not show statistical differences between patients with surgical paddle leads (n = 13) and percutaneous leads (n = 43), confirming the findings from a recent retrospective analysis with a larger number of patients [25]. Furthermore, any differences attributable to sex were not accounted for in our study design. Previous research has suggested that women are at increased risk of chronic and severe clinical pain [60]. However, no difference in the rate of SCS therapy nor in the outcomes between men and women have yet been identified [61, 62]. Accordingly, we did not expect there to be a difference in our study. Additionally, longer-term follow-up is needed to determine the durability of the therapy [58, 63]. The study is ongoing and longer-term evaluations are being planned up to 5-year follow-up.

Conclusions

This prospective multicenter study provides novel evidence supporting the use of subperception FAST-SCS therapy in patients with chronic low back and/or leg pain. Treatment with FAST-SCS was associated with rapid and durable pain relief, accompanied by clinically meaningful improvements across functional and psychosocial domains through 12 months of follow-up, highlighting the potential for this subperception therapy to deliver multidimensional clinical benefits. Collectively, these findings support FAST-SCS as a therapeutic option capable of addressing both physical and psychosocial aspects of chronic pain.
