Predictors, mediators, effect modifiers and preventive actions regarding secondary school teacher burnout, anxiety and depression—protocol for a systematic scoping review
Authors: Marius König, Jonas P Wallraff, Florian Glenewinkel, Ursula Wild, Thomas C Erren, Philip Lewis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe teaching profession plays a crucial role in society. From educating and forming future generations to fulfilling various administrative tasks and managing expectations and experiences that reach beyond the classroom—teachers face immense demands on their time, energy, and emotional resources. Consequently, they are subject to high work burden. This is reflected in the high prevalence of burnout, anxiety, and depression among teachers. A scoping review of factors associated with these outcomes in teachers is required to inform the further development of preventive occupational medicine strategies. In this scoping review, we aim to (i) identify and (ii) appraise the factors (eg, workplace, environmental, lifestyle, psychological) associated with burnout, anxiety, and depression specific to secondary school teachers and to (iii) synthesise the findings from the perspective of preventive occupational medicine.Methods and analysisThe scoping review will be performed following the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews guidance. A systematic literature search will be conducted in the Medline, Web of Science Core Collection, PsychInfo, and Cochrane Library databases using search terms pertinent to secondary school teachers as the population of interest and depression, anxiety, and burnout as the outcomes of interest. Returned articles from the database search published pre-2017 will be excluded for the following reasons; namely, (a) that current literature will more closely reflect the current demands of the teachers, (b) the timing fits with recent systematic reviews highlighting burnout, anxiety, and depression as major problems among teachers, and (c) it facilitates feasibility of review in terms of the volume of studies. Remaining records will then be deduplicated and screened against predefined eligibility criteria that also add focus on teachers and these outcomes. Relevant data concerning factors associated with burnout, anxiety, and depression in teachers will be extracted and mapped. A narrative appraisal of included studies will be employed that will be specific to the validity of the results regarding factors (exposures, mediators, effect modifiers) that may affect the outcomes of interest (as opposed to more generic appraisal of the entirety of individual articles). Findings will be narratively synthesised from the perspective of preventive occupational medicine.Ethics and disseminationFormal ethical approval is not required as primary data will not be collected in this study. The findings of this study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication, conference presentation, and condensed summaries for key stakeholders and partners in the field (including teachers, schools, and governing bodies).Registration detailsThe protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework on 26.06.2025 and can be identified using the following link: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/BKX56.

Introduction

The teaching profession in society is especially important by playing a role in educating and forming the future generations of adults. Furthermore, teachers represent a significant portion of the working population (eg, ~1.7% in Germany and ~2.2% in the USA).14 Despite the large numbers and the importance of the profession, a survey conducted for the European Commission among European teachers found that some 59% view the teaching profession as ‘unattractive’ or even ‘very unattractive’ and 68% think teacher workloads do not permit a healthy work-life balance. One of the three main challenges they see impacting the appeal of teaching as a career is heavy workload and stress (the other two being inadequate salary and benefits, and inadequate respect, recognition, and appreciation by authorities and the public).5 These results not only suggest extensive dissatisfaction but they are also suggestive of occupational health problems as we know that occupational stress can perturb mental and physical health. Indeed, a 2005 large meta-analysis of 485 studies identified associations between various adverse health outcomes and job satisfaction. The associations were stronger for mental than for physical health, and strongest for burnout, followed by depression and anxiety.6 Given the apparent job dissatisfaction among teachers and the social importance of the teaching profession, and that teachers represent a large portion of the workforce, preventive occupational medicine strategies to protect the health of the teaching workforce are potentially warranted.

The WHO describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon resulting from stress, leading to exhaustion, increased mental distance from the job, and reduced professional efficacy.7 They describe anxiety as excessive feelings of fear or worry when confronted with a specific situation and potentially resulting in avoidance behaviour.8 They describe depression as a sad, empty, low, or irritable mood and a loss of pleasure or interest in activities.9 All three can be disorder classifications and/or be described by symptom severities. A pilot search for literature pertaining to factors that affect burnout, anxiety, and depression in secondary school teachers that may be relevant to preventive occupational medicine identified the following: Teacher workloads appear to have increased compared with earlier years due to administrative tasks and preparation of online content.10 A systematic review from 2018 assessing the prevalence of burnout among secondary school teachers found a prevalence of 28.1% for severe emotional exhaustion, 37.9% for severe depersonalisation and 40.3% for low personal accomplishment.11 Most studies came from Europe (57.9%), followed by the USA (13.3%), China (11.1%), Colombia (8.9%), Australia (4.4%), and Italy (4.4%). Similarly, a systematic review from 2021 reported a large range of potential prevalence (10% –50%) for the outcomes of interest.12 A 2022 scoping review also reported a large variation in the prevalence found for primary and secondary schoolteachers’ stress, burnout, anxiety symptoms, and depression. The median prevalences across all included studies were about 30% (33%, 29%, 26% and 31%, respectively).13 Even at the low end of the ranges, the portion of affected teachers as described in these reviews is substantial. Given the possibility of healthy worker bias in these studies, the prevalences may also be underestimations.

The prevalence statistics above indicate that actions to protect the health of the teaching workforce are warranted. Previous scoping reviews have focused on factors influencing- and interventions regarding- well-being and burnout in teachers and have contributed to the design of our study.14 15 However, they have neither covered the major health sciences literature databases that index occupational medicine studies, nor do they include anxiety and depression as outcomes. Furthermore, they do not map studies according to ancillary data (e.g., differences in associations between predictors and outcomes by, for instance, sociodemographic characteristics) that may be important to preventive occupational medicine. Thus, the proposed systematic scoping review aims to map the extent, range and nature of the literature concerning predictors, mediators, effect modifiers, and prevention strategies in relation to burnout, anxiety, and depression in teachers as a novel angle. As potential added value, review findings may extend beyond preventive occupational medicine given that anxiety disorders are estimated to affect 4% of the global population8 and major depressive disorder is estimated to affect 3.7% of the population (climbing to 5% in adults).9 Furthermore, our review findings will complement the work already done on teacher well-being.

Methods and analysis

The proposed systematic scoping review will be reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guideline.16 This protocol is developed using the PRISMA-Reporting Guideline (Checklist 1) and is registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/BKX56) since 26.06.2025.17 All method steps until synthesis will be performed by at least two researchers independently. The initial screening of databases was completed on 17 June 2025. The planned end date for the review is 31 May 2026.

Eligibility criteria

Evidence sources

We will include primary research observational study full texts that are written in English or German that have been published since 2017. We set the 2017 criterion for several reasons; namely, (a) the teaching profession is regularly changing/updating in terms of digitalisation and globalisation (and the COVID-19 pandemic will likely have shaped the teaching profession in several ways), so the most recent literature is the most pertinent in terms of the preventive occupational medicine perspective and in terms of comparisons with teaching during the COVID-19 pandemic; (b) it fits with publication dates of systematic reviews identifying prevalence of burnout, anxiety and depression as major issues in school teachers; (c) the volume of pertinent research on this topic was identified as extensive in a pilot search and the time restriction of since 2017 allows for a manageable review. Studies that are solely qualitative in nature will also be excluded. Although these have the potential for building hypotheses, we lay our focus on objective assessments in teachers as quantitative findings can be considered toward the foundation for preventive occupational medicine strategies. Reviews, protocols, commentaries, editorials, meeting abstracts, posters, books, or book chapters will not be included, nor will studies that focus only on validation of outcomes assessment tools. Even though the latter may include quantitative data on factors of interest, we reason that data from validation study designs contribute more to hypothesis generating than to developing preventive strategies in occupational medicine.

Participants

Our participants of interest are fully qualified, working, secondary school teachers; however, we will not exclude studies wherein different teacher strata are studied together as long as the mix includes our participants of interest (e.g., primary and secondary school teachers studied side-by-side).

Outcomes

Included full texts must include some measures of association (or mediators or effect modifiers thereof) between quantitative measures (e.g., prevalence, incidence, status, or severity scores) of either burnout, anxiety, and depression (including mental exhaustion, emotional exhaustion, chronic fatigue or cynicism, depersonalisation, detachment from job or sense of ineffectiveness, reduced personal accomplishment) and another factor(s). Comparisons of prevalence or incidence or severity scores of either anxiety, depression, or burnout between different strata of the participants will also be included.

Search strategy

Databases

We will screen four large health and life sciences databases; namely, Medline, Web of Science Core Collection, PsychInfo and Cochrane Library.

Search strings

The search strings for the respective databases including specific search terms are presented in box 1. The search terms are based on the participant and outcome eligibility criteria. We also add terms related to burnout subscales like “emotional exhaustion”, “depersonalisation” and “personal accomplishment” to identify studies that only focus on these aspects of burnout. We do not include “teach*” in the search string because these terms also identify, for instance, “effects of teaching programmes” or “in teaching hospitals” or “teaching X to Y”, etc. “Teacher*” and similar population of interest terms are specified as to be found in article titles. This provides necessary focus on the string and allows the review volume to be manageable. Furthermore, we still identify a large volume of relevant literature with this search string. Using title search only may reduce sensitivity, but we consider the potential volume of missed relevant studies to be low given the need for specific teacher strata to have been studied with regard to the rest of our eligibility criteria. Citation searching should allow capturing potentially remaining articles not picked up by this slightly more restricted search string.

Search String

(“depress*” OR “anxi*” OR “burnout” OR “burn out” OR “mental exhaustion” OR “emotional exhaustion” OR “chronic fatigue” OR “cyni*” OR “depersonalis*” OR “detach*” OR “professional efficacy” OR “job efficacy” OR “personal accomplishment”) AND (“teacher*”[ti] OR “educator*”[ti] OR “tutor*”[ti] OR “schoolteacher*”[ti])

[ti] specifies that these words were searched for in titles only; the search for title words was adapted according to the user interface for each database (e.g., MedLine allows use of the “search term1”[ti] or “search term2”[ti] format as per above; Web of Science uses TI=(“search term1” OR “search term2”).

Screening steps

The studies identified by the search strings will be pooled using Endnote referencing software.18 Studies not in English or German will be immediately excluded. Duplicates will be excluded using the Endnote deduplication tool set to identify duplicates according to their digital object identifiers (DOIs). Studies without DOIs will be compared manually to identify duplicates. Next, titles and abstracts will be screened against the eligibility criteria that have been tailored for title and abstract screening (table 1). The tailored criteria will allow studies for which a reviewer is unsure about to pass through for full text screening (e.g., the abstract mentions ‘teachers’ generally but not ‘secondary school teachers’ specifically). Remaining article full texts will then be screened against the eligibility criteria that have been tailored for more stringent full text screening (table 1). After the title and abstract screening step and after the full text screening step, any disagreements between the two independent reviewers will be resolved by a third reviewer.

Data extraction, mapping, critical appraisal, and synthesis

Data extraction will be carried out on remaining studies and will include information on bibliographical data, population, study characteristics (design, assessment tools, analyses), and results. Extraction fields are shown in box 2. Study authors will be contacted in the event of unclear or inaccessible data. If they do not respond before the end of data extraction, the study (or at least the relevant part of the study) will be excluded. Mapping will consist of grouping studies by the factors they have studied in associations with the outcomes of interest (studies will inevitably appear in multiple groups). This mapping will allow not only an overview of how many studies have studied a factor of interest in terms of association with an outcome of interest but also allow quick identification of the studies of interest pertaining to specific factor. In order to guide the synthesis and interpretations of findings, this review employs narrative study appraisals but specific to the validity of the results regarding the factors (exposures, mediators, effect modifiers) that may affect the outcomes as opposed to more generic appraisal of the entirety of individual studies. This additional critical appraisal will add value to the scoping review as stakeholders will wish to know what is credible, to what extent, and why, in order to inform practice. Importantly, studies will not be excluded based on quality assessment.

Stakeholder involvement

Following the first draft of the synthesis, we shall independently ask a convenience sample of occupational medicine specialists and secondary school teachers in our locality for their perspectives on our literature synthesis and incorporate important feedback that is deemed appropriate and relevant by the authorship team.
