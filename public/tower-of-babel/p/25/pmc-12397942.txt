Protocol for dual-optical mapping of voltage and calcium sensors in human pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes
Authors: Faisal J Alibhai, Arya Masoumi, Ho-June Kim, Nathan Kim, Wesley Kim, Juliana Gomez-Garcia, Peter Lee, Michael A Laflamme
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummarySimultaneous acquisition of fluorescent voltage and intracellular calcium signals requires spectrally distinct indicators and a high-speed imaging setup. Here, we present a protocol for the optical mapping of these key physiological parameters in human pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocyte (hPSC-CM) monolayers. We describe steps for using spectrally compatible voltage and calcium sensors involving fluorescent dyes and genetically encoded sensors. We also detail procedures for building a driver capable of controlling light-emitting diodes (LEDs) in synchrony with a high-speed camera during image acquisition.Subject areas: Health Sciences, Molecular/Chemical Probes, Stem Cells

Before you begin

This protocol is intended for laboratories interested in acquiring optical voltage and intracellular calcium signals from monolayers formed using human pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hPSC-CMs). Such work obviously needs to be preceded by the differentiation of hPSCs to hPSC-CMs. Several protocols to generate cardiomyocytes have been previously described elsewhere, and these include the small molecule1 and growth factor-based cardiac differentiation protocols.2 Regardless of the differentiation method, hPSC-CMs are amenable to subsequent monolayer formation and optical mapping for assessment of cell electrophysiology. For optical mapping, an imaging rig comprised of three key modules should be used: 1) a camera capable of imaging at high frame rates (i.e. >400 frames-per-second; fps), 2) high-powered LEDs and an LED driver capable of receiving frame exposure signals from the camera, and 3) an environmental chamber capable of maintaining the cells at 37°C and >85% humidity (Figures 1 and 2). Dual voltage and calcium optical mapping using a single camera is achieved using multi-bandpass emission filters (Figure 3A) and synchronizing the light sources to the camera frame exposure signal (Figure 3B).3,4,5 First, hPSC-CMs are seeded into 35 mm dishes and cultured to form a continuous monolayer. Following monolayer formation, cells are stained using a fluorescent voltage dye such as FluoVolt or BeRST1 and a fluorescent calcium indicator such as the Calbryte acetoxymethyl (AM) dyes. Alternatively, one or both of these small-molecule dyes can be replaced by engineering hPSC-CMs to express a genetically encoded voltage sensor (GEVS), such as ASAP1,6 or a genetically encoded calcium indicator (GECI), such as GCaMP.7,8 Following image acquisition, fluorescent data is extracted and interpolated using the custom MATLAB scripts provided. In total, we validated four different combinations using the protocol below: Combo 1: FluoVolt dye and Calbryte 630 AM dye, Combo 2: the GEVS ASAP16 and Calbryte 630 AM dye, Combo 3: BeRST1 dye9 and Calbryte 520 AM dye, and Combo 4: BeRST1 dye and the GECI jGCaMP8f.8

Overall view of the dual voltage and calcium optical mapping system

Monolayers plated on a standard 35 mm dish were imaged from below through a quartz glass window. The chamber was temperature-controlled, and high humidity was maintained with the integrated heated water baths.

Close-up view of the chamber and imaging system

(A) Chamber: Graphite electrodes were used to electrically stimulate cardiomyocytes. An XYZ stage was used to position the electrode pair to the desired location. Imaging system: Two pairs of excitation light sources were used to excite voltage and calcium sensors. The monolayer is excited at an oblique angle, and fluorescence emission from both sensors is passed through a multi-bandpass emission filter.

(B) A schematic of the imaging system.

(C) A schematic of the excitation light source.

Method for dual voltage and calcium optical mapping using a single camera

(A) Filter sets for 4 combinations of voltage and calcium sensors. The multi-bandpass filters used comprised two transmission bands. Fluorescence from sensors excited with light through the lower- or higher-wavelength excitation filter (blue or red) was collected through the multi-bandpass filter’s lower- or higher-wavelength band, respectively.

(B) Modes 1 & 2: Only one of the two sensors was excited in these modes. These modes can be used to optimize the dye loading protocols and determine the appropriate excitation light powers for both sensors. Mode 3: Each sensor was excited in an alternating fashion at high speed. Interpolation was used to estimate the fluorescence signal from a sensor during frame exposures when it was not excited.

Construction of the LED driver

Timing: 2 h

Detailed circuit schematic of the excitation light driver synchronized with the high-speed camera

The camera frame exposure signal was used as the master clock during free-run mode, ensuring maximum frame rates. Excitation light powers were adjusted by hand with panel-mount potentiometers and synchronized to the camera frame exposure signal using a J-K flip-flop.

Forming hPSC-CM monolayers for optical mapping

Note: When harvesting hPSC-CMs for optical mapping from a 2D adherent culture, 0.125% Trypsin-EDTA supplemented with 10 U/mL DNase followed by inactivation with defined trypsin inhibitor can be used. CRITICAL: The optimal cell seeding density depends on several factors, including the differentiation protocol used, the age and size of the cells, and whether the cells were previously cultured in 3D or 2D formats. Optimal density must be determined empirically. Cell densities are based on viable cells/mL.

CRITICAL: When adapting cells from 3D to 2D, an extended culture duration could be required to ensure adequate monolayer formation. In this case, cells can first be plated in a separate culture vessel and maintained for ∼1 week. Following adaptation to 2D, the cells can be dissociated and seeded into the optical mapping culture dish.

Note: At a minimum, technical duplicates should be seeded for each independent biological replicate.

Note: The cells should have a high confluency with little space between adjacent cells. Poor seeding efficiency or low seeding density can limit monolayer formation.

5.Maintain cells for 3–4 days, changing media every other day using 3 mL of RPMI-B27 media.

Maintain cells for 3–4 days, changing media every other day using 3 mL of RPMI-B27 media.

CRITICAL: Care should be taken during media changes so as not to damage the monolayer.

Note: After 3–4 days of culture, the monolayer should be fully formed with no space between cells and beating in unison. Dyssynchronous beating is a sign of rotors or isolated/uncoupled patches of cardiomyocytes.

Note: A glass coverslip (e.g. 18 mm) can be used instead of a 35 mm dish to reduce the number of cells required for optical mapping. The optimal cell density can differ for coverslips (e.g. 0.5 × 105–1 × 105 cells/cm2).

Prepare the Tyrode’s solution and warm to 37°C in a water bath.

Expected outcomes

The protocol described here provides robust methods for the simultaneous assessment of optical action potentials and calcium transients in hPSC-CM monolayers. Traces were acquired from a region of interest in each monolayer under conditions of both spontaneous beating and electrical pacing. To demonstrate the fast response time of the LEDs in our system, the camera was also operated at 1000 fps (Figure 6). Transgenic or pharmacologically treated hPSC-CMs can easily be incorporated into this system, enabling the examination of hPSC-CM electrophysiological properties in response to a diverse range of stimuli.

Limitations

Compared to 3D construct preparations, such as engineered heart tissue, hPSC-CM monolayers do not show the same degree of long-term stability. For example, cardiac monolayers can detach from the culture surface, altering the monolayer’s functional properties and introducing motion artifacts. Careful attention should therefore be paid to the quality of attachment throughout the culture period. Monolayers showing a significant area of detachment should be discarded or enzymatically dispersed and replated prior to optical mapping. Rotors may also be observed during optical mapping of hPSC-CM monolayers (Methods video S5), particularly with preparations having lower cardiomyocyte purity. In our experience, rotor behavior is challenging to overcome with electrical stimulation. Lastly, in our experience, hPSC-CM monolayers should generally be stimulated at lower frequencies in the range of 0.5-3 Hz. In our hands, monolayers in 35 mm dishes exhibit rotor formation when subjected to point stimulation rates >3 Hz, thus limiting the assessment of normal wave propagation and monolayer voltage/calcium properties. This behavior can vary depending on the cardiomyocyte differentiation protocol and cell line used.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Rotors present during recording. Rotors typically arise due to local heterogeneities in the monolayer, creating substrates for re-entry.

Potential solution

•Rotors can be minimized by ensuring that the purity of hPSC-CMs being seeded as monolayers is >90% (based on flow cytometry for cardiac troponin T or another cardiomyocyte marker) and that the cells have been fully dispersed into single cells prior to seeding.

Rotors can be minimized by ensuring that the purity of hPSC-CMs being seeded as monolayers is >90% (based on flow cytometry for cardiac troponin T or another cardiomyocyte marker) and that the cells have been fully dispersed into single cells prior to seeding.

•In our experience, incomplete dissociation prior to seeding will lead to patches of cardiomyocytes with different densities within the monolayer. To prevent this, ensure that the hPSC-CMs are dissociated into single cells or small 2–3 cell clusters.

In our experience, incomplete dissociation prior to seeding will lead to patches of cardiomyocytes with different densities within the monolayer. To prevent this, ensure that the hPSC-CMs are dissociated into single cells or small 2–3 cell clusters.

•The use of an 18 or 22 mm coverslip reduces the likelihood of rotors due to the lower surface area compared to the 35 mm dish.

The use of an 18 or 22 mm coverslip reduces the likelihood of rotors due to the lower surface area compared to the 35 mm dish.

Problem 2

Detachment of the monolayer from the culture surface. Detachment can occur due to several factors, including an inappropriately high hPSC-CM density or poor matrix coating (step 2). Detachment is more common on glass coverslips than on tissue culture plastic.

Potential solution

•Poor dissociation is a common cause of inaccurate cell counts, leading to incorrect cell densities. Ensure that the dissociation is adequate and that the cell density seeded is adjusted for cell viability.

Poor dissociation is a common cause of inaccurate cell counts, leading to incorrect cell densities. Ensure that the dissociation is adequate and that the cell density seeded is adjusted for cell viability.

•To prevent premature gelling of the Matrigel, ensure that the Matrigel is thawed on ice overnight and that the DMEM/F12 used for Matrigel dilution is cold.

To prevent premature gelling of the Matrigel, ensure that the Matrigel is thawed on ice overnight and that the DMEM/F12 used for Matrigel dilution is cold.

•In rare instances, experimenting with other matrices may be required to ensure stable monolayer formation. In our experience, Laminin-521 (BioLamina; LN521-02) is a suitable alternative for seeding hPSC-CMs as a monolayer.

In rare instances, experimenting with other matrices may be required to ensure stable monolayer formation. In our experience, Laminin-521 (BioLamina; LN521-02) is a suitable alternative for seeding hPSC-CMs as a monolayer.

Problem 3

Poor staining of calcium dyes. Although we have not experienced poor loading of hPSC-CMs with the calcium dyes described here, different cell lines may exhibit different staining behaviors.

Potential solution

•The addition of Pluronic F-127 can help increase the solubility of the Calbryte AM dyes and staining intensity.

The addition of Pluronic F-127 can help increase the solubility of the Calbryte AM dyes and staining intensity.

•Alternatively, probenecid can be used to reduce dye extrusion and thus improve dye retention.

Alternatively, probenecid can be used to reduce dye extrusion and thus improve dye retention.

Problem 4

Phototoxicity resulting from intense illumination can impact normal cell function in hPSC-CM monolayers exposed for an extended duration.
