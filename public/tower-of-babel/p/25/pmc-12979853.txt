Green synthesis and enhanced photocatalytic activity of ZnSe nanoparticles capped with Artemisia herba-alba and calligonum plants extracts
Authors: Ahlam F Alshammari, Sabri Ouni, Mohamed Bouzidi, Abdullah S Alshammari, Elham A Alzahrani, Hanan Alismail, Hana Mohammed Almarri, Noureddine Chaaben, Dalaver H Anjum, Mohammad Khaled Shakfa, Nadeem A Khan, Bouzid Gassoumi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study reports, for the first time, the green synthesis of zinc selenide (ZnSe) nanoparticles (NPs) capped with Artemisia herba-alba and Calligonum extracts, benchmarked against conventional L-cysteine-capped ZnSe. Using plant extracts as natural capping agents provides an eco-friendly strategy to tailor the surface chemistry and photocatalytic behavior of ZnSe NPs. The NPs were synthesized hydrothermally and characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), UV–Vis spectroscopy, and photoluminescence (PL), and then evaluated for methylene blue (MB) degradation under UV irradiation. XRD revealed mixed cubic and wurtzite phases with crystallite sizes ranging from 5.6 to 7.7 nm, while the PL analysis suggested more effective charge separation in plant-extract-capped ZnSe. Photocatalytic tests demonstrated that Calligonum-capped ZnSe achieved ~ 40% MB degradation after 180 min, outperforming Artemisia-capped ZnSe (28%) and showing comparable performance to L-cysteine-capped ZnSe (38%). Kinetic analysis further revealed that Artemisia-capped ZnSe exhibited the highest rate constant, indicating superior intrinsic photocatalytic activity. The enhanced performance of plant-capped ZnSe was attributed to phytochemical-induced defect states that promote charge separation and reactive oxygen species generation. These findings establish Artemisia and Calligonum extracts as sustainable capping agents, offering a low-cost, environmentally friendly route for designing ZnSe nanocatalysts with significant potential in wastewater treatment and environmental remediation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39998-z.Keywords: Green synthesis; Biogenic nanoparticles, nano-catalysts; Plant extract; Photocatalysis; Methylene blue, zinc selenide (ZnSe)Subject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Many industries, including plastics, paper, textiles, and food production, use large quantities of water and organic dyes, either soluble or pigmentary, to color their products. These synthetic dyes are toxic and cause water coloration1. Even with fixing agents, certain colors may bond less well than others. The outcome is harmful, colored effluent that must be treated before being released. Regulations controlling wastewater discharge are becoming stricter, pushing companies to carefully treat their effluents. Furthermore, the majority of the synthetic dyes are non-biodegradable, posing a health risk and causing environmental problems2. It is therefore critical to minimize pollution from these dyes by establishing an adequate treatment system that includes effective decolorization units. Among the available promising procedures, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have emerged as one of the most effective methods for dye degradation3. Some AOPs, such as ozonation, have advanced to an industrial scale, while others are still at the research stage. These processes enable the degradation of organic pollutants by producing oxidizing and reactive radical species that can break down a wide range of molecules at ambient temperature and atmospheric pressure, potentially leading to the complete mineralization of pollutants. Heterogeneous photocatalysis is one of the most widely researched, ecologically friendly, and effective methods for degrading various non-biodegradable contaminants with solar energy4. In this regard, several semiconductor-based nanoparticle (NP) systems are proposed as effective nanoscale absorbers of solar energy, producing non-selective radical species required for the breakdown of organic compounds5. Zinc selenide (ZnSe) has received considerable interest among compound semiconductors because of its low cost, abundance, high stability, and strong optical absorption, especially in the visible range6. Several techniques have been established to synthesize ZnSe nanoparticles (NPs) with controlled size and shape, including electrochemical7, sol-gel8, chemical deposition9, and hydrothermal10. These approaches have enabled the production of monodisperse nanocrystals with excellent optical properties and a variety of compositions and morphologies. The hydrothermal technique, which utilizes water as a solvent, is particularly attractive due to its simplicity, environmental friendliness11, and the ability to yield high-purity, well-dispersed NPs12. This method is valued for its reliability, simplicity, and relatively low energy requirement. However, regulating NP size during hydrothermal synthesis necessitates avoiding aggregation and Ostwald ripening13. To overcome this difficulty, ligands (capping agents) are employed to achieve the desired nanoscale and prevent aggregation14. These ligands are adsorbed at the surface of NPs and consist of a polar anchoring group and either a polar hydrocarbon chain or a charged group. The functional tails of the ligands ensure dispersion in the liquid medium and prevent NP growth and aggregation, thereby enhancing optoelectronic properties and improving photocatalytic dye-degradation performance15–18.

The synthesis of NPs is commonly performed using artificial ligands such as L-cysteine, thioglycolic acid (TGA), and mercaptosuccinic acid (MSA)16,17. However, these ligands are often derived from complex-based chemicals and may pose environmental and health risks. To overcome this problem, the use of biological entities such as plant and algal extracts20–26is regarded as a novel, easy, rapid, inexpensive, and ecological alternative. The green-chemistry synthesis methods for producing ZnSe using plant extracts are currently gaining popularity27–30. Such methods offer several benefits, including non-toxicity and safe handling, as well as the ability to produce large quantities of NPs and act as both stabilizing and reducing agents of NPs31–34. Plant material can be prepared in various ways, including infusions, sometimes referred to as teas, leaf and fruit, or, more frequently, the creation of plant extracts that can be made from any part of the plant35–38. Each preparation method is designed to extract a specific group of molecules in order to assess their physicochemical properties, such as solubility and thermostability39–42.

Based on the above discussion, this work presents a comparative study of ZnSe colloidal nanocatalysts synthesized using L-cysteine as a stabilized ligand (ZnSe-P1) and for the first time, to the best of our knowledge, using plant extracts from Artemisia herba-alba (ZnSe-P2) and Calligonum (ZnSe-P3). Plant extracts were selected as green reducing/stabilizing agents because they are rich in phytochemicals (phenolic acids, flavonoids, terpenoids, sugars, alkaloids, and proteins) that can both reduce metal ions and cap the forming NPs, influencing nucleation, growth, size, and surface chemistry. Artemisia herba-alba contains high levels of flavonoids, phenolic compounds, and terpenoids (including artemisinin derivatives), which provide antioxidant/reducing capacity and can act as capping ligands. Calligonum likewise contains abundant phenolics and terpenoids that have been reported to contribute to reducing and stabilizing activity in green syntheses. On this basis, we expect these extracts to mediate NP formation and modify surface chemistry, thereby affecting catalytic or photocatalytic behavior. The plants mentioned above were collected from the Hail region in northern Saudi Arabia. The synthesized nanocatalysts were thoroughly characterized and employed in the photodegradation of methylene blue (MB), used as a model pollutant to evaluate their photocatalytic activity.

Experimental section

Materials

Analytical-grade chemicals were utilized as received without any further purification. Zinc (II) acetate dihydrate (Sigma-Aldrich, Zn [CH3COO]2.2H2O ≥ 98%), selenium dioxide (Sigma-Aldrich, SeO2 ≥ 98%), Sodium hydroxide (NaOH ≥ 98%), sodium borohydride (Sigma-Aldrich, NaBH4, 98%), L-cysteine (Sigma-Aldrich, HSCH2CH(NH2)CO2H ≥ 97%, MW = 121.16 g/mol), plant extracts (Artemisia, and Calligonum) and ultrapure water, produced by a Millipore purification unit, was utilized in all aqueous solutions (resistivity ~ 18 MΩ).

Plant extraction

Artemisia herba-alba and Calligonum plants were collected from the Hail region in Saudi Arabia. The plant species were identified by a specialist at the Department of Biology of the University of Hail, Saudi Arabia. Voucher specimens (Artemisia herba-alba Asso AN 005; Calligonum comosum L’Hér. AN 006) were also deposited at the Department of Biology. The institutional, national, and international guidelines were followed in all plants studies including the collection of the plant materials. Additionally, no endangered plant species were involved in the current study and the declared IUCN Policy was followed in all stages of the study. After being cleaned with deionized water, the selected leaves from the used plants were left to dry at room temperature before being crushed into a powder. 200 mL of distilled water was mixed with 11.5 g of each plant-extract powder, and after 2 h of boiling at 100 °C, the mixtures were filtered at ambient temperature. The filtered aqueous solution constitutes the extract, used to fabricate the ZnSe NPs of the present study.

Preparation of L-cysteine-capped ZnSe nanocrystals

An aqueous solution containing an organic capping agent (L-cysteine) was used to develop ZnSe nanocrystals (Fig. 1). These nanocrystals were produced using a technique reported in a prior work on II–VI semiconductor nanocrystals43,44. ZnSe NPs were obtained from a [Zn2+]/[Se2+]/[L-cysteine] precursor ratio of 1/0.5/3. In short, in a three-necked flask, we prepared a solution containing Zn(Ac)2.2H2O (1.32 g) and 1.09 g of the capping agent (L-cysteine) in 100 mL of distilled water at room temperature while stirring continuously for 15 min under nitrogen bubbling. Drop by drop, NaOH (1 M) was added to the resultant mixture until the pH value of the solution reached 12 and the mixture became clear and transparent while stirring continuously for 30 min. Another aqueous solution of Na2SeO3 was prepared by mixing SeO2 (3 mmol) and NaOH (6 mmol) in 100 mL of distilled water at room temperature, with constant stirring for 30 min. An amount of NaBH4 (30 mmol) was added then to obtain Se2− molarity. After that, this solution was injected at room temperature into the solution containing Zn-L-cysteine complexes under stirring. The mixture was then heated to 100 °C under N2 flow and was continuously refluxed for 2 h. The mixed solution was rapidly injected into the flask containing the stock solution using a syringe. The flask was then allowed to cool once the desired temperature was reached. Artemisia herba-alba- and Calligonum-capped ZnSe NPs were synthesized under the same hydrothermal temperature and reaction time conditions as those employed for the L-cysteine-capped ZnSe nanoparticles. The only modification was to replace L-cysteine with 10 mL of the corresponding plant extract, which was added to the zinc precursor solution at the initial stage of the synthesis, thereby enabling a direct and meaningful comparison of the effects of different capping agents.

Schematic illustration of ZnSe NPs synthesis.

Preparation of green ZnSe nanocrystals using plant extract

ZnSe nano-catalysts were prepared in detail utilizing the colloidal wet chemical technique, as reported elsewhere20. Artemisia herba-alba and Calligonum extracts were used as a reducing agent in the environmentally friendly synthesis of the ZnSe NPs (Fig. 1). An aqueous plant extract was combined with Zn[OOCCH3]2·2H2O solution in the standard synthesis procedure for plant-capped ZnSe NPs. After that, the mixture was stirred and degassed under N2 for 30 min. An aqueous solution of Na2SeO3 was prepared utilizing SeO2, NaOH solution, and a certain quantity of NaBH4 was added to obtain the Se2− precursor. After that, the solution was injected into the flask containing the stock solution using a syringe; the mixture was heated to 100 °C under N2 flow and continuously refluxed. As soon as the necessary temperature was reached, the flask was ultimately cooled to stop the creation of the nanocrystals.

Nanocatalysts characterization

ZnSe NPs were characterized using several methods. A PANalytical X’ Pert Pro diffractometer equipped with a CuKα radiation source (λ = 1.542 Å) was used to perform XRD measurements. Using a Perkin Elmer spectrophotometer (version 5.3), the transmission-mode Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum of ZnSe samples was obtained at room temperature in the 400–4000 cm− 1 spectral region. A double aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope (TEM) of the model Titan Themis Z from Thermo Fisher Scientific (TFS), with an accelerating voltage of 300 kV, was used to obtain morphological images of the samples. The bright-field TEM (BF-TEM) mode was used to acquire images at different magnifications to investigate the sample morphology. The images were recorded using a scintillator-based complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) detector, model Ceta, from TFS. TEM specimens were prepared by placing a drop of the nanocrystal solution on carbon-coated copper grids and allowing the solvent to evaporate. At least 100 randomly chosen nanocrystals were measured using an image-processing application (ImageJ, version 1.50) to determine their size and size-distribution data from TEM images. At room temperature, absorbance spectra in the 200–800 nm range were obtained using a SPECORD 210 Plus spectrophotometer and a quartz cuvette. Photoluminescence (PL) measurements were performed using a 325-nm He-Cd laser source. The PL emission was recorded using a Hamamatsu photomultiplier tube (model R-928) coupled to a SpectraPro 2500 spectrometer.

Photocatalytic degradation experiments

The photocatalytic activity of the synthesized ZnSe NPs was examined by means of the degradation of the MB dye pollutant. The identical technique has been used in earlier studies45. A water purification experiment was conducted on water samples contaminated with MB at a concentration of 2 × 10− 5 M and at a neutral pH value. A quantity of 10 mg of ZnSe from each sample was added to 10 mL of polluted water. Results and discussion

NPs characterization

Structural and morphological studies

The X-ray diffraction patterns of ZnSe nanocrystals synthesized with various capping agents are displayed in Fig. 2. For phase identification, reference data are collected from the powder diffraction standards JCPDS 05–0522 and 15–0105 for ZnSe, which correspond to the zinc-blende (ZB) and wurtzite (WZ) structures, respectively47. Firstly, Fig. 2 shows that the XRD peaks are broadened due to the nanoscale size of the synthesized samples. The XRD pattern of ZnSe-P1 exhibits diffraction peaks at 2θ = 29.50°, 41.18°, 45.2° and 52.58°. These peaks are attributed respectively to the diffraction planes (101), (102), (110), and (112) of the hexagonal structure. The peaks at 43.05° and 55.92 belong to the (202) and (222) crystal planes of the zinc-blende ZnSe structure. Therefore, the presence of the cubic phase in the sample is possible, as seen from Fig. 2, although its contribution is small48. Additionally, the higher intensity of the (101) peak indicates preferred growth along this direction and confirms the dominance of the hexagonal structure in the ZnSe-P1 sample48. For ZnSe-P2, several diffraction peaks are present at 27.64°, 34.38°, 36.22°, 45.66, 47.5° and 53.72 and are assigned to the crystal planes (002), (101), (102), (110), (103) and (112) of the hexagonal structure according to the standard JCPDS sheet (15–0105) of the hexagonal ZnSe structure. However, the peaks associated with the crystallographic planes (200) and (222) around 31.7° and 56.54°, respectively, indicate the presence of the cubic ZnSe phase in the sample. Nevertheless, the higher intensity of the peak (002) in comparison with other diffraction peaks suggests that the growth is preferred along this direction and confirms that the hexagonal structure is also the most dominant in the ZnSe-P2 sample. The same can also be said for the green-synthesized ZnSe-P3; the higher intensity of the (002) peak suggests that the structure is virtually hexagonal, with a small fraction of cubic structures, as evidenced by the presence of the planes (200) and (222). The disruption of lattice constants resulting from crystal defects such as dislocations is measured by the lattice strain, ε53. Due to size restriction, the lattice expansion or contraction in the nanocrystals is the primary cause of the lattice strain. Accordingly, the number of defects in the nanocrystal size is described by SF and δ. Because of their small dimensions, the nanocrystals are less organized, according to the dislocation density value54. Since tiny particles seek to stabilize their greater surface energy, they are most likely to form dislocations. ZnSe-P1 has the highest dislocation density compared to the green-synthesized ZnSe NPs; it decreases from 31.33 × 1015 to 16.79 × 1015 m− 2 when the crystallite size, D, increases from 5.65 to 7.72 nm. Strong, consistent, and well-organized bonds are usually formed between thiol ligands and ZnSe surfaces. This, in turn, guarantees a more crystalline structure with fewer flaws and lessens lattice distortions. Nevertheless, the range of biomolecules (proteins, flavonoids, polyphenols, etc.) found in green plant extracts may interact unevenly with ZnSe, leading to uneven surface coverage and increased stacking faults, thereby straining the crystal lattice.

TEM is used to examine the size distribution and morphological properties of the ZnSe NPs. The TEM analysis reveals that the ZnSe NPs synthesized with L-cysteine (Fig. 3(a)) exhibit a predominantly spherical shape and a relatively homogeneous size distribution. The particle size of around 8.1 ± 0.5 nm indicates that the amino acid ligand effectively controls the nucleation and growth processes of the NPs55. The effectiveness of L-cysteine in stabilizing the nanostructures is demonstrated by the well-dispersed and minimally agglomerated surfaces of these NPs50. In contrast, ZnSe NPs synthesized with an extract from Artemisia herba-alba (Fig. 3(b)) show slightly asymmetrical spherical particles with an average size of about 9.3 ± 0.5 nm. The phytochemicals in the extract, such as flavonoids and phenolic compounds, probably assisted in reducing the precursors of zinc and selenium, as well as capping the produced nanocrystals56. The complex organic matrix that covers the NPs, and probably the uneven surface coverage of the extract components on the particles, may be the origin of the notable mild aggregation observed in the TEM images. Similarly, the ZnSe NPs synthesized with Calligonum extract (Fig. 3(c)) display a mean size of around 11.1 ± 0.5 nm and vary in shape, primarily round to oval. Tannins and polysaccharides, two biomolecules included in Calligonum extract, appear to affect the kinetics of particle formation, resulting in a slightly wider range of shapes and sizes57. TEM studies also revealed moderate clustering, indicating that the natural extract components offer moderate stability with some particle–particle contact. Overall, the TEM analysis is in good agreement with the XRD results and confirms the successful synthesis of nanosized ZnSe particles using L-cysteine and plant extracts, with particle sizes and morphologies strongly influenced by the nature of the capping agents. However, it should be noted that the average crystallite sizes calculated from XRD (5.6–7.7 nm, Table S1) are smaller than the particle sizes observed by TEM (8.1–11.1 nm). This apparent discrepancy is expected, since XRD provides the coherent crystallite domain size, which reflects the crystalline core, whereas TEM measures the overall particle diameter, including surface layers and any amorphous or polycrystalline shells. Despite the ~ 30–40% size difference, both methods consistently confirm the nanoscale regime and the same size trend (P1 < P2 < P3). Thus, the TEM and XRD results are considered to be in qualitative agreement. Moreover, although the plant extracts affect NP size uniformity and dispersion differently than the traditional ligand L-cysteine, the results highlight the potential of Artemisia herba-alba and Calligonum extracts as environmentally friendly and sustainable substitutes for NP production.

FTIR spectroscopy is used to confirm the presence of various functional groups in the NP samples. Figure 4 shows the FTIR spectra of the P1, P2, and P3-capped ZnSe NPs. The spectra of L-cysteine and plant extract-capped ZnSe NPs revealed the presence of the used capping agents in the NP samples. The spectrum of the ZnSe-P1 nanocrystal sample revealed the absence of the characteristic thiol band between 2560 and 2590 cm− 1, indicating the breakdown of the S-H liaison and the fixation of thiol molecules on the surface of ZnSe-P1 nanocrystals. This occurs through interactions between sulfur atoms of the ligand molecules and the zinc atoms on the NP surface43. The broad absorption band around 3389 cm− 1 corresponds to the elongation vibration of L-cysteine ligand (O-H) groups58. The bands at 1050 and 2975 cm− 1 are possibly associated with the stretching vibrations of the C-O and C-C bonds of thiol alkyl chains, respectively. In addition, two peaks at 1400 and 1595 cm− 1 correspond to the COOH group (1595 cm− 1 for symmetrical COO, 1400 cm− 1 for symmetrical COO), confirming the link between L-cysteine and Zn sites via thiol groups59. For the two plant extract-capped NP samples, a broad absorption band is observed around 3300 cm− 1 corresponding to hydrogen-bonded O–H stretching in phenolic compounds, and flavonoids present in the plant extract60. The bands near 1050, 1410, and 2980 cm− 1 are possibly associated with the stretching vibrations of C-O, COO−, and C-C in the plant chains, respectively61. The absorption band near 845 cm− 1 in ZnSe-P3 could be ascribed to vibrations of C-N stretching belonging to aromatic and aliphatic amines61. Additionally, the interaction between these oxygen- and nitrogen-containing phytochemicals and ZnSe is mainly attributed to the coordination of Zn²⁺ ions with hydroxyl (–OH) and carboxylate (–COO⁻) groups, leading to the formation of Zn–O bonds at the NP surface. This metal–ligand interaction confirms the chelation of surface Zn atoms by polyphenols, flavonoids, and tannins, effectively passivating surface defects and stabilizing the NPs62. Such coordination also supports partial electronic coupling between the organic shell and the inorganic ZnSe core, which can influence band-edge states and photocatalytic response. The FTIR analysis demonstrates that the NPs synthesized via green methods are capped with the main phytoconstituents of plant extracts used for the ZnSe fabrication.

Optical study

The optical properties of the synthesized ZnSe NPs are investigated using UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy in the 200–800 nm range, as illustrated in Fig. 5, to examine their optical characteristics. All samples show a distinct blue shift in the absorption edge when compared to the bulk ZnSe bandgap (~ 460 nm), confirming the formation of nanocrystalline structures63. The samples also exhibit strong absorption in the ultraviolet region, with a sharp decline in absorbance toward the visible range. The absorption edges are observed from Fig. 5 at approximately 329 nm for ZnSe-P1, 359 nm for ZnSe-P2, and 363 nm for ZnSe-P3, indicating quantum confinement effects relative to bulk ZnSe (band gap ≈ 2.7 eV)63. In accordance with the TEM investigation, the observed blue shift in relation to the bulk is nearly indicative of nanoscale dimensions. These values indicate that the energy gap of these NPs exhibits a blue shift compared to the bulk ZnSe (Eg = 2.7 eV), which is mainly due to the NPs’ small size, confirming quantum confinement effects (QCE)65. The L-cysteine-capped NPs (ZnSe-P1) showed the strongest blue shift, indicating lower crystallite sizes because of the effective coordination and stability provided by the thiol and amine functional groups of L-cysteine. In contrast, the Artemisia herba-alba and Calligonum-capped ZnSe NPs showed slightly red-shifted absorption edges in comparison with the sample prepared using the L-cysteine capping agent. This can be associated with the size of the NPs, as the green-synthesized particles are slightly bigger, as well as with the impact of the organic phytochemicals present in the plant extracts on the surface states of the NPs66. Notably, the Calligonum-capped ZnSe NPs (ZnSe-P3) showed a more structured absorption profile, possibly due to the presence of flavonoids, phenolics, and polysaccharides, which could lead to differential surface passivation and localized electronic transitions67. The Artemisia herba-alba-capped ZnSe NPs exhibited an intermediate behavior, with moderate absorbance in the visible range that may reflect the contribution of phenolic and flavonoid compounds interacting with the NP surface. Overall, the UV-Vis spectra show that ZnSe NPs were successfully formed using both amino acid-assisted and plant-mediated synthesis pathways, with distinct optical characteristics that depend on surface chemistry and size.

The PL spectra of ZnSe NPs are measured to determine how surface chemistry and quantum confinement affect their optical properties. The PL spectra are recorded over 350–640 nm, and the results are shown in Fig. 6(a). Significant differences are observed in the PL features between L-cysteine-capped NPs and those obtained from plant extracts (Artemisia herba-alba and Calligonum), highlighting the impact of changes in particle size, crystallinity, and surface passivation. The PL peaks are deconvoluted to analyze the various recombination processes in each sample. ZnSe NPs capped with L-cysteine (P1) exhibit dual strong emission peaks in their PL spectra responsible for the blue luminescence, which are centered at 391 and 406 nm, and their corresponding energies are 3.17 and 3.05 eV, respectively. The first emission originates from the near-band-edge (NBE) emission of ZnSe, while the second originates from selenium vacancies VSe48. This notable blue shift relative to the bulk ZnSe bandgap (~ 2.7 eV) suggests strong quantum confinement, consistent with the growth of ultrasmall NPs63. The occurrence of a relatively narrower PL peak in this sample is associated with reduced defect states. It indicates that L-cysteine effectively passivates the surface and lowers the defect-related radiative recombination pathways68. Deconvoluted PL spectrum of ZnSe NPs developed with Artemisia herba-alba extract as a capping and reducing agent (ZnSe-P2) exhibits four PL emission peaks at 404, 416, 456, and 496 nm. The NBE emission is responsible for the peak at 404 nm (~ 3.07 eV). This peak results from photogenerated electrons in the conduction band directly recombining with valence band holes69. Excitonic recombination processes are usually responsible for such high-energy emissions in ZnSe, a direct bandgap semiconductor (~ 2.7 eV at room temperature)70. The appearance of this peak indicates that the ZnSe NPs retain strong quantum confinement effects and crystalline quality, allowing for efficient radiative transitions near the intrinsic bandgap. The second emission is the dominant band at 416 nm (~ 2.98 eV), a blue luminescence attributed to the recombination of an electron localized on a selenium vacancy (VSe) with a hole in the valence band70. Furthermore, the peak at 456 nm (~ 2.71 eV) may be due to recombination involving zinc interstitial atom donor states (IZn)71. The broad emission centered at 496 nm (~ 2.50 eV) can be assigned to deep surface-defect states in ZnSe–P2 NPs, originating from unsaturated surface sites and ligand–surface complexes introduced by the phytochemical capping layer72. In contrast, the PL study of ZnSe NPs produced from Calligonum plant extract revealed three different emission peaks at 395 nm (~ 3.14 eV), 428 nm (~ 2.89 eV), and 490 nm (~ 2.53 eV). The sharp emission at 395 nm is attributed to NBE excitonic recombination, reflecting strong quantum confinement effects due to the small crystallite size of the ZnSe nanocrystals. The 428 nm emission band corresponds to blue luminescence, which originates from the recombination of electrons trapped at selenium vacancy sites (VSe) with holes in the valence band, thereby indicating intrinsic defect-related states within the NPs. The broad emission centered at 490 nm is assigned to deep-level surface defect states, associated with unsaturated surface atoms and ligand–surface complexes introduced by biomolecules in the Calligonum extract. Such defect-related states not only influence the optical response but may also enhance the photocatalytic activity of ZnSe–P3 NPs by facilitating charge-carrier trapping and prolonging exciton lifetimes73. This multipeak behavior implies a more complex surface chemistry than that of L-cysteine-capped NPs, emphasizing the significant effects of the biogenic capping agents on the optoelectronic characteristics of ZnSe nanocrystals74. For a better understanding of the different emission bands of organic and green-capped ZnSe NPs, an emission mechanism is proposed to explain the possible transitions of charge carriers, as illustrated in Fig. 6(b). Table S2 summarizes the positions and the FWHM of PL peaks in all samples. These results show that the nature of the used capping agent has a significant impact on the optical characteristics of ZnSe NPs. L-cysteine shows NBE and shallow donor emissions, whereas plant extracts prompt multiple emissions related to the surface and the deep defect states, which may play a crucial role in modifying the photocatalytic performance of ZnSe NPs for efficient environmental applications.

Photocatalytic activity of MB using ZnSe nanocatalysts

The photocatalytic performance of the synthesized ZnSe NPs is evaluated through the degradation of methylene blue (MB) under UV irradiation. Figure 7 shows the absorbance spectra of MB in the presence of the synthesized ZnSe NPs using synthetic ligand (L-cysteine: ZnSe-P1) and natural plant extracts (Artemisia: ZnSe-P2 and Calligonum: ZnSe-P3) after different adsorption and UV irradiation times. For all catalysts, the characteristic absorption peak of MB at ~ 664 nm exhibits a progressive decrease in intensity under dark conditions and with increasing irradiation time, indicating efficient photodegradation75. An initial 30-minute dark adsorption period was conducted to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium between MB molecules and the NP surfaces. Figure 7 indicates that MB degradation occurs at different levels depending on the ligand type used to prepare the NPs. ZnSe-P3 and ZnSe-P1 displayed the highest adsorption capability, compared to ZnSe-P2. Indeed, as shown in Fig. 8(a), the reduction in the MB concentration for ZnSe-P1 and ZnSe-P3 is about 40% of the initial MB concentration after 30 min, which is more than twice that for ZnSe-P2 (around 15%). The use of different capping agents during synthesis, which caused changes in particle size, surface chemistry, and crystallinity, is the origin of this performance deviation. The good adsorption capability observed for ZnSe-P1 is likely due to the relatively smaller NPs size and to the strong interaction between the thiol/amine groups of L-cysteine and the dye molecules, which enhances surface affinity76. However, the higher adsorption capability observed for ZnSe-P3 NPs is primarily due to surface and crystallinity modifications induced by the Calligonum-based phytochemical extract. These modifications are evidenced by the enhanced deep surface-defect states arising from unsaturated surface sites and ligand surface complexes that crucially contribute to improved photocatalytic activity77. Comparable findings in related systems show that plant-derived capping agents significantly alter surface defect dynamics and crystalline properties, yielding superior photocatalytic performance78. The gradual decrease in the 664 nm peak with increasing irradiation time indicates good photocatalytic activity for the degradation of MB molecules. In L-cysteine-capped ZnSe NPs, the trapping of charge carriers is primarily associated with selenium vacancies (VSe). However, in plant extract-capped ZnSe nanocrystals, multiple charge-carrier traps are introduced due to surface and structural defects, including zinc/selenium vacancies79. These defect states generate discrete energy levels within the forbidden bandgap of ZnSe, thereby reducing the recombination rate of photogenerated charge carriers and, consequently, enhancing their migration to the NP surface, where they participate in the generation of reactive free radicals essential for dye degradation80. The increase in free radical production ultimately leads to improved photocatalytic degradation of MB dye molecules. Consequently, the photocatalytic efficiency of the naturally capped ZnSe increases, as reflected by the enhanced degradation of MB dye.

Figure 8(b) presents the evolution of Ln C/C0 as a function of the degradation time for different ZnSe nanocatalysts. The results show linear relationships, indicating that the MB photodegradation process for all samples follows pseudo-first-order kinetics81. From the linear fit of the experimental data, the reaction constant (K) is estimated for the different samples, and the results are presented in Fig. 8(c). Interestingly, although ZnSe-P1 and ZnSe-P3 exhibited the highest overall MB degradation efficiencies, the kinetic analysis revealed that ZnSe-P2 possessed the highest rate constant, indicating superior intrinsic photocatalytic activity. The enhanced degradation performance of ZnSe-P3 and ZnSe-P1 can primarily be ascribed to their superior dye adsorption capacity, which improves the removal of MB molecules from polluted samples82. Additionally, in terms of photocatalytic activity, ZnSe-P3 performed better than ZnSe-P1, likely due to a broader defect distribution in ZnSe-P3, as indicated by the PL results. The presence of such defects can effectively reduce the recombination of photogenerated electron-hole pairs and enhance the photocatalytic performance of the material83. However, in comparison with ZnSe-P1 and ZnSe-P3, ZnSe-P2 NPs capped with Artemisia herba-alba showed stronger and more complex defect-related emissions (e.g., the presence of IZn defects) as revealed by PL investigations, suggesting a higher photo-generated carriers’ recombination delay, which may explain the improved photocatalytic performance of this sample. On the other hand, the presence of the positively charged surface interstitial Zn defects (IZn) in ZnSe-P2 NPs can cause charge imbalance at the NP surface, making the surface adsorption of the cationic dye less effective and leading to the observed low adsorption ability of pollutant molecules by this sample in comparison with other ones84,85.

Figure 8(d) shows the variation of the degradation efficiency, calculated using (Eq. 1), as a function of the UV irradiation time. Obviously, this efficiency increases with increasing irradiation time and reaches 38%, 28%, and 40% for ZnSe-P1, ZnSe-P2, and ZnSe-P3, respectively, after 180 min of UV irradiation. Among the three types of ZnSe NPs, both green samples synthesized using Artemisia herba-alba and Calligonum extracts exhibit the best photocatalytic activity toward MB degradation; this is mainly linked to its PL features, which reveal multiple defect- and surface-related emission bands86. The presence of blue, green, and surface-defect emissions indicates a higher density of shallow and deep trap states that can effectively capture charge carriers, delay their direct recombination, and thereby enhance the generation of reactive oxygen species such as hydroxyl (•OH) and superoxide (•O2⁻) radicals87. In addition to the positive effects of the defects on the photocatalytic performance of the sample synthesized via the green route, the biomolecules present in the plant extract, particularly phenols and flavonoids, introduce ligand–surface complexes. The latter stabilizes the NPs, provides additional reactive species, and may promote efficient interfacial charge transfer. Therefore, it leads to improved photocatalytic efficiency of biologically capped ZnSe NPs compared to those capped with synthetic agents.

To elucidate the photocatalysis process, the possible photocatalytic mechanism for the MB dye degradation in the presence of the capped ZnSe nano-adsorbents can be summarized based on the adsorption and photocatalysis stages (Fig. 9). The adsorption phase of the dye molecules on the NPs surface serves as a summary of the first stage. The production of reactive oxygen species under UV irradiation is the main driver of the photocatalytic degradation of MB using capped ZnSe NPs88. When the material absorbs photons with sufficient energy, electrons are excited from the valence band into the conduction band, leading to the formation of positively charged holes in the valence band and the generation of electron–hole pairs89. The presence of capping agents on ZnSe NPs improves their colloidal stability and is essential for preventing charge-carrier recombination, thereby enhancing photocatalysis efficiency. While the holes oxidize water molecules or hydroxide ions to generate highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH), the photogenerated electrons reduce molecular oxygen adsorbed on the NP surface into superoxide radicals (•O₂⁻). Following their interaction with the MB molecules, these reactive oxygen species oxidatively degrade the dye into less complex and non-toxic intermediates.

The photocatalytic degradation of MB dye is known to rely mainly on superoxide and hydroxyl radicals. The phenols and flavonoids in the plant extracts can act as supplementary sources of these reactive species90,91. The presence of organic compounds, such as polyphenolic acids, flavonoids, tannins, and saponins, which possess multiple hydroxyl and carbonyl groups, may effectively contribute to a significant enhancement in photocatalytic degradation efficiency of NPs synthesized with plant extracts92. These functional moieties enable strong coordination with the NP surface, facilitating efficient electron transfer while simultaneously suppressing the recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs under UV irradiation. Moreover, the surface functionalized by biomolecules acts as an electron trap, thus increasing the generation of reactive species (•OH, •O2⁻) responsible for the rapid degradation of pollutants93–95. To initiate the degradation of the MB dye, hydroxyl radicals first target the C–S+=C bond and other bonds within the dye molecules96. The above-mentioned investigation demonstrates that ZnSe NPs capped with Calligonum extract are promising photocatalysts for the removal of organic pollutants, owing to their improved adsorption and photocatalytic properties. Beyond wastewater treatment, the defect-engineered and bio-capped ZnSe nanostructures synthesized in this work can serve in various functional applications. The synergistic action of reactive oxygen species generation and the presence of bioactive surface phytochemicals (such as phenolics and flavonoids) enable efficient inhibition of microbial growth, making them promising for antibacterial coatings97. Moreover, surface defects and oxygen vacancies can modulate charge-carrier density and adsorption dynamics, improving sensitivity and selectivity toward oxidizing and reducing gases, which is advantageous for gas-sensing applications98. In addition, the extended light absorption and efficient interfacial charge transfer provided by the organic capping make these nanomaterials suitable for solar-driven photocatalytic and photoelectrochemical energy conversion processes, including hydrogen evolution and CO₂ reduction99. Plant-based synthesis of ZnSe NPs offers clear environmental and economic advantages over conventional chemical routes, including the use of renewable resources, non-toxic reagents, and mild aqueous processing. Phytochemicals act as both reducing and stabilizing agents, simplifying synthesis and enhancing photocatalytic properties. However, natural variability in plant composition can affect batch-to-batch reproducibility and size control, posing challenges for large-scale production. While the approach is inherently scalable due to low energy and cost requirements, industrial implementation will require standardized extract preparation and process optimization to ensure consistent material quality.

Conclusion

A novel green method for preparing ZnSe nanocatalysts was demonstrated using extracts from Artemisia herba-alba and Calligonum, marking the first comparative study with a conventional L-cysteine ligand. Structural and optical analyses confirmed the presence of quasi-spherical ZnSe NPs (5.6–7.7 nm), with properties strongly dependent on the capping agent. Calligonum-capped ZnSe achieved the highest MB degradation efficiency (~ 40% after 180 min), while Artemisia-capped ZnSe exhibited the fastest rate constant, highlighting its superior intrinsic photocatalytic activity. The enhanced performance of both plant-derived capping agents is attributed to phytochemical-induced defect states that promote charge separation and the generation of reactive oxygen species. These results demonstrate that phytochemical extracts not only provide an eco-friendly alternative to synthetic ligands but also introduce functional surface states that improve photocatalytic performance. Beyond water treatment, the approach offers a scalable, low-cost pathway to design green nanomaterials with potential applications in environmental remediation, antibacterial coatings, and sustainable energy conversion.
