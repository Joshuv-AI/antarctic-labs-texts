A prospective multicenter trial of deep learning auto-segmentation for organs at risk in thoracic radiotherapy
Authors: Gengmin Niu, Yong Guan, Yifan Zhang, Yongchun Song, Meng Yan, Songfeng Li, Tao Liu, Sheng Huang, Jingru Chen, Xiaofeng Wang, Wencheng Zhang, Maobin Meng, Yeman Liu, Junjie Chen, Yintao Fu, Donghe Zhao, Jing Huang, Kunyu Yang, Jianzhong Cao, Hongqin Yuan, Shuanshuan Guo, Xiaofeng Pei, Dongmei Wu, Yang Nan, Ziye Yan, Yao Lu, Lujun Zhao, Zhiyong Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Widespread clinical implementation of rapidly evolving auto-segmentation tools remains constrained by a scarcity of high-quality prospective evidence. Here we show the results of a prospective, multicenter, observational trial (NCT05787522) evaluating the clinical performance of a deep learning model (iCurveE) for artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted delineation of organs at risk (OARs) in thoracic and breast cancer radiotherapy. Computed tomography images from 500 patients across five centers are annotated by 37 physicians using manual, AI-generated, and AI-assisted methods. Eleven thoracic OARs are evaluated based on the primary endpoints of volumetric Dice similarity coefficient (vDSC) and contouring time, alongside secondary metrics including 95% Hausdorff Distance (HD95). We prospectively annotate 2,483 OAR sets (27,043 OARs): 993 manual, 497 AI-generated, and 993 AI-assisted. AI-assisted delineation achieves significantly better vDSC (mean, 0.902) and HD95 (mean, 5.20 mm) than manual delineation (mean vDSC, 0.857; mean HD95, 8.01 mm; p < 0.0001) while improving time efficiency by 81.63% (median: 10.0 vs. 55.0 min; p < 0.0001). AI-assisted delineation reduces performance variability across centers and physicians with varying expertise. This study validates the clinical applicability of AI-assisted delineation in improving delineation performance and promoting healthcare equity.

Introduction

Thoracic and breast cancers are significant global health concerns owing to their high incidence and mortality rates1–4. In particular, lung cancer (LC) and breast cancer (BC) rank as the leading causes of cancer-related deaths in the general population and among women, respectively5.

Radiotherapy, a foundational cancer treatment modality, plays a crucial role in managing LC, BC, and esophageal cancers (EC), as well as thoracic metastases across almost all cancer types6–9. For these malignancies, accurate delineation of thoracic organs at risk (OARs) is essential for effective radiotherapy planning, as inaccurate OAR segmentation can compromise normal tissue dose calculations, increasing the risk of radiation-induced toxicity and negatively impacting patient survival and quality of life10–12. However, traditional manual slice-by-slice OAR delineation is labor-intensive, often requiring hours to complete and leading to significant inter- and intra-observer variabilities13,14.

Recent advances in deep learning (DL) algorithms, such as convolutional neural networks and transformers, have shown superior efficiency and consistency in the auto-segmentation of target volumes and OARs15–18. Although previous studies have made significant strides in developing sophisticated DL architectures, many have primarily focused on assessing the performance of standalone models, often relying on retrospective data or imaging from non-cancer patients for validation19–21. Nevertheless, given the “black-box” nature of DL algorithms and corresponding ethical concerns, human oversight of artificial intelligence (AI)-generated outputs is mandated by legal frameworks, such as the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act (EU AI Act), and reinforced by professional consensus22–25. Consequently, physicians are required to review and modify the outputs generated by auto-segmentation models before finalizing radiotherapy treatment plans. However, prospective multicenter studies incorporating end-user testing with physician revision remain notably limited.

Some recent studies have involved end-user testing of auto-segmentation models, where senior experts reviewed AI-generated outputs in small-scale cohorts17,26–28. However, considering the increasing demand for radiotherapy and significant regional disparities in resource availability29,30, less-experienced physicians rather than “experts” may often use AI tools in clinical practice. Both the revision workload and the accuracy of final delineations may thus be potentially affected by this variability in expertise31. Furthermore, the inexplicability of DL models complicates the understanding of how physicians and AI tools collaborate, divide tasks, and influence each other in clinical settings32. Additionally, acquiring prospective, multi-institutional physician-annotated data—whether delineated from scratch or revised from AI outputs—is resource-intensive. Therefore, despite the rapid development of model algorithms, the widespread clinical implementation of auto-segmentation tools may require more high-quality evidence.

In this work, we comprehensively validate the superior performance and clinical applicability of the DL model (iCurveE)-based AI-assisted delineation compared with traditional manual contouring for thoracic OARs, through large-scale end-user testing by physicians of varying expertise on planning computed tomography (CT) scans from heterogeneous patients with cancer. Based on the multicenter deployment and multi-user utilization of this model, we also show the impact of AI-assisted delineation in promoting healthcare equity and the potential mechanisms underlying physician–AI interactions in enhancing segmentation results.

Performance benchmark of the iCurveE model

We benchmarked the performance of iCurveE model on our five-center cohort against an nnU-Net model trained from scratch (using the identical development images) and the open-source TotalSegmentator21,33. The iCurveE model achieved a significantly superior performance, with a mean volumetric Dice similarity coefficient (vDSC) of 0.896 and a 95% Hausdorff distance (HD95) of 5.62 mm, compared to both the nnU-Net (vDSC, 0.850; HD95, 12.05 mm; p < 0.0001) and TotalSegmentator (vDSC, 0.781; HD95, 19.07 mm; p < 0.0001) models. As measured by both vDSC and HD95, iCurveE significantly outperformed nnU-Net in 6 out of 11 (54.5%) OARs and TotalSegmentator in 4 out of 8 (50.0%) OARs, while TotalSegmentator does not support the segmentation of the left and right humeral heads and the bronchial tree (Supplementary Fig. 2).

AI-assisted delineation demonstrates superior accuracy

In this study, delineation accuracy was assessed using three key metrics: vDSC, HD95, and the surface DSC (sDSC). Overall, AI-assisted delineation achieved significantly higher accuracy than the manual approach across these metrics (all p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Fig. 3). Specifically, the mean vDSC for manual (M-vDSC), AI (AI-vDSC), and AI-assisted (Aa-vDSC) delineations were 0.857 (SD, 0.102), 0.896 (SD, 0.072), and 0.902 (SD, 0.067), respectively. The corresponding mean HD95 values (M-HD95, AI-HD95, and Aa-HD95) were 8.01 mm (SD, 15.84), 5.62 mm (SD, 5.31), and 5.20 mm (SD, 10.16). Based on these three metrics, AI-assisted delineation consistently outperformed manual delineation for all 11 (100%) OARs (p < 0.0001), with the only exception of HD95 for the aorta (p = 0.461). AI-assisted delineation also achieved significantly higher accuracy than AI delineation for 6 out of 11 OARs (54.5%): the heart, aorta, liver, trachea, spinal cord, and esophagus (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Fig. 3). However, AI-assisted delineation failed to show an advantage over the manual method in a few specific instances: for the trachea both in Center 4 and in patients with EC, and for the aorta in Center 5 (Supplementary Figs. 4–6). Additionally, we calculated recall, precision, and relative volume difference (RVD) for reference, where AI and AI-assisted delineations demonstrated significantly superior recall (p < 0.0001) and RVD (p < 0.0001) compared to manual delineation in total (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Impact of clinical and imaging heterogeneity

Considering that tumor lesions may affect the position and morphology of OARs, we compared the delineation accuracy of OARs adjacent to lesions (e.g. the lungs in LC and the trachea in EC) between patients with and without LC or EC. In patients with LC, the model’s segmentation accuracy (i.e., AI-vDSC, HD95, and sDSC) for the heart, trachea, and esophagus was significantly lower than in non-LC patients; however, no consistent multi-metric differences were observed for the lungs and bronchial tree (Supplementary Fig. 8). In patients with EC, the AI delineation accuracy for the trachea was also significantly lower. In contrast, after physician revision, the trachea showed no significant difference in Aa-vDSC (p = 0.140) or Aa-sDSC (p = 0.191) between patients with and without EC (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Contrast-enhanced (CE) CT scans yielded higher delineation accuracy for well-vascularized OARs (heart, aorta, and liver) than non-contrast (NC) scans across all three delineation methods (Supplementary Fig. 10). Furthermore, compared to thinner slices (<5 mm), scans with a 5 mm slice thickness demonstrated lower model accuracy only for the left and right humeral heads (Supplementary Fig. 11).

AI-assisted delineation improves efficiency

AI-assisted methods (Aa-time, median, 10.0 min) showed a median improvement of 81.63% in time efficiency compared to manual delineation (M-time, median, 55.0 min) (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2c). This time-saving advantage was statistically significant across all subgroup analyses by center, cancer type, and CT slice thickness (all p < 0.0001, Supplementary Fig. 12). Notably, the delineation time for the AI-assisted method was significantly less dependent on the number and thickness of CT slices compared to the manual method (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2d, e).

The volumetric revision index (VRI), a measure of potential expert revision workload, was significantly lower for AI-assisted delineations (Aa-VRI, median, 0.168) compared to both manual (M-VRI, median, 0.221) and AI delineations (AI-VRI, median, 0.181) (p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Fig. 13). Furthermore, AI-assisted delineation achieved a greater reduction in VRI from the manual baseline (median, 24.2%) than the AI-only method did (median, 21.9%) (p < 0.0001).

Our study’s cross-center delineation design enabled a paired performance comparison between physicians with differing attitudes from two centers (Fig. 1). We found that the positive group demonstrated significantly better accuracy (Fig. 3e, f and Supplementary Fig. 14c, d) but required longer contouring times for both manual and AI-assisted delineations (Fig. 3g). Notably, beyond enhancing delineation performance, AI-assisted delineation significantly narrowed the performance gap in all metrics between the positive and neutral groups when compared to manual contouring (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3e–g and Supplementary Fig. 14c, d).

AI-assisted delineation reduces inter-center variability

Significant variability was observed in M-vDSC (F-value, 54.50; p < 0.0001), M-HD95 (F-value, 25.83; p < 0.0001) and M-sDSC (F-value, 153.2; p < 0.0001) across the five centers, indicating a high heterogeneity in contouring proficiency. Notably, AI-assisted delineation not only significantly outperformed manual delineation in these metrics (Supplementary Fig. 4) but also reduced the inter-center variability in Aa-vDSC (F-value, 7.53; p < 0.0001), Aa-HD95 (F-value, 2.07; p = 0.081) and Aa-sDSC (F-value, 11.30; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4a, b, Supplementary Fig. 15). AI assistance reduces experience-related variability

Similarly, a significant multi-metric performance hierarchy (AI-assisted > AI > Manual) was observed in both less-experienced and experienced physicians (Supplementary Fig. 16). Based on cross-center shared images, we performed a paired comparison of delineation performance between less-experienced physicians and experienced physicians from adjacent centers. Compared to manual delineation, AI-assisted delineation reduced the number of OARs with significant performance differences between less-experienced and experienced physicians from 9 (observed for manual vDSC, HD95, and sDSC) to 6, 5, and 8, respectively (Fig. 4f, g, Supplementary Fig. 17a), while significantly narrowing their performance gap across all parameters (p < 0.0001): vDSC (median absolute difference, 0.034 vs. 0.003), HD95 (1.957 vs. 0 mm), sDSC (0.077 vs. 0.010), VRI (0.062 vs. 0.006), and contouring time (26.0 vs. 5.0 min) (Fig. 4h, i, Supplementary Fig. 17b–e).

Interplay of AI assistance and manual performance

AI and manual delineation accuracy were positively correlated, indicating that both approaches faced challenges with difficult-to-segment OARs (p < 0.0001; Supplementary Fig. 18a, b). However, greater improvements from AI and AI-assisted methods occurred where manual accuracy was lower (p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Fig. 18c–d), suggesting the model’s potential advantages for certain manually-challenging OARs. Specifically, we observed that lower M-vDSC values were associated with difficulties in anatomical structure recognition and unfamiliarity with OAR boundary definitions. In contrast, the model excelled at identifying structures such as the lobar bronchus, esophagus-azygos boundary, dural sac, surgical neck of the humerus, and the cricoid cartilage (Supplementary Fig. 19a–e). Under these circumstances, physicians may achieve higher vDSC by slightly modifying or even fully accepting the AI-generated boundaries.

Discussion

This prospective, multicenter study comprehensively validates the superiority of DL model-based AI-assisted delineation over traditional manual contouring for OAR segmentation. We prospectively included patients with heterogeneous cancer states and annotated their CT images using manual, AI, and AI-assisted methods by multi-institutional physicians with varying levels of expertise. This design enabled both large-scale external validation and multicenter end-user testing, thus exploring the clinical significance of the DL model under physician supervision rather than merely the standalone performance of the model.

End-user testing is crucial for validating the clinical applicability of AI models, which is emphasized by both of the recent NRG Oncology guidelines and EU AI Act23,35. However, prior studies have largely focused on model training and standalone model performance evaluation, whereas end-user testing involving physician review has remained scarce or limited in scale (typically including <50 cases)15–17,20,26. Notably, this trial enrolled 500 patients from five centers and generated 993 sets of AI-assisted delineations (10,837 OARs), which were systematically compared with manual segmentation using a comprehensive multi-metric evaluation, making a large-scale end-user trial in the field of radiotherapy to date.

In previous studies, end-user testing has typically been conducted solely by expert physicians17,19,26–28,35,36. However, given the regional disparities in radiotherapy expertise and medical resources, auto-segmentation tools cannot be restricted to senior experts alone29,37,38. In exemplary studies, Hosny et al.39 and Lu et al.31 involved physicians with varying levels of expertise in their rigorous testing process. However, the generalizability of their model may still be constrained by the limited number of physicians, institutions, and test samples. To address this, we engaged 37 radiotherapy physicians with diverse expertise from five hospitals, spanning both developed and developing regions of China. Our findings indicate that AI-assisted methods markedly decreased inter-physician and inter-center variability while significantly improving contouring performance compared to manual delineation (Fig. 4a–i, Supplementary Fig. 15–17). These findings provide large-scale multicenter evidence supporting the substantial potential of this model in promoting equity in radiotherapy services.

Notably, two principles enhanced the clinical relevance throughout the model development and five-center end-user testing: (1) the use of ground truth established by multi-expert consensus based on OAR delineation guidelines, and (2) the exclusive use of thoracic radiotherapy planning CTs. This approach contrasts with previous studies that utilized retrospective clinical contouring by individual physicians or CT images from patients without cancer15,21,40,41. Consequently, although the model’s 2D architecture and the long trial duration may not reflect the latest model advancements, its performance as a clinical segmentation tool proved highly robust, achieving accuracy higher than both manual delineation and the recent TotalSegmentator (Supplementary Fig. 2)21.

Interestingly, for the same AI outputs, physicians with a more positive attitude tended to spend more time on both manual contouring (M-time) and modification (Aa-time), ultimately achieving higher accuracy in both manual and AI-assisted delineation, whereas their neutral counterparts spent less time and achieved lower accuracy (Fig. 3e–g). This may reflect two distinct cognitive-behavioral patterns in physician-AI interaction: one in which physicians may apply stricter standards to their own work but are more forgiving of the AI-generated results, leading to a positive attitude; and another in which physicians may hold a higher standard to AI rather than their own manual work, resulting in a more critical evaluation.

Given the “black-box” nature of DL algorithms and associated ethical and legal concerns23–25,42,43, we extended our analysis beyond in silico validation and end-user testing, placing a particular focus on the physician-AI interaction during the delineation. Specifically, we focused on two critical aspects that have been rarely explored in previous studies: (1) the role of AI and physicians in collaboratively improving delineation accuracy, and (2) the impact of using AI models on the original delineation abilities of physicians.

First, OARs with lower M-vDSC and higher M-HD95 exhibit greater accuracy improvement with AI assistance (Supplementary Fig. 18), which may partially explain the marked reduction in inter-physician and inter-center variability (Fig. 4a–i). Furthermore, we observed that this improvement is likely due to the model’s ability to accurately recognize OAR boundaries and differentiate surrounding anatomical structures (Supplementary Fig. 19). However, physicians remain essential in reviewing auto-segmentation, potentially contributing to improved segmentation by mitigating the impact of low-quality images (e.g., non-contrast CT with thick slices and artifacts) through anatomical knowledge and clinical experience, recognizing OAR structural anomalies caused by tumors or non-tumor factors, conducting corrective modification based on the boundary definitions, identifying model failure scenarios, and modifying contours based on patient-specific clinical information (Supplementary Fig. 22). Exploring physician-AI interaction may help physicians better understand the strengths of AI models and clarify their responsibilities during model implementation, thereby facilitating more effective collaboration in clinical tasks.

Second, we explored the impact of AI application on the clinical decision-making abilities of less-experienced physicians, going beyond the traditional focus on model performance evaluation. Although 12% of the less-experienced physicians demonstrated improved manual accuracy over time, it remains unclear whether this was attributed to learning from the AI-generated results or referencing atlases when discrepancies arose between the auto-segmentations and their own contouring habits. Conversely, for physicians with decreased manual accuracy, their delineation was often characterized by over- or under-segmentation at clear OAR boundaries and increased overlap among OARs (Supplementary Fig. 21). This decline may be attributed not only to fatigue from prolonged manual contouring, a limitation not experienced by AI, but also potentially to a behavioral shift; as physicians grew accustomed to the convenience of AI assistance, their motivation for time-consuming manual contouring may have diminished. Therefore, regularly reviewing contouring errors against atlases, ensuring adequate rest to avoid fatigue-induced errors, and mitigating the risks of over-reliance on AI tools remain essential in clinical physician-AI interaction.

Despite the strengths of this trial, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, a significant portion (75.6%) of our study’s CT scans had a 5-mm slice thickness. This large thickness may introduce “partial volume effects”44,45, potentially impacting model performance for small-volume structures like the humeral heads (Supplementary Fig. 11). However, given the prospective design of this study, this data distribution partially reflects real-world clinical practice, thereby validating the model’s robustness when faced with challenges in clinical imaging quality. Second, while we performed a dosimetric validation, the magnitude of dose deviation was determined less by overall contouring accuracy and more by the proximity of any inaccuracy to the prescription dose line11. Furthermore, dose to thoracic OARs can be significantly confounded by numerous factors, such as physicist-dependent plan optimization, dose-fractionation regimen, and respiratory motion29. Given these confounding factors, a large-scale, rigorously designed study comparing treatment plans generated from different delineation methods may offer deeper insights into further dosimetric impact of contouring accuracy. Finally, while our model showed high accuracy on conventional thoracic OARs, its development and validation for more anatomically complex and clinically challenging structures, such as cardiac substructures and the brachial plexus, remain an important area for future investigation.

Auto-segmentation tools will continue to improve their performance in geometric parameters with the advancement of algorithms (e.g., integration with large language models46) and the incorporation of high-quality training datasets. However, rigorous validation through well-designed, prospective, interventional clinical trials with follow-up for patient outcomes, despite the formidable challenges, is crucial to ensure the safety and clinical applicability of future models. Given the rapid advancement of AI technology and the disparities in regional medical resources, future research should further clarify the responsibilities and competencies that radiation oncologists must develop when integrating AI into clinical practice.

Methods

Ethics statement and patient cohort

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of all participating centers and complied with all relevant ethical regulations. The approving institutions include: Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute & Hospital (TMUCIH; No. E20220700), Wuhan Union Hospital (WHUH; No. [2022] L.SH.Z-0540), Shanxi Cancer Hospital (SXCH; No. QX-2022-011), The Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (FHSU; No. [2022] L.Z. Q200-1), and Nanxishan Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region (NXSH; No. 2022-003-01). The trial was prospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT05787522; https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/). This prospective, multicenter, observational study involved 500 patients (137 males and 363 females; median age, 54.0 years) with cancer from these five hospitals in China. Each hospital was randomly assigned a unique identification number ranging from 1 to 5. All enrolled patients were aged ≥18 years, received a diagnosis of lung, esophageal, or breast cancer, met the eligibility criteria for thoracic radiotherapy (with planning CT slice thickness ≤5 mm), and provided written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) thoracic deformities caused by non-tumoral factors (e.g., congenital anomalies); (2) indiscernible anatomical structure contours due to artifacts and implants; (3) CT images not conforming to DICOM standards; and (4) investigator-determined unsuitability. Further patient and imaging characteristics are provided in Table 1.

Computed tomography

Chest CT was performed using four scanners: Revolution CT (TMUCIH) and Discovery CT590 RT (TMUCIH, FHSU) (GE Healthcare, USA), Brilliance CT Big Bore (WHUH, SXCH, NXSH) (Philips, Netherlands), and SOMATOM Confidence (SXCH) (Siemens Healthineers, Germany). Patient positioning was consistent with the requirements of the subsequent radiotherapy plan, with no specific restrictions on supine/prone positioning or arm placement. The scanning range included at least from the cricoid cartilage level to the lower edge of the liver. Both non-contrast and contrast-enhanced scans were acceptable. Detailed scanning parameters are outlined in Table 1. Patient CT data underwent de-identification to ensure anonymity.

Study design

This study adopted a crossover design, enabling each delineator to serve as their own control while minimizing potential biases from the contouring sequence. Anonymized CT images were randomly assigned to two cohorts in a 1:1 ratio. Cohort 1 underwent initial manual delineation, followed by AI-assisted delineation after a washout period (>4 weeks), whereas Cohort 2 followed the inverse sequence. CT images acquired from each center were also circulated to the next center for manual and AI-assisted delineations (e.g., Center 1 to Center 2, Center 5 to Center 1), thereby creating a dataset of shared images, with each image set delineated by one physician from each of the two adjacent centers.

Consequently, each image set yielded two manual delineations and two AI-assisted delineations, forming pairs for comparisons: (1) one pair from a physician at the recruiting center for intra-physician comparison between manual and AI-assisted methods; and (2) one pair from a physician at the next center for inter-center and inter-physician comparisons (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, a single, identical AI delineation set was generated for each image. The CT images acquired for this trial were used for both research purposes and subsequent clinical radiotherapy planning. To ensure timely patient care, the study protocol did not mandate the clinical use of any specific research-generated contour set (e.g., AI-assisted delineation, or ground truth); instead, this decision was left to the discretion of the treating physician.

Generation of manual, AI, and AI-assisted delineations

Manual delineation was performed slice-by-slice by physicians, whereas AI-assisted delineation involved physicians manually modifying the AI delineations. The AI delineation results were generated using iCurveE before the AI-assisted delineation process. A total of 37 radiotherapy physicians (with ≥1 year of experience) from the five centers performed all manual and AI-assisted OAR delineations, but were not involved in the ground truth generation. Physicians were categorized as “experienced” if they had >3 years of radiotherapy experience and managed >50 thoracic and BC cases annually; otherwise, they were considered “less experienced.” Details of the participating physicians, image assignments, and their delineation counts are provided in Supplementary Table 3. In this study, none of the participating physicians were involved in the delineation of the model training dataset.

Ground truth determination

The ground truth for each image was generated by three senior radiation oncologists (A, B, and C), each with over 10 years of radiotherapy experience. Experts A and B independently delineated each CT image, resulting in two sets of OARs, which were then reviewed by Expert C, who compared and selected the most accurate delineations from Experts A and B (e.g., the right lung from Expert A and the spinal cord from Expert B), making minor modifications if necessary. To minimize bias, the OARs generated by Expert C were established as the ground truth only if they were based on a consensus among the three experts; otherwise, further modifications were made. These experts were not involved in the manual or AI-assisted delineation of the evaluated OARs nor in the generation of the model’s training set. Software and platforms used for delineation are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

OAR contouring standards

Eleven OARs were delineated per CT image: the left and right lungs, trachea, bronchial tree, aorta, heart, esophagus, liver, left and right humeral heads, and spinal cord. The bilateral lungs, heart, esophagus, aorta, spinal cord, trachea, and bronchial tree were delineated according to the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) 1106 atlas47, while the liver followed the RTOG consensus guidelines for upper abdominal normal organs48. The bilateral humeral heads were delineated as the bony structure from their apex to the surgical neck under a bone window, following standard radiological anatomy49. This guideline was applied to all delineations throughout the entire clinical trial.

Model information

We employed a DL model named iCurveE, which utilizes the Res-SE block as the fundamental unit, with U-Net as the backbone architecture of the network (Supplementary Fig. 23). This model has received Class III approval as a medical device from the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) of China (Registration Number: 20253210066). The model was developed on a retrospective dataset of 827 planning chest CTs from two institutions: Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC) (n = 518) and Shandong Cancer Institute and Hospital (SDCIH) (n = 309), ensuring no overlap in centers, patients, or delineators with the current study cohort (Supplementary Table 5). The ground truth contours were established by an independent expert panel using the same consensus process as this trial. The technical workflow for model development incorporated a standardized preprocessing pipeline, data augmentation, model training, and morphological post-processing. Specific parameters for data preprocessing, network architecture, and training implementation are comprehensively listed in Supplementary Table 6, while the model’s training performance is presented in Supplementary Table 7.

Dosimetric validation

To evaluate the dosimetric impact of delineation variability, we performed a direct dosimetric validation on a randomly selected cohort of 40 patients from Center 1 (19 LC, 18 BC, 3 EC). Using a fixed clinical dose distribution, we calculated the absolute differences in DVH parameters between the ground-truth contours and the manual, AI, and AI-assisted contours. For each contour set, we evaluated 38 DVH parameters, with the selection guided by the clinically widely used Quantitative Analysis of Normal Tissue Effects in the Clinic (QUANTEC) guidelines and the Timmerman Table50,51. Furthermore, we specifically correlated the vDSC with deviations in volume-dependent DVH parameters (e.g., V5 and V20), and the HD95 with deviations in boundary-sensitive DVH parameters (e.g., Dmax and D0.1cc).

Evaluation metrics

Multiple metrics were adopted to assess discrepancies between manual, AI, and AI-assisted delineations and the ground truth. The primary endpoints were the volumetric Dice similarity coefficient (vDSC) and contouring time. Secondary endpoints included the 95% Hausdorff distance (HD95), surface Dice similarity coefficient (sDSC), and volumetric revision index (VRI). Precision, recall, and relative volume difference (RVD) were also assessed as supplementary measures. The VRI, an optimized metric of RVD52, estimates the potential revision workload for an expert to align a contour with the ground truth. Prior to performing the AI-assisted task, the delineating physician rated their overall satisfaction with the initial set of 11 AI-generated OARs on a five-point Likert scale (from “Strongly Dissatisfied” to “Strongly Satisfied”) for each delineation set. Supplementary Table 8 presents the detailed calculation principles and formulas for each metric. Given the observational nature of this study, no method-related adverse events were anticipated. Only incidents leading to CT scan interruption (e.g., contrast allergy, severe anxiety) were documented (Supplementary Table 9).

Statistics and reproducibility

The Wilcoxon signed-rank and Mann-Whitney U tests were performed for paired and independent samples, respectively. For comparisons across multiple independent groups, we used Welch’s analysis of variance followed by the Games-Howell post-hoc test. For multiple paired group comparisons, we used the Friedman test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test. The relationship between two quantitative variables was quantified via linear regression. GraphPad Prism 10.5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for all statistical analyses and graph generation, with significance defined as a two-tailed p-value of <0.05.

Sample size was determined based on a non-inferiority design for the vDSC (one-sided α = 0.025, 90% power, non-inferiority margin = −0.05), with a pre-specified plan to test for superiority upon confirmation of non-inferiority. Assuming a standard deviation of 0.225 and a true mean difference of 0, the required sample size was calculated to be 426. To account for a 10% potential dropout rate and a further safety margin, the final sample size was determined to be 500. The randomization allocation sequence was generated using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) with a block size of 10, and concealed using opaque, sealed cards prepared by an independent statistician. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment due to the visible nature of the delineation methods.
