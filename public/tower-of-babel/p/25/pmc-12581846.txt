Usefulness of Automated Analysis System for Microvascular Blood Flow Rate Using Magnifying Endoscopy in Early Gastric Cancer: A Multicenter Retrospective Study (With Video)
Authors: Yoichi Akazawa, Hiroya Ueyama, Shunsuke Nakamura, Yasuko Uemura, Tomoyo Iwano, Momoko Yamamoto, Hisanori Utsunomiya, Ryota Uchida, Daiki Abe, Shotaro Oki, Nobuyuki Suzuki, Atsushi Ikeda, Tsutomu Takeda, Kumiko Ueda, Mariko Hojo, Naoto Iwai, Osamu Dohi, Takehiro Iwasaki, Kunihisa Uchita, Shuko Nojiri, Takashi Yao, Akihito Nagahara

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimEarly gastric cancer (EGC) diagnosis after Helicobacter pylori eradication and differentiation from patchy redness (PR) remains challenging. We developed a novel automated analysis system for microvascular blood flow rate using magnifying endoscopy. This study evaluated its diagnostic performance in differentiating EGC from PR compared with endoscopists in a multicenter setting.MethodsWe analyzed 76 magnifying endoscopy videos, including 36 EGCs and 40 PRs recorded at our institution. Lesions were diagnosed as cancer (EGC) or noncancer (PR) by the analysis system, based on the cut‐off value calculated from the validation data set. Additionally, 30 endoscopists (15 experts and 15 trainees) from three institutions, all skilled in magnifying endoscopy diagnosis, participated. We compared the diagnostic performance between the analysis system and endoscopists.ResultsDiagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value (NPV) were 84.2%, 86.1%, 82.5%, 81.6%, and 86.8%, respectively, for the analysis system, and 84.2%, 80.2%, 87.3%, 83.2%, and 84.9%, respectively, for the endoscopists. Sensitivity and NPV of the analysis system tended to be higher than those of endoscopists. Additionally, the accuracy of the analysis system was comparable to that of all endoscopists and tended to be superior to that of trainees (84.2% [95% CI: 74.0%–91.6%] vs. 81.7% [95% CI: 79.4%–83.8%], respectively).ConclusionsThe automated analysis system for microvascular blood flow rate showed diagnostic performance equivalent to or superior to that of endoscopists skilled in magnifying endoscopy, suggesting its utility as a new diagnostic modality for differentiating EGC and PR in clinical practice.Keywords: blood flow rate, blue laser imaging, early gastric cancer, magnifying endoscopy, patchy redness, subepithelial microvessel

Introduction

Recently, image‐enhanced endoscopy and magnifying endoscopy have been reported to be useful for the diagnosis of early gastric cancer (EGC) [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. However, some cases are difficult to diagnose endoscopically, such as EGC after Helicobacter pylori ( H. pylori ) eradication or H. pylori ‐uninfected EGC [2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. EGCs after eradication often show small reddish depressed lesions and are difficult to diagnose even using magnifying endoscopy [2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. The endoscopic findings of patchy redness (PR) defined by the Kyoto Classification [11] are also similar to EGC after eradication, making it challenging to differentiate between these two lesions. We previously reported a new dynamic diagnosis focusing on red blood cells visible with magnifying endoscopy and demonstrated that the microvascular blood flow rate was significantly slower in EGC than in PR [12]. However, the method was subjective and required complicated manual calculations. We have therefore developed a new analysis system that incorporates the automatic and more objective calculation of microvascular blood flow rate. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the new automated analysis system for microvascular blood flow rate in differentiating EGC from PR compared with endoscopists in a multicenter setting.

Methods

Methods of Microvascular Blood Flow Rate Analysis

We uniquely developed an automated analysis system for microvascular blood flow rate using magnifying endoscopy in collaboration with FUJIFILM Corporation, Tokyo, Japan (Ueyama H, Akazawa Y, Nagahara A: Patent JP 2021‐182768). The analysis system functions by processing each endoscopic video (5–20 s) of a lesion, recorded with magnifying endoscopy with blue laser imaging (M‐BLI). Each video is loaded into the application, and the algorithm proceeds as follows: (1) frame extraction, each video is automatically divided into still images at 30 frames per second; (2) frame‐to‐frame differencing, the system applies frame‐to‐frame differencing of the red color component to detect red blood cell displacement and identifies the two ends of the differential region; and (3) flow rate calculation and correction, frame‐to‐frame displacement is used to calculate the microvascular blood flow rate, while translational motion components are automatically removed and corrected by the system. Figure 1 and Video S1 show an example of microvascular blood flow rate assessment by our analysis system. Microvascular blood flow measurements in multiple microvessels are performed automatically, with the measurement site indicated by the red circle (Figure 1 white arrow). Only the red circles on the subepithelial microvessels were analyzed, and those outside a microvessel were excluded as noise. Based on preliminary statistical evaluation, it was determined that the average value from 15 locations would be used as representative of each case.

Endoscopic System and Examination

Magnifying endoscopy videos were taken using EG‐L600ZW with LASEREO4450 or LASEREO7000 system series (Fujifilm Corporation). All M‐BLI videos were performed at the maximum magnification (145×) with a water immersion technique using a black soft attachment (MAJ‐1989, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), which minimizes contact pressure on the lesion surface and thus suggests that the influence on blood flow rate is limited. All endoscopic investigations were performed by H.U. and Y.A., skilled endoscopists accredited by the Japan Gastroenterological Endoscopy Society (JGES) at our institution.

Validation Data Set Preparation and Determination of Cut‐Off Value for the Microvascular Blood Flow Rate Analysis System

To evaluate the outcome of this study, we first collected and analyzed a validation data set, as described below, to determine the cut‐off value. The validation data set included M‐BLI videos of 31 EGCs in 31 patients and 40 PRs in 40 patients diagnosed endoscopically that were recorded at our institution between December 2017 and September 2021 (including 10 EGCs and 10 PRs reported previously [12]). We excluded EGCs that were difficult to diagnose as cancer even using magnifying endoscopy, such as gastric adenocarcinoma of the fundic‐gland type (GA‐FG) and signet ring cell carcinoma (sig) occurring in H. pylori ‐uninfected stomach. We also excluded cases in which subepithelial microvessels were invisible due to mucus, bleeding, and white opaque substance. The microvascular blood flow rate at each lesion was calculated by the analysis system using the aforementioned method. To set a cut‐off value of microvascular blood flow, we performed receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis on the validation data set for differentiating between EGC and PR. Baseline characteristics of the validation data set are described in Table S1. The mean microvascular blood flow rate of EGC was significantly slower than that of PR in the validation data set (949.6 ± 76.4 μm/s vs. 1210.1 ± 104.9 μm/s, p < 0.01) (Table S2 and Figure S1). Based on the results of the ROC analysis in the validation data set, we set the cut‐off value as 1090.9 μm/s (sensitivity/specificity = 90.3%/89.7%) for EGC and PR (Figure S2). Namely, a microvascular blood flow rate lower than the cut‐off value was diagnosed as EGC, and higher than the cut‐off was diagnosed as PR.

Test Data Set Preparation and Microvascular Blood Flow Rate Analysis

In this study, we analyzed 76 M‐BLI videos as the test data set including 36 EGCs in 36 patients and 40 PRs in 40 patients that were different from the validation data set recorded at our institution between December 2017 and September 2023. One case of EGC was diagnosed by biopsy only, and endoscopic submucosal dissection was not performed. The exclusion criteria and the endoscopic instruments were the same as in the validation data set. We retrospectively analyzed clinicopathological and endoscopic findings such as age, sex, comorbidities (hypertension), H. pylori infection status, tumor location, endoscopic mucosal atrophy, tumor size, macroscopic type, main histological type, invasion depth, and lymphovascular invasion. Because hypertension prevalence differed significantly between the two groups, as described in Section 3, we additionally analyzed blood flow rate stratified by hypertension status. The microvascular blood flow rate at each lesion was calculated using the analysis system based on the method mentioned above. In this study, all lesions were diagnosed as cancer or noncancer (PR) according to the cut‐off value (1090.9 μm/s) calculated using the validation data set.

Participating Endoscopists and Evaluation of M‐BLI Videos

A total of 30 endoscopists (15 experts and 15 trainees) from three institutions, including Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine, Kochi Red Cross Hospital, and our institution, who were familiar with the magnifying endoscopic diagnosis, participated. Experts were defined as endoscopists certified by the JGES and skilled in diagnosing EGC using magnifying endoscopy. Trainees were defined as endoscopists who belonged to JGES but were not certified as specialists. The participating endoscopists evaluated all the same M‐BLI videos (not included in white light imaging and nonmagnifying BLI) used in the analysis system in no particular order and diagnosed as cancer or noncancer based on MESDA‐G. No information about the patients and lesions was provided in advance, and no interaction was allowed among the participants during the test.

Outcome Assessment and Objectives

We evaluated and compared the diagnostic performance including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) between the analysis system and endoscopists. Moreover, we calculated inter‐ and intra‐rater reliability grades using Fleiss' and Cohen's Kappa, respectively, which were classified as follows: “almost perfect”, Kappa value > 0.81; “substantial”, 0.80–0.61; “moderate”, 0.60–0.41; “fair”, 0.40–0.21; and “slight”, < 0.20 [13]. The intra‐rater reliability was calculated based on the results in which the endoscopists re‐evaluated the same videos rearranged in no particular order.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous data were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher's exact test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Patient and Lesion Characteristics

In this study, we collected 76 M‐BLI videos including 36 EGCs and 40 PRs. The baseline characteristics of all patients are shown in Table 1. There were more males than females in both groups (77.8% in EGCs, 77.5% in PRs). Patients in the EGC group were significantly younger than those in the PR group (mean age: 70.3 vs. 76.7 years, p < 0.05). The prevalence of hypertension was significantly lower in the EGC group compared with the PR group (33.3% vs. 67.5%, p < 0.01). Most of the patients in both groups had undergone H. pylori eradication, had lesions located in the middle to lower third of the stomach, and had atrophic gastritis with open‐type atrophy [14]. In the EGC group, the mean tumor size was 16.7 mm. Although most cases showed superficial macroscopically depressed lesions, four EGC cases presented as elevated type and one case as flat type. Except for two cases of undifferentiated‐type adenocarcinoma, all cases were differentiated adenocarcinomas. Most lesions were intramucosal cancer, and one case showed lymphatic invasion.

Comparison of Diagnostic Performance Between the Analysis System and Endoscopists

Table 2 summarizes the diagnostic performance of the analysis system and endoscopists. The accuracy of the analysis system was comparable to that of all endoscopists (84.2% [95% CI: 74.0%–91.6%] vs. 84.2% [95% CI: 82.6%–85.5%], respectively). Although the accuracy of the analysis system tended to be inferior to that of experts, it tended to be superior to trainees (84.2% [95% CI: 74.0%–91.6%] vs. 81.7% [95% CI: 79.4%–83.8%], respectively). Additionally, the sensitivity and NPV of the analysis system tended to be superior to those of all endoscopists (sensitivity: 86.1% [95% CI: 70.5%–95.3%] vs. 80.2% [95% CI: 77.7%–82.4%], respectively; NPV: 86.8% [95% CI: 71.9%–95.6%] vs. 84.9% [95% CI: 82.9%–86.6%], respectively). The inter‐rater reliability among endoscopists was “moderate” (0.47) for trainees, “substantial” (0.62) for experts, and “moderate” (0.53) for all endoscopists. The intra‐rater reliability among endoscopists was “moderate‐almost perfect” (0.42–0.75) for trainees and “fair‐almost perfect” (0.39–0.89) for experts (Table S3). Table S4 shows the detailed data of the microvascular blood flow rate of all cases in the test data set, and Figure S4 shows the comparison of the mean microvascular blood flow rate between EGC and PR. The test data sets showed a significantly lower microvascular blood flow rate in EGC as compared to PR (1030.6 ± 106.9 μm/s vs. 1228.4 ± 315.2 μm/s, respectively, p < 0.01).

Discussion

The first analyses of the blood flow of the gastric mucosa were conducted over 40 years ago using various methods [15, 16, 17, 18]. However, the past methods required specialized equipment and could not specifically measure the blood flow of the superficial mucosa observable by magnifying endoscopy. Here, we have developed a novel analysis system that automatically calculates the blood flow rate within subepithelial microvessels using magnifying endoscopy. This study is the first report to evaluate the usefulness of the analysis system for diagnosing EGC and to compare the diagnostic performance with that of endoscopists.

Our previous pilot study reported that the microvascular blood flow rate was significantly slower in EGC than in PR [12]. In the blood flow analysis conducted by the analysis system, both the validation data and test data sets showed a significantly lower microvascular blood flow rate in EGC as compared to PR (test data: 1030.6 [889.4–1291.9] μm/s vs. 1228.4 [832.7–2591.9] μm/s, respectively; validation data: 949.6 [825.0–1140.7] μm/s vs. 1210.1 [970.0–1424.2] μm/s, p < 0.01, respectively), consistent with the results of the pilot study [12]. On the other hand, the mean microvascular blood flow rate calculated by the analysis system in this study tended to be slower as compared to the microvascular blood flow rate in the pilot study (EGC: 1969 [1406–2344] μm/s, PR: 5132 [3238–7846] μm/s) [12]. Although the cause of these observed differences in blood flow rate is unclear, it may be due to differences in method, i.e., manual vs. automatic calculations by the analysis system. Based on the average capillary blood velocities that have been reported (300–1000 μm/s) [19], manual calculations tend to calculate higher blood flow rates and to overestimate the distance of red blood cell movement. In contrast, automatic calculations of the analysis system are considered to be closer to the actual microvascular blood flow rate and provide a more accurate assessment. However, the fundamental mechanisms of microvascular blood flow rate changes have not yet been clarified. It is known that blood flow rate is regulated by factors such as vessel diameter, viscosity, blood pressure, cardiac output, and vascular resistance [20]. In EGC, dilatation and caliber changes of subepithelial microvessels appear to be more frequently observed than in PR (Figure S5). Furthermore, the subepithelial microvascular network in EGC may collapse with increasing vertical branching, leading to a relative decrease in blood flow rate when observed using magnifying endoscopy, i.e., by two‐dimensional observation. Although such differences are one of the causes of changing microvascular blood flow rate, an accurate evaluation of the microvascular architecture based on a three‐dimensional assessment will be necessary to clarify the mechanisms.

In a multicenter randomized controlled trial conducted in Japan to evaluate the usefulness of an e‐learning system for the diagnosis of EGC using magnifying endoscopy, the accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity among all endoscopists were reported to be around 60% [21]. In this study, the endoscopists, including trainees, demonstrated relatively good diagnostic ability because all participating institutions were familiar with magnifying endoscopy, and the accuracy of the analysis system was comparable to that of all endoscopists and tended to be superior to that of the trainees. Furthermore, the sensitivity and NPV of the analysis system tended to be superior to those of all endoscopists, although the differences were not statistically significant. Considering these points, the accuracy of the analysis system may have been underestimated. This suggests that the system could serve as a new diagnostic modality, demonstrating diagnostic performance equivalent to or superior to that of endoscopists skilled in magnifying endoscopy for the differential diagnosis of EGC and PR.

There were six cases including two PRs and four differentiated‐type EGCs, in which the analysis system made correct diagnoses, whereas 40% or more of endoscopists misdiagnosed in the present study. In the two cases of PRs with a low correct diagnosis rate including a case shown in Figure 4a,b, although distribution and arrangement were regular, the microvascular pattern showed slight tortuosity and change in caliber. In the four cases of differentiated‐type EGCs with a low correct diagnosis rate including a case shown in Figure 4c–e, the irregularity of microvascular and microsurface patterns tended to be weak. H. pylori status showed that two cases were after H. pylori eradication, one was uninfected, and one had ongoing infection. These findings may have led to misdiagnosis by the endoscopists.

Previous reports have indicated that EGCs after H. pylori eradication are difficult to diagnose with magnifying endoscopy [2, 5, 8, 10]. Moreover, H. pylori ‐uninfected EGCs, such as sig, GA‐FG, and several types of differentiated gastric adenocarcinomas, are known to be difficult to diagnose endoscopically [1, 4, 9, 10]. Based on the cases of Figure 4, in which the analysis system made correct diagnoses while the majority of endoscopists misdiagnosed, changes in microvascular blood flow rate may still occur even if microvascular irregularities are weak. This suggests that blood flow analysis may be useful in such EGCs. Additionally, even in cancers completely covered by non‐neoplastic epithelium, such as intramucosal sig and GA‐FG, blood flow rate changes on the tumor surface may occur due to the influence of tumors beneath the surface epithelium, suggesting the utility of blood flow analysis. However, further investigation will be required for such EGCs in which qualitative diagnosis is challenging even using magnifying endoscopy.

The present study has several limitations. First, the test and validation data sets were derived from a single institution, and therefore selection bias cannot be avoided. External validation with multicenter data sets is required to enhance the generalizability of our findings. Second, microvascular blood flow analysis is difficult in cases where subepithelial microvessels are invisible. Exclusion of such cases may have introduced selection bias and potentially overestimated the diagnostic performance of the system, and the cut‐off value was calculated from a validation data set affected by this bias. Therefore, further evaluation including various types of EGCs and nontumorous lesions is necessary to determine whether the current cut‐off value is appropriate. Third, differences in baseline characteristics, such as a higher prevalence of hypertension in the PR group, and the small number of undifferentiated‐type adenocarcinomas (n = 2) raise concerns about confounding and limited representativeness. However, in a subgroup analysis stratified by hypertension status, no significant differences in blood flow rate were observed in either group (Figure S3). In addition, the blood flow rates of the two undifferentiated cases (1058.0 and 914.8 μm/s; Cases 17 and 18 in Table S4) were within the range of those observed in differentiated cases (mean 1033.2 μm/s, median 1004.6 μm/s, range 889.4–1291.9 μm/s), suggesting that the potential bias is limited. Fourth, PRs were diagnosed only endoscopically, and no histopathological diagnosis was made. However, we only included PRs that were definitively diagnosed by two expert endoscopists, and there was a very low chance of misdiagnosis with EGCs. Fifth, the mechanism of why microvascular blood flow rate slows in EGCs has not been clarified yet. As aforementioned, further analysis of the various factors that regulate the microvascular blood flow rate and the three‐dimensional evaluation of subepithelial microvessels will be necessary. Sixth, the magnifying endoscopic procedure with the water immersion technique is relatively difficult for most endoscopists. However, such a procedure is useful for the qualitative diagnosis and delineation of EGCs [6], and it is expected to become a widely adopted technique in the future. Seventh, the current analysis system cannot be used for real‐time diagnosis because it requires several processes. We are currently planning to develop a real‐time diagnostic system, aiming for its application in clinical practice.

In conclusion, we have developed a novel diagnostic modality that automatically calculates the microvascular blood flow rate within the subepithelial microvessel using magnifying endoscopy and evaluated its diagnostic performance compared with endoscopists in multicenter settings. As a result, the analysis system demonstrated equivalent or superior diagnostic performance compared to endoscopists in the differential diagnosis of EGC and PR. Therefore, the analysis system has the potential to be useful in clinical practice. We hope for further investigation of various types of lesions and the development of a next‐generation microvascular blood flow analysis system, which can be applied to clinical endoscopic practice.
