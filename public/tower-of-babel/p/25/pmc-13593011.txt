Intratumoral microbiota in tumorigenesis: A double-edged sword
Authors: Xinyue Fu, Hao Chen, Meihang Du, Peiheng Li, Yue Zhao, Huanzuo Yang, Hancong Li, Gongshuang Zhang, Puxing He, Shuang Wu, Miao Zhang, Zhenmei An, Zhihui Li, Binwu Ying, Juan Rául Hernandez Silva, Liwei An, Han Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid development of high-throughput sequencing technologies and bioinformatics has enabled the identification of diverse microbial communities within tumor tissues, giving rise to the concept of intratumoral microbiota. Comprising bacteria, viruses, fungi, and archaea, the intratumoral microbiota has been shown to play critical roles in tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic responses. This review systematically summarizes the composition, ecological characteristics, and functional roles of intratumoral microbiota, with particular emphasis on its dual regulatory mechanisms in tumorigenesis, progression, metastasis, and recurrence. We further delineate four principal mechanistic pathways through which intratumoral microbiota influence tumor biology, including receptor-mediated signaling, metabolic reprogramming, effector molecule secretion, and intracellular invasion. Additionally, we evaluate the translational potential of these findings in clinical oncology, offering innovative perspectives for tumor diagnosis, therapeutic intervention, and preventive strategies.

Introduction

The human microbiota, comprising bacteria, viruses, fungi,[1] and archaea, colonizes both surface and internal body sites with a total load comparable to the total human cell count,[2,3] with approximately 97% residing in the colon (≈1014 bacteria),[4] whereas the remainder is distributed to sites such as the proximal gut, skin (≈1012 bacteria),[5] and lungs. Archaea and eukaryotes represent less than 1% of this community, with bacteria exceeding them by 2–3 orders of magnitude.[6] Historically limited to open cavities, microbial colonization is now known to include tissues once considered sterile, such as breast,[7] liver,[8] pancreas,[9] prostate,[10] and kidneys,[11] due to advanced detection technologies. The association between tumors and microorganisms can be traced back to the late 13th century, when postresection infection in the Italian Friar Peregrine Laziosi correlated with tumor regression.[12] William Coley later developed inactivated bacterial preparations (“Coley’s toxins”) to induce immune-mediated tumor shrinkage,[13] whereas Malmgren and Flanigan[14] showed selective Clostridium perfringens proliferation causing tumor necrosis. A pivotal breakthrough came with Marshall and Warren’s 1982 discovery of Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) and its role in gastric carcinogenesis, which earned a Nobel Prize.[15] In 2007, the Human Microbiome Project used next-generation sequencing to map microbial genomes across body sites and explore microbiota–health interactions.[16] High-throughput sequencing later enabled systematic tumor microbiome studies, leading the International Tumor Microbiome Consortium (2021) to establish a consensus defining intratumoral microbiota as metabolically active, functionally engaged microbial communities within tumors.

In recent years, the role of the microbiota in cancer initiation, progression, and treatment response has emerged as a cutting-edge area in oncology research. Particularly noteworthy is the pivotal function of the gut microbiota in modulating responses to immunotherapy. Numerous preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated that the compositional and functional diversity of gut microbes significantly influences the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as anti-programmed cell death protein 1 [PD-1]/programmed death-ligand 1 [PD-L1] antibodies) across various cancer types.[17] The mechanisms primarily involve systemic regulation along the microbiota–immune axis. Specific bacterial taxa (such as Akkermansia muciniphila (A. muciniphila),[18] Faecalibacterium prausnitzii,[19] and Bifidobacterium spp.[20]) modulate host immune status through metabolites (such as short-chain fatty acids [SCFA],[21] indole derivatives,[22] and polyamines[23]), thereby enhancing CD8+ T cell infiltration and activation,[24] promoting dendritic cell maturation,[25] and inducing M1 macrophage polarization,[26] ultimately overcoming resistance to immunotherapy. Conversely, certain microbial genera (including some pathogenic members of Bacteroides) may contribute to treatment resistance by fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment, activating regulatory T cells (Tregs), or expressing mimics of immune checkpoint molecules.[27]

The exploration of intratumoral microbiota has emerged as a critical frontier in cancer biology and tumor immunology. Accumulating evidence indicates its roles in tumor development, progression, and treatment response. Although traditionally viewed as tumor-promoting through proinflammatory and genotoxic mechanisms, recent studies reveal a context-dependent duality, exhibiting both oncogenic and tumor-suppressive functions. Through diverse mechanistic pathways, including immunomodulation, inflammatory regulation, and genomic stability maintenance, these microbial communities demonstrate bidirectional regulatory capacities, capable of either facilitating neoplastic progression or mediating antitumor effects.

Current research has elucidated multiple mechanistic pathways through which intratumoral microbiota influences oncological processes. First, intratumoral microbiota can engage in direct molecular crosstalk by binding to surface receptors expressed on both neoplastic and immune cells.[28] Second, intratumoral microbiota-derived effector molecules, including small-molecule metabolites[29] and secreted proteins, systemically modulate tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic responsiveness. Notably, owing to the substantial size disparity between intratumoral microbiota and host cells, coupled with the high invasive capacity of certain species, some intratumoral microbiota can infiltrate host cells and exert direct cytoplasmic or nuclear effects following host cell internalization.

This review systematically summarizes the ecological and functional features of intratumoral microbiota, their dual effects of promoting and suppressing tumors, and four core interaction paradigms: cell surface receptor binding, small-molecule metabolites, protein secretion, and host cell invasion. We further explore ecological dynamics, such as symbiosis and competition within the tumor microenvironment (TME), and assess translational applications in diagnostics, microbiota-based therapeutics, and prevention. These advances provide a novel conceptual framework linking microbial mechanisms to cancer intervention, bridging basic science and clinical oncology.

Characteristics of the Intratumoral Microbiota

The intratumoral microbiota is characterized by tissue-specific distribution, dynamic variability, low biomass, and complex etiological origins.

Tissue-specific distribution

Substantial heterogeneity exists in both microbial composition and abundance across different tumor types.[30] In oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), Porphyromonas predominates, often coexisting with Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum) and Prevotella.[30] Although H. pylori is an established causative agent in gastric cancer (GC), only approximately 3% of infected individuals develop malignancy. Current evidence suggests H. pylori primarily functions in tumor initiation rather than sustained progression. The GC microbiome is largely dominated by Streptococcus and Fusobacterium genera.[31] F. nucleatum, first linked to colorectal cancer (CRC) in 2011,[32] promotes tumorigenesis through multiple mechanisms, with abundance correlating positively with disease severity. Additional CRC-associated microorganisms include Bacteroides fragilis[33] and Escherichia coli (E.coli)[34] strains. Breast cancer tissues harbor a relatively complex microbiome, primarily comprising Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteria.[35,36] Malignant tissues show significant enrichment of Methylobacterium and Sphingomonas compared to normal adjacent tissues.[37] In pancreatic cancer, Proteobacteria and Firmicutes are predominant colonizers.[38] Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) exhibits marked enrichment of γ-proteobacteria in tumors, whereas Streptococcaceae and Lactococcus are associated with HCC-related cirrhosis.[39] In lung cancer, Prevotella (Bacteroidetes) is the dominant genus, with species such as Bradyrhizobium japonicum exclusively detected in patients.[40] Prostate cancer tissues show significant enrichment of Propionibacterium acnes, implying a potential oncogenic role [Table 1].[41]

This heterogeneity reflects variations in tumorigenic mechanisms, microenvironmental characteristics, and host immune responses. TME displays cancer-specific physiological traits, including hypoxia, necrosis, specific metabolic byproducts (such as lactate[42]), and nutrient availability (such as mucins[43]) that favor colonization by metabolically adapted bacteria. For example, in GC, H. pylori colonization promotes its own enrichment through dual mechanisms; it upregulates PD-L1 expression[44] and induces Tregs infiltration along with M2 macrophage polarization,[45] thereby fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and also its urease activity generates ammonia to neutralize gastric acid, creating a pH-favorable niche while simultaneously disrupting gastric mucosal barrier integrity,[46] which further facilitates sustained bacterial colonization.

Consequently, the intratumoral microbiota is generally characterized by low diversity and dominance of specialized taxa.

Dynamic variablity

The intratumoral microbiota exhibits dynamic compositional and quantitative alterations throughout tumorigenesis and disease progression, mirroring both temporal tumor evolution and spatial heterogeneity across metastatic niches. For example, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) demonstrates up to 1000-fold bacterial enrichment compared to normal pancreatic tissue.[47] Contrastingly, breast cancer progression shows an inverse correlation between bacterial DNA load and disease advancement, highlighting its potential diagnostic utility for early-stage detection.[37]

Microbial community structure undergoes stage-specific remodeling during oncogenesis. In breast ductal carcinoma in situ, Lactobacillus spp. constitute the predominant microbiota, whereas invasive ductal carcinoma exhibits enrichment of proinflammatory taxa, including Fusobacterium, Actinobacteria, and Acetobacteraceae.[48] Prostate malignancies demonstrate increased Staphylococcaceae prevalence compared to benign tissues where Streptococcaceae dominates.[49] Patients with advanced-stage lung cancer show significant Thermus spp. proliferation, whereas metastatic lesions are characterized by Legionella (a member of the Phylum Proteobacteria) predominance.[50]

Therapeutic interventions substantially modulate intratumoral microbiota profiles. Chemotherapy promotes selection of drug-resistant microbial populations while increasing intestinal permeability, thereby facilitating gut microbiota translocation to tumor sites. Notably, in a cohort of 249 patients with advanced cancer (encompassing non-small cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and urothelial carcinoma) receiving PD-1 blockade (e.g., Nivolumab), responders exhibited 50-fold A. muciniphila expansion within 2 weeks posttreatment.[51] Parallel observations in melanoma immunotherapy revealed that Bifidobacterium abundance positively correlated with cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) inhibitor (e.g., Ipilimumab) treatment efficacy.[52] Furthermore, in PDAC, the chemotherapeutic agent gemcitabine induced intratumoral enrichment of γ-proteobacteria, which expressed cytidine deaminase, an enzyme that inactivates gemcitabine through metabolic conversion, thereby contributing to chemoresistance.[53] Beyond chemotherapy and immunotherapy, radiotherapy also exerts notable effects on the intratumoral microbial colonization. For example, in patients receiving radiotherapy for head and neck cancer, a decrease in Streptococcus and an increase in Actinobacteria have been observed.[54,55]

Low biomass

Unlike microbiota-rich niches such as the gastrointestinal tract, tumor tissues maintain remarkably low microbial biomass, a phenomenon potentially attributable to active immune surveillance within the TME. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) mediate microbial clearance through multifaceted mechanisms encompassing direct cytotoxic effects, immunomodulation, and metabolic competition.[56] Furthermore, an array of physiological barriers restricts microbial colonization in neoplastic tissues, including: (1) epithelial defenses (cutaneous, respiratory, and gastrointestinal mucosal barriers), (2) the dense fibrin–proteoglycan network of the extracellular matrix, (3) vascular endothelial barriers, and (4) specialized mucosal layers.

Current methodologies for evaluating intratumoral microbial communities integrate conventional culture-based isolation techniques, imaging-based approaches such as fluorescence in situ hybridization, and next-generation sequencing platforms, including 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene sequencing, 18S rRNA gene sequencing, and metagenomic sequencing. These multimodal approaches enable comprehensive characterization of microbial composition, taxonomy, and relative abundance within tumor specimens.

Endogenous migration

Intratumoral microbiota may originate from adjacent or anatomically connected sites, particularly those exposed to the external environment, or they may disseminate from contiguous anatomical sites, particularly those with environmental interfaces, such as the oral cavity, respiratory tract, and urogenital tract. For example, Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) from the oral cavity can migrate to and colonize OSCC or esophageal squamous cell carcinoma.[57] Furthermore, the hematogenous spread of P. gingivalis to pancreatic tissue may accelerate pancreatic carcinogenesis.[58] Similarly, gut-derived Klebsiella pneumoniae can translocate following intestinal barrier disruption (e.g., via gelatinase activity) and subsequently colonize the liver through enterohepatic circulation.[59]

Exogenous introduction

Exogenous microbial colonization may occur through environmental exposure or iatrogenic transmission. Environmental transmission involves pathogen acquisition through ingestion (food/water) or inhalation, facilitating subsequent tumor tissue colonization. Notably, H. pylori establishes gastric mucosal colonization through contaminated food or water exposure,[60] whereas airborne Mycobacterium species from soil can infect pulmonary tissues, including neoplastic lesions.[61] Iatrogenic transmission, alternatively, results from inadvertent microbial transfer during medical interventions including surgical procedures, catheterization, or biopsy. Clinical evidence demonstrates that colonoscopy procedures may translocate opportunistic pathogens such as E. coli, Clostridioides difficile, and Staphylococcus species,[62] whereas endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography has been associated with pancreatic microbiota alterations in patients with pancreatitis.[63]

Vertical transmission

Vertical transmission describes the perinatal transfer of maternal microbiota through transplacental passage, parturition, or lactation, enabling neonatal colonization of various tissues with oncogenic potential. A clinically significant example includes vertical transmission of human papillomavirus, which colonizes cervical epithelium and elevates lifetime cervical cancer risk in affected offspring.[64]

Dual Role of Intratumoral Microbiota in Cancer Development

The distinctive ecological characteristics of intratumoral microbiota, including their compositional heterogeneity, dynamic evolution, low biomass, and diverse origins, provide the foundation for their complex functional roles in cancer biology. Rather than exerting uniform effects, these intratumoral microbes exert a dual effect on tumor progression, acting as either oncogenic promoters through various mechanisms or, under specific conditions, as tumor suppressors. Elucidating this dualistic function and its determining factors is essential for deciphering the complex interplay between intratumoral microbiota and cancer.

Dual effects of inflammatory regulation

Chronic inflammation represents a fundamental pathological process in both tumor initiation and progression. Proinflammatory effects

Generally, intratumoral microbiota can activate innate and adaptive immunity, creating a procarcinogenic milieu. Chronic inflammation disrupts immune function, enabling tumor immune evasion. This state drives malignant transformation and also fosters therapeutic resistance. H. pylori cytotoxin-associated antigen A (CagA) potentiates the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway in GC.[65,66] In addition, P. gingivalis triggers a neutrophil-driven inflammation in pancreatic cancer.[58] F. nucleatum activates the Wingless-related integration site (Wnt)/β-catenin signaling pathway in CRC, promoting proliferation and invasion.[67] Additionally, the intratumoral microbiota can activate multiple signaling pathways mediated by inflammatory cytokines.[68,69] These mechanistic insights reveal promising intervention strategies for targeting proinflammatory microbes to disrupt protumor inflammation.

Anti-inflammatory effects

Emerging evidence reveals that specific intratumoral microbiota can suppress tumors through anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Notably, Lactobacillus produces lactic acid to inhibit proinflammatory cytokines while upregulating interleukin-10 (IL-10).[70] Furthermore, Propionibacterium generates anti-inflammatory molecules that modulate the TME.[71] Bifidobacterium metabolites alleviate inflammation through a Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/MyD88 and NLRP3/Caspase1 pathway.[72,73] A. muciniphila restores intestinal barrier integrity and alleviates dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis by secreting extracellular vesicles.[74]

These findings collectively establish microbial immunomodulation as a critical regulatory axis in tumor suppression. The ability of commensal microbiota to mitigate inflammatory responses within the TME presents promising therapeutic opportunities for attenuating cancer-promoting inflammation, preserving tissue barrier integrity, and restoring antitumor immunity.

Dual outcomes of immune effects

The immune system exerts dual functions in cancer, mediating tumor elimination while paradoxically supporting progression through formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Intratumoral microbiota similarly exhibits dichotomous immunoregulatory roles, promoting either immune evasion or antitumor immunity.

Immune evasion promotion

Immune evasion is a key oncogenic mechanism. This process enables malignant cells to circumvent immune recognition and destruction through multiple well-defined strategies. Intratumoral microbiota can drive immune evasion through diverse mechanisms. For example, F. nucleatum upregulates PD-L1 in CRC through alpha kinase 1 (ALPK1) activation.[75] Similarly, B. fragilis promotes the expansion of Tregs, recruiting immunosuppressive cells.[76] Certain intratumoral microbiota deplete arginine to suppress T-cell function.[77] Mycoplasma and H. pylori induce immune tolerance and immunosuppressive cytokines (IL-10 and transforming growth factor-beta [TGF-β]), facilitating Treg infiltration and immunosuppressive niche formation.[78] Lactobacillus iners correlates with poor survival and therapy resistance in cervical cancer.[79]

These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting microbiota-immune system interactions to overcome treatment resistance.

Immune response enhancement

Beyond their established role in immune evasion, intratumoral microbiota demonstrates significant capacity to potentiate antitumor immunity through multiple immunomodulatory mechanisms. Experimental evidence indicates that A. muciniphila activates C-C chemokine receptor type 9-positive (CCR9+)CD4+ T cells and promotes IL-12-dependent T helper type 1(Th1) polarization,[51] whereas its SCFA metabolites (particularly acetate) facilitate immune cell recruitment.[80] In addition, Bifidobacterium improves the function of dendritic cells (DCs) function to enhance PD-L1-based immunotherapy[81] and stimulates stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling to promote CD47-based immunotherapy.[82] In pancreatic cancer, Lactobacillus casei and Lactobacillus reuteri (L. reuteri) suppress TLR4 signaling, inhibiting cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and M2 macrophage polarization while promoting M1 macrophage polarization.[83] Additionally, specific probiotics remodel the immunosuppressive TME by modulating the numbers or functions of Tregs and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs).

Targeting the dual immunomodulatory capacity of intratumoral microbiota, which orchestrates both immune evasion and anti-tumor immunity, represents a transformative strategy to overcome immunotherapy resistance and enhance therapeutic efficacy, thereby advancing the development of next-generation microbiota-based cancer therapies.

Dual effects of genomic instability

Genomic instability, characterized by structural or numerical DNA alterations arising from replication, repair, or segregation errors, drives tumorigenesis by activating oncogenes or inactivating tumor suppressor genes. This process is involved in various malignancies, including breakpoint cluster region-Abelson murine leukemia viral oncogene homolog 1 (BCR-ABL)-driven chronic myelogenous leukemia and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-amplified breast cancer.

Promotion of genomic instability

The intratumoral microbiota disrupts the genomic stability of tumor cells through diverse mechanisms, including the production of metabolites or direct interactions with the host genome. H. pylori induces inflammation, recruiting neutrophils and macrophages that generate reactive oxygen species (ROS)/reactive nitrogen species (RNS), leading to oxidative DNA damage and strand breaks.[84] E. coli produces the genotoxic toxin colibactin, which causes double-strand breaks and mutations.[85] B. fragilis secretes enterotoxins promoting DNA methylation and genetic mutations in CRC.[86] Additionally, F. nucleatum impedes homologous recombination repair, further exacerbating genomic instability.[87] These microbial-induced disruptions in genomic stability enhance tumor aggressiveness and resistance to treatment.

Promotion of genomic stability

Conversely, specific microbiota maintains genomic integrity. For example, L. rhamnosus preserves intestinal barrier function, reducing DNA damage from environmental insults.[88,89] Certain intratumoral microbiota activate antioxidant enzymes and DNA repair pathways, lowering mutation rates and cancer risk.[23,90]

Investigating the effects of intratumoral microbiota on genomic stability could provide valuable insights into future cancer therapeutic strategies, including engineered bacteria to deliver DNA repair enzymes or the exploration of microbial metabolites in relation to DNA repair pathways.

Mechanisms of Intratumoral Microbiota in Oncogenesis

Having established the dual role of intratumoral microbiota in cancer progression, it is imperative to elucidate the underlying mechanisms through which these microbes exert their effects. Current evidence delineates four principal mechanistic pathways: direct engagement with host cell surface receptors, secretion of small-molecule metabolites, release of effector proteins, and intracellular invasion. These distinct yet interconnected strategies enable intratumoral microbiota to reprogram tumor behavior and remodel the TME, thereby orchestrating their dichotomous influence on cancer.

Intratumoral microbiota affect cancer progression through cell surface receptor binding

Intratumoral microbiota directly modulates neoplastic processes including proliferation, metastasis, and immune evasion through specific receptor-ligand interactions with both malignant and immune cells. These microbial-surface receptor engagements initiate downstream signaling cascades that profoundly influence tumor biology.

In CRC, F. nucleatum exemplifies this paradigm by engaging multiple host receptors[91]; TLR4 binding activates NF-κB/microRNA-21 (miR-21) signaling to drive proliferation,[92] and FadA-E-cadherin interaction stimulates Wnt/β-catenin-mediated inflammation and invasiveness.[93] Fusobacterium autotransporter protein 2 (Fap2) and collagen-binding protein F (CbpF) engage T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT) and carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule 1 (CEACAM1), respectively, to suppress natural killer (NK) and T cell cytotoxicity.[94,95] These interactions concurrently upregulate proinflammatory cytokines and recruit immunosuppressive populations, including MDSCs, tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs), and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), collectively reprogramming the immune microenvironment to accelerate CRC progression [Figure 3].[96]

This receptor-mediated mechanism operates across diverse malignancies. K. pneumoniae colonizes hepatic tissue and activates TLR4 on hepatoma cells through penicillin-binding protein 1B (PBP1B), promoting HCC.[59] In OSCC, P. gingivalis FimA engages TLR2/TLR4 to induce NF-κB-driven inflammation and DNA damage.[97,98] H. pylori blood group antigen-binding adhesin (BabA) binds gastric epithelial Lewis b antigens, facilitating persistent colonization and CagA-mediated gastric carcinogenesis.[99,100] In addition to bacteria, fungal constituents of the intratumoral microbiota similarly exploit host receptors. Malassezia in pancreatic cancer expresses cell wall components (β-mannans and phosphorylated polysaccharides) that activate the complement cascade through mannose-binding lectin (MBL) binding,[101,102] whereas Aspergillus sydowii utilizes surface β-glucan to engage dendritic cell-associated C-type lectin-1 (Dectin-1) in lung cancer.[103]

Collectively, these receptor-mediated interactions illustrate the diverse strategies by which intratumoral microbiota influence cancer pathogenesis across tumor types.

Acetate potentiates innate immune responses by activating free fatty acid receptor 2 (FFAR2) on neutrophils and group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s) in breast cancer models.[104,105] Butyrate exhibits concentration-dependent duality; low doses promote IL-10+ Treg expansion through G protein-coupled receptor 43 (GPR43)/histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) inhibition, whereas high doses drive Th1 differentiation through T-bet induction.[106,107]

Critically, the functional impact of intratumoral microbial metabolites is dichotomous. Conversely, specific metabolites exert tumor-suppressive effects. Akkermansia-derived SCFAs enhance immune cell recruitment and activation within the tumor niche, significantly augmenting antitumor responses.[80] Lactobacillus-derived indole-3-acetate (ILA) enhances CD8+ T-cell-mediated tumor clearance epigenetic IL-12A activation in DCs.[108] Butyrate induces apoptosis through GPR109A/NF-κB inhibition,[109] whereas Bacteroides ovatus-generated acetate promotes anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization and enhances CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity.[110] Additionally, propionate reactivates tumor suppressors through euchromatic histone lysine methyltransferase 2 (EHMT2) degradation in CRC.[111] Notably, indole-3-propionic acid (IPA) from L. johnsonii and Clostridium sporogenes preserves the function of progenitor exhausted T cells (Tpex) through TCF7 super-enhancer histone acetylation, significantly enhancing αPD-L1 immunotherapy efficacy across multiple cancers.[112]

Intratumoral microbial metabolites exert dual regulatory functions in cancer through epigenetic and immune modulation, providing promising therapeutic strategies to enhance immunotherapy and overcome treatment resistance.

Intratumoral microbiota influence cancer progression through protein secretion

Microbial-secreted proteins serve as crucial molecular mediators in host–microbe interactions, playing a fundamental role in shaping the TME. These effectors directly engage host receptors or intracellular targets while indirectly modulating oncogenesis through various pathways [Figure 4].

Various bacterial species produce distinct protein effectors that exhibit tumor-specific biological activities. H. pylori translocates CagA into gastric epithelial cells to activate the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling pathway to drive proliferation[113] and amplify rat sarcoma virus (Ras)/mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK)/ERK cascades through Grb2 interaction to promote neoplastic growth.[114] CagA also binds PAR1 kinase, disrupting cell polarity and adhesion, critical steps in gastric carcinogenesis.[115] Enterotoxigenic B. fragilis secretes Bacteroides fragilis toxin (BFT), which proteolytically cleaves E-cadherin to activate β-catenin[116] and NF-κB inflammatory pathways.[117] F. nucleatum drives Th17-mediated tumor immunity in CRC.[118] Similarly, E. coli colibactin induces DNA damage driving mutagenesis,[85] whereas uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) secretes cytotoxic necrotizing factor 1 (CNF1), which promotes angiogenesis through RhoC-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling in bladder cancer [Table 2].[119]

Intratumoral bacterial effector proteins represent critical mechanistic nodes in oncogenesis, operating through the activation of signaling cascades, modulation of inflammation, and induction of genotoxicity, thereby positioning themselves as strategic targets for microbiota-centric cancer therapies.

Intratumoral microbiota influence cancer progression through host cell invasion

Intratumoral microbiota exerts oncogenic influence through two distinct modes: extracellular residents modulate tumor biology via microenvironmental interactions, whereas invasive microorganisms penetrate host cells, proliferate intracellularly, and deliver cytoplasmic effectors. These intracellular microbes are further classified as facultative, and are capable of surviving both intra- and extracellularly, or obligate, which depend entirely on host cells for survival.

Pathogenic success is dependent on two critical processes: efficient cellular entry and immune evasion coupled with metabolic adaptation. Invasion typically initiates through ligand-receptor interactions, as exemplified by Listeria monocytogenes InlA and InlB binding E-cadherin and cellular-mesenchymal epithelial transition factor (c-Met) to induce endocytosis.[120] In contrast, Legionella pneumophila uses passive phagocytic entry by macrophages,[121,122] whereas Mycobacterium tuberculosis utilizes multiple pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) for cellular penetration.[123,124] Shigella actively invades through type III secretion system (T3SS)-mediated cytoskeletal manipulation.[125]

Upon entry, the intracellular microbiota must evade host immune clearance. Shigella escapes phagosomes through T3SS effectors, exploiting host actin for intracellular movement and intercellular spread [Table 3].[126] Similarly, Listeria secretes listeriolysin O (LLO) and phospholipases to disrupt the phagosomal membrane and enter the cytoplasm.[127]

In addition to immune evasion, nutrient acquisition represents another critical challenge; obligate intracellular Rickettsia hijack host ATP and amino acids to facilitate replication within the host cell.[128] Legionella forms an endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-like vacuole and uses the type IV secretion system (T4SS) to manipulate the host ubiquitin system for nutrient procurement.[129]

Investigation of intracellular microbiota provides crucial insights into oncogenic mechanisms and immune modulation. Although its direct role in tumorigenesis remains to be fully elucidated, intracellular microbiota hold significant promise as both previously unrecognized carcinogenic agents and valuable biological tools for dissecting host–pathogen interactions in cancer. Elucidating intracellular microbiota–host dynamics may identify novel therapeutic strategies aimed at disrupting protumor microbial activities and enhancing antitumor immunity.

Interactions between Intratumoral Microbiota

Tumor pathogenesis is not mediated by individual intratumoral microbial species in isolation, but rather emerges from complex ecological interactions among diverse microorganisms within the TME. These polymicrobial networks collectively modulate critical oncogenic processes, including tumor initiation, progression dynamics, and therapeutic responsiveness, through intricate interspecies crosstalk.

Synergistic effects

Microbial synergism manifests through several cooperative mechanisms that potentiate tumorigenesis.[130,131] The coadministration of Clostridium butyricum and A. muciniphila demonstrates multifaceted therapeutic potential in CRC, significantly alleviating colitis and enhancing the efficacy of αPD-L1 immunotherapy efficacy, which substantially improves both therapeutic efficacy and survival outcomes.[132]

Interspecies cooperation extends beyond bacterial consortia to include viral-bacterial and bacterial–fungal interactions. Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection facilitates H. pylori persistence and gastric carcinogenesis through immune suppression and epigenetic reprogramming.[133] Similarly, co-colonization of F. nucleatum and Candida enhances biofilm formation and immune evasion, thereby promoting the progression of CRC.[134] Moreover, bacterial–fungal interactions can also facilitate colorectal carcinogenesis by upregulating D-arginine and D-ornithine metabolism or activating butyrate-associated metabolic pathways.[135]

Microbial synergism is also involved in therapeutic resistance mechanisms. In pancreatic cancer, Malassezia fungi and γ-Proteobacteria (such as E. coli) consortia activate complement cascades, induce fibrotic chemoprotective barriers, directly degrade gemcitabine through cytidine deaminase, and reinforce immunosuppression through biofilm formation, collectively establishing multifactorial chemoresistance.[102]

Antagonistic effects

Beyond synergistic interactions, antagonistic effects can also occur between different intratumoral microbiota. L. rhamnosus secretes antimicrobial peptides that inhibit the colonization of F. nucleatum.[136] Probiotic lactobacilli (L. rhamnosus and L. reuteri) counteract P. gingivalis-driven pancreatic tumorigenesis.[137] Bifidobacterium suppresses F. nucleatum growth and remodels gut microbiota to combat CRC.[132] In bladder cancer, Lactobacillus competitively excludes uropathogenic E. coli through adhesion interference and secretes bacteriocins with direct antimicrobial activity.[138]

The interactions among intratumoral microbiota highlight the limitations of studying single microbe effects in isolation. These interactions show that bacteria, fungi, viruses, and other microorganisms collectively shape the tumor ecosystem through complex mechanisms. This broader view allows for a more comprehensive understanding of the ecological characteristics of the TME and its impact on tumor initiation and progression. From a diagnostic standpoint, microbial synergistic biomarkers provide greater diagnostic specificity than individual microbial markers. From a therapeutic perspective, strategies targeting the microbial community as a whole (such as the combined use of antibiotics and antifungal drugs) are more effective at disrupting the intratumoral microbial support network than targeting a single intratumoral microorganism. This approach provides a scientific foundation for developing precision anti-tumor strategies based on intratumoral microbiota modulation.

Clinical Application Potential of Intratumoral Microbiota

The expanding understanding of intratumoral microbiota has resulted in significant translational potential across the cancer continuum, from early detection to therapeutic intervention and prognostic assessment. This growing body of research is reshaping precision oncology through microbial-based strategies.

Microbial biomarkers in cancer diagnostics

The tissue-specific and tumor-type-dependent characteristics of intratumoral microbiota present novel diagnostic opportunities. F. nucleatum enrichment in CRC correlates with disease progression,[139] whereas integrative models combining serum markers (serum pepsinogen, gastrin-17, H. pylori antibodies, and proteomic markers) enhance GC detection.[140] Fecal and salivary microbiota represent potential noninvasive biomarkers for the early detection of PDAC.[141]

Advanced multi-omics platforms integrating microbial profiling of tumor specimens, fecal matter, saliva, and peripheral blood with machine learning algorithms are facilitating the development of highly specific microbial signatures. These biosignatures show promise for noninvasive cancer detection and differential diagnosis, providing improvements in both sensitivity and specificity compared to conventional biomarkers. However, clinical applicability remains limited by inter-individual heterogeneity, the absence of standardized protocols, and contamination susceptibility in low-biomass samples. Rigorous prospective validation across diverse populations is therefore imperative to establish clinical utility.

Therapeutic applications and adjuvant strategies

The therapeutic exploitation of intratumoral microbiota traces back to William Coley’s 19th-century bacterial toxin work. Despite inconsistent results and limited validation, it established a foundation for microbial-based cancer therapy.[142] The goal of this approach was to simulate an infection and stimulate the immune system. Today, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from healthy donors shows promise in enhancing immunotherapy efficacy. Despite persistent challenges, the intratumoral microbiota emerges as a highly promising target for both diagnostic and therapeutic applications in oncology, providing novel avenues for advancing precision medicine.

Microbial modulation

Probiotic and prebiotic formulations can reconfigure intratumoral microbiota to suppress pro-carcinogenic species, whereas enhancing anti-tumor immunity. Oral Bifidobacterium or Lactobacillus increase CD8+ T cell infiltration and improve PD-1 inhibitor response.[142] Beyond direct effects, targeting pro-carcinogenic bacteria is also a promising therapeutic approach. For example, the probiotic L. rhamnosus inhibits the colonization of F. nucleatum by secreting anti-microbial peptides.[136] High-fiber diets activate the GPR109A receptor to suppress colitis-associated CRC.[143] Furthermore, engineered probiotics show enhanced preclinical efficacy.[144]

Immunotherapy enhancement

Intratumoral microbial communities may enhance the effectiveness of cancer immunotherapy by modulating immune responses. FMT has emerged as a powerful strategy to overcome immunotherapy resistance, successfully restoring anti-PD-1 responsiveness in refractory melanoma cases.[145–147]

Targeted antimicrobial therapy

Targeting specific pro-carcinogenic intratumoral microbiota delays tumor progression. Nanoparticle-mediated F. nucleatum clearance restores immune checkpoint sensitivity in CRC.[143] Antibiotic removal of γ-Proteobacteria reverses gemcitabine resistance in PDAC.[53]

However, therapeutic application is confronted by substantial challenges. Probiotic efficacy is highly strain-specific and mechanistically ill-defined, limiting generalizability. FMT suffers from donor heterogeneity and ethical issues. Antimicrobial interventions have inherent risks of microbial dysbiosis and the selection of resistant pathogens. Moreover, regulatory frameworks governing live biotherapeutic products remain nascent, with unresolved issues pertaining to safety validation, manufacturing standardization, and long-term risk assessment. Overcoming these challenges will require the development of standardized protocols and the implementation of rigorously designed clinical trials.

Prognostic value of intratumoral microbiota

The composition of the intratumoral microbiota is closely linked to patient survival and the risk of recurrence. For example, an increased abundance of certain microorganisms, such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, is positively correlated with better immune responses and longer disease-free survival. In contrast, the enrichment of pro-inflammatory bacteria such as F. nucleatum suggests a higher risk of recurrence and poorer prognosis. As a result, intratumoral microbiota profiles are expected to become important tools for assessing treatment efficacy and prognostic outcomes.

However, several limitations constrain clinical application. For example, significant inter-individual variability and tumor heterogeneity complicate the establishment of universal prognostic cut-offs. Additionally, the low biomass of intratumoral microbiota renders them susceptible to contamination, potentially confounding prognostic signals. Currently, data demonstrating the utility of intratumoral microbiota as prognostic factors for clinical outcomes remain limited. Large-scale prospective cohort studies, mechanistic validation, and rigorous analytical standardization are urgently required to establish their clinical relevance.

Conclusions and Perspectives

The intratumoral microbiota, as a crucial component of the TME, has attracted significant attention in recent years. Current research has unveiled its multifaceted roles in regulating tumor initiation, progression, immune evasion, and treatment responses. However, this field is still in its early stages, and many challenges remain.

First, the origins, colonization mechanisms, and tumor-specific roles of intratumoral microbiota in different cancer types are not yet fully understood. Second, technical challenges such as sample contamination, low detection sensitivity, and inconsistent analysis methods still hinder the reproducibility and comparability of research.

In particular, microbial sequencing technologies harbor significant inherent limitations. A major concern is that conventional approaches, such as 16S rRNA gene sequencing or metagenomic sequencing, detect intratumoral microbial DNA indiscriminately. Stable DNA from dead intratumoral microbes can persist in tumor tissues long after death; thus, DNA detection does not confirm the presence of viable, metabolically active intratumoral microbiota. Moreover, dormant intratumoral microbes, despite minimal metabolic activity, retain detectable DNA, potentially obscuring signals from actively proliferating pathogens. Although emerging techniques, including isotope labeling, culture-based isolation, high-resolution electron microscopy, and spatial metabolomics (e.g., mass spectrometry imaging), are beginning to mitigate these issues, comprehensive and rigorous metabolic mapping of intratumoral microbiota across diverse cancer types remains an ongoing challenge.

Furthermore, much of the current research remains at the correlational level, with limited exploration of causal mechanisms. Future studies should integrate cutting-edge technologies such as single-cell sequencing, multi-omics integration, and spatial omics to systematically analyze the composition and functional networks of the intratumoral microbiota. In terms of clinical translation, large-scale, multi-center, prospective population studies are needed to validate the reliability and stability of intratumoral microbiota as diagnostic, therapeutic, and prognostic markers. Additionally, the safety, efficacy, and individual variability of microbiota-based therapies still require further evaluation and optimization.

The integration of intratumoral microbiota profiling into precision oncology frameworks holds tremendous potential to revolutionize cancer diagnosis, treatment stratification, and outcome prediction. With ongoing technological advancements and collaborative research efforts, microbial-based approaches are poised to become fundamental tools in our therapeutic arsenal against cancer.
