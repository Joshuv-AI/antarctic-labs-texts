Relationship between hemoglobin glycation index and all-cause mortality in patients with acute myocardial infarction: based on MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Lin Yu, Haizhu Chen, Jiwen Zhang, Wei Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Hemoglobin Glycation Index (HGI) assesses the discrepancy between actual and estimated Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels. Research exploring the connection between HGI and negative outcomes in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is notably lacking. The current study seeks to examine how HGI is related to the likelihood of death in people suffering from AMI.MethodsData on individuals suffering from AMI originated in the MIMIC-IV database. A proportional hazards regression model using the Cox method was applied to investigate the association between HGI and mortality. Furthermore, an investigation into the dose-response relationship between HGI and mortality was carried out using restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis. To confirm the reliability of our results, subgroup analyses were conducted across different variables.ResultsThe research involved a group of 3,972 patients who were critically ill and had been diagnosed with AMI. A fully adjusted Cox regression analysis revealed a significant association between lower HGI levels and increased mortality rates at both 30-day and 365-day follow-ups. Furthermore, higher HGI levels were also linked to an increased risk of mortality within the first 30 days. An RCS analysis detected a U-shaped relationship connecting HGI to mortality rates at the 30-day and 365-day time intervals. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests further substantiated these findings, demonstrating that the association held true across various patient subgroups.ConclusionAmong critically ill AMI patients, a marked link emerged between HGI levels and all-cause mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01961-9.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are a major cause of global mortality. Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) significantly contributes to the elevated death rates associated with CVD [1, 2]. AMI is characterized by the necrosis of cardiac muscle tissue. This necrosis results from prolonged periods of ischemia, which is a deficiency of blood supply and oxygen [3]. This disorder is strongly linked to intense illness, elevated death rates, and an overall unfavorable outlook [4]. There has been progress in reperfusion methods and the use of additional drug-based treatments. However, those afflicted by AMI still encounter frequent later cardiovascular incidents and death [5, 6].

Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) is commonly acknowledged as a trustworthy marker for evaluating extended blood glucose regulation. This metric shows the mean blood glucose levels across 8 to 12 weeks, a factor vital for tracking glucose control over an extended span [7]. The ability of HbA1c to predict results in AMI patients is firmly confirmed. Newly published research indicates that fluctuations in HbA1c readings are substantially impacted by blood glucose concentrations. Additionally, HbA1c levels are affected by individual biological factors and environmental influences [8].

In 2002, Hempe and colleagues presented the Hemoglobin Glycation Index (HGI) in order to determine variations among individuals regarding their HbA1c levels. The HGI was calculated as the difference between measured HbA1c and predicted HbA1c, estimated from fasting blood glucose (FBG) using a linear regression model. The estimated HbA1c was determined using the FBG measurement [9]. The research showed that HbA1c levels were elevated in the group with a high HGI. This finding implies that individuals in this group may experience greater glycemic variability. Consequently, the HGI could potentially serve as an indicator of glycemic variability to some extent [10]. Research has shown that HGI is a dependable indicator of chronic illnesses, such as complications related to type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) coronary atherosclerosis, and fatty liver disease [11, 12]. Furthermore, it has been shown that the HGI also predicts the response to glucose-lowering medications [13].

The connection between HGI and adverse outcomes among AMI sufferers is still ambiguous. This study aims to fill this gap by investigating the relationship between the HGI and all-cause mortality in patients with AMI.

Methods

Data sources

The data for this investigation were retrieved from the MIMIC-IV 3.1 database, a widely available and extensive collection created to support clinical research in the domains of emergency and critical care medicine. The dataset mainly comprises data from the intensive care units (ICU) of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC), spanning the years 2008 to 2022. The dataset covers an extensive array of information, such as fundamental patient demographics, vital signs, lab test outcomes, procedure specifics, medication records, diverse scoring metrics, and mortality statistics [14]. Those conducting the study secured the required credentials to gain entry to the database, then pulled out the pertinent factors (certification number: 13874481). MIMIC is a public database that maintains anonymity and follows review board guidelines, ensuring that all personal data has been de-identified and that individual patients are identified solely by random codes. The Institutional Review Board at BIDMC conducted a thorough examination and confirmed the accuracy of the patient data gathering process as well as the development of research materials. The board also approved an exemption from obtaining informed consent.

Study population

In our analysis, we included ICU patients who were admitted with a diagnosis of AMI, which was identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes 9 (410) and 10 (I21, I22). For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only data from their first hospital stay were included in the study. The criteria for excluding patients are outlined as follows: (1) A hospitalization in the ICU lasting under 24 hours, (2) absence of FBG or HbA1c data, (3) presence of significant comorbidities, including cirrhosis, end-stage renal disease, or cancer, (4) records indicating abnormal survival times (Abnormal survival time” was defined as survival < 1 day from ICU admission to death (implausible because ICU stay ≥ 24 h)). Data extraction

Data were extracted using Structured Query Language (SQL) with PostgreSQL (version 17.1) and Navicat Premium (version 17.0.8). This study considered a range of potential confounders, including: (1) demographic characteristics such as age, gender, and race, (2) vital signs including heart rate and respiratory rate (RR), (3) comorbid conditions such as hypertension, heart failure (HF), atrial fibrillation (AF), sepsis, diabetes, cirrhosis, end-stage renal disease, and cancer, (4) medications including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB), beta-blockers, diuretics, and insulin, (5) interventions such as continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), (6) laboratory values including white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), platelet count (PLT), hemoglobin, sodium, potassium, creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and (7) disease severity scores, including the Acute Physiology Score III (APS III), Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), sepsis-organ failure assessment score (SOFA), Glasgow Coma Score (GCS), and Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II). The analysis omitted variables that had over 20% missing data. For continuous variables with a missing rate below 20%, multiple imputations were performed using the ‘mice’ package in R with the missForest method. Five imputations were conducted, and a random seed of 2024 was set to ensure the consistency of results.

Clarification of study parameters and result events

A linear model was constructed to explore the connection of FBG with HbA1c using data from all study subjects. This model calculated the predicted HbA1c levels based on the equation (estimated HbA1c = 0.009 × FBG + 5.185). FBG refers to the fasting blood glucose level measured in mmol/L, and the estimated HbA1c is expressed as a percentage. The HGI was determined by the gap between the actual HbA1c levels and the forecasted values. Specifically, HGI = Actual HbA1c - Estimated HbA1c [15]. Figure 2 illustrates the correlation between HGI and HbA1c levels.

The main goal of this study was to evaluate all-cause mortality within 30 days following patient enrollment. Additionally, the study aimed to examine all-cause mortality over the subsequent 365 days following admission.

Statistical analysis

The participants were segmented into four categories according to their Quartiles of HGI: Q1 (n = 993, HGI ≤ −0.73), Q2 (n = 991, −0.73 < HGI ≤ −0.30), Q3 (n = 995, −0.30 < HGI ≤ 0.46), and Q4 (n = 993, HGI > 0.46).

At the start of the examination, tests for normality were carried out on the continuous measures. Since all continuous variables in this study were found to have a non-normal distribution, the data were reported as median (interquartile range). Group comparisons for these variables were made using the Kruskal-Wallis H-test. For categorical variables, the outcomes were displayed as counts (percentages), and chi-square analyses were employed to assess variations between groups.

In order to mitigate multicollinearity, Any variables that had a variance inflation factor (VIF) above 5 were omitted from the analysis. The hazard ratios (HR) for HGI as a predictor of outcome events were estimated using Cox regression models, with the Q2 group as the reference category. The Cox regression model adjusted for potential confounding factors, including age, gender, hypertension, diabetes, HF, AF, sepsis, WBC, RBC, PLT, blood sodium and potassium levels, use of ACEI/ARB, β-blockers, diuretics, insulin, CRRT, and severity scores such as SOFA, APS III, and SAPS II. To explore the link between HGI and outcomes, trend tests were additionally carried out. Linking HGI to outcome events was evaluated using restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves, and a model with a threshold response was created to determine the critical point of HGI. Subgroup analyses were performed to confirm the robustness of the findings. R Studio (version 4.4.2) was utilized to conduct all the analyses. A two-tailed p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of patients’ baseline information

A total of 3,972 patients with severe AMI were enrolled in the study, with a median age of 70 years (interquartile range: 62 to 79). Of these, 2,604 were male and 1,368 were female. During the 30-day tracking period, there were 461 patient deaths. Over the 365-day tracking period, the number of fatalities reached 822. The participants were segmented into four categories according to their Quartiles of HGI: Q1 (n = 993, HGI ≤ −0.73), Q2 (n = 991, −0.73 < HGI ≤ −0.30), Q3 (n = 995, −0.30 < HGI ≤ 0.46), and Q4 (n = 993, HGI > 0.46). The mortality rate was lowest in Q2 and higher in Q1 and Q4. Table 1 presents the initial data, which are categorized by HGI quartiles. The group with a high HGI was more susceptible to conditions such as diabetes, HF, elevated BUN levels, and had higher APSIII scores. Relative to the low HGI group, they experienced more frequent treatment with ACEI/ARB, diuretics, and insulin. Conversely, the low HGI group was more prone to sepsis, required CRRT, and exhibited increased levels of WBC, PLT, and hemoglobin, along with higher SOFA and OASIS scores. The distribution of survivors (in blue) is broader and has a higher peak, whereas that of non-survivors (in red) is narrower, mainly clustered between 0 and 2(Supplementary Figure S1).

Survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis curves were used to evaluate the occurrence of the main outcome. The subjects were categorized by HGI quartiles(Fig. 3). The Q1 group showed a notably increased 30-day mortality rate (P = 0.010) in comparison to the other groups(Fig. 3a). Moreover, the one-year mortality rate was substantially raised in the Q1 and Q4 groups relative to the Q2 group (P = 0.006). The study’s outcomes imply that patients with AMI face a less favorable long-term outlook when their HGI levels are either too high or too low(Fig. 3b).

Correlation of HGI with outcome events

In the Q2 group (−0.73 < HGI ≤ −0.30), the incidence of mortality was the lowest among the compared groups. Subsequently, We constructed a Cox regression model to analyze the connection linking HGI to outcomes, using the Q2 group as the comparator.

Our investigation showed that in the unadjusted Cox regression models, the Q1 group (HGI < −0.73) had a significant link with primary outcome events (HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.20–2.04, P = 0.001) as well as secondary outcomes (HR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.11–1.66, P = 0.003). In fully adjusted Cox regression analyses, HGI was linked to mortality rates at both 365 days (HR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.05–1.58, P = 0.015) and 30 days (HR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.09–1.88, P = 0.010). Furthermore, The Q4 group (HGI > 0.46) was found to have a significant association with 30-day mortality(HR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.03–1.95, P = 0.031), as revealed by the analysis. However, No significant correlation was detected between the Q4 group and 365-day mortality(Table 2).

Through the application of RCS curve analysis, we uncovered a nonlinear link connecting HGI to overall mortality among patients with AMI. The assessment revealed a U-shaped link connecting HGI levels with 30-day mortality, as shown in Fig. 4a. A similar pattern was observed in the incidence of death at 365 days, as shown in Fig. 4b.

Within the threshold impact examination, key values for HGI were pinpointed: −0.77 relating to deaths occurring within 30 days, and − 0.72 concerning the 365-day death rate (Table 3). If HGI dropped beneath − 0.77, a one-unit rise in this index corresponded to a 20% reduction in the chance of dying from any cause within 30 days. When HGI went above − 0.77, a one-unit gain in this measure was connected to a 10% greater chance of death from any cause in 30 days(HR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.01–1.21, p = 0.029). For HGI values below − 0.72, each one-unit increase in HGI was associated with an 18% decrease in the risk of all-cause mortality over 365 days (HR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.73–0.92, p < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis

A subgroup examination was carried out by us to find out if typical concurrent illnesses alter how HGI and death rates at 30 and 365 days are linked in AMI patients. The analysis controlled for variables including age, gender, diabetes, hypertension, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and sepsis (Fig. 5). No notable interactions were found between HGI and these subgroups, as all interaction p-values went beyond 0.05. This indicates that the link between HGI and mortality rates at 30 and 365 days is stable across different subgroups of patients with AMI.

These results are largely consistent with our initial analysis, indicating that our conclusions hold robustness under different imputation strategies(Table 4).

Discussion

A total of 3,972 individuals with severe AMI were included in the study, with data sourced from the MIMIC-IV database. The examination showed that in AMI patients, a lower HGI had a marked link to greater overall death rates within 365 days, but a higher HGI displayed no such connection. Moreover, the 30-day mortality rate was strongly associated with both low and high HGI levels. Through RCS analysis, it was found that overall mortality incidence has a U-shaped relationship with HGI. The critical thresholds were found to be −0.77 for 30-day mortality and − 0.72 for 365-day mortality. These results imply that HGI might act as an independent prognostic factor for death risk among critically ill individuals suffering from AMI.

AMI is frequently observed in patients with diabetes mellitus, underscoring its substantial role as a contributing factor to this condition. In our analysis comparing patients with and without T2DM, we discovered that those who experienced AMI and had T2DM had a significantly worse prognosis [16–19]. Cosentino et al. reported that among individuals with STEMI, the in-hospital mortality rate was significantly higher in patients with diabetes mellitus compared to those without diabetes [16]. Karayiannides et al. demonstrated that patients with diabetes mellitus experienced higher rates of recurrent cardiovascular events and mortality following PCI [17]. Baviera et al. emphasized that the duration of diabetes mellitus is directly associated with increased mortality rates in patients hospitalized with AMI [18]. In a community cohort study, Foraker et al. found that patients with diabetes mellitus had significantly lower survival rates following myocardial infarction [19]. Our results align with Malmberg et al. [20], showing that newly identified glucose issues, like impaired glucose tolerance and newly diagnosed diabetes, significantly predict long-term cardiovascular events and mortality in AMI patients. This highlights the necessity of recognizing and addressing glucose disturbances in this group of patients. HbA1c serves as an essential indicator for tracking blood glucose management in diabetic patients, representing the mean glucose concentrations over the preceding 8 to 12 weeks. Research has indicated that HbA1c amounts are able to pinpoint people in danger of developing diabetes and act as a sign of cardiovascular disease risk [21]. Numerous research trials have explored the connection between HbA1c levels and cardiovascular events [22, 23], emphasizing its predictive ability for cardiovascular risk. The amounts of HbA1c are affected by various elements, such as how long red blood cells live.

A robust connection between blood glucose fluctuations and various adverse outcomes has been confirmed by extensive research. In a comprehensive meta-analysis, Chen et al. illustrated that increased glycemic variability significantly elevates the risk of mortality and a spectrum of complications in diabetic patients [24]. In the case of severely ill people suffering from cerebrovascular conditions, shifts in blood sugar amounts have a strong link to a higher chance of changed mental states and in-hospital deaths [25]. Patients with cardiovascular disease who experience significant blood sugar fluctuations are at a higher risk of serious cardiovascular events and increased mortality [26, 27]. In ICU patients, blood glucose fluctuations are linked to an increased likelihood of ventricular arrhythmias and in-hospital fatality [28].

Stress hyperglycaemia is described by Marik & Bellomo as an adaptive neuro-endocrine response. It may enhance survival in critical illness [29]. However, admission hyperglycaemia is still an independent predictor of both short- and long-term morbidity and mortality after AMI. This is true for both diabetic and non-diabetic patients [30]. Monnier et al. further demonstrate that acute glycaemic excursions activate oxidative stress more potently than sustained hyperglycaemia. These excursions are quantified by MAGE [31]. Furthermore, population data from Eskesen et al. show that HbA1c values in the upper-normal range are linked to cardiovascular disease. These values are also associated with future diabetes [32].

Our results support a U-shaped association between HGI and mortality, consistent with previous studies. For example, a study of 18,285 participants found that before the inflection point, a 1-unit increase in HGI reduced mortality by 24%, but after the inflection point, the same increase raised mortality by 17% [33]. Similar patterns were seen in populations with prediabetes, diabetes, hypertension, and metabolic syndrome [34–37]. A study of 8,257 individuals with MASLD identified a threshold at HGI − 0.0564, beyond which mortality risk rose [38]. The distinct inflection points observed in our study, which are − 0.77 and − 0.72, may be due to critical illness or comorbidities that affect the AMI population differently. A reduced HGI may signify a slower rate of HbA1c formation, which could prolong the lifespan of hemoglobin. This extended lifespan can increase hemoglobin’s vulnerability to oxidation and elevate blood viscosity, thereby potentially raising the risk of cardiovascular thrombosis [39]. The lack of association between high HGI and 365-day mortality may be due to the acute effects of hyperglycemia dominating short-term outcomes, while long-term mortality may be influenced by other factors not captured in this study.

In fully adjusted Cox regression models, high HGI was significantly correlated with 30-day mortality. However, no such association was found between high HGI and 365-day mortality. This difference might be due to the significant rise in blood glucose levels caused by acute stress in critically ill patients [40]. Acute hyperglycemic states are linked to inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired endothelial function, which can result in myocardial injury. Furthermore, acute hyperglycemia augments inflammation, triggers the release of vasoconstrictors, prolongs the QT interval, diminishes ischemic preconditioning, and exacerbates the no-reflow phenomenon [41]. Worthley et al. have extensively explored the cellular mechanisms underlying the myocardial ‘no-reflow’ phenomena, highlighting the critical role of microvascular dysfunction in AMI outcomes [42]. In non-diabetic ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) patients, stress hyperglycemia is associated with an increased incidence of cardiogenic shock, contrast-induced nephropathy, and mortality [43]. Hyperglycemia may also be associated with sympathetic overactivity and hyperglycemia-mediated pro-inflammatory pathways, which can adversely impact prognosis. Inadequate blood glucose management can lead to detrimental clinical effects by exacerbating endothelial damage, reducing platelet nitric oxide sensitivity, increasing microvascular obstruction, and contributing to additional vascular injury due to hyperglycemia [44]. A high HGI may indicate a chronic state of hyperglycemia as well as the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), both of which can lead to vascular damage, inflammation, and oxidative stress, thereby increasing the risk of cardiovascular events and long-term mortality [45, 46]. Chronic hyperglycemia may adversely affect patient prognosis through the phenomenon of long-term metabolic memory [47]. Metabolic memory refers to the persistent response of cells to a previous state of hyperglycemia, which can persist even after blood glucose levels have returned to normal [48]. A higher HGI indicates metabolic instability characterized by insulin resistance, glucose fluctuations, disrupted energy metabolism, and oxidative stress [49]. The risk of arterial plaque development may be heightened due to endothelial dysfunction, which can result from the interaction between insulin resistance and oxidative damage.

An extremely low HGI may be indicative of significant metabolic disturbances or a shortened erythrocyte lifespan [50, 51], potentially compromising oxygen delivery to tissues and resulting in tissue hypoxia and organ dysfunction. Furthermore, a low HGI may be associated with oxidative stress, which can cause oxidative damage to proteins, lipids, and DNA, potentially leading to cellular dysfunction [45, 52]. Oxidative stress, by increasing the production of free radicals, can initiate chronic inflammation, damage the vascular endothelium, and accelerate atherosclerosis [53] —key processes in the development of cardiovascular disease. Consequently, through these mechanisms, a low HGI may be correlated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, impacting individual survival rates. Given that AMI is a severe cardiovascular event with high mortality rates, the compounded effects of low HGI, including metabolic disturbances, shortened erythrocyte lifespan, and increased oxidative stress, could substantially elevate the risk of adverse outcomes, especially mortality, among AMI patients. Therefore, low HGI may be intricately correlated with higher mortality rates in AMI patients through these multifaceted mechanisms.

Our study acknowledges several limitations. Firstly, missing data on echocardiography (e.g., left ventricular ejection fraction) and inflammatory markers (e.g., C-reactive protein) may have limited our ability to assess the full spectrum of cardiovascular risk factors, potentially underestimating the prognostic role of HGI. Secondly, since this study was conducted retrospectively at a single center, it is not feasible to establish a causal link between HGI and mortality in AMI patients. Thirdly, the database failed to include infarct size, which is a major determinant of short- and long-term outcomes post AMI. This omission may affect the comprehensiveness of our findings. Fourthly, while we adjusted for insulin use in the fully adjusted Cox regression model, we did not specifically consider the dosage of insulin use, which is an important aspect. Lastly, extensive missing data prevented us from incorporating additional metrics like the coefficient of variation (CV) of glucose into our analysis.

Additionally, this study did not include important variables such as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), continuous glucose monitoring data, and troponin levels. The absence of these variables may introduce potential bias in the calculation of HGI and its association with mortality. For instance, eGFR is a key indicator for assessing renal function, and its omission could affect the accuracy of HGI calculation, as renal impairment may influence the metabolism of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) [54]. Furthermore, the lack of continuous glucose monitoring data limits the ability to assess glucose variability comprehensively, which is a significant component of HGI. Troponin is an essential biomarker for the severity of acute myocardial infarction (AMI), and its exclusion may hinder a thorough evaluation of mortality-related factors. Future research should strive to conduct more meticulously planned, multicenter, multiethnic, large-scale prospective studies to confirm the results of this investigation.

Conclusion

In patients with severe AMI, this study identified a U-shaped association between HGI and overall mortality, highlighting its significance for 30-day mortality in particular. Close monitoring is advised for patients with low HGI, as it may function as a crucial prognostic marker for critically ill AMI patients. Moreover, HGI may emerge as a valuable measure for differentiating mortality risk across short-term and long-term horizons. Further comprehensive studies are essential to validate these observations.
