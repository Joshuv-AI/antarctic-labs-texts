Hepatoprotective effects of silibinin and chlorogenic acid standardised extract to alleviate the fatty liver syndrome and improve blood indices in caged-laying hens after the peak of production
Authors: Alessandro Guerrini, Luisa Vera Muscatello, Francesca del Zozzo, Valeria Harper, Lorenzo Fiorini, Ivonne Laura Archetti, Valentina Serra, Claire Carlu, Thibaut Chabrillat, Doriana Eurosia Angela Tedesco
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study was designed to assess the effectiveness of a mixture of Silybum marianum L. and Cynara cardunculus scolymus L. standardised extract (PHYTO-LAYER™) expressed as silibinin (2.4 g/L) and chlorogenic acid (2.2 g/L) content, respectively, provided intermittently to caged-laying hens to alleviate fatty liver syndromes, improve blood indices and health. In this study, 792 Lohmann LSL-White hens (41 weeks of age), randomly assigned to 2 replicated groups, control and treated (n = 396 each), were used. The treatment (1 mL/L) was supplied every 2 weeks for 7 days, at different time points (T0-T5) in a 7-week trial, by drinking water. At T0, T3 and T5, 13 identified hens/group were sampled for haematological analysis. At T5, all 13 hens per group were euthanised via cervical dislocation for organ weight, gut pH, and liver histological investigations. The treatment improved red blood cell, haemoglobin, hematocrit, and the serum antioxidant activity (P < 0.05). In treated hens, a reduction of liver weight and serum alanine aminotransferase activity was evidenced, associated with a reduction of liver steatosis cases and severity, evaluated histologically (P < 0.05). Other blood and serum indices were not affected by the treatment (P > 0.05). At T5, in treated hens, the duodenum (proximal tract) showed unexpectedly higher pH, compared to the control hens (P < 0.05). This study showed that the combined phytoextracts administration is useful in improving the liver health of caged-laying hens affected by liver disorders, reducing liver steatosis, associated with an upgrade of main blood indices.Keywords: Botanicals phytoextract, Bioactive compounds, Laying hen health, Liver steatosis, Hepatoprotective activity

Introduction

Traditional genetic selection programs target the realisation of high-feed and production-efficiency laying hen lines, thus allowing the extension of their production cycle, obtaining maximum egg production (Bain et al., 2016). High productivity and efficiency increase the risk of animals experiencing health and welfare problems due to high metabolic stress (Settar et al., 1999). Added to this, previous studies have also identified several disease problems associated with the type of layer housing breeding system (Fossum et al., 2009; Rodenburg et al., 2005; Shimmura et al., 2010), in particular in caged-laying hens (Shini et al., 2018).

The liver tissue of laying hens is responsible for a significant amount of fat synthesis (also de novo) and metabolization compared to adipose tissue in other species (Laliotis et al., 2010). Notably, due to the progressive ageing (> 60 weeks of age) and long production time of hens, the liver can be exposed to varying degrees of lipid deposition. Excessive liver lipid accumulation (i.e., mainly triglycerides) can lead to hepatocyte death and cause liver dysfunction (Yao et al., 2016). Moreover, the hens' ageing has been associated with an increased risk of imbalance in redox status and oxidative stress (Wang et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2023). Oxidative stress has also been identified as a significant contributor to liver damage, lipid accumulation through complex pathways (Li et al., 2015). Among the main liver diseases caused by this metabolic disorder, hepatic steatosis and fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS) are the most common non-infectious, and usually asymptomatic diseases frequently observed in laying hens at the peak of production (∼26–28 weeks of age) or after (40–60 weeks of age) (Gretarsson et al., 2023; Shini et al., 2018; Trott et al., 2014). Furthermore, FLHS is mostly observed in caged-laying hens, which causes sudden mortality associated with high egg production. In most cases, FLHS results from a substantial worsening of hepatic steatosis, although these two pathological conditions are substantially different (Diaz et al., 1994). Among the most prominent features, hepatic steatosis is characterised by excessive liver fat accumulation as lipid droplets in hepatocyte cells, which results from impaired lipolysis and excessive lipogenesis, while FLHS, which has similarities to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) or non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) in humans, is accompanied by severe hepatic steatosis, autophagy, apoptosis and haemorrhages of different grades and severity (Butler, 1976; Cheng et al., 2022; Zhuang et al., 2019). In both cases, their pathogenesis can be correlated to multifactorial causes (e.g. nutritional, metabolic, hormonal, genetic, etc), which negatively condition the lipid and liver metabolism, causing oxidative stress (Gao et al., 2019; Peng et al., 2019; Trott et al., 2014). Therefore, it is important to find valid strategies, including nutritional ones, to prevent them during the most critical laying hens’ production phases.

A great deal of scientific evidence indicates that liver injury-alleviating or preventive effects can be exerted by some medicinal plants such as milk thistle, Silybum marianum L. (Gaertn.) and artichoke, Cynara cardunculus scolymus L. (Hayek), authorised as feed additives, and successfully used also in poultry (Alhidary et al., 2017; Tedesco et al., 2004; Zaker-Esteghamati et al., 2021). The milk thistle is a main source of silymarin, a flavonolignan complex extracted from its seeds, where silybin, or silibinin (SIL, 2,3-Dehydrosilybin), is the major and most bioactive compound with well-documented antioxidant, hepatoprotective and hepatic-regeneration properties (Flora et al., 1998; Franz et al., 2007; Saller et al., 2007; Vargas-Mendoza et al., 2014). With complex mechanisms to maintain cellular integrity, silymarin's direct scavenging capabilities on cells prevent the free radicals, inhibiting the reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, involving also the cytochromes P450 activity, supporting the correct functionality of the mitochondrial metabolic activity (Surai, 2015; Wu et al., 2023). The literature reports that silymarin can modulate some physiological processes and enhance the overall health of laying hens and broiler chickens (Tedesco & Guerrini, 2023; Wang et al., 2020). In laying hens, silymarin shows hypoglycemic and cholesterol-lowering effects, reduces the liver enzyme activities (i.e. AST and ALT lowering activities), and lipid peroxidation products, such as malondialdehyde (MDA). Similarly, artichoke is known for its choleretic, diuretic, cholesterol-reducing, and hepatoprotective action (Rondanelli et al., 2013; Sahebkar et al., 2018), primarily attributed to caffeoylquinic acid compounds, such as chlorogenic acid (CGA) and cynarin (Bombardelli et al., 1977). The CGA (3-O-Caffeoylquinic acid), a bioactive polyphenol, is the most abundant caffeoylquinic acid isomer (commonly called coffee tannic acid or coffee ellagic acid), which shows strong antioxidant activity with protective effects on cell survival (Dkhil et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2018; Rashidi et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023), and lipid-lowering properties (decrease in serum and liver cholesterol-CL, and triglycerides-TG) observed in mice (Wang et al., 2012). Its anti-inflammatory potential has been well documented by in vivo and in vitro studies (Liang & Kitts, 2015). In poultry, studies reported that the CGA administration in broiler chickens alleviated diquat-induced OS and hepatic inflammation (Zha et al., 2023) and, in aged laying hens, enhanced serum antioxidant capacity (Bi et al., 2024). Studies also reported that the supplementation of CGA from artichoke improves poultry growth performance, decreases blood CL with a reduction of abdominal fat, has positive effects on liver health, and improves the serum protein profile (Kleessen et al., 2003; Zaker-Esteghamati et al., 2020). Even if the hepatoprotective and antioxidant effects of SIL or CGA are known, the potential effects of their association administration have been poorly studied. Therefore, with a view to improving laying hens’ health, this study aimed to assess if this association of standardised extracts could be an effective strategy for reducing or preventing fatty liver syndromes and liver damage in caged-laying hens after the peak of production (40 weeks of age), jointly with an upgrade of blood indices.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

This study was reviewed and authorised by the Ethical Committee of the University of Milan and the Italian Ministry of Health, with experimental protocol No. 766/2023-PR. All procedures involving live bird handling, management, and health care followed the regulations of animals used for scientific purposes, and raised in industrial farm conditions (European Union Directive 2010/63/EU). All animal experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Blended complementary feed tested

The study was designed to evaluate the intermittent administration by drinking water of an authorised complementary feed (PHYTO-LAYER™, provided by Phytosynthese, France). The product, administered at a dose of 1 mL/L, was based on an association of milk thistle (Silybum marianum L., CoE: 551) seed extracts and artichoke (Cynara cardunculus scolymus L. CoE: 565) leaves water standardised extracts (Regulation (EC) No. 1831/2003), expressed as content of silibinin (SIL, 2.4 g/L) and chlorogenic acid (CGA, 2.2 g/L), respectively.

Farm characteristics and management

A commercial caged-laying hen farm was selected for the field study to allow the use of enriched cages as treatment replicates. The farm included 2 nearby sheds, equally oriented and structured, with hens raised in enriched cages, in full-laying (egg production rate above 90 %). The typical cage battery consisted of 4 rows of 5-decker cages, and the single cages were placed on the right and left sides of each row and decker. Each cage housed a variable number of hens (25 to 26/cage), with a nest, scratching area, linear feeder, 6 nipple-type drinkers, and 3 plastic perches (20 cm/hen). All animals were maintained in identical management and commercial conditions and exposed to a standard lighting program (16L:8D, 20 lux of light intensity), level of NH3 and CO2 maintained at < 20 ppm, and < 3000 ppm, respectively, with environmental temperatures between 20 °C and 26 °C, and ∼60 % of relative humidity during the entire trial. The water and feed distribution line was separated and independent between the 2 sheds. To ensure a homogeneous, continuous and ad libitum water administration, the automatic Dosatron system (pulse dosing-pump, SmartDosing© D25AL5N, Dosatron, USA) was used.

Experimental design, animals and treatment

The experimental design is illustrated in Fig. 1. The laying hens raised were from the same hatchery, housed at the same time (14 weeks of age), and vaccinated according to the farm vaccination protocol. A total of 792 Lohmann LSL-White healthy laying hens (1.667 ± 0.13 kg/body weight-BW), non-beak-trimmed, and from the same commercial flock, upon reaching the 41st week of age, were randomly chosen from the 2 sheds and assigned to 2 replicated experimental groups, control and treated (396 hens/each). Each experimental group had 15 replicate cages (25 to 26 hens/each) randomly identified and alternated among the different rows and decker cages of the shed. A total of 13 hens (1 hen/cage, established based on preliminary statistical Power Analysis) were randomly identified in each experimental group by irremovable numbered rings, to sample and evaluate the same animals at each time point of the trial. The treatment was administered intermittently at a dose of 1 mL/L by drinking water during the 7-week trial, subdivided into 5 experimental time points (T0 to T5, Fig. 1), as follows:

-T0: 1st day of the start of the 41st week, before the start of the treatment;

T0: 1st day of the start of the 41st week, before the start of the treatment;

-T1: 41st week (7 days), first treatment administration (0.5 mL/L only the first day of the treatment to accustom the animals to any changes in the water taste and not to compromise the water intake and egg production);

T1: 41st week (7 days), first treatment administration (0.5 mL/L only the first day of the treatment to accustom the animals to any changes in the water taste and not to compromise the water intake and egg production);

-T2: 42nd to the 43rd week (14 days), first withdrawal period;

T2: 42nd to the 43rd week (14 days), first withdrawal period;

-T3: 44th week (7 days), second treatment administration;

T3: 44th week (7 days), second treatment administration;

-T4: 45th to the 46th week (14 days), second withdrawal period;

T4: 45th to the 46th week (14 days), second withdrawal period;

-T5: 47th week to the first day of the 48th week of age until the end of the trial (7 days), third and latest treatment administration.

T5: 47th week to the first day of the 48th week of age until the end of the trial (7 days), third and latest treatment administration.

Experimental design of the study.

At the end of the trial (T5), the 13 identified hens per group were euthanised via manual cervical dislocation for post-mortem organs (liver and gut) inspection, and histological investigations. Blood samples were also taken at the same time. During the experimental period, all hens were fed ad libitum with the same commercial basal diet to meet the requirements for the Lohmann LSL-White genetic line (www.lohmann-breeders.com). The composition and nutrient levels of the diet are shown in Table 1. The BW (kg-1) was recorded at different time points to monitor the weight trend, weighing the hens individually using a digital balance. The mortality rate (%) was recorded daily for each experimental group.

Blood sampling

Blood samples were collected from the same 13 identified hens per group. At T0 and at the end of the first withdrawal period (T3), 2.5 mL of blood was drawn from the brachial vein using a sterile 23 G (0.60 mm) needle. At the end of the trial (T5), 10 mL of blood was collected from the jugular vein cutting immediately after euthanisation by manual cervical dislocation. After each collection, an aliquot of 0.5 mL of the blood sample was placed in K3-EDTA tubes (APTACA, Canelli, Italy) for the hemochromocytometric evaluation, stored at 5 ± 3 °C and sent immediately to the laboratory of the Istituto Zooprofilattico Sperimentale della Lombardia e dell’Emilia Romagna (IZSLER) for the analysis. The remaining blood aliquot was kept in serum glass tubes without anticoagulant (BD Vacutainer, Becton Dickinson, Milan, Italy) to obtain the serum after centrifugation at 2,500 x g at 4 °C for 15 min, and subsequently stored in 5 mL plastic vials at -20 °C until analyses of serum biochemical indices, performed at IZSLER laboratory. An aliquot of serum was stored for total antioxidant capacity (T-OAC) and malondialdehyde (MDA) quantification.

Hemochromocytometric and serum biochemical analysis

The hemochromocytometric analysis was performed on blood aliquot samples with EDTA. The parameters evaluated included: erythrocytes (red blood cells-RBC, M/µL), haemoglobin (Hb, g/dL), mean globular haemoglobin concentration (MCH, pg), mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC, g/dL), hematocrit (HCT, %), mean corpuscular volume (MCV, fL), and erythrocyte distribution width (RDW, %). The analysis was performed using the automated haematology analyser, type XN-V-1000 (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan).

The white blood cell series, heterophils (H, %), lymphocytes (L, %) and their ratio (H/L) were counted by setting up blood smears from each hen’s blood samples at each time point, preparing from a drop of blood a single glass slide. After glass slide drying, the HEMACOLOR rapid blood smear staining (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) was used to stain smears, and the count was performed using a light microscope by two independent specialised operators, unaware of the treatments. A total of 100 leucocytes was counted in different fields on each slide. The H/L ratio for each hen was calculated by dividing the number of H by the number of L.

To oversee the hepatic functionality, serum biochemical indexes such as glucose (Glu, mmol/L, Glucose-Oxidase_Cod. 18250840), uric acid (UA µmol/L, Uric Acid_Cod. 18256940), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, IU/L, ALT/GPT liquid_Cod. 18257440), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, IU/L, AST/GOT_Cod. 18257540), alkaline phosphatase (ALP, IU/L, Alkaline Phosphatase_Cod. 18259740), total bilirubin (Bil, µmol/L, Total Bilirubin_Cod. 18258340), cholesterol (CL, mmol/L, Cholesterol_Cod. 18250540), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-CL, mmol/L, HDL_Cholesterol_Cod. 18258940), and triglycerides (TG, mmol/L, Tryglicerides_Cod. 18258740), were evaluated spectrophotometrically by a biochemical multi-analyser ILab 650 (Instrumentation Laboratory, Werfen Company, Milan, Italy), using commercial kits listed above, provided by the same manufacturer.

Serum and liver antioxidant indexes

The lipid peroxidation was measured in the serum samples of each hen at T0 and T5, and in the liver tissue samples, through the TBARS assay (Cat. No. 10009055, Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA), which quantifies the reactivity of MDA with thiobarbituric acid (TBA). In this kit, MDA and TBA react under high temperatures (90–100 °C) and acidic conditions, and the formed adduct is measured colourimetrically at 530 nm. The analysis was conducted following the protocol supplied with the kit. The results were expressed as µmol/L MDA equivalents. The detection range for this test was 0.625–50 µmol/L.

The T-OAC to ROS was analysed in the serum samples using the commercial kit Antioxidant Assay Kit (Cat. No. 709001; Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). The assay principle is the ability of antioxidants present within a sample to inhibit the oxidation of ABTS® (2,2’-azino-di-3-ethylbenzthiazoline sulphonate) to ABTS® + by metmyoglobin. The antioxidants cause suppression of absorbance at 750 nm or 405 nm proportionally to their concentration; this capacity to prevent ABTS® oxidation is compared to that of standard Trolox, a water-soluble tocopherol analogue, and is expressed as mM Trolox Equivalent (mM TE). The detection range for this test was 0.068–0.495 mM TE. The absorbance values of both assays were detected using a spectrophotometer (Epoch Biotek, Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Gross pathology, liver lesion scoring, and organ sampling

At the end of the trial, a postmortem examination was performed on 13 hens per group to assess their health status and detect any pathological lesions. At the same time, at the level of the proximal tract of the duodenum, cecum tract and rectal ampulla, the pH was measured by EDGE® pH instrument (HANNA Instruments S.r.l., Padova, Italy).

Of each hen, the coelomic cavity was immediately opened after cervical dislocation, and the whole gastrointestinal tract (stomach, gizzard, intestine, pancreas), and liver were removed, weighed, and the liver was scored according to the following methods. The liver colour and macroscopic lesion scoring were evaluated following the method illustrated by Choi and colleagues (2012) and Diaz and colleagues (1999), respectively. Briefly, each liver colour was evaluated assigning a score based on a visual evaluation, the intensity of the colour and variation in the shade of yellow (as an index of lipid accumulation), scoring the colour of liver parenchyma from 1 to 5, as follows: 1= normal liver colour; 2 to 5 (from dark red to light yellowish red), where the maximum score (score 5) was also attributed in case of putty-coloured livers and severe fatty liver steatosis. The eventual liver haemorrhage was scored from 0 to 5, as: 0= normal liver; 1= to 10 for subcapsular petechial or ecchymotic haemorrhages; 2= more than 10 subcapsular petechial or ecchymotic haemorrhages; 3 to 5= large and massive haemorrhages. The scoring procedures were performed by three independent qualified investigators, as well as the counting of parenchymal haemorrhages was also executed with the liver removed, observing the organ inside and outside the hepatic lobes (upper and lower). The evaluation results were expressed as a mean ± standard deviation (SD) of 3 scores assigned for each liver for each treatment group. Subsequently, liver sections of 2 × 2 cm from each hen were cut and immediately fixed in 10 % buffered formalin for histological evaluation, while 20 g of liver were immediately refrigerated at -80 °C for lipid peroxidation assays with previously described methods.

Histopathology

The liver samples (n= 13 control, n= 13 treated) were formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded and routinely trimmed. Four-micrometer sections were cut, placed on slides, deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), dehydrated, and coverslipped.

The histopathological diagnosis screening aimed to assess, through the application of a specific titer-score grading approach on the characterisation of cell damage, haemorrhages, and presence (but not quantification) of lipid droplets in hepatocytic cells, widely used and reported in the literature (Gao et al., 2019; Trott et al., 2014), the grade of liver steatosis and/or FLHS in both experimental groups. Specifically, the severity of steatosis was qualitatively scored in a 4-tier grading according to Trott and colleagues (2014): 0 (absent to very rare vacuolization); 1 (< 50 % of hepatocytes containing vacuoles of any size); 2 (50 % of hepatocytes containing vacuoles of any size); 3 (> 50 % of hepatocytes containing medium to large vacuoles). Any additional pathological findings were recorded and described.

Statistical analysis

Data results were expressed as mean ± SD, provided from the evaluation of the 13 hens sampled, for each experimental group. STATA-Statistical Software Package (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA, www.stata.com), version 16 (StataCorp, 2016) was used as statistical software to analyse the data. Previously, the data normality distribution was verified by the Shapiro–Wilk test. A One-Way repeated-measures MANOVA test followed by Duncan’s post hoc test was performed to assess the significance of the treatment (TRT), time (t), and their combination effect (TRT+t) at each time point of the trial, for blood, serum and antioxidant indices. The first of the two estimated models included the effects of TRT and t, and the second was their combination effect, to avoid multicollinearity issues. It was not necessary to test the sphericity assumption, which could affect only the ANOVA analysis, and not the MANOVA one (Spicer, 2005). In the cases of the assumption of normal data distribution was not respected, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was performed. The significance of the TRT+t combination effects, to highlight any significance of the treatment at each specific experimental time (T0, T3, and T5) between the two experimental groups, was indicated using different superscript letters (a, b). For all tests, the statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

General observation during the trial

Following and during the treatment administration, no adverse effects were observed in laying hens throughout the experimental period, as shown also by the serum biochemical indices (Table 2), confirming the treatment safety at the tested doses. The water palatability after mixing the product was not altered. No episodes of parasitism, pica, or cannibalism and no mortality in either experimental group were observed throughout the experimental period.

Hemochromocytometric indices

Table 2 reports the results of the haemocytometric analyses. During the trial, the effect of TRT, t and their combination effects showed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). The analyses evidenced that in treated hens, the TRT directly affects RBC and Hb content, with a consequent positive influence on HCT% (P < 0.05), compared to the control. The Hb, at the end of the trial (T5) after the latest treatment administration, was significant different between the two groups, for the TRT+t effect (P < 0.05), where the treated hens showed a higher Hb content compared to the control. However, correlated indices such as MCV, MCH, MCHC, and RDW were not affected by the TRT (P > 0.05), albeit some significant differences were evidenced by t (P < 0.05). For the t effect, RBC, Hb and HCT showed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) from T3 to T5 (end of the trial). The MCV and RDW values showed, however, an inverse trend between them. The t effects showed that from T0, the MCV significantly decreased in both groups (P < 0.05) and returned to a similar range of values to those found at the start of the trial. At T3, the RDW showed higher values compared to T0 in both groups (P < 0.05) and then decreased again at the end of the trial (T5).

Heterophils, lymphocytes and their ratio (H/L)

The H%, L% and their ratio results are reported in Table 2. Their trend was not influenced by TRT and TRT+t combination effects (P > 0.05). However, significant differences were detected due to the t (P < 0.05), where both groups showed a fluctuating variation trend of H, L and their ratio.

Serum biochemical indices

Table 3 reports the results of the serum biochemical analyses of the hens’ metabolic profile. The treatment did not alter the physiological range of the serum biochemical indices. However, during the trial, the treated hens showed an ALT enzyme reduction compared to the control. Furthermore, between the control and treated hens, statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) were detected at the different time points for t and TRT+t effects.

Glucose (Glu) and uric acid (UA)

The serum Glu and UA were not influenced by the TRT nor TRT+t effects (P > 0.05). However, the t effect showed a significant reduction of the level of Glu and UA at T3 (at the end of the first withdrawal period), compared to the other experimental time points for both treatment groups (P < 0.05). Compared to the values detected at T0, the UA did not differ from the values found at T5, for the t effect (P > 0.05).

Hepatic enzymes and total bilirubin (Bil)

The serum hepatic enzymes, such as AST, ALT and ALP, showed an interesting trend, although AST and ALP were not influenced directly by the TRT (P > 0.05). Serum AST, increased significantly its levels from T0 to T5 (end of the trial) for the t effect (P < 0.05), albeit no statistical differences due to the TRT+t effects between treated and control hens during the trial were detected (P > 0.05). Serum ALT was positively influenced by TRT, which reduced its levels in treated hens from T0 to T5, compared to the control group (P < 0.05). In this regard, between the control and treated groups, a statistical difference for the TRT+t effect at T3 and T5 (P < 0.05) was detected. Nonetheless, it can also be noted that its level increased by t effects at T3 in both groups, compared to those found at T0, and then decreased again at T5 (P < 0.05).

Serum ALP and the total serum Bil content showed a similar trend. An increase from T0 to T5 was observed to t effects in both groups (P < 0.05). No statistical differences due to the TRT+t effects were detected for serum ALP and total Bil levels at T0, T3 and T5 (P > 0.05).

Serum lipidic fractions

The serum TG and CL were not affected by the TRT, t, and TRT+t effects (P > 0.05). However, the cholesterol HDL-CL was affected by t (P > 0.05) in both groups, where the HDL-CL level was significantly improved (P < 0.05).

Liver weight, lesion score and histopathology findings

Immediately after suppression by manual cervical dislocation, each hen of each experimental group was necropsied. At necropsy, no macroscopic pathological lesions were observed in the heart, lungs and gastrointestinal tract, including cases of enteritis, ovary and oviduct. The ovary and oviduct were active and with abundant follicles in all hens. The liver was immediately observed in situ (colour, haemorrhages, etc.) as previously described. Subsequently, each liver of each hen was removed and weighed. The liver weight was lower in the treated group compared to the control (P < 0.05) (Table 4).

The liver parenchyma colour and haemorrhagic lesion score evaluated during necropsy are shown in Fig. 2, as representative examples of both treatment groups. The anatomopathological findings show that the livers of all control and treated hens were enlarged compared to the normal anatomy (although the treated hens' livers showed significantly less weight), average smooth, glossy, and of a more or less intense yellow colour. No livers corresponded to the colour of a physiologically normal liver, such as dark brown and shiny (Fig. 2Ca-Td), ascribing to both experimental hen groups suspected cases of full-blown steatosis. Macroscopically, each hen showed suspected cases of overt hepatic steatosis or FLHS, more or less severe. Nonetheless, the control hens showed, globally, a significantly intense and uniformly yellow-orange colour, compared to the treated hens (Fig. 2Ta-Td), which displayed a light brown-yellow colour liver score (P < 0.05, Table 4), with haemorrhages and friability. Indeed, the control hens' livers possessed a high tendency of the parenchyma to cause ruptures when touched, due to their higher softness and friability (Fig. 2Ca-Cb, 2Cd, black circle). Although no significant severe liver bleeding was recorded in either group, in 6 out of 13 control hens, the liver showed subcapsular locally extensive hematomas (Fig. 2Cb, 2Cd, white arrow) or disseminated to petechiae or multifocal haemorrhages (Fig. 2Ca-2Cd, white arrow), while the treated hens evinced a lower score of haemorrhages (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2Ta, 2Tc).

From the histological results, cases of suspected steatosis or FLHS were confirmed. Hepatocellular changes were observed in both controls and treated hens. Hepatic steatosis, histologically characterised by optically empty cytoplasmic micro- and macro-vacuoles, was significantly present in control hens (12 out of 13 hens) of mild to moderate severity (P > 0.05), compared to the treated hens. In the treated hens, hepatic steatosis with indicative hepatocyte vacuolization was present (8 out of 13 hens) with a mild severity, where 5 hens showed no steatosis. In the control group, 2 cases had locally detected extensive subcapsular haemorrhages (numerous extravasated erythrocytes) suggestive of FLHS. As an additional finding, mild to moderate lymphoplasmacytic chronic cholangiohepatitis was detected in all samples analysed.

Discussion

Liver disorders, including hepatic steatosis and FLHS, are non-infectious diseases that commonly affect the laying hens in the later laying stages. The evaluation and monitoring of haematology indices during the production cycle can be a valid support in identifying or preventing these pathologies and assessing the health and physiological status of birds (Etim et al., 2014). In this study, haematology revealed significant effects in treated hens on an increase of RBC, Hb and HCT indices during the 7-week trial, indicating a better physiological response followed by SIL+CGA administration. A previous study conducted in broiler chickens supplemented with silymarin reported similar effects in an upgrade in RBC, Hb and HCT indices associated with its strong antioxidant activity, protecting the cellular membrane from oxidation by free radicals (in particular, membrane lipids) (Abdalla et al., 2018). Specifically, SIL can avoid RBC hemolysis due to the erythrocyte cell membrane stabilisation, as also evidenced in animal models exposed to water-soluble radical generators (Karimi et al., 2006; Psotová et al., 2004). With the membrane cell protection, the silymarin can act effectively to prevent Hb-polymerisation, with tissue oxygen-delivery improvement (Adhikari & Arora, 2016; Soltanian et al., 2020). Similar interaction and protection mechanisms of the cellular membrane to avoid Hb-polymerisation were also reported for CGA (Bonarska-Kujawa et al., 2015). In this trial, the experimental hens of both groups assumed a daily water intake of ∼200 mL (data not shown). Due to the reported findings (i.e. RBC, Hb and HCT upgrade), it can be hypothesised that the dose of phytoextracts assumed (∼0.48 mg/day of CGA, and ∼0.44 mg/day of SIL), is sufficient ad efficient to exhibith positive effects on blood indices, probably due to the meachanisms previously described, supporting and improving laying hens' health and welfare after the peak of production.

In the treated hens, some effects were noted on the serum liver enzyme concentrations. In particular, AST and ALT, two important transaminases produced in hepatocytes and involved in the synthesis of non-essential amino acids, are released from the cytosol when the liver cell is damaged or destroyed, with their levels increasing (Sun et al., 2015; Yousefi et al., 2005). In this study, although the AST and ALP enzyme levels remained within the physiological ranges in both groups (Board et al., 2018; Hong et al., 2021), the ALT enzyme was significantly decreased after the phytoextracts treatment in treated hens. Considering the three different administrations alternating with withdrawal periods, the study suggests that the phytoextracts' effect is maintained even during the suspension time, as observed at T3 of the trial. The administration of the two phytoextracts, translated in hepatoregenerative activity (Abenavoli et al., 2010; Lu et al., 2020; Naveed et al., 2018), helps to restore proper liver function. Studies reported that the silymarin and CGA administration (SIL ranging from 250 to 1.000 mg/kg diet; CGA ranging from 600 to 800 mg/kg diet), in laying hens reduces the serum ALT and AST activity, showing hepatoprotective hepatoregenerative, and cholerectic action (Guo et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024), and alleviates the hepatic lesions in steatotic-affected hens, as found in this study. This liver condition improvement in treated hens (also confirmed histologically), due to the hepatoregenerative action of SIL and CGA, probably prevented a massive hepatocyte degeneration, and, therefore, also an increase in serum ALT enzyme levels.

Both experimental hen groups presented gross-anatomopathological lesions ascribed to a suspected case of full-blown steatosis. The overall liver colour score (due to the high or lower hepatic lipid storage), hemorrhagic score and liver weight were found to be lower in treated hens compared to control ones, in direct correlation with absent cases of steatosis, or of lesser severity, confirmed histologically. The hallmark of liver steatosis is TG accumulation in the cytoplasm of hepatocytes, which may lead to additional histological changes. The histopathology performed on the liver samples assigned a steatosis severity score based on the absence or presence of lipid droplets stored in the hepatocytes, as reported in literature (Gao et al., 2019; Trott et al., 2014), but the liver lipid content was not quantified. The absence or minimal presence of lipid droplets in the hepatocytes of treated hens may suggest a greater lipid mobilisation. Nonetheless, presumably, hepatocyte lipid droplets could be the cause of hepatocellular injury and severe inflammation, resulting in liver tissue swelling and capsule rupture, observations not found systematically macroscopically and histologically in the treated hens. However, these findings were not associated with a significant reduction in serum lipid fraction in this trial. The discrepancies can be attributed to other suppositions. Generally, TG and CL are used as indicators of lipid metabolism functionality, where HDL-CL fraction is responsible for the transportation of CL from tissue cells into the liver, to be further metabolised (San et al., 2023). It should be emphasised that SIL and CGA also show a lipid-lowering activity on TG, CL serum indices (Faryadi et al., 2024; Naveed et al., 2018; Sahebkar et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2022), but this effect is not constant in poultry or animal model studies (Blevins et al., 2010; Doostkam et al., 2022; FaniMakki et al., 2013; Ghohari et al., 2013; Kamali & Mostafaei, 2014; Sourani et al., 2016; Zaker-Esteghamati et al., 2021). This inconsistency could be attributable to the low doses of phytoextracts administered, the different administration protocol used, laying hens' genetic lines, age, diet formula and composition, or possible antagonistic/synergistic effects, which can condition or not the serum lipid variations.

Liver steatosis and FLHS are also characterised by hepatocyte damage (Sanyal et al., 2001; Sihali-Beloui et al., 2016), at the mitochondrial level, with consequent homeostasis imbalance due to massive lipid storage (San et al., 2023). Lipid oxidation or an alteration of these metabolic processes is the main source of ROS production (Zhu et al., 2018). In this process, mitochondria, cellular organelles responsible for metabolic functions, represent one of the primary sites of their production (Zhao et al., 2024). No less, it must be considered that the ability to remove free radicals gradually decreases over time as hens age (Faryadi et al., 2024), but antioxidant supplying may be effective in slowing ageing-induced oxidative stress and cell apoptosis in hens (Yang et al., 2020). Based on serum T-OAC results, it can be postulated that the antioxidant agent, such as SIL+CGA administration, positively conditioned the hepatic metabolism, improving the release and metabolisation of lipids from the liver, with a consequent steatosis advancement, acting on the laying hens’ antioxidant defence.

A total or partial oxidation of lipids by hepatic metabolism could have influenced the slight increase (although not significantly) of serum MDA levels in treated hens. This could be caused by their enhancing rapid or partial oxidation, mitochondrial efforts. Laying hens with fatty liver-affected or aged, usually exhibit physiological processes typically accompanied by elevated levels of ROS and MDA production, and significantly lowered activities of T-OAC, which reduces the ability of the intrinsic antioxidant system to neutralise free radicals and peroxides, disturbing the redox balance (Miao et al., 2024; Peverill et al., 2014). In this study, as previously hypothesised, the opposite was partially verified. A slight upgrade in serum MDA was, firstly, not related to the worsening of steatosis in treated hens. However, an improvement in the serum T-OAC may have counteracted and limited the excessive MDA increase level, maintaining a buffering environment to counteract the free radical damage, due to SIL and CGA antioxidant potential. Results regarding these specific biochemical mechanisms remain partially clear and would require further investigation to elucidate the biochemical regulatory or compensatory actions in laying hens' redox system, from a systemic perspective, when steatosis-affected, and exposed to the phytoextracts' biological effects. Specifically, further studies should be conducted to better explain the discrepancy between the lower presence and severity of steatosis, without serum lipid fraction variations, better serum T-OAC, but associated with a potential and variable increase in serum MDA. Overall, considering that the oxidative stress provide a significant contributor to liver damage, a suitable supplementation of SIL+CGA applying the illustrated administration protocol was satisfactory to recovery the serum antioxidant capacity with a general positive effect on hens’ organism, consistent with those reported in literature (Lu et al., 2020; Lukitasari et al., 2020; Miranda et al., 2000; Saeed et al., 2017), and confirmed by other studies on poultry (Baradaran et al., 2019; Faryadi et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). No less, despite these potentially controversial findings, its phytoextracts association tested improves the egg quality indices, in particular antioxidant characteristics, as reported in the study conducted by Serra and co-workers (2025), who analysed the egg qualitative characteristics laid by the same hens enrolled in this study.

Lastly, pH variations at the level of the proximal tract of the duodenum were measured in treated hens compared to the control ones, observed at the end of the trial. It has been proven that there are strict correlations between gut microbiota dysbiosis, altered metabolites production, liver function and the onset of steatohepatitis in laying hens (Hamid et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2024). It is known that the amount and microbiota composition vary between intestinal sites (Rychlik, 2020), conditioned by the gut pH values (Cao et al., 2021). No less, the digestion of the lipids provided by the diet requires the action of gastric, pancreatic and hepatic enzymes, which act predominantly in an acidic gut-environment (Krogdahl, 1985). In this study, even if no data on microbiota are available, it can not be excluded that the basic pH values in the duodenum in the treated hens due to the SIL+CGA administration could have conditioned their microflora composition, affecting its composition or the gastric/pancreatic enzyme secretion, with relative positive effects on the liver health (e.g. choleretic function). Certainly, these observations relating to pH lay the foundation for further studies to gain greater knowledge on the combined use of these phytoextracts and in the reduction of hepatic steatosis severity.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that the association of SIL and CGA, tested at a blended dose of 1 mL/L, administered intermittently by drinking water, can be proposed as a valid, promising natural feed support strategy to prevent or limit the progression of several chronic liver diseases during the critical production phases. Its administration improves the caged-laying hens' health after the peak of production through positive effects on some blood and serum indices (e.g. lowering AST activity, upgrade in RBC, Hb, and serum T-OAC levels, etc.), reducing the liver steatosis/FLHS severity, evaluated and confirmed histologically.

In this study, a single dose of the two phytoextracts was tested. Although this may be considered a limitation for a complete comparison of the observed effects (which were also evaluated under real field conditions), the observed results offer a distinct new perspective for elucidating the combined effects of the SIL and CGA administration in the regulatory o reparatory mechanism of liver steatosis in laying hens, and also verify if the observed results occur: (i) different administration doses of SIL and CGA, administered also alone; (ii) applying different administration programs (e.g, continuously); (iii) testing during other laying hens production phases. In addition, this can allow, in the future, to use of these bioactive compounds with a major flexibility, including economical sustainability (including the phytoextract/product production costs), and on a large scale and with fewer limitations, as a valid alternative feed additives to improve laying hens’ health during their production cycle.
