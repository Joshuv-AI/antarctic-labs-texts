Balancing electric vehicle uptake with congestion-related emissions in large Chinese cities
Authors: Bingying Huang, Yingji Xia, Fadong Chen, Xiqun (Michael) Chen
Published: 2026

Main text

Electrification is key to mitigating tailpipe emissions from road transport, which amounted to 5.87 Gt CO2 globally in 2022. Consequently, many governments have unequivocally promoted the adoption of electric vehicles (EVs) through policy measures and regulatory benefits. For example, China has reduced or exempted the vehicle purchase tax for EVs, deployed a “green channel” for EV license plate approvals, facilitated a better charging experience, and removed vehicle usage rationing policies in large cities during peak commuting hours. However, in these large cities, unrestricted EV ownership can result in more on-road vehicles and even congested traffic conditions, subsequently generating more emissions from conventional internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles. We discuss the potential trade-offs that balance rapid (electric) vehicle adoption in large cities that can produce unintended side effects and advocate for careful management to mitigate these effects.

More (electric) vehicles on the road

EVs were invented more than 100 years ago, but their ownership was only spurred by technological advances and government support in the last decade. Global EV sales exceeded 17 million in 2024, with a market share of over 20%. EV ownership in China has surged over the past decade, supported by strong national and municipal incentives that began in 2009 (Figure 1). In 2025, China’s annual EV sales reached 16 million, accounting for 47.9% of the market share, with a nationwide ownership of 43.97 million and a penetration rate of 12.01% by 2025, thanks to these promoting policies and subsidies. On the contrary, ICE vehicles receive prudential supervision in large cities to reduce their tailpipe emissions.

Spiking electric vehicle ownership and promotion policies in China and their impacts on traffic congestion and tailpipe emissions

Support measures in China have been implemented to prioritize the adoption of EVs. A fast increase in EV use can cause worse traffic congestion, lead to more emissions from ICE vehicles, and negatively impact sustainability.

The effects of these policies are obvious. For example, EV ownership in Hangzhou, China (population 12.6 million), exceeded 1.25 million in just a few years, aided by the government providing free license plates and lifting certain vehicle usage restrictions. Annual EV sales surpassed 327,000 by 2025. In contrast, license plates issued for ICE vehicles are strictly constrained to 80,000 per year (∼24% of EV sales) and can only be obtained through a lottery system (80%) or by bidding (20%). As a result, EVs rapidly achieved a penetration rate of 24% in 2025. The case is prevalent in other large Chinese cities, where ICE vehicles are strictly controlled, EVs are unequivocally promoted and subsidized, and more customers are subsequently attracted to buying EVs.

More congestion and increased emissions

From a nationwide perspective, EVs will gradually replace ICE vehicles on the road to eliminate tailpipe emissions, and policies should therefore promote EV uptake without hesitation. However, from a city-wise perspective, where a high proportion of ICE vehicles will remain on the road for years, EV adoption should be promoted with caution to ensure a sustainable transition and avoid potential increases in congestion-related emissions. Of note, the total number of on-road vehicles is a major factor in traffic congestion, while other contributing factors, such as signal timing, traffic routing, etc., remain relatively stable. Moreover, more on-road vehicles and relatively looser restrictions on driving for EVs (such as allowing driving during peak hours, the opportunity to use high-occupancy-vehicle [HOV] lanes, and being free from license-plate-based driving restrictions) may further worsen traffic congestion in large cities where vehicle ownership has already exceeded the road network capacity.

For instance, the average congestion index—the ratio of vehicle free-flow speed to actual travel speed during peak commuting hours—showed that vehicles required 1.6–2.0 times the free-flow travel time to reach a destination among China’s 19 large cities (with over 5 million permanent population in this paper) from 2019 to 2023, according to the China Urban Transportation Report. Consequently, these cities continue to be confronted with heavier traffic burdens due to limited road space. Moreover, the congestion index in Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou increased by 4%–12% during this period, even as EV ownership in these cities expanded by 2- to 4-fold. Such co-evolution indicates that EV growth has not relieved traffic pressure in large urban networks.

Furthermore, because the charging cost for EVs is much lower than the fuel cost for conventional vehicles, EV drivers tend to exhibit larger vehicle kilometers traveled (VKT),1 which may further worsen traffic conditions in large Chinese cities. Recent research also provides evidence suggesting that an increase in EV market penetration rate can lead to additional traffic congestion during morning peak commuting hours, particularly in the time window around the preferred arrival time.2 Their experimental outcomes also indicate that the lower operating costs of EVs make the penalty for delayed arrival less severe; thus, drivers are less motivated to avoid delays.

Notably, EVs themselves are emission-free, and the surge of EVs and their usage does not directly induce additional tailpipe emissions. However, by increasing the number of on-road vehicles and thereby aggravating congestion in large cities, ICE vehicles would travel at lower speeds, undertake more stop-and-go maneuvers, and spend more time on the road for a given travel pattern (such as a daily commute). In turn, emissions from ICE vehicles increase, and the total transport emissions may not decline proportionally with respect to the increase in EV penetration rate. A study showed that ICE vehicles traveling below 20 km/h can even emit more than 50% extra CO2, CO, NOx, and PM2.5 emissions than at 60–80 km/h.3 Notably, the average travel speed during peak commuting hours among all of China’s supercities has already dropped below 30 km/h. This suggests a threatening tendency and a higher marginal effect to add unrestricted EV growth to the large cities.

Balancing sustainable EV uptake and congestion

Transition from ICE vehicles to EVs is essential and fundamental to decarbonize road transport. Indeed, the transition phase could span up to two decades, given the Chinese government plans to ban the sale of conventional vehicles by 2030–2035. We estimate that China will take over a decade to fully scrap existing ICE vehicles, based on the vehicle survival rate curve and the average vehicle age in China (only ∼6 years in 2023).4 This long transition period implies that congestion externalities from mixed fleets will persist, and thus, timely reactions from governments and policymakers are crucial to optimizing the vehicle electrification roadmap to ensure a sustainable vehicle transition phase, which is also the major claim of this commentary.

Meanwhile, as transportation scientists, we are aware that there should be an analytical solution for the best (electric) vehicle uptake curve. Here, we take the classical macroscopic fundamental diagram (MFD) model as an example,5 where network travel speed almost linearly decreases with network traffic density, given that on-road vehicles already exceed the critical traffic density of urban road networks. The accumulated on-road vehicle emissions under different EV uptake rates can be estimated and compared following this pipeline. Such a framework would allow researchers to derive the optimal city-wise EV adoption curve that minimizes total emissions during the transition phase. We therefore recommend urgent investigations to further explore this emission trade-off both theoretically and experimentally.

Recognizing that achieving a sustainable vehicle electrification roadmap is complex and challenging, we recommend a number of additional actions that academics, policymakers, and city planners can take to minimize environmental impacts during the transition phase, including proposing vehicle replacement schemes (i.e., replace ICE vehicles with EVs) to control the total vehicle numbers (which was implemented in China and achieved notable outcomes); crafting certain congestion policies for EVs, such as introducing time- or area-based charges for EVs during peak periods to align their travel costs more closely with the social cost of congestion, thereby discouraging non-essential peak travel; and offering carbon credits to promote commuting with public transport modes or shared mobility. Continuous and coordinated efforts are needed to refine the balancing strategy for vehicle electrification and ultimately mitigate traffic emissions through effective approaches.
