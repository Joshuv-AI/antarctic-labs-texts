Impact of open markets on zoonotic threats and antimicrobial resistance: A One Health concern
Authors: Md. Abdullah Evna Hasan, Md Saiful Islam, Naeem Ahammed Ibrahim Fahim, Md. Tabeer Hossain Antor, Samia Salam, Rownak Jahan, Rony Ibne Masud, Zuhayr Bakhtiyar, Dilruba Akter Jany, Md. Liton Rana, Sadia Afrin Punom, Al Muksit Mohammad Taufiquer Rahman, Md. Tanvir Rahman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Open markets play a significant role in global food distribution, particularly in East and Southeast Asia, where fresh produce, seafood, and live animals are commonly sold. However, they pose significant public health risks by facilitating the transmission of zoonotic pathogens and contributing to the spread of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). High human-animal interactions, poor hygiene, and unregulated antimicrobial use contribute to the emergence and spread of resistant microorganisms. Despite these risks, the role of open markets in the spread of zoonotic pathogens and AMR has received limited attention. Thus, this review explores the possible mechanisms by which AMR may spread in open markets, the role of microbial genetic exchanges in the emergence of new resistant strains in these settings, and the zoonotic risks associated with the live animal trade. It also discusses strategies and future directions to mitigate these issues, emphasizing the urgency of taking the required One Health actions to minimize the spread of AMR and the zoonotic infections globally. These actions should include robust surveillance, regulatory measures, public health initiatives along with advocacy and training, all crucial for protecting global health.

Introduction

Open markets, such as wet markets, live animal and bird markets, seafood markets, wild animal markets, and traditional or informal marketplaces, are widespread in many East and Southeast Asian countries, including the Philippines, China, Taiwan, Thailand, Indonesia, India, Bangladesh, Pakistan, and Vietnam. These markets are deeply embedded within the cultural and socioeconomic fabric of their respective regions, serving as vital sources of nutrition, livelihood, and tourism [1,2]. However, they have also been linked to the emergence of numerous epidemics and pandemics, serving as a significant source of foodborne infections and many zoonotic outbreaks. The close human interaction with food products and other people, live animals, contaminated surfaces, and other individuals within these markets facilitates the rapid spread of infectious agents and antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Public health concerns surrounding these markets have been intensified due to their potential role in the transmission of zoonotic pathogens and the propagation of AMR determinants [1].

The majority of the open markets sell perishable items, fresh meats, fish, seafood, vegetables, fruits, and live animals, such as poultry, livestock, and wild species, often in unhygienic conditions, to meet local consumer demands [3,4]. They represent critical interfaces for human-animal-environment interactions, creating optimal ecological conditions for zoonotic spillover events and AMR amplification [[3], [4], [5]] Furthermore, many of these markets, often embedded within densely populated residential areas, facilitate cross-species transmission of pathogens through high human traffic, contaminated fomites, and interactions with wild and peri-domestic animals. These settings also facilitate horizontal gene transfer (HGT) of AMR genes among microbial communities residing in humans, animals, and environmental reservoirs, thereby intensifying the risk of multidrug-resistant infections [6,7].

Most people in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) rely on these traditional outdoor markets for their daily food needs. For instance, approximately 95 % of residents in Vietnam's capital procure their vegetables from over 400 traditional open marketplaces [8,9]. Similarly, another study in Africa found that between 86 % and 99 % of milk selling happens through informal or open-market channel [4,10] This trend is also common in Bangladesh, India, Pakistan, Nepal, and other countries in Southeast Asia [[11], [12], [13]] This widespread reliance on open markets exposes a substantial portion of the population to persistent risks associated with zoonotic pathogens and AMR.

Given these exposures, addressing AMR and emerging zoonotic threats necessitates a One Health perspective, an integrated, multidisciplinary framework that recognizes the interconnection between human, animal, and environmental health [14,15] The indiscriminate and excessive use of antimicrobials across human healthcare, veterinary medicine, and agricultural sectors, combined with direct and repeated exposure in open market settings, underscores the need for coordinated surveillance systems, risk assessment protocols, and robust policy interventions. This review, therefore, highlights how open markets act as hotspots for the spread of AMR and zoonotic pathogens and points out several knowledge gaps and inconsistencies in the literature. Although cross-contamination is a common incidence in open markets, there is limited understanding of broader transmission pathways, which include the roles of different animal species, environmental reservoirs, regional variations in the prevalence of resistant bacteria, and the effectiveness of intervention strategies. Addressing these gaps is essential to guide effective interventions and One Health strategies to reduce public health risks and protect ecological balance.

Open markets: a hub of pathogen exchange

Open markets serve as critical nodes for the dissemination of pathogenic microorganisms, including Listeria monocytogenes, Escherichia coli O157, Salmonella, Shigella, Staphylococcus aureus, MS2 bacteriophage, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, various viruses, and fungi, due to the close and often unregulated interactions among humans, animals, and environmental contaminants [16,17]. About 60–75 % of emerging infectious diseases in humans are zoonotic [18,19]. In addition, animals and poultry have historically been the source of many infections, such as H1N1 and H5N1 avian influenza, Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (SARS-CoV), Middle East Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (MERS-CoV), and, more recently, Mpox [20]. The emergence of Mpox has been particularly associated with live animal markets in West and Central Africa, where viral reservoir species are frequently traded under minimal biosecurity measures [21]. Recently, the Huanan Seafood Market in Wuhan, China, has been implicated as a key site in the early spread of SARS-CoV-2, the causal agent of Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) [22,23]. Despite the rise of supermarkets, open markets remain the preferred source of fresh food for up to 77 % of consumers in many regions [[24], [25], [26]]. However, high-risk animal species, combined with poor hygiene and inadequate infrastructure, continue to pose a significant threat to the interspecies transmission of zoonotic pathogens and the emergence of novel infectious diseases [1].

Open marketplaces are linked to the spread of infectious diseases, especially zoonotic diseases carried from animals to people. These settings often exhibit a convergence of risk factors that facilitate the amplification and spillover of pathogens [[27], [28], [29]]. Key contributors include the sale and presence of live animals, inadequate sanitation, poor hygiene habits, high animal densities, and the complex supply chains that unite diverse species from different geographical and ecological areas. The co-location of multiple species in confined, poorly regulated environments increases the likelihood of interspecies transmission and pathogen evolution. Such conditions create an ideal ecological niche for zoonotic pathogens to emerge, adapt, and ultimately infect human hosts [3,24,30].

Human behavioral patterns are critical determinants in facilitating the spread of different microorganisms within open market environments. Frequent and close contact with animals, contaminated surfaces, and animal products is common among market workers, vendors, and customers, subsequently increasing the risk of pathogen transmission through the direct handling of live animals, animal waste, or products such as meat or eggs [31]. Handling animals, poultry, or fish and their products without proper safety precautions, and eating or drinking close to animal territory, further increase the risk of zoonotic and foodborne pathogen transmission [32]. High population density in these markets contributes to aerosol transmission of pathogens through the generation of dust, fomites, etc. [17]. In addition, pathogen transfer via contaminated hands, clothing, utensils, or surfaces enhances the likelihood of cross-contamination between humans, animals, and market commodities [33,34] (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Transmission pathway of zoonotic and/or resistant pathogens from open markets.Fig. 1

In open market settings, the proximity of meat and fish shops often fosters substandard sanitary conditions, where the accumulation of animal waste, offal, blood, and other biological components creates a conducive environment for pathogen proliferation and transmission [35,36]. Such environments favor the presence and breeding of various vectors, including flies and rodents, that facilitate the mechanical transfer of infectious agents from contaminated surfaces and organic waste to food products, preparation areas, and humans. These interactions significantly increase the risk of cross-species and human-to-human transmission of infectious pathogens [37].

Vector-borne diseases alone contribute to approximately 17 % of the global burden of infectious diseases [30,[38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. Additionally, the caging of various animal species together in unsanitary conditions, often with shared food, water, and parasites, amplifies the risk of zoonotic disease emergence and interspecies pathogen spillover [45]. On-site slaughtering of animals and seafood processing, particularly in uncontrolled environments, exposes market workers and consumers to infectious agents present in gastrointestinal contents, blood, and other processing by-products [46,47]. Fresh produce and animal products are also vulnerable to contamination during various stages of handling, transportation, and sale, particularly in the absence of cold-chain infrastructure or hygienic protocols [48]. The frequent presence of live animals, including fish, poultry, and exotic or wild species, transported from rural or diverse geographic regions into open markets, further heightens the risk of pathogen spillover [4]. Open markets: potential hotspots for AMR emergence and spread

Open markets are critical potential hotspots for the formation and spread of AMR, particularly those that deal in live animals, animal products, fresh goods, seafood, food handlers, water, and uncontrolled trade (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Factors related to the transmission of antimicrobial-resistant organisms.Fig. 2

Factors related to the transmission of antimicrobial-resistant organisms.

Open marketplaces create a convergence of high-risk factors that facilitate the transmission of antimicrobial-resistant organisms (AROs) among humans, animals, and the environment. Although the global prevalence of AMR within the food supply remains incompletely characterized [75], foodborne exposure to resistant pathogens poses a significant and growing public health concern. Contamination of food products with antimicrobial-resistant bacteria (ARB) or resistance genes can occur at various points along the farm-to-fork continuum, from primary production to retail distribution [76]. Animal-derived foods, including pork, beef, poultry, turkey, and eggs, are primary reservoirs for ARB and resistance genes [[77], [78], [79], [80], [81], [82], [83]].

Aquaculture farm systems are recognized as AMR hotspots due to their intensive use of antibiotics and the potential for HGT among aquatic microbial communities. This can facilitate the emergence of resistant seafood-associated pathogens and compromise antibiotic efficacy in humans [[84], [85], [86]]. Pathogenic and resistant bacteria can enter fish at any stage of the production and supply chain, from the original aquatic habitat to handling, storage, and processing, especially in raw or undercooked fish [[87], [88], [89]]. For instance, a study in Saudi Arabia detected extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing E. coli resistant to third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins in frozen freshwater fish imported and sold in supermarkets [90].

Importantly, non-animal origin foods, including leafy greens, vegetables, fruits (e.g., melons), legumes, and grains, have also been implicated in the transmission of AMR pathogens such as E. coli and Salmonella, highlighting the broader scope of foodborne AMR risks [91]. Environmental vectors such as dust particles have been shown to transport AMR organisms, e.g., S. aureus, across long distances, allowing for their deposition on soil, water, and vegetation, and subsequent entry into the food chain [92,93]. Additionally, contaminated drinking water, often polluted by human or animal waste, acts as a vehicle for the dissemination of ARB, ARGs, and residual antibiotics, promoting gastrointestinal colonization in both humans and animals [93]. The role of food handlers in the transmission of ARB is also notable. Poor hygiene practices, inadequate handwashing, and cross-contamination during food preparation contribute to the spread of resistant bacteria. Although evidence is still emerging, studies have identified the presence of AMR E. coli on foods handled by individuals with substandard hygiene practices, suggesting a potential route for transmission [94].

Contamination of meat and seafood during handling or processing can introduce resistant bacteria, like pathogenic E. coli, especially in ready-to-eat seafood, posing significant public health risks [95]. A previous study has identified Enterococcus faecalis harboring both virulence factors and resistance genes in seafood, further implicating aquatic food products as reservoirs of AMR [96]. Similarly, milk and eggs can introduce antibiotic residues or resistant bacteria into the food chain, with eggs from diverse farming systems (e.g., organic, cage, and free-range) serving as sources of pathogenic and multidrug-resistant E. coli [97]. In open markets, high population density, frequent human-animal interactions, and poor hygiene practices facilitate the transmission of AMR organisms via direct contact or contaminated surfaces [77,98]. The overuse and misuse of antibiotics in human and veterinary medicine, including self-medication, over-prescription, and non-therapeutic dosing, exert selective pressure favoring the survival of resistant strains and diminishing antibiotic efficacy globally [99,100].

Environmental contamination further amplifies these risks. Agricultural waste, animal excreta, and wastewater from market operations serve as reservoirs of ARB, with resistant gut microbiota shed into the environment via feces [101]. Slaughterhouses and animal waste contribute to the spread of MDR bacteria among workers and surrounding communities, while airborne transmission via windblown dust has also been documented [102]. Vectors such as fleas, flies, cockroaches, ants, mosquitoes, and rodents act as mechanical carriers, transmitting resistant pathogens across shops, households, and humans [[103], [104], [105]].

Transport and trade of infected animals further facilitate the spread of pathogenic and resistant microorganisms in the environment, with transmission influenced by pre-existing resistance, fecal shedding, hygiene status, contact with infected animals, contaminated locations, and duration of transit [106]. Open markets also enable HGT of resistance determinants between microbial species, driven by mobile genetic elements (MGEs) such as plasmids, transposons, and integrons via mechanisms including conjugation, transformation, and transduction [97,98]. Frequent human-animal contact in markets allows zoonotic transmission of pathogens like S. aureus, including livestock-associated methicillin-resistant S. aureus (LA-MRSA) strains that adapt through MGE acquisition and host-switching events [107].

Open markets are also hotspots for human-to-human transmission due to mass gatherings, with increased mobility, including cross-border travel and displacement, further accelerating global AMR dissemination; over 1.4 billion international travelers in 2018 contributed significantly to the spread of AMR, with numbers projected to rise to 1.8 billion by 2025 [108]. The extensive and often unregulated use of antibiotics in food animal production, aquaculture, and agriculture has transformed the food chain into a critical conduit for AMR spread [109,110]. Intensified animal farming practices, particularly in LMICs, have driven increased antibiotic consumption to meet rising protein demands [111]. Antibiotic families such as sulfonamides, penicillins, quinolones, tetracyclines, and phenicols are of special concern due to their widespread use in both therapeutic and growth-promotion contexts, significantly contributing to AMR evolution across humans and animals [112,113].

Companion animals in open markets are also increasingly recognized as vectors of AMR transmission, with pathogens such as MRSA, vancomycin-resistant enterococci, carbapenemase-producing enterobacteria, and ESBL-producing Gram-negative bacteria posing both direct and indirect risks to human health [114]. Moreover, the role of agrichemicals in spreading AMR is a growing concern. The use of pesticides, fertilizers, rodenticides, herbicides, and insecticides in agriculture, directly and indirectly, promotes AMR by altering microbial environments in soil and water, fostering the selection of antibiotic-resistant microbes [77,115].

Mitigation strategies

Open markets function as critical interfaces where zoonotic pathogens and AMR can emerge, amplify, and disseminate due to the close interactions between humans, animals, and the surrounding environment. Mitigating these interconnected threats necessitates the implementation of a One Health approach, an integrated, multisectoral strategy that acknowledges the interdependence of environmental, human, and animal health [116,117].In recognition of this, the World Health Organization (WHO), the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH), and the United Nations Environment Program (UNEP) jointly launched the One Health Joint Plan of Action (2022–2026) [118] This global initiative underscores the importance of coordinated action across sectors to address zoonotic disease risks and build resilient health systems. Complementing this, the WHO's Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance provides a comprehensive One Health framework aimed at tackling AMR by promoting the responsible use of antimicrobials, strengthening global surveillance systems, and enhancing infection prevention and control measures. These global strategies serve as foundational blueprints for national and local-level efforts to reduce the burden of zoonotic diseases and AMR within high-risk environments such as open markets (Fig. 3). To address these intertwined public health threats, especially in low-resource settings, the One Health approach emphasizes integrated, multisectoral strategies that promote the health of people, animals, and ecosystems simultaneously. One of the most critical strategies involves the development of advanced surveillance systems and comprehensive monitoring frameworks. These systems should collect detailed data on antibiotic usage, the sale of diseased animals, and the health impacts of such practices across humans, animals, and environmental compartments. By analyzing and integrating this information, early detection of zoonotic disease outbreaks and resistance patterns becomes feasible, allowing for timely and evidence-based interventions.

Reinforcing hygiene and implementing strict biosecurity measures within open markets are equally essential. Personal hygiene, sanitation of market spaces, proper waste disposal, and vector control (targeting flies, cockroaches, and mosquitoes) must be prioritized to minimize the risk of environmental and cross-species contamination. Zoning strategies should be enforced to ensure physical separation between different sectors, live animal stalls, slaughterhouses, seafood, produce, and prepared food vendors, to prevent cross-contamination and pathogen spillover. For instance, as illustrated in Fig. 1, improper zoning and spatial proximity between animal stalls, meat shops, and fresh produce increase the risk of pathogen circulation and exposure.

Strengthening antibiotic stewardship across the animal production systems and food supply chains is also vital. Regulatory frameworks must enforce strict guidelines on the sale and administration of antimicrobials, ensuring that such drugs are used only under the supervision of qualified veterinary, public health, or environmental professionals. This includes banning the non-therapeutic use of antibiotics and reducing reliance on antimicrobials for growth promotion in animal agriculture.

Equally important is educating vendors, consumers, and farmers through targeted awareness campaigns, particularly in LMICs. Disseminating information through training sessions, print and social media platforms, as well as traditional media (radio and television) can raise awareness about zoonotic diseases, proper hygiene, prudent antibiotic use, and early disease recognition. Improved knowledge among the stakeholders that supports behavioral changes is necessary for effective AMR containment and zoonotic disease prevention.

The One Health approach also necessitates stronger collaboration between the public health and veterinary sectors in low-resource countries, especially in South and Southeast Asia. A unified national action plan should facilitate cross-sectoral communication, joint decision-making, and data sharing among human, animal health, and the ecosystem. Legal and regulatory enforcement must complement these strategies by mandating food safety compliance, prohibiting the sale and transport of visibly sick animals, and requiring veterinary inspection and documentation before entering the market. National policies should be harmonized with global health governance frameworks involving the Quadripartite working group (e.g., WHO, FAO, WOAH, and UNEP) to ensure consistent application of best practices for managing zoonotic and AMR risks.

Future directions

Further research should prioritize several critical areas to enhance understanding of zoonotic diseases and AMR dynamics in open markets. Currently, there is limited knowledge regarding the origin, development, pathophysiology, survival, transmission, and rate of zoonotic disease occurrence in the market environment; thus, in-depth investigations are needed to identify microbial hotspots, including animal cages, slaughtering zones, drainage systems, and waste disposal areas. Ecological and molecular studies should also focus on key vectors like flies, rodents, and insects, which are significant contributors to pathogen dissemination. In parallel, antibiotic usage patterns in poultry and livestock, especially in areas with weak regulatory frameworks, require a systematic assessment. Understanding the behaviors, risk perceptions, and hygiene practices of vendors, consumers, and regulators is equally essential for developing effective and culturally appropriate interventions [119].

Advancing AMR mitigation requires strengthening antimicrobial stewardship, enhancing infection control, developing novel diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, and initiating public and professional education campaigns within a One Health framework [120]. Genomic technologies are pivotal for tracing the emergence, persistence, and transmission of AMR genes in microbial populations, and their integration with advanced computational tools allows prediction of AMR trends, outbreak detection, and real-time response planning [121]. National Action Plans (NAPs) for AMR should be fully implemented to guide coordinated, cross-sectoral efforts in controlling resistant infections [122,123]. Evidence-based programs that promote responsible antibiotic use and reduce AMR across humans, animals, and the environment are urgently needed [124]. Robust surveillance systems are vital for monitoring AMR trends across sectors, and interdisciplinary collaboration is essential to antibiotic use and to limit the spread of resistance [108,109,111]. Active engagement of AMR and zoonotic experts in global policy dialogues, including Codex discussions, is necessary to create sustainable and prosperous health policies aligned with One Health goals [83].

Moreover, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) emerged as powerful tools for AMR surveillance, enabling predictive modeling, rapid data analysis, and decision-making support through clinical and laboratory datasets [125].These technologies accelerate the identification of resistance genes, novel mutations, therapeutic targets, and risk factors by processing large-scale, multi-source data [115,126] Integrating AI and genomics can also offer a promising frontier for combating AMR and advancing One Health strategies.

Conclusions

Open markets remain a crucial component of food distribution systems in many LMICs, serving as primary access points for fresh produce, seafood, vegetables, and live animals. However, these markets simultaneously pose significant public health threats by facilitating the emergence and dissemination of MDR pathogens and zoonotic diseases. The convergence of high-risk factors, including close human-animal interactions, inadequate sanitation, improper waste disposal, and unregulated antibiotic use, creates an ideal environment for microbial evolution, environmental contamination, and cross-species transmission. These conditions not only sustain ongoing outbreaks but may also promote the genetic exchange of AMR determinants, potentially leading to the development of novel resistant strains and superbugs. This review underscores the critical role of open markets in boosting both zoonotic threats and AMR, calling for a comprehensive and coordinated response grounded in the One Health framework. Addressing these challenges requires the integration of environmental, veterinary, and human health strategies to reinforce market biosecurity, improve sanitation, monitor antimicrobial usage, and establish robust surveillance systems. Public education campaigns targeting vendors, consumers, and policymakers are essential for fostering behavioral change and promoting adherence to hygiene and antibiotic stewardship practices. Future research should prioritize the identification of high-risk zones within open markets, the development of rapid diagnostic tools for zoonotic and resistant pathogens, and the application of advanced technologies, including AI and genomic surveillance, for predictive risk assessment and outbreak prevention. Ultimately, mitigating the health risks associated with open markets will require sustained international collaboration, policy reform, and cross-sectoral investment to ensure food safety, antimicrobial stewardship, and global public health resilience.
