Effect of spatial resolution and heating duration on accuracy of dynamic proton resonance frequency shift temperature measurement with k-space weighted image contrast reconstruction during focused ultrasound
Authors: Nicholas Richards, Michael Malmberg, Henrik Odéen, Robb Merrill, Rock Hadley, Dennis L Parker, Allison Payne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:Single-reference variable flip angle Stack-of-Stars (SR-VFA-SoS) thermometry with KWIC reconstruction provides simultaneous aqueous and fatty tissue temperature monitoring. This work evaluates the effect of k-space weighted image contrast (KWIC) reconstruction of a SR-VFA-SoS sequence on the accuracy of both proton resonance frequency shift (PRFS) and T1-based thermometry with different temporal footprints, spatial resolutions, echo times, and heating durations.Methods:Magnetic resonance-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) sonications were performed using an asymmetric KWIC-reconstructed single-reference variable flip angle Stack-of-Stars (SR-VFA-SoS) sequence and a validated segmented echo planar imaging (segEPI) thermometry sequence in tissue-mimicking phantoms and an in vivo rabbit model. KWIC windows of projection length 89–377, voxel resolutions of 1.2 mm isotropic and 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm, three different sets of echo times, and heating durations of 30.2–123.3 s were evaluated. Peak temperature accuracy, calculated cumulative thermal dose (CTD), and temporal lag were assessed.Results:In phantoms and the animal model, significant differences in peak temperatures were observed between the SR-VFA-SoS and segEPI thermometry at 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm voxel size, but not 1.2-mm isotropic. Temperature discrepancies were independent of echo time. Prolonged heating duration with lower power at the larger voxel size resulted in lower peak temperature error. Asymmetric KWIC-reconstructed SR-VFA-SoS peak temperatures were consistently temporally shifted later than segEPI peak temperatures.Conclusion:SR-VFA-SoS thermometry with KWIC reconstruction offers promising temporal resolution for simultaneous aqueous and fatty tissue temperature monitoring during MRgFUS. However, temporal blurring due to KWIC reconstruction results in underestimation and temporal shifting of peak temperatures for large voxel sizes and short heating durations. These observations highlight the need for careful KWIC and MRgFUS parameter optimization.Keywords: Thermometry, Focused ultrasound, Interventional, Reconstruction

Background

MR-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) surgery is a noninvasive therapeutic technique that combines the targeted energy delivery of focused ultrasound (FUS) with the imaging and temperature monitoring capabilities of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). For thermal MRgFUS applications, a significant advantage of MR guidance is the ability to monitor temperature changes within the focal zone and surrounding tissues noninvasively in real-time with sufficiently high spatial resolution [1] during treatment using magnetic resonance temperature imaging [2] (MRTI), eliminating the need for inserted temperature probes. The required MRTI spatial and temporal resolutions are determined in part by the ultrasound beam characteristics and application parameters [3,4] as well as the clinical target and objectives. [5,6] In general, spatial resolution on the order of 1–2 mm [7-9] is sufficient for most clinical applications. The necessary temporal resolution is dependent on the heating duration and rate of temperature change, with an image acquired for every 2–3 °C of change recommended. [10] Additionally, a comparatively large temporal footprint, the amount of time taken to acquire the k-space data used to reconstruct an image, leads to increased temperature measurement error. [11]

Clinically, the proton resonance frequency shift [12,13] (PRFS) method of MRTI is employed for aqueous tissues. PRFS thermometry relies on a linear change [14] in the resonance frequency as the protons' environment changes temperature. This frequency change is due to changes in the electron cloud shielding of the hydrogen protons within water molecules as they heat or cool. The electron cloud around the hydrogen proton reduces the applied magnetic field at the nucleus. In water, density of this electron cloud is reduced by hydrogen bonding between hydrogen atoms and the oxygen atom of adjacent water molecules. Increasing motion of water molecules with increased temperature causes hydrogen bonds to break, increasing the electron cloud and shielding around the proton, and thereby reducing the applied magnetic field at the nucleus. The net effect is that rising temperature leads to a decreased proton resonance frequency, which is captured in the phase of an image series. However, monitoring temperature in regions of tissue containing fat remains a challenge, as hydrogen atoms in fat are bound in triglycerides, which do not exhibit hydrogen bonding, meaning the PRFS MRTI method is not sensitive to temperature changes in fat. However, T1 of fat changes approximately linearly with temperature, [15] so fat temperature can be measured using T1 mapping-based methods. [16,17]

Svedin et al. [18] developed an efficient single reference variable flip angle sequence using a multi-echo stack-of-stars trajectory (SR-VFA-SoS) capable of measuring both PRFS and T1 changes with the same temporal resolution. PRFS is calculated at every time point using the image phase. T1 change is calculated using a low flip angle reference image acquired before heating paired with sequential high flip angle dynamic images. This allows for continuous measurement with both the PRFS and T1 methods without alternating flip angles during treatment. This sequence has been evaluated in healthy volunteers under no heating conditions and heated cadaver breasts, [18] during MRgFUS heating in phantoms, [19] and with MRgFUS heating in an in vivo rabbit model. [20]

The SR-VFA-SoS sequence leverages oversampling at the center of k-space in radial acquisitions using the k-space weighted image contrast (KWIC) reconstruction method developed by Song et al. [21] This allows for increased temporal resolution by continually updating the center of k-space at regular intervals during the image acquisition. Projections through k-space are often acquired at golden angles [22-24] or pseudo-golden angles [18,19,25,26] (Fig. 1A) and sorted by the time of acquisition (Fig. 1B). The center of k-space is masked out of most projections. For asymmetric KWIC windows (as shown in Fig. 1), the most recently acquired projections do not receive any masking and are used as the “central” projections, or the projections that contain the center of k-space. Non-central projections are binned according to acquisition time and the center of each projection is masked out, with older projections having more k-space data removed than newer projections, forming “rings” of projections in k-space, where outer rings contain successively more projections than inner rings (Fig. 1C). The amount of masking performed on bins of projections is calculated to maintain enough projections in each ring to satisfy the Nyquist criterion. For symmetric KWIC windows (not shown), the newest and oldest projections are binned in the outer ring together and have the greatest portion of k-space data masked out, with the middle projections being the central projections that receive no masking. For both symmetric and asymmetric KWIC window types, as new projections are acquired, the KWIC window is “slid” to incorporate the new projections while dropping the oldes projections (Fig. 1D). Using Fibonacci numbers for the number of projections included in each KWIC ring reduces uneven spacing between projections. [22]

The center of k-space is then masked out of non-central projections, with the amount of masking calculated to maintain the Nyquist criterion for the number of included projections in each ring. This k-space data is then regridded into Cartesian points and the Fourier transform is used to reconstruct an image. D) New projections are added to the KWIC window while dropping the oldest projections. Projections are rebinned and masked to form an updated k-space, which is again regridded and transformed into another image, with the process repeating through all of the projections.

Several fundamental parameters of KWIC reconstruction affect the temporal resolution and resultant MRTI temperature curve, and ultimately the accuracy of both the PRFS and T1 measurements. KWIC window length, or the number of projections included in the KWIC window, and sequence repetition time (TR) determine the total temporal footprint of each image. Longer KWIC windows with more total projections reduce streaking and blurring artifacts in images but also blur dynamic changes between projections, which can lead to decreased accuracy. [27] Each ring of the KWIC window has a unique temporal footprint based on the number of projections included. The number of projections included at the center of k-space determines the temporal footprint of the lowest frequency data used in each image. The number of projections the KWIC window advances between images determines the temporal resolution of the image series, sliding fewer projections increases the temporal resolution and vice versa. To fully sample the image, the width and number of projections included in each KWIC ring is determined by the Nyquist sampling rate, such that Δkaz,max(Nj,ϕ,kmax,j)∕ΔkRO≤1 , where Nj is the number of projections in the jth KWIC ring, ϕ is the view angle increment, kmax,j is the k-space distance from the center to the outer edge of the jth KWIC ring, ΔkRO is the required distance between k-space samples for the given imaging field of view, and Δkaz,max is the maximum azimuthal k-space spacing produced with a given Nj, ϕ, and kmax,j. This reduces to kmax,j≤NjΔkRO∕π for uniform spacing and kmax,j≤0.854NjΔkRO∕π for golden angle spacing and a Fibonacci number of views in each ring.

Svedin et al. [19] demonstrated some of the effects of using the KWIC reconstruction in conjunction with the SR-VFA-SoS method (1.2 mm isotropic resolution, TE = 7.5 ms, TR = 10 ms) both in simulation and experimentally at 1.2 mm isotropic resolution, with KWIC window lengths of 144, 233, and 377 projections. Results were compared with a 3D segmented echo planar imaging (segEPI) MRTI sequence (1.2 mm isotropic resolution, TE = 10 ms, TR = 20 ms, EPI factor = 7). Simulated heating showed good agreement between the MRTI acquired with the segEPI sequence and the SR-VFA-SoS sequence, with shorter KWIC windows producing better agreement in temperatures. Experimental temperature curves showed similar peak temperatures with a lag in heating and cooling. While this work successfully demonstrated utilizing KWIC with the SR-VFA-SoS sequence, larger voxel sizes were not modeled and only a single experimental heating at each condition was conducted.

Richards et al. [20] demonstrated simultaneous PRFS and T1-based MRTI in an in vivo rabbit model using the SR-VFA-SoS sequence (1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm resolution; TE=(2.46+1.32•n) ms, n = 0 to 5; TR = 20 ms; 13 center projections; 377 total projections per image) with PRFS sensitive to temperature changes in muscle and insensitive in fat and T1 changes notable in both tissue types. Temperature precision was found to be 0.76 °C ± 0.18 °C for PRFS thermometry in muscle. This study did not attempt to evaluate temperature accuracy.

Zhang et al. [28] utilized a similar hybrid VFA sequence using an efficient golden-angle-ordered 3D SoS technique in ex vivo in FUS ablated porcine fat and muscle with long sonication times (8 min). Absolute mean differences between temperature probes and PRFS temperatures of 1.89 °C, 1.23 °C, and 0.9 °C, depending on distance from the focus and transducer used. The PRFS accuracy found for these relatively long heating durations may not represent the accuracy of shorter, higher intensity MRgFUS ablative treatments.

In this study, we seek to investigate the effects of the KWIC reconstruction method with the SR-VFA sequence for MRgFUS surgery applications and address outstanding uncertainties that previous studies have not addressed. By analyzing how voxel size, heating duration, KWIC window size, and echo time affect the accuracy of the measured peak temperature, temporal resolution, and the timing of thermal events, we aim to provide a deeper understanding of the benefits and limitations of combining the SR-VFA-SoS sequence with the KWIC reconstruction approach. While the SR-VFA-SOS sequence can simultaneously measure temperature through both PRFS and T1 measurements, this work comprises evaluations of KWIC reconstruction effects on PRFS measurements as well as the accuracy of both the PRFS and T1 temperature measurements. MRTI obtained with a segEPI sequence thoroughly characterized through comparison to gold standard fiberoptic probes [29] is used for comparison for the PRFS measurements. Accuracy of the SR-VFA-SoS sequence with different voxel size, heating duration, KWIC window size, and echo time are assessed by comparing peak heating temperature and timing, temperature curves, and cumulative thermal dose [30,31] (CTD) to MRTI obtained with the validated segEPI sequence.

Voxel size experiment - phantom

The effect of the KWIC window reconstruction on peak measured temperature and temperature temporal shift at different voxel sizes was tested using MRTI voxel sizes of 1.2-mm isotropic (used by Svedin, et al. [19]) and 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm resolution in two gelatin phantoms. T1 change was also calculated to investigate if PRFS and T1 measurements display similar KWIC reconstruction effects. A series of 8 sonications were performed on each side of each phantom. Low flip angle (5°) nonheated baseline SR-VFA-SoS images were acquired before sonications to allow for T1 change to be calculated. Eight sonications (30.2 s duration, 17–18 acoustic W) were performed with each MRTI sequence, with temperature change measured with 1.2-mm isotropic voxel size segEPI (FOV = 153 × 100 × 12 mm, matrix size = 128 × 84 × 10, TE = 9.8 ms) for sonications at points 2 and 7 in the first phantom and 4 and 5 in the second phantom, 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm voxel size segEPI sequence (FOV = 192 × 126 × 20 mm, matrix size = 128 × 84 × 10, TE = 9.8 ms) at points 1 and 6 in the first phantom and 3 and 8 in the second, 1.2-mm isotropic SR-VFA-SoS (FOV = 154 × 154 × 12 mm; matrix size = 128 × 128 × 10; TE=(2.46+1.02•n) ms, n = 0 to 5; FA = 15°) at points 4 and 5 in the first phantom and 1 and 6 in the second, and 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm SR-VFA-SoS sequence (FOV = 192 × 192 × 20 mm; matrix size = 128 × 128 × 10; TE=(2.46+1.06•n) ms, n = 0 to 5; FA = 15°) at points 3 and 8 in the first phantom and 2 and 7 in the second.

B0 field drift was corrected using a k-space-based method [33] for both the segEPI and SR-VFA-SoS sequences. PRFS temperature maps were reconstructed from both the segEPI and SR-VFA-SoS sequences and T1 change maps were reconstructed from the SR-VFA-SoS sequence.

Analysis was performed to evaluate the effects of KWIC reconstruction on image quality. Signal-to-noise (SNR) ratios were calculated for all image series using the signal intensity of the phantom prior to heating and the standard deviation of a subtraction image generated from the first two images in the series. [34] The precision of the measured PRFS temperatures and T1 change was determined for each reconstructed map. Temperature data contained in an annulus encompassing the unheated region of the phantom outside of the sonication points was analyzed. For each voxel in the annulus, the standard deviation of the temperature through time was calculated, then the calculated temperature standard deviation of all voxels in the annulus were averaged to determine the temperature precision.

Echo time experiment - phantom

To determine the effect echo time has on peak measured temperature and peak temperature temporal shift, sonications (30.8 s duration, 16–17 W) were performed in two phantoms each with spatial resolution of 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm for both segEPI (FOV = 192 × 114 × 20 mm, matrix size = 128 × 76 × 10, TE = 9.4 ms) and SR-VFA-SoS (FOV = 192 × 192 × 20 mm, matrix size = 128 × 128 × 10). Three sets of echo times likely to be of clinical interest were chosen; one to rotate in-and-out of phase with fat, the second to always be out phase with fat, and the last to match the echo times of 1.52–8.42 ms used in Svedin et al. [19] In the first phantom, two sonications at points 1 and 6 were measured with segEPI, three sonications at points 2, 4, and 5 were measured with SR-VFA-SoS with TE=(2.46+1.23•n) ms, n = 0 to 5, (in-and-out of phase), and three sonications at points 3, 7, and 8 were measured with SR-VFA-SoS with TE=(1.23+2.46•n) ms, n = 0 to 5, (out of phase). In the second phantom, two sonications at points 2 and 7 were measured with segEPI, three sonications at points 1, 3, and 8 were measured with SR-VFA-SoS with TE=(2.46+1.23•n) ms, n = 0 to 5, (in-and-out of phase), and three sonications at points 4, 5, and 6 were measured with SR-VFA-SoS with TE=(1.52+1.38•n) ms, n = 0 to 5, (Svedin et al.'s timing). Because T1 measurements were not assessed in this echo time experiment, two flip angles were not required; therefore, all SR-VFA-SoS images were acquired with a 15° flip angle.

Extended heating time experiment - phantom

The effect of KWIC reconstruction parameters with longer heating schemes at a lower power was also investigated. Two phantoms were used, with 4 sonications (points 1–4, 123.32 s duration, 6–7 W) performed in each. SegEPI (FOV = 192 × 114 × 20 mm, matrix size = 128 × 76 × 10) was used to measure temperature change in the first phantom and SR-VFA-SoS (FOV = 192 × 192 × 20 mm; matrix size = 128 × 128 × 10; TE=(2.46+1.23•n) ms, n = 0 to 5) was used to measure change in the second phantom. Acquired resolution was 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm for all MRTI sequences in this experiment. All SR-VFA-SoS images were acquired with a 15° flip angle.

In vivo rabbit experiment

The animal study was carried out under the guidance of the local Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. The in vivo experiment was performed using a different 256-element MRI-compatible phased-array transducer (13 cm radius of curvature, f = 950 kHz, 1.84 × 1.84 × 10.3 mm FWHM of pressure profile as measured in water, Image Guided Therapy, Pessac, France, and Imasonic, Voray-sur-l'Ognon, France). A female New Zealand white rabbit (N = 1) was anesthetized with ketamine and xylazine (25 and 5 mg/kg, intramuscular injection, respectively), intubated, and maintained with isoflurane (1–3%, inhaled, 100% oxygen). The animal's legs were clipped with electric clippers and a 40 blade and then shaved with a single blade straight razor to remove the hair prior to treatment.

The rabbit was placed in lateral decubitus position on an MR-compatible FUS system (Fig. 2B and D), with the leg coupled to the transducer via degassed and deionized water (35 °C). Each leg was sonicated (36.8 s duration, 17–21 W) by electronically steering to 4 distinct points for each MRTI sequence (Fig. 2F), with the validated segEPI sequence (right leg: FOV = 192 × 156 × 20 mm, matrix size = 128 × 104 × 10, TE = 9.4 ms; left leg: FOV = 231 × 158.8 × 12 mm, matrix size = 192 × 132 × 10, TE = 13 ms) used for MRTI for points 1–4 and the SR-VFA-SoS sequence (right leg: FOV = 192 × 192 × 20 mm; matrix size = 128 × 128 × 10; TE=(2.46+1.23•n) ms, n = 0 to 5; left leg: FOV = 231 × 231 × 12 mm; matrix size = 192 × 192 × 10; TE=(2.46+1.23•n) ms, n = 0 to 5) for points 5–8. MRTI of all eight sonications in the right leg was performed with a voxel size of 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm and for all eight sonications in the left leg with a voxel size of 1.2 mm isotropic. Sonicated points were grouped to form squares with 4 mm spacing. 14 mm separated each of the groups of sonications. A cooling time of 5 min was allowed to elapse between the end of one sonication and the beginning of the next.

Temperature precision was calculated for the reconstructed PRFS maps of both the segEPI and SR-VFA-SoS sequences using a nonheated region of rabbit muscle in the same manner as the previous voxel size phantom experiment.

SR-VFA-SoS reconstruction

All reconstruction was performed retrospectively using MATLAB 2024b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) on an Intel® Xeon® Silver 4210 CPU (2.20 GHz). Reconstruction of the SR-VFA-SoS images was performed using a KWIC method [21] for increased temporal resolution, with a sliding asymmetric window, 13 central projections per dynamic, and 13 projections between KWIC frames. Reconstruction was performed using KWIC windows of 89, 144, 233 and 377 projections. Echo combination was done by weighting the individual echoes by TE•∣m∣2 (where m is the complex MRI signal) for PRFS thermometry. [35] Trajectory-matched baseline temperatures were subtracted from dynamic temperatures. Temporal resolution for the PRFS temperatures is the product of the number projections slid between KWIC frames, the TR, and the number of partitions.

Results

Voxel size experiment

Results from this experiment are summarized in Table 2. As shown in Fig. 3A, for an MRTI voxel size of 1.2-mm isotropic, mean peak segEPI temperatures reached 14.2 ± 0.5 °C and mean SR-VFA-SoS peak temperatures ranged from 13.1 to 14.8 °C dependent on the number of projections used in the reconstruction. Peak temperatures measured with SR-VFA-SoS were delayed in comparison with the peak segEPI temperatures, with larger KWIC window lengths producing larger peak temperature delays. Peak temperatures were determined to not be significantly different between the segEPI and SR-VFA-SoS thermometry (p > 0.05). The mean CTD for all runs (Fig. 3C) calculated from the segEPI MRTI data reached 31.3 CEM at 43 °C, while CTD calculations from the SR-VFA-SoS data reached the same or higher for all KWIC window lengths (range 31.3–84.0 CEM at 43 °C). The calculated temperature precision for the segEPI sequence was 0.53 ± 0.09 °C. Temperature precision for the SR-VFA-SoS sequence ranged from 0.32 to 0.39 °C. Peak T1 change (Fig. 3E) ranged from 29.7 to 39.9% and T1 precision ranged from 4.5 to 6.4% for SR-VFA-SoS (it was not calculated for SegEPI).

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrate that the SR-VFA-SoS method in combination with the KWIC reconstruction method has potential to be an effective MRTI sequence for measuring temperature changes in both water and fat based tissues at a temporal resolution needed for MRgFUS. Utilizing the KWIC method decreased the time between images from ~1 min down to ~2 s. However, higher effective temporal resolution is achieved when the dynamic change is relatively large in volume, such that much of its MR signal is represented at the center of k-space. Because outer rings of the KWIC window contain successively more projections, volumetrically small dynamic changes represented in the outer rings of the KWIC k-space would have lower effective temporal resolution. Care must be taken to understand and compensate for the temporal blurring effect of KWIC reconstruction. If the ultrasound focal spot is small, the dynamic temperature data will be represented away from the center of k-space, areas which are comprised of projections acquired over a relatively large temporal window. This causes apparent delays in both heating and cooling. When the number of projections included at the edges of k-space is reduced, the overall temporal footprint of high frequency data is likewise reduced, allowing for the reconstructed images to more accurately capture heating and cooling timings.

As shown in Fig. 3A, at high (1.2-mm isotropic) spatial resolution, peak temperatures measured with SR-VFA-SoS thermometry reconstructed with KWIC overestimated or underestimated segEPI peak temperatures by up to 1.1 °C. KWIC window lengths of 233 and 144 projections provided the highest temperature increases, slightly overestimating the peak segEPI thermometry, while KWIC window lengths of 377 and 89 underestimated the peak segEPI thermometry. Even with the under- and overestimation of peak temperature, there were no significant differences found between SR-VFA-SoS and segEPI peak temperatures. Shorter KWIC windows led to smaller timing delays between peak segEPI and SR-VFA-SoS temperatures, with peak temperatures for KWIC window lengths of 377 and 233 lagging by 8.8 and 6.9 s and KWIC window lengths of 144 and 89 lagging by 3.0 and 1.1 s.

Unfortunately, a 1.2-mm isotropic voxel size is not currently a clinically attainable resolution for many applications of MRgFUS, as it cannot reasonably cover the field-of-view required for treatment and lacks the necessary SNR required for desired measurement precision. Feddersen et al., [36] in a review of 10 clinical studies for MR thermometry in hyperthermia treatments, found that reported spatial resolutions varied from 8.8 to 156.0 mm3. In this work, segEPI SNR was 64% lower in the 1.2-mm isotropic voxel size images than the 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm voxel size images. For the SR-VFA-SoS images, SNR of the 1.2-mm isotropic voxel size images ranged from 15 to 49% lower than the 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm voxel size images, depending on the KWIC window length. Higher SNR values of the 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm voxel size images did not decrease the error between SR-VFA-SoS and segEPI peak temperatures. Improved temperature and T1 precision was achieved at the higher SNR 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm voxel size images when compared with the 1.2-mm isotropic voxel size images.

At a spatial resolution of 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm shown in Fig. 3B, SR-VFA-SoS temperatures at all KWIC window lengths underestimated measured peak heating compared to the validated segEPI thermometry, demonstrating significant difference at KWIC window lengths of 377 and 89. This suggests as voxel sizes approach currently used clinical dimensions, the effect of temporal blurring results in lower measured peak temperatures. While a large, evenly distributed heating may be captured in the low frequency center of k-space and thus may be adequately measured with larger voxel sizes, heating at a small ultrasound focus extends farther out into high frequency k-space, requiring smaller voxel sizes to adequately capture the dynamics of the heating. This temporal blurring reduces the initial apparent heating and cooling rates, extending the apparent duration and delaying the timing of the peak temperature. Temporal delays between peak segEPI temperatures and SR-VFA-SoS temperatures at 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm resolution were 0.035 ± 0.004 s per projection included in the KWIC window.

CTD calculations had high variance dependent on the KWIC window size for both spatial resolutions. Lagging heating and cooling shown in the SR-VFA-SoS sequence extends the apparent time tissue temperature is elevated, increasing the calculated CTD, while overestimated or underestimated peak temperatures increase or decrease the calculated CTD accordingly. For the 1.2-mm isotropic voxel size SR-VFA-SoS sequence, the KWIC reconstructions with 377 and 89 projections fell closest to the segEPI dose calculation, while calculated CTD of the 233 and 144 projection lengths more than doubled that of the segEPI dose. At the larger voxel size, the CTD of the SR-VFA-SoS sequence exceeded that of the segEPI sequence for KWIC window lengths of 233 and 144 and underestimated CTD at lengths of 377 and 89. These inaccuracies in thermal dose calculations can potentially lead to overestimation or underestimation of thermal exposure for MRgFUS patients, resulting in excessive heating or lower efficacy of treatments. This can potentially have severe effects on the accuracy of lesion size calculation in therapies such as essential tremor, [37] prostate treatment, [38] delivered drug dose in FUS-mediated drug delivery, [39] and treatments relying on a thermal dose-regulating controller. [40]

Calculated T1 changes followed the same pattern as the PRFS temperatures from the reconstructed SR-VFA-SoS images, demonstrating that KWIC window effects on amplitude and timing are thermometry-method agnostic. KWIC window lengths of 233 and 144 projections showing larger changes in T1 than seen in lengths of 377 and 89. The rate of change of T1 was near 2.5%/°C for all sonications, within the range of 1–3%/°C found in many biological tissues. [1]

Significant improvement of temperature and T1 precision (p < 0.001 and p = 0.002, respectively) at larger voxel sizes was shown for the SR-VFA-SoS sequence. Temperature precision also improved at the higher voxel size for the segEPI sequence. At the 1.2-mm isotropic voxel size, the SR-VFA-SoS sequence had improved precision when compared with the segEPI sequence, while both sequences had similar precision at the 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm voxel size. For both voxel sizes, SR-VFA-SoS temperature precision was best at the 377 projection KWIC window length.

As shown in Fig. 4, the temperature inaccuracies from temporal blurring due to the KWIC window observed in SR-VFA-SoS MR thermometry appear to be independent of echo time, indicating that the primary factors driving these discrepancies are voxel size and temporal footprint rather than sequence timing. However, future work should evaluate echo time effects in combined fat-water tissues or phantoms, as the phantom in this study had lower than 5% fat composition.

For slower and longer heating, as shown in Fig. 5, the temporal lag in heating seen in SR-VFA-SoS temperature measurements in comparison to the segEPI temperatures can eventually be overcome, though apparent heating time is still elongated compared to segEPI temperatures. Extended heating time, often seen in hyperthermia or drug delivery applications, [41-43] reduces the difference in phase changes between the beginning and end of the KWIC window, reducing the apparent temporal blurring of the heating. While the heating curve of the 89 projection KWIC window never reached the segEPI heating, those of the 377, 233, and 144 projection length windows did after approximately one full image acquisition. Accurate slow, long heating thermometry using a similar sequence was also demonstrated by Zhang et al. [28] with low intensity 8 min heating, suggesting that the inaccuracies introduced by KWIC reconstruction with the SR-VFA-SoS sequence may be more pronounced in high intensity, short duration heating.

As the KWIC method has a large temporal footprint for high frequency k-space data, even for accurate peak temperatures, the calculated temperature curve will always lag in both the heating and cooling phases of short, high intensity heating as demonstrated in both this work and by Svedin et al. [19] KWIC windows with large temporal footprints exaggerate this effect, while shorter temporal footprints partially mitigate it, as seen in Fig. 5, where the absolute differences in temperature from the segEPI in both the heating and cooling phases directly correlate with temporal footprint length. Short KWIC windows, however, can produce undersampled images with increased blurring and streaking, as shown in both the in vivo magnitude and thermometry images in Fig. 7, resulting in underestimated peak temperatures. The trade-off between temperature accuracy and artifact severity must therefore be carefully considered when selecting KWIC parameters for MRgFUS applications.

High variation in temperature during in vivo treatment, as seen in Fig. 5 at both voxel sizes and across both MRTI sequences, are likely due to physiological conditions rather than the sequences themselves. In vivo thermal MRgFUS applications experience confounding factors that impact temperature accuracy, such as motion [44-46] and dynamically changing physiological tissue properties. [47-52] In the in vivo model, the SR-VFA-SoS sequence showed improved temperature precision at both voxel sizes when compared with the segEPI sequence, likely due to the longer temporal footprint of each SR-VFA-SoS image. Lower variation seen in the MRgFUS experiments in phantoms indicate a controlled environment free of many of these confounding factors.

Several potential strategies may mitigate the limitations of SR-VFA-SoS with KWIC in MRgFUS. Choosing KWIC window lengths that reduce the number of projections included but still fully sample the heating could balance between accurate peak temperatures and reduced temporal delay while still maintaining adequate temperature precision. Alternative ways of handling subsampled k-space data, such as model predictive filtering, [53,54] could also be applied to compensate for the effects of undersampling in measured temperatures and reduce temporal delay. Extending sonication durations when appropriate would allow for a more even distribution of heating through the KWIC window, reducing peak temperature inaccuracies. Focus enlargement due to heat conduction would also allow for the heating to be more adequately captured at the center of k-space used in the most recent projections of the KWIC window.

The significant advantage of the SR-VFA-SoS sequence over other sequences is the simultaneous PRFS and T1-based thermometry for aqueous and fatty tissues at adequate temporal resolution for short-duration FUS using only a single baseline acquisition. The implementation of KWIC reconstruction leverages the inherent oversampling of the radial sequence to increase the temporal resolution of the MRTI to a small fraction of the total image acquisition time. As shown in this work, the KWIC reconstruction method has similar effects on the accuracy of calculated T1 change compared to the PRFS temperature, with temporal blurring apparent in changing T1 at both voxel sizes. Before clinical use, it is recommended that the accuracy of both the T1 and PRFS-based thermometry techniques with KWIC during FUS heating should be assessed using gold-standard fiber optic probes in the same manner as the validated segEPI sequence used as a comparison in this work [29]. Additional work could also be conducted comparing SR-VFA-SoS T1 measurements using KWIC reconstruction to T1 obtained using conventional VFA sequences.

These findings highlight the complex interplay between k-space sampling strategies, temporal resolution, and temperature accuracy in MRgFUS thermometry. Further research is needed to refine KWIC-based acquisition techniques for MRTI and to develop robust imaging parameters suitable for clinically viable MRgFUS with the SR-VFA-SoS sequence in both phantom and in vivo environments, with the parameters tuned on an application-specific basis to meet the safety and efficacy needs of the clinical application and desired outcome. While a limitation of the presented work was comparing the SR-VFA-SoS measured T1 to another technique, the presented SR-VFA-SoS sequence with KWIC reconstruction would provide clinicians insight into the thermal effects of MRgFUS on both aqueous and fatty tissues at temporal and spatial resolutions suitable for clinical applications.
