Study of memory-dependent dynamics of a predator–prey model with hyperbolic mortality
Authors: R. Mahalakshmi, M. Sivakumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper employs the Caputo fractional derivative to analyze a fractional-order prey–predator model distinguished by hyperbolic mortality, incorporating memory effects to augment the realism of species interactions. According to fixed point theory, it is proven that the system is well-posed ensuring the existence, uniqueness, positivity, and boundedness of the solution. Local stability is studied using the Jacobian matrix and fractional-order stability criteria. We conduct numerical simulations to corroborate the theoretical findings, and an error analysis utilizing step-size refinement demonstrates that the numerical scheme converges and is precise. Also, a sensitivity analysis is done to see how important parameters affect the dynamics of the system.

Introduction

The mathematical modeling is an effective methodological tool, which gives the theoretical ecologists the ability to deconstruct and capture the dynamic behavior of complex ecological assemblages. The study of predator–prey interactions has continued to hold central place in the discipline, as inter specific interactions are widespread in nature and essential for maintain ecological balance. Predator–prey relationships have been studied using classical population theory as well as more complex models that incorporate behavioral, spatial and fractional dynamics. The notable study by Holling1 was the first attempt to establish the types of functional responses by explaining the dynamics of prey density dependence of the rate of predator consumption. This main idea is basis of the further variation of the Lotka-Volterra model. Based on these classical theories, Ma et al.2 took prey refuge and predator mutual interference into account, which shows the predator mutual interference can make the system more stable and can avoid unreasonable growth. Likewise, a parallel extension of this framework by Kumar et al.3 which incorporates the effects of fear and gestation delay and shows that delayed reproduction and prey refuge can lead to Hopf bifurcation and longer period oscillations in population densities.

With the integration of behavioral ecology into mathematical modeling, scientists have started investigating the role of predation fear in prey dynamics. Wang et al.7 used this idea and developed a fear-based predator–prey model that showed the stress derived through fear may inhibit prey reproduction, influence the efficiency of predators, and change the equilibrium positions. Building on it, a high degree of fear can indirectly control the disease’s transmission across population of prey. Moreover fractional derivative is also used in study of disease’s transmission such as to model and analyze monkey pox4, in study of integral derivative control on cancer dynamics5, in simulation of COVID-19 model with booster dose6. According to Barman et al.8 investigation of trade-off between fear and rate of infection. These pieces of work compelled the world to accept that fear is not a psychological force but an ecological one, which is very powerful tool of influencing the stability of a system. Subsequently, Mandal et al.9 studied the aspects of forming spatiotemporal patterns in a snowball demonstration of fear and anti-predator strategies in a reaction-diffusion model and found transitions between turing, stripe, and spot patterns depending on the level of fear.

In another attempt to enhance realism in ecological experiments, Gong10 used SIR model with medical observation to analyze with fractional derivative with Hattaf-Yousfi functional responses. The scientists incorporated social and cooperation behaviors. Berec observed the mechanism of foraging among predators11. The results indicate that the effective rate of predation is changed by the cooperation of hunters and predation oscillates with the intensity of cooperation by stabilization or destabilization. Capone et al.12 recognize the turing patterns, the result of spatial interactions among predators. Equally, Meeroupaghi et al.13 and Sounah et al.14 investigated the compressible wave of spatiotemporal behavior due to herd movement, cross-diffusion, and fear on the example that oscillating behavior patterns and patchy population distributions produced by behavioral grouping.

Allee effects and nonlinear mortality38 was also noted in the works in recent decades. Pal et al.15 made a finding that hunting pressure and Allee effects might result in pattern formation by bifurcation in an altered Leslie Gower system, and Mandal et al.16 found that bubbles might appear and drop in nonlinearities that are created by allee. In the paper17 Sambath et al. also previously examined a diffusive predator prey hyperbolic mortality system found the conditions of the Hopf bifurcation and diffusion induced instability, thus forming the foundation of investigation of realistic diffusion models in biology. Coexisting with these ecological developments, the invention of fractional calculus afforded new possibilities for the description of systems with memory effect and hereditary effects. Ahmed et al.18 generalized the Routh-Hurwitz stability criterion for the fractional order system and applied it to chaotic systems like the Lorenz system and the Chua system thereby giving the mathematical tools which can be applied to the dynamics of the predator–prey in various fractional-orders of a system. Based on this framework, a few new ecological models make use of the concept of fractional derivatives to characterize ecological memory and ecological dependence.

Besides this, an age-structured model with infected predators and prey presented by Bentout et al.19 showed that the age dynamics, distinctions and new oscillatory behavior could be observed in the infected prey. Their results relate disease ecology to predator prey theory and relate behavioral dynamics to epidemiological dynamics. Collectively, the contributions, including functional responses, fear effects, fractional-order stability, as well as diffusion-driven patterns, form a complete picture of the mechanisms of the persistence, stability, and pattern formation of populations in predator–prey interactions due to complex interactions and nonlinear feedbacks.

Recent research has shown that fractional-order models are effective in representing memory and non-local dynamics in ecological and epidemiological studies. Specifically, fractional formulations have been widely used in models of infectious disease co-infection, plankton dynamics, and predator–prey interactions, as shown in20,23–28. This highlights the influence of non-singular kernels and temporal memory on how these systems behave. In the fields of control theory and intelligent systems, the combination of fractional calculus with neural networks, multi-agent systems, and data-driven methods has been used to improve synchronization, consensus, and system identification. Adaptive control strategies, sliding mode techniques, and artificial intelligence-based methods have recently been proposed for fractional-order systems, leading to enhanced stability and robustness21,22,29,30. Furthermore, fractional stochastic and reaction diffusion systems, as well as spatial predator prey models that include diffusion and nonlinear interactions, have been the subject of considerable research, revealing stability properties and complex spatiotemporal dynamics shaped by memory effects and cross-diffusion31–35.

Model formation

The classical Lotka Volterra equations provide a conceptual description of the dynamics but the models assume unlimited predation rate, and they do not take it into account. The saturation effects which occur when predator consumption is near its maximum capacity. In order to address these drawbacks, a variety of functional responses have been suggested, due to its mathematical tractability and ecological realism, the Holling type-II response has been the most often employed. It leads to the assumption that the rate of change in the population is only based on the current state. Actual ecological systems do possess a memory and heritage effect, i.e. gestation, maturation, and slowness in responding to environmental perturbations. Such dynamics are better represented using fractional-order derivatives, since they are the generalization of classical calculus with an introduction of non-local time dependence.

Based on these, researchers have generalized classical integer-order prey predator models to counterparts of fractional-order models, replacing the standard derivative with a Caputo fractional derivative of order lies between 0 to 1. The change permits the model to sum the effects of earlier states on existing phenomena in order to provide more realistic and generalized model of studying long term ecological behavior and multifaceted oscillatory behaviors of prey species and predator species.

The model follows the the non dimensional form derived in17, where the system was non dimensionalized using appropriate scaling of population densities and time. The model is based on the use of logistic growth of the prey, a nonlinear functional response of the predator, and a density-dependent mortality response of the predator. Under our current formulation, the state variables, i.e. the density of the prey and the predator population respectively are represented by v and w. The different parameters and functions that are incorporated in the model have clear biological interpretations. v is the prey’s population density, whereas w is the density of predation. The r in this case is the inherent rate of growth of the prey and K is the ecological carrying capacity. Constant b indicates the highest rate at which the predator takes up their prey, and a indicates the density of prey at which the predator achieves its highest rate, which is often known as the half saturation constant. The coefficient m is the effectiveness of conversion, or birth rate, of the predator. The natural mortality rate of the predator is a natural phenomenon that is represented by the functional term of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h(w)=\displaystyle \frac{\gamma w^2}{e+\eta w}$$\end{document} and can be formulated as a density dependence. Also, the form and the severity of the mortality of the predator are modulated using the constants of the γ, e, and η.

There is a logistic growth in the prey population distorting without predation and the predation effect is represented using Holling type-II functional response, which is the fraction of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\displaystyle \frac{v}{\beta +v}$$\end{document}. This is an effective response curve that captures the saturation of predator at elevated levels of prey density. Both prey consumption and nonlinear, density-dependent mortality term, h(w), define the predator dynamics, thus consistent with previous studies into predator self-limitation processes. The efficiency of predation versus prey growth, relative predator growth rate, ratio of half-saturation parameter and carrying capacity expressed as the dimensionless parameters of δ, α, and β. To simplify the mathematical treatment as well as to highlight important ecological interactions, these non-dimensionalized have been used widely in analyzing fractions of predator–prey models36,37,39,40.

This model then also has a number of ecologically relevant phenomena simultaneously as the logistic growth of the prey, predator feeding saturation and the refuge effects in addition to density-dependent predator mortality. We will strictly examine the well-posedness of the system and quantify the stability characteristics of system equilibria under the effects of fractional order dynamics in the ensuing sections.

Fractional order system

The present investigation expand the integer-order predator–prey model used by Sivakumar et al. which included a hyperbolic predator mortality to capture self-restrictive behavior and studied spatiotemporal patterns. In this paper we push this construct to the higher level to the fractional-order, so that the Caputo derivative is taken in order to reflect the memory-related processes and inheriting traits.

A fractional model is a mathematical system where the dynamics of systems are modeled using the aid of fractional calculus26. Unlike standard integer order differential equations, the derivatives of non integer order are used in a fractional model. In many situations, the current configuration of the system would not be determined by instantaneous structure alone but the total of its historical development. Thus expressing the attribute of memory and heredity.

Traditional ODEs do not possess these subtle, and so the memory effect naturally implants itself in a fractional differential equation, whose operators are non-local in nature. Without any doubt, the suitability of the use of the fractional models lies more particularly in the systems whose future development would be dependent not only on the present state but also on the past dynamics of those systems (Fig. 1).

Well-posedness of the solution

The solution of the fractional order predator prey model is well-posed, which is the necessary condition of their application in theoretical ecology and bio mathematics. It attempts to ensure that the mathematical models properly represent the real world population dynamics by ensuring that the solution is existent, unique, positive and bounded. These are verified in the following sections.

Conclusion

This paper examines a fractional order predator–prey model that includes hyperbolic mortality. The goal is to understand how memory and inheritance affect how populations interact. The existence, uniqueness, positivity, and boundedness of the solutions have been established, ensuring the well-posedness and biologically feasibility of the model. The local stability of both the predator free equilibrium \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_1$$\end{document} and the co-existence equilibrium \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E^*$$\end{document} has been investigated and sufficient conditions for their asymptomatic stability have been derived. Furthermore, numerical simulations based on a suitable predictor-corrector scheme have been performed to validate the theoretical results and illustrate the influences of the fractional order parameter on system dynamics.

From an application standpoint, the suggested model gave significant insights into ecosystem stability and species coexistence, which are crucial in ecological management and conservation. The fractional framework allows for a more accurate representation of biological systems by incorporating memory effects hence enhancing the understanding of population dynamics over time. Future study may focus on extending the present model to accommodate additional complexity such as time delays, stochastic disturbance, and spatial diffusion and testing the model with real ecological data.
