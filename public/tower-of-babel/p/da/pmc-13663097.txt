EBER-associated promoter contacts mark accessible but transcriptionally constrained RNAPII promoter hubs in EBV-positive B cells
Authors: Shaik Ismail Mohammed Thangameeran, Hemalatha Mani, Chih-Wen Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-encoded small RNAs (EBERs) are abundant viral noncoding RNAs that associate with host chromatin, but the promoter-scale consequences of those contacts remain unclear. We re-analyzed GSE281522 in EBV-positive GM12878 cells integrating RNA–DNA contacts, RNAPII ChIA-PET, RNA-seq and internal ATAC-seq after EBER depletion. Promoters were classified according to increased RNAPII-associated promoter-looping signal after EBER depletion relative to the matched control conditions, defined as LoopRecovery; increased transcriptional output, defined as ExprUp; or the coordinated occurrence of both responses, defined as Recovery + Up. EBER-contacted promoters were markedly depleted for Recovery + Up relative to non-contacted promoters (0.42% vs 2.00%; OR ~ 0.21; Fisher’s exact p ~ 6.3 × 10–5), and the association between increased promoter looping and transcriptional upregulation was attenuated among EBER-contacted promoters. This depletion remained directionally stable across alternative threshold grids, loop-support and expression filters, and promoter-window definitions using TSS-centered intervals of ± 1 kb, ± 2 kb and ± 5 kb (ORs 0.416, 0.208 and 0.110, respectively). To test whether the constrained class simply reflected inactive chromatin, we integrated internal ATAC-seq from the same study. Constraint-core promoters, defined as EBER-contacted promoters that did not enter Recovery + Up, remained accessible, with ATAC overlap observed in 89.38% versus 68.38% of comparator promoters in any replicate and higher replicate-averaged ATAC signal (median 42.55 vs. 10.43). Ranked Hallmark enrichment and top-contact over-representation converged on TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB as the strongest pathway-level signal, and candidate loci including RIPK2, IL15RA, ICAM1, TNFAIP3 and NFKBIA defined accessible but constrained promoter hubs. Control RNA contact maps did not reproduce a uniform EBER-like phenotype. Together, these analyses support a model in which EBER-associated contacts mark accessible yet transcriptionally constrained RNAPII promoter hubs in EBV-positive B cells.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13072-026-00688-9.

Introduction

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) establishes lifelong latent infection in the majority of humans and is causally linked to multiple lymphoid and epithelial malignancies, making EBV one of the most clinically significant persistent human viruses [1]. A defining feature of latent EBV infection is the extraordinary abundance of viral noncoding RNAs, particularly EBV-encoded small RNAs (EBER1/EBER2), which can accumulate to very high copy numbers and have been repeatedly implicated in host pathway modulation [2, 3]. EBER1 and EBER2 were initially identified as abundant viral ribonucleoproteins associated with the host La protein, and subsequent studies demonstrated their predominant nuclear localization in the EBV-infected cells, thus providing an important basis for investigating their nuclear and chromatin-proximal functions [4–6].

Beyond serving as markers of infection, EBERs engage innate immune sensing pathways: EBERs can be recognized by retinoic acid-inducible gene (RIG-I) to activate type I interferon signaling, and extracellular EBER1 has been reported to stimulate inflammatory programs through Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3)-dependent mechanisms [7, 8]. Earlier biochemical studies also linked EBER1 to the double-stranded RNA-dependent protein kinase PKR. EBER1 was reported to bind PKR and influence its activation in vitro [9, 10]. However, subsequent cell-based work showed that although EBER expression could protect some EBV-positive B-cell models from interferon-α-induced apoptosis, this protection was not mediated by inhibition of PKR activation [11]. Consistently, studies using recombinant EBER-deleted EBV did not identify a uniform EBER-dependent resistance to the interferon-mediated effects in transformed B cells [12]. These findings indicate that the functional consequences of EBER-PKR interactions depend on the cellular and experimental contexts. In parallel, EBERs can participate in more direct nuclear regulatory interactions, for example, EBER2 has been shown to guide host factors to specific genomic loci, supporting a model in which EBV ncRNAs can influence gene regulation through chromatin-proximal mechanisms [13]. Additional work has shown that EBER2 functions within ribonucleoprotein assemblies involving host RNA-binding proteins and host RNAs, reinforcing the idea that EBV noncoding RNAs can act as locus-selective regulatory scaffolds rather than passive by-products of latency [3, 14].

Although EBER1 and EBER2 are frequently considered together, they should not be assumed to act through identical molecular mechanisms. EBER1 has been shown to bind and relocalize ribosomal L22, whereas EBER2 can interact with nascent RNA and recruit the B-cell transcription factor PAX5 to specific sites on EBV chromatin [13, 15]. Consistent with these mechanistic differences, a study using recombinant EBV carrying selective deletion of EBER1 or EBER2 identified partially distinct host gene-expression profiles in lymphoblastoid B-cell lines [16] .

Transcriptional output is fundamentally constrained by three-dimensional (3D) genome organization. RNA polymerase II (RNAPII)-associated enhancer-promoter and promoter-promoter interactions concentrate transcription machinery and regulatory factors, enabling coordinated gene activation. Promoter-centered RNAPII ChIA-PET maps have provided a quantitative framework for linking transcription-associated loops to gene regulation [17]. EBV itself is a chromatinized episome whose latent programs are shaped by epigenetic regulation and 3D architecture, including CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF)/cohesin-associated organization and latency-type–specific conformational states [18]. Together, these observations motivate the idea that EBV can tune host transcription by intersecting viral RNA biology with host 3D regulatory architecture. Recent quantitative and mechanistic work has further emphasized that enhancer-promoter communication is dynamic and tightly coupled to RNAPII behavior rather than being a static wiring diagram, which strengthens the rationale for analyzing coordinated changes in promoter looping and transcriptional output [19, 20] .

Recent RNA-chromatin contact mapping technologies (e.g., GRID-seq, RADICL-seq, and ChAR-seq) have enabled genome-wide measurement of RNA–DNA interaction landscapes and have accelerated efforts to connect chromatin-associated RNAs to candidate regulatory target regions [21–23]. However, an unresolved challenge is translating RNA-chromatin contact measurements into interpretable promoter-level regulatory associations. Contact maps can reflect coordinated changes across multiple regulatory layers, including looping, transcription, complicating inference from contact frequency alone. Recent comparative analyses of RNA-chromatin interactome datasets have underscored that resolution, completeness, and specificity differ substantially across assays, making functional interpretation strongest when contact maps are integrated with orthogonal perturbation and regulatory readouts [24] .

A recent multi-omics study in GM12878 cells integrated RNA–DNA contact mapping with RNAPII ChIA-PET and RNA-seq following EBER depletion and provided evidence that EBV noncoding RNAs, particularly EBERs, are linked to host gene repression and altered RNAPII-associated chromatin looping. That study established the underlying EBV-host chromatin interactome and provides a strong resource for promoter-centered integrative reanalysis of coordinated architectural and transcriptional responses [25]. Building on this resource, we asked whether EBER-linked promoter contact marks promoters that are less likely to undergo coordinated RNAPII loop recovery and transcriptional upregulation after EBER depletion. To address this, we decomposed promoter responses into looping recovery, transcriptional upregulation, and a coordinated “Recovery + Up” state, and then tested whether EBER contact status modulates loop-expression coupling after adjusting for baseline loop signal and expression. Because RNA-associated contact maps need not be functionally equivalent, we also examined whether the EBER-associated pattern was distinguishable from other RNA-linked contact profiles within the same dataset. This framework enables a concise evaluation of whether EBER-linked chromatin contact marks reduced coordinated promoter recovery and attenuated RNAPII loop-expression coupling.

Here we extend the promoter-centred reanalysis by testing whether EBER-linked promoter constraint is robust to response thresholds, promoter-window definition, and filtering strategy. We further integrate orthogonal ATAC-seq from the same study to distinguish constrained-but-accessible promoters from simply inactive promoters, and we use ranked Hallmark analyses to refine the biological context of EBER contact burden. This revised framework asks whether EBER-associated contacts identify accessible RNAPII promoter hubs that remain constrained from coordinated transcriptional recovery after EBER depletion.

Materials and methods

Data source and study design

All analyses were performed as a computational reanalysis of the publicly available multi-omics dataset GSE281522 [25]. The dataset includes genome-wide RNA–chromatin interaction profiles for EBV noncoding RNAs, RNAPII-associated chromatin interaction maps, and transcriptome measurements generated under EBER depletion and matched control conditions in GM12878 cells. No new data were generated in this study. Primary analyses focused on promoter-level integration of EBER contact, RNAPII loop dynamics, and transcriptional response after EBER depletion. Additional public-data layers were incorporated for orthogonal validation and sensitivity analyses, including internal ATAC-seq peak calls from GSE281522, external cytokine-response differential expression tables from GSE252480, promoter coordinated reconstruction using GENCODE v49 basic gene annotation, and Hallmark pathway gene sets obtained from MSigDB. The source dataset did not provide EBER1- and EBER2-resolved RNA-chromatin contact maps or selective depletion conditions. Therefore, the terms “EBER contact” and “EBER depletion” used throughout this study refer to aggregate EBER-associated measurements and a non-isoform-resolved EBER perturbation respectively.

Promoter selection and pre-processing

We loaded the processed gene/promoter summary object from analysis/expr2.rds and required the following columns for downstream analyses: gene_name, log2FC_loop, log2FC_TPM, eber_contacted, NC_mean_TPM, KD_mean_TPM, NC_mean_loop, KD_mean_loop, and anchor_pet_sum. Promoters were filtered to retain loop-quantified and transcriptionally measurable genes by applying: (i) non-missing loop fold-change, (ii) expression threshold (NC_mean_TPM ≥ 0.2 OR KD_mean_TPM ≥ 0.2), and (iii) loop-support threshold (NC_mean_loop + KD_mean_loop ≥ 5). For analyses integrating external promoter-contact summaries derived from other RNA-associated loop files, gene identifiers were standardized by removing Ensembl version suffixes prior to merging. Baseline covariates were defined as log1p(NC_mean_TPM) and log1p(NC_mean_loop). For external validation analyses, host-gene tables were matched to the filtered promoter universe using version-stripped Ensembl gene identifiers, and duplicate gene entries were collapsed by retaining a single record per gene. Promoter windows were reconstructed from transcription start sites using GENCODE v49 basic gene annotation. The primary promoter definition used transcription start site (TSS) ± 2 kb. Sensitivity analyses recomputed promoter-contact assignment and promoter-based overlaps using TSS ± 1 kb and TSS ± 5 kb windows.

EBER contact definition and promoter response-state classification

Promoters were categorized according to whether they show measurable EBER–chromatin contacts, and contacted promoters were further stratified by contact strength (weak vs strong) using an anchor-level contact-intensity metric (anchor_pet_sum) from the processed RNA–chromatin interaction outputs. EBER-contacted promoters were defined using the processed indicator eber_contacted, and contact strength was quantified by the anchor-level contact metric anchor_pet_sum. Unless otherwise stated, contacted promoters were split into “weak” versus “strong” contact strata using a pre-specified cutoff on anchor_pet_sum (median split within contacted promoters). Promoter responses to EBER depletion were decomposed into two components: (i) increased RNAPII-associated promoter looping after EBER depletion relative to the matched control condition, defined as LoopRecovery, and (ii) increased gene expression, defined as ExprUp. Here, “recovery” refers only to an increase in promoter-looping signal following EBER depletion and does not imply restoration to a known normal or pre-infection state. These components were combined into a four-state framework: no response (Neither), Loop Recovery only, ExprUp only, and a coordinated Recovery + Up state indicating concurrent increase in promoter looping and gene expression. Primary response thresholds were defined as LoopRecovery = log2FC_loop ≥ 1 and ExprUp = log2FC_TPM ≥ 1; “Recovery + Up” required meeting both criteria. This coordinated state was treated as the primary endpoint because it captures promoter-scaled concordance between architectural and transcriptional response rather than either marginal component alone. For downstream accessibility, external-validation, and candidate-locus analyses, the constraint-core class was defined as EBER-contacted promoters that did not enter the Recovery + Up state under the primary response definition.

Primary depletion analyses

To test whether EBER contact was associated with reduced coordinated promoter activation, we compared the frequency of the Recovery + Up state across contact groups using contingency-based inference and effect-size estimates. Primary depletion tests used two-sided Fisher’s exact tests and reported odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For within-contacted comparisons of contact strength between response groups, we used two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. The main descriptive comparison contrasted EBER-contact versus non-contacted promoters; weak versus strong contacted strata were retained as a stratified descriptive analysis rather than as sole evidence for a continuous dose–response relationship.

Loop-expression coupling analysis

To assess whether EBER contact modifies the functional relationship between promoter loop recovery and transcriptional upregulation, we fit logistic regression models of the form: ExprUp ~ LoopRecovery x contact_bin + log1p(NC_mean_TPM) + log1p(NC_mean_loop) where contact_bin represents promoter contact stratum. The principal model tested whether, the effect of loop recovery on transcriptional upregulation differed between non-contacted and contacted promoters after accounting for baseline expression and baseline loop support. When sparse strata produced instability in standard logistic regression, bias-reduced logistic regression was used. Specifically, Firth bias-reduced estimation was applied to obtain stable coefficient estimates, and stratum-specific coupling effects were reported as ORs with 95% CIs. This analysis was used to evaluate whether EBER-linked promoters show attenuated loop-expression coupling rather than simply fewer positive loop or expression events considered separately.

Robustness and sensitivity analyses

To test whether the primary depletion signal depended on a single response definition or filtering choice, we performed a structured sensitivity framework. First, Recovery + Up depletion was recalculated across a grid of alternative loop-recovery and transcriptional-upregulation thresholds, generating 49 threshold combinations. Second, analyses were repeated under alternative loop-support cutoffs and expression filters while keeping the filtered promoter universe explicit for each model. Third, baseline-adjusted and matched analyses were used as additional controls for baseline expression and loop support. Fourth, promoter-contact assignment was recomputed using TSS-centered promoter windows of ± 1 kb, ± 2 kb, and ± 5 kb. For each sensitivity setting, depletion of Recovery + Up among EBER-contacted promoters was re-estimated with two-sided Fisher’s exact tests and odds ratios using the same endpoint definition for that setting. To test whether the main signal reflected a stable continuous contact relationship rather than only a thresholded grouping, we performed additional robustness analyses using alternative parameterizations of EBER contact. First, we modeled Recovery + Up as a function of continuous contact burden using log1p(anchor_pet_sum) with adjustment for log1p(NC_mean_TPM) and log1p(NC_mean_loop). Second, within contacted promoters only, we tested order decile trend models and quadratic contact models to evaluate monotonic and non-linear within-contact relationships. Third, we tested alternative grouped contrasts including three-group models (non-contacted, low-contact, high-contact) and extreme-threshold models comparing non-contacted promoter with the weakest and strongest contacted subsets. These analyses were interpreted as robustness tests of the contact-strength hypothesis rather than as replacements for the primary depletion comparisons.

RNA-specificity control analyses

To test whether Recovery + Up depletion was specific to EBER-linked contacts rather than a generic property of RNA-associated promoter contact maps, we constructed promoter-contact summaries for three comparison RNAs present in the same dataset: the host long noncoding RNA MALAT1 and the viral RNAs miR-BART and v-snoRNA1. For each RNA, raw Host-Host RNA–DNA loop files were expanded into anchor-level intervals, and either anchor was intersected with gene promoter windows defined as transcription start site ± 2 kb using GENCODE v49 basic gene annotation. For each target gene, promoter contact was summarized by aggregated PET support and number of unique loops across replicates. Nuclear-only promoter-contact summaries were then merged into the expr2 promoter universe by version-stripped Ensembl gene ID.

For each control RNA, genes were classified into four mutually exclusive groups relative to EBER contact status: Neither, control-RNA only, EBER only, and Both. Recovery + Up frequencies were first summarized descriptively across these groups and then tested in bias-reduced logistic regression models adjusting for baseline expression and baseline loop support, using the Neither group as the reference. These analyses were designed to determine whether EBER-associated depletion of coordinated promoter recovery was reproduced by other RNA-linked contact maps.

Pathway analysis

Hallmark gene sets were obtained from MSigDB using msigdbr for Homo sapiens (collection H). Two complementary pathway analyses were performed. First, promoters in the filtered universe were ranked by continuous EBER contact burden using log1p(anchor_pet_sum), where anchor_pet_sum represents anchor-level summed EBER RNA–DNA contact support assigned to each promoter and non-contacted promoters were assigned a contact burden of zero. This ranked list was tested by preranked gene set enrichment analysis with fgseaMultilevel (minSize = 15, maxSize = 500, eps = 1e-10, gseaParam = 1). Second, over-representation analysis tested Hallmark enrichment among the top 5%, 10%, and 20% of genes ranked by EBER contact burden, using the filtered promoter universe as background and Fisher/hypergeometric inference with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Pathway results were interpreted conservatively, with TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB treated as the most robust convergent signal when supported by both ranked and over-representation analyses.

Internal ATAC-seq validation of promoter accessibility

To test whether the constraint-core class reflected promoter inactivity or a constrained-but-accessible state, ATAC-seq peak calls from GSE281522 were intersected with promoter windows derived from GENCODE v49 basic annotation using bedtools. For each promoter, accessibility was summarized as overlap with at least one ATAC peak in any replicate, overlap in a majority of replicates, overlap in all replicates, and replicate-averaged peak score/signal across overlapping peaks. Comparisons between constraint-core promoters and the relevant comparator promoter set were evaluated using two-sided Fisher’s exact test for categorical overlap metrics and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous accessibility signal.

External cytokine-response validation

To assess whether the constrained promoter class identified in GSE281522 showed altered responsiveness under independent stimulation conditions, we analyzed host-gene differential expression tables from GSE252480 for GM12878 cells treated with IL-15 relative to matched controls. The processed supplementary tables were imported from the GEO-hosted workbook, and host-gene records were matched to the filtered expr2 promoter universe using version-stripped Ensembl gene identifiers. For each condition, genes were classified as strongly upregulated using pre-specified combinations or log2 fold-change and adjusted p-value thresholds. The primary threshold was defined as log2FoldChange ≥ 1 and padj < 0.05, and robustness analyses were additionally performed across log2 fold- change thresholds of 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 3.0 together with adjusted p-value thresholds of 0.10, 0.05, and 0.01. For each threshold combination, overlap between the constraint-core gene class and the set of strongly upregulated genes was evaluated using two-sided Fisher’s exact test. External validation was interpreted as context-specific support rather than as a universal cytokine-response phenotype. The strongest supportive signal was observed in GM12878 IL-15 stimulation, and all analyses for IL-15 were performed using the same threshold-grid workflow.

Locus-level RNA-chromatin contact visualization and force-directed RNAPII promoter-contact networks

To provide an illustrative locus-level view of an EBER-associated promoter-hub candidate, we visualized the GMEB1 neighbourhood on chr1 using EBER-RDD host contact arcs and replicate-level RDD anchor-density tracks from GSE281522. The same locus was then represented as a force-directed RNAPII promoter-contact network using RNAPII ChIA-PET loop-anchor graphs. Loop-anchor graphs were constructed with igraph, and locus-restricted 3D force-directed network layouts were rendered with rgl; interactive HTML outputs were produced with rglwidget and static publication panels were post-processed with magick. Nodes represent promoter/loop-anchor regions and edges represent RNAPII-mediated contacts. These images were topology visualizations of an RNAPII contact graph and are not intended as physical 3D reconstructions of chromatin architecture.

Software

All analyses were performed in R (v4.3.3) using dplyr, ggplot2, brglm2, readx1, msigdbr, fgsea, igraph, rgl, htmlwidgets, and magick, together with command-line processing using bedtools. Promoter coordinate reconstruction used GENCODE v49 basic annotation. Genome-browser panels were generated from the processed GSE281522 outputs using the retained browser workflow or optional pyGenomeTracks exports archived as source files.

Data availability

The raw datasets analyzed during the current study are publicly available in the GEO repository under accession GSE281522. The processed data, analysis scripts, and figure files supporting the conclusion of this article are available in the Zenodo repository (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19435095).

Results

A four-state framework separates looping recovery, transcriptional upregulation, and their coordinated response after EBER depletion

To quantify how promoters respond to EBER depletion at the level of transcription-associated 3D architecture, we integrated promoter-scale RNAPII ChIA-PET loop changes with RNA-seq expression changes and decomposed each promoter into four mutually exclusive response states: Neither, LoopRecovery only (loop increase), ExprUp only (expression increase), and the coordinated Recovery + Up state (concurrent loop increase and expression gain). In the primary definition used throughout, LoopRecovery was called at log2FC (loop) ≥ 1, ExprUp at log2FC(TPM) ≥ 1, and Recovery + Up required meeting both criteria. Across loop-change bins, transcriptional upregulation alone occurred across a broad range of loop fold-changes, whereas the coordinated Recovery + Up response was strongly gated by positive loop change, emerging primarily when loop gains reached the higher bins (peaking around moderate-to-strong loop increase) (Fig. 1A). We next asked how these marginal and joint responses redistribute across EBER contact strata. LoopRecovery, ExprUp, and especially Recovery + Up all showed a stepwise reduction moving from not contacted to contacted (weak) to contacted (strong), with the most pronounced drop observed for the coordinated Recovery + Up state (Fig. 1B). At this stage, these patterns are best interpreted as an initial global view of response distribution across contact strata rather than as stand-alone evidence for a continuous contact-strength relationship. Importantly, these patterns are not explained by a global shift in gene expression abundance between conditions or contact groups. Mean expression in KD vs NC largely tracks the diagonal for both EBER-contacted and non-contacted promoters, indicating broadly comparable overall expression levels and no obvious global bias that would trivially force one group into fewer “upregulated” calls (Fig. 1C). Finally, we summarized two key effect-size signals that motivate the subsequent analyses: (i) ExprUp is enriched among LoopRecovery promoters, consistent with loop-expression coupling overall, and (ii) EBER contact is depleted among Recovery + Up promoters, indicating that EBER-contacted promoters are underrepresented in the coordinated response state (Fig. 1D). This primary response definition was subsequently stress-tested across alternative thresholds, loop-support cutoffs, expression filters, and promoter-window definitions. The filtered promoter-universe counts and promoter-class summaries are provided in Additional file 2: Tables S1 and Additional file 3: Table S2.

EBER-contacted promoters are selectively depleted for the coordinated “Recovery + Up” response

Having defined the four promoter response states, we next tested whether EBER-chromatin contact marks promoters that are less likely to undergo coordinated RNAPII loop recovery and transcriptional upregulation after EBER depletion (Recovery + Up). When plotting loop fold-change against expression fold-change, Recovery + Up promoters occupy the upper-right quadrant (log2FC (loop) ≥ 1 and log2FC(TPM) ≥ 1). We quantified this pattern using 2 × 2 association analysis contrasting Recovery + Up vs not Recovery + Up across EBER-contacted vs not contacted promoters. Recovery + Up promoters were markedly less frequent among EBER-contacted genes (0.42%) than among non-contacted genes (2.00%), corresponding to a strong depletion effect (odds ratio ≈ 0.21, Fisher’s exact p ≈ 6.3 × 10⁻5) (Fig. 2B; Additional file 4: Table S3). Thus, EBER contact is not associated with a subtle shift in mean response; rather it is linked to a pronounced reduction in the probability of entering the joint loop-and-expression recovery state.

We next examined whether this depletion differed across the weak and strong contacted strata used in the primary descriptive framework. Stratifying contacted promoters into weak and strong contact groups revealed a clear gradient: the Recovery + Up frequency decreased stepwise from not contacted (2.0%) to weak (0.6%) to strong (0.3%), and the corresponding odds ratio vs the not contacted reference were progressively smaller, suggesting greater depletion with strong grouped contract strata and reduced coordinated promoter activation (Fig. 2C). These grouped comparisons are consistent with heterogeneity across contact strata, but should be interpreted descriptively rather than as definitive evidence for a stable monotonic dose relationship. In additional robustness analyses, continuous-contact, contacted-only decile-trend, and contacted-only nonlinear models did not support a consistent within-contact monotonic gradient (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A-C). Accordingly, the strongest supported conclusion from Fig. 2 is that EBER-contacted promoters are selectively depleted for the coordinated Recovery + Up response, while contact-strength stratification provides exploratory evidence of heterogeneity rather than a stand-alone dose law. Across the expanded sensitivity framework, the depletion of Recovery + Up among EBER-contacted promoters remained directionally stable. All 49 tested loop/expression threshold combinations yielded odds ratios below 1 (median OR = 0.337, range = 0.000–0.932; 32/49 nominal p < 0.05 and 28/49 BH-adjusted p < 0.05; Additional file 1: Fig. S2A-B; Additional file 5: Table S4), and the signal persisted under alternative loop-support thresholds and expression filters (loop-support sensitivity: 7/7 cutoffs with OR < 1, 5/7 BH-adjusted p < 0.05; Additional file 1: Fig. S2C; Additional file 6: Table S5; expression-filter sensitivity: 7/7 cutoffs with OR < 1, 4/7 BH-adjusted p < 0.05; Additional file 1: Fig. S2D; Additional file 7: Tables S6). Recomputing promoter-contact assignment using TSS-centred promoter windows of ± 1 kb, ± 2 kb, and ± 5 kb preserved the depletion (OR = 0.416, 0.208, and 0.110, respectively; full p-values and confidence intervals are provided in Additional file 8: Table S7). These results indicate that the core depletion signal is not an artifact of a single cutoff set or promoter-window definition.

We next asked whether EBER contact strata modulate the coupling between promoter loop recovery and transcriptional upregulation. In a stratum-aware Firth logistic model (including a LoopRecovery x contact_bin interaction and adjusting for baseline expression and baseline loop signal), loop recovery strongly increased the odds of transcriptional upregulation in non-contacted promoters (OR = 2.72, 95% CI 1.11–6.63), whereas this loop expression coupling was near-null in weakly contacted promoters (OR = 1.02, 0.66–1.58) and remained attenuated in strongly contacted promoters (OR = 1.20, 0.69–2.09) (Fig. 3B).

To assess whether these promoter-response patterns were specific to EBER-linked contacts rather than a generic feature of RNA-associated contact maps, we performed parallel control analyses using metastasis associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1), EBV microRNA (miR-BART), and EBV small nucleolar RNA 1 (v-snoRNA1) contact profiles derived from the same dataset. Across these comparisons, none of the control RNAs reproduced a uniform EBER-like depletion pattern. In the MALAT1 comparison, Recovery + Up frequencies remained lower in the EBER-only and Both groups than in the Neither or MALAT1-only groups, but adjusted contrasts were not significant. In the miR-BART comparison, Recovery + Up frequencies were again lower in the EBER-only and Both groups than in the Neither reference group, but the adjusted contrasts remained below significance. In contrast, the v-snoRNA1 comparison showed a distinct pattern, in which the v-snoRNA1-only group showed a higher Recovery + Up probability than the Neither group (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). Together, these control analyses did not reproduce a common RNA-contact signature matching the primary EBER-associated phenotype, supporting the interpretation that the main depletion signal is not readily explained by a generic RNA-contact effect. Baseline-adjusted and matched analyses remained directionally consistent with the primary model, although effect sizes were attenuated in matched comparisons, supporting a cautious interpretation of baseline-dependent confounding rather than a reversal of the primary signal (Additional file 1: Fig S4; Additional files 9–12: Table S8–11).

Discussion

In this computational reanalysis of a published GM12878 multi-omics dataset integrating RNA–chromatin contacts, RNAPII, ChIA-PET, RNA-seq, and internal ATAC-seq EBER depletion, five linked conclusions emerged. First, EBER-contacted promoters are strongly underrepresented among promoters that enter the coordinated Recovery + Up state. Second, this depletion is robust to threshold choice, loop-support and expression filters, and promoter-window definitions. Third, loop-expression coupling remains attenuated in contacted strata even after baseline adjustment. Fourth, orthogonal ATAC-seq indicates that the constraint-core class is largely accessible, shifting the interpretation from promoter inactivity to constrained activation competence. Fifth, ranked pathway and candidate-locus analyses converge on TNFA/NFκB-associated promoter programs as the most reproducible biological context of strong EBER contact burden. Together, these findings support a model in which EBER-associated chromatin contacts mark accessible yet transcriptionally constrained RNAPII promoter hubs rather than simply inactive promoters.

A central conceptual contribution is treating Recovery + Up as a promoter-hub endpoint rather than a trivial overlap of two marginal responses. The promoter-window sensitivity analyses and ATAC results together strengthen this interpretation by showing that the signal is robust to promoter assignment and is not explained by systematic chromatin closure at the constrained class. Promoter-centered RNAPII interactions maps generated by ChIA-PET and related approaches support the view that promoter-promoter and enhancer-promoter connectivity helps organize transcriptional machinery and regulatory factors for coordinated gene activation [17, 19, 20]. More broadly, work on 3D genome organization and loop extrusion has established that transcription operates within multiscale structural constraints and can both influence and respond to chromatin architecture [26, 27]. In this context, the concentration of Recovery + Up events among promoters with weak or absent EBER contacts supports a parsimonious interpretation: strong EBER-promoter contact marks promoters whose RNAPII interaction neighborhoods are less “convertible” into a coordinated activation trajectory upon EBER depletion, reducing the probability that loop gain and transcription gains co-occur in the same promoter state transition. This promoter-centered framing is important because it moves the interpretation away from single-loop logic and toward a neighbourhood-level model of coordinated transcriptional competence [28] .

Importantly, this framework does not require EBERs to directly block loop formation at every contacted promoter. Instead, contact strength may reflect higher-order promoter context; for example, promoters embedded in interaction neighborhoods that are stabilized, buffered, or otherwise constrained such that perturbation-induced loop recovery does not translate into proportional transcriptional upregulation as often. The locus-level visualization (Fig. 5) reinforces the intuition that promoter “loop recovery” can represent multi-edge, neighborhood-level shifts rather than a single isolated loop change, consistent with promoter output emerging from networked RNAPII interactions. This interpretation is also consistent with the broader EBV studies showing that latent programs are maintained on a chromatinized episome whose transcriptional states are shaped by higher-order genome organization, including CTCF/cohesion-associated architecture and long-range chromatin control [18, 25, 28] .

An important qualification is that the present analysis does not distinguish between EBER1- and EBER2-specific contributions. Studies using recombinant EBV carrying selective deletion of either EBER1 or EBER2 have shown that the two transcripts influence partially distinct host gene-expression programs in lymphoblastoid B-cell lines, with broader transcriptional changes reported following EBER2 deletion, although neither deletion markedly impaired primary B-cell transformation or subsequent lymphoblastoid cell-line growth [16]. These differences are consistent with their direct molecular interactions, including the association of EBER1 with ribosomal protein L22 and the ability of EBER2 to interact with nascent RNA and recruit PAX5 to specific site on EBV chromatin. Accordingly, the promoter-hub phenotype identified here cannot be attributed specifically to EBER1 or EBER2 and may reflect the activity of either transcript, their combined effects, or indirect consequences of combined EBER depletion [13, 15] .

Previous gene-expression studies provide important context for the present findings. A study using lymphoblastoid B-cell lines infected with recombinant EBV carrying selective deletion of either EBER1 or EBER2 identified 54 host genes associated with EBER1 expression and 115 associated with EBER2 expression. The EBER1-associated gene set included genes involved in biosynthetic and interferon/antiviral-response functions, whereas the EBER2-associated set included genes involved in apoptosis and membrane-signaling regulation [16]. These findings demonstrated that EBER1 and EBER2 can influence distinguishable host transcriptional programs in B cells. The present study provides a complementary promoter-architecture perspective by showing that aggregate EBER-associated promoter contacts mark accessible promoters that are less likely to undergo coordinated RNAPII loop recovery and transcriptional upregulation after non-isoform-resolved EBER depletion. Thus, the earlier transcript-specific expression results support the broader conclusion that EBERs influence the host immune and signaling programs, while our findings extend this framework by linking EBER-associated contacts to altered promoter-level RNAPII response states. However, because the source dataset does not resolve EBER1 and EBER2 separately, direct correspondence between the previously identified transcript-specific genes and the TNFA/NFκB-associated constrained promoter phenotype cannot be established.

Genetic studies have also produced context-dependent findings regarding the contribution of EBERs to EBV-induced transformation. An early study using recombinant EBV lacking both EBER genes showed that EBER-deficient virus retained the ability to transform primary B lymphocytes and replicate in vitro, indicating that EBERs are not absolutely required for these processes [29]. Subsequent studies using independently generated recombinant viruses found that EBER deletion substantially reduced the efficacy of B-cell growth transformation and further identified EBER2, rather than EBER1, as the principal contributor to efficient transformation in the experimental system [30, 31]. Together, these findings indicate that EBERs are dispensable for the basic establishment of transformed B-cell lines but can influence transformation efficiency in a transcript- and model-dependent manner. This genetic evidence further supports a cautious interpretation of the collective EBER-associated promoter phenotype identified in the present study.

Mechanistically, why might stronger EBER contact track with reduced loop–expression coupling? EBERs are among the most abundant viral noncoding RNAs expressed during EBV latency and have established roles in innate immune signaling and nuclear regulatory biology [2, 7, 32]. Prior work has shown that EBER2 can participate in locus-selective nuclear regulatory interactions. EBER2 binds nascent RNA and recruits PAX5 to EBV chromatin, while also assembling with host RNA-binding proteins involved in viral gene regulation [3, 13]. At the host UCHL1 locus, EBER2 interacts with UCHL1 RNA and, through a NONO-containing complex, promotes PU.1 recruitment to the UCHL1 promoter, thereby increasing UCHL1 expression and B-cell growth [33]. This provides a direct precedent for EBER2-mediated regulation of a cellular promoter; however, the present non-isoform-resolved analysis does not establish whether the broader EBER-associated promoter-hub phenotype operates through the same mechanism.

In parallel, extensive literature supports the idea that transcription occurs within concentrated RNAPII “factories” or compartments and that enhancer-driven activation can involve coactivator/RNAPII clustering and condensate-like assemblies that tune transcriptional output and dynamics. These models are supported by imaging and mechanistic studies showing that RNAPII can operate in discrete transcriptional compartments and that mediator/coactivator assemblies can form condensate-like clusters linked to active transcription [34–36]. Within this conceptual landscape, our results are consistent with at least two non-mutually exclusive hypotheses. First, strong EBER contacts may preferentially occur at promoters positioned within RNA- and RBP-rich transcription compartments, where depletion perturbs loop edges without yielding proportional steady-state transcription gains because the effective transcriptional state is buffered by compartment composition or kinetics. Second, if EBER-associated RNPs sequester or redistribute specific bridging factors needed to convert loop recovery into productive transcription (e.g., factors linking architecture to elongation competence), then stronger contacts could correspond to stronger sequestration and thus weaker coupling, matching the near-null coupling observed in contacted strata (Fig. 3). These mechanisms remain inferential rather than directly demonstrated here, but they convert the observed coupling defect into a concrete set of experimentally testable predictions about EBER-associated RNPs and promoter-hub competence.

Our results also speak to a broader interpretive challenge in RNA–chromatin contact maps. Contact frequencies reflect composite signals shaped by RNA abundance, processing state, subnuclear localization, and assay-specific biases; thus, they are best viewed as empirical proximity/crosslinking propensities rather than simply occupancy at regulatory elements. This caution is well aligned with the RNA-chromatin interactome field, where technologies such as GRID-seq, ChAR-seq, and RADICL-seq have made it clear that contact maps capture structured but assay-dependent proximity landscapes whose functional interpretation is strongest when integrated with orthogonal regulatory layers and perturbation data [21–23]. Accordingly, rather than interpreting each contact as a direct regulatory event in isolation, our analysis asks whether contact status and contact burden track with a reproducible shift in promoter response topology. In that sense, the four-state decomposition can be viewed as a pragmatic decoder that maps RNA–chromatin strata into interpretable promoter-scale outcomes. This is particularly useful for viral ncRNA biology, where abundance alone can complicate interpretation of contact frequency. [21]

The pathway analyses are most consistent when interpreted narrowly: TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB, rather than “inflammatory pathways” in general, is the strongest reproducible pathway-level signal across both ranked and top-contact analysis. This is plausible because EBV latency is deeply intertwined with inflammatory signaling networks, and EBERs have repeatedly been implicated in innate immune activation and cytokine-associated phenotypes [2, 7, 32]. Many inflammatory programs are also regulated through enhancer-driven chromatin architecture and can display distinctive transcriptional dynamics, including rapid inducibility and transcriptional bursting. Accordingly, one testable interpretation is that strongly contacted inflammatory promoters occupy promoter-enhancer neighborhoods with greater regulatory redundancy or kinetic buffering, thereby lowering the frequency with which they satisfy a stringent joint Recovery + Up threshold after EBER depletion. The independent cytokine-response analysis (Fig. S5; Additional files 17–19: Table S16 – S18) further supports this view by showing that the constraint-core class is less likely to behave as a strongly inducible transcriptional group under external stimulation, suggesting that the constrained state extends beyond the immediate depletion setting [37].

Limitations

Several limitations shape interpretation. First, all analyses are computational reanalyses of public datasets from a single EBV-positive B-cell system, and generalization across latency states, cellular backgrounds, and perturbation contexts remains to be tested. Second, the source dataset does not resolve EBER1- and EBER2-specific RNA-chromatin contacts or selective depletion effects. Therefore, the observed promoter-contact, RNAPII-looping, accessibility, and transcriptional phenotypes cannot be attributed specifically to either EBER1 or EBER2. Third, the ATAC analysis supports promoter accessibility but do not establish that accessibility is causally responsible for the constrained transcriptional phenotype. Fourth, the 3D network panels are topology visualizations of RNAPII contact graphs and not physical reconstructions of chromatin architecture. Fifth, the independent cytokine-response signal was strongest in GM12878 IL-15 and should be interpreted as context-specific support rather than a universal inducible-response phenotype. Sixth, continuous control-RNA signal tracks for HSPA9/NCL were not used for mechanistic claims because reliable local bedGraph-style signal recovery was not available in the final workflow. Seventh, the constraint-core class is intentionally broad; therefore, pathway interpretation is strongest when combined with ranked contact burden, ATAC accessibility, and candidate-locus prioritization rather than by treating all constraint-core genes as mechanistically equivalent.

Conclusions

Overall, this computational reanalysis supports a model in which EBER-linked promoter contact is associated with accessible but constrained coordinated RNAPII promoter-hub activation following EBER depletion. The key empirical signature is not simply fewer loop gains or fewer expression gains, but a selective loss of the coordinated Recovery + Up state, attenuation of loop-transcription coupling in contacted promoters, and preservation of chromatin accessibility within the constrained class. By explicitly translating RNA-chromatin contact patterns into promoter-scale response topologies and integrating orthogonal accessibility evidence, this framework moves viral RNA-chromatin mapping beyond qualitative target lists toward testable models of how abundant EBV noncoding RNAs intersect with host chromatin architecture and transcriptional competence.
