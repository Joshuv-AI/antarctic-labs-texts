Redefining the modern paradigm of vasopressor therapy for vasodilatory shock
Authors: Emily J. See, Lakhmir S. Chawla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Vasodilatory shock remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in the intensive care unit. Vasopressors are the cornerstone of treatment when vasodilatory shock persists despite adequate fluid resuscitation, yet their effects on organ-specific blood flow, perfusion, and oxygenation are complex and may contribute to harm. This review summarises the extensive contributions of Professor Rinaldo Bellomo to advancing our knowledge of vasopressor therapy in clinical practice. Central to his work was the concept of personalised haemodynamic targets, introduced through the concept of “mean perfusion pressure deficit”, which linked premorbid perfusion pressure to outcomes and challenged the universal application of a mean arterial pressure threshold of ≥65 mmHg. In collaboration with Professor Clive May, Bellomo established a chronically instrumented large animal model of hyperdynamic sepsis, yielding fundamental insights into the discordance between the macrocirculation and microcirculation, the vulnerability of the renal medulla to hypoxia, and the mechanisms of septic acute kidney injury. This model enabled direct comparison of vasopressor drugs in both healthy animals and during septic shock, demonstrating their heterogeneous effects on global and regional blood flow, perfusion, and tissue oxygenation. Knowledge translation to the bedside was achieved through the conduct of pivotal clinical trials, from early studies refuting the utility of “renal-dose dopamine” to landmark contributions to the ATHOS (angiotensin II treatment of high-ouput shock) program, which established angiotensin II as a novel vasopressor in refractory vasodilatory shock. Collectively, Bellomo’s work has transformed vasopressor therapy from empirical convention towards individualised practice, and it continues to inform clinical investigation and guideline development.

Introduction

Vasodilatory shock, especially when due to sepsis, is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in the intensive care unit (ICU).1 The majority of deaths in patients with vasodilatory shock occur due to multiple organ dysfunction.2 Vasopressor drugs are the cornerstone of pharmacological management for vasodilatory shock that is unresponsive to fluid resuscitation. Such drugs are used to restore blood pressure and optimise vital organ perfusion, while the underlying cause of shock is investigated and treated. Although vasopressors are reliably efficacious at increasing blood pressure, this may not translate to improvements in organ-specific global and regional blood flow, perfusion, and tissue oxygenation. Moreover, administration of vasopressor drugs may be accompanied by important adverse effects, including tachycardia, arrhythmia, hyperlactataemia, myocardial cell band necrosis, and excessive vasoconstriction.[3], [4], [5] Until recently, all vasopressor drugs in clinical use worldwide belonged to just two classes: catecholamines or vasopressin-like drugs.

This review explores Professor Rinaldo Bellomo’s multifaceted contributions to vasopressor therapy in the ICU. His work on mean perfusion pressure deficit was fundamental to the concept of personalised haemodynamic thresholds for the initiation and titration of vasopressor drugs in vasodilatory shock and initiated ongoing research aiming to identify appropriate physiological targets. Next, in collaboration with Professor Clive May, his development of a chronically instrumented experimental model of hyperdynamic sepsis delivered important insights into the alterations of global and regional blood flow, perfusion, and tissue oxygenation during septic shock. This model also enabled direct comparison of the haemodynamic and metabolic effects of vasoactive drugs both in healthy animals and in animals with hyperdynamic sepsis. Bellomo translated this knowledge to the bedside by conducting large clinical trials comparing vasopressor strategies in the management of vasodilatory shock. In particular, his contribution to research on angiotensin II shifted the paradigm of vasopressor drug prescription, delivery, and monitoring and was instrumental in the eventual regulatory approval of angiotensin II for the treatment of vasodilatory shock.

Exploration of haemodynamic thresholds for initiation and titration of vasopressor drugs

Haemodynamic thresholds for the initiation and titration of vasopressor drugs reflect the careful balance between the benefits of maintaining adequate blood pressure and the risks of excessive vasopressor use, such as arrhythmia, vasoconstriction, and organ ischaemia. Since capacity for vascular autoregulation is understood to vary substantially between individuals, Bellomo hypothesised that patients may require higher or lower mean arterial pressure levels to maintain vital organ perfusion. He sought to individualise such thresholds by proposing the concept of “mean perfusion pressure deficit” during the initial management of shock, which described the relationship between premorbid and current perfusion pressure.6 From an operational standpoint, this metric was calculated as the percentage difference between a patient’s estimated premorbid mean perfusion pressure (mean arterial pressure minus central venous pressure) and the time-weighted average mean perfusion pressure achieved during treatment. Where premorbid central venous pressure was unknown, it was assumed to be 0–5 mmHg.

This approach to individualised haemodynamic thresholds was examined in a prospective cohort of vasopressor-dependent critically ill adults who had at least two organ dysfunctions.6 Bellomo et al. identified that achieved blood pressure during vasopressor therapy had no relationship to the premorbid level. Moreover, patients with a higher mean perfusion pressure deficit were more likely to develop severe acute kidney injury. Similar findings were confirmed in patients with sepsis and in those undergoing cardiac surgery.7,8 In a large multicentre study of critically ill adults with vasodilatory shock, mean perfusion pressure deficit was independently associated with adverse kidney outcomes.9 This work challenges the prevailing dogma that a mean arterial pressure ≥65 mmHg should be applied universally in a “one-size-fits-all” approach, a concept that has been institutionalised in international sepsis consensus recommendations and continues to dominate clinical practice.10

Development of a chronically instrumented experimental model of hyperdynamic sepsis

The aetiology of organ dysfunction in vasodilatory shock is complex and poorly understood. However, the adequacy of global and regional blood flow, perfusion, and tissue oxygenation of vital organs is considered to be central to its pathogenesis.11 Bellomo and May developed and validated a nonlethal large animal model of hyperdynamic sepsis, enabling deeper insights into the macrocirculatory and microcirculatory pathophysiological changes that occur under conditions closely replicating human sepsis. This approach was prescient in highlighting the discordance between the macrocirculation and microcirculation in sepsis and exposed the macrocirculatory bias that arises when intensivists treat the values they can most easily measure.

In this model, adult merino ewes underwent a series of operative procedures over an 8-week period, including carotid loop creation, placement of flow probes in the renal, cardiac, cerebral, and mesenteric circulations, and insertion of carotid and internal jugular catheters.12 This instrumentation allowed continuous invasive monitoring of arterial and venous pressures, cardiac output, and global and regional blood flow to vital organs. In later models, direct measurement of renal cortical and medullary tissue oxygenation was also incorporated.13,14 The staged surgical approach facilitated studies in conscious animals, thereby eliminating the confounding haemodynamic effects of anaesthesia.

In contrast to earlier animal models that induced septic shock with a bolus of endotoxin, which results in a hypodynamic circulatory state, this model used a bolus of Escherichia coli, producing a hyperdynamic circulation that more closely resembled human sepsis. Characteristic features of hyperdynamic sepsis, including tachycardia, hypotension, increased cardiac output, and increased conductance, were observed to occur at 6 h after injection. During sepsis, coronary, mesenteric, and renal blood flows increased compared with the control period, primarily due to regional vasodilatation (i.e., increased conductance). Cerebral blood flow remained unchanged. The hyperdynamic circulatory state in septic animals was accompanied by typical features of sepsis, including impaired myocardial contractility, hyperlactataemia, oliguria, and increased serum creatinine. These findings, particularly the reproducible observation of increased renal blood blow in sepsis, contradicted the prevailing belief that acute kidney injury in sepsis was caused by acute tubular necrosis secondary to decreased renal blood flow. Another key finding from these studies was the observation that, in septic shock, decreased urine sodium occurred as a direct consequence of decreased intraglomerular pressure. The combination of low urine sodium and decreased urine output reflected a clear microcirculatory mechanism, which had previously been ascribed to decreased renal blood flow.

Alterations in the renal macrocirculation and microcirculation during sepsis were a particular focus of Bellomo’s work. In his hyperdynamic experimental model, global renal blood flow was observed to increase yet there was a significant redistribution of regional perfusion, with relative preservation of cortical blood flow but marked reduction of medullary blood flow.14 Cortical tissue oxygenation remained largely intact, supported by sustained cortical perfusion and reduced cortical oxygen extraction. In contrast, medullary oxygenation declined substantially, driven by both reduced medullary perfusion and the medulla’s intrinsically high metabolic demand for active solute transport. These observations underscored the dissociation between global renal oxygen delivery and the adequacy of oxygenation in specific intrarenal regions, highlighting the medulla’s particular vulnerability to hypoxic injury during sepsis. Importantly, they again challenged the prevailing assumption that acute kidney injury in sepsis arises primarily from global renal ischaemia.

Exploration of the differential effects of vasopressor drugs on organ-specific macrocirculation and microcirculation

Bellomo and May used their experimental large animal model to investigate the complex haemodynamic effects of vasopressor drugs both under physiological conditions and during hyperdynamic sepsis. Continuous measurement of global and regional blood flow, perfusion, and tissue oxygenation was possible across multiple vital organ beds during infusions of noradrenaline, adrenaline, vasopressin, angiotensin II, terlipressin, phenylephrine, and dopamine. This work yielded pivotal insights into how these agents redistribute organ-specific blood flow and oxygen delivery, advancing our understanding of the mechanisms by which vasopressor drugs may either support or impair tissue oxygenation in critical illness. Notably, they demonstrated that alterations in regional renal blood flow, perfusion, and tissue oxygenation cannot be reliably predicted from vasopressor-mediated changes in global renal blood flow alone.

Noradrenaline

Noradrenaline was evaluated in both healthy animals and during septic shock (in anaesthetised animals). Under physiological conditions, noradrenaline infusion (0.4 μg/kg/min) increased mean arterial pressure, heart rate, and cardiac output.15 Renal and coronary blood flows were increased, whereas mesenteric and cerebral blood flows were unchanged. Urine output and creatinine clearance increased. In the renal circulation, noradrenaline produced a dose-dependent reduction in tissue oxygenation, driven by a combined decrease in cortical and medullary blood flow and an increase in renal oxygen consumption.16 During hyperdynamic sepsis, noradrenaline infusion increased renal blood flow and medullary blood flow but only induced a nonsignificant trend towards improved cortical blood flow.17 These changes were accompanied by increased urine output.

Adrenaline

The effect of adrenaline was studied in conscious sheep during hyperdynamic sepsis.18 Adrenaline infusion (0.4 μg/kg/min) increased mean arterial pressure and cardiac output and decreased heart rate. Renal blood flow decreased, whereas coronary, mesenteric, and cerebral blood flows were unchanged. Vasoconstriction was induced across renal, coronary, and cerebral vascular beds. Numerous adverse metabolic effects were observed including hyperlactataemia, metabolic acidosis, hyperglycaemia, and hypokalaemia. Urine output increased, but creatinine clearance did not change.

Vasopressin

Arginine vasopressin was studied in both healthy animals and in conscious septic shock. In healthy animals, infusion of arginine vasopressin (0.02 IU/min) did not significantly change mean arterial pressure, heart rate, or cardiac output.19 Its effects on organ-specific circulations were variable: mesenteric blood flow decreased, whereas renal and coronary blood flows remained unchanged. Urine output and creatinine clearance were unaffected. Within the renal circulation, arginine vasopressin did not modify cortical or medullary blood flow or oxygenation.16 In septic animals, arginine vasopressin decreased heart rate and cardiac output without changing mean arterial pressure. It decreased coronary and mesenteric blood flows but had no measurable effect on renal blood flow. Urine output and creatinine clearance were increased.

Terlipressin

Terlipressin was studied in conscious sheep with septic shock.20 Because of its longer half-life, terlipressin was administered as a single bolus (1 mg) or as multiple boluses (1 mg then 0.5 mg at 2 h and 4 h). A single terlipressin bolus increased mean arterial pressure but decreased heart rate and cardiac output. It increased renal blood flow but decreased coronary and mesenteric blood flows. With multiple terlipressin boluses, the increased renal blood flow was attenuated. Urine output and creatinine clearance increased. These data implied that caution should be applied when terlipressin is prescribed to septic patients at a risk of coronary or mesenteric ischaemia.

Phenylephrine

Bellomo studied phenylephrine in healthy animals and during hyperdynamic sepsis. In healthy sheep, phenylephrine infusion (1–2 μg/kg/h) increased mean arterial pressure and decreased cardiac output and heart rate.21 Renal blood flow increased, coronary blood flow did not change, and mesenteric blood flow decreased. In septic sheep, similar changes were identified in mean arterial pressure, cardiac output, and heart rate. However, the effect of phenylephrine on regional blood flow was different: renal blood flow increased, coronary blood flow decreased, and mesenteric blood flow was unchanged.

Dopamine

Low-dose dopamine (2 μg/kg/min) was studied in healthy animals.22 It did not alter mean arterial pressure, cardiac output, or heart rate. Coronary, mesenteric, and cerebral blood flow were not different to placebo. However, it increased renal blood flow and urine output without changing creatinine clearance. The effect of low-dose dopamine on renal blood flow was less pronounced than with noradrenaline. These findings did not support the use of low-dose dopamine to increase renal blood flow and/or attenuate renal ischaemia.

Angiotensin II

The effects of angiotensin II on the macrocirculation and microcirculation were evaluated in healthy animals and during awake hyperdynamic sepsis. In healthy animals, angiotensin II infusion (10 μg/h) increased mean arterial pressure and reduced heart rate without affecting cardiac index.16 Renal blood flow decreased to preseptic levels, but cortical and medullary blood flows were unchanged. Medullary tissue oxygenation decreased, whereas cortical tissue oxygenation remained stable. During hyperdynamic sepsis, angiotensin II increased mean arterial pressure, reduced cardiac output, and had no effect on heart rate.23 Renal and mesenteric blood flows decreased, while coronary blood flow was preserved. Despite the reduction in renal blood flow, angiotensin II increased urine output, creatinine clearance, and fractional sodium excretion. These studies proved experimentally that the increased renal blood flow, decreased urine sodium, decreased creatine clearance, and decreased urine output induced by septic shock were all normalised by the infusion of angiotensin II. Translation from experimental models to clinical trials

Bellomo played a central role in advancing the clinical evaluation of the comparative effectiveness of vasopressor drugs. A consistent theme in his approach was the view that vasopressor drugs should be assessed by their mechanism of action, receptor profile, and appropriateness to the clinical context. He was an early advocate for catecholamine-sparing strategies, supporting investigation into the use of vasopressin and angiotensin II in patients with vasodilatory shock who were requiring escalating doses of noradrenaline. His contributions also extended to the concept of multimodal vasopressor therapy, in which agents with complementary mechanisms were used to optimise efficacy while minimising toxicity.

One of Bellomo’s earliest and most influential international randomised controlled trials on vasopressors, informed by insights from experimental animal work, compared low-dose dopamine to placebo in patients with systemic inflammation and early renal dysfunction.26 Among 328 patients, continuous infusion of low-dose dopamine did not confer clinically meaningful protection against progression of renal dysfunction. Secondary outcomes, including length of stay, survival, and renal recovery, were also unaffected. Coupled with growing concern over the potential adverse effects of dopamine in critically ill patients, the lack of clinical benefit in this trial was a key factor in the abandonment of dopamine for the prevention of renal ischaemia. Although “renal-dose dopamine” may continue to be used in a handful of post–cardiac surgery ICUs worldwide, clinical trials have clearly demonstrated the futility of this approach.

More recently, Bellomo was an important contributor to the ATHOS research program, which evaluated angiotensin II in catecholamine-refractory vasodilatory shock. ATHOS-3, a multinational, double-blind, placebo-controlled randomised trial, demonstrated the efficacy of angiotensin II as a potent vasopressor in this setting, leading to its Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) approval for this indication.27 Secondary and post hoc analyses yielded several important observations. First, renin levels correlated with angiotensin I/II ratios and decreased following angiotensin II administration.28 Second, in patients with a baseline renin level above the median (>173 pg/mL), angiotensin II was associated with a marked reduction in mortality compared to placebo.24 Third, patients with severe acute kidney injury appeared to derive substantial benefit from angiotensin II: among those receiving continuous renal replacement therapy, angiotensin II shortened the time to liberation from renal replacement therapy,29 while in patients with stage 3 acute kidney injury at enrolment, angiotensin II was associated with significantly lower mortality.30

The use of angiotensin II in vasodilatory shock is physiologically intuitive in light of the renin angiotensin aldosterone system disturbance hypothesis. Building on this rationale, Bellomo extended his investigations of its efficacy to less severe illness through the Acute Renal effects of Angiotensin II Management In Shock (ARAMIS) program, which is examining angiotensin II as a potential first-line vasopressor in vasodilatory hypotension. In the nonrandomised ARAMIS pilot study, angiotensin II was found to be safe, effective at augmenting blood pressure, and potentially associated with a lower incidence of serum troponin elevation than noradrenaline.31 Secondary analyses established catecholamine-equivalent dosing for angiotensin II and examined the prognostic significance of baseline renin levels in this population.32,33

Bellomo’s influence on the field continues through several major ongoing investigations into vasopressor therapy in critical illness. The ARAMIS-2 randomised trial is evaluating the safety and efficacy of angiotensin II compared with noradrenaline in vasodilatory states, while ARAMIS-3 will investigate angiotensin II versus vasopressin as the preferred second-line agent. The PORTHOS and D’ARTAGNAN trials are examining angiotensin II in the settings of cardiac surgery and liver transplantation, respectively. Additional studies include EAVES, which is testing the optimal timing of adjunctive vasopressin initiation in vasodilatory shock, and MEGA-MAP, which aims to define the most appropriate mean arterial pressure target in critically ill patients receiving vasopressor therapy.

Conclusion

Professor Rinaldo Bellomo’s contributions have reshaped vasopressor therapy in the ICU, advancing clinical practice from empirical convention towards precision care. Through the development of novel concepts such as mean perfusion pressure deficit, Bellomo laid the groundwork for personalised haemodynamic targets in vasodilatory shock, informing both current practice and ongoing research into optimal perfusion thresholds. By integrating insights from experimental animal models and clinical trials, he demonstrated that vasopressor agents exert heterogeneous effects on global and regional blood flow, perfusion, and tissue oxygenation. Moreover, his work highlights the fundamental notion that such effects cannot be inferred from changes in systemic blood pressure alone.

Bellomo’s investigations into novel vasopressor agents, including angiotensin II, expanded the therapeutic armamentarium and validated a mechanistic, context-specific approach to vasopressor selection. Collectively, this body of work has been instrumental in shifting the field towards an individualised, physiology-driven, and evidence-based model of care. Ongoing trials initiated by Bellomo will test angiotensin II across clinical contexts of vasodilatory shock, cardiac surgery, and organ transplant; timing of adjunctive vasopressor initiation; and biomarker-guided vasopressor selection. Accordingly, his enduring legacy will continue to influence clinical decision-making and improve outcomes for critically ill patients worldwide.
