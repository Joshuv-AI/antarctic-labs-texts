Spatial profiling of METex14-altered NSCLC under tepotinib treatment: Shifting the immunosuppressive landscape
Authors: Manon A Simard, Carlos Cabrera-Galvez, Santiago Viteri, Felix Geist, Nadine Reischmann, Michael Zühlsdorf, Niki Karachaliou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Tepotinib downstages NSCLC from IIIB to IA1 in a patient with a METex14 skipping alteration.•GeoMx spatial analysis reveals increased immune cell diversity post-tepotinib.•Tepotinib treatment reduces immunosuppressive genes andincreases tumor cell killing.•Post-tepotinib treatment shifts the tumor microenvironment to a more immune-permissive state.Keywords: Tepotinib, Spatial profiling, GeoMx, NSCLC, METex14

Introduction

For advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), thorough biomarker analysis is crucial to assess programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and identify actionable genetic alterations like EGFR, ALK, RET, NTRK, ROS1, KRAS G12C, BRAF V600E, and ERBB2 mutations, as well as MET exon 14 (METex14) skipping alterations [1]. Tepotinib, a once-daily, highly selective oral MET inhibitor, targeting METex14 skipping is approved for first- and subsequent-line treatment in Japan and the USA, and is the first MET inhibitor with full approval in China for patients with advanced NSCLC harboring METex14 skipping alterations [2]. In the EU, it is approved as a second-line therapy for patients who have progressed after platinum-based chemotherapy, with or without immunotherapy [2]. Capmatinib has received similar approvals. Savolitinib and bozitinib are approved in China, while glumetinib is approved in China and Japan [[2], [3], [4]].

After the success of targeted therapies in advanced stages of NSCLC, these treatments have recently been incorporated into early-stage disease, where they have been demonstrated to reduce recurrence. Osimertinib and alectinib are approved for adjuvant treatment in resected stage IB-IIIA EGFR-mutant NSCLC and ALK-positive NSCLC, respectively, while osimertinib is also approved for patients with unresectable, stage III EGFR-mutant NSCLC after chemoradiotherapy [[5], [6], [7]]. Presently, only immunotherapies have received approval for neoadjuvant use, while no targeted therapies have been approved in this setting [8].

Aside from the genetic changes which often characterize tumors, the tumor landscape comprises a complex interplay among cancer cells, fibroblasts, immune cells, neurons, and vascular components. This tumor microenvironment (TME), characterized by unique nutrient zones and communication, is increasingly acknowledged for its role in cancer progression and therapy responses [9,10]. In NSCLC, the TME has been characterized as immunosuppressive, with the release of factors by regulatory T cells, neutrophils, and polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells enabling evasion from immune detection, resulting in a tumor-promoting environment [11]. Moreover, signaling through the MET receptor has been shown to further play a role in regulating the immunogenetic balance toward immuno-suppression [12]. The role of MET inhibition on the tumor immune microenvironment in a clinical setting has yet to be reported.

In recent years, numerous advancements in the field of spatial profiling have offered a new lens to understand the TME's organizational complexity. These cutting-edge techniques can measure numerous gene and protein expression levels within tissue samples without disturbing their original structure and enable the evaluation of cell-cell interactions. Over the past decade, these techniques have evolved, becoming more cost-effective and efficient in terms of both time and the level of detail they can provide [13].

In this report, we present the utilization of GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiling (DSP) for a comprehensive spatial transcriptomic and proteomic analysis. This analysis was conducted on paired biopsy samples from a patient diagnosed with resectable locally advanced NSCLC harboring a METex14 skipping mutation. The patient received tepotinib as neoadjuvant treatment.

Materials and methods

Patient samples

Human lung cancer tissue samples were obtained with informed consent of the patient and according to local hospital regulations.

Digital spatial profiling & data analysis

Spatial Transcriptomics: GeoMx DSP (NanoString Technologies) was performed on 5 µm thick formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections within a week of microtome sectioning to ensure sufficient RNA quality. An adjacent section was subjected to hematoxylin and eosin staining using a Ventana Symphony H & E autostainer (Roche) and was used to better inform region of interest (ROI) selection by overlaying it within the GeoMx software. GeoMx DSP was performed according to manufacturer's instructions. For the slide preparation, the semi-automated BOND Rx (Leica Biosystems) protocol was used. Other than the SYTO 13 nuclear stain from NanoString Technologies, TTF-1 (Abcam, clone SP141), CD3 (Abcam, clone F7.2.38), and CD33 (ImmunoLogic, clone PWS44) morphology markers were used. These were self-conjugated using the Abcam Lightning-Link conjugation kits for AF555, AF594, and AF647 according to manufacturer's protocol. The Cancer Transcriptome Atlas (CTA) RNA probes were used for spatial transcriptomics supplemented with custom probes for METex14 generated by NanoString Technologies. ROIs were selected in all regions of the tissue sections, with care taken to sample both within and outside the tumor boundary. A total of 94 circular ROIs with a diameter of 250 µm were selected. Following collection of probe barcodes, library preparation was performed, and samples were sequenced using an Illumina NextSeq 6000 at Medicover IMGM Laboratories (Germany).

Spatial Proteomics: As for transcriptomics, the GeoMx DSP was performed according to manufacturer's instructions. In this case, the Immune Cell Profiling, Immune Cell Typing, Immune Activation Status, IO Drug Target, and PI3K/Akt signaling protein panels were included. Readout was performed with an nCounter (NanoString Technologies). This was performed on a section roughly 20 µm away from the spatial transcriptomics. Nevertheless, ROIs were selected in similar regions, to better compare the results from the two methods.

Data and statistical analysis

Sequencing data was processed in the GeoMx analysis suite and normalized using a third quartile normalization. ROIs were annotated based on morphology marker staining. Comparisons between samples and annotated groups were performed using linear mixed models with Benjamini-Hochberg correction, and ROI number was used for random effect. Dimension reduction analyses and spatial deconvolution were also performed. Outside the GeoMx software environment, unsupervised clustering from the spatial deconvolution results was obtained by k-means clustering using R. Gene Ontology (GO) analyses were performed using significantly differentially expressed genes (>1.5-fold), and all genes targeted by the CTA probes as a reference list, in the GOrilla tool [14] for output of enriched GO terms.

Results

Neoadjuvant tepotinib treatment in a patient with METex14 NSCLC

A 54-year-old Caucasian female without a smoking history presented with a cough. A chest computed tomography (CT) scan identified a left lung mass, which was confirmed as primary lung adenocarcinoma (CK7+, Napsin+, TTF1+, p40) through endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA). Positron emission tomography/CT (PET/CT) revealed a 53 × 28 mm left lower lobe tumor with an SUVmax of 16.2, and lymph node metastases in the subcarinal region (SUVmax 5.6) and left hilum (SUVmax 7). Genomic profiling revealed a METex14 skipping alteration. Consequently, the patient's lung cancer was staged as IIIB (cT3 cN2 cM0). The multidisciplinary tumor board advised against upfront thoracic surgery and recommended neoadjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy with carboplatin (AUC5) and pemetrexed (500 mg/m2). Following two cycles of chemotherapy, a chest CT scan showed no evidence of radiological response. Subsequently, targeted therapy with tepotinib (500 mg [450 mg active moiety], once daily), as an off-label use, began on January 28, 2021. A chest CT scan 42 days later, indicated a partial radiological response (Fig. 1A). After multidisciplinary review, the patient underwent thoracotomy and left lower lobectomy with radical mediastinal lymph node dissection. Pathological examination of the resected specimens revealed three residual foci of invasive acinar adenocarcinoma measuring 5 mm, 3 mm, and 2 mm, each with less than 10 % residual viable tumor cells, with evidence of pleural retraction but no pleural infiltration. The mediastinal lymph node dissection—encompassing both N1 and N2 stations—showed no signs of tumor involvement, indicative of a major pathological response. The post-treatment pathological TNM classification was ypT1a, ypN0, ypM0, corresponding to a stage IA lung adenocarcinoma. No adjuvant therapy was administered.

The patient developed brain metastases and pleural effusion 8 months later. At that time, treatment with tepotinib was re-initiated, and a partial remission was achieved, despite a dose reduction due to peripheral edema, which was interfering with daily activities. As of this report, the patient continues tepotinib treatment with a sustained partial radiological response.

Tumor and microenvironment changes occurring upon tepotinib treatment

The availability of paired biopsies from the pre-treatment biopsy and the post-tepotinib surgical resection allowed for a longitudinal comparison of the tumor and microenvironment. This was assessed by spatial transcriptomics and proteomics using the GeoMx DSP. Regions of interest (ROI) from the paired biopsies were selected based on TTF-1, CD3 and CD33 staining to cover the heterogeneity present within the tissue, with 15 and 79 ROIs in the pre-tepotinib biopsy and post-tepotinib surgical resection, respectively, representative of the biopsy size (Fig. 1B). As evidenced by the morphological marker and hematoxylin and eosin staining (Fig. 1C), the pre-tepotinib biopsy demonstrated little to no immune cell infiltration compared with the post-tepotinib surgical resection.

Transcriptomic analysis between samples demonstrated a significant decrease in the expression of MET and immuno-suppressive genes such as CXCL8, IDO1, CD274, HLA-E, VSIR, and PDCD1. In contrast, expression of pro-inflammatory genes such as CD68, CD81, and A2M was significantly upregulated in the post-tepotinib surgical resection (Fig. 2A/C). Furthermore, the post-tepotinib surgical resection possessed a high expression of antigen-presentation and T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity-related genes. These transcriptomic findings were confirmed by spatial proteomic analysis in these biopsies (Fig. 2B/D), demonstrating an increased presence of immune-related proteins and down-regulation of PD-L1.

Spatially resolved changes in the tepotinib-treated tissue

In the post-tepotinib surgical resection, spatial profiling of the transcriptome and proteome of the TTF-1+ tissue, compared with peripheral normal tissue, was performed. These results indicated a higher expression of immune cell recruitment and activation genes, such as CD68, CHIT1, CD63, CD14, LYZ, ITGAX, and CCL18, and genes involved in antigen presentation, such as HLA-DR, HLA-DM, CD74, CTSS and B2M in the tumor regions, whereas no genes of the CTA were upregulated in the surrounding peripheral regions (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Cell type deconvolution of immune and stromal cell types of ROIs was also performed (Fig. 3A). As already seen in the morphology marker staining and differential gene expression results, there was an increase in immune cell abundance in the post-tepotinib surgical resection ROIs. Some ROIs were particularly enriched for specific immune cells, such as B-cells. Unsupervised k-means clustering indicated five distinct clusters based on transcriptomic results, with their cell type composition summarized in Fig. 3B. These clusters were transposed onto the tissue section images for spatial context evaluation (Fig. 3C). The pre-tepotinib tumor tissue ROIs all clustered together (yellow), along with a few of the ROIs in the sample from the post-tepotinib surgical resection, which primarily appeared to be sampling normal epithelium. While batch effects could potentially explain this result, dimension reduction of these ROIs using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and principal component analysis (PCA) showed overlap between both biopsies, thereby diminishing this likelihood (Supplementary Figure 2). In the post-tepotinib surgical resection, the purple cluster was largely excluded from the TTF-1+ regions, representing the tumor, and contained fewer monocytes or T and B cells, but showed higher levels of natural killer (NK) cells, which had previously been reported to be excluded from NSCLC malignant tissue [15,16]. Of the three remaining clusters, the pink and blue were closer to the tumor margins. In brief, the pink cluster’s gene expression profile is enriched for B-cell proliferation, activation, and differentiation, as well as T-cell activation and co-stimulation, assessed through gene ontology evaluation. In contrast, the blue cluster is enriched for phagocytosis, vesicle-mediated transport, and antigen processing and presentation via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II. The red cluster, although also characterized by immune cell activation, differentiates itself from the other tumor-associated clusters through higher expression of genes involved in collagen fibril biosynthesis and extracellular structural organization. Moreover, it appears to be more centrally located in the TTF1+ tissue, though the 3D tumor structure can only be speculated from one tissue section.

For the characterization of gene expression changes occurring during tepotinib treatment, gene ontology analysis of differentially expressed genes for each sample and cluster was performed (Supplementary File). Fig. 4 presents a summary of the significantly enriched biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components representing the tumor milieu before and after tepotinib treatment, with representative stromal cell types inferred from the deconvoluted sequencing results. Overall, a shift from a proliferative, immuno-suppressive tumor to an immune-rich, inflammatory microenvironment with enhanced antigen presentation and tumor cell killing was observed.

Discussion

In this investigation, the patient presented with resectable, locally advanced NSCLC harboring a METex14 skipping alteration, which did not respond to initial neoadjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy. Employing tepotinib in a neoadjuvant setting, upon identifying the METex14 skipping alteration, resulted in a significant downstaging of the tumor from IIIB to IA1. Tepotinib treatment enabled a successful lobectomy and resulted in a major pathological response. Since paired biopsies were available, spatial transcriptomic and proteomic analysis using the GeoMx DSP, along with the CTA and immune-related protein panels, was performed, revealing a shift from an immuno-suppressive TME to an immune-permissive one, with a high prevalence of antigen-presenting cells and an increased presence of pro-inflammatory immune cells.

The immuno-suppressive microenvironment of NSCLC tumors, with or without MET alterations, is well documented [17,18]. Our spatial profiling of the pre-tepotinib biopsy corroborates these findings, revealing elevated levels of genes, proteins, and cell types that promote immune suppression and tumorigenesis [18]. However, the role of tepotinib treatment on the TME, particularly its effect on immune cells, had not been elucidated until now. Following tepotinib treatment, we observed a marked increase in the diversity and presence of immune cells in the TME, as evidenced by morphological marker staining, transcriptomic analysis, and cell type deconvolution. Moreover, the enhanced recruitment of pro-inflammatory immune cells with tumoricidal activity likely contributed to the patient's partial radiological response to tepotinib. Interestingly, clustering of the ROIs revealed heterogeneity in the analyzed tissues, particularly in the post-tepotinib surgical resection. The TTF1+ tissue was characterized by three clusters, which were not confined to specific areas of the tissue section but were instead distributed according to the TTF1+ staining pattern, with the marginal and core regions corresponding to different clusters. This highlighted the distinct cell types and biological processes occurring in these different tumor regions and supports the acquisition of multiple ROIs with similar morphological marker staining to better capture the heterogeneity present in solid tumors.

Patients with NSCLC with METex14 skipping alterations are known to have high PD-L1 expression, yet respond poorly to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) [2,19]. We observed a significant decrease in the expression of the immune checkpoints and immunosuppressive genes PDCD1, CD274, IDO1, CXCL8, and FOXP3, as well as in the protein levels of PD-L1, following tepotinib treatment. Expression of IDO1 is associated with resistance to ICI therapy and is a known driver of NSCLC progression [20]. Since tepotinib reprogrammed the TME to be more immuno-permissive, it could potentially prime the tumor for a better response to immunotherapies, as previously described [21]. Combinations of MET inhibitors with ICI are currently being evaluated in ongoing trials, including the POTENT trial, which assesses the combination of tepotinib plus pembrolizumab (NCT05782361).

While we have highlighted the anti-tumorigenic nature of many upregulated genes and proteins in the post-tepotinib tissue, we must also acknowledge the double-edged sword of some of these genes. For example, CD68 is a pan-macrophage marker which plays a critical role in promoting phagocytosis but it is also associated with higher tumor grades and aggressiveness. Similarly, we detected higher expression and phosphorylation of AKT, indicating a potential signaling feedback loop triggered by MET inhibition, which may drive further tumorigenesis. Nevertheless, the significant reduction in tumor size and the recruitment of immune cells to the tumor site support an overall anti-tumorigenic response. From a mechanistic perspective, we must consider that the observed effects in the TME may be attributed either to the direct modulation of myeloid and lymphoid cells by tepotinib, the increased tumor cell death induced by tepotinib treatment, or a combination of the two.

Spatial technologies are revolutionizing our understanding of tumors and their microenvironment. However, certain limitations must be considered, such as the large size of our ROIs, which comprise a small group of cells and may confound the results. On the other hand, this approach enables the detection of not only medium- and high-expressing genes but also low-expression genes in this local sub-group of cells, allowing us to perform robust cell type deconvolution, differential expression, and pathway enrichment analyses [22]. According to Baker and colleagues, the use of larger ROIs increases the probability of detecting rare cells when cell organization is heterogeneous, as is the case in most cancers [23]. The potential impact of prior chemotherapy on the effects observed in the post-tepotinib surgical resection cannot be discounted, despite the lack of radiological response. We must also acknowledge the size and sampling discrepancy between the EBUS and tumor resection samples, which enabled us to better characterize the post-tepotinib tissue but likely did not capture the heterogeneity of the pre-tepotinib sample. Nevertheless, the different ROIs were representative of the available sample area, and this difference was accounted for in the statistical analysis using linear mixed models. Aditionally, the transcriptomic and proteomic results were obtained from tissue sections approximately 20 microns apart, rather than from identical ROIs. Finally, another limitation of this study is the absence of ROI segmentation to analyze tumor and stromal contributions invidually to gene and protein expression levels. This was not performed due to the strong background from the TTF-1 staining, which interfered with proper segmentation based on fluorescent signal intensity.

To our knowledge, this report represents the first instance of neoadjuvant tepotinib use in a patient with locally advanced NSCLC. The application of GeoMx DSP has delineated cellular transitions within the TME from immunosuppression to immune activation following MET inhibition. These findings emphasize the value of spatial profiling in guiding cancer therapies and pave the way for further investigation into combinatory treatments with MET inhibitors and immunotherapies.
