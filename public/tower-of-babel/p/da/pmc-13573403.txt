Development and internal validation of a nomogram for predicting prolonged ICU length of stay after oral free flap reconstruction without prophylactic tracheostomy
Authors: Longwen Zhang, Yaping Song, Hongsheng Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop and validate a nomogram for predicting prolonged ICU length of stay (LOS) in patients undergoing oral free flap reconstruction without prophylactic tracheostomy.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 419 consecutive patients treated between January 2019 and July 2025. Patients were randomly assigned to a training set (n = 294) and a validation set (n = 125) at a 7:3 ratio. LASSO regression was used for variable selection in the training set, followed by multivariable logistic regression to build the prediction model, which was visualized as a nomogram. Model performance was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC), calibration plots, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsSeven predictive factors were ultimately included in the model: primary site (Location), flap source, smoking history, intraoperative blood loss (per 10 mL increment), postoperative blood glucose, postoperative procalcitonin (PCT), and the International Normalized Ratio (INR). The AUC was 0.722 (95% CI: 0.652–0.793) in the training set and 0.769 (95% CI: 0.667–0.872) in the validation set. The model demonstrated good calibration and significant net clinical benefit.ConclusionThe developed nomogram model shows good discrimination and calibration. Based on routine perioperative indicators, it can be used for rapid bedside identification of high-risk patients, providing a quantitative basis for ICU resource allocation and individualized enhanced recovery pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08687-8.

Background

Microvascular free flap transplantation is a crucial technique for reconstructing complex defects following head and neck tumor resection. While this surgery significantly improves patient aesthetics and function, it is characterized by its complexity, significant trauma, and prolonged operative time. In the early postoperative period, two core challenges impacting outcomes are flap perfusion management and airway safety. Stable blood flow perfusion is fundamental for flap survival. Furthermore, edema and hematoma in the surgical site adjacent to the airway, which are prone to occur postoperatively, can precipitate acute respiratory obstruction, posing a life-threatening risk [1–4]. Perioperative airway management in patients undergoing microvascular free flap surgery, particularly the indications for prophylactic tracheostomy, remains a subject of clinical debate. To avoid the complications associated with tracheostomy, a widely adopted strategy is planned delayed extubation for patients assessed as not requiring prophylactic tracheostomy. This involves retaining the endotracheal tube to ensure they safely navigate the high-risk period for postoperative airway edema [5–9]. Patients undergoing this procedure routinely require admission to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) for close monitoring [10].

The length of ICU stay directly impacts healthcare resource utilization and costs [11, 12]. More importantly, prolonged ICU stay is not merely a financial concern; it is closely linked to nosocomial infections, delirium, deconditioning, and delayed recovery. For this patient population, each additional day in the ICU delays critical postoperative milestones such as oral intake, mobilization, and timely discharge, ultimately affecting long-term quality of life. However, clinicians currently lack an objective tool to identify high-risk patients early before complications arise.

Based on early risk stratification, targeted interventions can be initiated for patients identified as high-risk during the early postoperative period, including: (1) enhanced ICU care — allocating higher nurse-to-patient ratios, early consultation with respiratory therapists, and structured daily extubation readiness assessments; (2) proactive planning for step-down care — identifying available ward beds earlier and coordinating rehabilitation services in advance; (3) early multidisciplinary involvement — engaging nutritionists, physical therapists, and case managers early to address factors that commonly delay discharge; and (4) family communication — setting realistic expectations to reduce anxiety and improve satisfaction with care.

Therefore, developing a tool for the individualized prediction of ICU length of stay holds significant clinical and practical value for optimizing resource allocation and optimizing postoperative ICU resource allocation and guiding early postoperative management decisions. In recent years, several models predicting ICU length of stay have been published, yet their clinical utility and generalizability remain unclear, and there is also a lack of consensus on optimal modeling methodologies [13, 14]. While numerous studies in other surgical fields have focused on constructing and validating predictive models for ICU stay [15–18], and a substantial body of literature in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) has analyzed risk factors for both overall hospital stay and ICU admission [19–21], there is currently no specific predictive tool for the risk of prolonged Length of Stay in the ICU (ICU-LOS) in patients undergoing oral free flap reconstruction without tracheostomy. This gap leads to a lack of precise guidance for clinical resource allocation and rehabilitative interventions. Developing a targeted predictive model would facilitate early identification of high-risk patients, optimize management strategies, and support Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols. To address this unmet need, this study aims to develop and validate a nomogram for assessing the risk of prolonged ICU-LOS in this specific patient population, thereby advancing precision postoperative management.

Methods

Study objective

This study aims to develop and validate a clinical prediction model for assessing the risk of prolonged ICU-LOS in patients undergoing oral free flap reconstruction without prophylactic tracheostomy. Through retrospective data analysis, we seek to establish a mathematical model capable of providing individualized risk assessment, which entails identifying independent predictors from baseline characteristics, intraoperative variables, and routinely measured postoperative indicators within the first 24 h. The model’s discriminative ability, calibration, and clinical utility will be internally validated. Ultimately, the model will be converted into an intuitive nomogram for direct clinical application.

Research methods

Study design

This is a single-center retrospective study. It included adult patients admitted to the Intensive Care Unit of Shandong Provincial Hospital between January 2019 and July 2025. Eligible patients were those who underwent radical resection for oral and maxillofacial diseases with concurrent free vascularized flap reconstruction and did not receive a tracheostomy.

Study subjects

A total of 419 patients were included. The inclusion criteria were: (1) Patients who underwent radical resection for oral and maxillofacial diseases with concurrent free vascularized flap reconstruction and did not receive a tracheostomy; (2) Postoperative routine transfer to the ICU for initial resuscitation and monitoring; (3) Availability of complete perioperative electronic medical records and ICU nursing records.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) Age < 18 years; (2) Etiology of trauma or infection; (3) Discharge against medical advice; (4) Missing key information or contradictory medical records.

Grouping method

Using stratified random sampling (stratified by prolonged ICU-LOS status), the 419 patients were divided into a training set (n = 294) and a validation set (n = 125) in a 7:3 ratio. The incidence of prolonged ICU-LOS events was kept consistent between the two sets.

Candidate predictors were selected based on prior clinical knowledge and literature, rather than including all available variables.

Outcome definition

Patients were categorized into a prolonged ICU-LOS group and a non-prolonged group based on the 75th percentile of ICU-LOS. In this study, this percentile was 6.66 days (see Table S1). Therefore, an ICU stay > 6.66 days was defined as prolonged ICU-LOS. This threshold was selected as the 75th percentile of ICU-LOS in our cohort, which represents the top quartile of patients with the highest resource utilization. The 75th percentile cutoff has been widely used to define prolonged hospital stay in other surgical populations [22, 23]. Previous studies have shown that prolonged hospital stay is associated with increased complication risks and adverse outcomes in patients undergoing major head and neck surgery and free flap reconstruction [19, 20, 24, 25], supporting the clinical relevance of identifying this high-risk population.

Predictor screening and selection

To identify key predictive factors from the candidate variables and avoid overfitting, we performed LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression analysis on all candidate variables in the training set (n = 294). The optimal λ value (λ.1se) was determined via 10-fold cross-validation, yielding a parsimonious model containing 10 variables with non-zero coefficients. The 1se criterion was chosen to ensure model simplicity and enhance generalizability, effectively preventing overfitting.

Variable retention was based on a combination of LASSO selection, clinical relevance, and model performance, rather than strict statistical significance. Therefore, predictors with P > 0.05 were retained if they contributed to model performance and were supported by prior literature.

Sample size

Post-hoc sample size assessment indicated that the training set contained 74 prolonged ICU-LOS events and 7 candidate predictor variables, resulting in an events-per-variable (EPV) ratio of 10.6:1, which satisfies the recommended minimum of 10:1. To further validate the model’s generalizability, 5-fold cross-validation was performed to assess the model’s stability and performance across different data splits.

Missing data imputation

Missing data (< 20% for all variables) were assumed to be missing at random (MAR) based on clinical plausibility and observed data patterns. Multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was performed with 5 imputations. All candidate predictors and the outcome (prolonged ICU length of stay) were included in the imputation model.

To avoid information leakage, the dataset was split into training and validation sets prior to imputation, and imputation was conducted separately within each set. Estimates were combined using Rubin’s rules.

After imputation, no missing data remained. Model coefficients were consistent across imputations, indicating robustness of the results.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 and R software (version 4.3.3). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR). Intergroup comparisons were made using the independent samples t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on the data distribution. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages), with intergroup comparisons performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All predictor screening and model construction steps were conducted within the training set. First, to prevent overfitting and directly identify key factors from all candidate predictor variables, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was employed for variable selection. The optimal penalty coefficient (λ) was determined via 10-fold cross-validation, selecting the model adhering to the “one standard error” (1se) rule to achieve a parsimonious model. Subsequently, variables with non-zero coefficients from the LASSO regression were incorporated into the final multivariable logistic regression model. However, we acknowledge that this two-stage selection strategy may introduce some instability in variable selection. Odds ratios (OR) with their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for each variable in this final model. A nomogram was constructed based on the final model.

Model performance was comprehensively evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. Discrimination was quantified by calculating the AUC in both the training and validation sets. Calibration was visually assessed using calibration curves and statistically verified using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Clinical utility was evaluated via DCA to assess the net clinical benefit.

Baseline characteristics of the training and validation sets

To ensure the validity of model development and validation, this study employed stratified random sampling to divide the 419 patients into a training set (n = 294) and a validation set (n = 125) in a 7:3 ratio, while maintaining a consistent proportion of prolonged ICU-LOS cases. Model visualization and performance validation

Based on the final multivariable logistic regression model, a nomogram for predicting the risk of prolonged ICU-LOS was constructed (Fig. 3). In the nomogram, for the “Primary Site” variable, the assigned scores corresponding to “Floor of mouth/Tongue/Oropharynx”, “Mandible/Gingiva”, and “Maxilla/Buccal/Parotid” are 45, 23, and 0 points, respectively. For the “Flap Source” variable, the scores for “Fibula flap”, “Forearm flap”, and “Anterolateral thigh flap” are 0, 14, and 41 points, respectively. Smoking history is categorized as “No/Yes”, with corresponding scores of 0 and 18 points. Blood_Loss10 represents the value of intraoperative blood loss (ml) divided by 10. Users can obtain the corresponding score for each variable axis based on the patient’s specific characteristics, sum these scores, and then read the individualized predicted probability of prolonged ICU-LOS on the total points/risk axis.

Model performance validation indicated the following: Regarding discrimination, the model achieved an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.722 (95% CI: 0.652–0.793) in the training set and 0.769 (95% CI: 0.667–0.872) in the validation set, with the corresponding ROC curves shown in Fig. 4. Comparison of the ROC curves between the training and validation sets using DeLong’s test revealed no statistically significant difference in AUC (D = -0.739, p = 0.461), suggesting the model possesses moderate-to-good discriminative ability. The 5-fold cross-validation in the training set yielded a mean AUC of 0.685 (95% CI: 0.612–0.757), while the validation set AUC was 0.760 (95% CI: 0.657–0.863). The differences compared to the single model AUCs (0.722 vs. 0.769) were all < 0.02, indicating good model stability (Figure S2).

Regarding model performance metrics, in the training set, the model achieved an accuracy of 0.731 (95% CI: 0.677–0.781), sensitivity of 0.595, specificity of 0.777, positive predictive value of 0.473, and negative predictive value of 0.851. In the validation set, the model achieved an accuracy of 0.784 (95% CI: 0.702–0.853), sensitivity of 0.516, specificity of 0.872, positive predictive value of 0.571, and negative predictive value of 0.845.

Regarding calibration, the calibration curve (Fig. 5) demonstrates good agreement between the predicted probabilities and the actual observed probabilities. We further assessed calibration using calibration intercept, slope, and Brier score. In the training cohort, the calibration intercept was 0.000, the calibration slope was 1.000, and the Brier score was 0.160. In the validation cohort, the calibration intercept was 0.555, the calibration slope was 1.248, and the Brier score was 0.152. The results of the Hosmer-Lemeshow test further support the favorable calibration of the model. In the training cohort, the test yielded a chi-square statistic of 6.98 (degrees of freedom = 8, P = 0.539); in the validation cohort, the chi-square statistic was 9.11 (degrees of freedom = 8, P = 0.333). Both P-values are greater than 0.05, indicating a good fit between the predicted and observed probabilities.

Regarding clinical utility, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA, Fig. 6) demonstrated that applying this model provides a net clinical benefit within a threshold probability range of approximately 20% to 60%. This means that when a clinician’s threshold for considering a patient at high risk for prolonged ICU stay falls between 20% and 60%—for example, if they would consider intervening when the predicted risk exceeds 20% but would not intervene below 20%—using the model to guide decisions (such as intensified monitoring, early mobilization, or proactive ICU resource allocation) leads to better outcomes than either treating all patients or treating none. The wide range of net benefit indicates that the model remains clinically useful across various risk tolerance levels.

Discussion

Main findings

This study aimed to develop and validate a clinical prediction model for assessing the risk of prolonged ICU-LOS in patients undergoing oral free flap reconstruction without prophylactic tracheostomy. Through LASSO regression and multivariable logistic regression analysis, the final model incorporated the following seven independent predictors: primary site (Location), flap source, smoking history (Smoking), intraoperative blood loss (per 10 mL increment), postoperative blood glucose (Glucose), postoperative procalcitonin (PCT), and the International Normalized Ratio (INR). The nomogram prediction tool constructed based on these factors demonstrated good discriminative ability, calibration, and clinical utility.

Comparison with existing research and significance of predictors

Multiple studies have systematically investigated various factors influencing the length of stay following oral free flap reconstruction. Mohamed et al. identified preoperative renal dysfunction, peripheral arterial occlusive disease, postoperative complications, and NYHA class Ⅲ as high-risk factors for prolonged ICU-LOS [19]. Wallner et al. further confirmed the association of prolonged ICU-LOS with preoperative renal dysfunction, peripheral vascular disease, and higher NYHA class, while also identifying advanced age, prolonged operative time, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and intraoperative blood transfusion as risk factors [21]. The study by Denis et al. indicated that prolonged ICU-LOS was closely linked to COPD, history of pulmonary disease, intraoperative transfusion, and operative time, with postoperative infections, particularly pneumonia, playing a significant role [26]. Regarding intraoperative management, a study on fluid management, through an analysis of 1047 patients, was the first to systematically demonstrate that unnecessary intraoperative blood transfusion and inadequate fluid volume within 24 h were independent risk factors for prolonged hospital stay [27]. Research focusing on free fibula flap reconstruction, via a matched analysis of 375 patients, further confirmed that intraoperative blood transfusion was independently associated with longer hospital stay, remaining significant even after excluding confounding factors such as preoperative anemia, history of radiotherapy, and massive intraoperative blood loss [28]. The prospective study by BuSaba established a comprehensive predictive framework encompassing preoperative characteristics (age, functional status, alcohol use, COPD, diabetes), intraoperative course (operative time, transfusion), and postoperative adverse events [24]. Lindeborg et al. identified flap take-back, hospital-acquired pneumonia or urinary tract infection, wound complications, and prior radiotherapy as significant predictors of increased LOS [25]. The recently developed nomogram model by Garip et al. integrated multiple variables including flap type, operative time, postoperative complications, and unplanned reoperation to create a quantitative tool for predicting prolonged hospitalization risk [20]. Additionally, a meta-analysis on the impact of smoking found a significant association between smoking and increased overall surgical complications as well as hematoma risk, providing important references for preoperative risk assessment [29]. In summary, existing research has systematically identified a multidimensional array of influencing factors spanning preoperative comorbidities, intraoperative management, and postoperative complications, providing comprehensive evidence for risk assessment.

However, most studies focus on overall hospital length of stay and lack the integration of early postoperative dynamic indicators. This study focuses on the critical juncture of prolonged ICU-LOS, incorporates early postoperative physiological markers, and builds upon existing research on risk factors to enhance the timeliness and clinical applicability of prediction.

Potential clinical applications

The predictive model developed in this study integrates preoperative baseline data (demographic characteristics), intraoperative variables, and key monitoring indicators from the first 24 h postoperatively. The resulting nomogram can provide individualized, dynamic risk assessment during the early postoperative period. This facilitates prospective planning for ICU bed and staffing allocation to optimize resource utilization and offers an objective, quantitative basis for formulating personalized Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) pathways.

strengths, limitations, and future directions

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first to develop a predictive model specifically for patients undergoing oral free flap reconstruction without prophylactic tracheostomy—a population often overlooked in existing prediction tools. The model was developed and internally validated using a well-characterized cohort (n = 419) with thorough data collection, and we employed multiple evaluation metrics (AUC, calibration curves, DCA) to assess performance.

Several limitations should be noted. The single-center retrospective design limits generalizability, and the 7:3 split for training and validation was derived from the same institution, meaning external validation is still needed. Data collection was restricted to variables available in our electronic medical records; as a result, we were unable to capture certain factors that may influence ICU stay, such as sedation protocols (e.g., propofol, dexmedetomidine) and variations in physician preferences. The inclusion of early postoperative variables (glucose, PCT, INR) reflects acute physiological responses to surgery and inflammation, which enhances predictive accuracy. However, as these variables are only available postoperatively, the model is not suitable for preoperative decision-making. Additionally, the inclusion criteria did not specify an upper age limit, which may result in extreme age values in the cohorts and could affect model generalizability. Furthermore, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test is sample-size dependent and was used here as a supplementary rather than primary calibration assessment. In this study, the AUC in the validation cohort (0.769) was slightly higher than that in the training cohort (0.722), which may be attributable to sampling variability and the relatively smaller size of the validation cohort (n = 125). The AUC values indicate moderate discriminative ability, suggesting room for improvement. Future studies with larger samples and external validation are warranted.

The cutoff of 6.66 days for defining prolonged ICU-LOS was derived as the 75th percentile of ICU-LOS in our cohort, which is a data-driven threshold. In the absence of a universally accepted clinical definition for this patient population, using the 75th percentile is a commonly accepted approach in prediction modeling studies to identify patients with resource-intensive outcomes. Clinically, this threshold represents the top quartile of patients who consume the greatest ICU resources and are most likely to experience complications such as nosocomial infections, delirium, and delayed recovery. To address concerns about external applicability, we performed a sensitivity analysis using a clinically intuitive cutoff of 7 days. The model performance in the validation cohort remained stable (AUC: 0.763 vs. 0.769), supporting the robustness of our model regardless of the specific cutoff used. In addition, the combination of LASSO and backward stepwise selection may introduce some instability in variable selection, which should be interpreted with caution.

Additionally, the INR effect in the final model showed a wide confidence interval (OR 39.429, 95% CI 2.387–651.276), indicating instability in the estimate. Examination of INR distribution in the overall cohort revealed no extreme outliers or significant skewness; the wide CI is likely due to the relatively small number of patients with elevated INR, which may have led to sparse-data effects. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate the association between INR and prolonged ICU-LOS.

Future research should prioritize multi-center external validation to assess whether the model performs consistently across different patient populations and practice settings. Prospective studies could also standardize the collection of sedation-related data to better account for their potential impact on ICU-LOS. Incorporating dynamic postoperative variables—such as hourly vital sign trends or daily fluid balance—might further improve predictive accuracy. If these efforts confirm its utility, the model could eventually support clinical decision-making by helping teams identify high-risk patients early and allocate ICU resources more efficiently.

Conclusions

This study developed the first predictive model for prolonged ICU stay in patients undergoing oral free flap reconstruction without prophylactic tracheostomy. Using routine perioperative variables—including primary site, flap source, smoking history, intraoperative blood loss (per 10 mL), postoperative glucose, PCT, and INR—the model achieved AUCs of 0.722 (training) and 0.769 (validation). It helps identify high-risk patients early during postoperative monitoring, supporting timely adjustments to extubation plans, ICU resource allocation, and individualized recovery pathways.
