TOP3A pathogenic variants presenting as dilated cardiomyopathy and heart failure in three pediatric cases
Authors: Yipu Zhang, Jiayi Xu, Wenxiu Chan, Hao Chen, Jinjin Wu, Ying Guo, Ru-en Yao, Lijun Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is the most prevalent form of cardiomyopathy in children, characterized by left ventricle dilation and impaired systolic function. The etiology critically influences clinical trajectory and prognosis. Mitochondrial disorders represent a rare but increasingly recognized cause of DCM. Herein, we report three pediatric patients, diagnosed with early-onset DCM at ages of 8, 9, and 8, who progressed rapidly to end stage heart failure, resulting in two fatalities and one cardiac transplantation. Whole exome sequencing (WES) analysis identified compound heterozygous TOP3A pathogenic variants in all three cases, accompanied by reduced mitochondrial DNA copy number. Therefore, this report expands the recognized etiologies of childhood DCM and delineates a severe cardiac phenotype within the TOP3A pathogenic variant spectrum. Introduction

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a clinically and genetically heterogeneous disorder, characterized by left ventricular dilation and concomitant systolic dysfunction [1]. DCM remains the most common cardiomyopathy in children and the leading indication for cardiac transplantation in this population [2, 3]. Genetically mediated DCM arises from pathogenic variants in genes encoding sarcomeric proteins, cytoskeletal elements, nuclear envelope components, and ion channels. Commonly implicated pathogenic genes include TTN, LMNA, MYH7, and MYBPC3 [4, 5]. Despite extensive research into the etiology and prognosis of pediatric DCM in recent decades, a substantial proportion of cases are still classified as idiopathic [6]. Among mitochondrial disorders associated with DCM, Barth syndrome is the best-characterized cause of DCM in children [7]. However, DCM attributable to TOP3A deficiency has been scarcely reported. In this study, three unrelated patients carrying the TOP3A gene pathogenic variant admitted to Shanghai Children’s Medical Center presented with DCM; notably, one patient also fulfilled diagnostic criteria for left ventricular noncompaction cardiomyopathy (LVNC).

The TOP3A gene, located at chromosome 17p11.2, encodes two functionally distinct isoforms of topoisomerase IIIα: a nuclear isoform (TopIIIα) and a mitochondrially targeted isoform (mtTopIIIα) [8]. Accumulating evidence indicates that mtTopIIIα has partial functional autonomy to regulate the transcription, replication, and segregation of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) independence from its nuclear counterpart [8, 9]. Pathogenic TOP3A variants disrupt these critical processes, leading to two interrelated molecular consequences: First, nuclear genomic instability, typically manifested by elevated sister chromatid exchange [10, 11]. Second, mitochondrial instability, leading to mtDNA depletion and consequent functional defects [11–13]. Clinically, the presentation of TOP3A pathogenic variants correlates with residual enzyme activity [9]. Profound loss of function typically manifests in childhood as a Bloom-like syndrome, featuring intrauterine growth retardation (IUGR), microcephaly, and dwarfism [10]. In contrast, a moderate loss of activity often underlies adult-onset canonical mitochondrial symptoms, including sensorineural hearing loss, peripheral neuropathy, diabetes, and chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia (CPEO) [14, 15]. Although this establishes a spectrum from developmental growth disorders to late-onset neuromuscular degeneration, TOP3A-associated DCM has not been systematically delineated in the pediatric population.

Herein we report three pediatric patients with DCM harboring novel compound heterozygous pathogenic variants in the TOP3A gene. Following their DCM diagnosis, all three patients deteriorated rapidly to end-stage heart failure within months of diagnosis, including two fatalities and one heart transplant. While classic TOP3A deficiency often presents with a mixed phenotype driven by both nuclear DNA instability (Bloom-like syndrome) and mitochondrial DNA maintenance defects [10, 16], our cohort demonstrates that the disease can manifest predominantly, and devastatingly, as a mitochondrial cytopathy. One of the patients was also the first reported case of progressive extraocular muscle paralysis in children caused by TOP3A gene pathogenic variant.

Case report

Case presentation

Patient 1 (P1)

Patient 1, a 9-year-old female, initially presented with extra-cardiac manifestations, specifically bilateral sensorineural hearing loss diagnosed at age 5. Her cardiac symptoms emerged at age 9, characterized by exercise-induced cyanosis. Physical and diagnostic evaluations at our center confirmed the onset of DCM. To investigate the underlying etiology of this clinical presentation, WES was performed during this admission, which subsequently identified a pathogenic variant in the TOP3A gene. Despite initial stabilization, the clinical course was marked by rapid deterioration. Ten months after the initial cardiac presentation, the patient was readmitted in cardiogenic shock complicated by ventricular tachycardia. This progressed into multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), involving respiratory, hepatic, and renal dysfunction, alongside coagulopathy. The patient succumbed to the illness 10 months post-DCM onset. Notably, her family history revealed an older brother who exhibited an identical clinical triad of DCM and hearing loss at age 8, passing away at age 11. The parents were nonconsanguineous.

P1 developed premature ventricular contractions along with electrical conduction blocks such as complete right bundle branch block (CRBBB), Based on the constellation of patient’s history, symptoms, laboratory evidence for myocardial damage and classical manifestations of cardiac imaging examinations, P1 fulfilled diagnostic criteria for DCM.

P1 received digoxin to enhance cardiac function, followed by angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI) (captopril), β-blockers (carvedilol) and inhibitors of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) (Dapagliflozin). In response to the poor effect of improving cardiac function, Entresto was subsequently administered as a replacement for ACEI. Phosphocreatine was administered for myocardial energetic support, while loop diuretics and strict fluid restriction were used for fluid management. P1 underwent treatment with coenzyme Q10, lecithin, and vitamin B2, which is called cocktail therapy [17]. Following adjustment of the treatment regimen, she experienced a transient improvement in both cardiac function and clinical symptoms. After clinical status deteriorated, escalating vasoactive support was initiated to maintain blood pressure and heart rate stability; amiodarone was used to manage paroxysmal ventricular tachycardia.

Additionally, despite extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support, P1 experienced irreversible multiple organ dysfunction with LVEF declining to 8.16%. Following multidisciplinary discussion and shared decision-making with the family, life-sustaining therapies were withdrawn. After ECMO removal and extubation, the patient developed apnea and progressed to clinical death 10 months after DCM onset.

Patient 2 (P2)

Patient 2, a 10-year-old male, presented with a complex clinical constellation of multisystemic anomalies and progressive functional decline. His medical history was notable for multiple congenital malformations, including pectus excavatum, congenital hallux valgus, spinal scoliosis, and postnatal growth retardation. The onset of his current illness occurred at age 8, marked by exertional fatigue and progressive sensorineural hearing loss. By age 9, the clinical phenotype expanded to include CPEO. During a subsequent evaluation at age 10, laryngoscopy identified left vocal cord paralysis, which was soon followed by the development of distal limb weakness. Concurrently, a comprehensive cardiac evaluation led to the diagnosis of DCM. To establish a unifying diagnosis for these multisystemic and cardiac manifestations, genetic testing was performed during this hospitalization, confirming a pathogenic variant in the TOP3A gene. Within 14 months of the DCM diagnosis, the patient’s disease underwent rapid progression, ultimately developing terminal MODS.

Laboratory tests of the P2

NT-proBNP 5730 pg/ml, cTnI showed no abnormalities (0.01 ug/L). A comparable elevation was observed in the indices of P2 during clinical deterioration (NT-proBNP: 74,295 pg/ml; cTnI: 0.82 ug/L at the terminal stage).

Initial echocardiography showed left atrial and left ventricle enlargement (LVDD-z score + 4.60), and reduced global contractility (LVEF 40.83%). CE-CMR confirmed the diagnosis and showed extensive delayed enhancement of the LV myocardium. At age 10, brain MRI with contrast showed symmetric T2-hyperintense signal abnormalities in the globus pallidus and cerebral peduncles, in line with mitochondrial encephalomyopathy. The electrocardiogram performed on P2 at the age of 10 years revealed a CRBBB.

P2 met diagnostic criteria for DCM. Pharmacologic management mirrored that of P1: digoxin for cardiac strengthening, and various vasodilators, diuretics, and fluid restriction. Additional therapeutic measures included sodium creatine phosphate for cardioprotection, alongside a multidimensional cocktail therapy (coenzyme Q10, L-carnitine, and vitamin B2) for the management of the primary condition. Additional supportive interventions included Aspirin was used to prevent thrombosis, febuxostat was used to lower uric acid, Tenascin was used to protect the liver and prevent infection. At last follow-up, significant reduction in LVEF to 21.58% and obvious enlargement of the left atrium and left ventricle (LA 3.72 cm, LVDD-z score + 4.85) indicated that the patient entered the end stage of the disease 14 months after the diagnosis of myocardial involvement. He eventually succumbed to the illness following the development of irreversible MODS.

Patient 3 (P3)

Patient 3, an 8-year-old female, with no remarkable prior medical history or developmental delays, presented with systemic fluid overload secondary to acute heart failure following an upper respiratory tract infection. This presentation was characterized by bilateral lower extremity edema, pulmonary congestion, and pericardial effusion. Despite initial treatment for the inciting infection at a local clinic, her rapid clinical deterioration and significant weight gain (approximately 3 kg) prompted emergency admission. Diagnostic evaluations confirmed DCM in a state of acute decompensation. WES was subsequently initiated during the hospitalization, which definitively revealed a TOP3A pathogenic variant.

Laboratory tests of the P3

NT-proBNP 7100 pg/ml, cTnI were mild increased (0.04 ug/L). Then cTnI(0.02 ug/L) showed a modest decline following the initiation of treatment.

Cardiac ultrasound revealed left ventricular dilation (LVDD-z score + 5.80) severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF at 25%), and mild to moderate mitral regurgitation, mild tricuspid regurgitation (regurgitation velocity 2.73 m/s). CE-CMR showed revealed extensive late gadolinium enhancement involving the anterior septal and inferior left ventricular walls, with sparing of other myocardial segments. Within two months of diagnosis, Patient 3 developed an incomplete right bundle branch block (IRBBB).

Initial management focused on aggressive diuresis and inotropic support with milrinone and digoxin, followed by a transition to carvedilol and Entresto. Upon identifying TOP3A pathogenic variants and mitochondrial DNA depletion, metabolic ‘cocktail’ therapy was added for cardioprotection. Following adjustment of the treatment regimen, a slight decline in cTnI levels was observed; however, echocardiographic parameters showed no significant improvement. Within two months of the DCM diagnosis, the patient proceeded to cardiac transplantation.

Discussion

In this study, all three subjects were hospitalized due to varying degrees of heart failure and were diagnosed with DCM based on characteristic cardiac imaging findings (left ventricular dilation accompanied by reduced left ventricular ejection fraction). Initially, none of the three subjects showed significant improvement following conventional DCM treatment. It is noteworthy that no prior genetic workup had been conducted for the early extra-cardiac presentations in P1 and P2. In our clinical practice, whole-exome sequencing (WES) remains a high-cost investigation and is rarely utilized as a first-line test for early, isolated symptoms. Instead, WES is strongly indicated for patients presenting with fulminant disease progression or a refractory response to standard therapies. Driven by their rapid clinical deterioration and poor response to initial heart failure treatments, subsequent comprehensive WES was initiated, identifying compound heterozygous pathogenic variants in the TOP3A gene in all three cases. Quantitative PCR assays were performed on peripheral blood samples from all three patients to assess mitochondrial gene copy numbers. The results revealed that the mitochondrial DNA copy numbers in these individuals were significantly reduced compared to the average levels observed in the control group. Based on these clinical manifestations and auxiliary examination results, we propose that the cardiomyopathy observed in these three patients may be attributable to mitochondrial dysfunction resulting from pathogenic variants in the TOP3A gene. Consequently, the treatment strategy was supplemented with a cocktail therapy regimen. Conventional heart failure therapy failed to arrest the rapid disease progression in this cohort. Following a brief period of symptomatic relief, all patients progressed to terminal multiorgan failure. Fatal outcomes occurred for P1 and P2 at 10 and 14 months post-onset, while P3 required heart transplantation after only 2 months. We therefore hypothesize that in children with TOP3A gene pathogenic variants, a predominant mitochondrial disease phenotype accompanied by early-onset DCM may correlate with a poor prognosis. Mitochondrial disorders account for a small proportion of pediatric DCM and have not been identified as a prognostic factor in previous decades of research [18]. However, studies in the field of mitochondrial diseases suggest that patients with cardiac involvement have a worse prognosis compared to those without, which may explain the outcomes observed in our patients [19].

P1 discussed herein presented with DCM and left ventricular non-compaction (LVNC), resembling myocardial damage seen in Barth syndrome, a rare X-linked genetic disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the TAFAZZIN (also known as TAZ) gene located at chromosome Xq28. This condition predominantly affects males and is characterized by cardiomyopathy, skeletal myopathy, neutropenia, growth retardation and recurrent infections [20]. Nevertheless, a consistent pattern of electrophysiological deterioration was observed: both P1 and P2 exhibited premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) during the early stages of their myocardial involvement, which gradually progressed to complete right bundle branch block (CRBBB) atrioventricular block and left anterior hemiblock as the cardiomyopathy advanced. Meanwhile, P3 demonstrated IRBBB two months after initial diagnosis. In a cohort of 11 patients with TOP3A gene pathogenic variants, five developed heart block necessitating pacemaker implantations, and one experienced paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia due to left ventricular hypertrophy [9], which is largely consistent with the manifestations in our patients.

When comparing the clinical features between our three patients against those typically seen within mitochondrial diseases or Bloom-like syndromes cases outlined in Table 1, all primarily manifested signs indicative of reduced mitochondrial copy number functionality, including cardiomyopathy, cardiac conduction abnormalities and exercise intolerance. None of the patients reported here presented with dysmorphic features or café-au-lait macules, P2 was the only patient with reported postnatal growth delay. Their presentations aligned more closely with primary mitochondrial cardiomyopathy. A reported cohort of ten patients with TOP3A variants included three pediatric DCM cases, and each presented with Bloom syndrome-associated manifestations, specifically characteristic dysmorphic facies and a history of Prenatal-onset growth restriction [10]. In another study of two siblings, both subjects developed DCM in childhood and subsequently died. Similarly, these two individuals were also affected by Bloom-like syndrome [16]. Our findings build upon and expand the previously described spectrum of TOP3A-related diseases. The rapid progression to cardiogenic shock and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome observed in our cohort, notably without classic Bloom-like stigmata, broadens the known phenotypic spectrum of TOP3A pathogenic variants in the pediatric population.

P2 developed mild CPEO at age 9 and experienced decline in limb muscle strength at age 11, marking the first documented instance of CPEO in a pediatric-onset associated with a TOP3A pathogenic variant. To date, all reported cases of CPEO associated with TOP3A gene pathogenic variants have been characterized by adult onset [8, 9, 14, 15]. Despite this early presentation, symptoms such as ptosis contributed to delayed diagnosis. With respect to hearing impairment, previous studies indicate that approximately 40% of patients with mitochondrial diseases develop hearing loss [22]. Both P1 and P2 presented sensorineural hearing loss from an early age and exhibited irreversible conditions, consistent with typical mitochondrial disease manifestations.

Impact and limitations

Our reports indicate that pathogenic variants in TOP3A result in mitochondrial dysfunction in children, in the absence of growth failure, whereas Bloom-like syndrome manifestations—such as microcephaly and growth retardation—have traditionally been reported as the predominant features in this age group. Fresh skeletal muscle biopsy still remains the gold standard for diagnosing mitochondrial disease [23]. However, endomyocardial biopsy may constitute an alternative diagnostic approach in cases with cardiac involvement or tissue-specific pathogenic variants [24]; this procedure was not performed in our study due to impaired cardiac function in all three subjects. Functional analyses could elucidate whether these three cases align more closely with Bloom-like syndrome or broader mitochondrial diseases based on pathogenic variantal profiles identified. Although we confirmed significant reductions in mitochondrial gene copy number via qPCR analysis of peripheral blood samples, functional assessments of residual mitochondrial gene activity were not conducted. Direct functional assays, such as enzyme activity tests or animal models, were also lacking.

Based on our findings, we strongly recommend routine baseline and longitudinal cardiac screening (ECG and echocardiography) for patients harboring TOP3A pathogenic variants or suspected mitochondrial disorders, even when presenting with isolated extra-cardiac symptoms. Furthermore, given the high mortality of TOP3A-associated cardiomyopathy, we advocate for the early, pre-symptomatic initiation of a ‘metabolic cocktail’ therapy. In our cases, some clinical benefits were observed in the early stages of the disease, despite subsequent progression. For patients with end-stage mitochondrial-related DCM, heart transplantation may also represent a viable therapeutic alternative.

Moreover, the selection of age-matched control groups would enable comparative analyses via microscopic examination of sister chromatid exchange events in fresh blood samples. Regrettably, both subjects succumbed prior to this investigation, precluding acquisition of fresh peripheral blood samples necessary for such comparisons. Nevertheless, our report broadens the disease spectrum of the population with TOP3A gene pathogenic variants and augments the repertoire of known genetic loci implicated in DCM pathogenesis.

Conclusion

This report expands the known etiologies of pediatric DCM and suggests that early‑onset DCM due to TOP3A pathogenic variants may correlate with a poor prognosis. Furthermore, the observed reduction in mtDNA copy number confirms that children carrying TOP3A pathogenic variants can exhibit mitochondrial dysfunction in the absence of concurrent Bloom-like syndrome. This finding challenges prior reports.

This study constitutes the first report of pediatric CPEO associated with a TOP3A gene pathogenic variant—a notable contrast to previous literature. Consequently, it is imperative for clinicians managing children with DCM who exhibit clinical features indicative of mitochondrial disease to pursue comprehensive genetic testing to rule out mitochondrial-related gene pathogenic variants.
