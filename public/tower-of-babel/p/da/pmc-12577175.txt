The role of lifestyle factors in type 2 diabetes in children and adolescents: a systematic review
Authors: Kathryn Fortnum, George Thomas, Kiara Too, Athena Tam, Kate Tilley, John Cairney, Genevieve N Healy, Sjaan Gomersall
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRates of type 2 diabetes in children and young people are rising worldwide; however, the role of lifestyle factors in the management of type 2 diabetes are less clear for this age group. The purpose of this systematic review was to examine associations between lifestyle factors (physical activity, sedentary behaviour, sleep, diet/nutrition), and effects of lifestyle modification interventions, on (1) primary type 2 diabetes indicators (i.e., HbA1c) and (2) secondary (i.e., insulin-related variables; anthropometry; cholesterol; triglycerides; cardiac, liver and kidney health/function; blood pressure; mental health and quality of life; fitness and strength) indicators in children and adolescents with type 2 diabetes.MethodsSeven databases (CINAHL, Cochrane, Embase, PsycInfo, PubMed, Scopus) were searched from inception to February 2025 for primary peer-reviewed articles, written in English. Articles must have evaluated one-or-more lifestyle factors and primary or secondary diabetes indicators in children and adolescents (< 18 years) with type 2 diabetes. Both observational and experimental studies were included. Study quality was assessed using the National Collaborating Centre for Methods and Tools Assessment Tool for Quantitative Studies. Data synthesis was guided by the Cochrane Synthesis Without Meta-analysis guidelines.ResultsTwenty six articles were eligible (63% were conducted in the USA); 15 experimental, targeting combined diet and physical activity (n = 9, duration 16wks-12mo), physical activity alone (n = 3, duration 12-16wks), and diet alone (n = 3, duration 9-34wks). The remaining studies were observational and evaluated combined diet and physical activity (n = 2), physical activity (n = 4), sedentary behaviour (n = 1), sleep (n = 1), and diet/nutrition (n = 5). Increased engagement in a combination of physical activity and healthy diet, and improved sleep quality, were associated with lower HbA1c. An unclear or no relationship existed between physical activity or diet/nutrition alone with HbA1c, and secondary indicators. Evidence was provided for the positive impact of diet on HbA1c, and for physical activity on anthropometric variables.ConclusionsCompared to the body of literature available in adults, evidence for the role of lifestyle factors in children and young people is limited. Lifestyle factors show promise for managing disease burden in children and adolescents, but outcomes remain unclear as existing research is heterogeneous.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23767-0.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes, Lifestyle, Physical activity, Sleep, Diet, Nutrition, Sedentary behaviour, Management, Young people, Endocrinology

Background

Type 2 diabetes is historically recognised as a disease presenting in the middle and later years of life [1]; however, the incidence in younger people is increasing rapidly. In the United States of America, the incidence of early onset type 2 diabetes in 2002–2003 was nine new cases per 100,000 individuals, which jumped to 13.8 in 2014–2016; corresponding to an age-and sex-adjusted annual percentage increase of 4.8% [2]. Between 2010 and 2050, prevalence rates in children and adolescents in America have been predicted to increase by 178% [3]. Similar increases in incidence and prevalence of type 2 diabetes in children and adolescents have also been demonstrated across Asia, Oceania, and Europe [4–9].

The rise in diagnostic rates should be cause for public health concern, especially given that children and adolescents suffer from a more aggressive disease progression than adult onset [10]. Early onset of type 2 diabetes also translates to prolonged lifetime exposure to hyperglycaemia, resulting in the development of severe long-term diabetes-related microvascular and macrovascular complications and comorbidities (e.g., diabetic kidney disease, retinopathy and peripheral neuropathy) much earlier in life [11–13]. Rhodes, Prosser [14] determined the rates of diabetes related complications in people living with type 2 diabetes who were diagnosed between the ages of 15–24 years reporting lifetime cumulative incidences as follows; end stage renal disease up to 29.0%, blindness up to 18.5%, and lower limb amputation up to 4.7%. Diagnosis of type 2 diabetes in childhood and adolescence is also associated with a poorer quality of life and decreased life expectancy, with a predicted loss of 15 years compared to peers without type 2 diabetes [14].

Pharmacological or lifestyle interventions may be introduced to manage and/or treat type 2 diabetes [15]. Lifestyle management encompasses a range of approaches including dietary, physical activity, and sleep modifications [15]. Lifestyle management has been demonstrated to elicit significant improvements in glycaemic control, risk factors associated with cardiovascular disease (e.g., body mass index, cholesterol, blood pressure), quality of life, and diet and exercise behaviours in adults [16, 17]. However, the evidence for children and adolescents with type 2 diabetes is more limited. In 2010, Johnson, Newton [18] conducted a systematic review of lifestyle management interventions and glycaemic control in children and adolescents with type 2 diabetes [18], identifying only one study examining an intervention, specifically diet modification, which was rated ‘poor’ quality. Since then, there has been an increase in publications related to type 2 diabetes in children and adolescents, including both observational and intervention studies [19]. As such, a rigorous review and update of the evidence is warranted to better understand the role of lifestyle behaviours in the management of type 2 diabetes in children and adolescents.

Correspondingly, the aims of this systematic review were to (i) examine the associations of lifestyle factors (physical activity, sedentary behaviour, sleep, diet/nutrition) with primary (i.e., HbA1c) and secondary (e.g., anthropometry, cholesterol, triglycerides, quality of life) type 2 diabetes indicators; and, (ii) investigate the effects of lifestyle management interventions on primary and secondary indicators, in children and adolescents living with type 2 diabetes. Findings are expected to raise awareness and inform approaches to address this escalating public health concern.

Methods

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed with the support of a research librarian and prospectively registered in Prospero (CRD42023405493). Searches to identify eligible studies were conducted in CINAHL, Cochrane EMBASE PubMed, PyscInfo, Scopus, and Web of Science from inception to February 2025. Key search terms included: type 2, diabetes, management, glycaemic control, exercise, sleep, diet, lifestyle, teen*, adolesc*, child* with MESH terms used where appropriate. An example of the search strategy is provided in Supplementary Material S1. The reference lists of all included papers were also manually reviewed.

Study selection

Identified studies were imported into Covidence software [20] and duplicates removed. Study selection was performed by two reviewers (KF and KT). A third reviewer (SG) was included in the case of disagreements. Title and abstract screening was performed initially, followed by full text review. Studies were included if they (a) met the population, inclusion, comparison and outcomes (PICO) [21] inclusion criteria defined in Table 1; (b) were primary research, including interventional studies (e.g. randomised and non-randomised controlled trials, mixed methods search and pilot studies) or observational studies (e.g. cross sectional, longitudinal and cohort studies); and, (c) were written in English. Studies were excluded if participants were: above the age of 18 years or had a mean age of ≥ 19 years (unless results were reported separately for those aged < 19 years); had pre-diabetes; or, had a primary diagnosis of another type of diabetes (e.g. type 1 diabetes). Studies were also excluded if they were not peer reviewed; were reviews; were not published in English; and if they focused solely on pharmacological interventions.

Data extraction

Data from eligible studies were extracted using Covidence software according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews [20, 22]. Data extraction was performed independently by one reviewer (AT or KT), and cross checked by a second reviewer (KF or GT) for accuracy. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and a third reviewer included if required (SG). Extracted data included: author and publication details; study design, location, year and duration of study; intervention/exposure details; number and demographics of participants; relevant outcomes; and data pertaining to adherence, adverse events, and acceptability.

Quality assessment

Study quality was assessed using the National Collaborating Centre for Methods and Tools Assessment Tool for Quantitative Studies [23]. Studies were assessed based on selection bias, study design, confounders, blinding, data collection methods, withdrawals and drop-outs, intervention integrity and analyses, and received a global quality rating of weak (two or more weak ratings), moderate (one weak rating) or strong (no weak ratings) as per the assessment tool data dictionary. Study quality was independently assessed by two reviewers (KF, KT, or AT). In the event of conflicts of opinion, the opinion of a third reviewer (SG or GT) was sought and results discussed until consensus was achieved.

Data synthesis

Data synthesis was guided by the Cochrane Synthesis Without Meta-analysis guidelines [24]. Data from studies were grouped by exposure evaluated or intervention type (i.e., lifestyle [studies that targeted two or more behaviours], physical activity, sedentary behaviour, diet, or sleep). Heterogeneity of study designs, outcomes and exposure variables precluded further data synthesis or quantitative meta-analytical approaches. Within each exposure or intervention type, results were prioritised based on study design (with interventional studies reported first) and participant numbers (higher sample sizes were reported first).

Results are reported in an effect direction plot to provide a visual representation of reported effects. To generate the effect direction plot, secondary outcomes were grouped into categories (e.g., anthropometry, cholesterol, triglycerides; see Fig. 2 for the effect direction plot). The following rules were applied to outcomes within the categories: (1) where multiple outcomes within a study all report effects in same direction, effect direction was reported; (2) where the direction of effect varied across multiple outcomes within a study and 70% of outcomes reported a similar direction, direction of effect was reported; (3) where direction of effect varied across multiple outcomes within a study and < 70% of outcomes reported consistent direction of effect, no clear effect/conflicting findings were reported (i.e., ◄►) [25]. Adherence, and the impacts of adherence on outcomes, as well as acceptability were also reported for the intervention studies when available.

Results

Characteristics of included articles

The search identified 3368 articles. Following the screening process (see Fig. 1 for PRISMA diagram), 26 articles were considered eligible and included in the review. Eight articles were associated with a single intervention, the Treatment Options for Type 2 Diabetes in Adolescents and Youth (TODAY) Clinical Trial, so were grouped as a single study for the remainder of the review (i.e., 19 studies [eight experimental] across the 26 articles). A summary of the study (combined observational and experimental) and intervention characteristics are available in Table 2. Detailed article characteristics, including citations of the included articles, are available in Supplementary Material S2.

Summary of study (n = 18) and intervention (n = 7) characteristics

Eight studies were interventional. The interventions targeted lifestyle management (diet and physical activity, n = 2), physical activity (n = 3), and dietary changes (n = 3). Two studies included a qualitative component; one of these utilised semi-structured interviews to determine perceived benefits plus intervention acceptability, the other utilised semi-structured interviews to determine intervention acceptability only. Participant numbers with type 2 diabetes ranged 8-699. The remaining eleven studies were observational designs. Behaviours of interest evaluated were lifestyle (diet and physical activity, n = 2), physical activity (n = 4), sedentary behaviour (n = 1), sleep (n = 1), and diet/nutrition (n = 5). Participant numbers with type 2 diabetes ranged 42–699.

Quality assessment

The quality of all included articles excluding one was determined to be ‘weak’; the final article was considered ‘moderate’ quality. Weak quality ratings were attributed to not reporting, or using unvalidated or unreliable outcome assessment measures (n = 23 articles, 92.0%), selection bias (n = 17 articles, 68.0%), study design (n = 13 articles, 52.0%), a follow-up rate of < 60% or drop-outs not being reported (n = 13 articles, 52.0%), not adequately controlling for confounders (n = 7 articles, 28.0%), and blinding concerns (n = 1 article, 4.0%). Quality assessment results are available in Supplementary Material S3.

Lifestyle

Two experimental studies examined the effects of a holistic lifestyle intervention (physical activity and diet; TODAY Clinical Trial [n > 300] and Bright Bodies Curriculum [n < 50]) on HbA1c and secondary indicators, specifically insulin-related measures; anthropometry; cholesterol; triglycerides; cardiac, liver and kidney function; and blood pressure. Holistic lifestyle management did not positively impact HbA1c or any of the secondary T2D indicators. Adherence to the TODAY clinical trial was 60% overall [26]; adherence ≥ 75% compared to < 75% did not have an impact on glycaemic control, but did have an impact on percent overweight. The impacts of adherence on other secondary indicators were not reported [26]. Retention in the Bright Bodies Curriculum was > 75%; however details on how ‘retention’ was determined were not provided. Adverse events were reported for the TODAY Clinical Trial only, with significantly less hypoglycaemic events evident in the lifestyle management compared to the medication only groups; and no significant differences across muscle pain/injury-related events or serious adverse events.

Two small-to-moderate observational studies (< 50 or 50–300 participants) examined the association between lifestyle modification/management and HbA1c, with results indicating lifestyle modification/management was associated with lower HbA1c.

Physical activity and sedentary behaviour

Three small experimental studies (n < 50) examined the impacts of physical activity on HbA1c and secondary indicators, with all secondary indicators assessed across at least one of the studies except for blood pressure. None of the physical activity interventions demonstrated efficacy for reducing HbA1c. The only intervention demonstrated to be effective in improving any of the secondary measures was 12-weeks of high intensity physical activity, which resulted in improved anthropometric outcomes. Adherence was reported by one study only, and was estimated at 38% based on accelerometery outcomes. Adverse events relating to hypoglycaemia were reported in one study only, with authors indicating there were no hypoglycaemic events.

Four observational studies evaluated the relationship of physical activity participation with HbA1c, with two reporting inverse associations of physical activity with HbA1c (i.e., higher levels of physical activity were associated with lower levels of HbA1c; n > 300 and n = 50–300), and two reported no relationship between physical activity participation and HbA1c (n < 50 and n = 50–300). Two studies examined anthropometric outcomes with one observing significant inverse associations (n > 300) and the other no associations (n = 50–300). Two studies examined associations of physical activity with blood pressure, with one observing inverse associations (n > 300), and the other no association (n = 50–300). One study examined associations of physical activity with cardiac, liver and kidney function reporting inverse associations for those participating in vigorous physical activity three or more times per week only (n = 50–300). No—or no clear—associations, were observed between physical activity and cholesterol, triglycerides, or quality of life.

Only one study (observational, n = 50–300) reported on sedentary behaviour (specifically TV viewing time), finding no significant associations of TV viewing time with HbA1c and anthropometric variables.

Sleep

Sleep was examined in one study which was observational in nature (n = 50–300). Good sleep quality was inversely associated with HbA1c, but no relationships were identified with any of the secondary indicators.

Diet/nutrition

Three experimental studies explored the effect of dietary interventions (i.e., a high protein, low-fat, low-carbohydrate, very-low calorie diet; a very-low-energy-diet; and late 8 h time restricted eating; all n < 50) on HbA1c. Adherence to a high protein, low-fat, low-carbohydrate, very-low calorie diet and a very-low-energy-diet significantly reduced HbA1c; however, effects of time restricted eating were non-significant. Several secondary indicators including insulin-related measures, anthropometry, triglycerides, blood pressure and mental health/quality of life were also demonstrated to improve; however, results were inconsistent. Adherence was reported in two studies. Adherence to 8 h late time restricted eating (prescribed for 7 days per week) was an average of 6.2 ± 1.1 days per week, with no negative effects on functioning reported. Adherence to a Shakes and Meal Replacement intervention was 62.5%, with adherers demonstrating a significant improvement in insulin-related measures and higher weight loss than the non-adherers (defined by ≥ 5% body weight loss during weeks 0–8).

Five moderate-to-large observational studies (n = 50–300 or n > 300) examined the relationship of dietary behaviours with HbA1c and secondary indicators, with findings demonstrating no—or no clear—associations for all outcomes.

Discussion

This systematic review summarised evidence examining associations of lifestyle factors, and effects of lifestyle management interventions, with primary and secondary indicators of diabetes management in children and adolescents living with type 2 diabetes. According to the observational studies, increased engagement in a combination of physical activity and healthy dietary behaviours (i.e., lifestyle behaviours), and sleep quality were associated with lower HbA1c. The relationship between combined physical activity and diet behaviours and the secondary indicators remains unclear. Mixed results were also evident for the relationship between physical activity alone, HbA1c, and the secondary indicators. No relationship was evident between diet/nutrition, HbA1c and all secondary indicators.

Results of the experimental studies provided evidence for the positive impact of dietary interventions, specifically a very-low calorie and very-low energy diet on HbA1c, but no impact of holistic lifestyle management (i.e., physical activity and diet) or physical activity on HbA1c. A consistent positive intervention effect existed for physical activity alone and anthropometric variables, but not any of the other secondary indicators. Dietary interventions did not impact any of the secondary indicators. Holistic lifestyle management was also not shown (consistently) to impact secondary indicators. Results pertaining to the impact of holistic lifestyle management (i.e., physical activity and diet) should be considered with caution as the majority of experimental literature focusing on holistic lifestyle management (i.e. physical activity and diet) in children and adolescents is largely from only one trial, the TODAY clinical trial. Although the TODAY study utilised a large cohort, limitations existed in their study design. Specifically, a 2–6 month run-in period was utilised prior to randomisation during which all participants had to demonstrate mastery of a standard diabetes education program delivered by a diabetes educator, with topics including nutrition and weight management and physical activity [27]. The run-in period was considered important from an adherence perspective; however, likely facilitated behaviour change prior to the intervention period which may have overshadowed the efficacy of the lifestyle management intervention compared to medication management.

There remains a scarcity of literature focusing on sleep or sedentary behaviour, with only one observational study identified for each behaviour, and neither behaviour being included in the studies considering a more holistic ‘lifestyle’ intervention or behaviours. A dose response relationship exists between sedentary behaviour and cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors (e.g., anthropometric variables, risk for metabolic syndrome [e.g., insulin resistance, fasting glucose], risk for cardiovascular disease [e.g., cholesterol levels, blood pressure]) and self-esteem in children and adolescents [28]. Sleep has also been shown to be significantly associated with cardiovascular, metabolic and mental health in children and adolescents [29, 30]. Given the demonstrated impact of sedentary behaviour and sleep on the health of children and adolescents, and the positive relationship between sleep and HbA1c identified in this review, both behaviours therefore warrant additional research attention specifically in children and adolescents living with type 2 diabetes.

There has been a considerable increase in the number of studies focusing on lifestyle behaviours or lifestyle management in children and youth living with type 2 diabetes since the Johnson, Newton [18] review in 2010 (n = 1 eligible article). 26 articles (19 studies [8 interventional]) were identified in the current review; however, the identified evidence is heterogenous (i.e., intervention specifics, outcome metrics) making synthesis difficult. Additionally, the evidence base remains sparse compared to that for the adult population. For example, a systematic review published in 2021 evaluating the impact of lifestyle interventions on glycaemic control in adults living with type 2 diabetes in low and middle income countries alone, identified 30 randomised controlled trials [17]. The disparities between available evidence in children and adolescents compared to adults may be a result of the challenges associated with conducting research with children and adolescents including being a difficult population to reach, recruit and retain, and paediatric-specific ethical considerations. Despite these challenges, more research needs to be conducted that includes children and adolescents. Although evidence from studies on adults is useful, it may not be appropriate to extrapolate results from adult studies to children and adolescents. While the pathophysiology of type 2 diabetes is similar between the distinct age groups, children and adolescents may require different management and treatment approaches that better reflect the accelerated disease progression and associated health implications [10, 31].

Appreciation for the specific needs of children and adolescents living with type 2 diabetes, and the appropriate design of services to meet these needs, have been recognised as an important action area for minimising the impact of type 2 diabetes in this population group [32]. Development of programs specific to unique needs—in this case children and adolescents—has been demonstrated to positively impact acceptance, uptake and efficacy [33–35]. Only the two lifestyle interventions and one of the physical activity interventions reported that they were based on theories that have established evidence of success in children and adolescents, and only two studies reported on intervention acceptance. The Bright Bodies Curriculum was the only intervention for which details on retention and acceptance were provided; with children and adolescents providing positive feedback, and retention reportedly 75%, which was higher than the average of 60% for the TODAY clinical trial and 38% for the personalised exercise program [26, 36]. Though the Bright Bodies Curriculum did not demonstrate evidence of effect on HbA1c or any of the secondary indicators, the approaches utilised to deliver the Bright Bodies Curriculum appear to be well accepted by children and adolescents [37]. While this provides support for their inclusion in future lifestyle management interventions for children and adolescents living with type 2 diabetes, further research is needed to demonstrate efficacy of the intervention components on disease burden (i.e., HbA1c and secondary indicators).

Beyond acceptance, consideration should also be made towards the reporting of patient-important outcomes (e.g., wellbeing, quality of life, pain, functional status [38]). The majority of outcomes in available studies were physiological, surrogate measures (i.e., HbA1c, insulin-related measures, cholesterol, triglycerides). This is despite the fact that the importance of evaluating patient-important outcomes has been endorsed by individuals living with type 2 diabetes [39]. Outcomes identified as important and relevant to children and adolescents living with type 2 diabetes include: diabetes-related complications (e.g., infections, losing limbs, blindness and kidney disease); being fearful of judgement of others; wanting treatment option to be discrete, effective and painless; and, weight loss [40, 41]. Until child and adolescent-specific focused outcomes are better considered, the full value of the interventions on children and adolescents living with type 2 diabetes will remain unclear [38].

Limitations and strengths

There are some limitations which must be acknowledged. Eligible studies were limited to those written and published in English language, which may have introduced language and publication bias. The direction of results in the effect direction plot were only reported if > 70% of the grouped outcomes within each study were in the same direction. In cases that these were not in the same direction, an unclear result was indicated. Though chosen to concisely present the available results, the groupings utilised may have minimised or overshadowed the available evidence. The main strength of the review is the provision of a comprehensive analysis, synthesising all identified observational and interventional research pertaining to lifestyle behaviours and type 2 diabetes in children and adolescents.

Future recommendations

More interventional research is required to establish a more confident understanding of the impact of lifestyle-focused interventions on children and adolescents living with type 2 diabetes. The available interventions show promise; however, should be replicated across other cohorts and settings (e.g., The TODAY study), or in randomised controlled trials (e.g., Beating Diabetes Together). Lifestyle management programs should also consider the inclusion of sleep and sedentary behaviour to ensure they are truly ‘holistic’. Furthermore, studies should report on clinical as well as statistical significance. For example, though a reduction in HbA1c of 1% may not be considered statistically significant (as reported in one of the included studies), the finding is likely clinically significant given every 1% reduction in HbA1c has been associated with a 37% reduction in risk of microvascular complications along with a 21% lowered risk of any end point or diabetes-related death [42]. Considering the early emergence of comorbidities and the accelerated progression of disease in children and adolescent type 2 diabetes, any improvement in HbA1c may be of clinical significance. Along this same line of argument, the significance of slowing, rather than only reversing disease progression (e.g., increases in HbA1c, weight, or blood pressure), should not go unnoticed.

Conclusion

This systematic review aimed to synthesise observational and experimental research pertaining to the impact of lifestyle management—or its component parts—on HbA1c and secondary type 2 diabetes indicators in children and adolescents. Some preliminary efficacy data are available to support the impacts of physical activity and dietary interventions with HbA1c and secondary indicators, with observational data demonstrating associations between lifestyle behaviours, specific dietary behaviours and sleep with HbA1c and several secondary indicators. However, there remains a sparsity of literature on the topic. With diagnostic rates on the rise, it is critical that additional research is conducted to better understand the potential for lifestyle management to mitigate the serious complications of type 2 diabetes diagnosis in childhood and adolescence.
