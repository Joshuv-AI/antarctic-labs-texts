Protocol to characterize mouse intestinal epithelial cell lineage using Opal multiplex immunofluorescence
Authors: Anxo Martinez-Ordoñez, Hiroto Kinoshita, Tania Cid-Diaz, Angeles Duran, Marta Osrodek, Maria T. Diaz-Meco, Jorge Moscat
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryApplying Opal multiplex immunofluorescence (OMI) to characterize intestinal tissues of genetically engineered mouse models provides an excellent tool for studying complex processes. However, detecting appropriate signals from multiple target molecules is challenging. Here, we present a protocol to characterize mouse intestinal epithelial cell lineage using OMI. We describe steps for processing small intestine and colonic mouse tissues and designing and optimizing panels for OMI in mouse intestinal tissues. We then detail procedures for performing a quantitative evaluation of acquired images.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Kinoshita et al.1

Before you begin

This protocol outlines how to prepare formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) mouse intestinal tissues, design optimal panels for OMI, and provide a detailed manual of staining procedures. Following this protocol allows visualization of intestinal epithelial cell differentiation, cellular identity, and the activity of related signaling pathways. This procedure uses an intestinal tumor model mice (Prkcif/fPrkczf/f; Villin-Cre mice), an intestinal damage/metaplasia model mice (Prkcif/fPrkczf/f; Villin-CreERT2 mice), and their corresponding littermate controls (Prkcif/fPrkczf/f mice). However, we have also validated this protocol in colonic adenomas and human colorectal cancer (CRC) samples.2

Expected outcomes

In this protocol, we describe a step-by-step guide for tissue preparation and immunolabeling of the spatiotemporal dynamics of the adult stem cells, revival stem cells (RSCs), and fetal-metaplastic cells (FMCs) during homeostasis and tissue regeneration. Current approaches to delineate Clu+ cells (RSCs) in the small intestine often rely on fluorescent reporter systems and single-cell RNA sequencing (sc-RNA-seq), which present certain limitations. Reporter systems rely on laborious genetic manipulation, whereas sc-RNAseq is devoid of spatial context and its high cost prohibits routine application. Here, we provide a versatile antibody panel to study the molecular mechanisms of epithelial homeostasis and tissue regeneration in the small intestine with several alternatives for extending it to colonic tissues and human samples (Figures 4 and 5).

Limitations

In the current protocol, we describe each step of the OMI analysis, including harvesting and processing mouse intestinal tissues for FFPE, manual staining procedures, and the analyses of scanned images. Fixation conditions are essential for detecting appropriate signals of some antibodies. Further optimization of the tissue preparation may be necessary for tissues other than mouse intestines. When using archival tissues with other fixation protocols, modification and re-optimization of the staining procedures may be required.

The current protocol is appropriate for tissue microarrays to increase the throughput of the experiment. It is also possible to detect up to 6 targets simultaneously with slight protocol modification. However, establishing an even higher throughput system of fully automated staining, image capture, and image analyses is out of the scope of the current protocol.

Problem 2

The hydrophobic barrier around the tissues is compromised, which causes drying of the tissues and heterogeneous signal strength within the same tissue (major step 4).

Potential solution

We recommend using ImmEdge Hydrophobic Barrier PAP Pen. Make sure that the hydrophobic barrier is not compromised after each round of heat-induced antigen retrieval and reapply the hydrophobic barrier when necessary.

Problem 3

Signal from one target overlaps nearly completely with another (major step 63).

Potential solution

Carry-over of antibody and HRP conjugate can occur even after the stripping/heat-induced antigen retrieval steps. Consider the following to address this issue.•Change the order of staining: stain the target which causes carry-over as the last one of multiplex staining.•Increase the time and/or power of the heating step after the staining of the target which causes carry-over (e.g., 10 min at 20% and 5 min at 10%).•Increase the dilution of the primary antibody for the carry-over-causing target.
