Introduction

Recent advances in the understanding of type 2 inflammation have reshaped the therapeutic landscape of allergic diseases. The identification of central cytokines such as interleukin IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, together with epithelial-derived alarmins including thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), IL-25, and IL-33, has clarified key upstream and downstream regulatory nodes driving chronic allergic inflammation.1

In addition to adaptive Th2 cells, innate lymphoid cells type 2 (ILC2s) have emerged as critical early drivers of type 2 inflammation. ILC2s constitute a rapid, antigen-independent immune response and are directly activated by epithelial-derived alarmins, often preceding the full development of the adaptive Th2 response. This early activation positions ILC2s as key initiators of allergic inflammation and provides a strong rationale for upstream therapeutic targeting.2,3

Despite optimized use of inhaled corticosteroids, antihistamines, topical therapies, and immunosuppressants, a substantial proportion of patients with moderate-to-severe allergic diseases remain poorly controlled, experience frequent exacerbations, or develop treatment-limiting adverse effects. Steroid dependence, cumulative toxicity, and persistent disease activity represent major unmet clinical needs. Targeted biologic therapies were developed to address these gaps by selectively interrupting key molecular drivers of Type 2 inflammation, offering improved disease control while minimizing systemic toxicity.

Methods

This state-of-the-art narrative review was developed by the World Allergy Organization (WAO) Biologic Therapies Committee. A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases, including publications up to December 2025. The search strategy combined MeSH terms and free-text keywords related to type 2 inflammation, biologic therapies, monoclonal antibodies, nanobodies, small-molecule inhibitors, and type 2 mediated allergic diseases, including asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, atopic dermatitis, and chronic spontaneous urticaria.

Original research articles, phase I–III clinical trials, systematic reviews, and key translational studies published in English were included. Literature selection and evidence synthesis were performed qualitatively, with emphasis on biological plausibility, mechanisms of action, therapeutic targets, clinical efficacy, safety profiles, and emerging trends in biologic and small-molecule development.

In recent decades, advances in the understanding of type 2 inflammation have enabled the development of targeted therapies aimed at specific molecular pathways involved in allergic diseases. Beyond classical Th2 lymphocytes, multiple immune cell populations including ILC2s, basophils, mast cells, and eosinophils contribute to cytokine production and disease expression across organs.1

Type 2 inflammation as a therapeutic target

In type 2 inflammation, Th2 lymphocytes are key to directing this form of tissue damage by producing cytokines such as IL-4, IL-13, and IL-5; however, they are not the only cells that can produce these cytokines, and little by little, other cells, such as type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2), were discovered. Stem cells, basophils, and even eosinophils can also produce these chemical messengers to direct this form of inflammation.4,5

Usually, the immune system uses this form of type 2 immune and inflammatory response to defend the host from large parasites such as helminths or harmful chemicals such as some insect, arachnid, and reptile venoms. However, in some exceptional circumstances in which a genetic predisposition is combined with damaging environmental factors and poor regulation of the immune response, chronic and harmful type 2 inflammation towards different tissues can be favored, manifesting itself as atopic dermatitis, urticaria, allergic or eosinophilic asthma, allergic rhinitis, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyposis, or food allergy.5

Advances in antibody engineering and novel biologic formats

In the field of research into the pathophysiological processes of allergic diseases, we increasingly have more therapeutic options to stop the inflammatory process. Further enhancing patient care is the application of methods to extend the functional half-life and reduce dosing frequency of these biologics, such as YTE modification of the antibody Fc portion to enhance FcRN binding and improve recirculation to the serum.12

Another example is nanobodies, which have been developed thanks to the discovery of the IgG2 and IgG3 subclasses in camelids, which only have 1 heavy chain in their structure with 1 variable domain and 2 constant domains.

With the advances of genetic engineering, it has been possible to develop variable fragments of very low molecular weight (nanobodies) that can be directed against specific epitopes of different antigens and assembled as modular blocks according to needs.13,14 Nanobody-based therapeutics have the potential to improve efficacy and clinical outcomes by enhancing the stability of the molecule, thereby improving tissue penetration, reducing immunogenicity, and offering alternative administration methods beyond SQ/IV/IM injection.15,16 Small-molecule inhibitors can often be taken orally, providing another therapeutic option for patients for whom injectable medications are less desirable or less available.

Emerging small-molecule inhibitors

Multiple clinical studies are currently being conducted that will give us valuable information about the most effective and safe options to help patients with allergic diseases. Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors disrupt signaling pathways activated by a wide range of cytokines involved in type 2 and non–type 2 inflammation. They have demonstrated efficacy particularly in atopic dermatitis and are being explored in other allergic conditions. However, their broader immunomodulatory effects raise important safety considerations, including infection risk and long-term tolerability.5

Phosphodiesterase-4 (PDE4) inhibitors reduce inflammatory mediator production by increasing intracellular cyclic AMP levels and provide an alternative non-biologic approach for selected patients, particularly those with milder disease or contraindications to biologics. Additional intracellular targets, including spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) and STAT6 degraders, are under investigation and may further expand therapeutic options.17

These oral agents may complement biologic therapies, serve as step-up or step-down options, or provide alternatives for patients who are unwilling or unable to receive injectable treatments. Their integration into treatment algorithms will require careful evaluation of long-term efficacy, safety, and positioning relative to biologics.

Discussion

Comparative efficacy and mechanistic considerations

Biologic therapies targeting IgE and the IL-4/IL-13 axis have demonstrated the broadest efficacy across multiple T2-mediated diseases. IgE-targeted therapy has shown consistent benefit in allergic asthma and chronic spontaneous urticaria, whereas IL-4Rα blockade, by simultaneously inhibiting IL-4 and IL-13 signaling, provides multi-organ efficacy in asthma, atopic dermatitis, and chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps. In contrast, IL-5 and IL-5Rα–targeted therapies exhibit more disease-specific effects, particularly in eosinophilic asthma and selected eosinophilic phenotypes.

Upstream targeting strategies, such as TSLP inhibition and dual-pathway nanobody constructs, represent a promising approach by modulating early immune activation before downstream amplification occurs. These agents may offer broader applicability across heterogeneous disease endotypes, although long-term comparative data remain limited.

The efficacy data summarized in (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3, Table 4) highlight clinically meaningful reductions in exacerbation rates, improvements in disease control scores, and decreased reliance on systemic corticosteroids across multiple allergic conditions. Safety profiles across biologic classes remain favorable, with low rates of serious adverse events and acceptable long-term tolerability. However, variability in magnitude of response between agents and across disease phenotypes reinforces the importance of individualized treatment decisions and cautious interpretation of cross-trial comparisons.

Safety, limitations, and clinical challenges

Overall, biologic therapies demonstrate favorable safety profiles; however, variability in treatment response persists. Major challenges for clinicians include high cost, restricted access, lack of head-to-head comparative trials, and uncertainty regarding optimal patient selection. From a prescriber's perspective, overlapping indications, similar efficacy endpoints, and payer restrictions further complicate decision-making. The presence of partial responders and non-responders underscores the need for improved stratification strategies and predictive tools.

Unmet needs

Despite significant advances in biologic therapies, important unmet needs persist. Patients with T2-low asthma and other non–type 2 inflammatory endotypes derive limited benefit from currently available agents, highlighting the constraints of therapies focused predominantly on classical T2 pathways. In parallel, allergic rhinitis despite its high global prevalence and substantial impact on quality of life remains largely underserved, with few approved targeted therapies and limited long-term safety data, particularly in pediatric populations. Addressing these gaps will require expansion beyond traditional type 2 paradigms and the development of novel strategies that integrate broader inflammatory mechanisms and precision medicine approaches.

Biomarkers and precision medicine

Selecting the right biologic for the right patient remains a central challenge. Current biomarkers, including blood eosinophils, serum IgE, and fractional exhaled nitric oxide, provide incomplete guidance. There is a critical need for validated, disease-specific biomarkers capable of predicting therapeutic response and guiding personalized treatment decisions.

One of the central challenges in the current biologic era is selecting the right therapy for the right patient. Although several biologics target overlapping type 2 pathways and share similar clinical indications, individual responses vary considerably. Current biomarkers such as blood eosinophil counts, total serum IgE levels, and fractional exhaled nitric oxide offer only partial guidance and lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity to reliably predict treatment response across diseases.

This biomarker uncertainty complicates clinical decision-making, often leading to empirical treatment selection, delayed therapeutic optimization, and increased healthcare costs. The development of robust, disease-specific, and treatment-predictive biomarkers integrating immunologic, genetic, transcriptomic, and clinical data is therefore essential to fully realize the promise of precision medicine in allergic diseases.

Future directions and novel drug platforms

Emerging drug platforms aim to optimize efficacy, durability, and patient convenience. Fc engineering strategies, such as YTE modification, extend antibody half-life by enhancing neonatal Fc receptor binding. Nanobody-based therapeutics, derived from camelid single-domain antibodies, offer improved tissue penetration, modular design, and potential alternative routes of administration.

In parallel, small-molecule inhibitors targeting intracellular signaling pathways including Janus kinase (JAK), spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK), and STAT6 degraders represent an important expansion of the therapeutic horizon. These oral agents may complement biologics or provide alternatives for patients unable or unwilling to receive injectable therapies, although safety and long-term risk–benefit profiles require careful evaluation.

Small-molecule inhibitors as an expanding therapeutic horizon

In parallel with biologic therapies, small-molecule inhibitors targeting intracellular signaling pathways represent a rapidly expanding therapeutic frontier in allergic and inflammatory diseases. Unlike monoclonal antibodies, these agents can be administered orally and modulate downstream signaling shared by multiple cytokine receptors, potentially offering broader anti-inflammatory effects.

Conclusion

Targeted biologic therapies and small-molecule inhibitors are transforming the management of allergic diseases by enabling pathway-specific and increasingly personalized interventions. Advances in upstream targeting, molecular engineering, and intracellular inhibition hold promise for addressing persistent unmet needs and improving long-term outcomes. Future research should prioritize biomarker development, head-to-head comparative studies, and long-term safety assessments to support optimal integration of these therapies into clinical practice.
