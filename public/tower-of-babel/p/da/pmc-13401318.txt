Bilateral parietal tRNS improves processing speed on the paced auditory serial addition test (PASAT): behavioral and ERP evidence
Authors: Jiahong Cui, Hongjun Chen, Xiaoshuang Wang, Fengyu Cong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCognitive control is essential for goal-directed behavior and is often impaired in neurological conditions. Transcranial random noise stimulation (tRNS) has shown potential for cognitive enhancement, but its electrophysiological mechanisms remain unclear. This study examined whether bilateral parietal tRNS modulates paced auditory serial addition test (PASAT) performance (reaction time and accuracy) and event-related potential (ERP) indices of cognitive control.MethodsIn a randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled crossover study, 40 healthy adults received active high-frequency tRNS (100–640 Hz, 2 mA, 20 min) or sham stimulation over bilateral posterior parietal regions. PASAT performance (reaction time, accuracy) and EEG were recorded pre- and post-stimulation. ERP analyses focused on N200, P300, and late slow wave (LSW).ResultsActive tRNS significantly reduced reaction times compared to sham, with no stimulation-specific effect on accuracy. Neurophysiologically, active tRNS reduced frontal N200 negativity and increased frontal P300 amplitude. Additionally, reductions in reaction time were significantly associated with increases in frontal P300 amplitude.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that a single session of bilateral parietal tRNS reduced response latency and was associated with changes in frontal ERP markers during a demanding cognitive task. These results provide preliminary electrophysiological evidence that parietal tRNS may influence task-related neural activity expressed at frontal sites, with ERPs serving as potential markers of stimulation-related changes in performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12993-026-00357-7.Keywords: Transcranial random noise stimulation, Posterior parietal cortex, Cognitive control, Paced auditory serial addition test, Event-related potential

Background

Cognitive control is essential for goal-directed behavior, particularly when individuals must resolve competing information and resist distraction [1]. It relies on core executive functions such as selective attention, suppression of irrelevant inputs, and the maintenance and updating of task-relevant information in working memory [2]. These demands are especially pronounced under strict time constraints, which intensify the need for rapid information processing and timely response selection [3]. At the neural level, cognitive control is supported by a distributed frontoparietal network encompassing the dorsolateral and ventrolateral prefrontal cortices, premotor areas, and posterior parietal regions [4]. Deficits in cognitive control are common in neurological and psychiatric conditions, including traumatic brain injury (TBI) and multiple sclerosis (MS), and are linked to poorer functional outcomes and reduced quality of life [5]. Accordingly, there is a growing interest in nonpharmacological approaches that can enhance cognitive control and processing efficiency and help clarify their underlying neural mechanisms.

Transcranial random noise stimulation (tRNS) is a non-invasive brain stimulation technique that delivers alternating current with a broad random frequency spectrum and has been shown to modulate cortical excitability and behavior, particularly at high frequencies (100–640 Hz) [6]. Although its neurophysiological mechanisms remain incompletely understood, two main accounts have been proposed, involving stochastic resonance that amplifies subthreshold neural activity and modulation of membrane excitability via prolonged opening of voltage-gated sodium channels [7, 8]. In line with these proposed mechanisms, electrophysiological evidence suggests that tRNS-induced behavioral effects may be accompanied by measurable changes in neural signal processing [8–10]. Since its initial demonstration over the motor cortex, tRNS has been reported to improve performance across multiple domains, including perception [9], learning [11], and higher cognitive function [12, 13]. Compared with other transcranial electrical stimulation (tES) methods such as transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) and transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS), tRNS is polarity-insensitive, generally well tolerated, and can yield reliable neuromodulatory effects, making it attractive for translational cognitive neuroscience [14–16]. However, despite growing interest in applying tRNS to cognition, its effects on cognitive control appear more variable than those reported in perceptual or motor domains [8, 12, 17]. Recent evidence suggests that the cognitive effects of parietal tRNS may involve network-level modulations of large-scale attentional and control networks, extending beyond local excitability changes, making it a valuable tool for probing distributed neural processes underlying cognitive control [18]. While tDCS and tACS studies have identified the posterior parietal cortex (PPC) as a promising target for modulating cognitive control, reporting benefits for working memory [19, 20], attentional selection [21], and interference control [22], tRNS studies have less frequently targeted parietal regions. This leaves the specific contribution of PPC to tRNS-induced cognitive control effects relatively underexplored [16]. Moreover, many tES studies have relied primarily on behavioral outcomes, often lacking concurrent neurophysiological data, which limits the characterization of underlying neural mechanisms [12, 23, 24]. Thus, further investigation is needed to test the behavioral and neurophysiological effects of tRNS over the PPC on cognitive control.

The Paced Auditory Serial Addition Test (PASAT) is a well-established neuropsychological paradigm that imposes continuous demands on cognitive control by requiring sustained attention, working memory updating, and rapid information processing under time pressure [25]. During the task, digits are presented sequentially and participants must add each new digit to the one presented immediately before it, necessitating the rapid manipulation of information while suppressing proactive interference from earlier responses. Because PASAT performance depends on both temporal constraints and interference resolution, it is highly sensitive to individual differences and clinical impairments in cognitive control and is widely used in both healthy and clinical populations, particularly in TBI and MS [3]. Neuroimaging studies consistently demonstrate that successful PASAT performance engages a bilateral frontoparietal control network, including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and PPC [26, 27].

Electroencephalography (EEG) enables millisecond-level characterization of the temporal dynamics of PASAT-related cognitive control [28, 29]. Several event-related potential (ERP) components are particularly relevant. The N200, a frontal negative deflection, is commonly linked with conflict monitoring and inhibitory control [28, 30]. The P300 reflects attentional resource allocation and working memory updating, and its scalp distribution can shift toward more frontal regions as task demands increase [31, 32]. Additionally, demanding continuous performance tasks like the PASAT often elicit a late slow wave (LSW), recorded over frontal and parietal regions, which is linked to sustained executive engagement and the maintenance of task-relevant representations [33, 34]. Together, these components provide temporally precise markers of distinct processing stages supporting cognitive control during the PASAT.

Although PASAT performance relies strongly on prefrontal control, posterior parietal regions are also task-relevant nodes within the frontoparietal network and contribute to attentional allocation, working memory updating, and task-relevant information processing [4, 19–22]. The bilateral frontoparietal activation reported during PASAT provided further rationale for using bilateral parietal stimulation [26, 27]. To the best of our knowledge, no neuromodulation study has systematically examined whether bilateral parietal tRNS modulates both behavioral performance and ERP measures during the PASAT. Therefore, in this double-blind, sham-controlled, crossover study, we investigated whether bilateral parietal tRNS modulates PASAT performance and ERP components associated with cognitive control. We hypothesized that active parietal tRNS, compared to sham, would (1) improve PASAT performance, (2) modulate the amplitudes of relevant ERP components (N200, P300, LSW), and (3) individual differences in behavioral change would correlate with changes in ERP components. By integrating bilateral parietal tRNS with EEG during the PASAT, this study examined whether stimulation-related ERP changes are associated with behavioral performance, providing preliminary electrophysiological evidence for the effects of parietal stimulation on task-related neural activity.

Methods

Participants

Based on a priori power analysis conducted with G*Power 3.1, a medium effect size (f = 0.25), an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired statistical power of 0.90, a minimum sample size of 30 participants was required. To ensure adequate power and to account for potential data loss, forty healthy students from Dalian University of Technology were recruited for this study (20 males; mean age = 23.39 ± 1.35 years; 20 females; mean age = 23.38 ± 1.33 years). All participants were native Chinese speakers, right-handed (Edinburgh Handedness Inventory [EHI] mean score = 94.59, all ≥ 80) [35], with normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing, and without any history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. Additionally, all participants showed normal Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) and State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-Y) scores, and completed a safety questionnaire assessing contraindications to tRNS. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study was approved by the Biological and Medical Ethics Committee of Dalian University of Technology (approval number: DUTSFL230905–01) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Procedure

The study employed a double-blind, sham-controlled, crossover design, with stimulation condition (active vs. sham) and time (baseline vs. post-stimulation) as within-subject factors (see Fig. 1). We opted for a within-subject design to control for inter-individual differences in current responsiveness and cognitive abilities [36]. Each participant completed two stimulation sessions: one with active tRNS and the other with sham stimulation. The order of the two sessions was randomized and counterbalanced across participants, with at least a three-day interval between sessions to minimize potential carry-over effects of tES [37]. During the experimental phase, all participants performed the PASAT task in two phases: a pre-stimulation baseline phase and a post-stimulation phase. Before the task began, participants sat quietly for approximately 3–5 min to stabilize the EEG recording setup. Stimulation was delivered for 20 min using either active tRNS or sham stimulation via a bilateral parietal montage. Participants did not perform any task during this stimulation period. EEG was continuously recorded throughout both the baseline and post-stimulation phases. After each stimulation session, participants completed a sensation questionnaire to assess blinding efficacy and subjective sensations (e.g., tingling, itching, or discomfort).

Task

The PASAT was administered as the experimental task using E-Prime 3.0 software to record reaction times (RTs) via key press synchronized with oral responses and to trigger EEG recordings (Fig. 2). A fixation point was continuously displayed at the center of the screen while randomized single auditory digits from 1 to 9 were presented through a loudspeaker at 70 dB. Each auditory digit stimulus was presented as a 500 ms sound clip and was followed by a 1500 ms inter-stimulus interval (ISI). In each trial, participants were instructed to maintain fixation and to continuously add each newly presented digit to the preceding one, responding as accurately as possible within the response window. The experiment followed a block design, with each phase (baseline and post-stimulation) consisting of three blocks. Each block included a 60 s task period followed by a 30 s rest period. During each task period, 30 digits were presented, requiring 29 successive responses. Different PASAT versions were used across blocks to minimize learning and repetition effects [38]. Participants were instructed to give oral responses while simultaneously pressing a response key themselves. Response accuracy was manually recorded by the same behavioral recorder based on the oral responses, with responses scored as correct only when the correct sum was provided within the 2 s response window; incorrect, missing, or late responses were treated as incorrect. RT was defined as the latency from digit onset to the participant’s key press, which served as a manual marker synchronized with the verbal response and reduced variability related to speech-onset detection [28, 39, 40]. Before the main task, participants completed practice trials to confirm task understanding and ensure reliable synchronization between verbal and key-press responses. During the experiment, this synchronization was monitored, and ambiguous trials were checked using temporarily stored video recordings.

Experimental task design

Transcranial stimulation protocol

Stimulation was delivered using a DC-Stimulator Plus (neuroConn, Ilmenau, Germany) via two saline-soaked sponge electrodes (5 × 7 cm, 0.9% NaCl) positioned over P3 and P4 according to the international 10–20 system, following previous bilateral parietal tRNS studies [12, 18]. This montage was intended to engage bilateral posterior parietal regions (Fig. 3). In the active tRNS condition, participants received high-frequency tRNS (hf-tRNS; 100–640 Hz) with a 2 mA peak-to-peak amplitude for 20 min. The resulting maximum current density was approximately 0.029 mA/cm2. The electric field distribution was modeled in SimNIBS 4.0.1, showing higher field intensities over bilateral posterior parietal regions (Fig. 3b). In the sham condition, no current was delivered during the 20 min period except for the initial 60 s (with a 30 s ramp-up and -down) to maintain effective blinding. Electrode impedance was maintained below 5 kΩ throughout all sessions. Active and sham stimulation were administered using anonymous pre-programmed codes. Participants and the behavioral/EEG recorder were blinded to stimulation condition, and the stimulation operator was not involved in behavioral or EEG data recording.

EEG recording and processing

EEG was recorded using a 64-electrode Ag/AgCl cap and BrainAmp amplifier system (BrainProducts GmbH, Munich, Germany) according to the extended 10–20 system. The online reference was FCz, and the ground electrode was placed at Fpz. Electrode impedances were maintained below 5 kΩ. The EEG signals were sampled at 1000 Hz and amplified with a band-pass filter of 0.01–100 Hz. Offline preprocessing was performed in EEGLAB v2025.0 in MATLAB 2024b (The MathWorks, Inc.) [41]. All preprocessing steps were performed by an investigator blinded to stimulation condition. The data were re-referenced to the average of the TP9 and TP10 channels as a linked mastoid reference, following previous PASAT ERP research [34], and filtered using a zero-phase FIR band-pass filter (0.5–30 Hz). Noisy channels were identified via manual visual inspection based on abnormal signal patterns (e.g., flat lines, excessive voltage) and were interpolated using a spherical spline method. Epochs were segmented from 200 ms before to 800 ms after auditory digit onset, with digit onset defined as time zero. Baseline correction was performed using the − 200 to 0 ms pre-stimulus interval relative to auditory digit onset. Incorrect trials were excluded. Independent component analysis (ICA) was then used to remove artifacts related to eye movement and muscular activity. ICA components were manually inspected, and one or two components corresponding to vertical or horizontal EOG artifacts were removed from each EEG dataset. Following ICA, trials exceeding ± 200 µV at any electrode were automatically rejected. After rejecting trials, the average number of artifact-free EEG epochs from correct trials per condition was 71 ± 12 (baseline) and 77 ± 8 (post-stimulation) in the active tRNS condition, and 74 ± 11 (baseline) and 78 ± 10 (post-stimulation) in the sham condition.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed significance level of 0.05 was adopted for all tests.

Behavioral analysis

Behavioral performance was assessed using accuracy (percentage of correct responses) and RT. RT analyses were conducted only on correct trials with valid key press responses. Trials with incorrect responses, missing responses, invalid key presses, or RTs shorter than 200 ms were excluded from the RT analysis. Accuracy and RTs were analyzed using a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with stimulation condition (active, sham) and time (baseline, post-stimulation) as within-subject factors in the crossover design, after checking normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (η2p). Post-hoc comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni correction to decompose significant interactions or main effects.

ERP analysis

Electrode clusters were defined based on prior ERP literature and the typical scalp distributions of the components of interest. The frontal cluster included F3, Fz, and F4, and the parietal cluster included P3, Pz, and P4. Consistent with previous literature, the N200 component was analyzed at the frontal electrode cluster [30], whereas P300 and LSW were analyzed at both frontal and parietal clusters. The amplitudes of the N200, P300, and LSW were quantified as the mean voltage within 200–300 ms [30], 280–380 ms [31], and 500–700 ms [33, 34] after stimulus onset, respectively. For each participant, mean amplitudes were computed by averaging across electrodes within each cluster and time window. Two-way repeated-measures ANOVAs were conducted separately for N200, P300, and LSW with stimulation condition (active, sham) and time (baseline, post-stimulation) as within-subject factors. For P300 and LSW, analyses were performed separately for the frontal and parietal clusters. Significant effects were followed by Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons and effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (η2p). To control the risk of Type I error arising from multiple ERP analyses, false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied across the primary stimulation × time interaction tests for the ERP measures.

To account for the crossover design, supplementary analyses examined sequence and period effects and tested for carryover; details are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Correlation analysis

Pearson correlation analyses were performed to examine the relationships between changes (Δ) in RT and the changes in ERP component amplitudes (ΔN200 at the frontal cluster, and ΔP300/ΔLSW at both frontal and parietal clusters) in the active tRNS condition. Accuracy was not included in the correlation analysis because both stimulation conditions (active and sham) showed similar improvements from baseline to post-stimulation, and no significant stimulation-related effects were observed (as detailed in the Results section). All change scores (Δ) were calculated by subtracting baseline values from post-stimulation values. Bonferroni correction was used to adjust for multiple comparisons. Significance was defined as corrected p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic data

All participants completed the experiment without task-related difficulties. Three participants were excluded from the behavioral analyses because no RT data were recorded in one of their sessions, and five participants were excluded from the EEG analyses due to excessive EEG artifacts. Thus, the final sample comprised 37 participants for behavioral analyses (19 females and 18 males, mean age = 23.22 ± 1.11years; active–sham order: n = 19; sham–active order: n = 18), 35 participants for ERP analyses (18 females and 17 males, mean age = 23.49 ± 1.29 years; active–sham order: n = 18; sham–active order: n = 17), and 32 participants for correlation analyses (17 females and 15 males, mean age = 23.31 ± 1.03 years).

Adverse effects and blinding

None of the participants reported significant adverse effects. Perception of stimulation did not significantly differ between the active and sham conditions (see Table 1). In the blinding assessment, 37.5% of participants in the active tRNS condition guessed they received real stimulation, 45.0% guessed placebo, and 17.5% were uncertain. In the sham condition, 45.0% guessed received real stimulation, 32.5% guessed placebo, and 22.5% were uncertain. There was no significant difference in guessing between the sham and active tRNS conditions (χ2 = 1.33, p = 0.51).

P300 (280–380 ms)

In the frontal cluster (F3, Fz, F4), a repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant stimulation × time interaction (F [1, 34] = 6.414, p = 0.016, pFDR = 0.040, η2p = 0.159). The main effects of stimulation (F [1, 34] = 0.423, p = 0.520, η2p = 0.012) and time (F [1, 34] = 0.602, p = 0.443, η2p = 0.017) were not significant. Post-hoc tests showed that P300 amplitudes were significantly more positive in the post-stimulation phase (1.12 ± 0.71 µV) than in the baseline phase (-0.12 ± 0.73 µV) in the active tRNS condition (t [34] = 2.22, corrected p = 0.034, 95% CI: 0.10–2.38, Cohen’s d = 0.37). No significant differences were found in the sham condition or in any other comparisons (all corrected p ≥ 0.100) (Fig. 5). In the parietal cluster (P3, Pz, P4), neither the stimulation × time interaction (F [1, 34] = 1.166, p = 0.288, pFDR = 0.288, η2p = 0.033) nor the main effects of stimulation (F [1, 34] = 0.019, p = 0.893, η2p = 0.001) or time (F [1, 34] = 0.727, p = 0.400, η2p = 0.021) reached significance. Collectively, the P300 effect was observed in the frontal cluster, whereas no significant effects were detected in the parietal cluster.

LSW (500–700 ms)

In the frontal cluster (F3, Fz, F4), the repeated measures ANOVA showed a significant main effect of time (F [1, 34] = 4.284, p = 0.046, η2p = 0.112), but no significant stimulation × time interaction (F [1, 34] = 2.376, p = 0.132, pFDR = 0.220, η2p = 0.065) or main effect of stimulation (F [1, 34] = 0.512, p = 0.479, η2p = 0.015) (Fig. 5). In the parietal cluster (P3, Pz, P4), the ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of time (F [1, 34] = 6.745, p = 0.014, η2p = 0.166), whereas the interaction (F [1, 34] = 1.627, p = 0.211, pFDR = 0.264, η2p = 0.046) and the main effect of stimulation (F [1, 34] = 0.114, p = 0.738, η2p = 0.003) were not significant (Fig. 5).

There was a significant negative correlation between ΔRT and frontal ΔP300 (r = -0.45, corrected p = 0.049) in the active tRNS condition, indicating that participants who showed larger reductions in RT tended to exhibit greater increases in frontal P300 amplitudes (Fig. 6). No other significant correlations were found between ΔRT and ΔERP measures in the active tRNS condition (all corrected p > 0.05).

Discussion

The present study investigated the behavioral and electrophysiological effects of a single session of tRNS over the bilateral posterior parietal regions on cognitive control performance in the PASAT. At the behavioral level, active tRNS was associated with a greater reduction in response latency, without inducing stimulation-specific changes in accuracy. At the neurophysiological level, active tRNS was associated with task-evoked ERP amplitude changes, with the most reliable effects observed for frontal N200 and frontal P300. In addition, stimulation-induced reductions in RT were associated with increases in frontal P300 amplitudes. Taken together, these findings suggest that parietal tRNS may improve response efficiency under high cognitive demand conditions, and that stimulation-related effects may be expressed at functionally relevant sites beyond the stimulation electrodes.

Behaviorally, active tRNS was associated with a greater reduction in RTs during the PASAT, whereas accuracy improved comparably in both active tRNS and sham conditions. Although different PASAT versions were used across blocks to minimize repetition effects, the similar accuracy gains across conditions are consistent with the well-documented sensitivity of the PASAT to repeated task exposure [33]. RT is widely regarded as a sensitive behavioral index of cognitive control efficiency, particularly when accuracy shows limited differentiation across conditions [31, 42]. The observation that RT decreased without a concomitant loss of accuracy argues against a simple speed–accuracy trade-off and supports improved response efficiency [43, 44]. Such efficiency gains are consistent with prior tRNS studies reporting facilitation of response speed or task performance under demanding cognitive tasks [13, 45–47]. Future studies may use individually calibrated PASAT difficulty levels to better detect stimulation-related changes in accuracy [18].

Within the context of the PASAT, which places continuous demands on rapid information processing, working memory updating, and interference control under time pressure [3], the PPC represents a critical node supporting the integration and manipulation of task-relevant information, including numerical operations [48–50]. Importantly, the PPC is not an isolated module but a central hub within the frontoparietal control network, maintaining dense reciprocal connections with prefrontal regions that support top-down attention and executive control [51]. We therefore propose that tRNS delivered using a bilateral parietal montage may have facilitated the efficiency of task-relevant control processes, resulting in faster behavioral responses [52].

At the neurophysiological level, active tRNS was associated with changes in task-evoked ERP amplitudes, with the most reliable stimulation-related effects observed for frontal N200 and frontal P300. These effects may reflect PASAT-related cognitive processing, although broader excitability-modulating effects of active tRNS may also have contributed to the observed amplitude changes. The N200 has been widely associated with conflict monitoring and inhibitory control, especially in tasks requiring rapid response selection under interference [28, 30]. In the PASAT, where participants must continuously suppress interference from previous responses while updating task-relevant information, the observed reduction in N200 negativity after active tRNS may reflect reduced conflict signals or a diminished inhibitory demand, consistent with more efficient control operations during task execution [34, 53]. In addition, active tRNS led to a significant increase in P300 amplitudes at frontal sites, with no corresponding modulation at parietal electrodes. Although the P300 is traditionally associated with stimulus evaluation and context updating, its scalp distribution is known to shift anteriorly as task demands place greater emphasis on executive control rather than perceptual processing [31]. Given the high working memory load and continuous control demands of the PASAT, the frontal P300 enhancement may reflect increased engagement of processes supporting attentional allocation and rapid updating [28]. The absence of parietal P300 modulation may suggest that tRNS influenced control-related processing expressed over frontal sites rather than parietal stimulus-evaluation signatures. By contrast, although LSW amplitudes showed a main effect of time, the stimulation × time interactions for LSW were not significant. Therefore, the LSW findings may reflect general time-related changes and do not provide strong evidence for stimulation-specific modulation of sustained control processes.

Notably, the most reliable electrophysiological effects of active tRNS were expressed over frontal electrode clusters, despite stimulation being delivered using a bilateral parietal stimulation montage. This spatial dissociation is consistent with accounts suggesting that non-invasive brain stimulation can influence distributed, functionally connected control systems rather than producing strictly local effects [18, 54]. As a pivotal hub within frontoparietal control networks, the parietal cortex may modulate the efficiency of information transfer to prefrontal regions that support executive control [4]. Consequently, stimulation-related changes may be expressed at frontal sites where control processes are implemented [18]. This aligns with computational modeling studies showing that tES can induce cascading changes in network dynamics, affecting both locally and in remote but connected cortical areas [55–57]. At the mechanistic level, tRNS has been proposed to enhance neural responsiveness via stochastic resonance or changes in membrane excitability, which could facilitate the integration and transmission of task-relevant information within control networks [8]. However, because tRNS can also induce broader changes in cortical excitability, the present ERP findings cannot fully distinguish component-specific cognitive modulation from a general stimulation-induced excitability shift.

Finally, the correlation analyses provide converging evidence for the behavioral relevance of these neurophysiological changes. Greater reductions in RTs were associated with greater increases in frontal P300 amplitudes in the active tRNS condition, suggesting that individuals who exhibited the greatest behavioral benefits also showed stronger modulation of frontal neural activity related to attentional allocation and context updating [28]. In contrast, RT changes were not reliably associated with frontal N200 or LSW amplitudes, nor with parietal P300 or LSW amplitudes. Collectively, these results are consistent with the view that behaviorally relevant stimulation effects can be expressed in frontal control-related processing dynamics, even when stimulation targets parietal regions [18].

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample consisted of healthy young adults, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to clinical populations with cognitive control impairments. Second, RTs were based on participants’ key presses synchronized with oral responses rather than direct vocal onset detection. Third, only post-stimulation effects of tRNS were examined. Fourth, the relatively large sponge electrodes used for stimulation produced a diffuse electric field with limited spatial focality, and the absence of an alternative stimulation site limits anatomical specificity. Finally, the present ERP analyses may not fully capture broader or more spatially distributed neurophysiological effects of tRNS. Future studies using voice-key or acoustic onset recording, online stimulation protocols, individually calibrated task difficulty, more focal stimulation montages, and source-resolved or multimodal neuroimaging approaches may help clarify the behavioral and neural mechanisms of tRNS effects.

Conclusions

In summary, the present study provides evidence that a single session of bilateral parietal tRNS reduced response latency during the PASAT, suggesting improved response efficiency, and was associated with changes in task-evoked frontal N200 and P300 activity. Although stimulation was delivered over bilateral parietal regions, the most reliable ERP effects were observed over frontal electrode sites, and RT reduction was associated with increased frontal P300 amplitude. Together, these findings suggest that bilateral parietal tRNS may modulate task performance under high cognitive demand, with behaviorally relevant ERP changes expressed over frontal electrode sites.
