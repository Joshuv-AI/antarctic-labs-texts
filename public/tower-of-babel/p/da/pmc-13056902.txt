Knowledge graph–large language model fusion approach for emergency knowledge recommendation in gas tunnels
Authors: Na Xu, Xi Chen, Jinpei Luo, Fenghua An, Liang Wang, Xinyu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTimely and accurate decision-making is critical during gas tunnel emergencies, yet relevant knowledge is typically fragmented across unstructured reports, manuals, and case records, hindering rapid access. To address this challenge, a knowledge recommendation approach was proposed that synergistically combines knowledge graph (KG) and large language model (LLM). This method first constructs a domain-specific KG from heterogeneous emergency documents using prompt-optimized LLMs. During inference stage, it retrieves the most contextually relevant subgraph via semantic similarity-based vector search and re-ranks candidates to enhance precision. This retrieved knowledge dynamically augments the LLM, grounding its responses in verified domain facts and reducing hallucinations. Evaluations show that approach substantially outperforms both standalone LLMs and alternative architectures in accuracy, completeness, and coherence. The method proposed in the study improves the speed and quality of emergency response for gas tunnels, offering a structured and interpretable approach to knowledge-driven decision support in this specific high-risk context.Keywords: Gas tunnel, Emergency response, Large language model (LLM), Knowledge graph (KG), Knowledge managementSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

China is endowed with abundant and unevenly distributed coal and natural gas resources, which often necessitate the construction of tunnels through strata containing gas1. Given the enclosed nature of tunnel environments, construction frequently encounters safety hazards such as gas leaks and explosions, which not only endanger the lives of workers but can also disrupt transportation, leading to economic losses and social impacts. In recent years, significant progress has been made in research on gas tunnel safety management. Regarding risk assessment and control, Wang et al.2 employed a fuzzy Bayesian network to evaluate safety risks during gas tunnel construction; Kang et al.3 developed a safety evaluation system, using a fuzzy hierarchy analysis method to assess the risk level of gas tunnel construction crossing structural coal seams. In terms of safety technology, Cheung et al.4 proposed an integrated real-time construction safety monitoring system for harmful gases in tunnels based on wireless sensor networks and BIM (Building Information Modeling); Lei et al.5 developed a smoke dispersion simulation method based on numerical simulation and real-time monitoring. However, most existing studies primarily focus on accident prevention and monitoring stages, with less attention paid to rapid response mechanisms after incidents occur.

Current emergency response efforts face issues of low efficiency in information acquisition and a lack of systematic emergency knowledge management6. Knowledge graph (KG) can integrate and associate multi-source heterogeneous data, thereby enhancing the accessibility and usability of this knowledge. However, some limitations exist: emergency plans involve multiple departments, various types of disasters, and cross-domain collaboration, with significant differences in content and terminology, increasing the difficulty of knowledge construction7. Existing knowledge retrieval technologies have limited capabilities in processing unstructured data, especially in complex scenarios where traditional methods struggle to provide accurate and timely information support, affecting the effectiveness of emergency responses.

By the end of 2022, the application of ChatGPT, based on large language model (LLM), demonstrated LLMs’ powerful natural language understanding and reasoning abilities in general domains. In professional applications, however, the phenomenon of “hallucination” due to the limitations of model knowledge8 sometimes results in meaningless or inaccurate content generation. To address this, some studies adopted the method of fine-tuning the model9 to improve output quality, although this increases implementation costs and the generation process remains less transparent, lacking interpretability. Currently, integrating KG10 has become a mainstream solution to compensate for these shortcomings. This integration not only facilitates effective knowledge management and utilization but also provides new approaches to solving practical engineering problems.

To address these challenges, this study centers on the following core research question:

How can we design a knowledge recommendation approach that synergistically integrates LLM with KG to generate accurate, complete, and timely emergency response decisions for gas tunnel incidents—while ensuring the decision process is interpretable and the content dynamically adaptive to evolving situational inputs?

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section “Related work” reviews related work on tunnel emergency response, KG construction, and the integration of LLMs with external knowledge. Section “Methodolog” details the proposed KG–LLM approach, including KG construction via prompt-tuned LLMs and knowledge-augmented response generation. Section “Experiment and results” presents the experimental setup, evaluation metrics, and comparative results. Section “Discussion” discusses the implications of the findings, limitations, and future directions. Finally, section “Conclusion” concludes the study and highlights its contributions to emergency knowledge management in high-risk engineering environments.

Related work

Tunnel emergency response

Traditional tunnel emergency measures, such as manual alarm systems, automatic sprinkler fire suppression systems, and ventilation systems, have improved safety to a certain extent. However, as conventional emergency measures, they struggle to meet the increasingly complex and dynamic safety demands of tunnel environments. The rapid development of information technology has provided new tools for tunnel emergency management. For instance, IoT-based emergency management systems have significantly enhanced the accuracy and timeliness of data collection. Barros-Daza et al.11 utilized feedforward artificial neural networks (ANN) to achieve precise classification of fire types, aiding emergency personnel in swiftly taking targeted actions. Jin et al.12 introduced image recognition technology that effectively detects the exact location of fires and triggers alarms instantly, enhancing the speed and accuracy of emergency responses. Wang et al.13 explored the integration of ultra-wideband (UWB) technology and artificial intelligence, providing precise disaster location and predicting fire trends, thus offering scientific support for emergency decision-making.

An integrated decision support system (DSS) not only overcomes the limitations of single technologies but also provides more scientifically sound and reasonable decision recommendations under conditions of incomplete information, further improving the efficiency and effectiveness of emergency responses. The development of multifunctional data models and DSS is particularly critical for addressing these challenges. Wu et al.14 proposed a multi-purpose tunnel data model (MTDM) that integrates building information modeling (BIM) and topological maps, achieving a comprehensive understanding of the internal structure and environment of tunnels, supporting a series of emergency response tasks from fault detection to path planning. Alvear et al.‘s15 DSS, which combines predictive algorithms and dynamic decision models, provides effective decision guidance even with limited information, greatly enhancing the speed and quality of emergency handling.

Beyond purely data-driven or sensor-based systems, there is a well-established line of research employing semantics-driven frameworks—such as ontologies, metamodels, and conceptual representations—to structurally organize emergency knowledge and support scenario-based reasoning. These approaches facilitate formal representation of entities, relationships, and processes in emergency contexts, enabling interoperability, reuse, and systematic reasoning across heterogeneous sources. While such semantics-aware models provide a strong foundational layer for knowledge organization, they often require extensive expert involvement for ontology design and struggle to adapt dynamically to unseen or rapidly evolving incident conditions.

In this context, KG, as the underlying technology of DSS, have become increasingly important. KG can efficiently organize and integrate various emergency-related knowledge resources, such as emergency plans, operating procedures, and historical cases, constructing a structured and easily searchable knowledge system.

Construction of tunnel emergency knowledge graphs

The construction of tunnel emergency knowledge graphs can be broadly categorized into ontology-driven and data-driven approaches. Ontology-driven methods, such as the tunnel fire emergency response ontology (T-FERO) proposed by Bjelland et al.16, rely on formally defined concepts, properties, and axioms to create a structured schema of the emergency domain. These approaches benefit from clear semantic consistency and logical inferencing capabilities, making them suitable for standardization and interoperability in multi-agency settings. However, they often demand significant expert knowledge for ontology development and maintenance, and may lack flexibility when integrating new, unstructured data sources or adapting to atypical emergency scenarios. In contrast, data-driven methods aim to extract entities and relations directly from textual corpora using rule-based, statistical, or deep learning techniques, offering greater automation and scalability at the cost of potential noise and semantic ambiguity. Several scholars have conducted research on the construction of KGs in the field of tunnel emergencies, reflecting these two complementary paradigms. For example, Xu et al.17 used a rule-based Chinese natural language processing (C-NLP) method to automatically extract domain knowledge entities and their relationships from large amounts of unstructured text. This method reduces human intervention and improves extraction efficiency. However, rule-based methods also have limitations, such as poor generalization ability and difficulty in handling complex language expressions and contextual changes.

In recent years, deep learning techniques have made significant progress in natural language processing and have been widely applied to the construction of KGs. Hu et al.18 proposed an entity extraction method based on the BERT-BiLSTM-MUL-CRF model, effectively addressing the issues of nested and discontinuous entities in text. Despite this, deep learning methods still face challenges such as high demand for training data and poor model interpretability.

Graph neural networks (GNNs), a type of deep learning model specifically designed for graph data, have started to be applied to the construction of KGs. Wang et al.19 proposed a knowledge extraction method based on graph attention networks (GAT), which enhances the recognition capability of target entities and their relationships by assigning weights. However, GNN models have high computational complexity and require substantial hardware resources, limiting their application in large-scale data scenarios.

To fully leverage the strengths of various methods, a hybrid approach is becoming a trend. Mu et al.20 constructed an emergency resource KG by categorizing and analyzing emergency measures, defining eight interrelated entity types, and designing extraction schemes for entities and relationships. This study also developed a template-based question-answering system for querying and displaying information in the KG. Hybrid methods can, to some extent, overcome the limitations of single methods, improving the quality and application effects of KG construction.

This study proposes a KG–LLM co-driven approach that fundamentally advances prior work in three ways: (1) it replaces static ontologies16 and fixed extraction rules17 with prompt-optimized LLMs to dynamically induce KGs from raw text, enabling rapid adaptation without manual re-engineering; (2) it avoids black-box fine-tuning9 by keeping the LLM frozen and augmenting it with retrievable, traceable subgraphs—ensuring explainable and updatable recommendations; and (3) it integrates knowledge construction and decision generation into a closed-loop emergency response pipeline, unlike modular DSS architectures that decouple sensing, reasoning, and action14,15.

Methodology

Framework of method

The proposed emergency knowledge approach integrates two synergistic processes: (1) the automated construction of a domain-specific KG using LLM, and (2) the retrieval-augmented generation of actionable recommendations by feeding relevant subgraphs back into the LLM. Large Language Model-Driven Knowledge Graph Building.

Large Language Model-Driven Knowledge Graph Building.

LLM-driven KG building is an efficient and automated process for systematically integrating knowledge scattered across multi-source heterogeneous data. By applying prompt tuning to segmented documents, the LLM extracts associated entities and relationships from document fragments, forming triplets. These triplets are then weighted and connected to nodes, ultimately transforming into a comprehensive KG that reflects the knowledge system of the documents.

(2)Knowledge Graph-Driven Enhancement of Large Language Models.

Knowledge Graph-Driven Enhancement of Large Language Models.

The aim of enhancing LLM through KG-driven methods is to improve the model’s understanding capabilities by introducing knowledge subgraphs relevant to user queries, thereby reducing the risk of generating inaccurate information. To enhance the efficiency of knowledge retrieval, the entities and relationships within the KG are converted into vector form. Vector retrieval techniques are then used to quickly find the most relevant knowledge subgraphs to the user’s query, serving as external knowledge to augment the LLM. This approach provides high-quality, targeted knowledge decision support.

Prompt tuning-based knowledge graph extraction method

Traditionally, KG construction from text relies on rule-based systems or statistical NLP models, which require extensive feature engineering or large annotated corpora. While effective in constrained domains, these methods often struggle with generalization across diverse document types and evolving terminology. In contrast, LLMs possess strong few-shot learning and contextual reasoning capabilities. By employing prompt tuning we can leverage these capabilities to extract structured triples from heterogeneous emergency documents. This approach reduces reliance on manually crafted rules or large labeled datasets, adapts more readily to varied writing styles present in guidelines, reports, and cases.

Prompt tuning does not alter the model’s parameters; instead, it activates the model’s inherent logical reasoning and generalization capabilities through prompts. Compared to full fine-tuning, prompt tuning reduces computational overhead and avoids catastrophic forgetting, while enabling deterministic control over output structure—critical for generating consistent triplets21. To better align with the structured decision-making process required in gas tunnel emergencies, this work adopts a stepwise prompt template inspired by chain-of-thought prompting22. To further harness the data generation capabilities of large models, a one-shot prompting approach is adopted, which enhances model performance by including task examples.

The prompt design follows a structured chain-of-thought paradigm, comprising four interdependent elements: (1) a clear task description to define the extraction objective; (2) stepwise reasoning instructions to decompose complex relation identification; (3) explicit output JSON schema rules to ensure machine-readability23; and (4) a one-shot exemplar to anchor the model’s understanding of domain-specific entity types. This composition balances flexibility with constraint, as shown in Fig. 3. To better organize the input section, separators are used within the prompts to distinguish the rule texts to be processed from other parts, effectively preventing conflicts between prompts.

Entity extraction and vector retrieval

To bridge natural language queries with structured knowledge, the system first identifies salient entities in the user’s query, which improves retrieval precision by aligning query intents with node identifiers in the KG.

The selection of an appropriate semantic similarity measure is crucial for effectively matching user queries with relevant subgraphs within the KG. While a rich design space exists—including information content–based methods, graph-based similarity, and purely embedding-based approaches. Considering the choice must align with the system’s operational requirements and knowledge representation, in the context of gas tunnel emergency response, where knowledge is extracted from heterogeneous texts and structured into a KG primarily for real-time retrieval, vector-based similarity offers a pragmatic balance. It provides computational efficiency for rapid response, seamless integration with pre-trained language model embeddings, and the ability to capture nuanced semantic relationships beyond literal keyword matching. Although measures like cosine similarity in embedding space may not fully capture the intricate relational or hierarchical structures that graph- or ontology-based similarities could, they are well-suited for the initial high-recall retrieval phase in our pipeline, where the primary goal is to quickly surface a broad set of contextually relevant triplets.

Vector retrieval is used to find vectors most similar to a given query vector within a large set of vectors. In the KG, entities and relationships are encoded into high-dimensional vectors containing semantic features. Using vector retrieval can quickly identify the subgraph of the KG most relevant to the user’s query. Experiment and results

This section presents the experimental setup, results, and analysis of the proposed gas tunnel emergency response decision support system. The experiments are designed to evaluate the performance of the method in terms of both quantitative and qualitative metrics, comparing it with baseline models to demonstrate its effectiveness.

Experimental environment and parameter settings

Dataset

The dataset consists of three sources: (1) web-scraped open data (81%, ~ 16,470 characters), including public incident reports, safety guidelines, and technical bulletins from government and industry websites; (2) peer-reviewed literature on tunnel emergencies (7%, ~ 1423 characters), covering journal articles and conference proceedings; and (3) internal operational documents (12%, ~ 2441 characters) provided by the National Emergency Management Agency and two provincial tunnel safety centers, which include anonymized post-incident analysis reports, standard operating procedures (SOPs), and drill records from 2019 to 2023.

Model and parameter

The Qwen-14B LLM, open-sourced by Alibaba, is selected as the base model. This model is responsible for information extraction from documents, keyword extraction, and generating final answers. The model is deployed via the Ollama platform and runs in a local environment.

Parameter tuning plays a crucial role in balancing the quality and consistency of the generated content. Two key parameters, top_p and temperature, are configured based on the requirements of gas tunnel emergency response tasks:

top_p: Controls the probability threshold for nucleus sampling, with values ranging from 0 to 1. A higher top_p value increases the diversity of the generated text.

temperature: Regulates the randomness and creativity of the output, with values ranging from 0 to 2. Higher values lead to more unpredictable outputs.

Given the critical nature of gas tunnel emergency response, ensuring consistency and accuracy is paramount. Therefore, top_p is set to a relatively high value of 0.8 to maintain diversity while ensuring relevance, and temperature is set to a low value of 0.05 to prioritize reliability and precision over randomness.

Evaluation indicators

Quantitative evaluation

To quantitatively assess the similarity between the generated decisions and the actual case decisions, traditional NLP metrics such as BLEU and ROUGE scores are used to evaluate lexical similarity. These indicators have already appeared in many NLP related literature24–26 and will not be further elaborated.

Considering that the generated and actual decisions may be semantically similar but differ in expression, an additional evaluation metric, Cosine Similarity (CS), is added to assess semantic vector similarity. This choice aligns with our retrieval mechanism and provides a consistent metric for comparing semantic alignment across models. While other similarity measures could be applied in different settings, CS is appropriate here because it directly reflects the vector-space semantics used in our KG encoding and retrieval module. The evaluation is conducted through a questionnaire distributed to 10 users, consisting of 50% industry practitioners and 50% researchers. The weighting scheme for the qualitative evaluation was established based on two principles: (1) prioritizing the critical dimensions of decision quality in emergency response, as underscored in safety management literature, and (2) consensus feedback from our domain-expert collaborators during the pilot testing phase. This scheme reflects the higher importance assigned to the correctness and thoroughness of recommendations over sheer speed in the targeted high-stakes scenario.

Result analysis

Based on the evaluation results, the proposed method performs exceptionally well across all selected evaluation metrics, significantly outperforming the native model. While the method scores highly in accuracy, completeness, and coherence, it slightly lags behind direct generation models in response speed. Specific analyses are as follows:

Accuracy and Completeness.

Accuracy and Completeness.

The proposed method achieves significantly higher qualitative scores in accuracy and completeness compared to other baseline models. This indicates that by using a KG as external augmentation, the model can more accurately understand the context of the questions and provide comprehensive information. In contrast, Qwen-14B and GPT-4o score lower in these areas, suggesting that they are more prone to information omission or inaccuracies when dealing with complex problems in gas tunnel emergency response.

(2)Coherence.

Coherence.

In terms of coherence, the proposed method also excels, indicating that the system generates responses with strong logical flow and ease of understanding. Compared to native models, the generated content from the proposed method is less likely to suffer from logical breaks or information jumps, which is crucial in emergency response scenarios where precise and coherent information is essential.

(3)Response Speed.

Response Speed.

Regarding response speed, the proposed method takes longer to generate content due to the additional step of knowledge retrieval. However, this increased processing time is acceptable in emergency response scenarios where highly reliable and specialized information is required. The trade-off between speed and the quality of information is justified in such critical applications.

(4)Evaluator background differences.

Evaluator background differences.

A comparative analysis of the scores from industry practitioners and academic researchers reveals noteworthy alignments and slight variations. Both groups rated the proposed method highest in Accuracy and Completeness, confirming its core strength in providing factually correct and comprehensive information. However, practitioners assigned marginally higher scores for Accuracy, likely reflecting their prioritization of actionable, protocol-aligned steps. Conversely, researchers gave slightly higher scores for Coherence, possibly due to a greater emphasis on logical narrative flow and structural consistency. The close agreement on the overall weighted sum indicates that the proposed KG–LLM framework effectively meets the essential criteria for reliable decision-support from both practical and academic viewpoints.

(5)Automatic Evaluation Metrics.

Automatic Evaluation Metrics.

Automatic evaluation metrics such as BLEU and ROUGE measure the n-gram overlap between the system-generated text and the actual reference decisions. The proposed method achieves the highest scores in these metrics compared to the baselines, showing substantial improvements over the native model. This indicates that the generated answers are highly consistent with actual case decisions and contain a significant amount of correct information. Particularly, the performance on ROUGE-L and CS metrics further validates the method’s superiority in content quality and semantic consistency.

(6)Impact of Base Model Replacement.

Impact of Base Model Replacement.

When using ChatGLM-6B and Llama2-7B as base models, the overall scores and performance are comparable to those of the model used in this study, demonstrating good business performance. This suggests that the proposed approach has flexibility and can adapt to different base models while maintaining high output quality. This flexibility is a significant advantage, as it allows the method to be applied in various contexts and with different language models.

While the primary evaluation focused on the final recommendation quality, we conducted a preliminary analysis of the upstream effects of the base model. Although the core extraction pipeline was developed with Qwen-14B, we observed variations in the initial knowledge graph construction when using ChatGLM-6B and Llama2-7B for the same prompt-tuned extraction task on a subset of documents. ChatGLM-6B, being optimized for Chinese, showed a slight advantage in recognizing domain-specific entity names and technical terms present in Chinese guidelines. In contrast, Llama2-7B occasionally generated triplets with more generic relationship labels. However, these variations in the extracted knowledge base did not proportionally translate to significant degradation in final recommendation scores, as evidenced by the competitive performance of the alternatives in Table 3. We attribute this robustness in the downstream task to our retrieval and re-ranking module, which acts as a filter by selecting the most relevant sub-graphs from the available KG, thereby mitigating inconsistencies introduced during the initial extraction phase. This suggests that while the choice of LLM influences the granularity and style of the constructed knowledge, our overall framework possesses a degree of resilience to such variations, provided the model possesses sufficient fundamental capability.

In summary, the graph-model-driven gas tunnel emergency response decision support system developed in this study is capable of covering multiple critical stages in the emergency response process. The system’s responses are more aligned with real-case decision recommendations compared to native models and general large models in quantitative evaluations. In qualitative assessments, the system scores highly in accuracy, completeness, and coherence, indicating its ability to rapidly provide accurate and detailed decision information to decision-makers.

Comparison of content generated by different models.

While baseline models—including the native Qwen-14B and even GPT-4o—struggle to deliver actionable guidance in gas tunnel emergencies, the proposed approach generates precise, context-aware recommendations grounded in domain expertise. Specifically, Qwen-14B, lacking specialized knowledge, often produces generic or incomplete advice such as “evacuate immediately” without addressing critical ventilation controls. GPT-4o exhibits improved coherence but still omits essential technical details due to its general-purpose training. In contrast, when augmented with KG, smaller models like ChatGLM-6B and Llama2-7B achieve substantially higher ROUGE and CS scores, demonstrating that external domain knowledge can effectively compensate for model size limitations.

Further more, subgraphs encoding historical incident protocols and operational guidelines and injects them into the LLM’s context. This enables the system to move beyond vague instructions like “assess the situation” or “activate emergency plans” and instead recommend high-priority, technically specific actions such as “avoid airflow reversal” and “direct high-concentration gas to the main return airway via the shortest path.” The resulting recommendations align closely with real-world emergency workflows, achieving the highest human-rated accuracy (4.75/5) and semantic alignment (CS = 0.83). These results confirm that precise subgraph selection, coupled with KG-augmented generation, is key to delivering reliable, scalable decision support in high-stakes tunnel emergency scenarios.

Limitations and future work

Despite the promising results, the study has several limitations:

Data Limitations: The KG in this study was constructed using a bespoke dataset. While test set encompasses the most common emergency scenarios—such as gas outbursts, fires, and ventilation failures—it may underrepresent rare yet high-consequence events. This data limitation not only challenges independent validation but also constrains the method’s generalizability across diverse geological settings or regulatory environments with distinct safety protocols. Future work will focus on expanding the dataset through broader inter-agency collaboration, thereby enhancing scenario diversity, model robustness, and real-world applicability.

Conclusion

This study proposes a novel knowledge recommendation approach for gas tunnel emergency response, synergizing KG and LLM to address critical gaps in dynamic decision support. By integrating prompt-tuned LLMs for automated KG construction and subgraph-augmented retrieval for LLM enhancement, the framework achieves efficient extraction of structured knowledge from heterogeneous data while suppressing domain-specific hallucinations. Experimental results demonstrate significant improvements over baseline models, with a 0.83 CS score, indicating strong alignment with real-case decisions. By comparing the generated examples, the ability of this method to generate context aware suggestions highlights its practicality for emergency response in high-risk environments.

Despite these advancements, challenges remain in data diversity, real-time performance, and user-centric interpretability. Future efforts will focus on integrating multimodal data to enrich the KG’s coverage and deploying lightweight models for edge computing. By bridging structured domain knowledge with LLMs’ semantic reasoning, this work advances gas tunnel safety management through a transparent, knowledge-augmented decision support framework.
