Calcium signaling in human and mouse microglia exhibit differential susceptibility to phytocannabinoids
Authors: Ivana Pilchova, Andrea Fleig, Reinhold Penner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeurological disorders affect over 40% of the global population and are driven in part by microglia-mediated neuroinflammation that depends on calcium (Ca²⁺) signaling. Cannabis-derived compounds (CBx) modulate microglial activation and cytokine release, however, the impact of understudied CBx on Ca2+ signaling pathways controlling inflammatory responses remains largely unknown.MethodsHere, we systematically examined the effects of over 22 CBx on key microglial Ca2+ signaling pathways. Using pharmacological modulators, live-cell Ca2+ imaging, immunofluorescence, and cytokine and nitric oxide assays, we characterized store-operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE) and purinergic signaling dynamics, inflammatory responses, and CBx effects in human (HMC3) and mouse (BV2) microglia under resting and activated conditions.ResultsWe found that microglial SOCE in both mouse and human cell line models were potently inhibited by the same three, minor, acidic CBx – CBGA, CBGVA, CBDVA. In BV2, at least seven CBx (CBD, CBG, CBDVA, CBDA, CBGA, CBDV, CBNM) inhibited LPS-induced proinflammatory secretion of nitric oxide (NO) and TNF-α. Despite the profound SOCE inhibition in HMC3, CBx failed to inhibit downstream proinflammatory cytokine release in TNF-α - or IL-1β-activated cells. We found major differences in Ca2+ signaling between the models, including purinergic pathways, where HMC3 cells appear to express a more limited purinome with more subdued signaling responses. Purinergic Ca2+ responses to ATP in BV2, especially the delayed phase, was suppressed by at least eight CBx, and most prominently by CBDVA, CBGVA and CBGA. We observed partial, indirect involvement of P2X4, P2 X7, and P2Y13 purinoceptors and propose additional Ca2+ signaling targets mediating the anti-inflammatory properties of CBx. Additionally, we documented the pro-inflammatory potential of CBCA and CBNA that is likely facilitated by their ability to mobilize intracellular Ca2+ levels in both, human and mouse microglia.ConclusionsThese findings provide a comprehensive qualitative and quantitative assessment of how individual CBx influence main Ca2+ signaling pathways in microglia and identify novel anti-inflammatory candidates with therapeutic potential for targeting microglial activation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03031-7.Keywords: Microglia, Calcium signaling, Cannabinoids, Neuroinflammation, Purinergic receptors, Store-operated calcium entry

Background

As of 2021, nearly 43% of the global population – over 3.4 billion people – were affected by neurological conditions [1]. Neuroinflammation is an important determinant of neurodegeneration linking infection, acute injury and chronic disease – but its mechanisms, especially the involvement of calcium (Ca2+) signaling, remain insufficiently understood.

In microglia, the brain’s resident immune cells, Ca2+ signaling is crucial for the regulation of homeostasis, immune responses, and neuroinflammation. Microglial Ca2+ signaling represents a robust system of intertwined pathways including (1) store-operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE) (2) receptor-operated Ca2+ entry (ROCE) (3), Ca2+ release from intracellular stores and organelles (4), transient receptor potential (TRP) channels (5), voltage-gated Ca2+ channels (VGCCs), and (6) intercellular microglial communication through Ca2+ waves [2, 3].

SOCE plays a principal role in sustaining the pool of intracellular Ca2+ by facilitating the transfer of Ca2+ ions through plasma membrane in response to depletion of Ca2+ in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). This process is triggered by activation of luminal Ca2+-sensing stromal interaction molecules (STIM) localized in membrane of the ER and binding to Calcium Release-Activated Calcium CRAC channels (ORAI) in the plasma membrane, leading to their opening and enabling sustained influx of Ca2+. Activation of SOCE in microglia results in increase of intracellular Ca2+ levels, which modulates microglial activation, polarization, migration, phagocytosis, cytokine release and overall determines how microglia respond to pathological conditions. All of these cellular responses are critical in neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration [4, 5]. Dysregulation of SOCE in microglia is associated with either excessive inflammation or impaired immune responses, highlighting its targeting as a potential therapeutic strategy for broad range of ailments that share neuroinflammation as a cause or symptom. Elevated intracellular Ca2+ levels activates the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α) and reactive oxygen species (ROS), further enhancing the immunological response [6].

Chronic activation of microglia sustains neuroinflammation that contributes to development of neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer’s (AD), Parkinson’s, Huntington’s disease, ALS or epilepsy [7, 8]. SOCE is involved in formation of the inflammasome and contributes to neurovascular dysfunction in the inflamed AD brain, LPS induced neuronal damage and Aβ formation [9, 10]. Genetic downregulation of SOCE was shown to attenuate the microglial pro-inflammatory and pro-algesic signaling, thereby alleviating neuropathic pain [11]. Conversely, negatively dysregulated Ca2+ signaling can also lead to microglial dysfunction, impairing their efficacy in clearance of debris, promoting the accumulation of inflammatory mediators, neurotoxicity, and neuronal damage. As an example, the loss of TREM2 function impairs microglial autophagy and contributes to AD development. The absence of TREM2 negatively affects SOCE function in mouse microglia, highlighting SOCE as possible therapeutic target for AD treatment [12].

Phytocannabinoids (CBx) are bioactive compounds unique to cannabis, a versatile plant with a long medicinal history with increasing biomedical research interest, with significant potential for therapeutic use. Cannabis contains over 560 distinct chemical compounds, including 150 CBx, along with flavonoids, terpenes, phenols, alkaloids and other phytochemicals [13–16]. Each Cannabis strain represents a chemically diverse mixture of phytochemicals and their interactions often translate to biological responses with combinatorial entourage effects. This effect diversity depends on the plant’s specific CBx, flavonoid, and terpene composition. Investigations into the entourage effect targeting specifically microglia are limited. However, several studies provide evidence of how combinations of cannabis-derived chemicals, such as whole plant extracts, CBD/THC mixtures or enrichment with terpenes such as β-caryophyllene, modulate microglial activity and inflammatory responses [16, 17].

The human endocannabinoid system was named after cannabis-derived chemicals based on the chemical structure similarity of human endocannabinoids - anandamide and 2-arachidonoylglycerol, and the ability of phytochemicals to interact with CB1 and CB2 receptors of the endocannabinoid system [18, 19]. Additionally, CBx interfere with a broad range of receptors including TRP channels (TRPV1, TRPA1, TRPM8), nuclear PPAR receptors (PPAR-γ), G protein-coupled receptors (GPR5, GPR18, GPR119), opioid receptors (µ, δ), adenosine receptors (A2A) or serotonin / dopamine receptors (5-HT1A), and they can modulate major intracellular signaling pathways, including NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, MAPK, JAK/STAT pathways or the NLRP3 inflammasome in immune cells, including microglia [16, 17, 20–26].

Currently, there are no peer-reviewed studies specifically focused on the effects of CBx on store-operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE) in microglia. We previously have shown that certain acidic CBx, such as CBGA, are potent SOCE inhibitors in other immune cell types, like lymphocytes and mast cells. This inhibition leads to suppression of proinflammatory cytokine release, indicating their potential anti-inflammatory properties [27].

Cannabis has been used for the treatment of inflammation for millenia, but its use in modern medicine has been limited by the lack of scientific knowledge. Cannabis extracts have been reported to inhibit inflammatory responses in vitro, in pre-clinical and clinical studies [28, 29]. Up to date, more than 40 clinical studies focused on cannabis in treatment of inflammation and have shown reduced systemic inflammatory status in patients with HIV [30], thumb basal joint arthritis [31] or Crohn’s disease [32]. A review by Anil et al., 2022, the authors emphasize that in order to utilize the full potential of cannabis it is important to define the cellular and molecular mechanisms that underlie the anti-inflammatory activity of bioactive molecules [33]. Neuroinflammation, which is predominantly triggered by microglia, has been reported to be suppressed by several cannabis-derived extracts and isolated compounds, e.g., CBD, THC, and CBDV, and a few studies have reported inhibitory effects of grossamide, hemp protein hydrolysates (HPH), or coumaroylaminobutanol glucopyranoside (CLG), all bioactive molecules derived from hemp seeds [34–41].

Since microglia rely heavily on Ca2+ signaling to activate the inflammatory response and several CBx have been reported to modulate Ca2+ channels and inhibit inflammation, we hypothesized that individual CBx might inhibit SOCE in microglial cells, leading to suppression of Ca2+-dependent pro-inflammatory signaling pathways and thereby contributing to an anti-inflammatory response [23, 42, 43]. Additionally, this study provides a comprehensive comparison of a broad panel of individual CBx and their effect on main Ca2+ signaling pathways in two widely used human and mouse microglial cell models.

Methods

This comparative profiling study aimed to systematically assess the effects of multiple individual phytocannabinoids on microglial calcium signaling using a human and mouse microglial cell models.

Cell culture

BV2 mouse microglial cell culture (RRID: CVCL_0182) was kindly provided by Dr. Stefan Feske (Department of Pathology, NYU Grossman School of Medicine, US). Cells were maintained in RPMI Medium 1640 supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine (Corning, 10-040-CV) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco #10082147) and passaged with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich, T4049).

The human microglial cell line HMC-3 (RRID: CVCL_II76) derived from the fetal brain was obtained from ATCC®, USA and kept in EMEM containing 2 mM L-glutamine (ATCC, 30-2003 or Corning, 10-009-CV) and 10% FBS. Cells were passaged with Accutase (Sigma-Aldrich, A6964).

The wild type of human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293 was obtained from Dr. Andrew Scharenberg’s laboratory (Department of Immunology, University of Washington, Seattle, USA) and kept in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Corning, 10-014-CV) containing 10% FBS.

The HEK293 stably overexpressing human His-tagged P2X4 channels was kind gift from Dr. Philippe Séquéla (Department of Neurology & Neurosurgery at McGill University, Quebec, Canada). Cells were kept in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Corning, 10-014-CV): F12 (Corning, 10-025-CV) (1:1) containing 10% FBS and supplemented with G-418 (0.25 mg/ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10131035) for selection.

The HEK293 cell line that stably expresses the human P2X7 receptor channels was generously provided by Dr. Francesco di Virgilio (Department of Medical Sciences, University of Ferrara, Italy). Cells were grown in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, 11995-040) supplemented with 10% FBS and G-418 (0.2 mg/mL).

Cell cultures were grown in 75 cm2 flasks (VWR #BD353136) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Experiments were performed on cells with passage numbers lower than 35.

Cell viability

Cell viability and metabolic activity was determined using a colorimetric MTT assay. Cells were seeded in complete medium in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere before the start of the experiment. Cell culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the tested CBx and stimulus for microglial activation (LPS, TNF-α, IL-1β). After 24 h of incubation, a 10x concentrated solution of Thiazolyl Blue tetrazolium bromide in PBS (Alfa Aesar, L11939) was introduced to reach a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL and incubated for 4 h to allow metabolic conversion of MTT to formazan. Formazan crystals were dissolved by adding 10% SDS to each well and the absorbance at 570 nm was read on a multimode plate reader (Victor Nivo, PerkinElmer, USA).

Nitric oxide production

A Griess assay was used for the evaluation of microglial NO production determined as levels of nitrite released into culture media. BV2 cells were seeded in tissue culture treated 24-well plates (VWR, #82050-894) at a density 1.2 × 105/well and grown overnight. The next day, cells were washed twice with serum-free medium and treated for 1 h in serum-free medium containing LPS (250 ng/ml, Thermo Scientific, #00-4976-03) ± solvent or tested compounds. After 1 h, serum was reintroduced to the final concentration of 10% and cells were incubated for another 23 h (24 h in total).

The next day, cultured medium was transferred in duplicates to a 96-well plate (VWR, #29442-056) and the Griess assay (Promega, #G2930) was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 50 µL of cultured medium was incubated with 50 µL of sulfanilamide and 50 µL N-1-naphthylethylenediamine dihydrochloride (NED) under acidic (phosphoric acid) conditions. The standard curve was generated from 0.1 M sodium nitrite diluted in complete medium. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm on a Benchmark Plus spectrophotometer (BioRad, USA).

Fluorescent live-cell Ca2+ imaging assay

Cells were seeded at the density 3 × 104/well in black 96-well optical plates with flat clear bottom (Greiner, #655090) coated with poly-L-lysine and let to adhere. Microglia were activated by incubation with LPS (250 ng/mL), TNF-α (10 ng/mL) or IL-1β (1 ng/mL) for 24 h. The following day, medium was replaced and cells were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with Fura-2 AM loading medium (complete medium supplemented with 2 µM Fura-2 AM (Thermo Fisher, F1221), 2.5 mM Probenecid (Biotium, #50027), and 1x PowerLoad (Thermo Fisher, P10020)). The plates were washed 3 times with the Assay buffer (mM: 140 NaCl, 2.8 KCl, 1 CaCl2, 2 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 11 glucose, pH 7.2, 300 mOsm) and real-time measurements of intracellular Ca2+ [Ca2+]i were acquired in a high-throughput kinetic plate reader instrument (Hamamatsu FDSS-7000EX, Hamamatsu Photonics KK, Japan). The Fura-2 indicator was excited at 340 and 380 nm wavelengths, and the emitted signal was detected at 540 nm.

The standard SOCE protocol had a sampling rate of 1 Hz and included a 1 min baseline recording followed by the application and 3 min preincubation of the compounds tested. SOCE was stimulated by the addition of thapsigargin (Tg, 2 µM, Molecular Probes, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #T7459) at 240 s. For the removal of external Ca2+, EGTA (2 mM) was added at 600 s. Each plate layout included control wells with SOCE inhibition induced by addition of gadolinium (Gd3+, 2 µM at 60 s) or EGTA (2 mM at 60 s), as well as solvent control relevant to the tested compounds and a control well without thapsigargin.

The purinergic signaling protocol was recorded at a sampling rate of 0.5 Hz. The baseline was recorded for 2 min, followed by application of the tested compounds and 3 min of preincubation. Purinoreceptors were stimulated by the addition of individual nucleotides – ATP (Sigma-Aldrich, A2383), ATPγS (A1388), ADP (A2754), AMP (A1752), UTP (U6625), UDP (U4125), UMP (U6375), UDPG (U4625), BzATP (TargetMol, TP2226) at 300 s.

The ratio 340 / 380 nm was plotted over time to represent changes in [Ca2+]i. Correction for the baseline Ca2+ signal or normalization to the untreated control was applied as indicated.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

HMC3 cells were activated with TNF-α (10 ng/mL) or IL-1β (1 ng/mL) for 24 h. The total RNA was isolated from cell pellet containing 5 × 106 PBS-washed cells using TRI reagent (#T9424, Sigma) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After quantification, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA with Superscript™ III First-Strand Synthesis System (#18080051, Fisher Scientific). The cDNA template (20 ng per 10 µL reaction) was used in SYBR green-based qRT-PCR with primers targeting the ACTB (QT00095431), GAPDH (QT00079247), ORAI1 (QT00202587), ORAI2 (QT00215229), ORAI3 (QT00231910), STIM1 (QT00083538) and STIM2 (QT00023744) genes (QuantiTect® primer assay, Qiagen). Cycling conditions involved 15 min of 95 °C denaturation followed by 40 cycles of 15 s / 94 °C, 30 s / 55 °C, 30 s / 72 °C and finishing with melting curve analysis of amplified products. The cycle threshold (Ct) was determined for each sample in triplicate. The target gene expression was calculated as the relative expression of the housekeeping genes ACTB and GAPDH using the 2(−∆∆Ct) method. Measurements were performed in the Applied Biosystems 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR System at the Shared Resource of Genomics, Cancer Center, University of Hawaii.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

HMC3 cells were seeded in tissue culture treated 48-well plates at the density 4 × 104 cells / well and grown for 24 h. The next day, cells were washed twice with serum-free medium and treated for 1 h in serum-free medium containing IL-1β (1 ng/mL, PeproTech, #200-01B) ± solvent or tested compounds (at 5 µM). After 1 h, serum was reintroduced to the final concentration of 10% and cells were incubated for another 23 h (24 h in total). Levels of IL-6 and IL-8 cytokines secreted in culture medium were determined using commercially available pre-coated ELISA kits (PeproTech, BioGems IL-6 kit BGK05231and IL-8 kit BGK10145) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The samples of supernatants were aliquoted, frozen and stored at -80 °C. All samples were assayed in parallel. Absorbances were measured at 450 nm on a Benchmark Plus spectrophotometer (BioRad).

Cytokine array

Cytokine profiling was determined using a human cytokine antibody array kit (Proteome profiler human XL cytokine array kit, ARY022B, R&D Systems). HMC3 cells were seeded in a 6-well tissue culture-treated plate (VWR, #29442-036) with a density 2 × 105 / well and allowed to adhere. To remove serum, cells were washed two times with PBS and exposed to 5 min of preincubation with CBGA (5 µM) or solvent (DMSO, 0.1%). Microglial activation was evoked with the addition of IL-1β (1 ng/mL) or TNF-α (10 ng/mL). The total incubation time was 24 h, of which 1 h under serum-free conditions and 23 h in complete medium. Concurrently, parallel MTT and SOCE Ca2+ imaging experiments were set up to confirm cell viability and microglial activation. The following day, after evaluating the cell morphology by light microscopy, the supernatants were frozen and stored at -80 °C. Meanwhile, the confirmation of unaffected cell viability was determined in the MTT assay and microglial activation was confirmed in the SOCE Ca2+ imaging assay. The protein concentration was evaluated in the BCA protein assay (Thermo Scientific, 23227) and an equal amount of sample proteins were incubated with arrays according to the manufacturer’s protocol. 105 cytokine levels were visualized and captured in ChemiDoc MP (Bio-Rad), and the intensities of each spot were evaluated in ImageStudio (LICORbio, USA) software.

Immunofluorescence staining

BV2 cells were seeded to tissue culture treated 24-well plates at the density 1.2 × 105 cells / well and grown for 24 h. The next day, cells were washed twice with serum-free medium and treated for 1 h in serum-free medium containing LPS (250 ng/ml, Thermo Scientific, #00-4976-03) ± solvent or tested compounds (at 5 µM). Following day, cells were washed twice in ice cold divalent-free phosphate buffered saline (DF-PBS), fixed in ice cold methanol for 10 min at room temperature, and washed again twice in ice cold DF-PBS. Results

Mouse and human microglia activations require differential stimulation protocols

To determine whether CBx interfere with microglial Ca2+ signaling and microglial inflammatory status, we evaluated suitable stimulation protocols that would reliably trigger inflammatory microglial activation.

Nitric oxide released from activated microglia was indirectly assessed using Griess assay. Mouse BV2 microglia responded to 24 h LPS stimulation (0.25 mg/mL) with an elevated concentration of nitrite in culture medium from 0.91 ± 0.7 µM in unstimulated control to 15.46 ± 4.13 µM in LPS-treated BV2 (Fig. 1B) without negative effects on cell viability (Fig. 1C). In line with previous reports [44], iNOS activity is absent in HMC3, and our data confirmed the lack of NO production in these cells (Fig. 1B).

In HMC3, the IL-6 cytokine is reported to be one of the most abundantly secreted cytokines [45] and its production can be further enhanced with LPS-stimulation [46, 47]. The robustness of the IL-6 response to LPS in HMC3 cells is variable across the available literature. In line with several studies [48, 49], our data confirmed a four-fold increase in IL-6 secretion after LPS stimulation (Fig. 1A). However, the absolute concentration of IL-6 was low (115 pg/mL). A substantially larger response was achieved with TNF-α (10 ng/mL, 24 h) or IL-1β (1 ng/mL, 24 h) stimulation, where IL-6 concentrations in the medium reached 2.15 ng/mL and 5.53 ng/mL, respectively. For further experiments with CBx, we decided to activate BV2 with LPS (through the TLR-4 receptor) and HMC3 cells with TNF-α and IL-1β (TNFR and IL1R receptor pathways, respectively). None of the selected treatments negatively affected viability and differences in metabolic activity did not exceed 10% (Fig. 1C).

These results illustrate that mouse BV2 and human HMC3 microglia can be activated to release inflammatory markers, but require different stimulation protocols, indicating differences in prevalent signaling pathways involved in mechanism of activation.

Microglial activation modulates SOCE with differential effects in mouse versus human microglia

Understanding the similarities and differences in SOCE characteristics between mouse and human microglia was a necessary prerequisite to determine the effect of CBx on SOCE. Figure 1D shows the basic characteristics including cell morphology and typical SOCE response to thapsigargin (Tg) in human (Fig. 1D1) and mouse (Fig.1D2) microglial cell lines.

SOCE, the machinery consisting of STIM1/2 and Orai1/2/3 proteins, drives the activation of immune cells. Previous work in our lab [27] demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of SOCE inhibits the activation of Jurkat T-cells. STIM1/2 and Orai1 are regulators of SOCE as well as purinergic activation of primary microglia [4], so we evaluated SOCE in both the resting and activated states of our microglial cell lines.

The magnitude of SOCE (Fig. 1D) was evaluated using Ca2+ imaging after stimulation with Tg (2 µM). Tg-induced inhibition of SERCA depletes Ca2+ stores in the endoplasmic reticulum. The absence of Ca2+ facilitates the association of ER-located STIM proteins with ORAI subunits of CRAC channels located in the plasma membrane and opens the channel pore for Ca2+ influx. Gadolinium (Gd3+, 2 µM) ions prevent Ca2+ influx, so Gd3+ preincubation was used to distinguish residual internal Ca2+ signaling from Ca2+ influx. External Ca2+ chelation with EGTA (2 mM) was used to observe the proportion of Ca2+ released from intracellular stores.

We observed a decrease in the overall Tg-induced SOCE-mediated Ca2+ influx in HMC3 microglia activated with TNF-α and IL-1β (Fig. 1E), while residual Gd3+-resistant Ca2+ signaling remained unchanged, implying the negative effect of activation on SOCE function. To determine whether microglial activation limits SOCE by deregulating the gene expression of individual SOCE components, we analyzed the mRNA levels of the STIM1/2 proteins located in the ER and the ORAI1/2/3 CRAC channel subunits in RT-qPCR: (Fig. 1F). We found that SOCE in HMC3 microglia consists predominantly of STIM1 proteins and ORAI1 channels. Both TNF-α and IL-1β-mediated HMC3 activation increased the level of ORAI1 mRNA suggesting the attempt of the cell to compensate for the functional deficit of SOCE by increasing the abundance of the main CRAC channel subunit.

By contrast, in mouse BV2 microglia, LPS-triggered activation of the TLR4 cascade slightly enhanced the Ca2+ signaling response (Fig. 1G1). The increase in intracellular Ca2+ induced by Tg in activated BV2 was also proportionally elevated (Fig. 1G4) even after eliminating Ca2+ entry when preincubating cells with Gd3+ (Fig. 1G2) and EGTA (Fig. 1G3). These results suggest that the impact of LPS activation on Ca2+ signaling is largely based on the capacity of internal Ca2+ stores rather than augmentation of SOCE itself.

Overall, we observed that microglial activation leads to changes in Ca2+ homeostasis and is specific to receptor signaling pathways that trigger activation. The activation of the TNF and IL1 receptors in HMC3 interfered with the function of CRAC channels, while the activation of the TLR4 receptor pathway in BV2 microglia increased intra-organellar storage levels of Ca2+. It is also important to distinguish the magnitude of changes in Ca²⁺ homeostasis induced by microglial activation, as alterations in BV2 microglia are markedly more subtle than those observed in human HMC3 microglia.

Cannabinoids inhibit SOCE in both mouse and human microglia

We have previously demonstrated that some CBx potently inhibit SOCE in several types of immune cells [27]. Therefore, we evaluated whether CBx modulate SOCE in mouse and human microglia.

In our standard SOCE protocol (Fig. 1D) we analyzed 3 phases of Ca2+ signaling: (1) application of the tested compounds for 3 min to detect Ca2+ signals evoked by the cannabinoids themselves (2), SOCE stimulation with Tg (2 µM) to induce SOCE and test inhibitory effects on Ca2+ entry, and (3) removal of external Ca2+ with EGTA (2 mM) to assess potential effects of the cannabinoids on cellular Ca2+ reuptake / extrusion mechanisms. Comparison of Gd3+-treated HMC3 and BV2, which assesses intracellular Ca2+ mobilization in the absence of SOCE (dotted lines in Fig. 1D), revealed a substantially larger residual capacity of intracellular Ca2+ in mouse BV2 microglia. For SOCE quantification, this residual internal Ca2+ capacity was subtracted from the total signal (grey areas in Fig. 1D).

We tested both, major and minor CBx along with some of their metabolites. The scheme in Fig. 2F shows the origin and classification of the CBx that were selected for SOCE screening. In the Cannabis plant, the first biosynthesized CBx are cannabigerolic (CBGA) and cannabigerovarinic acids (CBGVA). Both also serve as precursors of three main downstream metabolic pathways that produce (1) tetrahydrocannabinolic (THCA) / tetrahydrocannabivarinic (THCVA) acids, (2) cannabidiolic (CBDA) / cannabidivarinic (CBDVA) acids, and (3) cannabichromenic (CBCA) / cannabichromevarinic (CBCVA) acids. Subsequently, conversion to their respective decarboxylated forms is facilitated by heating (red panel) or ageing (grey panel).

Overall, 22 CBx were tested in the resting and activated states of both microglial cell lines (Fig. 2 and Figs. S1-S2). In general, we found that SOCE in HMC3 human microglia was more sensitive to CBx than in murine BV2 cells (Fig. 2E). Several CBx were strong inhibitors of SOCE in both lines and the three most potent CBx overlapped in both lines: CBGA, CBGVA, and CBDVA (Figs. 2A/C). We tested all CBx in a dose-dependent manner and observed that acidic CBx (Fig. 2B/D; red) were typically more effective SOCE inhibitors compared to their corresponding decarboxylated forms (Figs. 2B/D; green). Next, the IC50 values were calculated and CBx were sorted according to their potency of SOCE inhibition in HMC3 (Fig. 2E, red). We identified 12 CBx in HMC3 cells (CBGA, CBGVA, CBDVA, CBDA, THCVA, CBG, CBND, CBGV, CBDV, CBLA, THCA, CBD) and 6 CBx in BV2 microglia - CBGA, CBGVA, CBDVA, CBDA, CBND, CBDV - (Fig. 2E, grey) with IC50 values below the 50 µM threshold.

Further comparison of CBx effects on SOCE in resting and activated microglia (Fig. S1) revealed that TNF-α- or IL-1β-induced activation of HMC3 had minimal impact on SOCE susceptibility towards CBx and minor changes in IC50 values were observed with CBDA, CBLA, CBD and THCA (Fig. S1B). In BV2 cells, LPS-induced activation led to increased SOCE sensitivity towards 6 CBx (Fig. S2A) and the reduction in IC50 values for CBGA, CBDVA, CBDV, CBND, CBDA, THCVA was in the range of 25–46% (Fig. S2B).

Interestingly, with several CBx especially at higher concentrations (10–30 µM) we observed CBx-induced mobilization of intracellular Ca2+ (Fig. S3). The most potent were CBCA and CBNA with comparable effect in both HMC3 and BV2 microglia. When assessing the impact of microglial activation, we discovered that the Ca2+ response to 6 CBx at 30 µM concentration (CBNA, CBCA, CBDV, CBGV, CBGA, CBGVA) in LPS-activated BV2 cells was 21–116% higher when compared to resting BV2 (Fig. S3D). In contrast, only minimal differences in CBx-induced Ca2+ mobilization were observed when comparing resting and TNF-α or IL-1β-activated HMC3 (Fig. S3B).

These results demonstrate that SOCE in both human and mouse microglia was potently inhibited with acidic phytocannabinoids CBGA (human IC50 = 0.8 µM; mouse IC50 = 3.8 µM), CBGVA (human IC50 = 1.1 µM; mouse IC50 = 3.7 µM), and CBDVA (human IC50 = 3.9 µM; mouse IC50 = 10.8 µM). In general, SOCE in HMC3 was more sensitive to CBx than in BV2. Furthermore, microglial activation did not show a significant role in the sensitivity to CBx in human HMC3 (Fig. S1) but potentiated SOCE sensitivity towards 6 CBx in mouse BV2 microglia (Fig. S2). Notably, higher concentrations of several acidic CBx mobilize intracellular Ca2+ and may synergize with other Ca2+ signaling mechanisms.

Cannabinoids suppress LPS-induced effects in mouse microglia

Since SOCE in both mouse and human microglia were susceptible to inhibition by several CBx and SOCE is a mechanism responsible for maintaining Ca2+ signaling and mediating activation of the immune response, we hypothesized that the limited Ca2+ supply caused by SOCE inhibition might interfere with microglia activation, leading to suppression of production and secretion of pro-inflammatory molecules. Here, in addition to our standard panel of tested CBx, we included three additional catabolites of Δ9-THC. Two products of primary downstream metabolic cascade, when hydroxylation of Δ9-THC by hepatic CYP 450 enzymes produce the psychoactive catabolite 11-hydroxy Δ9-THC that is further oxidized into inactive 11-nor-9-carboxy-Δ9-THC. Third was 8β-dihydroxy-Δ9-THC representing a minor secondary metabolite with a variation in oxidation site [50, 51].

First, we tested whether the presence of CBx in culture medium would interfere with LPS-induced NO production in BV2 cells (Fig. 3C). We administered and kept LPS (0.25 mg/mL) with or without CBx under serum-free conditions for 1 h to prevent binding of CBx to serum proteins. Next, we incubated cells for 23 h in complete medium and determined the extracellular nitrite concentration in the Griess assay. We found 7 CBx that significantly inhibited NO production (CBD, CBDVA, CBDA, CBG, CBDV, CBNM, 8β-dihydroxy-Δ9-THC) and 2 CBx that significantly enhanced the pro-inflammatory response (CBCA, CBNA).

NO biosynthesis is a simple and fast biological process. Therefore, we assessed whether CBx interfere with a more robust mechanism, such as protein synthesis, by evaluating the levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α. Immunofluorescence analysis of the TNF-α stained BV2 microglia (Fig. 3A) revealed a panel of CBx that were able to effectively inhibit LPS-induced activation in both mechanisms: CBD, CBDVA, CBDA, CBGA, CBG, CBDV, and CBNM (Fig. 3B).

These results suggest that at least 7 CBx overlap in affecting inflammatory responses and inhibiting 2 distinct signaling pathways that produce pro-inflammatory mediators, NO and TNF-α. Both pathways also share a Ca2+ mobilization step in their upstream signaling cascade; however, this effect cannot be explained solely by inhibition of SOCE, since SOCE in BV2 was not sensitive to CBD, CBG or CBNM.

In a comprehensive array of cytokines, we evaluated the levels of 105 relevant modulators of immune response. Mouse and human microglia exhibit different purinergic responses and susceptibilities to cannabinoids

In order to explore alternative routes of CBx-sensitive Ca2+ signaling that could contribute in mediating the immune response of microglia, we decided to explore the purinome in both cell lines. We exposed both microglial cell lines to various concentrations of ATP using FDSS Fura-2-AM-based Ca2+ imaging (Figs. 5A-C). We observed differential and complex Ca2+ mobilization responses that were caused by both Ca2+ release and Ca2+ influx, in agreement with previous reports [4, 12, 52]. In HMC3 cells (Fig. 5B), the Ca2+ response was dependent on the ATP-concentration, where a low dose of 0.5 mM ATP was ineffective and a high concentration of ATP (5 mM) induced a robust slow increase in intracellular Ca2+ that was independent of Ca2+ influx, because inhibition of SOCE with Gd3+ (2 µM, Fig. 5B; green open circles) or removal of external Ca2+ with EGTA (2 mM, Fig. 5B; red open circles) did not alter the observed intracellular Ca2+ signals.

Conversely, in BV2 cells, we observed a biphasic rise in internal Ca2+ upon simulation at 0.5 mM of ATP (Fig. 5C; black circles), where a transient Ca2+ peak occurred immediately after exposure to ATP (see inset) and was followed by a delayed secondary phase of Ca2+ mobilization. Both of these phases involved Ca2+ release from intracellular stores as well as Ca2+ entry as EGTA (2 mM) reduced the Ca2+ transient by about 50% and the secondary phase maximum by about 70%. Interestingly, Gd3+ (2 µM) had no effect on either of these Ca2+ mobilization phases (Fig. 5C; green trace), indicating that Ca2+ entry evoked by 0.5 mM ATP was not mediated by SOCE but rather by purinergic P2X channels. These results indicate that the ATP-induced Ca2+ responses in human HMC3 cells are substantially muted and require relatively high concentrations of ATP compared to mouse BV2 microglia, where low ATP doses mobilize Ca2+ through Ca2+ release and Ca2+ influx, while high ATP concentrations cause mostly Ca2+ release.

The purinome in microglia is a complex signaling system consisting of (1) intracellular and extracellular purine nucleotides, nucleosides, and nucleobases, (2) P1 adenosine sensing receptors and P2 ATP/ADP sensing receptors, (3) related metabolizing enzymes and (4) transporter mechanisms for nucleosides and bases [53]. To assess the complexity of purinome-mediated Ca2+ signaling in microglia, we tested 7 additional molecules as potential agonists for Ca2+ influx; namely ADP, AMP, ATPγS (a slowly hydrolyzed ATP analogue), UTP, UDP, UMP and UDP-glucose in human (Fig. 5D) and mouse (Fig. 5E) microglia. Aside from ATP, human HMC3 microglia were only sensitive to ADP (5 mM) albeit with a weaker Ca2+ mobilization compared to ATP.

As seen in Fig. 5E, exposure of the mouse BV2 microglia to 0.5 mM ATP triggered a large biphasic response also seen in Fig. 5C (black). Aside from ATP, other nucleotides produced smaller transient peaks relative to ATP (set as 100%): ATPγS (90%), UTP (61%), ADP (49%), and UDP (13%). However, neither of these generated a secondary phase and all other tested nucleotides produced no Ca2+ response whatsoever (Fig. 5E). Thus, ATP emerged as the most suitable agonist to determine the effect of CBx on purinome-mediated Ca2+ signaling.

We next evaluated how 3 min preincubation with 5 µM CBx would affect the purinergic Ca2+ response to high ATP (5 mM) in HMC3 (Fig. 5F; red) and to low ATP (0.5 mM) in BV2 cells (Fig. 5F; black). Minimal differences were observed between the signals of control and CBx pretreated HMC3 cells, suggesting that the tested CBx fail to interfere with the release of internal Ca2+ stores after stimulation with high a concentration of ATP.

In contrast to HMC3 cells, the results in Fig. 5F (black) show that 9 CBx can significantly inhibit ATP-induced Ca2+ mobilization in mouse BV2 cells. Among them, the strongest antagonistic properties were observed with acidic CBx; The inhibition was mainly during the delayed secondary phase of intracellular Ca2+ mobilization, which is largely sustained by Ca2+ influx. Dose-response assays conducted with CBDVA, CBGVA, CBGA, CBDA (Fig. 5G1-G4) established the half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) at 1.5 µM (CBDVA), 2.1 µM (CBGVA), 3.9 µM (CBGA) and 4.1 μm (CBDA) (Fig. 5H). At the highest concentration tested (30 µM), we observed intrinsic Ca2+ facilitation induced by acidic CBx themselves, which was later further enhanced by synergy with ATP (Fig. 5G1-G4, red).

We additionally assessed the impact of microglial activation (Fig. S4) on ATP signaling and found that the ATP-induced purinergic Ca2+ response in LPS-activated BV2 microglia was slightly more sensitive towards acidic CBx. The dissection of biphasic Ca2+ response to ATP showed that microglial activation-induced sensitization influenced primarily the delayed secondary Ca2+ phase (Fig. S4D) while the transient Ca2+ peak remained largely unaffected (Fig. S4C). Taken together, these results show that acidic CBx, especially CBDVA, CBGVA, CBGA, and CBDA are potent inhibitors of ATP-induced purinergic Ca2+ influx in mouse BV2 microglia, and their effect is more profound during the delayed secondary phase of ATP-induced Ca2+ mobilization. In contrast, ATP-evoked Ca2+ release from internal stores in human HMC3 microglia remains largely unaffected by CBx likely due to the lower abundance of purinome-mediated pathways in this cell line.

P2X4 and P2X7 channels are not molecular targets of CBx

To determine whether the ion channels P2X4 and P2X7 are molecular targets of acidic CBx, we performed FDSS Fura-2 based Ca2+ imaging using HEK293 cell lines stably expressing human P2X4 or P2X7 purinoceptors. Assays were optimized using wild-type HEK293 cells as a control (Figs. 7A2 / C1). The P2X4-mediated Ca2+ influx was evoked by 3 µM ATP (Fig. 7A) and abolished by removal of external Ca2+ with EGTA or using the selective inhibitor 5-BDBD (Fig. 7A3). Surprisingly, no inhibition of the P2X4-mediated Ca2+ influx was observed as the inhibition did not exceed 15% of signal in solvent control. A similar protocol was used to assess the Ca2+ influx mediated by P2X7 that was stimulated with 100 µM 3’-O-(4-benzoyl) benzoyl ATP (BzATP) (Fig. 7C). The specificity of P2X7 was verified by inhibition with the P2X7-selective inhibitors A740003, A804598, and JNJ-47965567 (Fig. 7C2). Subsequently, we tested the complete panel of CBx at a 10 µM concentrations (Fig. 7C3). This revealed a partial inhibition of the P2X7-mediated Ca2+ influx by CBG, CBDVA, CBDA and CBND, but the overall inhibitory effect was small and did not exceed 30% (Fig. 7D). These results indicate only partial participation of the P2X7 channel in CBx-induced inhibition of the purinergic response in BV2 cells. Lastly, in agreement with our previous findings, CBNA and CBCA evoked Ca2+ facilitation also in P2X7 overexpressing cells, which later synergized with the Ca2+ response to BzATP.

Together, these findings demonstrate that several CBx possess activity toward purinoceptors that translate into inhibition of Ca2+ signaling evoked by ATP and may contribute to ameliorate the pro-inflammatory signaling pathways in the mouse microglia. However, mapping the mechanism of action of CBx is not straightforward, as it might reach other targets in the purinergic signaling cascade that are parallel or upstream of the P2X4/7 channels. Further experiments would be required in order to identify the possible involvement of the P2Y13 purinoceptor and/or to find other regulatory mechanisms that could be specific for BV2 microglia and that are lacking in HEK293 and HMC3 cell lines.

Discussion

The present study systematically compared the effects of a broad panel of phytocannabinoids (CBx) on calcium (Ca2+) signaling pathways in human and mouse microglial cell lines, with particular focus on store-operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE) and purinergic signaling. Our findings identify acidic CBx, especially CBGA, CBGVA, and CBDVA, as potent inhibitors of microglial SOCE in both HMC3 and BV2 cells. However, the downstream functional consequences of SOCE inhibition differed markedly between the two microglial models. In BV2 microglia, multiple CBx attenuated ATP-dependent Ca2+ signaling as well as LPS-induced inflammatory responses, whereas in HMC3 microglia profound SOCE inhibition was not accompanied by suppression of cytokine secretion following TNF-α or IL-1β stimulation. Together, these findings suggest that the anti-inflammatory effects of CBx in microglia cannot be explained solely by SOCE inhibition, but instead depend on broader context-dependent Ca2+ signaling networks that differ substantially between human and mouse microglial models.

Although CBx have repeatedly been reported to modulate intracellular Ca2+ signaling, microglial SOCE as a molecular target has been largely overlooked [43, 61, 62]. Our group previously demonstrated that acidic CBx inhibit SOCE in lymphocytes, mast cells and monocytes. In lymphocytes, the suppression of Ca2+ influx was associated with reduced cytokine production [27]. The present study extends those findings to microglia and demonstrates that the rank order of potency is conserved across immune cell types, with CBGA, CBGVA, and CBDVA consistently emerging as the strongest SOCE inhibitors.

Limited knowledge is available for these understudied compounds. Anderson et al., 2021 observed that CBGA, CBDVA, and CBGVA have anticonvulsant effects on hyperthermia-induced seizures in mouse model of Dravet syndrome and CBGA was found to interact with GPR55, TRPV1 channels and GABAA receptors [63]. CBGVA and CBDVA inhibit GPR55 and T-type Ca2+ channels, mainly CaV3.1 and CaV3.2 [64]. In contrast, Ca2+ responses in dorsal root ganglion neurons remain unaffected by CBDVA [65]. CBGA was also reported to inhibit TRPM7 and to alleviate kidney damage [66]. CBGA-conjugated nanoparticles has been shown to stimulate TRPV1-mediated Ca2+ influx, with potential implications for pain management [67]. Furthermore, proprietary formulations of CBGA in combination with other non-psychoactive CBx such as CBDA, CBDVA or CBGVA have been patented for a broad spectrum of therapeutic uses [68, 69] including neurological disorders [70, 71], pain [72], inflammation [73, 74], cancer [75–77], viral infections [78, 79], skin damage [80] or oral care [81].

Notably, in our study, acidic CBx were generally more effective than their decarboxylated counterparts, suggesting that structural determinants associated with the carboxylic acid moiety contribute importantly to their interaction with Ca2+ machinery. Consistent with this, a study on the nuclear transcription factor PPARγ reported that acidic CBx activated PPARγ transcriptional activity in Neuro-2 A cells with higher potency than their decarboxylated analogues [82]. Furthermore, several acidic CBx exhibited a concentration-dependent switch from antagonistic to agonistic effects on microglial Ca2+ signaling. At higher concentrations (≥ 30 µM), HMC3 cells were more sensitive to CBx-induced Ca2+ facilitation than BV2 mouse microglia. A comparable concentration-dependent inversion of effect was reported in a mouse model of temperature-induced seizures, where CBGA was anticonvulsant at low concentrations but proconvulsant at higher doses [63]. Together, these findings underscore the importance of dosage, biodistribution, and local compound availability, as the effects of CBx on Ca²⁺ signaling are strongly concentration-dependent and may even reverse.

Despite the overlap in SOCE susceptibility, HMC3 and BV2 microglia differed substantially in their overall Ca2+ signaling behavior. SOCE in HMC3 was more sensitive to CBx inhibition, yet inflammatory cytokine release remained largely resistant to CBx treatment. In contrast, BV2 microglia exhibited more modest SOCE inhibition but robust suppression of NO and TNF-α production. These observations suggest that the functional contribution of SOCE to inflammatory signaling differs between the two models. In HMC3 cells, TNF-α- and IL-1β-driven inflammatory pathways may rely less on sustained SOCE-dependent Ca2+ influx and more heavily on alternative signaling mechanisms such as TRP channels, downstream pathways of NF-κB, MAPK, or cascades that are less sensitive to transient reductions in cytosolic Ca2+ [83–85]. Conversely, in BV2 cells, inflammatory activation appears to remain tightly coupled to ATP-dependent Ca2+ mobilization pathways that are susceptible to CBx modulation.

The divergence between HMC3 and BV2 responses also highlights important limitations inherent to immortalized microglial models. HMC3 cells exhibited markedly attenuated purinergic responsiveness compared to BV2 cells, requiring supraphysiological ATP concentrations to evoke Ca2+ signals. ATP responses in HMC3 were dominated by intracellular Ca2+ release and were largely insensitive to extracellular Ca2+ removal or SOCE blockade, suggesting reduced contribution of plasma membrane Ca2+ influx mechanisms. In contrast to HMC3 and in line with previous studies [52, 86], BV2 microglia displayed robust biphasic ATP responses consisting of an initial transient peak followed by a delayed sustained phase that depended strongly on extracellular Ca2+ entry. These differences in HMC3 cells suggest a potential dysregulation or reduced engagement of key components of purinergic signaling that appear to be functionally active in BV2 cells and primary microglia [6, 87, 88]; however, definitive conclusions require targeted functional validation in future studies.

The ATP concentration dependence of these responses is mechanistically important. Extracellular ATP functions as a danger-associated signaling molecule whose biological effects vary dramatically with local concentration, duration of exposure, and receptor composition [89, 90]. Lower ATP concentrations typically preferentially activate high-affinity metabotropic P2Y receptors that mobilize intracellular Ca2+ stores through phospholipase C and IP3 signaling [55, 91]. In contrast, higher ATP concentrations recruit ionotropic P2X receptors, including low-affinity receptors such as P2X7, resulting in direct Ca2+ influx and prolonged inflammatory signaling [92, 93]. In BV2 microglia, the biphasic response observed at 0.5 mM ATP likely reflects coordinated activation of both P2Y-mediated intracellular Ca2+ release and P2X-mediated Ca2+ influx. The delayed secondary phase was particularly dependent on extracellular Ca2+ and highly sensitive to CBx, implicating sustained purinergic influx pathways as a major site of CBx action.

Importantly, ATP concentrations in vivo are highly heterogeneous and transient. Under physiological conditions, extracellular ATP is normally maintained in the nanomolar range due to rapid degradation by ecto-nucleotidases [94, 95]. However, local ATP concentrations can rise dramatically within confined extracellular microdomains during tissue injury, neuroinflammation, synaptic activity, or cell death [96]. Such transient high-concentration ATP signaling is especially relevant for microglial activation because receptors such as P2X4 and P2X7 are strongly associated with inflammatory cytokine release, inflammasome activation, chemotaxis, and neuropathic pain signaling [97]. Therefore, although the ATP concentrations used experimentally exceed average extracellular bulk concentrations, they likely model localized pathological signaling environments that occur during neuroinflammatory conditions.

Our pharmacological dissection suggests that the delayed ATP-dependent Ca2+ phase in BV2 cells involves contributions from P2X4, P2X7, and P2Y13 signaling pathways. However, experiments in heterologous HEK293 overexpression systems demonstrated that P2X4 channels were largely insensitive to CBx, whereas P2X7 channels showed only partial inhibition. These findings argue against direct high-affinity antagonism of either receptor as the primary mechanism underlying CBx-mediated suppression of ATP signaling in BV2 microglia. Instead, the data support a more distributed mechanism involving modulation of upstream or parallel signaling processes. In contrast, a recent study by the Puopolo group reported that 13 CBx (including CBGVA, CBGA or THCA) act as P2X4 channel binders in a surface plasmon resonance competitive assay using recombinant human P2X4 immobilized on a chip. CBD and CBV were shown to bind P2X4 and to be displaced by the antagonist 5-BDBD, both experimentally and in silico via computational docking [98]. The discrepancy between these findings and our results may reflect differences between isolated protein-based binding assays and intact cellular systems, where membrane context, receptor interactions, and intracellular signaling pathways can influence ligand accessibility and functional outcome. Notably, species-dependent variability may also contribute, as differential pharmacological responses to compounds such as amitriptyline [99], phenylurea BX430 [100], and 5-BDBD [101] have been reported between rodent and human P2X4 receptors.

Our observation, that the P2X7-mediated Ca2+ response to BzATP remains largely unaffected by CBx does not exclude the possibility of alternative mechanisms engaging CBx with P2X7 channels. For example, CBD and THC have been shown to inhibit immunological responses in activated monocytes in a study of a P2X7 genetic variant (SNP rs7958311) associated with high risk of psychosis in cannabis users [102]. The synthetic CBD analog 4’-fluoro-cannabidiol (4’F-CBD) has been shown to inhibit BzATP-stimulated (P2X7-mediated) TNF-α production in primary mouse microglial cultures [103]. In addition, THCV, CBC and CBN have been reported to downregulate ATP efflux through the PANX-1 transmembrane transporter in macrophages, resulting in reduced P2X7 activation, NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, and subsequently decreased IL-1β production [104]. Together with our findings, these results support the involvement of additional indirect regulatory components that may be required for P2X4 and P2X7 channels to function as effective molecular targets of CBx.

In broader context, several mechanistic possibilities may explain our observations of CBx effects on microglial purinergic Ca2+ signaling. Acidic CBx could alter membrane biophysical properties that indirectly affect purinergic receptor gating or receptor-channel coupling. Alternatively, CBx may interfere with intracellular signaling nodes downstream of purinoceptor activation, including IP3-dependent Ca2+ release, mitochondrial Ca2+ buffering, store refilling, or secondary activation of Ca2+-permeable channels [105, 106]. The strong preferential inhibition of the delayed ATP response further suggests that CBx primarily disrupt sustained Ca2+ influx mechanisms rather than the initial release of intracellular stores. Such a mechanism would also be consistent with the observed overlap between SOCE inhibition and suppression of delayed purinergic signaling.

An additional notable finding was the sensitization of BV2 microglia following LPS activation. Activated BV2 cells showed enhanced susceptibility to several CBx in both SOCE and ATP signaling assays, particularly during the delayed secondary purinergic phase (Figs S2-S4). LPS stimulation is known to remodel microglial Ca2+ signaling through altered receptor expression, inflammatory priming, metabolic reprogramming, and changes in ER Ca2+ homeostasis [107, 108]. The increased CBx sensitivity observed after activation may therefore reflect inflammatory-state-dependent remodeling of Ca2+ signaling machinery. In contrast, TNF-α- or IL-1β-mediated activation of HMC3 cells did not substantially alter SOCE sensitivity, again emphasizing fundamental signaling differences between the two models.

The finding that CBCA and CBNA directly elevated intracellular Ca2+ levels and enhanced inflammatory readouts is also mechanistically significant. Whereas most effective anti-inflammatory CBx suppressed sustained Ca2+ influx pathways, CBCA and CBNA instead consistently promoted intracellular Ca2+ mobilization in both microglial models and in purinoceptor-overexpressing HEK293 cells. This suggests that some CBx may exert pro-inflammatory actions through facilitation of Ca2+ signaling rather than inhibition. Because elevated intracellular Ca2+ is tightly linked to inflammatory gene transcription, ROS generation, inflammasome activation, and cytokine secretion [109], excessive or sustained CBx-induced Ca2+ mobilization may shift microglia toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. These observations further underscore that CBx cannot be considered functionally interchangeable and instead possess highly distinct signaling profiles. CBCA and CBNA have been reported to have a necrotic effect on the colorectal carcinoma cell line HCT-116, and are included in multiple patents as a potential treatments of cancer, including colon, skin, ovarian and liver cancer [68, 75, 110–112]. Both compounds have anti-microbial activity that is been utilized in oral care [81] and exhibit anti-nociceptive effects [113, 114]. CBCA demonstrated anticonvulsant activity in a mouse model of intractable pediatric epilepsy [115]. Overall, these findings indicate that despite the unwavering utilization of CBCA and CBNA in oncology and neurology, their proinflammatory potential should not be overlooked.

Our study also has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the data. First, both BV2 and HMC3 are immortalized cell lines that incompletely recapitulate the transcriptional and functional properties of primary human microglia [116]. The attenuated purinergic signaling observed in HMC3 cells particularly highlights the importance of validating these findings in primary human microglia or induced pluripotent stem cell-derived systems. Second, the broad pharmacological profile of CBx complicates precise target identification because multiple ion channels, receptors, enzymes, and membrane processes may be affected simultaneously. Third, while our experiments demonstrate potent modulation of Ca2+ signaling, they do not yet establish whether the identified CBx reach sufficient concentrations in the central nervous system in vivo to reproduce these effects under physiological conditions. Our study also lacks the direct evaluation of CBx effect on P2Y13-mediated Ca2+ signaling that should be assessed in overexpression systems in future research.

Nevertheless, the present findings provide a novel and detailed comparative framework linking CBx structure to microglial Ca2+ signaling phenotypes. The consistent activity of acidic CBx across multiple immune cell types suggests that these compounds target conserved components of inflammatory Ca2+ signaling networks. More broadly, our results support the concept that modulation of sustained Ca2+ influx pathways represents an important mechanism underlying the immunomodulatory effects of specific CBx. Future studies integrating electrophysiology, receptor-specific genetic approaches, lipid membrane analyses, STIM1 clustering assays, and primary human microglial systems will be necessary to define the precise molecular targets involved.

In conclusion, this study identifies understudied acidic CBx as potent modulators of microglial Ca2+ signaling and demonstrates that suppression of inflammatory responses correlates more strongly with inhibition of sustained ATP-dependent Ca2+ influx pathways than with SOCE inhibition alone. The marked divergence between human and mouse microglial models further emphasizes the importance of cellular context in CBx pharmacology and cautions against assuming mechanistic equivalence across experimental systems. These findings advance understanding of how individual CBx regulate microglial signaling and identify Ca2+-dependent inflammatory pathways as promising targets for future cannabinoid-based therapeutic development.
