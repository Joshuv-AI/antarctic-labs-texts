Integrated plant and rhizosphere response to gadolinium exposure in hydroponically grown tomato plants
Authors: Francesca Giuliano, Giovanna Petta, Mattia Terzaghi, Dario Nicola Tangredi, Francesco Guarino, Stefano Castiglione, Angela Cicatelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rare earth elements (REEs), comprising the lanthanides, scandium, and yttrium, are increasingly released into the environment due to anthropogenic activities but are not routinely monitored as conventional environmental contaminants. Gadolinium (Gd), used as a contrast agent in magnetic resonance imaging, is continuously discharged into aquatic ecosystems. While Gd effects on plants have been partially explored, its impact on plant physiology and plant-microbiome interactions remains poorly understood. This study investigated the effects of 150 µM Gd (23.6 mg L− 1, 23.6 ppm) on tomato plants (Solanum lycopersicum L.) grown in hydroponics using a phosphate-free nutrient solution to prevent Gd precipitation and ensure its bioavailability. A multidisciplinary approach was employed, integrating morphological, physiological, biochemical, and molecular analysis. Plant growth, root system architecture, antioxidant defence responses, calcium content, Gd accumulation and translocation were assessed, together with changes in the composition and predicted functional potential of the rhizosphere microbiome. Prolonged Gd exposure significantly impaired tomato growth reducing leaf fresh weight from 7.90 g in control plants to 3.15 g in treated seedlings and markedly altering root morphology. Stress conditions triggered strong oxidative responses, while Gd accumulated predominantly in roots (5.32 mg g− 1), with no detectable translocation to aerial tissues. Rhizosphere microbial community composition was severely altered (e.g., Burkholderiales declined to 42% in Gd-treated plants). This work represents one of the first integrated assessments of plant morphological, physiological, biochemical and microbiome-level responses of a crop plant and its rhizosphere microbiome to Gd exposure, providing new insights into the ecological and agronomic implications of emerging REEs contamination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09341-9.

Introduction

According to the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC), rare earth elements (REEs) are a group of 17 elements with atomic numbers from 57 to 71, including fifteen lanthanides, scandium (21) and yttrium (39). REEs exhibit similar physical and chemical properties and are widely used in many industrial sectors such as glass, ceramics and electronic devices production, renewable energy, military equipment, agriculture and medicine [1, 2]. In recent years, REEs have increasingly been recognized as contaminants of emerging concern (CECs), a class of unregulated and insufficiently monitored compounds (e.g., antibiotics, pharmaceuticals, etc.) whose environmental and ecotoxicological impacts are still poorly understood [1, 3, 4]. Although REEs share several characteristics with other CECs, they have only recently been considered within this framework [1]. Among REEs, gadolinium (Gd, a lanthanide with atomic number 64) has received particular attention due to its widespread use in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as a contrast agent. In gadolinium-based contrast agents (Gd-BCAs), Gd is chelated with organic ligands to reduce its toxicity and prevent interference with biological processes. In fact, because of its ionic radius similarity to calcium (Ca; 179 pm for Gd and 197 pm for Ca) Gd can interfere with Ca-dependent physiological pathways [5, 6]. Following clinical use, Gd-BCAs are excreted into urine, collected in sewage systems and, since wastewater treatment plants cannot effectively remove them discharged into surface waters leading to their environmental accumulation [5, 7]. Since its clinical approval in 1988, environmental Gd concentrations have increased significantly, with documented anthropogenic anomalies in surface waters, including rivers, lakes, and even drinking water [5, 8, 9]. Recent studies have shown that Gd can be taken up by plants, accumulate in tissues, and affect physiological processes, including growth inhibition, oxidative stress, and alterations in DNA integrity and methylation patterns (e.g. Citrus × limonia Osbeck, Oryza sativa L., Solanum lycopersicum L., Triticum durum L., Vigna radiata L. and Zea mays L [10–12]). may enter agricultural systems through fertilisers and contaminated irrigation water [13, 14]; in fact, it has been detected in food products like rice [15], flour and carrots (3.05–10.5 µg/kg) [16], pointing to its entry into the food chain. Although REEs generally accumulate more in roots than in aerial tissues [13], their biological effects appear to be dose-dependent, with low concentrations sometimes stimulating growth and higher concentrations causing toxicity [17–19]. One of the primary mechanisms underlying Gd phytotoxicity involves the alteration of reactive oxygen species (ROS) balance. ROS, including superoxide anion (O2−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hydroxyl radicals (OH−), are normally produced in plant cells (by chloroplasts, mitochondria and peroxisomes) but can accumulate under stress conditions, leading to oxidative damage. To counteract this, plants activate antioxidant defence systems, including enzymes such as catalase (CAT) [20, 21]. However, the extent to which Gd-induced oxidative stress varies among plant organs and developmental stages remains insufficiently understood. In addition to direct effects on plant physiology, Gd contamination from water bodies may impact on the rhizosphere microbiome. Plants actively recruit specific microbial communities that promote nutrient uptake, metabolic regulation and tolerance to biotic and abiotic stress. Abiotic stressors, including heavy metals, are known to alter microbial diversity, community composition, and functional activity in the rhizosphere [22, 23]. However, despite increasing concern about REEs as environmental contaminants, there is currently a lack of detailed studies addressing the effects of Gd on plant-associated microbial communities. In particular, it remains unclear how Gd-induced changes in plant physiology—such as alterations in root system architecture, oxidative stress responses, and nutrient balance—are linked to shifts in the structure and functional potential of the rhizosphere microbiome. In this context, Gd represents a suitable model element for investigating the interactions between environmental contaminants, plant physiology, and microbial communities, given its increasing anthropogenic input and traceability in aquatic ecosystems [5, 7, 9]. Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) is one of the most important crops in the world and tomato fruit can be considered a staple food for billions of people [24]. The cultivar MicroTom is a dwarf cultivar characterised by two recessive genes that reduce considerably its size. This tomato variety combines agronomic relevance with features typical of model plants, including small genome (950 Mb), small size, short life cycle, and ease of genetic and physiological analyses. MicroTom cultivar represents a perfect model organism that combines agronomic relevance with the advantages of a model plant, allowing detailed physiological, biochemical and microbiome-related investigations under controlled conditions [24, 25]. However, it is still unclear how Gd-driven physiological changes in plants are mechanistically connected to alterations in rhizosphere microbial structure and function.

Aim of the study

The aim of this study was to investigate the mechanisms underlying Gd phytotoxicity by integrating plant physiological responses and rhizosphere microbiome dynamics. Specifically, we evaluated the effects of 150 µM Gd (23.6 mg L− 1, 23.6 ppm) on tomato plants grown in a hydroponic system using a phosphate-free Hoagland solution to minimise REE–phosphate precipitation. An integrated, multi-level approach was employed to assess plant growth, root system architecture, oxidative stress responses, elemental accumulation, and the composition and predicted functional potential of the rhizosphere microbiome. We hypothesized that (i) Gd exposure primarily targets the root system, leading to impaired plant growth and oxidative stress, (ii) Gd interferes with calcium uptake and distribution, and (iii) these physiological disruptions are associated with significant shifts in the structure, diversity, and functional potential of the rhizosphere microbiome.

Materials and methods

Experimental setup

Tomato seeds were sterilized in 70% ethanol with constant shaking for two min, then washed three times for one min with distilled sterile water. Subsequently, seeds were soaked in a solution of sodium hypochlorite 1.5% for 15 min and finally washed again three times with distilled water. Seeds were placed on filter paper in Petri dishes, irrigated with distilled water and allowed to germinate in a phytotron at 21° C and 60% humidity, in the darkness. After that, a total of 16 seedlings were transferred to two different hydroponic systems: one considered as control (CNT) and the other one treated with 150 µM Gd (Gd 150 µM). Each system was filled with three liters of Hoagland nutrient solution. The Hoagland solution is one of the most commonly media used for hydroponic plant growth and it provides all the essential nutrients (macroelements: KNO3 5mM, Ca(NO3)2·4H2O 5mM, KH2PO4 1 mM, MgSO4·7H2O 2 mM, and micronutrient H3BO3 46 µM, MnCl2·4H2O 9 µM, ZnSO4·7H2O 0.77 µM, CuSO4·5H2O 0.32 µM, MoO3·H2O 0.10 µM, Fe-EDTA 50 µM; [26]. Gadolinium was supplied as Gd(NO3)3·6H2O (Sigma-Aldrich; Saint Louis, MO). The addition of lanthanides to the media resulted in the production of white milky precipitates due to complexation between REEs and phosphates. Therefore, during Gd exposure, plants were treated using a phosphate-free modified Hoagland solution to prevent precipitation and maintain Gd bioavailability. Seedlings received three weekly Gd treatments at a concentration of 150 µM. Each treatment lasted approximately 24 h, resulting in a total exposure time of about 70 h. Between Gd treatments, both control and treated plants were maintained in standard Hoagland solution containing phosphate to ensure adequate nutrient supply and to avoid potential phosphate deficiency effects. The concentration was chosen after preliminary laboratory tests and selected as a sublethal dose. In addition, this concentration might reproduce extreme exposure conditions that could occur in localized pollution hotspots to probe the potential for acute biological effects under high Gd loads. Plant samples were collected at two different time point: the first was done around the 45th day after seeds germination (1st sampling), while the second one around 120th day (2nd sampling). During both samplings, four seedlings/plants were collected for each treatment.

Morphological parameters

For both samplings, morphological assessments of seedlings/plants were conducted by dividing them into their main organs: stems, leaves, roots, and fruits (2nd sampling). Fruits were then harvested, counted and weighed. Special attention was given to root traits, specifically length and diameter classes. Immediately after harvesting, plant organs were weighed to accurately determine their fresh biomass. Leaves and stems were scanned using a calibrated flatbed scanner (Epson Expression 13000 XL) at a resolution of 300 dpi. From these images, total leaf area and stem length were quantified using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). Roots were submerged in water and then scanned at 800 dpi using the same flatbed scanner equipped with a transparency unit. The resulting images were analysed with WinRhizo Pro v. 2007d (Regent Instruments Inc., Quebec, Canada), using appropriate settings to determine root length and volume across different diameter classes at a 0.05 mm resolution [27]. Moreover, due to the high abundance of very fine roots (d < 0.5 mm), a sub-sampling approach was applied to accelerate quantification [28]. Plant material was dried in an oven at 70 °C for three days until constant weight was achieved to determine dry biomass.

Evaluation of Gd and Ca content

Dry mineralization is an effective methodology to decompose organic material and release metals in an analysable form. In the present study, this technique was used to evaluate the Gd and Ca content in roots, stems, leaves and fruits. The dried plant material was pulverized with liquid nitrogen, and approximately 2 g were used for subsequent analyses. The powdered material was then subjected to heat treatment in a muffle at 500 °C for 4 h to achieve complete combustion of the organic component. The ashes were then dissolved in 25 ml of 65% nitric acid solution. Dissolved samples were diluted to a concentration of 3% and filtered through 0.45 µM syringe filter and then analysed by Inductively Coupled Plasma with Optical Emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES Optima 7000 DV - Perkin Elmer, USA). Standard solutions of REEs were used to generate the calibration curves to assess the analyte concentrations. Three biological replications and three technical reads were carried out.

Oxidative stress

Hydrogen peroxide

Hydrogen peroxide content was detected according to Velikova [29]. Plant materials were first pulverized with liquid nitrogen and then 0.1 g of plant biomass was weighed. The samples were homogenized with 1.0 ml of trichloroacetic acid (TCA) 0.1%. After that they were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min at 4° C. Subsequently, 500 µL of phosphate buffer (10 mM pH 7.0) and 1.0 mL of 1.0 M potassium iodide were added to 500 µL of supernatant. Absorbance was measured at 390 nm by means of a spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Milano, Italy).

Malondialdehyde

The concentration of MDA was assessed as reported by Oliva et al. [30]. Pulverized plant materials were weighed (0.1 g) and homogenized with 1.0 ml of TCA 0.1%. Samples were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min at 4° C and 400 µL of supernatant solution were added to 1.0 ml of TCA (20%) and barbituric acid (0.5%). After that, the samples were incubated for 30 min at 95° C then the reaction was stopped by placing the sample tubes on ice. Malondialdehyde content was estimated using the same spectrophotometer employed for H2O2 determination, measuring the absorbance at 532 and 600 nm (Shimadzu UV-1800, Milano, Italy).

Catalase

Catalase activity was determined according to Shahzadi [31]. Pulverized plant materials (0.25 g) were homogenized with 4.0 ml of phosphate buffer (pH 7.8 at 0.2 M) and EDTA (0.1 mM). After that the samples were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min at 4° C, then 100 µL of H2O2 (300 mM) and 2.8 ml of phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0) were added to 100 µL of the supernatant. Finally, the absorbance at 240 nm was measured (Shimadzu UV-1800, Milano, Italy).

Rhizosphere microbiome DNA extraction and amplification

Microbial communities of the rhizosphere were analyzed as follows: 2.5 g of roots were collected in 50 mL tubes containing 25 mL of sterile physiological solution; the tubes were stirred for 30 min at 25° C and 200 rpm; subsequently, the roots were removed with sterile tweezers, and the remaining solution was centrifuged for 10 min at 600 rpm. The supernatant (about 12 mL) was transferred to new tube and centrifuged for 15 min at 3,000 rpm. The supernatant was discarded, and the microbial pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of sterile physiological solution (0.9% NaCl) and then centrifuged again as described above. Finally, it was suspended in 2.0 mL of glycerol-water solution (20%) and stored at -80° C. DNA was extracted from the microbial pellets using Extract-N-Amp™ Plant PCR kit (Merck Life Science Srl, Milan-Italy). After centrifugation for 15 min at 3,000 rpm, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet resuspended in 200 µL of sterile physiological solution. Four µL of the resuspended pellet were added to 20 µL of extraction buffer and incubated at 94° C for 20 min. Subsequently, 20 µL of dilution buffer were added to the samples. The hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene were amplified using 341 F (5′-CCTACGGGRSGCAGCAG-3′) and 909R (5′-TTTCAGYCTTGCGRCCGTAC-3′) primers. The following thermal profile was adopted for their amplification: the purified DNA was initially denatured at 94° C for 3 min, after this first denaturation step it was amplified thanks to 35 cycles of denaturation at 94° C for 1 min, annealing at 58° C for 1 min and extension at 72° C for 1 min, with a final extension of 72° C for 5 min. The amplicons were sequenced by means Next Generation Sequencing (NGS; Genomics4life company, Baronissi, SA, Italy).

Sequences analysis

The obtained NGS data were analyzed using Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME 2), an open source platform for bioinformatics analysis of sequencing data, with plugins-demux2 dada2 [32] and feature-Table [33]. Alfa diversity analyses was performed by using QIIME 2 diversity [34]. For taxonomic analysis, the 16 S rDNA sequences (trimmed to include the V3–V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene amplified by the 341 F/909R primer pair) were classified using a pre-trained Naive Bayes classifier based on the SILVA 138 Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) database was used. Taxonomic and compositional analyses were conducted by using plugins feature-classifier [35], taxa and composition [36]. The data were used for α-diversity analyses through the Qiime2 α-diversity plugins [37]. The predicted metagenome functional profiles were analyzed according to Zeng et al. (2020) to predict the potential changes in metabolic function [38]. Briefly, the 16 S rRNA sequences were used to the functional analysis of the microbiome performed through PICRUSt2 on the Galaxy platform. The analysis was performed using both fasta files, containing the representative ASV sequences, and biom tables, reporting the corresponding feature abundance across samples. These files were used as input for the PICRUSt2 full pipeline to infer putative metagenomic functional profile. To compare the functional profiles among samples, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the predicted KEGG pathway to explore the similarities and differences among samples based on their functional profiles.

Statistical analysis

Morphological data (biomass and length) distribution was tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions were violated, nonparametric statistical methods were employed. Differences among experimental groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with a significance level set at p < 0.05, followed by Dunn’s test for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed in RStudio software using the packages “pgirmess” and “rcompanion”.

Results

Morphological parameters

Biomass

Figures 1 and 2 show plant growth as biomass production in seedlings and tomato plants grown with or without 150 µM Gd. Statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) were detected for every plant organ in fresh biomass at the 1st sampling (Fig. 1a). In fact, the fresh weight of leaves decreased from 7.90 ± 1.35 g in CNT to 3.15 ± 0.25 g in Gd-treated seedlings. Furthermore, stem biomass in the CNT group was approximately three times higher than in Gd-exposed plants, and significant effects of Gd were also observed in the roots (Fig. 1a). At the 2nd sampling, Gd did not affect fresh weights of the leaves and stems, except for the roots (Fig. 1b). A noticeable reduction in the fresh weight of Gd-treated plants was observed; in fact, the root weight decreased from about 12.20 ± 1.65 g in the case of the CNT to about 4.00 ± 0.35 g for Gd-treated plants.

In contrast, Gd did not significantly affect the dry biomass of all three organs, at both collection points (Fig. 2). Although the differences were not statistically significant, dry biomass was reduced at both samplings (Fig. 2a, b) after Gd exposure (by about 50% for all organs).

Root traits

The root system (Figures S1 and S2) was strongly affected by Gd treatment. The root system of the CNT (Fig. S1a and S2a) was longer and less thick compared to that of the Gd-treated plants (Figure S1b and S2b). The plant roots exposed to Gd were visibly shorter and thicker at both sampling points. The mean total length of the roots was severely affected by Gd at both samplings, with a significant reduction of about 90% of the mean total length of the root system (Fig. 3).

The analysis of root diameter distribution clearly revealed a marked reduction in both the emergence and radial growth of lateral roots in plants exposed to Gd. In particular, compared to the CNT, the coarse roots of Gd-treated plants were skewed toward smaller diameters, with a mean diameter of approximately 0.55 mm and a maximum diameter of 0.7 mm. In contrast, CNT plants exhibited coarse roots with significantly larger diameters, with a mean diameter of approximately 0.95 mm and a maximum of 1.85 mm (Fig. 6). Moreover, fibrous roots (d < 0.4 mm) were significantly reduced in Gd-treated plants.

Leaves and stem traits

Significant morphological changes were observed in Gd-treated seedlings with respect to the CNT ones. Seedlings treated with Gd showed a reduction of about 40% in leaf area (0.045 ± 0.01 m²) when compared with the CNT (0.075 ± 0.001 m²) at the 1st sampling. At the 2nd sampling, a reduced leaf area was also observed in treated plants (0.038 ± 0.003 m²) compared to the CNT (0.060 ± 0.010 m²) (Fig. S3). Stem length showed a different response to Gd treatment: in a short period, Gd caused a reduction of about 30% of stem length, resulting in 6.95 ± 0.40 cm in the case of the treated plants (mean length of 9.95 ± 0.90 cm for the CNT); at the 2nd sampling, the average length of the stem recorded in the CNT was equal to 15.40 ± 1.90 cm, while in the Gd-treated plants the average value was 16.40 ± 1.0 cm (Fig.S4). Finally, plants were harvested at the end-of-life cycle, at the fruit stage, allowing an overall evaluation of their growth and development. This approach offered the opportunity to examine not only the effects of the Gd treatment on vegetative growth, but also on the productive aspects, through the harvest of the fruits from both treatments. Based on the data collected, no statistically significant differences in the weight of the fruits harvested from the CNT and Gd-treated plants were observed, with average weights (per treatment) of 3.31 ± 1.20 g and 2.76 ± 0.79 g, respectively. However, when the number of fruits was considered, a higher average number of fruits per plant was observed in the plants treated with Gd compared to the CNT (22.5 ± 1.5 and 18.6 ± 2.1, respectively).

Oxidative stress

The content of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), malondialdehyde (MDA), and catalase (CAT) in tomato leaves, collected at the 1st and 2nd sampling, is reported in Table 1. The analysis of H₂O₂ content did not reveal statistically significant differences between CNT plants and those exposed to Gd. Conversely, MDA content exhibited a significant increase (p < 0.05) in the leaves of Gd-treated plants, indicating enhanced lipid peroxidation as a result of Gd exposure. Specifically, the MDA concentration in control plants was 165.15 ± 1.20 nmol·g⁻¹ fresh weight (FW), whereas in Gd-exposed plants it increased up to 214.15 ± 2.50 nmol·g⁻¹ FW, corresponding to an increase of approximately 29%. However, at the 2nd sampling, this trend was reversed, with higher MDA levels observed in the CNT plants compared to the Gd-treated ones. Regarding CAT activity, statistically significant differences were observed, with the CNT plants exhibiting higher enzymatic activity than those exposed to Gd.

Evaluation of Gd and Ca content

The ICP analysis revealed distinct distribution patterns of Gd and Ca within plant organs, in accordance with their respective chemical properties and translocation mechanisms (Tables 3 and 4). The roots showed significantly higher Gd concentrations, with a marked decrease in the aerial organs. In particular, Gd concentrations were substantially lower in stems and leaves, and below the detection limit (LOD) in the fruits. In contrast, Ca showed an opposite distribution pattern, with concentrations progressively increasing from roots to aerial organs, reaching the highest values in leaves (leaves> stems> roots). Notably, in Gd-treated plants, Ca concentrations were consistently lower than those observed in CNT plants.

Rhizosphere microbiome

The rhizosphere microbial communities associated with tomato roots of either seedlings or plants were analyzed in both CNT and Gd-treated samples to assess the effect of Gd exposure on microbial biodiversity. At the 1st sampling, the analysis revealed that Gd treatment significantly altered the composition of the rhizosphere microbiome (Fig. 7). In CNT plants, the most abundant Operational Taxonomic Unit (OTU) was Burkholderiales, representing 44% of the community, but this group was markedly reduced to about 26% in Gd-treated plants. Caulobacterales and Sphingomonadales followed, accounting for 13.0% and 12.5%, respectively, in CNT plants. Gd exposure caused a decrease of Caulobacterales to 4.7%, whereas Sphingomonadales increased up to 14.8%.

Biodiversity analysis

Microbial α-diversity was assessed by calculating the Shannon index, which accounts for both species richness and evenness. A significant increase in Shannon index values was observed in Gd-treated seedlings (1st sampling) and plants (2nd sampling). In particular, at the 1st sampling, the Shannon index increased from approximately 4.8 in the CNT seedlings up to 5.8 in Gd-exposed ones. At the 2nd sampling, the difference was even more pronounced, with an increase from 3.0 (CNT) to about 5.0 in the Gd-treated ones (Fig. S5). At both sampling times, the results were statistically significant (p-value < 0.05) according to the Kruskal-Wallis test. Overall, Gd-treated samples consistently showed higher Shannon index values compared to CNT at both sampling times, indicating a marked change in microbial diversity of the tomato rhizosphere.

Discussion

Effects of Gd exposure on plant growth and physiology

Exposure to Gd significantly affected plant growth and physiological responses, highlighting the potential ecological impact of this emerging contaminant. In recent years, anomalous concentrations of Gd have been increasingly reported in various aquatic environments, including lakes, coastal waters, and even tap water, mainly as a consequence of its extensive use as a contrast agent in MRI and the limited efficiency of wastewater treatment plants in removing this element [5, 7, 9]. These observations raise concerns about the potential ecological consequences of Gd release into natural environments. In the present study, tomato seedlings and plants (cv. MicroTom) were exposed to 150 µM Gd under controlled hydroponic conditions using Hoagland nutrient solution, with Gd supplied under phosphate-deprived conditions in order to avoid precipitation phenomena. This concentration was selected based on our previous experiments, in which it proved sufficient to induce measurable physiological and biochemical responses without causing toxicity or plant death. Although higher than levels typically encountered in natural aquatic environments (from 90 to 110 ng L− 1 in wastewater treatment plants [39], from 0.4 to 18 ng L− 1 in tap water, and from 1 to 30 ng L− 1 in surface waters [8]), this concentration allows a clear evaluation of Gd uptake and its effects under controlled conditions, with phosphate deprivation preventing precipitation and ensuring Gd availability. Under these conditions, Gd exposure primarily affected root development, indicating that the root system was the most sensitive plant compartment to Gd stress. Previous studies have shown that exposure to REEs can influence plant growth depending on species, concentration, and exposure conditions. In several plant species, low concentrations of REEs have been reported to stimulate plant growth, whereas higher concentrations generally exert inhibitory or toxic effects [40]. Experimental studies confirm these dose-dependent effects in multiple species. For example, growth stimulation has been observed in A. thaliana exposed to low concentrations of Gd, while higher doses resulted in growth inhibition [41]. In Lemna minor, REEs elicited a hormetic response, with growth stimulation at low concentrations and toxicity at higher doses [42]. The same trend has been observed for Gd on Lemna gibba L., finding no significant growth inhibition at concentrations up to 5 mg L⁻¹, while concentrations above 10 mg L⁻¹ resulted in toxicity [19]. In Medicago sativa L [43]., treatments with La, Ce, and terbium (Tb) improved growth at low concentrations but reduced chlorophyll content and biomass at higher concentrations. Similar experiments on crops such as radish, tomato, and spinach demonstrated enhanced root development and photosynthesis at low REE doses, followed by growth inhibition at elevated concentrations. Likewise, species including Oryza sativa, Glycine max, and Vicia faba exhibited hormesis, with growth stimulation at moderate REE levels and reduction at higher concentrations in the substrate [18]. Overall, these findings confirm that the effects of REEs on plants are multifactorial and strongly dependent on species and concentration, emphasizing the need to carefully assess the ecological risks associated with REE release into natural environments. In contrast with these stimulatory responses at low doses, our results showed a clear inhibition of growth in tomato plants exposed to Gd, particularly evident as a reduction in fresh biomass during the early stages of exposure. However, dry biomass remained largely unchanged, suggesting that Gd exposure may primarily affect plant water relations rather than carbon assimilation. The reduction in fresh biomass without significant changes in dry biomass may indicate impaired water uptake or retention, possibly associated with alterations in root hydraulic conductivity or membrane permeability. Similar effects on plant water balance have been reported for other REEs. For instance, scandium improved osmotic stress tolerance in rice by increasing leaf relative water content under drought or salinity conditions [44], while yttrium exposure in maize reduced stomatal size and leaf area, potentially limiting water loss [45]. Roots represent the primary interface between plants and the growth medium and play a crucial role in water and nutrient uptake. In hydroponic systems, roots are directly exposed to dissolved REEs, which may regulate their absorption and distribution within plant tissues [46]. Fine roots are particularly important for nutrient acquisition and rapidly respond to environmental changes, influencing plant–soil interactions and nutrient cycling [47]. In the present study, Gd exposure severely affected root morphology, particularly reducing the development of fine and lateral roots. Since these structures are essential for water and nutrient acquisition as well as for rhizosphere interactions, their reduction may represent a functional limitation for plant growth and resilience. Similar root responses have been reported under metal stress conditions [48]. However, contrasting results exist: Liu et al. (2022) [49] observed no significant root alterations in Boehmeria nivea exposed to mixtures of REEs except at low concentrations, where root growth was stimulated. Likewise, Jia et al. (2024) [50] reported modifications in lateral root development under phosphorus deficiency, while Fan et al. (2020) [51] described stimulatory effects of certain REEs on root growth at moderate concentrations. Overall, our findings indicate that Gd exposure can severely impair root system development in tomato plants, particularly affecting lateral root formation and reducing root diameter. These alterations resemble responses observed under heavy-metal stress conditions, where contaminants such as cadmium inhibit lateral root development and limit the plant’s ability to explore the surrounding environment [52]. Together, these results highlight the high sensitivity of the tomato root system to Gd exposure and suggest that root morphological traits may serve as early indicators of REE-induced stress. These results support our first hypothesis that Gd exposure primarily targets the root system, leading to impaired plant growth.

Gd accumulation and interference with calcium metabolism

The strong accumulation of Gd in the roots of tomato plants (about 5 mg g− 1) and its limited translocation to aerial tissues suggest restricted mobility within the plant. This pattern is consistent with previous observations for REEs, which bind strongly to root cell walls, mainly to pectic and carboxyl-rich domains, and are restricted in xylem transport [53]. The observed decrease in Ca concentration, particularly in roots (from 73.73 ± 5.98 mg g− 1 to 62.98 ± 2.42 mg g− 1), suggests competition between Gd and Ca, consistent with the known role of Gd as a Ca channel blocker. Gadolinium is known to interfere with Ca-dependent signaling pathways and membrane transport processes. Given the central function of Ca in cell signaling, membrane stability, and growth regulation, Gd-induced disruption of Ca homeostasis may affect chlorophyll function, photosynthesis, proline accumulation, and ROS levels [2, 54], contributing to the alterations observed in plant growth and nutrient balance. These findings align with the general accumulation pattern of REEs in plants (roots > leaves > stems > fruits), reflecting xylem-mediated transport that is hindered by apoplastic barriers in roots [2, 17]. Moderate REE levels may enhance cell membrane integrity and stress tolerance, but excessive accumulation can block Ca channels and disrupt Ca-dependent processes. Moreover, agricultural products cultivated near mining sites exhibit higher REE concentrations than those from control areas, confirming the environmental relevance and bioaccumulative potential of these elements [55]. These results support our second hypothesis that Gd interferes with calcium uptake and distribution.

Oxidative stress responses and antioxidant defense mechanisms

The biochemical responses observed in this study suggest that Gd exposure induced oxidative stress in tomato plants. Lipid peroxidation, as indicated by increased MDA levels, reflects membrane damage likely associated with ROS production. Hydrogen peroxide, a key ROS and stress indicator, showed no statistically significant differences between CNT and Gd-treated plants in both roots and leaves (H₂O₂ content: CNT = 0.027 ± 0.018 mmol · g FW− 1 and 0.062 ± 0.014 mmol · g FW− 1 in leaves and roots, respectively; Gd = 0.027 ± 0.011 mmol · g FW− 1 and 0.059 ± 0.013 mmol · g FW− 1 in leaves and roots, respectively). This apparent discrepancy may indicate that ROS accumulation occurs transiently or that the plant antioxidant defence system efficiently buffered oxidative damage, preventing detectable H₂O₂ accumulation. The modulation of antioxidant enzyme activities, particularly CAT, supports the hypothesis that plants activate protective mechanisms to mitigate ROS accumulation and maintain cellular homeostasis under Gd exposure. This effective defence response may mask subtle oxidative stress, which may underlie more extensive biochemical or morphological alterations. Several studies have reported that low doses of REEs stimulate antioxidant enzyme activity, whereas higher concentrations can inhibit it [31, 44, 45]. In line with these findings, the concentration of Gd used in our experiment (150 µM) likely fell within the stimulatory range, explaining the absence of significant H₂O₂ accumulation. Previous studies with Ce nanoparticles also highlight the dose-dependent nature of REE-induced oxidative stress: increased ROS and MDA levels were observed at higher Ce concentrations, often accompanied by reduced biomass and impaired growth [56]. Taken together, these results suggest that Gd exposure can trigger oxidative stress responses in tomato plants, but the concurrent activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms mitigates ROS accumulation, maintaining cellular redox balance and limiting immediate damage. Together, these findings are consistent with our hypothesis that Gd induces oxidative stress, although partially mitigated by antioxidant responses.

Rhizosphere microbiome responses to Gd exposure

The rhizosphere microbiome plays a fundamental role in plant health by contributing to nutrient acquisition, stress tolerance, and metabolic interactions between plants and soil microorganisms [57]. Plant roots selectively recruit specific microbial taxa, resulting in a rhizosphere community distinct from bulk soil. Several microorganisms, including genera such as Pseudomonas, Bacillus, Achromobacter, Burkholderia, Enterobacter, Azotobacter, Methylobacterium, and Trichoderma, are recognized as plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) due to their ability to enhance nutrient availability, stimulate plant growth, and mitigate abiotic stresses [58]. The structure of rhizosphere communities is shaped by plant traits, soil properties, developmental stage, and environmental stressors, including contaminant exposure [59]. Recent studies have also demonstrated that REEs can influence microbial diversity and community structure in plant-associated compartments, highlighting their potential ecological impact on the soil–plant system [54, 60]. In this study, Gd exposure significantly altered bacterial diversity and community composition, suggesting that this element acts as a selective pressure in the rhizosphere environment. The microbial community was initially dominated by the order Burkholderiales, a group widely recognized for its plant growth-promoting capabilities and contribution to stress tolerance. However, Gd treatment led to a marked reduction of this taxon at both sampling times, indicating a potential disruption of beneficial plant–microbe interactions. In contrast, the relative abundance of Actinomycetales increased markedly in Gd-treated samples (from 0.48% in CNT to approximately 8%), suggesting selective enrichment of metal-tolerant microorganisms capable of coping with Gd-induced stress. A decrease in Rhizobiales was also observed, supporting previous findings that metal contamination can negatively affect sensitive microbial taxa, even in the presence of plants [22]. Interestingly, Shannon diversity increased in treated samples, likely reflecting the reduction of dominant sensitive taxa and the concurrent proliferation of microorganisms capable of tolerating or adapting to Gd exposure. These microbiome shifts may also contribute to the physiological responses observed in Gd-treated plants, potentially influencing nutrient availability, oxidative stress regulation, and overall plant performance under REEs exposure [57]. Functional predictions based on PICRUSt2 analysis revealed substantial shifts in the metabolic potential of the bacterial community under Gd exposure. Enrichment of KEGG pathways related to sulfur metabolism, oxidative stress response, DNA repair, metal transport, and membrane-associated functions suggests a functional reorganization toward cellular defense and detoxification mechanisms. Increased activity in DNA repair systems and metal transport proteins likely reflects microbial strategies to mitigate Gd toxicity and maintain cellular integrity. However, the energetic investment required for these stress-response pathways may reduce the resources available for growth-related processes, leading to a functional trade-off that favors microbial survival over plant growth-promoting activities. Moreover, rhizosphere microorganisms can influence metal mobility and bioavailability through processes such as redox transformations, chelation, and biosorption, potentially affecting Gd availability and plant uptake [61]. Recent studies have also demonstrated that microbial activities such as phosphate solubilization can enhance REEs mobilization and accumulation in plants, highlighting the important role of rhizosphere microbiota in regulating REEs cycling in soil–plant systems. In addition to the direct effects of Gd on microbial taxa, the observed community shifts may also be influenced by plant-mediated mechanisms. Physiological stress in plants can modify root exudation patterns, alter the availability of carbon substrates, and signal compounds in the rhizosphere. These changes may further contribute to microbial community restructuring by favoring taxa adapted to the modified chemical environment surrounding stressed roots. Therefore, the microbial changes observed in this study likely result from a combination of direct Gd toxicity and indirect effects mediated by plant physiological responses, as reported for other metal contaminants affecting soil microbial communities [23]. Overall, Gd exposure appears to promote a rhizosphere microbiome that is taxonomically more diverse but functionally oriented toward stress tolerance and detoxification processes. Such shifts may influence long-term plant–microbiome interactions, potentially affecting nutrient acquisition, microbial ecosystem services, and plant resilience under prolonged REE exposure. Overall, these findings support our hypothesis that Gd exposure alters the structure, diversity, and functional potential of the rhizosphere microbiome. Importantly, the observed microbial shifts appear to be linked to plant physiological responses, suggesting a tight coupling between plant stress and microbiome dynamics under Gd exposure. Microbial changes may also contribute to the physiological responses observed in Gd-treated plants, including alterations in nutrient balance and oxidative stress regulation. Although PICRUSt2 provides useful predictive insights into microbial functional potential, it should be noted that PICRUSt2-based functional predictions represent inferred metagenomic potential and require further validation through metagenomic or transcriptomic analyses to confirm these functional shifts and better understand their ecological consequences. Understanding how emerging contaminants such as Gd influence rhizosphere microbial communities is therefore essential to predict their potential ecological impacts on plant–soil systems [1, 62].

Conclusions

The widespread use of Gd-BCAs in MRI has resulted in their increasing release into aquatic environments. Because conventional wastewater treatment processes are largely ineffective at removing Gd, its concentration in surface waters is progressively rising, raising concerns about potential risks to ecosystems, animals, and human health. In this study, we investigated the effects of Gd exposure at a relatively high concentration (150 µM) on tomato plants (Solanum lycopersicum L. cv. MicroTom) grown in a hydroponic system. Our results demonstrate that prolonged Gd exposure negatively affected plant growth, particularly by impairing root development and morphology. The accumulation of Gd in roots, coupled with a reduction in Ca content, suggests potential interference with calcium-dependent physiological processes. Biochemical analyses revealed the activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms, indicating the occurrence of oxidative stress responses in Gd-treated plants. Furthermore, Gd exposure significantly altered the composition and predicted functional profile of the rhizosphere microbiome, promoting microbial communities more oriented toward stress tolerance and detoxification processes rather than plant growth-promoting functions. Overall, these findings highlight that Gd contamination may affect both plant physiology and plant–microbiome interactions, potentially influencing plant performance and soil ecological functioning. Given the increasing environmental occurrence of Gd derived from medical applications, our results underline the need for systematic monitoring of rare earth elements in aquatic and agricultural environments and for the development of improved strategies to limit their release and accumulation in natural ecosystems. Future research should focus on environmentally relevant concentrations of Gd, long-term exposure scenarios, and the role of soil microbial communities in modulating the bioavailability and ecological impact of rare earth elements in plant–soil systems.
