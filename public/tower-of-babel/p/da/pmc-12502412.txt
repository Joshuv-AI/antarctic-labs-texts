Enhanced diagnostic performance for subcentimeter hepatocellular carcinoma using a novel criterion integrating serum AFP levels and gadolinium-based contrast-enhanced MRI features
Authors: Xuying Chen, Haoran Dai, Jing Liu, Siyuan Ji, Yuyao Xiao, Xinde Zheng, Kai Hou, Chun Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo assess the effectiveness of LI-RADS v2018 and r-LI-RADS in diagnosing subcentimeter hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and to evaluate the potential value of serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) in conjunction with gadolinium-based contrast-enhanced MRI (CE-MRI) for assessing these lesions.MethodsThis retrospective study included 179 untreated, high-risk patients with microlesions (< 1 cm) from 2015 to 2023. Of these, 92 lesions were pathologically confirmed as HCC, the remaining 87 were non-HCC. Two radiologists independently rated imaging features using LI-RADS and r-LI-RADS. The optimal AFP threshold for HCC diagnosis was determined by the Youden index. Logistic regression analyses identified independent predictors of micro-HCC, leading to the development of new diagnostic criteria.ResultsMultivariate analysis identified AFP > 12.15 ng/mL, non-peripheral arterial phase enhancement, diffusion restriction, fat deposition, and enhancing capsule as key independent factors for diagnosing micro-HCC. A new criterion requiring at least three positive factors improved sensitivity to 78.3% vs. 58.7% for LR-4 (p = 0.001), with similar specificity (83.9% vs. 81.6%, p = 0.824). It also outperformed r-LR-5/4 and r-LR-4 in sensitivity (78.3% vs. 59.8% and 58.7%, both p = 0.001), without impacting specificity.ConclusionA new criterion (at least three positive findings among AFP > 12.15 ng/mL, non-peripheral arterial phase enhancement, diffusion restriction, fat deposition, and enhancing capsule) significantly improves the diagnostic sensitivity for subcentimeter HCC while maintaining high specificity, demonstrating clear advantages over the LR-4, r-LR-5/4, and r-LR-4 standards of LI-RADS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-025-01949-x.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common primary liver cancers worldwide and has raising global incidence and mortality [1–4]. Early diagnosis is critical to improve patient outcomes, particularly in the identification of subcentimeter HCC [5, 6]. For patients eligible for surgical resection, subcentimeter HCC demonstrates a significantly different overall survival (OS) rate compared to larger tumors, indicating that early diagnosis and treatment can markedly enhance survival [5].

Thus, given that early diagnosis of HCC provides more therapeutic options and can notably improve prognosis in patients suitable for surgery, more sensitive diagnostic tools for detecting subcentimeter HCC remain a critical clinical need [6]. Additionally, with the increasing incidence and mortality associated with HCC, refining current diagnostic criteria, especially for challenging small lesions, would improve patient management and optimize treatment.

Several guidelines currently recommend contrast-enhanced MRI as the primary diagnostic modality for HCC, with gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI (EOB-MRI) being widely used due to its hepatocyte-specific contrast agent [7–9]. However, the diagnosis of subcentimeter HCC on EOB-MRI remains challenging, especially when lesions lack typical enhancement patterns [10].

Recommendations for the diagnosis and treatment of subcentimeter HCC vary by region and organization. The Asia-Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver (APASL) and the Japan Society of Hepatology (JSH) suggest diagnosing HCC based on typical radiologic features regardless of size [11, 12]. Conversely, the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) recommends that subcentimeter lesions with typical imaging features be classified as “likely” HCC, requiring close monitoring [13]. Similarly, the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) highlights the complexity of diagnosing subcentimeter lesions [14].

Serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), a key biomarker for HCC, plays a critical role in the diagnosis of subcentimeter HCC [15]. Although the role of AFP in the diagnosis of HCC is recognized in various guidelines, the focus is primarily on larger lesions [16]. For subcentimeter HCC, the diagnostic value of AFP and its integration with imaging features warrant further investigation.

Previous studies have used hepatobiliary-specific contrast agents to improve the diagnostic accuracy of subcentimeter HCC [5, 17], while fewer studies have examined the diagnostic utility of traditional gadolinium-based contrast agents, such as Gd-DTPA, for small liver cancers. In addition, data-driven approaches have been used to refine the LI-RADS feature system to improve diagnostic sensitivity and specificity [18]; however, these refinements have largely focused on hepatobiliary-specific contrast agents, with less exploration of Gd-DTPA-enhanced MRI in subcentimeter HCC.

The aim of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of a diagnostic algorithm combining serum AFP levels with Gd-DTPA-enhanced MRI imaging features in subcentimeter HCC and to compare it with both the conventional LI-RADS criteria and the data-driven modified r-LI-RADS criteria [18].

Materials and methods

Patient

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Ethics Review Committee of Zhongshan Hospital (Approval No.: B2021-113R). In view of the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

To identify patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), an initial electronic search was conducted within the database for pathology reports containing the term: “hepatocellular carcinoma.” The search yielded a total of 15,254 entries between January 2015 and December 2023. In addition, 11,884 upper abdominal contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (CE-MRI) images were obtained from the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) over the same period for the non-HCC patient cohort.

Inclusion criteria: patients with high-risk factors for liver cancer (including hepatitis B virus (HBV) or hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, excessive alcohol consumption, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and/or cirrhosis due to other causes, and a family history of liver cancer); those who had not undergone prior treatment for HCC; those with no history of other malignancies; those without evidence of vascular liver disease; and those with single subcentimeter lesions.

Exclusion criteria: (a) a time interval exceeding two months between contrast-enhanced MRI and pathological examination; (b) poor quality of MRI images. (c) Lack of serum alpha-fetoprotein level within 1 month of MRI before treatment. ​For the included patients, the serum AFP levels used in the analysis were those measured closest to the date of the MRI scan, with the median interval between AFP measurement and MRI being 3 days (interquartile range [IQR]: 1–7 days; range: 0–28 days).​Furthermore, the exclusion criteria for the non-HCC cohort included patients who had been diagnosed with HCC or other malignancies by pathology, as well as those with follow-up periods of less than 24 months. In conclusion, the analysis included 92 HCC lesions and 87 non-HCC lesions. The patient selection process is depicted in Fig. 1.

MRI protocol

Liver CE-MRI was conducted on participants using a 1.5-tesla MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Aera, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). The MRI protocol included axial T2WI, T1WI, in- and out-of-phase sequences, and DWI. For dynamic imaging, a fat-suppressed T1-weighted 3D volume interpolated breath-hold sequence was employed. The contrast agent gadopentetate dimeglumine (Bayer Healthcare, Germany) was administered at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg and a rate of 2 mL/s, triggered by contrast detection in the descending aorta. The portal venous phase images were acquired 70 to 90 s post-injection, and the delayed phase images were obtained 160 to 180 s post-injection. Detailed sequence parameters are available in previous research literature [19].

Standard of reference

The diagnoses of all HCCs were confirmed by two specialized gastrointestinal pathologists, based on the World Health Organization (WHO) classification criteria from 2010 to 2019 [20, 21]. Tissue samples obtained via hepatectomy underwent histopathological examination. The assessment encompassed a comprehensive range of characteristics, including histological type, nuclear grading, vascular invasion, bile duct invasion, capsule formation, serosal infiltration, and the presence of satellite nodules. All HCCs were graded using the Edmondson and Steiner system following hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining was employed to assess the expression of cytokeratin 19 (CK19) and Ki-67.

Benign lesions required histopathological confirmation or clinical evidence of size stability in cases where no treatment had been administered. In the absence of histopathological confirmation, a diagnosis of benign disease required at least 24 months of continuous follow-up with CE-MRI every three to six months, demonstrating lesion stability, regression, or resolution.

MRI feature evaluation

Two board-certified abdominal radiologists (X.Y.C. and J.L., with 7 and 15 years of professional experience, respectively) independently evaluated the MRI features. All MR images were retrieved from a picture archiving and communication system workstation (Centricity RA1000, General Electric) and de-identified by a radiologist (Y.Y.X.), who did not participate in the subsequent image analysis. To mitigate the potential for bias, the de-identified images were presented to the readers in a randomized order.

The radiologists were blinded to the serum AFP levels during the independent evaluation of the MRI features and assignment of LI-RADS and r-LI-RADS categories. This blinding was maintained to ensure the objective assessment of imaging characteristics without the potential influence of biomarker information.

The integration of serum AFP levels with the imaging features (to apply the newly proposed criterion) was performed only after the independent and blinded imaging assessment was complete. Following the LI-RADS v2018 guidelines, the evaluators measured the maximum diameter of each observation and assessed signal intensity on pre-contrast images, non-peripheral arterial phase hyperenhancement (APHE), non-peripheral washout, enhancing capsule appearance, corona enhancement, T2 low signal intensity, restricted diffusion, fat deposition, fade and iron retention. The mean observation size was employed, and each observation was assigned a LI-RADS category based on the primary features, with adjustments for auxiliary features (AF) [22]. Furthermore, each observation was assigned an r-LI-RADS category in accordance with the data-driven modified r-LI-RADS criteria [18]. In instances where there was a discrepancy in opinion between the readers regarding the imaging features or categories, a consensus was reached through discussion.

Statistical analysis

Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test was employed to compare categorical variables. The optimal threshold for serum AFP in diagnosing HCC was determined by plotting receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and utilizing the Youden index. This threshold was then used to dichotomize the serum AFP levels. Subsequently, univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to evaluate imaging characteristics and serum AFP levels, with the aim of identifying predictive factors for HCC diagnosis. Factors with a p-value less than 0.1 in univariate analysis were included in the subsequent multivariate analysis. For strongly correlated variables with pairwise correlation coefficients exceeding 0.8, the variable with the highest odds ratio (OR) was selected for multivariate analysis. The study reported odds ratios (ORs), their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and two-sided p-values.

Furthermore, the diagnostic performance of LR-4, r-LR-4/5, and r-LR-5 categories, encompassing all newly proposed imaging criteria, was assessed. To compare the sensitivity and specificity of different criteria, McNemar’s test was used. Interobserver agreement was quantified using the kappa statistic (κ); κ values ranging from 0.41 to 0.60 indicated moderate agreement, from 0.61 to 0.80 substantial agreement, and greater than 0.81 excellent agreement. All statistical analyses were conducted using the IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 26.

Results

Table 1 shows baseline characteristics of 179 patients, including 92 with HCC and 87 without HCC. No statistically significant differences were observed in age (p = 0.248) or sex distribution (p = 0.112) between the two groups. However, the presence of cirrhosis was more prevalent in the HCC group than in the non-HCC group (81.5% vs. 58.6%, p = 0.001). The etiology of chronic liver disease differed significantly between the two groups, with a higher prevalence of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection observed in the HCC group (p = 0.002) and metabolic dysfunction associated steatohepatitis (MASH) present only in the non-HCC group (n = 9).

Serum AFP levels were measured a median of 3 days (IQR: 1–7 days; range: 0–28 days) before the MRI examination.​​ AFP levels were significantly higher in the HCC group in comparison to the non-HCC group (median: 32.6 ng/mL vs. 2.9 ng/mL, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the HCC group exhibited a shorter prothrombin time (PT) than the non-HCC group (median: 12.0 s vs. 12.5 s, p = 0.008). Other laboratory parameters, including carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA 19 − 9), platelet count, total bilirubin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and des-gamma-carboxy prothrombin (DCP), did not exhibit significant differences between the groups.

The mean interval between MRI and surgical resection for the 92 patients with HCC was 13.6 ± 8.8 days (range: 1–49 days). With regard to the activity of liver inflammation, 12 patients (13%) were classified as grade I, 63 patients (68.5%) as grade II, and 17 patients (18.5%) as grade III. A total of 126 patients (74.1%) were diagnosed with liver cirrhosis based on histological examination. According to the Edmondson grading system, 6 patients (6.5%) were categorized as grade I, 72 patients (78.3%) as grade II, and 14 patients (15.2%) as grade III (Supplementary Table 1).

Pathologically confirmed non-HCC lesions (n = 21) included two cases of high-grade dysplastic nodules, five cases of focal nodular hyperplasia, one case of biliary adenoma, two cases of angiomyolipoma, two cases of inflammatory pseudotumors, seven cases of hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma, and two cases of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. The remaining 66 lesions (75.9%) were determined to be benign and confirmed through follow-up. During the follow-up period (mean: 35.2 ± 11.4 months; range: 24–68 months), six (10.3%) of the benign lesions were observed to have disappeared.

All 179 subcentimeter observations were classified according to their major features in accordance with the LI-RADS and r-LI-RADS diagnostic algorithms. In our study, 16 observations were classified as LR-2, 83 as LR-3, 70 as LR-4, and 10 as LR-M. Furthermore, 16 observations were classified as r-LR-2, 82 as r-LR-3, 2 as r-LR-4, 70 as r-LR-5, and 13 as r-LR-M.

Serum alpha-fetoprotein levels

In the HCC cohort, the area under the curve (AUC) for AFP was 0.781 (95% CI: 0.708–0.856) (Fig. 2). Using the currently recommended clinical cutoff of 20 ng/mL, the sensitivity of AFP for detecting subcentimeter HCC was 50%. However, if the optimal cutoff point was defined as the point where the sum of sensitivity plus specificity reached the maximum value on the ROC curve for the HCC cohort, the best performance of AFP was observed at a cutoff point of 12.15 ng/mL, achieving a sensitivity of 60.9% and a specificity of 93.6%.

Univariable and multivariable analyses of imaging features and serum AFP levels

The univariable analysis demonstrated that an elevated serum AFP level (> 12.15 ng/mL) and all MRI features, except for iron sparing and fade, were significant predictors of subcentimeter HCC (Table 3). Specifically, the following MRI characteristics showed statistical significance: T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) hypointensity (OR = 2.348, 95% CI: 1.207–4.566, p = 0.012); T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) mild-to-moderate hyperintensity (OR = 3.340, 95% CI: 1.716–6.503, p < 0.001); restricted diffusion (OR = 8.759, 95% CI: 3.442–22.291, p < 0.001); fat deposition (OR = 2.332, 95% CI: 1.087–5.002, p = 0.03); non-peripheral arterial phase hyperenhancement (Norim APHE) (OR = 3.038, 95% CI: 1.454–6.348, p = 0.003); non-peripheral washout on portal venous phase (PVP) and delay phase (DP) (ORs = 4.780 and 3.741, respectively, with 95% CIs: 2.366–9.655 and 1.973–7.095, p < 0.001 for both); and enhancing capsule (OR = 15.033, 95% CI: 5.579–40.507, p < 0.001).

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analysis for the diagnosis of subcentimeter HCC

In the multivariable analysis, AFP > 12.15 ng/mL (OR = 12.339, 95% CI: 4.555–33.438, p < 0.001), Norim APHE (OR = 4.389, 95% CI: 1.320–14.590, p = 0.016), restricted diffusion (OR = 10.671, 95% CI: 3.117–36.533, p < 0.001), fat deposition (OR = 3.578, 95% CI: 1.256–10.189, p = 0.017), and enhancing capsule (OR = 16.815, 95% CI: 4.080-69.303, p < 0.001) emerged as independent predictors of subcentimeter HCC. Notably, in the adjusted model, T1WI hypointensity and T2WI mild-to-moderate hyperintensity did not retain their initial statistical significance, while iron sparing and fade were not included in the final multivariable model due to lack of significance in the univariable analysis.

Diagnostic efficacy of fat deposition​

Given the significant association of fat deposition with HCC despite its lower incidence (21.7% in HCC vs. 9.2% in non-HCC, p = 0.03), we further calculated its standalone diagnostic performance. The positive likelihood ratio (LR+) for fat deposition was ​2.36​ (95% CI: 1.12–4.98), indicating that a lesion with fat deposition is approximately 2.4 times more likely to be HCC than a non-HCC lesion.

Evaluation of diagnostic criteria for subcentimeter HCC

Five independent predictive factors generated 32 unique combinations (Supplementary Table 2). From these combinations, 29 distinct diagnostic criteria were formulated (Supplementary Table 3). These criteria were then compared with the LI-RADS 4 (LR-4) category and the revised LI-RADS 4/5 (r-LI-RADS 4/5) and 5 (r-LI-RADS 5) categories. Table 4 summarizes the diagnostic performance of the major new criteria for subcentimeter hepatocellular carcinoma derived from consensus reading. The traditional LR-4 category had a sensitivity of 58.7% (95% CI: 48.0-68.7; 54/92) and a specificity of 81.6% (95% CI: 71.6–88.8; 71/87). The revised r-LI-RADS 4/5 category demonstrated slightly improved sensitivity at 59.8% (95% CI: 49.0-69.7; 55/92) and a slight decrease in specificity to 80.5% (95% CI: 70.3–87.9; 70/87). The r-LI-RADS 5 category had similar performance to LR-4, with a sensitivity of 58.7% (95% CI: 48.0-68.7; 54/92) and a specificity of 81.6% (95% CI: 71.6–88.8; 71/87).

Among the novel criteria, Criterion A, which required all five findings (APHE, restricted diffusion, fat deposition, enhancing capsule, and AFP > 12.15 ng/mL), showed a low sensitivity of 6.5% (95% CI: 2.7–14.2; 6/92) but a very high specificity of 100.0% (95% CI: 94.7–100.0; 87/87) (p < 0.001 for both sensitivity and specificity compared to LR-4, r-LI-RADS 4/5, and r-LI-RADS 5).

Criterion G, requiring at least four of the five findings, demonstrated a sensitivity of 32.6% (95% CI: 23.4–43.3; 30/92) and a specificity of 97.7% (95% CI: 91.2–99.6; 85/87) (p < 0.001 for both sensitivity and specificity compared to LR-4, r-LI-RADS 4/5, and r-LI-RADS 5).

Criterion R, which required at least three of the five findings, showed a significant improvement in sensitivity to 78.3% (95% CI: 68.2–85.9; 72/92) while maintaining a high specificity at 83.9% (95% CI: 74.1–90.6; 73/87) (p = 0.001 for sensitivity and p = 0.824 for specificity compared to LR-4, p = 0.001 for sensitivity and p = 0.664 for specificity compared to r-LI-RADS 4/5, and p = 0.001 for sensitivity and p = 0.824 for specificity compared to r-LI-RADS 5). These results indicate that Criterion R can significantly improve the diagnostic sensitivity for subcentimeter HCCs while maintaining a high level of specificity, demonstrating a clear advantage over the LR-4, r-LI-RADS 5, and r-LI-RADS 4/5 standards (Figs. 3 and 4).

Discussion

Our study identified independent predictive factors for subcentimeter hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), including alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels exceeding 12.15 ng/mL, non-peripheral arterial enhancement, diffusion restriction, fat deposition, and the presence of an enhancing capsule. Based on these findings, we propose a novel diagnostic criterion requiring at least three of the five features to be positive. This criterion maintains high specificity and significantly improves diagnostic sensitivity for subcentimeter HCC compared with LI-RADS LR-4, r-LR-5, and r-LR-4/5 criteria.

Guidelines remain cautions to the radiological diagnosis of subcentimeter HCC, partly because existing imaging standards were primarily designed for tumors ≥ 1 cm and do not fully account for the unique radiological and pathophysiological characteristics of subcentimeter lesions [6]. A reliable and targeted diagnostic method is thus crucial for improving detection and treatment of this disease.

Although AFP is commonly used internationally for HCC screening, few studies have explored its effectiveness in diagnosing subcentimeter HCC. Recent Western scholarship has questioned AFP’s sensitivity and specificity as a screening marker and excluded it from the latest HCC diagnostic guidelines [23, 24]. Conversely, the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) recommends periodic reevaluation of AFP and ultrasound for liver nodules < 10 mm [25]. Asian guidelines generally recommend biannual ultrasound examinations and serum AFP measurements [26, 27].

Given the correlation between AFP levels and tumor burden, we determined a new AFP threshold of 12.15 ng/mL, which demonstrated a sensitivity of 60.9% and a specificity of 93.6% for diagnosing subcentimeter HCC. This finding aligns with previous research, such as that by Huang et al. [28], who established an AFP threshold of 13.7 ng/mL, showing similar diagnostic performance. Notably, this threshold is comparable to the currently recommended clinical threshold of 20 ng/mL, with sensitivity ranging from 58% to 68% and specificity from 80% to 94% [15].

The sensitivity of LI-RADS LR-4, r-LR-5, and r-LR-4/5 criteria in diagnosing subcentimeter HCC is less than optimal, potentially due to the lower frequency of portal venous phase (PVP) and delay phase (DP) clearance. In our study, PVP and DP clearance were not considered independent predictive factors for subcentimeter HCC. However, Huang et al. [5, 28] included low signal intensity during the PVP and TP in their diagnostic criteria, possibly related to the use of liver-specific contrast agents (Gd-EOB-DTPA). We used extracellular contrast agents (Gd-DTPA), which differ significantly in lesion-to-liver contrast (LLC) during the PVP and TP, likely due to pharmacokinetics and timing.

In accordance with the 2018 LI-RADS classification system, PVP is defined as the point at which the portal vein is filled with contrast and the liver parenchyma exhibits slight enhancement, typically occurring around 60 s after injection [26]. The TP is defined as the point at which the signal intensity of the liver vessels is equivalent to that of the liver parenchyma, with scanning commencing approximately two to five minutes after injection. Some Asian guidelines permit the concurrent use of PVP and TP for the assessment of “non-peripheral clearance,” whereas the majority of major international guidelines allow only PVP for this purpose [29]. The LLC during PVP/TP is constituted of two elements: the clearance of contrast within the lesion and the progressive enhancement of the surrounding liver parenchyma [30]. Following the administration of Gd-EOB-DTPA, hepatic cells commence the absorption of the agent approximately three minutes later, reaching a maximum level of enhancement within a timeframe of 10 to 20 min. Hepatic cells exhibit a slower rate of Gd-DTPA uptake, with maximum enhancement occurring approximately two hours post-injection. Therefore, Gd-EOB-DTPA displays superior efficiency at PVP compared to the arterial phase (AP), reaching optimal levels at TP, which surpasses the efficiency of Gd-DTPA.

Enhancing capsules are considered a distinctive feature of HCC, with reported incidences ranging from 43% to 64% [31]. The biological relevance of such imaging features is further supported by studies correlating MRI characteristics with molecular markers of tumor aggressiveness [32]. An enhancing capsule is typically defined as a smooth and uniform margin surrounding a lesion, becoming evident on images obtained during the PVP or the TP/delayed phase (DP). Consistent with previous studies, our research confirms the enhancing capsule as an independent predictive factor. This finding may differ from prior investigations due to the use of different contrast agents.

Although fat deposition was observed in only 21.7% (20/92) of subcentimeter HCCs, its high multivariable OR (3.578, p = 0.017) and significant LR+ (2.36) support its role as a specific predictor. This aligns with prior studies reporting fat deposition in 10–40% of small HCCs, where it serves as a specific but non-sensitive marker [33, 34]. In our proposed criterion requiring ≥ 3/5 features, fat deposition contributes primarily to ​specificity enhancement​ (e.g., Criterion G specificity: 97.7%). Its inclusion is justified not as a standalone requirement, but as one of multiple options to achieve the composite endpoint, thereby mitigating its low sensitivity while leveraging its high specificity.

Previous studies have shown that enhancing capsules are more visible with Gd-DTPA than with Gd-EOB-DTPA in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [35]. Gd-DTPA is an extracellular contrast agent, while Gd-EOB-DTPA is a liver-specific agent that is selectively absorbed by hepatocytes in addition to the vascular system [36]. As a result, Gd-EOB-DTPA leads to greater signal enhancement intensity and speed in the liver parenchyma compared to Gd-DTPA, potentially masking the enhancing capsule, especially during the PVP and TP phases. Additionally, the lower dose of Gd-EOB-DTPA used may contribute to less pronounced visibility of enhancing capsules in MRI.

​Limitations of this study should be acknowledged.​​ First, the retrospective single-center design inherently carries risks of ​selection bias​ and limits generalizability. Second, the ​sample size of non-HCC lesions was relatively limited (n = 87)​, and only a subset (n = 21) had pathological confirmation, though follow-up is a standard criterion for benignity. Third, while the identified ​AFP threshold (> 12.15 ng/mL)​​ demonstrated diagnostic value, its implementation in practice may be influenced by ​variability across different commercial assay kits; standardization or kit-specific validation would be necessary for broader application. Finally, the predominance of HBV etiology and the modest prevalence of features like fat deposition warrant consideration in interpreting the results. ​Prospective, multicenter validation in larger, diverse cohorts is essential to confirm the clinical utility of the proposed criterion.

Conclusion

A new criterion requiring at least three of the five features (AFP > 12.15 ng/mL, non-peripheral arterial enhancement, diffusion restriction, fat deposition, and enhancing capsule) to be positive significantly enhances the diagnostic sensitivity for subcentimeter HCC while maintaining high specificity, offering a distinct advantage over LI-RADS LR-4, r-LR-5, and r-LR-4/5 criteria.
