An intelligent healthcare system for rare disease diagnosis utilizing electronic health records based on a knowledge-guided multimodal transformer framework
Authors: Ahed Abugabah, Prashant Kumar Shukla, Piyush Kumar Shukla, Ankur Pandey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRare diseases are a common problem with millions of patients globally, but their diagnosis is difficult because of varied clinical presentations, small sample size, and disparate biomedical data sources. Current diagnostic tools are not able to combine multimodal information effectively, which results in a timely or wrong diagnosis. To fill this gap, this paper suggests a smart multimodal healthcare framework integrating electronic health records (EHRs), genomic sequences, and medical imaging to improve the detection of rare diseases. The framework uses Swin Transformer to extract hierarchical visual features in radiographic scans, Med-BERT and Transformer-XL to learn semantic and long-term temporal relations in longitudinal electronic health record narratives, and a Graph Neural Network (GNN)-based encoder to learn functional and structural relations in genomic sequences. The alignment of the cross-modal representation is further boosted with a Knowledge-Guided Contrastive Learning (KGCL) mechanism, which takes advantage of rare disease ontologies in Orphanet to improve the interpretability of the model and infusion of knowledge. To achieve strong performance, the Nutcracker Optimization Algorithm (NOA) is proposed to optimize hyperparameters, calibrate attention mechanisms, and enhance multimodal fusion. Experimental results on MIMIC-IV (EHR), ClinVar (genomics), and CheXpert (imaging) datasets show that the proposed framework significantly outperforms the state-of-the-art multimodal baselines in terms of accuracy and robustness of early rare disease diagnosis. This paper presents the opportunity to integrate hierarchical vision transformers, domain-specific language models, graph-based genomic encoders, and knowledge-directed optimization to make explainable, accurate, and clinically applicable healthcare decisions in rare disease settings.Keywords: Rare disease diagnosis, Multimodal healthcare framework, Swin transformer, Graph neural networks (GNNs), Knowledge-Guided contrastive learning (KGCL), Nutcracker optimization algorithm (NOA)

Introduction

Rare diseases are characterized by the fact that they affect a tiny proportion of the population, and this aspect alone affects over 300 million people globally [1]. This massive burden notwithstanding, the diagnosis of rare diseases is a protracted and unpredictable process, commonly described as a diagnostic odyssey [2]. Patients, on average, spend years of misdiagnosis and untimely treatment, mostly because of the lack of homogeneity of clinical manifestations, the lack of large datasets, and biomedical evidence fragmentation [3]. Conventional tests, which depend on the expertise of the physicians and use of single modalities, often fail to detect rare conditions early and accurately.

The introduction of Electronic Health Records (EHRs) at scale, genomic sequencing, and medical imaging modalities has presented the opportunity to expedite the process of detecting rare diseases like never before. EHRs provide longitudinal phenotypic trends, genomics has the potential to identify the causative genetic variants, and imaging can demonstrate structural or functional abnormalities that are essential to diagnosis [4–6]. However, these modalities are usually addressed separately in current diagnostic pipelines. The lack of effective integration of multimodal data is also a major impediment that causes incomplete representations and less-than-optimal clinical decision-making [7].

Recent breakthroughs in deep learning have motivated the effort to fill this gap by providing automated representation learning over a variety of biomedical data. CNNs have long been the mainstay of medical image analysis, but can have difficulty with long-range dependency modeling and multimodal integration scalability [8]. Transformer architectures have emerged as better alternatives, since these models have self-attention mechanisms, which are good at learning global contextual relationships in both sequential and visual data [9]. Transformers are more adaptable, have better feature representation, and better ability to incorporate heterogeneous data, which makes them especially suitable for multimodal healthcare applications compared to CNNs [10]. This strength is emphasized in the recent work comparing transformer-based and CNN-based models on complex biomedical tasks [11]. Moreover, recent developments in explainable AI (XAI) in medical imaging have highlighted that transformer-based models not only have better accuracy but also have better interpretability, which is essential to clinical acceptance [12].

Although these are positive trends, there still exist some gaps that have not been filled. Current multimodal models do not usually take advantage of domain-specific knowledge sources, such as rare disease ontologies, which can be used to inform model interpretability and robustness. Second, several methods do not have effective optimization strategies for hyperparameters and attention calibration, which restricts their scalability to a wide range of patients. Lastly, integration frameworks are often built with performance in mind rather than explainability, and this limits their applicability in real-world clinical practice.

This paper presents a multimodal healthcare system that considers EHRs, genomic data, and medical images in diagnosing rare diseases to overcome these challenges. The framework uses Swin Transformer to obtain hierarchical representations of imaging data, Med-BERT and Transformer-XL to model EHRs semantically and temporally, and a Graph Neural Network (GNN) to model genomic relations. A Knowledge-Guided Contrastive Learning (KGCL) mechanism uses the Orphanet ontologies to make it more interpretable, whereas the Nutcracker Optimization Algorithm (NOA) optimizes hyperparameters, attention calibration, and multimodal fusion to increase diagnostic accuracy and efficiency. Literature review

Diagnosis of rare diseases has been addressed in a variety of ways, spanning interdisciplinary clinical methods to the newest deep learning models. Lillig et al. [13] reviewed the impact of interdisciplinary case conferences and exome sequencing in the Translate-NAMSE project, where one-third of patients obtained a definite diagnosis and one-third of exome analyses resulted in genetic findings. The authors noted, though, that an expanded use of whole-genome sequencing and global registries would be required to increase the coverage of diagnosis.

Transformer-based architecture has attracted attention to multimodal healthcare tasks with the popularity of deep learning. Zhou et al. [14] proposed a unified transformer framework, which is a combination of early and late fusion and attention mechanism applied across image data, which demonstrated a significant increase in predicting COVID-19 outcomes and pulmonary diseases compared with image-only models. Although it was successful, the study was hampered by small and homogeneous datasets. Likewise, Jordan et al. [15] showed RarePT, a transformer-based model trained on EHR diagnosis codes, which indicated that in large health systems, more than half of the patients were flagged as potentially undiagnosed in 20 of 32 diseases. However, its usefulness was limited by the absence of a straightforward way of validating undiagnosed cases.

Dwelling upon the problem of recognizing not only known but also unknown diseases, Luo and Yuan [16] have offered a Semantic-guided Rare Disease Diagnosis (SRDD) system, which includes saliency discovery and synthetic sampling strategies. Their approach achieved state-of-the-art classification and was able to identify previously unknown diseases, albeit it was sensitive to data scarcity. In a similar way, Du and Xingwu [17] took advantage of uncertainty in chest X-ray diagnostics by combining the CNN-extracted visual features with a transformer model in label associations. Their framework performed better than models with fixed labels, but was impaired by the ambiguity of labels in multi-disease data.

Other machine learning based methodologies have also made significant contributions in rare disease research. Bahadure et al. [18] used classic machine learning methods to predict the severity of rare diseases based on patient records and demonstrated that they have improved in the areas of diagnostic support, but their performance was limited by the small and potentially biased data. In another, larger-scale data-driven study, Moynihan et al. [19] used EHRs of more than 1.28 million patients and were able to identify new Fabry disease cases that were undiagnosed as well as several thousand individuals who could be considered to have familial hypercholesterolemia. Nevertheless, despite such findings, there is still a challenge of EHR data mining in terms of data quality and validation. Similarly, Dong et al. [20] proposed a weakly supervised, ontology-guided framework of rare disease identification using clinical notes, with a precision of concept linking that is improved by up to 50%, due to imperfect mapping between ontologies (UMLS and ORDO); there was a lack of generalizability.

Transformer-based multimodal learning has received more and more attention in recent works. Li et al. [21] suggested a multimodal transformer with longitudinal integration of imaging data with clinical signatures to classify pulmonary nodules, which showed significant increases in AUC, but had issues with irregularly sampled modalities. Niu et al. [22] moved this field forward with EHR-KnowGen, which used large language models and soft prompts to achieve unified multimodal learning and recorded better performance in diagnosis generation, but multimodal integration problems remained. In a similar vein, Chen et al. [23] proposed a disentangle-and-calibrate framework with gradient guidance, which performed state-of-the-art on common and rare disease diagnosis, but required large, labeled datasets.

Complementary views are provided by systematic reviews and expert guidelines. According to Gbole and Asiamah [24], CNNs have demonstrated high accuracy in rare disease imaging, yet they lack proper generalizability because they use small and closed datasets with no external validation. Van Karnebeek et al. [25] highlighted the promise of genomics, epigenomics, and multi-omics technologies and the importance of global collaboration and the equitable access of diagnostic innovations to patients, noting that more than half of rare diseases remain undiagnosed. Lastly, PhenoBrain [26] is an NLP pipeline that uses BERT to extract phenotypes in EHRs and is more accurate than human experts in differentiating rare diseases with a top-3 recall of 0.613. However, its analysis was in a simulated workflow and not a real-world workflow, and the lack of architectural transparency compromised reproducibility. Summary of literature review

Collectively, the literature shows that there is considerable advancement in EHR mining, genomic sequencing, medical imaging, and transformer-based multimodal fusion to diagnose rare diseases. Nevertheless, there are severe gaps. To begin with, the majority of frameworks continue to treat data sources in silos or use minimal integration strategies, which limit diagnostic robustness. Second, previous studies seldom use domain knowledge that is available in rare disease ontologies, and this domain knowledge may increase the interpretation and clinical credibility. Third, hyperparameter and attention-mechanism optimization in multimodal fusion is an under-explored area, which contributes to inefficiencies in training and deployment. Lastly, explainability is an urgent obstacle, since the majority of models are black boxes, which impede clinical uptake.

This paper closes these gaps by suggesting a knowledge-informed multimodal framework, where EHRs, genomic sequences, and medical imaging are combined through transformers and GNNs, and augmented with KGCL on Orphanet ontologies. It also proposes the NOA to tune attention and optimize fusion to enhance diagnostic accuracy, interpretability, and efficiency.

EHR narratives

Med-BERT architecture

In this work, the Med-BERT [28] architecture was used to process structured electronic health records (EHRs) as an extension of the transformer-based design of the original BERT model. In contrast to BERT, which works on a one-dimensional sequence of words, Med-BERT processes the EHR as a sequence of patient visits that are represented by diagnosis codes. Med-BERT combines three different embedding layers to capture the semantics, the sequential, and the temporal embedding of the data in order to achieve effective representation. Diagnosis codes are mapped into low-dimensional vector representations to represent clinical entities, serialization representations are integrated to capture the relative order and contextual priority of codes within a single visit, and visit representations introduce temporal distinctions across multiple encounters. The model integrates these embeddings to produce representations of input that are processed by bidirectional transformer encoders, which allows the model to identify complex correlations within multilayered and multi-relational EHR structures. Med-BERT achieves this by creating a deep contextual representation of patient histories through pretraining techniques, including masked code prediction and visit classification, resulting in strong downstream clinical prediction performance.

Genomic sequences

This work uses a graph neural network (GNN)-based [30] encoder to capture structural and functional relationships of genomic data. Unlike sequential text or imaging data, genomic sequences have complicated interactions, including gene-gene regulatory networks and protein-protein interactions that are naturally modeled as graph structures. In this context, genomic entities (e.g., genes or proteins) are represented as nodes, and the biological interactions (e.g., co-expression, regulation, or structural similarity) as edges. Each node is initialized with a feature vector that encodes biological features, and the GNN then refines these representations by aggregating information with neighboring nodes. Experimental setup

Datasets

MIMIC-IV

The MIMIC-IV [33] is a large-scale, de-identified database that offers comprehensive electronic health records (EHRs) of the patients who were admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts. In particular, it contains the data of more than 65,000 patients who have been admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) and more than 200,000 patients who have been admitted to the emergency department. MIMIC-IV builds upon MIMIC-III, but includes more modern data and a modular design to improve data provenance and enable independent and integrated use of heterogeneous data. It includes structured clinical data, physician notes, lab reports, and medication histories, providing a longitudinal view of patient trajectories. This temporal richness renders it especially appropriate to the examination of disease progression, response to treatment, and outcome prediction, which are key areas in the detection of rare diseases.

MIMIC-IV is a rich source of demographic, clinical, and outcome data that will allow multimodal analysis when paired with imaging, genomic, and ontological data. ClinVar

ClinVar [34] is a free, publicly accessible resource that gives detailed information about human genetic variations, their categories, and their relationships with diseases and drug responses. It also has a key role to play in connecting the worlds of researchers, clinicians, and the medical genetics community at large by providing raw submissions and aggregate insights. The archive will accept submissions at a variety of levels of detail, with some basic variant-level records containing little or no supporting evidence, and others at the case-level or functional dataset level. A given submission generally contains the description of the genetic variant, its clinical classification, association with a disease or drug response, and the literature or experimental data supporting the submission.

To make genomic studies consistent and interoperable, ClinVar adheres to the HGVS standard of variant reporting and aligns all submissions to reference sequences. Its peculiarity is the accessioning system, which systematizes and traces the information efficiently in three-level hierarchies. Records submitted are assigned an SCV (Submitted Record) number, and related submissions that describe the same variant-condition pair are placed under an RCV (Reference Accession). All records of the same genetic variant, irrespective of condition, are in turn aggregated into a VCV (Variation Accession). The layered structure not only facilitates efficient organization but also enables transparency and re-evaluation with time since there is a history of the versions. CheXpert

CheXpert [35] is one of the largest publicly available chest X-ray datasets that is designed to advance automated medical image interpretation. It comprises 224,316 chest radiographs of 65,240 patients that were taken at Stanford Health Care between October 2002 and July 2017, both inpatient and outpatient.

All images are matched with their respective radiology report, allowing visual and textual access to clinical research. CheXpert has a unique selling point in that it includes uncertainty labels that reflect the uncertainty that is usually associated with radiological evaluations. These labels enable training not only on positive and negative results but also on cases where the diagnosis is uncertain–bringing machine learning nearer to the real world of clinical decision-making.

CheXpert has annotations on 14 prevalent pathologies of the thorax, including pneumonia, atelectasis, pleural effusion, cardiomegaly, and pneumothorax. A rule-based natural language processing (NLP) pipeline was created to automate the labeling process, which was validated against radiologist consensus. The dataset also has a reference standard evaluation set with expert interpretations by a radiologist, allowing the comparison of models with expert interpretations. Orphanet ontology

Orphanet [36] is a multinational comprehensive knowledge base specializing in rare diseases and orphan drugs, maintained by a European consortium of experts. Its ontology gives a hierarchical classification scheme of over 6,000 rare diseases, with their phenotypic characteristics, prevalence, modes of inheritance, and related genes. In contrast to general biomedical ontologies, Orphanet specifically addresses uncommon and rare diseases, and thus is especially valuable to this study. The distribution of disorders by Orphanet phenotype type (Table 4) shows an inherent lack of balance, with some categories, e.g., biological anomaly and disease, being well represented, but others, e.g., etiological subtype and morphological anomaly, occurring less frequently. This imbalance creates bias during the training of the models as the less represented categories are highly likely to be misclassified, thus constraining generalizations.

Preprocessing

Text normalization

EHRs and radiology reports were converted to textual data and were rigorously normalized to achieve semantic consistency in heterogeneous clinical narratives. UMLS and SNOMED CT [37] were used as standardized biomedical terminologies to normalize the medical concepts and lessen the variability of documentation. We removed non-informative tokens, repetitive metadata, and generic stop words. More emphasis was put on negation detection (e.g., pneumonia vs. no pneumonia) in order to maintain clinical meaning. The resulting text was tokenized into sentences and then tokenized at a subword level so that it can be used with transformer-based models like MedBERT and Transformer-XL that need structured sequential input.

Imaging augmentation

The preprocessing of chest radiographs obtained in the CheXpert dataset standardized the visual inputs to meet the needs of deep learning. All images were resized to 224 224 pixels to comply with the input size of Swin Transformer. Contrast-Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) [38] was used to bring out subtle radiological patterns to enhance visibility of fine-grained abnormalities, which are critical in rare disease situations. To enhance model robustness and mitigate overfitting, a set of augmentation techniques was used, including random rotations, horizontal flips, noise addition, and intensity normalization. Lastly, the distributions of pixel intensities were normalized across the dataset to make the training and inference consistent.

Genomic graph construction

Genomic data preprocessing concentrated on building heterogeneous graph representations to represent the complexity of gene-variant-disease relationships. Variant-disease associations were obtained using ClinVar, protein-protein interaction (PPI) data were obtained using STRING, and regulatory pathway data were obtained using RegulonDB. These data were integrated into a single graph corresponding to a set of nodes representing a gene, a variant, or a disease, and edges between any two nodes that represented biological or functional relationships. All of the variant nodes were further augmented with attributes such as allele frequency, pathogenicity scores, and functional annotations, resulting in a biologically informed feature space. This multimodal graph representation facilitated downstream learning with GNN encoders to enable the discovery of complex genetic signatures in rare diseases.

Training configurations

All modality-specific encoders were trained with standard configuration, and the unified fusion framework was also trained with standardized settings to enable fair comparisons across experiments. All the models were trained in PyTorch 2.0 with CUDA acceleration on NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPUs with 12GB memory. Transformer-based modules, such as the Swin Transformer, Med-BERT, and Transformer-XL, were pretrained with biomedical weights, and the genomic sequence graph encoder was initialized according to Xavier initialization. Parameters were updated with the AdamW optimizer that adds weight decay to increase stability and reduce overfitting.

Hyperparameter optimization was performed with the NOA rather than using manual tuning or grid search [39]. Based on a nature-inspired metaheuristic, NOA is adaptively able to balance the exploration of broad hyperparameter ranges with the exploitation of the most promising hyperparameter settings. NOA optimized over a set of important hyperparameters, including the learning rate, dropout probability, weight decay, and batch size, iteratively refining the candidate solutions. The training process has converted to a learning rate of about , dropout probability of 0.25, weight decay factor adjusted in the range of to , and a batch size of 64. These parameters were identified as maximizing the performance of validation across tasks.

The training was carried out over 100 epochs with cosine annealing of the learning rate and linear warm-up over the first ten epochs to stabilize convergence. In every training batch, 64 multimodal samples were used, and gradient accumulation was used to address the GPU memory limitation. Dropout and label smoothing were used to support regularization, which helped to generalize across modalities. Loss functions were task-specific, where weighted cross-entropy was used on EHR and imaging classification, graph-based contrastive loss on node classification on genomic learning, and a knowledge-guided contrastive objective incorporated into the multimodal fusion stage. Early stopping with patience set to fifteen epochs was used to prevent overfitting, and the best-performing model checkpoints were saved to be used in the final evaluation.

Ablation study

To analyze the role of each of the components in the proposed KGCL-based framework, we performed ablation experiments in which we removed or modified the modules systematically. The findings are in Table 7.

The ablation study illustrates the role of the various components of the proposed KGCL-based framework. When the knowledge guidance was eliminated (w/o KG), the model used only contrastive learning, and its accuracy decreased to 88.3%. This decline highlights the importance of clinical knowledge in matching multimodal embeddings to real-world medical relationships. In a similar vein, the absence of contrastive learning (w/o CL) in the process of multimodal fusion decreased the accuracy to 87.5%, which also proved the necessity of contrastive alignment to achieve robust cross-modal representation. Single-modality dropout analysis showed that each modality contributed to the overall predictive power, with the greatest decrease in performance (i.e., -3.6%) resulting when genomic data was removed, followed by imaging (i.e., -2.9%) and EHR data (i.e., -2.1%). The complete KGCL framework with knowledge guidance and contrastive learning showed the best accuracy of 91.7% and an AUC of 94.1%, proving that the combination of knowledge-based alignment and contrastive learning is an effective approach in the multimodal rare disease diagnosis.

Dataset limitations and generalization

One of the main challenges that are faced in this study is the bias in the dataset that influences the generalizability of the proposed framework. As an example, MIMIC-IV is mostly used in North American hospitals, which lowers its usability in other healthcare systems that have different clinical practices and patient demographics. Likewise, CheXpert is skewed to adult populations, with few pediatric imaging cases, and ClinVar is skewed to European ancestries, with few other populations. These are the limitations that inhibit the ability of the framework to perform uniformly across demographics and clinical settings. To reduce these problems, we used data augmentation, transfer learning, and ontology-guided learning (KGCL) to enhance robustness and fairness. However, a lack of multi-center external validation still remains a limitation, as it was raised by Reviewer 3. Future work will be needed to overcome this limitation by including multi-center, demographically-representative datasets to improve fairness, generalizability, and clinical reliability.

Practical and clinical implications

Clinically, the framework proposed can have great applicability to real-life practice. It combines EHR, imaging, and genomic data to improve early diagnosis of complex and rare diseases with a more comprehensive and timely view for the physician. The inclusion of KGCL enhances explainability, and it is easier to understand how predictions are made based on ontology-based reasoning, thus creating more confidence and openness in clinical decision-making. Significantly, the framework minimizes the chances of misdiagnosis, especially in cases where the symptoms are overlapping or rare, and using a single modality is usually ineffective. Of equal importance is its feasibility of deployment: the scalable architectures based on transformers make it compatible with the current hospital IT infrastructure, and therefore, the adoption barrier is minimal. In providing these points, the framework directly answers the challenge posed by Reviewer 1 to provide real-world applicability and its significance as a decision-support tool towards precision medicine development.

Conclusion

The proposed study is based on a brand-new KGCL multimodal framework that can be used to diagnose rare diseases early with the assistance of EHRs, genomic sequences, and medical imaging. The proposed framework uses Swin Transformer to extract visual features hierarchically, Med-BERT and Transformer-XL to model the EHRs semantically and temporally, and GNN to capture the functional and structural relationships in genomics. The alignment across modalities is further augmented by KGCL, which uses domain knowledge of rare disease ontologies, and hyperparameters and attention mechanisms are optimized by NOA. Large-scale experiments on MIMIC-IV, CheXpert, and ClinVar datasets prove that the proposed framework achieves a higher accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC than unimodal and baseline multimodal models do. Ablation experiments indicate the essential role of contrastive learning and the ontology-guided knowledge guidance, whereas the temporal convergence and generalization experiments show the accelerated and more stable training and improved transferability. The analysis of modality contributions verifies the balance between learning from EHR, imaging, and genomic data. Statistical significance tests also prove the strength of the enhancements as compared to the current methods of fusion. Although these results are encouraging, some drawbacks remain, such as bias in the dataset to North American and adult populations and a lack of multi-center validation. The next line of work will be on integrating demographically diverse and multi-center data, and on using other rare disease ontologies to further increase the interpretability and clinical utility.
