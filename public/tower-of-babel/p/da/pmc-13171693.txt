Icotrokinra: An Oral Interleukin-23 Receptor Antagonist Peptide for the Treatment of Psoriasis
Authors: César Ferreira, Tiago Torres
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease with substantial impact on quality of life. While biologic therapies targeting interleukin (IL)-23 and IL-17 have set new efficacy and safety benchmarks, currently available oral agents offer limited potency or tolerability. There remains an unmet need for an oral therapy that combines the convenience of oral administration with the efficacy and safety of biologic agents. Icotrokinra, a first-in-class, orally administered macrocyclic peptide that selectively targets the IL-23 receptor (IL-23R), has been evaluated across phase 2 and 3 clinical trials in moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis. This review aimed to provide an overview of icotrokinra in psoriasis, based on a literature search up to December 2025 using PubMed and supplemented by conference abstracts, industry communications, and ClinicalTrials.gov. In the phase 2b FRONTIER-1 trial, icotrokinra demonstrated a clear dose–response relationship, with sustained efficacy through 52 weeks in the FRONTIER-2 long-term extension. In the pivotal phase 3 ICONIC-ADVANCE-1 and ICONIC-ADVANCE-2 studies, icotrokinra was superior to placebo in achieving the primary endpoints of Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA) score 0/1 (clear or almost clear skin) and ≥ 90% improvement in the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI90) at week 16, and demonstrated greater efficacy than deucravacitinib at week 24, while maintaining a favorable safety profile. Long-term results from ICONIC-LEAD confirmed durable efficacy through 52 weeks, while ICONIC-TOTAL demonstrated consistent outcomes in scalp, genital, and palmoplantar psoriasis. Adverse events were mostly mild and comparable to placebo, with no new safety signals or pharmacokinetic concerns. Icotrokinra is the first oral IL-23R antagonist to achieve high and durable levels of skin clearance with a favorable safety profile, highlighting the potential of selective oral IL-23R blockade with minimal off-target or drug–drug interaction potential, thereby overcoming key limitations of oral small molecules.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disorder of the skin and joints, affecting approximately 2–3% of the global population [1]. It is driven by complex dysregulation of the innate and adaptive immune systems, resulting in keratinocyte hyperproliferation and the formation of well-demarcated erythematous, scaly plaques [2]. Beyond its cutaneous manifestations, psoriasis is associated with several comorbidities and substantial physical, psychologic, and social burden, profoundly impairing patients’ quality of life [1].

Over the past two decades, the therapeutic landscape for psoriasis has undergone substantial evolution [2]. Treatment has progressed from topical agents and conventional systemic therapies, such as methotrexate and cyclosporine, to targeted biologic agents directed against pivotal cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL)-17, and IL-23, as well as to novel oral small molecules that inhibit phosphodiesterase-4 (PDE4) and tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) [2, 3].

Despite these advances, important unmet needs remain. Biologic therapies, although highly effective, require parenteral administration and may be associated with immunogenicity or treatment fatigue. Conversely, currently available oral therapies, including apremilast and deucravacitinib, offer greater convenience but have comparatively modest efficacy and tolerability profiles [4].

Thus, there remains an unmet need for orally administered therapies that can offer the convenience of oral dosing while maintaining the efficacy, safety, and durability of response characteristic of biologic agents [3, 4].

In this narrative review, we provide a comprehensive overview of icotrokinra, a first-in-class, orally administered macrocyclic peptide that selectively targets the IL-23 receptor (IL-23R). We summarize current evidence from preclinical, phase 2, and phase 3 studies, focusing on its mechanism of action, clinical efficacy, and safety profile, and on its emerging role within the evolving therapeutic landscape of psoriasis. A structured search of the published literature was conducted (up to December 2025) using the PubMed database, published abstracts from scientific meetings, data from industry press releases, and results published on ClinicalTrials.gov.

The Role of IL-23 in Psoriasis

IL-23 plays a pivotal role in the immunopathogenesis of psoriasis, serving as a central regulator of T helper 17 (TH17) and T cytotoxic 17 (Tc17) cell differentiation, expansion, and maintenance [1, 2]. Structurally, IL-23 is a heterodimeric cytokine composed of a unique p19 subunit and a p40 subunit shared with IL-12 [2, 5].

Its receptor complex, consisting of the IL-12 receptor β1 (IL-12Rβ1) and IL-23R subunits, becomes activated upon IL-23 binding. This interaction initiates intracellular signaling through the Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) and TYK2 pathway, leading to phosphorylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3). The downstream cascade promotes the production of key effector cytokines, notably IL-17A and IL-17F from TH17/Tc17 lymphocytes, and IL-22 from IL-22–producing lymphocytes. Collectively, these cytokines drive keratinocyte activation and hyperproliferation, neutrophil recruitment, and amplification of cutaneous inflammation [2, 5].

Based on the central role of IL-23 in psoriasis pathogenesis, monoclonal antibodies selectively targeting the IL-23p19 subunit, including guselkumab, risankizumab, and tildrakizumab, were developed, demonstrating high and durable efficacy together with a favorable safety profile in both clinical trials and real-world settings [6–9]. These advances have transformed the therapeutic landscape of psoriasis, establishing IL-23 inhibition as a cornerstone of systemic treatment for moderate-to-severe disease.

Mechanism of Action of Icotrokinra

Icotrokinra is an oral macrocyclic peptide that directly antagonizes the IL-23R (Fig. 1), representing a novel therapeutic class distinct from both small molecules and monoclonal antibodies. By binding IL-23R with sub-10-picomolar affinity, it prevents activation of the JAK2/TYK2-STAT3 signaling cascade, thereby suppressing downstream cytokine responses including IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22, and interferon (IFN)-γ [10, 11]. Preclinical studies demonstrated consistent, dose-dependent inhibition of IL-23 induced cytokine release in human whole blood, and robust anti-inflammatory activity in IL-23 mediated cutaneous inflammation models [10]. Oral icotrokinra reduced dermal thickening and down-regulated IL-17A, IL-17F, and IL-22 expression, achieving efficacy comparable to parenteral anti-IL-23 antibodies at 100 mg twice daily. The molecule exhibits high gastrointestinal stability, enabling therapeutic plasma concentrations without absorption enhancers, and displays linear pharmacokinetics after oral administration. It is not metabolized by cytochrome P450 enzymes, shows no relevant interaction with hepatic transporters, and therefore carries a negligible risk of pharmacokinetic drug–drug interactions [10, 11].

In a first-in-human, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 1 study involving 95 healthy participants, icotrokinra demonstrated rapid absorption (time to peak plasma concentration 2–5 h) and a terminal half-life of 9–16 h, supporting once or twice-daily oral dosing [10]. Systemic exposure increased in a dose-proportional manner across the 10–1000 mg range. Ex-vivo pharmacodynamic assessments confirmed dose-dependent target engagement, with near-complete inhibition of IL-23 induced responses at the upper dose levels. The drug was well tolerated, and adverse events were mostly mild and transient, with no dose-related toxicity signals [11].

This mechanism, combined with predictable pharmacokinetics and low immunogenicity potential, may allow biologic-like efficacy and safety through oral administration [11].

Clinical Development Program of Icotrokinra

The clinical development of icotrokinra has advanced rapidly across multiple immune-mediated inflammatory diseases, with psoriasis as the lead indication. The phase 2b FRONTIER-1 trial, together with its long-term extension FRONTIER-2, evaluated the efficacy, safety, and optimal dosing of oral icotrokinra in adults with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis, supporting the selection of a once-daily regimen [20, 21].

These studies served as the basis for two ongoing pivotal phase 3 trials, ADVANCE-1 and ADVANCE-2, which are multicenter, double-blind, placebo- and active-comparator–controlled studies designed to assess the efficacy and safety of icotrokinra compared with placebo and the oral comparator deucravacitinib [13].

The ICONIC-LEAD study is an ongoing trial that further expands the development program to include both adults and adolescents [14–16]. Adults who achieve clinical response at week 24 are re-randomized to treatment continuation or withdrawal to assess maintenance of effect and recapture upon re-initiation, while adolescents continue therapy through week 52 [14–16].

Complementing this, the ongoing ICONIC-TOTAL study focuses on psoriasis involving high-impact sites anatomic sites including scalp, genital, palmar, and plantar areas, providing additional insight into the therapeutic potential of icotrokinra in high-burden disease locations [17, 18].

In parallel, the ongoing ICONIC-ASCEND trial is evaluating once-daily icotrokinra versus placebo and the biologic agent ustekinumab in adolescents and adults with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis [19].

Beyond chronic plaque psoriasis, icotrokinra is also under investigation in generalized pustular psoriasis and erythrodermic psoriasis. An ongoing phase 3 trial (NCT06295692) is evaluating once-daily icotrokinra in these severe variants, with the primary endpoint being treatment success at week 16 according to the Clinical Global Impression (CGI) scale based on the Japanese Dermatological Association (JDA) total score (Table 2) [23]. The development program has further expanded into psoriatic arthritis, with two parallel, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 trials currently underway: NCT06878404, enrolling biologic-naïve patients (n = 540), and NCT06807424, enrolling biologic-experienced patients (n = 750). In both studies, the primary endpoint is the proportion of participants achieving a ≥ 20% improvement in American College of Rheumatology score (ACR20) at week 16 [24, 25]. Given the central role of IL-23 in intestinal inflammation, icotrokinra is also being evaluated in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) (Table 2). A randomized phase 2 trial in ulcerative colitis (NCT06049017; n = 252) established proof-of-concept and informed dose selection, leading to advancement into a phase 3 study (NCT07196748) in ulcerative colitis and a phase 2b/3 program (NCT07196722) in Crohn’s disease (Table 2) [26–28]. Efficacy of Icotrokinra in Psoriasis

Phase 2 Trials

In the phase 2b FRONTIER-1 trial, 255 adults with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis were randomly assigned to receive oral icotrokinra across five dosing regimens (25 mg once daily, 25 mg twice daily, 50 mg once daily, 100 mg once daily, or 100 mg twice daily; n = 212) or placebo (n = 43) for 16 weeks [20]. Baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups, with a mean baseline Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) score of approximately 19 and one quarter of participants previously exposed to biologic therapy.

The primary endpoint, the proportion of patients with ≥ 75% improvement in the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI75) response at week 16, was met with statistical significance across all active treatment groups (p < 0.001 vs. placebo), showing a clear dose–response relationship. [20] Clinical improvement was evident as early as week 4, with progressive gains through week 16. PASI75 responses ranged from 37% with 25 mg once daily to 79% with 100 mg twice daily, compared with 9% in the placebo group. Higher-level responses followed a similar pattern: PASI90 and PASI100 were achieved by up to 60% and 40% of patients in the 100-mg twice-daily group, versus 2% and 0%, respectively, for placebo. Similarly, 64% of patients receiving 100 mg twice daily achieved an Investigator’s Global Assessment score of 0 or 1 (IGA 0/1) (clear or almost clear skin), and 45% achieved complete clearance (IGA 0), compared with 12% and 0% for placebo.

Clinically meaningful improvement in health-related quality of life paralleled clinical response. Among patients with a baseline Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) > 1, 56% receiving 100 mg once daily and 44% receiving 100 mg twice daily achieved a DLQI of 0 or 1 at week 16, compared with 2% in the placebo group [20].

Long-term efficacy and durability of response were evaluated in FRONTIER-2, the 52-week extension of FRONTIER-1, which enrolled 227 participants (89% of the original cohort) [21]. Patients initially assigned to placebo transitioned to icotrokinra 100 mg once daily at week 16. At week 52, responses were sustained or further improved across endpoints, confirming the durability of oral IL-23R blockade. In the 100-mg twice-daily group, 76% achieved PASI75, 64% achieved PASI90, 40% achieved PASI100, and 74% achieved IGA 0/1. In participants switched from placebo to icotrokinra 100 mg once daily, corresponding response rates were 66%, 57%, 34%, and 66%, respectively.

Beyond clinical efficacy, icotrokinra induced a clear and dose-dependent pharmacodynamic response consistent with selective IL-23R blockade [22]. Treatment was associated with early (by week 4) and sustained (through week 52) reductions in circulating biomarkers of the IL-23/IL-17 axis, with maximal effects observed at the highest dosing regimens. In parallel, transcriptomic and proteomic analyses demonstrated normalization of psoriasis-associated inflammatory signatures in lesional skin, without evidence of broader immune pathway suppression.

Phase 3 Trials

The pivotal phase 3 ICONIC-ADVANCE-1 (NCT06143878; n = 774) and ICONIC-ADVANCE-2 (NCT06220604; n = 731) trials were multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo- and active-controlled studies evaluating once-daily icotrokinra 200 mg in adults with moderate-to-severe chronic plaque psoriasis [13]. Participants were randomly assigned in a 2:2:1 ratio to receive icotrokinra 200 mg, deucravacitinib 6 mg, or placebo. At week 16, patients in the placebo group switched to icotrokinra, whereas those receiving deucravacitinib crossed over to icotrokinra 200 mg once daily at week 24. The coprimary endpoints were the proportions of patients achieving an IGA 0/1 with a ≥ 2-grade improvement from baseline and a PASI90 response at week 16 versus placebo. Key secondary endpoints included comparisons with deucravacitinib at weeks 16 and 24 for PASI75, PASI90, PASI100, IGA 0/1, and IGA 0.

Clinical improvement was observed early, with separation from placebo evident by week 4 for PASI75 and by week 8 for PASI90 [13]. At week 16, icotrokinra achieved superior efficacy versus placebo in both studies. In ADVANCE-1, 68% of patients receiving icotrokinra achieved IGA 0/1 and 55% achieved PASI90, compared with 11% and 4% with placebo (both p < 0.0001). In ADVANCE-2, corresponding rates were 70% versus 9% and 57% versus 1% (both p < 0.0001). Complete clearance was achieved in a notable proportion of icotrokinra-treated patients, with IGA 0 attained in 37% versus 1–2% and PASI100 in 31–32% versus 1–2% (all p < 0.0001).

Efficacy was consistent across disease domains. In scalp psoriasis, 72% and 74% of patients receiving icotrokinra achieved a scalp-specific IGA score of 0 or 1 (ss-IGA 0/1) at week 16 compared with 21% and 18% with placebo (both p < 0.0001) [13]. Patient-reported outcomes mirrored clinical responses, with approximately 60% of participants achieving clinically meaningful improvement in itch and 21–24% attaining complete resolution of psoriasis symptoms, compared with 3% receiving placebo, as measured by the Psoriasis Symptoms and Signs Diary (PSSD).

At week 16, icotrokinra also demonstrated significantly greater efficacy than deucravacitinib across key secondary endpoints [13]. In ADVANCE-1, PASI75, PASI90, and IGA 0/1 were achieved in 74%, 55%, and 68% of patients receiving icotrokinra compared with 57%, 30%, and 50% of those receiving deucravacitinib (all p < 0.0001). In ADVANCE-2, corresponding rates were 77%, 57%, and 70% for icotrokinra versus 61%, 34%, and 54% for deucravacitinib (all p < 0.0001).

At week 24, icotrokinra demonstrated superior efficacy compared with deucravacitinib across all endpoints [13]. In ADVANCE-1, PASI75, PASI90, and PASI100 were achieved in 82%, 66%, and 41% of icotrokinra treated patients versus 64%, 41%, and 16% with deucravacitinib (all p < 0.0001). IGA 0/1 occurred in 74% versus 52% and IGA 0 in 48% versus 21% (both p < 0.0001). In ADVANCE-2, corresponding rates were 83% versus 66% for PASI75, 65% versus 43% for PASI90, 33% versus 16% for PASI100, 68% versus 55% for IGA 0/1, and 40% versus 21% for IGA 0 (all p ≤ 0.0002).

ICONIC-LEAD (NCT06095115) is a phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study evaluating once-daily icotrokinra 200 mg in adults and adolescents (≥ 12 years) with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis (body surface area [BSA] ≥ 10%, PASI ≥ 12, IGA ≥ 3) [14]. Participants were randomized 2:1 to icotrokinra or placebo for 16 weeks; at week 24, adults achieving PASI75 or IGA 0/1 responses were re-randomized 1:1 to continue icotrokinra or switch to placebo for a 28-week withdrawal phase (with retreatment permitted upon loss of ≥ 50% of the week-24 PASI improvement), while adolescents continued active treatment through week 52.

At week 16, icotrokinra demonstrated superior efficacy versus placebo, with 65% of participants achieving IGA 0/1 and 50% attaining PASI90, compared with 8% and 4%, respectively, for placebo (p < 0.001 for both comparisons) [14]. Complete skin clearance was also significantly more frequent with icotrokinra, with 33% of participants achieving IGA 0 versus 1% with placebo, and 27% achieving PASI100 versus < 1% with placebo (p < 0.001 for both endpoints).

Early separation from placebo was evident, with PASI75 responses observed as early as week 4 (15% vs. 2%; p = 0.002) and PASI90 responses by week 8 (21% vs. 1%; p < 0.001) [14]. Among participants with baseline scalp involvement (scalp IGA ≥ 2), 72% achieved an ss-IGA 0/1 response at week 16 compared with 15% receiving placebo (p < 0.001), with sustained improvement through week 24 (80% in the continuous icotrokinra group). Participants who switched from placebo to icotrokinra at week 16 achieved comparable scalp responses (77%).

Efficacy continued to improve through week 24, with 74% of participants achieving IGA 0/1, 65% achieving PASI90, 46% attaining complete skin clearance (IGA 0), and 40% achieving PASI100 [14]. Improvements in scalp psoriasis were sustained and further enhanced, with 80% of participants receiving continuous icotrokinra achieving an ss-IGA 0/1 response by week 24. Participants who switched from placebo to icotrokinra at week 16 demonstrated comparable scalp responses, with a scalp IGA 0/1 rate of 77%.

In adolescents (n = 66), efficacy was similarly robust. At week 16, 84% achieved IGA 0/1 and 70% achieved PASI90 with icotrokinra, compared with 27% and 14% with placebo (p < 0.001 for both) [14]. Complete clearance was observed in 41% (IGA 0) and 30% (PASI100) of icotrokinra-treated patients, compared with 5% and 5% among those receiving placebo (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, respectively). At week 24, 86% achieved IGA 0/1 and 89% achieved PASI90, with complete clearance rates of 75% for IGA 0 and 64% for PASI100.

Based on conference abstract data, among adults re-randomized at week 24 (341 responders; 169 continued icotrokinra and 172 switched to placebo), continued icotrokinra treatment maintained high levels of skin clearance through week 52, with PASI75 and PASI90 maintained in 89% and 84% of prior responders, respectively, versus 30% and 21% among those switched to placebo (both p < 0.001) [15, 16]. Median time to loss of PASI75 or PASI90 response was not reached with continued therapy, compared with 16.9 weeks and 10.1 weeks after withdrawal. Maintenance of IGA 0/1 similarly favored continued treatment (82% vs. 23%; p < 0.001) [15]. In adolescents, efficacy responses were maintained through week 52 with 82% achieving IGA 0/1, 86% achieving PASI90, and 95% achieving PASI75.

Icotrokinra has also been evaluated in psoriasis affecting high-impact sites [17]. The ICONIC-TOTAL trial (NCT06095102) is an ongoing phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study enrolling 311 adolescents and adults (≥ 12 years) with at least moderate plaque psoriasis involving the scalp, genital area, and/or palms and soles, with BSA involvement as low as 1%. Participants were randomly assigned to receive icotrokinra 200 mg once daily (n = 208) or placebo (n = 103), with placebo to icotrokinra transition at week 16, followed by longer term observation through 52 weeks. The study included participants with at least moderate scalp (n = 252), genital (n = 140), and/or hand/foot (n = 71) psoriasis, each evaluated using site-specific global assessments. The primary endpoint was the proportion of participants achieving an IGA 0/1 with a ≥ 2-grade improvement from baseline at week 16. Key secondary endpoints included site-specific assessments using the ss-IGA, static Physician’s Global Assessment of Genitalia (sPGA-G), and Physician’s Global Assessment of hands and/or feet (hf-PGA), together with patient- and clinician-reported outcome measures.

At week 16, icotrokinra met the primary endpoint, with 57% of participants achieving IGA 0/1 versus 6% with placebo (p < 0.001) [17]. Complete skin clearance (IGA 0) was achieved by 25.5% of participants receiving icotrokinra compared with 1.0% receiving placebo (p < 0.001). Consistent benefits were observed across high-impact sites: 66% of participants with scalp psoriasis achieved ss-IGA 0/1 compared with 11% receiving placebo (p < 0.001), and 77% of those with genital psoriasis achieved sPGA-G 0/1 versus 21% with placebo (p < 0.001). In the hand-and/or-foot subgroup, clearance rates were numerically higher with icotrokinra (42% vs. 26%), although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.144).

Based on conference abstract data, among 311 randomized participants, 88% (275/311) completed 52 weeks of treatment [18]. Clinical responses observed at week 16 increased through week 24 and were maintained through week 52, with 67% achieving overall IGA 0/1, 72% achieving scalp ss-IGA 0/1, 85% achieving genital sPGA-G 0/1, and 62% achieving hand/foot hf-PGA 0/1. Comparable week 52 response rates were observed among participants who transitioned from placebo to icotrokinra at week 16. Complete clearance (score 0) was achieved by 44%, 57%, 73%, and 58% of participants in these respective domains.

ICONIC-ASCEND (NCT06934226) is an ongoing, phase 3, multicenter, randomized, double-blind trial evaluating once daily icotrokinra in adolescents and adults with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis [19]. The study includes matching placebo and the biologic agent ustekinumab as active comparator arms. Co-primary endpoints are the proportions of participants achieving an IGA 0/1 with a ≥ 2-grade improvement from baseline and a PASI90 response at week 16. The trial plans to enroll approximately 752 participants, with study initiation in April 2025, an anticipated primary completion in March 2026, and final completion in August 2027.

Safety

Across phase 2 and 3 studies, icotrokinra demonstrated a favorable and consistent safety profile, with an incidence and spectrum of treatment-emergent adverse events comparable to placebo and aligned with expectations for patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis. No dose-dependent toxicity or unexpected safety concerns were identified [13–18, 20, 21].

In the phase 2b FRONTIER-1 trial, adverse events were reported in 44–62% of participants receiving icotrokinra and in 51% of those receiving placebo [20]. The most frequent events (≥ 5% in any group) were mild upper respiratory infections, principally nasopharyngitis and pharyngitis, followed by diarrhea (5% vs. 2% with placebo), headache, and cough. Nervous system disorders occurred in 5% of icotrokinra-treated patients compared with 2% in the placebo group. Three serious adverse events (SAEs)—coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), an infected cyst, and a suicide attempt—were reported, none considered treatment related. No deaths, malignancies, or major adverse cardiovascular events were observed, and no dose–response trend was evident.

In the long-term extension FRONTIER-2, through week 56, 59% of patients reported at least one adverse event, most commonly nasopharyngitis (18%), upper respiratory infection (10%), and COVID-19 (5%) [21]. Gastrointestinal events remained stable over time (11% at week 16; 6% through week 56). SAEs occurred in 4% of participants, including isolated cases of cerebrovascular accident, coronary artery disease, and diverticulitis, none related to study drug. Laboratory and hematologic parameters remained stable, and no deaths occurred.

In the pivotal phase 3 trials ADVANCE-1 and ADVANCE-2, up to week 24, adverse events were reported in fewer patients receiving icotrokinra than in those treated with deucravacitinib (359 [57%] vs. 411 [65%]) [13]. The most common events were nasopharyngitis and upper respiratory tract infection. SAEs were infrequent (3% in both groups), and serious infections were rare (< 1%). One additional malignancy, a melanoma in situ, was reported in a participant receiving deucravacitinib.

In ICONIC-LEAD (all treatment groups), through week 16, the overall safety profile of icotrokinra was comparable to placebo [14]. Adverse events occurred in 49% of participants in both groups, with nasopharyngitis and upper respiratory tract infection being the most common events (7% each). SAEs were infrequent (1% with icotrokinra vs. 3% with placebo), and gastrointestinal adverse events occurred in 6% of participants in both groups. Infections were reported at similar rates (23% vs. 22%), while serious infections were rare (< 1%). Two malignancies (< 1%) were reported in the icotrokinra group, with none in the placebo group, and no deaths occurred.

Among adolescents in ICONIC-LEAD, the safety profile of icotrokinra 200 mg once daily (mean follow-up 16 weeks) was consistent with that observed in adults [14, 16]. Any adverse event occurred in 50% of icotrokinra-treated adolescents versus 73% of those receiving placebo; infections were reported in 32% and 27%, respectively, primarily upper respiratory tract infections. Two SAEs (pancreatitis secondary to choledocholithiasis and transient hospitalization for joint pain) were deemed unrelated to study drug and resolved without sequelae. No tuberculosis, malignancy, deaths, or treatment-limiting laboratory abnormalities were observed.

In ICONIC-TOTAL, which evaluated high-impact sites, overall adverse event rates were comparable between icotrokinra and placebo (50% vs. 42%), with nasopharyngitis and mild gastrointestinal symptoms being the most frequently reported events [17]. SAEs were infrequent, occurring in 0.5% of icotrokinra-treated participants and 1.9% of placebo-treated participants. Gastrointestinal adverse events were reported in 7.2% and 7.8% of participants, respectively. Infections occurred in 28.4% of the icotrokinra group and 21.4% of the placebo group, while serious infections were rare (0% vs. 1.0%). One melanoma was reported in the icotrokinra group in a participant with a prior history of melanoma. No deaths were reported.

Adverse event and SAE rates remained consistent through week 52 compared with week 16 across treatment groups, with no emerging safety signals [18]. The overall safety profile was consistent with that observed across the broader icotrokinra clinical program.

Taken together, across phase 2 and 3 studies, adverse events with icotrokinra were predominantly mild or moderate in severity, with no evidence of cumulative or dose-related toxicity. Serious events were uncommon and unrelated to treatment [13–18, 20, 21]. No safety concerns emerged regarding malignancy, major cardiovascular events, or laboratory abnormalities, supporting a favorable and stable safety profile with extended exposure.

Discussion

Despite the substantial efficacy of biologic agents, many patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis remain reluctant to initiate or sustain long-term injectable therapy. Currently available oral agents have historically traded potency and selectivity for convenience [29]. The development of icotrokinra, a first-in-class, orally administered macrocyclic peptide that selectively targets the IL-23R, was intended to bridge this gap, aiming to achieve biologic-level efficacy through a highly targeted, non-immunogenic oral formulation with predictable pharmacokinetics and minimal potential for off-target effects or drug–drug interactions [30].

Across two pivotal phase 3 trials, ICONIC-ADVANCE-1 and ICONIC-ADVANCE-2, icotrokinra consistently demonstrated efficacy superior to both placebo and deucravacitinib across all prespecified endpoints. Clinical separation from placebo and deucravacitinib occurred early, by week 8 for PASI90, highlighting a rapid onset of action that is highly valued in psoriasis, where prompt improvement in signs and symptoms strongly influences patient satisfaction and adherence. The magnitude of benefit was highly consistent between studies favoring icotrokinra across PASI75, PASI90, PASI100, and IGA outcomes. Importantly, the advantage extended to complete clearance (PASI100 and IGA 0), with response rates roughly double those achieved with deucravacitinib. The reproducibility of these findings in two independent phase 3 trials underscores the robustness of the efficacy signal and validates selective IL-23R blockade as a relevant therapeutic strategy in psoriasis [13].

Data from ICONIC-LEAD and ICONIC-TOTAL further expands this evidence base. Sustained responses through 52 weeks in adults, together with similarly high response rates in adolescents, confirm durable efficacy across age groups, disease severities, and high-impact sites. In the withdrawal phase of ICONIC-LEAD, loss of response after treatment discontinuation occurred gradually, with a median time exceeding 10 weeks, suggesting persistence of disease control beyond drug exposure [14–18]. This observation is biologically consistent with the central role of IL-23 in maintaining pathogenic Th17 and Tc17 cells, as well as tissue-resident memory T cells, and with previous findings from other IL-23 inhibitors [5].

Supporting this clinical observation, pharmacodynamic and molecular analyses from phase 2 studies demonstrated dose-dependent and sustained modulation of IL-23/IL-17–axis biomarkers, together with normalization of psoriasis-associated inflammatory signatures in lesional skin, providing a mechanistic link between selective IL-23R inhibition and durable disease control [22].

Furthermore, consistent efficacy across high-impact sites, such as scalp, genital, and acral areas, reinforces the broad therapeutic potential of this treatment in high-impact areas that disproportionately affect patients’ quality of life [17, 18].

The ongoing ICONIC-ASCEND trial, comparing icotrokinra with the biologic agent ustekinumab, will provide a rigorous benchmark for clinical comparability and help define the role of oral IL-23 blockade in future treatment algorithms [19]. Demonstration of sustained efficacy and safety in this head-to-head setting would substantiate the concept that targeted oral IL-23R inhibition can reproduce the depth of response characteristic of biologic therapy, potentially offering biologic-like outcomes with the practicality of oral administration.

When placed within the current therapeutic landscape, icotrokinra occupies a distinct position. Its clinical efficacy clearly exceeds that of currently available oral targeted therapies, like apremilast and deucravacitinib, representing a substantial advance among non-injectable systemic options. Although no direct head-to-head comparisons with biologic agents are available, the magnitude and consistency of skin-clearance responses observed across phase 3 trials place icotrokinra within the established efficacy spectrum of biologic therapies, with response rates approaching those reported for IL-23 inhibitors, recognizing the limitations of cross-trial comparisons. Ongoing and future comparative studies will be important to more precisely define its relative position within the hierarchy of high-efficacy systemic treatments for psoriasis.

The safety profile of icotrokinra was favorable and consistent across phase 2 and 3 studies. Overall adverse event rates were lower than with deucravacitinib and comparable to placebo during the controlled phases, with nasopharyngitis and mild upper respiratory tract infection as the most frequent events. SAEs and serious infections were uncommon, and no new or unexpected safety signals were identified. The absence of dose-dependent toxicity, laboratory abnormalities, or organ-specific effects distinguishes this compound from conventional systemic therapies, which have broader, non-selective mechanisms of action and carry potential for drug–drug interactions [13–18, 20, 21].

Moreover, unlike existing oral targeted therapies that act on widely shared intracellular kinases and may be associated with off-target effects, icotrokinra exerts highly selective, receptor-level blockade of IL-23 signaling [3].

Beyond efficacy and safety, treatment adherence and patient preference remain key determinants of long-term outcomes. While oral therapies are generally perceived as less burdensome than chronic injections, adherence to daily dosing may still vary with patient behavior and disease perception [29–31].

Nevertheless, an oral therapy capable of achieving biologic-level outcomes could meaningfully expand access to advanced treatment, particularly among individuals deterred by injectable regimens or in settings where parenteral administration poses logistical challenges.

Beyond psoriasis, the therapeutic potential of icotrokinra extends to other immune-mediated inflammatory disorders driven by IL-23 dependent pathways. Phase 3 trials are ongoing in psoriatic arthritis, and phase 2/3 development is progressing in ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease [24–28]. Collectively, these studies constitute an extensive oral IL-23 pathway development program, positioning icotrokinra as a potential first-in-class, orally administered IL-23R antagonist with broad therapeutic potential across dermatologic, rheumatologic, and gastrointestinal inflammatory disorders.

Demonstrating efficacy across these conditions would establish the first oral IL-23 targeted therapy capable of addressing the spectrum of IL-23–driven diseases, paralleling the experience with biologic IL-23 inhibitors while potentially improving convenience, accessibility, and treatment continuity across comorbid immune-mediated disorders.

Conclusion

Icotrokinra represents a novel class of oral therapy that combines the pharmacologic precision of targeted biologics with the practicality of once-daily administration. Across phase 2 and 3 studies, icotrokinra demonstrated high clinical efficacy together with a favorable, consistent, and well-tolerated safety profile. Importantly, it showed superior efficacy compared with the oral TYK2 inhibitor (deucravacitinib).

Taken together, evidence from the ICONIC program supports selective oral IL-23R inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy that may combine high levels of clinical efficacy with the convenience of oral administration. Longer-term data will further clarify the durability of remission, safety with sustained exposure, and potential immunologic persistence after treatment discontinuation.

By selectively blocking the IL-23R, icotrokinra achieves biologic-like pathway specificity through an oral route, addressing one of the principal limitations of biologic therapy, long-term injectable administration, while overcoming key limitations of small molecules, such as off-target activity and pharmacologic interactions.

If approved, icotrokinra could expand access to IL-23 pathway inhibition, improve patient acceptance and adherence, reduce the burden of injections, and potentially reshape expectations for oral therapy in moderate-to-severe psoriasis.
