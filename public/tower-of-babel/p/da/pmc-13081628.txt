Perceptions of parents with children six or younger on adhering to the well-child care visit schedule
Authors: Meghan E Meadows, Amy Wiltse, Cristina Carias, Jack E Turman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdherence to the well-child care (WCC) visit schedule is suboptimal. Parents play an essential role in promoting their child’s health. We asked parents of children six and younger to inform us about their beliefs regarding, experiences with, and barriers to attending WCC visits.MethodsWe were guided by constructivist grounded theory (CGT) in our qualitative study of parents’ perceptions of WCC visits and the barriers they face to adherence. Forty-five English-speaking parents, all residents of Indiana, participated in semi-structured interviews from December 2023 to June 2024.ResultsParents adhered to their provider’s instructions on when to schedule WCC visits, regardless of their lack of understanding about what guides the provider’s instructions. Provider trust was a key factor in deciding to go to a visit. Once deciding to attend, preparation was needed, which included getting time off work and finding time on a provider’s schedule that aligned with their personal work schedule. The physical environment, office staff, and the provider’s behavior influenced perceptions of the visit. Analyses revealed that respondents with lower incomes and Medicaid reported the most barriers to adherence.ConclusionsParents in this study demonstrated a belief that WCC benefits their children by following their provider’s instructions to attend WCC visits even though they do not know the schedule or its purpose. Efforts to improve future adherence could include improving Medicaid processes, extending clinic hours beyond a typical workday schedule, and informing employers of the need to make provisions so families can attend WCC visits.Keywords: Health promotion, Medicaid, Social determinants of health, Parent decision making

Introduction

Routine well-child care (WCC) is the standard of care to promote health in children. Adherence to the WCC schedule is, however, suboptimal [1]. An analysis of 2011–2016 data, inclusive of 20 states, observed that adherence to the recommended WCC was lowest among children 1–2 and 3–5 [2]. Furthermore, children who had public insurance were more likely to have poor attendance (odds ratio of 2.7), as were children with mothers who had additional prior pregnancies (odds ratio of 1.2) [3]. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends a periodicity schedule that involves a WCC visit for children 3 to 5 days old, then at 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 24, and 30 months old, and then yearly throughout development [4].

Despite increased adherence in 2016 and 2017 compared with 2006 and 2007, from an average of 47.9% to 62.3% by one measure, concerning gaps remain [5]. The Medical Expenditure Survey (MEPS) found an overall compliance ratio of 61.4% for attending WCC visits in the general United States population, with a ratio of 83.2 for children < 1 year and 63.6 for children 6 to 12 years old [6]. Other studies also found rates decreasing with older age [7]. Social determinants of health significantly influence adherence to WCC, but findings are often contradictory, based on variables such as sex, age, race, insurance status, and household structure [2]. For example, being within 400% of the poverty level or less is associated with slightly lower adherence to the recommended WCC [6]. Lower adherence was observed among children less than two years of age who have experienced intrauterine opioid exposure, with one study finding a median of six WCC visits compared with eight among children who had not been exposed [8]. The same could not be said of immunizations or lead screening in this same population [8].

When parents establish a pattern of vaccination, they seem to be more likely to continue. Early vaccination at 3 months is associated with full completion of recommended vaccination at 24 months with 82.3% compared with 48.1% [9]. The AAP-recommended vaccination schedule is complex, recommending multiple vaccines in a single visit on five or more well-child care visits [10]. This may contribute to falling behind on recommendations because missing one visit may result in missing multiple vaccines.

Adherence to recommended WCC is associated with reduced childhood morbidity and avoidable healthcare utilization through immunizations and chronic disease management [11]. WCC and other types of preventive care for children can result in later health care cost-savings both in childhood and along the life course [12]. WCC can even impact injury prevention, with one study showing an estimated cost reduction of $230 million when pediatricians participated in injury prevention counseling with parents of young children aged two years or younger [13].

Parental factors associated with receipt of WCC include college education, having a usual source of care, adequate prenatal care, and Medicaid coverage [14]. In short, parental use of preventive care for themselves increases the likelihood of seeking preventive care for their children [14]. Little is known about whether and how parents understand the WCC schedule and purpose, something that these authors seek to address. Some is known about perceived barriers and facilitators among parents for attending health care appointments, which generally fall into the categories of transportation, competing priorities for the child and family, and challenges encountered in scheduling and delivery of health care services [15]. In a study of 20 semi-structured focus groups with parents, the most cited reasons for WCC visit attendance were reassurance of both child and parent, an opportunity to discuss priorities, and an ongoing relationship with a single clinician who was child-focused and respected parental expertise [16].

Examining parents’ perspectives on the barriers and facilitators to adhering to the recommended WCC visit schedule, as this study does, provides a context for why WCC adherence rates continue to remain relatively low in some communities. While there are quantitative analyses regarding WCC [17], parental perceptions of the drivers of WCC adherence are not well understood. Considering that parents are best qualified to elaborate on factors that impact their ability to access care for their children, we interviewed parents of young children to gain insight into WCC adherence drivers. Our findings will help pediatricians and others providing WCC better understand the factors associated with WCC adherence.

Methods

We used a constructivist grounded theory (CGT) approach [18] to implement the study and analyze the results to elucidate the intersection between the daily life challenges of parenting and taking children to WCC visits. This approach was recently utilized to understand parental perceptions of the care provided to their child with a serious illness [19]. CGT aims to co-create new knowledge by integrating the parents’ perspectives with the researchers’ knowledge and other available data.

Recruitment and data collection

This study used qualitative data from interviews with key informants across Indiana to characterize drivers of adherence to the American Academy of Pediatrics recommended WCC visit schedule (≤ 6 years). The study’s methods were approved by the university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) before initiation. All participants provided informed consent to participate in the study.

We set out to recruit English-speaking informants aged 18 or older, with children aged 0–6 years. We followed the guidelines of a systematic review of qualitative studies to determine optimal sample size, which recommends sample sizes of 9–17 participants, depending on the heterogeneity of the sample [20]. The research team recruited parents by working with local parent and family advocates, agencies that provide wraparound services for impoverished families, faith-based organizations, and early childhood education organizations serving children and their families. Through purposeful sampling and snowballing methods, organization representatives and project researchers used word of mouth to recruit parents from counties representative of the different levels of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) metropolitan statistical area classification scheme, making sure we focused on several counties characterized by high infant mortality rates as identified by the state’s Department of Health (Table 1-insert table here) [21, 22]. Some of the parents knew two of the university affiliated authors, through programming that they are associated with. Interested parents granted permission to share their contact information with the principal researcher, who followed up to further explain the study, its goals and processes and to obtain consent. One parent dropped out of the interview as she did not understand the questions. Our recruitment methods resulted in a final sample of 45 parents. Our sample size of 45 allowed for theme saturation in parent responses and stratification by some demographic variables to analyze differences between variable subgroups (e.g., demographic group by insurance type, subgroups of private vs. Medicaid insurance coverage).

The team developed a demographic questionnaire and a semi-structured interview guide to gather parents’ perspectives on attending WCC visits and identify associated barriers and facilitators. The question content was based on literature about WCC adherence and previous research with similar populations, reflecting how the social determinants of health might influence WCC adherence [11]. The interview questions are provided in Table 2. Questions were divided into five categories: relationship with the child’s primary caregivers, experiences at WCC visits, barriers to attending WCC visits, decision-making processes, and recommendations for improving the WCC process. Over a one-week period, cognitive debriefing to validate the question content and structure was conducted with two pediatricians, and two women (both mothers) residing in low-income communities, who work to advance grassroots efforts to improve state maternal and child health outcomes. Independent reviewers evaluated the interview guide to accommodate health literacy standards. (insert Table 2 here in manuscript)

Overview of interview questions and prompts

Parents were recruited and interviewed from December 2023 to June 2024. Depending on availability and geographical feasibility, one or two researchers (females, MPH trained, research assistants, formally trained in qualitative research through graduate coursework) interviewed each parent. The interviews were conducted either in person, over the phone, or via videoconferencing software, depending on the parents’ preference and accessibility needs, at a time and location convenient for them. Each interview was audio recorded to assist with transcription.

During the interview, the interviewer reviewed the consent form with the parent and addressed any questions they had. Next, the interviewer provided a brief orientation statement outlining the purpose, duration, and type of the interview. The orientation statement was followed by completing the demographic questionnaire and asking semi-structured interview questions, along with possible follow-up questions. Interviews lasted approximately 30 to 60 min. Each parent received a gift bag valued at $15 for their participation.

Data analyses

Both interviewers analyzed the verbatim transcriptions of interview recordings following Smith and Shinebourne’s Six-Step Protocol [23], which includes (i) reading/re-reading –immersing in the transcript, (ii) coding –identify codes and group them into preliminary themes, (iii) clustering – subthemes emerge within themes, (iv) iteration – several revisions are made, including checking themes, subthemes, and quotes, (v) narration – based on the findings, the team creates a narrative, describing the themes and illustrating them with quotes, and (vi) contextualization –interpretation of the findings in relation to existing literature.

Thematic analyses

The thematic analysis transitioned from a descriptive to an interpretive mode by categorizing each transcript into broad themes, and then, through further review, translating the data into more specific subthemes. For methodological uniformity, two researchers separately coded the transcribed interviews [11]. The emerging ideas were discussed and analyzed as a group to arrive at the final themes, achieving a 100% coding agreement. The qualitative analysis software NVivo 14 (QSR International Pty Ltd, 2018, Melbourne, Australia (NVivo, 2018)) was used to manage and code the data. The present study’s reporting is based on the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) framework [20].

Stratification analyses

After finalizing the themes, the team analyzed the demographic data for dichotomous variables, which each had a significantly high, and similar number of responses to allow for stratification comparisons. Four areas were selected: marital status (single vs. married), income (<$25,000 vs. >$100,000), insurance provider (private insurance vs. Medicaid), and geographic context (large central or fringe metro vs. small metro or micropolitan/non-core). These dichotomies within each demographic variable provided a clear distinction between the participants, and each had enough participants to allow for saturation. Cross-tabulation tables were created within NVivo, and the research team reviewed the tables for response disparities.

Summary of thematic analysis

Knowledge of WCC visits

Responses to questions about the parents’ knowledge of the WCC visit schedule revealed that parents had little awareness of the schedule and its recommendations. Responses also showed that parents adhere to their providers’ recommendations on attending WCC visits despite not knowing what drives the timing and purpose of those visits. As stated by one participant, “Um, I don’t necessarily know what it is. Honestly, I just follow what the doctors tell me to do.” (WCC028). Although questions about vaccinations were not asked, parents would refer to them and the order in which they are given when responding to questions about their WCC schedule knowledge.

Most parents felt the WCC visits provided reassurance that they were doing what was best for their children, provided access to information and recommendations, and were essential for their children’s health and development.

“…. they’ve made it a lot easier for me to worry a little less that I’m not doing a terrible job. You know, because I think that most moms’ big fear is they’re not doing a good job and they’re ruining everything…. my pediatrician has put at least a decent amount of faith in myself that I’m doing an okay job, and I’m not messing everything up.” (WCC011).

Disadvantages reported by parents included the frequency of recommended visits, healthcare experiences with previous children making the frequency unnecessary with subsequent children, and providers who failed to perform thorough examinations.

“I feel I don’t need the appointments as much as I did before. I know that she’s healthy. I know that she’s growing. She stands on the scale now herself. So, there’s no need to take her and say are you growing? Are you losing weight? Are you okay? What’s wrong with you? …. she actually tells us now if she is hurting.” (WCC018).

Decision making - why parents go to well-child visits

Parents identified trust in providers based on positive past experiences as a factor in the decision to attend WCC visits, and those who had past experiences of discrimination based on class, race, or criminal history were left distrustful of providers.

“…. And then I feel like if you do have Medicaid, doctors do look at you a little bit differently…. I’m not really sure what it is. I don’t know if it’s relying on the government or not being independent enough for them. Or maybe they’re just not brought up in the same kind of life, I guess.” (WCC034).

Parents reported they rely most on their personal feelings and beliefs about healthcare when making decisions for their children, including whether to attend WCC visits. However, they also sought information from multiple sources to assist decision-making. Online resources were commonly reported as sources of information for decision-making, including general Google searches, YouTube, Reddit, Facebook, WebMD, and TikTok. Trusted family and social connections, as well as various healthcare professionals, were additional sources of information.

“I actually have different providers I use; I use [social service agency supporting families], who is obviously a really good one. For any questions regarding my kids or what I should be giving them, what I should not be giving them. [Social service agency for women], I use that one as well. [State home visitation program] is another provider that I look up to. And then just my mom’s groups.” (WCC014).

Parents combine their personal experiences, beliefs, and information gathered to guide their decision-making about their children’s healthcare, including adherence to WCC visit recommendations.

Preparing to attend well-child visits & addressing barriers

Once deciding to attend a WCC visit, selecting a healthcare provider for their children was the next step in preparing for the visit. The process by which parents selected a provider was like the process by which they sought information to inform their decision-making. The most reported source used for choosing a provider was the experience and recommendations of trusted family and social connections.

“I was in a parenting class at the [social service agency for women]. And one of the moms there [has] like seven kids. And I was like, ‘Who do you go to?’ And so, I just chose it like that.” (WCC009).

Parents also searched online for provider reviews and recommendations from members of online social groups. Personal experiences with providers were also a common source of information. As one parent stated, “He has been our family doctor for my family, who has 26 kids, [for] over ten years.” (WCC010).

Parents reported several barriers when preparing for WCC visits, including structural, provider, and parent-level barriers. Structural-level barriers were primarily related to insurance. Public and private insurance policies can limit parents’ choice of providers by specifying the providers, hospital systems, and services that will be accepted and covered.

“I consistently have been changing [doctors]…. I go where I’m allowed to go on a sliding scale, [and] because Medicaid doesn’t always stay consistent. If you miss a checkmark or something or paperwork, you lose that doctor. And then…. You [Medicaid] may shift, and they will assign a doctor. And so that’s how we go, go where we’re allowed.” (WCC002).

Medicaid members found that program resources were sometimes outdated, and approved provider lists included those no longer accepting Medicaid or new patients. Medicaid members also experienced difficulties navigating program requirements and administrative processes, as well as problems getting support for program-related questions, and delays in receiving care due to administrative errors made by Medicaid staff.

Provider-level barriers included difficulties scheduling appointments. Parents often found that public and private insurance-approved providers had limited availability, either being closed to new patients or having long waiting lists to become new patients.

“But one of the major problems right now is even getting in to see a physician. Establishing care, physicians are booked out for months. You know, and I, I get it. Like I said, I understand the nature of the beast, but what do I do with my kids?” (WCC037).

When providers were accepting new patients, it was sometimes challenging for parents to schedule timely appointments due to full schedules. In addition to limited appointment availability, many parents found the office hours of the providers to be restrictive. Few parents reported the availability of evening or weekend appointment times.

“…. I work so much. I work Monday through Friday. And I have to do that because I need to make ends meet…. I work Monday through Friday; it’s really challenging for me to make an appointment for one [child] at a time. And our schedule is [made], like, every month. So usually, I try to schedule all of them at the same time, especially when I’m off a day within the week.” (WCC038).

Parent-level barriers often involved conflict with work schedules. Many parents reported difficulty taking time off from their jobs to attend WCC visits, which was further complicated for those with multiple children. Many parents also had to consider that time off work meant income lost, which for some was prohibitive. Other considerations for parents with multiple children, especially those with limited help from family or friends, were the need to manage multiple children during an appointment or arrange for childcare.

“Yes. taking multiple kids to a wellness visit is really hard to do…. if it’s just for one kid, but you have to take both kids, that’s hard. That is no fun at all, because your toddler, they, they know what the doctor is, and they don’t like the doctor at this point.” (WCC016).

Experiences at the well-child visit

After parents decided to attend a WCC visit and completed the necessary preparations, several factors influenced their perceptions of the visit experience, including trust-building, conflict resolution, the physical environment of the provider’s office, and the treatment they received from the providers and office staff. Trust-building was most closely associated with parents feeling their providers were effectively listening to their concerns and respecting their opinions. The more effort a provider puts into effective communication and answering questions, the more parents trust their recommendations.

“… listening is one thing. Sometimes doctors, I feel like because they’re so intelligent and they know their field, it’s kind of like they will just tell you a whole bunch of things they think is …. what’s going on with you without really talking with you to hear your point of view. But I felt like they were really patient with us on several occasions and was allowing us to …. state our opinions, really talk through stuff. And then …. gave us their thoughts around what we could do. So listening was huge, and then doing things thoroughly was a big part.” (WCC042).

Further contributions to trust-building included when providers followed up with parents after WCC visits to check on treatment progress and answer additional questions. Parents felt that this demonstrated the provider’s engagement and interest in their children’s well-being.

The way providers resolved conflicts or confusion about their recommendations with parents also influenced the level of trust parents had in the providers. Satisfactory resolution and trust-building depended significantly on whether parents felt that their provider respected their views and opinions, demonstrated concern, allowed them to ask questions, and provided comprehensive answers and information. Unsatisfactory resolution and mistrust resulted not only from providers failing to listen or show respect, but also from their failure to adequately answer parents’ questions or exhibiting impatience or frustration with them.

“So, they wanted me to start giving them formula. Um, I had never [done] formula [before]. So, I just had a lot of questions, and I felt like the nurse practitioner who was with the pediatrician, I just felt like I was annoying her…. and I was getting half answers…. I couldn’t wait till she would leave my room so then I could just ask my nurse.” (WCC026).

The general cleanliness and condition of the office space influenced parents’ perceptions of their WCC visit. When talking about their experiences during office visits, one parent recalled, “It feels like we aren’t really in America…. I can’t believe the nastiness…. You’re locked in it. Medicaid clinics have very heavy doors. Like an institution…. it’s a degrading experience. It makes you grateful to go home….” (WCC002). Interactions with rude office staff also impacted parents’ perception of the WCC visit experience, with some feeling the behavior was directed specifically toward them. Lengthy wait times were a problem for some parents, and those managing children during these times reported that a lack of entertainment in waiting rooms was an issue. Many also acknowledged the potential for illness and disease transmission if entertainment were to be made available.

After the interviews, we asked parents an open-ended question for suggestions on how they could attend WCC visits more easily and consistently. An overview of their responses reveals perspectives that adherence could be improved with a multipronged approach. This approach should include (a) increasing awareness among employers of WCC importance, (b) working with providers on establishing service hours outside the routine workday schedule, (c) creating clinic office spaces and staff that are supportive to entire families during WCC visits, and (d) building the skills of parents to manage WCC of their children.

Decision making - why parents go to well-child visits

In comparing differences between stratification groups, our results showed that those who were married or from the higher-income group reported more trust in their current healthcare providers. Insurance type and geographical context showed nearly equal levels of provider trust. The lack of adequate health insurance coverage and medical care costs were more common barriers to attending WCC visits for those who were single or from the lower-income group. Additionally, Medicaid members also found the costs of medical care to be a greater barrier to healthcare than the privately insured. Participants who were married, had a higher income, were privately insured, or lived in a large metro area were more likely to trust online sources for healthcare information than their counterparts. Married participants also relied on advice from their healthcare providers, friends, and family for information. Medicaid members were least likely to be influenced by their friends’ and family’s healthcare beliefs, and the higher-income participants were most likely to have healthcare beliefs that aligned with those of their community.

Preparing to attend and experiences at the well-child visits

When choosing a provider for their children, the experiences and recommendations of trusted friends and relatives were most important to those who were married, had higher incomes, were privately insured, or lived in small metro areas. Single parents, those with lower incomes, or those living in large metro areas were more likely to report difficulties scheduling appointments with providers. These difficulties included obtaining time off work, access to dependable transportation, limited office hours for providers, and managing childcare.

We asked parents to recall experiences during their WCC visits. For those who had experienced confusion about or disagreement with their providers’ recommendations, parents with higher incomes, those who were privately insured, or those living in small metro areas were more likely to feel their providers addressed their concerns satisfactorily. Medicaid members were more likely to think their concerns were either not addressed satisfactorily or unresolved. Other problems reported during WCC visits more often by single, low-income, or Medicaid participants included long wait times, unclean environments, and rude or inappropriate treatment from office staff.

Discussion

Parents attempted to adhere to their provider’s recommended visits regardless of barriers. While many knew that regular checkups were necessary, particularly for vaccinations in the first year, few knew the recommendations for WCC visit timing. Parents’ impression that WCC visits are important is mirrored in the literature showing that a lack of WCC can increase health and social risks for children, such as susceptibility to communicable diseases [20], undetected neurodevelopmental disabilities [7], and reduced access to screenings for social needs [24]. Since parents are willing to adhere to their providers’ recommendation to attend WCC visits, especially when they trust their provider, we should continue to find ways to strengthen the relationship between parents and providers.

In line with the literature, respondents with lower incomes reported more structural barriers to adherence and higher rates of emergency services use than those with higher incomes [25]. Possible drivers for increased use of emergency services could include particular challenges associated with having low socioeconomic status resulting in complex living situations that may include single parenthood and food insecurity, which often become prioritized over preventive care and WCC [26]. Gaps in detecting conditions such as asthma, material deprivation, or a lack of anticipatory guidance for injury prevention, resulting in the need for emergency services, are also possible contributors [25]. Given the higher medical, societal, and family costs involved in attending emergency visits, interventions to increase adherence to WCC could potentially be valuable and even cost-saving for society, providers, and families.

The findings of our analyses suggest that caregivers report a more positive experience of WCC when they feel the providers listened to their concerns and when any past disagreements have been resolved satisfactorily. In line with this finding, interventions that highlight caregivers’ autonomy and emphasize time to listen to concerns, such as motivational interviewing, have been found to be effective in raising vaccine coverage in a large sample of individuals [9].

When asked about their level of trust in their children’s primary care providers, some parents commented about their level of trust in vaccines, as well. This could have been in response to recent public conversations about vaccine hesitancy, particularly around the COVID-19 vaccines [27]. However, public mistrust in healthcare systems has been a topic of concern for health professionals long before the COVID-19 pandemic [28].

Findings suggest that adherence to the American Academy of Pediatrics periodicity schedule is influenced more by factors external to the parents, such as finding a Medicaid provider who has availability, and managing work and childcare schedules, rather than intentional non-adherence by caregivers. Most parents we interviewed believed that the visits have value for their children and themselves, similar to findings in other studies [29]. A few parents expressed reluctance to vaccinate or viewed the visits as merely “checking a box” with minimal added value, but most appreciated the opportunity to confirm their child’s health or ask questions. Most (44 out of 45) of the parents endorsed a usual source of care for their children’s primary care; a usual source of care is also linked to improved adherence [30]. Being well-connected to a provider can influence the level to which the parents accept the provider’s guidance. In this study, the parent who did not have a usual source of care for the child also did not trust most guidance from the healthcare system generally, citing a level of medicalization that she felt was unnecessary.

We found evidence that even when parents self-reported following their provider’s recommendations, they still might not attend within the time frames recommended by the AAP, demonstrating the need for provider-centered interventions to increase WCC schedule adherence. Previously, reminder systems showed some degree of success [31]. More effective technology use and interactive planners could improve parent knowledge and self-efficacy related to healthcare [32]. Self-efficacy plays a mediating role between knowledge and behavior; for example, increased self-efficacy helps promote parental self-confidence and competence in learned skills such as navigating healthcare systems, scheduling appointments, and following through on providers’ recommendations [33]. Building the capacity of professional or personal support systems to aid problem-solving around barriers could improve parental adherence [34].

Implications of this research include an understanding among WCC providers that parents of young children are usually open to following clinicians’ guidance, if they also feel they and their children’s concerns are adequately heard and addressed. Additionally, it is important to note that parents often do not have a good understanding of why the WCC schedule is arranged the way it is, even as they recognize the value in a long-term relationship and guidance from a primary care provider for their children. Given these insights, it is critical to focus on supporting the patient-parent-provider relationship for WCC while also mitigating and removing as many structural and provider barriers as possible. Policy implications of this research also include ensuring the importance of WCC is understood not just among parents and healthcare professionals but also among employers and policy makers at the federal, state, and local levels.

While the present study may not be generalizable to the entire country due to significant regional differences around the United States [35], it provides insight into the Midwest region and a framework for others to use in different contexts. Additionally, due to concerns about the impact of responses in multiple languages on analysis and the limitations of the research team, only parents proficient in English were included. As such, the study is not generalizable to non-English speaking populations [31]. Despite efforts, the research team could not identify and recruit individuals without insurance. Challenges faced while recruiting parents included caregivers’ reluctance to reveal personal information for fear of negative consequences, including challenges to caregiver custody by government agencies. Furthermore, only one father participated in the study, compared to 44 mothers, which could potentially bias the results toward mothers’ experiences. It is noteworthy that most caregivers who take their children to the doctor are mothers [36], although this may be changing [37]. Finally, this study did not collect information about whether a child had special health care needs, which limits the understanding of drivers for these families. Literature suggests that parents of such children are generally more adherent, except when difficulties arise due to complex needs and scheduling appointments with multiple providers [15].

Conclusion

Despite parents’ lack of knowledge of the WCC schedule, they showed a willingness to follow providers’ recommendations and saw value in WCC. However, multiple barriers complicate adherence. Lower-income parents using Medicaid reported more barriers and increased use of emergency services. Targeted interventions have the potential to increase WCC adherence, which could be beneficial for families and cost-saving for the health care sector.
