Optimizing myocardial infarction detection: a hybrid CNN-GRU deep learning approach
Authors: Zahra Aghababaei, Leili Tapak, Mahdi Rasoulinia, Mahlagha Afrasiabi, Seyed Kianoosh Hosseini, Irina Dinu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyocardial infarction (MI) is a life-threatening condition caused by sudden interruption of blood supply to the heart. Electrocardiogram (ECG) is the primary tool for MI diagnosis, but interpretation challenges exist. This study aimed to optimize MI detection by developing a hybrid CNN-GRU Deep Learning model (DLM) based on ECG as a diagnostic support tool.MethodsThis retrospective diagnostic study included a total of 56,354 ECGs, comprising 41,871 from patients diagnosed with (MI) and 14,474 from healthy patients. Each ECG record consisted of a 20-second 15-lead recording per individual, sampled at 1000 Hz. The CNN-GRU model was trained on 85% of these ECGs and validated on the remaining 15%. The CNN-GRU model was executed on the pre-processed data using the Pan-Tompkins algorithm obtained from the PhysioNet website (PTB Diagnostic ECG Database), and all recordings were labelled by expert cardiologists. We examined a new model for classifying ECG heartbeats and found that it can compete with advanced models. The performance of the DLM was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, Macro Average, and Weighted Average.ResultsThe area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of the CNN-GRU model for MI detection was close to one. The CNN-GRU model achieved excellent performance with 15 leads (ACC = 99.43%, sensitivity = 99.71%, specificity = 98.59%). Using lead II alone, performance improved slightly (ACC = 99.73%, sensitivity = 99.75%, specificity = 99.66%). The high AUC and other metrics indicate strong diagnostic ability. Based on the reported results, the CNN-GRU model using lead II was the best model.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that the proposed model can support clinical decision-making and guide future research in cardiovascular medicine.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading global cause of death, accounting for over 17 million deaths annually and representing around 31% of worldwide mortality. Key risk factors contributing to CVD include obesity, tobacco use, hypertension, diabetes, and genetic predisposition [1]. Among the various manifestations of CVD, Myocardial Infarction (MI), commonly referred to as a heart attack, is the leading cause of death. It is particularly prevalent in individuals with a prior history of heart disease [2, 3]. The term “myocardial infarction” is derived from three components: “myo,” which signifies muscle; “cardial,” referring to the heart; and “infarction,” which denotes tissue death. This terminology underscores the condition’s nature, where heart tissue suffers damage or dies due to inadequate blood [4]. MI occurs when the blood flow to a specific area of myocardium (the heart muscle) is disrupted, typically due to a blockage in one of the coronary arteries. These arteries supply oxygen-rich blood to the heart muscle. When a blockage forms, often as a result of the accumulation of plaque, it can severely restrict or entirely obstruct blood flow to that region of the heart. If blood flow is not restored promptly, the affected heart muscle begins to undergo necrosis, leading to significant and potentially life-threatening complications [5, 6].

An electrocardiogram (ECG) is instrumental in promptly diagnosing MI [6]. The electrocardiogram (ECG) is a widely used, easy-to-set-up, painless, and inexpensive diagnostic tool for heart disease, as it records the heart’s electrical activity from a specific view. Each lead, representing a distinct view, measures voltage changes between electrodes positioned at various points on the body. The bipolar leads include I, II, and III, while the unipolar leads consist of aVR, aVL, aVF, and V1 through V6. The six chest leads do not adequately visualize certain heart regions, such as the right ventricular wall and the posterior wall of the left ventricle. To enhance the detection of MI in these areas, a 15-lead ECG is used, incorporating the standard 12 leads along with V4R, V8, and V9. Viewing the heart from multiple angles provides a more comprehensive perspective compared to a single angle [7]. In many cases, MI involving these areas can be missed or misinterpreted due to the absence of typical ST-elevation patterns. The addition of posterior leads—V7, V8, and V9—extends the standard ECG and improves the detection of infarctions in these hidden regions. reported that in approximately 15% of patients with chest pain and suspected acute coronary syndrome, the diagnosis of STEMI was possible only after recording the posterior leads, while the standard 12-lead ECG failed to indicate it. Their study highlighted the diagnostic value of the 15-lead ECG in enhancing early detection and appropriate treatment decision-making for MI [8]. The interpretation of ECG signals to diagnose MI involves identifying specific features such as the Q wave’s unnatural appearance, the ST segment’s elevation, and the T wave’s curvature [9]. However, this process can be complex and subtle, often necessitating the expertise of skilled cardiologists [10]. Additionally, ECG findings can be equivocal or normal early in some MIs, leading to potentially detrimental diagnostic delays [11].

Given these challenges, automated computer-based analysis offers a promising solution to enhance the accuracy and consistency of MI diagnosis from ECG signals. The intelligent diagnosis of MI based on ECG is essential to ensure patient safety and timely treatment. With the advent of deep learning techniques, there has been significant advancement in the automated analysis of ECG signals for diagnosing MI. These technologies not only improve diagnostic accuracy but also reduce the workload on doctors, allowing them to focus on patient care [12]. Therefore, research in this field deserves further attention.

Deep learning models, such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) networks, have demonstrated the ability to accurately classify ECG data and detect MI with high sensitivity and specificity [13].

In addition, Lidan Fu et al. utilized the PTB dataset to detect and locate MI via 12-lead ECG records. A novel multi-lead attention (MLA) mechanism has been proposed, integrating a convolutional neural network (CNN) with a bidirectional gated recurrent unit (BiGRU) framework, referred to as MLA-CNN-BiGRU. In MI detection under the intra-patient scheme, they achieved an average accuracy of 99.93%; in MI detection under the inter-patient scheme, they achieved an average accuracy of 96.50% [24]. Cao Y et al. developed a multi-scale ResNet model with an attention mechanism for detecting and localizing different types of MI. The model, combined with a residual network and attention mechanism, acquired multi-dimensional ECG feature information, achieving an average accuracy of 99.98 for MI detection and 99.79 for MI localization [11]. Jahmunah & E.Y.K. Ng present a Dirichlet DenseNet model for MI diagnosis with uncertainty quantification. They utilized PTBs database. The model was trained on the pre-processed ECG segments using reverse KL divergence. Following training, the model was tested using newly synthesized ECG signals augmented with electromagnetic (em) and motion artifact (ma) noise samples. The model’s accuracy is 80% for eM noise and 82.4% for ma noise [25]. Firoz et al. proposed a deep learning-based predictive model to detect MI from 15 ECG leads, a combination of the dilated CNN(ConvNetQuake) and an LSTM network. They achieved an accuracy of 97.7% [4]. Chuang Han & Jiajia Sun et al. proposed a model for automating MI detection using the MLB-ResNet-SENet-BL model with interpretability analysis. The ECG database was preprocessed with denoising, down-sampling, and QRS detection. The model achieved over 99% performance for MI detection and localization. Accuracies were 96.55% and 67.89% for inter-patient MI detection [26]. Peng Xiong et al. proposed a multi-branch network for IMI detection with DenseNet and GRU. Their model combines deep and shallow features of ECG signals. They achieved 99.95% high accuracy in intra-patient and inter-patient and 88.86% accuracy in inter-patient schemes [27].

Kiymac et al. developed a CNN-based arrhythmia classification model with hyperparameter optimization using a memory-enhanced artificial hummingbird algorithm (MAHA). Their method, applied to raw ECG signals from the MIT-BIH database, achieved 98.87% accuracy and outperformed several other metaheuristic algorithms. The model requires no manual feature extraction, making it efficient for real-time ECG analysis [28].

Research gap

Despite significant progress in deep learning-based MI detection, current approaches exhibit several critical limitations. First, while existing models demonstrate impressive accuracy (93.5–99.9%), their reliance on 1–12 ECG leads [6, 24, 26, 27] inherently limits their ability to detect regional ischemia in cardiac areas not well visualized by standard leads. Second, the generalizability of these models remains questionable as they are primarily trained and validated on limited datasets such as PTB or MIT-BIH [28], without adequate testing across diverse patient populations. Third, most studies fail to address practical clinical challenges including lead placement variability and motion artifacts [25], significantly limiting real-world applicability. Finally, although recent CNN-GRU hybrid models [24, 26, 27] show promise, they do not fully exploit the rich 3D spatiotemporal information available in comprehensive 15-lead ECG recordings.

Study aims and innovation

To address these limitations, our study introduces several key innovations. We developed a specialized 15-lead optimized model that uniquely combines CNN-based spatial feature extraction with GRU-based temporal dynamics analysis, enabling comprehensive characterization of cardiac electrical patterns. This approach specifically enhances diagnostic precision for challenging MI localizations, particularly in posterior and lateral walls, through advanced multi-lead analysis. Furthermore, drawing inspiration from recent advances in noise-robust architectures [28], our framework incorporates features designed to improve reliability in clinical environments where signal quality may be compromised.

Theoretical and practical significance

Our work bridges three crucial domains in cardiovascular diagnostics. Clinically, it addresses the critical need for earlier detection of occult MIs through sophisticated multi-lead analysis. Technically, the novel CNN-GRU architecture represents a significant advancement in 3D ECG feature fusion, providing new insights into spatiotemporal relationships in cardiac electrical activity. From a translational perspective, the adaptable nature of our framework shows particular promise for implementation in resource-limited settings, effectively narrowing the gap between theoretical research and practical clinical application. Together, these contributions position our work as both a methodological advancement and a practical solution to current diagnostic challenges in MI detection.

Many existing methods for MI detection rely on the standard 12-lead ECG and have not fully explored the potential of using additional leads. In this study, we propose an innovative model for MI detection using 15-lead ECG data. Utilizing 15 leads provides a more comprehensive view of the heart’s electrical activity, leading to more precise identification of affected areas and reducing diagnostic errors.

To achieve this, we introduce a novel model that capitalizes on the strengths of convolutional neural networks to capture spatial features and gated recurrent units to model temporal dependencies in ECG signals. This framework underscores the significant potential of using deep learning representations in ECG signal analysis and can contribute to improving the accuracy and efficiency of medical diagnoses.

Methods

Study design and data preprocessing

The PTB database was obtained from the “Physionet” website [29]. This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (approved by “The Ethical Committee of the Hamadan University of Medical Sciences”; NO. IR.UMSHA.REC.1403.044). The PTB database with a sampling rate of 1000 Hz comprises 15-lead ECGs of 52 healthy control (HC) subjects and 148 MI subjects [30]. The database comprises 549 records collected from 290 individuals, aged 17 to 87 years, with a mean age of 57.2 years. This cohort includes 209 men (mean age: 55.5 years) and 81 women (mean age: 61.6 years). Age data were unavailable for 14 male and 1 female subject. Each subject has between one and five records. No subjects have the numbers 124, 132, 134, or 161. Each record contains 15 simultaneously recorded signals, including the standard 12 leads (I,II, III, avr, avl, avf, v1, v2, v3, v4, v5, v6) and the 3 Frank lead ECGs (vx, vy, vz) [2, 31]. Most ECG recordings were typically 2 minutes long, and all measurements were recorded for at least 30 seconds [32]. The preprocessing steps included denoising, down_sampling, QRS detection, and beat segmentation [33]. Given that ECG beats serve as the primary input for our proposed method, we utilized a straightforward yet effective preprocessing technique for ECG signal processing and beat segmentation. In this stage, the first of the five recorded sessions for each individual was selected for processing. To standardize and unify the duration of the signals, a 20-second segment was chosen from each signal for every individual [34]. Subsequently, noise removal and beat segmentation were performed on the selected 15-lead signals using the Pan-Tompkins algorithm [35].

The proposed method

In this section, a brief description of the proposed method was provided. Fig. 4 shows the general block diagram of the proposed CNN-GRU method. The raw ECG from the 15 leads, after being collected, pre-processed, and segmented are fed into the network and classified into two classes: normal and MI.Fig. 4The CNN-GRU model

The CNN-GRU model

This model aims to extract complex features from ECG signals and classify them into two groups: MI and healthy control. This model consists of a combination of convolutional layers, residual blocks, GRU layers, and fully connected layers. The model works with ECG signals of length 500 samples and one channel. This input is fed as raw data into the neural network. The model begins with a convolutional layer responsible for extracting basic features from the input signal. This layer uses 32 filters with a kernel size of 5. The model includes five residual blocks, each consisting of two convolutional layers and one additive layer. These blocks are designed to learn more complex features from the ECG signal. Overall, the structure of each residual block is as follows: Convolutional layers, additive layer, activation layer, pooling layer.

Following the residual blocks, the model incorporates a GRU layer with 40 units. This layer is designed to model temporal dependencies in ECG signals and facilitates the learning of complex temporal patterns. Subsequent to the GRU layer, the flattened output is passed through several fully connected layers:

Flatten Layer: This layer converts the output from the GRU layer into a one-dimensional vector.

Dense Layers: Two consecutive Dense layers, each consisting of 32 neurons with ReLU activation functions.

Output Layer: A Dense layer with a final size corresponding to the number of classes, and a SoftMax activation function for final classification.

ReLU activation functions are employed in both the convolutional and fully connected layers to allow the model to capture nonlinear and complex features. Additionally, the SoftMax activation function is used in the output layer to transform the output values into classification probabilities.

The model training process involved several key components to ensure effective learning from the data. Each model was trained for a total of 30 epochs. During each epoch, the entire training dataset was divided into smaller subsets called batches. In this study, each batch consisted of 256 samples. The training was performed using the Adam optimizer, which is well-regarded for its efficiency in adjusting the model’s weights. We employed a learning rate scheduling strategy to dynamically adjust the learning rate during the training process. Instead of using a fixed learning rate, we utilized a scheduling method to optimize the training efficiency and model performance.

The learning rate was adjusted according to a predefined schedule, which allowed it to decrease progressively over time.

Performing criteria

In this study, the performance of the proposed method for classifying ECG signals from individuals with MI and healthy control individuals was evaluated using a comprehensive set of performance metrics. These metrics are based on fundamental concepts such as True Positive (TP), False Positive (FP), True Negative (TN), and False Negative (FN). Specifically, True Positives refer to correctly identified instances, while True Negatives denote correctly rejected unknown instances. On the other hand, False Positives (Type-I errors) occur when the system mistakenly accepts an unknown sample as a legitimate class, and False Negatives (Type-II errors) happen when a legitimate sample is misclassified as an incorrect class. These foundational metrics form the basis for calculating several key performance indicators.

To assess the models’ effectiveness, various metrics were computed, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The following equations illustrate the calculation of each metric:

Accuracy (ACC): measures the proportion of correctly classified instances out of all instances. 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rm{Accuracy}} = {{TP + TN} \over {TP + FP + FN + TN}}$$\end{document}

Accuracy provides an overall view of the model’s performance and was specifically evaluated for MI localization models in this study.

Precision: assesses the proportion of relevant instances retrieved among the total instances retrieved. 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rm{Precision}} = {{TP} \over {TP + FP}}$$\end{document}

Precision highlights the model’s ability to correctly identify positive cases out of the total predicted positives.

Recall (Sensitivity): measures the proportion of actual positive instances that were correctly identified by the model. 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rm{Recall}} = {{TP} \over {TP + FN}}$$\end{document}

F1-Score: provides a balance between precision and recall, offering a single metric that accounts for both false positives and false negatives 4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rm{F}}1 - {\rm{Score}} = {{{\rm{Precision }} \times {\rm{ Recall}}} \over {{\rm{Precision }} + {\rm{ Recall}}}} \times 2$$\end{document}

Additionally, for a more nuanced understanding of performance across various classes, both Macro Average and Weighted Average metrics were computed. The Macro Average provides an unweighted mean of the performance metrics across all classes, treating each class equally, while the Weighted Average adjusts for class imbalances by weighting metrics according to class prevalence.

Discussions

In this research, we proposed a new method for ECG heartbeat classification. The model focused on MI classification using the 15 ECG leads. This paper’s main contribution was designing a CNN-GRU model based on CNN to classify the ECG signals to healthy control and MI. Our model using 15 leads achieved an accuracy of 99.43% in terms of the general classification in addition to a recall of 99.51% and an F1-score of 99.61% in terms of MI detection.

According to the results presented, the proposed model exhibited high accuracy and performance in detecting MI. Additionally, the CNN model, with the addition of the GRU layer, demonstrated very high performance in identifying MI, and the number of misdiagnosed cases with the CNN-GRU model using lead II was minimized. These results indicated that the correct combination of neural network layers, such as the GRU layer, along with the optimal use of ECG leads, could significantly enhance the performance of diagnostic models. This layer allowed the model to extract more detailed and deeper features from ECG signals. Several studies used this dataset for the detection of MI. Considering that the data influenced the results of machine learning methods, an effort was made to compare the results of this thesis with the findings of studies that have proposed new methods on the same dataset. The superior performance of our model using lead II can be explained by its well-established diagnostic value in ECG-based studies. Lead II is commonly used in ECG-based studies due to its ability to provide a clear view of the heart’s electrical activity, particularly the P wave, QRS complex, and T wave, which are essential for accurate classification of cardiac events. Several studies have demonstrated the diagnostic strength of lead II when used alone.

For instance, the study by Issa et al. (2023) utilized a deep learning model with single lead-II ECG signals and successfully classified six types of arrhythmias with high accuracy, attributing this performance to the clarity of morphological features in lead II [39]. Similarity, Park et al. (2021) showed that among multiple ECG leads, lead II consistently yielded superior classification results in rhythm-type ECG classification tasks [40]. Furthermore, Julian S et al. (2023) also highlighted that lead II, either alone or in combination with V1, provides sufficient discriminatory information for detecting atrial fibrillation, outperforming many other lead combinations [41]. Based on these findings, it can be reasoned that the outstanding performance of lead II in our model is likely due to its proven ability to capture the most informative features relevant to heart rhythm and structure, which is consistent with its clinical usage and prior research.

Also, the proposed model, which used 15 leads, has performed very well with 99.43% accuracy and 99.71% sensitivity, which reflected an excellent balance between accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and predictive accuracy. This model has also achieved favorable results in terms of specificity (98.59%). This model offered very good performance and could have been an ideal option for systems that used multiple leads. The use of 15 leads also increased diagnostic confidence in patients whose cardiac patterns were complex or who may have had early-stage disease, where higher accuracy could have made a big difference in diagnosis.

Compared to previous models, the model [36] which used 15 leads, had an accuracy of 97.56% and a sensitivity of 97.97%. This model had a weaker performance compared to the proposed model, especially in the Precision criterion, which was equal to 95.67%. Also, the model [37] using lead II had an accuracy of 97.44% and a sensitivity of 99.34%, but due to the lack of precision and F1-score values, a comprehensive comparison of the performance of this model was not possible compared to the proposed models.

Generally, the proposed model had a significant advantage over previous models due to its high accuracy and optimal performance in all criteria, especially prediction accuracy and the balance between sensitivity and specificity. Therefore, the use of these models was recommended in clinical applications to diagnose heart disease through ECG.

Comparing the proposed model with models presented in the literature review section, their models used different leads, resulting in inferior performance compared to our proposed method. The CNN-GRU model had a higher accuracy (99.43%) than that of the ML-CNN [14]. In subsequent analysis, increasing the number of leads from 4 to 15 significantly enhanced the model’s ability to diagnose diseases with greater accuracy. Moreover, the sensitivity of the CNN-GRU model (99.70%) was higher than the 95.40% sensitivity of the ML-CNN model, which meant a better detection of positive cases. Furthermore, the specificity of the CNN-GRU model (98.68%) was higher than that of the ML-CNN model, so this model was more successful in preventing wrong diagnoses (false positives).

Compared to the CNN-LSTM model, it was more accurate than this model and the proposed model performed better in terms of features.

More complex models such as CNN based on ResNet (2019), which used multiple leads including Ι, ΙΙ, and V1–V6, provided 93.3% sensitivity and 89.7% specificity. The accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of the proposed model were much higher than the resnet model.

The MFB-CBRNN (2020) model shown very high accuracies using complete leads (Ι, ΙΙ, ΙΙΙ, V1–V6, aVR, aVL, aVF) and evaluation based on classified and individual data. In classification evaluation, this model achieved 99.90% accuracy, but in individual evaluations, its accuracy decreased to 93.08%.

Overall, the proposed model demonstrated a marked improvement over previous models across all parameters. With near-perfect accuracy and high sensitivity and specificity, this model stood out as one of the most effective in its field. This enhanced performance was largely attributed to its innovative network architecture, particularly the incorporation of GRU layers, which enabled more efficient handling of sequential ECG data.

Conclusions

Considering that the CNN model also has high performance in detecting MI, using a model based on CNN also yields high performance. The proposed method in this study had higher accuracy compared to the aforementioned studies. It seems that this is due to the GRU layer, which has effectively extracted more complex features from the model. Additionally, the use of a greater number of leads has also contributed to improving the model’s performance. The present study is the first to focus on the combination of these two networks for ECG classification and heart attack detection.

Strengths and limitations

Limitations

This model focuses on classifying 20-second segments of ECG signals. This limitation may overlook important contextual information present in longer ECG recordings, which can be crucial for the accurate diagnosis of MI. The imbalance between MI and health cases is a potential limitation, however our model showed good performance.

Running code related to networks used with large volumes of data requires a powerful system.

One of the main limitations of this study is the lack of evaluation of the model’s generalizability across different ECG acquisition devices and patient demographics. The dataset used in this work was collected from a single source, which may limit the model’s performance when applied to data obtained from other devices or more diverse populations. Future studies should include external validation on multiple datasets to better assess the robustness and generalizability of the proposed model.

The dataset was randomly divided into training and test sets (85%/15%) without applying stratified sampling. Since the dataset was imbalanced, this may have led to class distribution bias in the subsets. Second, no specific overfitting prevention techniques—such as dropout, regularization, or early stopping—were employed during training. Although training and test losses were monitored to assess model performance, the lack of explicit overfitting control may have impacted the generalizability of the model. It is important to note that several similar studies have followed a similar approach without formal overfitting prevention, which is a common trend in this field. Nevertheless, incorporating such techniques in future work could improve model robustness and performance.

Strengths

Model CNN-GRU whether using lead II or 15 leads, consistently delivered stable results in terms of precision and sensitivity. This performance stability indicates that the model can achieve high accuracy under different conditions and with varying numbers of leads.

Model CNN-GRU, using only one lead (Lead II), performs very closely to the results obtained from 15 leads. This can be a significant advantage in practical applications, as using fewer leads typically results in reduced costs and increased patient comfort.

Future work

The model has a considerable number of components that can be changed, modified, and applied in several different ways. Thus, although the fine-tuning performed in this study may have provided a good starting point, performing a broader search of hyperparameters and different configurations would be interesting. In future studies, it is recommended to extend the application of the proposed neural network model to other ECG datasets in order to evaluate its generalizability and robustness across diverse data sources. Additionally, the network’s performance in multi-group classification, such as differentiating between various heart diseases, should be further explored to assess its effectiveness beyond binary classification tasks.
