Nerve-rattling infections! Clinical and radiological tracing of cranial nerve palsies to infectious skull base osteomyelitis
Authors: R Sharanya, Pankti Shah, Karthik K Mani, Pushkaran Muralitharan, Rajkumar Ramasamy, Virna M Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To analyze skull base osteomyelitis as a cause of isolated and multiple cranial nerve palsies. Report presentations of otogenic and non-otogenic types. Osteomyelitis is an inflammatory process of bone, primarily caused by pyogenic organisms, which can involve one or multiple parts of a bone and the surrounding soft tissues. It can affect any bone of the body, but skull base osteomyelitis (SBO) is rare,[1] with an overall mortality rate of 14.3%–22% and residual long-term neurologic sequelae being reported in 31% of cases, despite aggressive treatment.[2] In this study, we aim to analyze the incidence of SBO as the etiology of isolated and multiple cranial nerve palsies, report the various presentations of otogenic and non-otogenic SBO, determine whether the site of biopsy has an effect on the isolation of causative organism, and further emphasize on the importance of neuroimaging with contrast in patients with cranial nerve palsies at the first visit.

Methods

This was a single-center, retrospective study. We saw a total 276 patients with cranial nerve palsies over a period of four months. A total of 14 patients had multiple cranial nerve palsies, and 262 had isolated cranial nerve palsies (62 had 4th cranial nerve palsy, 79 had 3rd cranial nerve palsy, and 121 had 6th cranial nerve palsy). Out of these, 20 patients who presented with cranial nerve palsies and were diagnosed to have SBO were included over a period of four months. We did complete ocular examination, including slit lamp and fundus examination, neuroophthalmological examination, color vision testing with Ishihara chart, visual field by Humphrey field analyzer, and all patients underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and orbit with contrast. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from all patients. Institutional review board approval was obtained with study code number RET202300434.

Results

We had a total of 20 patients who clinically had cranial nerve palsy and were radiologically diagnosed as SBO. Incidence was 7.246 per 100,000. 75% (15 patients) were male, and 25% (five patients) were female. A total of 20% of our patients were in the age group of 70 to 75 years (four patients), 15% each (three patients each) in the age groups of less than 50 years, 56 to 60 years, 61 to 65 years, and 66 to 70 years. A total of 10% (two patients were between 51 and 55 years and 5% each (one patient each) between the ages of 76 to 80 years and 81 to 85 years.

Time of onset of symptoms to presentation to us was variable, with 30% (six patients) presenting between one week and one month, 25% (five patients) within 1 week, 25% (five patients) presenting between one and three months, and 20% (four patients) presenting after three months.

A total of 75% (15 patients) had diabetes mellitus, 30% (six patients) has hypertension, 35% (seven patients) had cardiac comorbidities, 15% (three patients) had hyperlipidemia, 10% (two patients) had nephritis, and 5% each (one patient each) had previous ear surgery, dental caries, thyroid disorders, stroke, seizures, a history of head trauma and epilepsy.

A total of 90% (18 patients) had a unilateral presentation, and 20% (two patients) had bilateral presentation.

The most common presenting symptom was double vision (75%, 15 patients), followed by headache seen in 45% (nine patients). A total of 30% (six patients) had defective vision, with one patient having bitemporal hemianopia. A total of 30% (six patients) had facial pain, and 25% (five patients) had ear pain. A total of 20% (four patients) had drooping of the upper eyelid, 20% (four patients) had eye pain, 15% (three patients) had inability to close their upper eyelid, and 15% (three patients) had decreased hearing. A total of 5% each (one patient each) presented with fever, tinnitus, nausea, and vomiting.

A total of 60% (12 patients) were clinically diagnosed to have multiple cranial nerve palsy, and 40% (eight patients) had isolated cranial nerve palsy. Out of these, the sixth cranial nerve was most commonly affected as seen in 65% (13 patients), followed by third nerve (50%, 10 patients). A total of 40% (8 patients) had fourth cranial nerve involvement, and 25% each (five patients each) had second and trigeminal nerve involvement. A total of 15% each (three patients each) had involvement of the facial and vestibulocochlear nerve.

A total of 70% (14 patients) had a non-otogenic source of infection, and 30% (six patients) had an otogenic source. Sphenoid sinus involvement was seen in 50% (10 patients), followed by a mastoid source in 35% (seven patients). A total of 20% each (two patients each) had ethmoid and frontal sinus involvement, and 5% (one patient) had maxillary sinusitis.

MRI of the brain and orbit with contrast showed sinusitis in 45% (nine patients), followed by otomastoiditis in 35% (seven patients) and pachymeningitis in 30% (six patients). A total of 20% (three patients) had abscess formation. A total of 15% (three patients) had bony erosions. Cavernous sinus involvement was seen in 25% (five patients), and the superior orbital fissure and orbital apex were involved in 10% each (two patients). A total of 5% each (one patient) each had involvement of the periparotid and perivertebral space and internal carotid artery occlusion.

Biopsy was done in all our patients with 35% (seven patients) showing only inflammatory cells. Fungi were isolated in 30% (six patients), bacteria were seen in 15% (three patients), acid-fast bacilli were seen in 5% (one patient). No pathogen was isolated in 20% (four patients). One of these patients had polymicrobial growth showing Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Aspergillus species.

All our patients were referred to an ENT surgeon and a neurosurgeon. Intravenous antibiotics were given to 55% (11 patients), and intravenous antifungal was given to 25% (five patients). A total of 5% each (one patient each) received intravenous steroids, antiepileptics, and antitubercular treatment. Transnasal sinus surgery was performed in 30% (six patients), and abscess drainage, sphenoidectomy, and mastoidectomy were performed in 10% each (two patients each). One patient underwent ethmoidectomy. A total of 40% (eight patients) were lost to follow up.

Discussion

Typical SBO occurs secondary to uncontrolled infection of the temporal bone region, most often from necrotizing external otitis. Atypical SBO may be secondary to advanced sinusitis or deep face infection and preferentially affects the central skull base and occipital bone or clivus.[3] From the ear and paranasal sinuses, it spreads to the periosteum of the tympanic bone, then via Santorini fissures and tympanomastoid sutures to the temporal bone apex to clivus and contralateral side. Intracranial spread can also occur by extension through the petroclival synchondrosis, resulting in meningitis, epidural abscess, and cavernous sinus thrombosis. If the cavernous sinus is affected, multiple upper cranial nerves can be involved. Infection can spread to the spinal canal and also spread through the parotid and parapharyngeal spaces to stylomastoid and jugular foramina, involving the facial nerve and lower cranial nerves [Fig. 1]. Carotid canal spread causes vascular occlusion,[4] resulting in infectious arteritis, thrombosis, pseudoaneurysm, and stroke.

SBO is predominantly seen in older patients.[567] In our study, the most vulnerable patients were elderly males aged 70 to 75 years. One male patient was aged 19 years. In the literature, acquired immune deficiency syndrome patients who develop malignant external otitis tend to be younger than the typical patient, and most of them are not diabetic.[8] In our study, there was no such patient, including the youngest one.

The skull base inflammations described in the literature most often affect diabetic or immunodeficient patients.[1] In our study, 75% were diabetic. Diabetics have a combination of immune dysfunction and microvascular angiopathy. The microangiopathy caused due to diabetes hampers tissue regeneration. Other diseases that cause vascular damage or poor oxygen delivery predispose to the development of SBO.[8]

Presenting symptoms of early SBO are non-specific.[9] In our study, most of the patients presented between one week and one month from the time of onset of symptoms. The course of the disease is often polysymptomatic, which may delay its diagnosis. Symptoms are treated locally for some time, usually with short courses of antibiotics without extensive investigations.

Most of our patients had unilateral presentation. Cranial nerve involvement is typically unilateral but can be bilateral in advanced SBO crossing the midline.[3]

Patients with typical SBO have otorrhea and severe otalgia, with pain often out of proportion to the physical findings, and atypical SBO presents with headache and facial pain.[51011] Headache was commonly present in our patients as the majority had non-otogenic source of infection, with many having symptoms of cranial nerve palsies as described above, and double vision was most frequently reported.

In previous studies, there has been more incidence of otogenic than non-otogenic source.[21213] However, in our study, infection from the paranasal sinuses was more common. This could be due to geographic factors and demographics of our study population. In a review of 84 cases of head and neck osteomyelitis from India by Prasad et al.,[13] sinusogenic, odontogenic, and otogenic infections accounted for 27%, 12%, and 5%, respectively. However, in contrast to our study, the mandible was the most common bone involved in this study (38%). In our study, common bones involved were the sphenoid, temporal, and clivus of the occipital bone. Frontal bone involvement was rare and seen in one patient. The study by Prasad et al.[13] also included a significant number of patients with malignancy and osteoradionecrosis of the jaw, which was different from our patient population. Rarely, cranial osteomyelitis can occur following head trauma.[141516] Our study group had one patient with post-traumatic SBO developed following a road traffic accident.

Multiple cranial nerve palsy was reported in a majority of our patients. Most common was the involvement of the sixth cranial nerve, which is seen in petrous apex involvement.[17] In another study, the facial nerve was reported to be the most common and first cranial nerve involved when the infection spreads subtemporally at the stylomastoid foramen.[18] With further spread, cranial nerves IX, X, and XI may be affected, with jugular foramen involvement.[1617] Lower cranial nerve involvement was not seen in any of our patients, possibly because such patients do not present to an ophthalmologist first. The occurrence of cranial nerve paresis indicates progression of the disease and such patients usually experience worse outcomes.[51011]

Due to non-specific clinical features and laboratory findings, radiological evaluation plays a prominent role in establishing a diagnosis in cases of SBO. Generally, a combination of computerized tomography (CT, with or without contrast enhancement) and contrast-enhanced MRI is used to diagnose and monitor SBO.[19] Although the CT scan is sensitive to bone erosion or periosteal remodeling, it is a poor choice for SBO to represent an intracranial extension, soft-tissue or bone marrow involvement, and to measure treatment response. MRI is more superior in the detection of early SBO also more sensitive in determining the extent of the disease.[1819] All our patients underwent MRI of the brain and orbit with contrast. As the disease progresses, repeat neuroimaging may need to be done to establish the diagnosis. Four of our patients had to undergo repeat imaging as the first imaging was done without contrast.

In atypical SBO, there is usually pre-clival soft-tissue involvement on MRI, producing nasopharyngeal swelling. The infection of the bone marrow results in a loss of normal fat signal. Soft-tissue infiltration of the pterygopalatine fossa with obliteration of normal fat in these regions and paranasal sinus opacification is typical of invasive sinusitis associated with SBO. In our study, invasive sinusitis was most commonly seen, followed by otomastoiditis, pachymeningitis, and abscess formation in necrotic bone marrow, seen as peripheral contrast enhancement.[319] Bony erosions seen in several of our patients can be subtle and involve only the cortical part of bone in early cases. In areas without prominent marrow and trabecular bone, this may be the only clue to SBO.[3] The most common differential diagnosis of SBO on imaging is neoplasms like nasopharyngeal carcinoma, sinonasal neoplasm, and lymphoma [Figs. 2-5].

Skull base or nasopharyngeal biopsies need to be performed in a timely manner to rule out these differential possibilities as well as to obtain tissue samples due to the potential for rapid progression of SBO.[1319] Our patients underwent transnasal endoscopic collection of pus and tissue specimens for microbiological and histopathological examination. Most of the biopsy results showed non-specific inflammatory cells. The isolation of an organism depends on several factors, such as the site where the biopsy is taken. Most of our patients had sphenoid sinus involvement. It is difficult to approach by the endoscopic route. The yield of organisms isolated depends on whether we pass the areas that are inhabited by normal microbiota, leading to the isolation of normal permanent or transient microbiota of the upper respiratory tract. Also, the growth of microorganisms can be delayed if the tissue is less oxygenated or if the patient receives prior antimicrobial treatment.[19] Among the isolated bacteria, Pseudomonas species was the most common in our study, as has been reported before.[567] The virulence of this Gram-negative bacterium is related to angioinvasion and small-vessel thrombosis. Among the fungal isolates, Aspergillus species was the most common. Otologic fungal infections, especially Aspergillus species, are increasingly reported in immunocompromised patients and can lead to fungal SBO. Mycobacteria species are being increasingly recognized in the immunocompromised population. Some cases are polymicrobial.

All our patients were referred to an ENT surgeon and a neurosurgeon for further management. Factors affecting the outcomes include the extent of involvement, the sensitivity profile if the pathogen is isolated, polymicrobial versus monomicrobial growth, and comorbidities. Our patients underwent medical management with parenteral antibiotics (meropenem, piperacillin-tazobactam, vancomycin) or antifungals (amphotericin B). One patient received antiepileptics, and one patient was treated with intravenous steroids as he had an associated traumatic optic neuropathy. One patient also received antitubercular therapy as acid-fast bacilli were isolated. Some of our patients also underwent surgical debridement as described above. There is no definite protocol for management, but mainstay treatment for SBO includes a course of culture-guided long-term intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy for three to six months until clinical recovery or radiological resolution. In cases of widespread soft-tissue involvement, early aggressive surgical removal of infectious sequestra is required. Even with early treatment, SBO can take several months before a complete resolution is achieved. Early intervention can lead to a better prognosis, fewer neurologic sequelae, and a lower mortality rate. Strict glycemic control in diabetic patients and improvements in the immune status of immunocompromised patients also play vital roles in the successful resolution of SBO.[113]

Conclusion

Diagnosis requires a high index of suspicion and is based on a combination of clinical findings, history, imaging, microbiology, and histology with a multidisciplinary approach involving ophthalmologists, otorhinolaryngologists, radiologists, pathologists, and neurosurgeons. Isolated cranial palsy does not exclude infectious etiology, even if the patient has comorbidities like uncontrolled diabetes, particularly if associated with headache and pain. Hence, neuroimaging with contrast is required. It is also important to check all the cranial nerves, as the ophthalmologist may be the first to pick up this fatal, yet underdiagnosed disease. Clinicians need to remain vigilant for recrudescent infection after completion of antimicrobial therapy owing to the continued exposure of bone to oral and nasal flora.

Limitations

Absence of ENT and neurosurgery facilities at our center, due to which several patients were lost to ophthalmology follow-up. The majority of the population we cater to are on the less privileged rural side, with constraints for expensive imaging modalities.
