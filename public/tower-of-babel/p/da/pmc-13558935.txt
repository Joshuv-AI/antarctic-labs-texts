Return to Sport After Excision of Os Trigonum of Talus in Athletes
Authors: Alexis Watson, Gokul Kalyanasundaram, Alexander J. Wahl, Wen Chao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Os trigonum syndrome is a common cause of posterior ankle pain in athletes performing repetitive hyper‑plantarflexion of the ankle. An accessory bone posterior to the talus, the os trigonum, may remain unfused or form a Stieda process, leading to pain from overuse, trauma, or impingement. Failure of conservative management often requires surgical excision. The objectives of this study are to evaluate the sustainability of long‑term pain relief and return to sport outcomes following open excision for bony impingement in athletes with os trigonum syndrome.Methods:A retrospective chart review identified 33 athletes who underwent open posteromedial os trigonum excision after at least 3 months of failed conservative treatment for os trigonum syndrome between 2014 and 2024. Exclusions were concomitant procedures not related to os trigonum syndrome or lost to follow‑up. Demographics, return to sport status, complications, and patient‑reported pain scores were collected. Pain scores were recorded preoperatively and at <1 month, 6 weeks, 3 months, and >1 year and compared with preoperative values using paired t tests. All athletes were interviewed as their most recent follow‑up.Results:The analysis included 26 athletes (35 ankles). Mean follow‑up was 7.3 ± 3.0 years. The average age was 17.4 ± 4.5 years, and 85% were female. Most were ballet dancers (n = 23), with 12% of athletes from other sports. Twenty‑five of 26 athletes returned to their sport. There was 1 reoperation for postoperative hematoma and no nerve complications. The mean preoperative pain score was 3.3 ± 3.3, with significant improvement at each time interval (P ≤ .033 for all comparisons). All athletes reported no pain at >1 year of follow-up.Conclusion:In this cohort of athletes with symptomatic os trigonum syndrome due to bony impingement after failed conservative management, open posteromedial excision was associated with substantial pain improvement, no neurovascular complications, and a high rate of return to sport.Level of Evidence:Level IV, retrospective case series

Introduction

Os trigonum syndrome is a well-recognized cause of posterior ankle pain in athletes undergoing repetitive hyperplantarflexion, occurring when an accessory os trigonum or a fused Stieda process becomes symptomatic because of trauma, overuse, or mechanical entrapment between the tibia and calcaneus (Figures 1 and 2).1-4 This pathology frequently impacts ballet dancers, soccer players, and gymnasts, necessitating targeted treatment to resolve impingement and restore function (Figure 3). The incidence of os trigonum occurs in about 9% of the feet, with higher prevalence in the East Asian population, and 33% are bilateral, based on the most recently meta-analysis study. 5 A magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study reported that 30% of the ballet dancers who have posterior ankle impingement syndrome have an os trigonum of the talus. 6 Surgical excision of the os trigonum or Stieda process, causing os trigonum syndrome, can be performed after failing nonoperative treatment, especially in athletes who want to resume their sport.

Os trigonum syndrome has been defined in the literature as a cause of posterior ankle pain. 2 Specifically, posterior ankle impingement syndrome (PAIS) represents a clinical diagnosis characterized by the mechanical entrapment of soft tissue or bony structures during terminal plantarflexion and should be distinguished from an isolated os trigonum, which refers strictly to the anatomical failure of secondary ossification of the talus that may or may not cause symptomatic impingement.1,2,7 Common causes of pain in the posterior ankle with plantar flexion of the ankle include, but are not limited to, bony impingement due to os trigonum or Stieda process, soft tissue impingement, flexor hallucis longus (FHL) pathology, Achilles tendinitis, retrocalcaneal bursitis, and posterior ankle and subtalar arthritis. Therefore, evaluation of posterior ankle pain should begin with a focused history and physical examination, as posterior ankle impingement is primarily a clinical diagnosis. Standard ankle radiographs, including a lateral view, are typically obtained initially to identify osseous abnormalities such as an os trigonum, whereas MRI may be used to further assess associated soft tissue pathology. When clinical findings and imaging are inconclusive, image-guided diagnostic injection can aid in confirming the source of pain.2,6-9

Current literature on os trigonum excision has demonstrated favorable outcomes after surgical excision.1,2,7,10-20 Open and arthroscopic approaches have been investigated, with the arthroscopic methods gaining popularity for their minimally invasive nature, fewer complications, and shorter recovery time.2,11-20 However, these methods are technically challenging and call for precision in a small space. 2 Open excision techniques include either a posteromedial or posterolateral approach, both of which provide direct access to the os trigonum and facilitate excision of larger ossicles.1,2,7,10 The posteromedial approach provides visualization of the FHL tendon and the neurovascular structures within the tarsal tunnel, allowing protection of these structures during dissection.1,7,15 In contrast, the posterolateral approach provides direct access to the posterior talus through the interval lateral to the Achilles tendon but does not require exploration of the tibial neurovascular bundle.1,7,15 Additionally, the posteromedial approach avoids complications related to the sural nerve that may occur with the posterolateral approach.2,15

The objectives of this study are to evaluate the sustainability of long-term pain relief using pre- and postoperative patient-reported pain scores and the ability to return to sports following an open posteromedial surgical approach for bony impingement for os trigonum syndrome in athletes after failing conservative management.

Methods

Approval was obtained by the institutional review board at our institution. A retrospective chart review was conducted to identify athletes with os trigonum syndrome due to bony impingement who underwent isolated os trigonum or Stieda process excision using an open posteromedial approach between March 2014 and March 2024. All procedures were performed by the senior author. Athlete was defined as patients who were actively participating in an organized sport at the time of the surgery, with the goal to get back to their sport after recovery. Inclusion criteria included all athletes diagnosed with os trigonum syndrome who underwent excision of the os trigonum or Stieda process of the talus after failing at least 3 months of nonoperative management. Exclusion criteria included athletes who underwent concomitant ankle procedures that were not related to os trigonum syndrome or who were lost to follow-up (Figure 4).

Study flow diagram outlining patient identification, exclusions, and final analytic cohort.

All eligible athletes identified during chart review had os trigonum syndrome due to bony impingement. Diagnosis was based on physical examination showing posterior ankle pain with passive maximum plantarflexion, no pain with active or passive FHL tendon movements, especially in full dorsiflexion. Imaging studies like radiographs, MRI, computed tomography (CT), and dynamic ultrasonography confirmed bony impingement from os trigonum or Stieda process as the pain source.

Demographic data were extracted, including age, gender, type of sport, ability to return to sport, and complications. Diabetes and smoking status were recorded. Operative reports and preoperative studies were used to classify os trigonum vs Stieda process excision. Patient-reported pain scores (0-10) were collected at predefined time points: preoperative, <1 month postoperative, 6 weeks, 3 months, and >1 year. All athletes were contacted by phone interview at most recent follow-up. Bias was minimized by using routinely recorded pain scores documented with a standardized numeric rating scale at predefined time points, and because of the study design, no additional analytic methods to control for confounding were implemented.

Statistical analysis used paired t tests to compare each postoperative pain score with the corresponding preoperative score. Missing data were handled by complete case analysis; patients without both preoperative and postoperative scores for a given time point were excluded from that comparison. All tests were 2-sided with a significance level of .05.

Surgical Technique

All surgical procedures were performed by the senior author under general anesthesia using an open posteromedial approach with patients in the supine position. Thigh tourniquet and Bipolar electrocautery were used on every case. A curvilinear incision centered along the neurovascular bundle through the skin was made. The flexor retinaculum was carefully released. The neurovascular bundle was then retracted posteriorly. The sheath of the FHL tendon was incised through the fibro-osseous tunnel, and the FHL tendon was retracted posteriorly. After identification of the os trigonum or Stieda process, passive ankle plantarflexion was performed to confirm bony impingement between the tibia and calcaneus. The os trigonum or Stieda process was excised using rongeur and/or osteotome. Passive plantar flexion of the ankle was performed again, confirming no further bony impingement with direct visualization and palpation. Intraoperative fluoroscopy was done to ensure that the entire os trigonum or Stieda process was excised. Skin closure was made using nonabsorbable sutures after thorough irrigation of the surgical wound. A padded splint was placed with the ankle in a neutral position.

Postoperative Protocol

Following surgery, patients were placed in a nonweightbearing short-leg splint for 10-14 days. At the first postoperative visit, the splint and sutures were removed, and patients were transitioned to a tall, controlled ankle motion (CAM) boot. Active ankle range of motion exercises were initiated at that time, and patients were instructed to gradually progress weightbearing as tolerated while wearing the CAM boot. Once full weightbearing in the CAM boot was achieved, formal physical therapy was initiated to facilitate rehabilitation and progression toward return to sport.

Results

A total of 33 athletes were identified. After exclusion of 2 athletes who underwent concomitant procedures that were not related to the os trigonum syndrome and 5 athletes who were lost to follow-up, 26 athletes were included in the final analysis. The final cohort consisted of 23 ballet dancers, 1 football player, 1 lacrosse player, and 1 soccer player. The mean age at the time of surgery was 17.5 ± 4.6 years (range, 12-35 years). Eighty-five percent of the athletes were female (n = 22). Nine athletes, who were all ballet dancers, underwent staged bilateral procedures. Of the 9 athletes who underwent bilateral procedures, the majority initially presented with bilateral posterior ankle pain and underwent staged procedures approximately 1 month apart; several underwent procedures approximately 1 year apart, and 1 patient underwent staged procedures nearly 2 years apart after initially presenting with unilateral symptoms. This resulted in a total of 35 procedures in 26 athletes undergoing final analysis. Nineteen procedures (54%) were performed on the right ankle and 16 (46%) on the left ankle. No patients had diabetes, and all were nonsmokers (Table 1).

The mean duration of follow-up was 7.3 ± 3.0 years (range, 1.7-12.4 years). Twenty-seven ankles had os trigonum, and 8 ankles had the Stieda process as the underlying bony anatomy. Only 1 patient who underwent staged procedures had a right ankle Stieda process and left ankle os trigonum.

Twenty-five of 26 athletes returned to sport without limitations (96.2%; 95% CI, 80.4%-99.9%). The average time to resume their sport was 12.5 ± 7.8 weeks (range 4.1-35.1 weeks). There was 1 postoperative complication, which required reoperation for incision and drainage of a hematoma. This occurred after the second surgery in the series of staged bilateral procedures. This athlete subsequently returned to full participation in ballet and eventually became a professional ballet dancer. One dancer who underwent bilateral staged procedures decided not to return to ballet due to other reasons. At the most recent follow-up interview, she had no pain and was able to resume all activities. There were no neurovascular complications or FHL tendon pathology. At final follow-up, 9 ballet dancers were participating at the professional level, and 1 athlete continued to play Division I football (Table 2).

Discussion

Our study suggests favorable clinical outcomes following open posteromedial excision of symptomatic os trigonum or Stieda process lesions in athletes with posterior ankle impingement due to isolated bony pathology. By limiting our cohort to athletes with symptomatic bony impingement and excluding patients undergoing unrelated concomitant procedures, we isolated the effect of os trigonum or Stieda process excision on postoperative outcomes. In this cohort, 96% (25/26) of athletes returned to sport at a mean of 12.5 weeks, with significant improvement in pain scores from a preoperative mean of 3.3 to 0 at final follow-up. Additionally, no neurovascular complications occurred, supporting the safety of the posteromedial approach in this patient population.

Several surgical techniques have been described for the treatment of symptomatic os trigonum syndrome, including arthroscopic/endoscopic, open posteromedial, and open posterolateral approaches. Arthroscopic techniques have gained popularity because of their minimally invasive nature and reported shorter recovery timelines.2,11-19 Prior studies in athletes have demonstrated reliable improvements in pain and functional outcomes following arthroscopic excision, with return-to-sport timelines often occurring within approximately 6-10 weeks depending on the population studied and the presence of associated pathology.13,16,18 However, direct comparisons between surgical approaches remain challenging because of differences in patient populations, inclusion criteria, concomitant procedures, and outcome measures. Importantly, differences in return-to-sport timelines should be interpreted cautiously given substantial variation in patient populations, concomitant procedures, and rehabilitation protocols among published studies.

Open excision remains an important treatment option for athletes with symptomatic os trigonum syndrome, particularly when direct visualization of the posterior talus or management of associated FHL pathology is required. Hamilton et al 1 reported favorable long-term outcomes following open excision in dancers, with the medial approach utilized most frequently and demonstrating the advantage of addressing concomitant FHL pathology when present. Similar to their findings, our cohort showed excellent pain relief, a high return-to-sport rate, and no neurovascular complications following isolated posteromedial excision. Similarly, prior studies evaluating open posteromedial excision in dancers have demonstrated significant improvements in pain and functional outcomes with high patient satisfaction rates and low complication rates.7,14 These findings are consistent with our cohort, which similarly demonstrated substantial pain relief, a 96% return-to-sport rate, and no neurovascular complications following isolated posteromedial excision.

The posteromedial approach provides several potential advantages, including improved visualization of the os trigonum and Stieda process, direct access to the FHL tendon, and reduced risk of sural nerve injury compared with posterolateral approaches. Prior reports of open posterolateral excision have described complications including sural nerve injury and wound-related issues. 10 In contrast, studies evaluating posteromedial approaches have demonstrated lower rates of neurovascular complications, consistent with the absence of sural or tibial nerve complications observed in our cohort.2,7,14 These findings suggest that the posteromedial approach may be particularly advantageous in athletes where preservation of posterior ankle function and reliable return to high-demand activity are critical.

Our study also evaluated outcomes following excision of os trigonum compared with Stieda process lesions. We found no difference in postoperative pain outcomes between these groups at >12-month follow-up. These findings align with prior literature demonstrating comparable outcomes following surgical treatment of symptomatic os trigonum and Stieda process lesions, suggesting that both represent effective surgical targets when the source of posterior ankle impingement is appropriately identified. 19

The strength of this study is the focused inclusion of athletes with isolated symptomatic bony posterior ankle impingement. Much of the existing literature on surgical treatment of os trigonum syndrome includes heterogeneous patient populations with combined bony and soft tissue pathology, including FHL tendinopathy and other concomitant procedures.1,2,7,10-20 By excluding unrelated concomitant procedures, our study provides a more specific evaluation of outcomes following isolated os trigonum or Stieda process excision in athletes. Additionally, the predominance of ballet dancers in our cohort represents a population with substantial biomechanical demands requiring repetitive extreme plantarflexion, providing a rigorous test of postoperative recovery following os trigonum excision.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design and relatively small sample size. Although all patients followed the senior author’s standard postoperative protocol, rehabilitation progression was individualized and therefore not strictly standardized. Additionally, we relied on patient-reported pain scores rather than validated functional outcome measures, and objective postoperative ankle range of motion data, including plantar flexion, were not consistently documented in the medical record and therefore could not be analyzed. Future studies are warranted to investigate the potential role of contralateral ankle pathology, prior surgery, and compensatory biomechanical adaptations in the development of symptomatic os trigonum syndrome. Although our cohort was primarily composed of ballet dancers (88%), the extreme plantarflexion and biomechanical demands of dance, combined with the successful outcomes observed in our other athletes, provide a robust performance benchmark generalizable to a broad range of athletes with posterior bony impingement.

Conclusion

Open posteromedial excision of symptomatic os trigonum syndrome due to bony impingement caused by an os trigonum or Stieda process was associated with favorable clinical outcomes in athletes who failed conservative management, including substantial pain improvement and a high rate of return to sport.
