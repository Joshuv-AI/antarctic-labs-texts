Stress, Transition-to-Practice, Burnout, and Turnover Intention in Early-Career Nursing (1981–2025): A Bibliometric and Science-Mapping Analysis
Authors: Alex S. Borromeo, John Paul E. Mendoza, Fritzie V. dela Cruz, Magdalena D. Soyosa, Walton Wider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStress during training, transition-to-practice strain, burnout, and turnover intention are recurring early-career nursing concerns and can be viewed in occupational safety and health terms as work-related psychosocial hazards.MethodsWeb of Science records (1970–31 Aug 2025) were screened, yielding 664 eligible articles (earliest eligible year: 1981). Using VOSviewer (version 1.6.20; Centre for Science and Technology Studies [CWTS], Leiden University, Leiden, the Netherlands) and Biblioshiny (version 5.0; web interface of bibliometrix, developed by Massimo Aria and Corrado Cuccurullo, University of Naples Federico II, Naples, Italy), we mapped the intellectual base via cocitation (minimum cocitation = 12), themes via coword analysis (minimum occurrence = 15; thesaurus-cleaned keywords), and change over time via thematic evolution (1981–1999, 2000–2009, 2010–2019, and 2020–2025).ResultsFive cocitation clusters centered on transition theory, stress/coping, burnout measurement, resilience, and organizational determinants of retention. Coword mapping yielded four thematic clusters spanning transition supports, student stress/mental health, early-career burnout/turnover intention, and well-being/self-efficacy interventions. The literature frequently links stress, transition-to-practice strain, burnout, and turnover intention; when these constructs are framed in a sequential narrative, we interpret this as thematic convergence rather than evidence of a causal or developmental mechanism. Retention was often operationalized via intention rather than observed turnover.ConclusionThe mapped literature depicts an interconnected psychosocial hazard domain linking education and early practice to retention-risk signals with implications for staffing stability, patient safety, and safety culture. We propose a three-pillar heuristic synthesis: structured residency/preceptorship with protected preceptor time, longitudinal screening using validated stress/burnout tools, and psychologically safe leadership. This framework is an interpretive synthesis informed by mapped themes and central empirical literature, not a validated intervention model and not a direct algorithmic output of the clustering procedure.

Introduction

Stress, transition, and retention remain central challenges affecting the stability of the nursing workforce. One of the most demanding points in a nurse’s trajectory is the shift from student to professional practice. Classic contributions offer enduring perspectives on this process. Kramer’s Reality Shock described how new graduates become disillusioned when idealized academic expectations collide with workplace realities [1]. Benner’s Novice to Expert framework explains how competence develops progressively with experience [2], while Duchscher’s transition shock theory highlights the intense emotional, cognitive, and physical strain experienced during the first year of practice [3]. Collectively, these theories underscore that early-career stress is not simply an individual weakness; it is shaped by educational preparation, workplace conditions, and organizational culture. From an occupational safety and health (OSH) perspective, these exposures can be interpreted as work-related psychosocial hazards because they emerge from job demands, role transition pressures, and unit-level conditions that can undermine well-being, functioning, and safe practice during a safety-critical early employment period.

Empirical evidence reinforces these insights. In the cited longitudinal literature, stress during nursing education is often reported to be associated with later burnout and turnover intention [4]. However, the present bibliometric mapping describes how these constructs are linked in the literature, rather than testing causal mechanisms. Systematic reviews identify persistent stressors such as academic pressures, heavy clinical workloads, and fear of making errors [5]. Stress is also associated with anxiety, depression, and reduced confidence, particularly during clinical placements and evaluation periods [[6], [7], [8]]. Measurement tools such as the Stress in Nursing Students scale and the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) have enabled cross-study visibility of these patterns across diverse settings [9,10].

Beyond individual outcomes, early-career stress has clear relevance for OSH and patient safety. Stress and burnout may impair attention, recovery, and confidence during high-demand periods and are discussed in the literature alongside safety-related outcomes (including patient safety concerns) and organizational conditions that shape safe practice [11,12]. Similarly, turnover intention is not only a workforce metric; it is a leading indicator of staffing instability, which can disrupt team cohesion, increase workload strain for remaining staff, and weaken organizational safety culture. In this sense, the transition-to-practice period is both a developmental phase and a safety-critical period for organizations seeking to sustain staffing and protect quality and safety of care [13].

Recent literature suggests three continuing trends. First, mental health concerns—including distress, emotional exhaustion, and low self-efficacy—remain common among nursing students and early-career nurses (i.e., new graduate/newly qualified/novice nurses) [14]. Second, coping strategies and structured supports (e.g., mentoring, resilience programs, and stress management training) are increasingly framed as core components of preparation rather than optional add-ons [14]. Third, the transition-to-practice period remains a vulnerable phase: across empirical studies, limited organizational support is frequently reported and is associated with dissatisfaction and higher turnover intention among early-career nurses [15]. Although cohort studies report associations among stress, transition experiences, burnout, and turnover intention, the present study uses bibliometric mapping to describe how these topics cluster in the literature and how their conceptual connections have shifted over time.

Despite the maturity of this field, scholarship remains dispersed across countries, methods, and theoretical traditions. Few studies integrate foundational transition theories, validated stress measures, and contemporary themes within a single synthesis. Moreover, organizational structures, leadership practices, cultural environments, and workforce policies are often examined separately rather than as interacting contexts. Bibliometric methods can help address these gaps. Cocitation analysis can trace influential works that shape the field’s intellectual base, while coword analysis can map evolving topics and emerging trends [[16], [17]].

To our knowledge, few bibliometric studies have mapped stress in training, transition-to-practice experiences, and early-career retention outcomes within a single integrated science-mapping analysis across multiple decades. Using cocitation and coword analyses of 664 Web of Science (WoS)–indexed articles (1970–2025), this review connects classic transition theories and validated stress measures with contemporary thematic clusters. As a conceptual synthesis derived from the mapped structure of the literature, the study proposes a three-pillar heuristic framework: structured residency or preceptorship with protected preceptor time, longitudinal screening using validated stress/burnout tools, and psychologically safe leadership. This framework should be read as an interpretive synthesis informed by mapped themes and central empirical literature, not as a validated intervention model or a direct algorithmic output of the clustering procedure. Accordingly, this study aimed to (1) map the intellectual foundations of research on stress, transition, and retention in nursing; (2) identify major thematic clusters over time; and (3) describe implications for nursing education, organizational practice, and workforce policy that remain consistent with the descriptive nature of bibliometric evidence.

Methods

Bibliometric analysis

Bibliometrics applies mathematical and statistical techniques to analyze scholarly outputs, map knowledge structures, and identify prominent themes within a field [16]. This systematized bibliometric review aimed to (1) provide a reproducible account of research on stress, transition-to-practice, and retention in nursing and (2) identify hotspots, thematic relationships, and conceptual foundations that may be difficult to summarize through narrative synthesis alone.

The bibliometric workflow followed five stages: data collection, data cleaning, descriptive analysis, network analysis, and visual interpretation [18]. The cocitation analysis mapped intellectual foundations by identifying references frequently cited together, whereas the coword analysis mapped thematic structures through keyword co-occurrence and linkages [18]. Coauthorship and coaffiliation analyses were not conducted because the focus was on knowledge structures rather than collaboration patterns. Bibliometric mapping does not appraise study quality; therefore, network relations are interpreted as thematic associations rather than causal links.

Data source, search strategy, and eligibility

The WoS Core Collection was selected because the primary aim of this study is knowledge structure mapping (cocitation and coword science mapping) rather than exhaustive evidence synthesis across multiple databases. WoS provides stable, standardized citation metadata and cited-reference fields, which are essential for reproducible cocitation network construction and science-mapping workflows [19]. Although nursing education and workforce research are also extensively indexed in Scopus, PubMed, and CINAHL, WoS was used as a single, consistent source to maximize citation-link reliability and minimize database-driven variation in reference indexing and export structures, consistent with the methodological focus of bibliometric mapping [19].

A topic search (TS) (Title, Abstract, Author Keywords, and Keywords Plus) was executed and exported on 31 August 2025 with the WoS timespan set to 1970–2025. The search was run in WoS Core Collection using the following indexes: Science Citation Index Expanded (SCI-EXPANDED), Social Sciences Citation Index (SSCI), and Emerging Sources Citation Index (ESCI). To support reproducibility, the query is reported verbatim:TS=((“student nurse∗” OR “undergraduate nurs∗” OR “nursing student∗” OR (“new NEAR/3 graduate NEAR/3 nurse∗”) OR “newly qualified nurs∗” OR “novice nurs∗” OR “graduate nurse program∗”) AND (stress OR burnout OR “mental health” OR wellbeing OR “well-being” OR anxiety OR depression OR fatigue) AND ((“transition NEAR/3 practice” OR “transition shock” OR “nurse residency” OR “residency program” OR preceptorship OR mentorship OR orientation OR (“clinical NEAR/3 readiness”) OR (“practice NEAR/3 readiness”)) OR (retention OR (intent∗ NEAR/3 (stay OR leave)) OR turnover OR attrition OR “work engagement”)))

Records were limited to English-language journal articles indexed in WoS as document type “Article”; conference proceedings, editorials, letters, commentaries, and book chapters were excluded. To enable replication, the full WoS query string is reported verbatim elsewhere (See Supplementary Table S1).

Network parameterization and thresholds

Thresholds were set through iterative exploratory runs to balance (a) interpretability of the visual maps, (b) inclusion of conceptually meaningful nodes, and (c) network density, consistent with bibliometric recommendations [16,20]. For the cocitation analysis, we applied a minimum cocitation threshold of 12 to reduce map saturation and retain a manageable set of foundational references for interpretive cluster labeling. For the coword analysis, we retained keywords with a minimum occurrence of 15 to focus on recurrent thematic signals while minimizing fragmentation from low-frequency terms. To limit overfragmentation in the long-span (1981–2025) cocitation map, we set the minimum cluster size to 6 so that cluster labeling was based on well-populated groups rather than very small clusters that are more likely to be unstable. For coword mapping, the minimum cluster size was retained at 1 to avoid suppressing small but coherent thematic groupings. For clarity, the minimum cluster size was specified separately for each analysis: 6 for the cocitation map and 1 for the coword map. Robustness was assessed by rerunning maps with slightly lower/higher thresholds (cocitation = 10 and 14; keyword occurrence = 12 and 18); dominant clusters and central nodes remained substantively consistent, with changes mainly in peripheral terms and minor boundary reassignments (Supplementary Table S2).

Keyword cleaning and normalization

Because coword results are sensitive to preprocessing, keyword cleaning was performed using a transparent, rule-based workflow prior to network mapping. Author Keywords and Keywords Plus were exported and processed as follows: (1) case normalization (all terms converted to lowercase); (2) character and punctuation cleaning (punctuation removed; corrupted hyphens/encoding artefacts corrected; consistent hyphenation applied for compound terms, e.g., co-citation, co-word, and well-being); (3) plural/singular harmonization (plural forms converted to singular where the concept was identical, e.g., nursing students → nursing student); (4) spelling-variant harmonization (e.g., wellbeing → well-being; British/American spelling aligned where applicable); and (5) synonym merging using a curated thesaurus file in comma-separated values (CSV) format to consolidate conceptually equivalent expressions while avoiding conflation of distinct constructs.

Synonym-merging rules were applied consistently, for example, intention to leave and intent to leave → turnover intention; new graduate nurse and newly qualified nurse → early-career nurse when used as population descriptors (while preserving original wording in verbatim titles and the WoS query). No automated stemming or lemmatization was applied to reduce the risk of collapsing distinct concepts (e.g., retention vs. retained). The keyword cleaning and normalization rules (with examples) are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

Thematic evolution analysis

To examine temporal change, a thematic evolution analysis was conducted in Biblioshiny across four time slices: 1981–1999, 2000–2009, 2010–2019, and 2020–2025, using three cutting points (1999, 2009, and 2019). Because the Biblioshiny thematic evolution interface operates on standard database fields, the analysis was conducted using the All Keywords field, which was the closest available representation of the keyword universe examined in the coword analysis. Thematic evolution was therefore aligned with the same overall keyword cleaning principles described earlier, although this step did not use a separately selectable custom merged field. The analysis used the following Biblioshiny settings: Number of Words = 250; Min Cluster Frequency = 5 per thousand documents; Weight Index = Inclusion Index weighted by Word-Occurrences; Min Weight Index = 0.1; Number of Labels = 3; and Clustering Algorithm = Louvain. The software-generated temporal structures were then interpreted by the authors to summarize dominant, persistent, and emerging themes across slices. Theme summaries were based on (1) recurrent terms within each slice; (2) their co-occurrence relationships in the software output; (3) conceptual coherence of grouped terms; and (4) continuity, emergence, or decline across adjacent periods.

Results

The search identified 664 WoS-indexed journal articles on stress, transition, and retention in nursing across the 1970–2025 search window; however, the earliest eligible publication year was 1981. Accordingly, findings cover 1981–31 August 2025 (export date), while annual publication trends are plotted for 1981–2024 because 2025 represents a partial year at export. Collectively, the corpus received 15,728 citations (14,820 excluding self-citations), averaging 23.69 citations per article, and achieved an h-index of 64. A total of 11,456 citing articles referenced this body of work, indicating broad uptake across nursing science and workforce scholarship. As shown in Supplementary Figure 1, publication output increased gradually from the 1980s, accelerated after 2010, and surged during the COVID-19 period, when stress and workforce retention became especially salient. Citation counts peaked earlier than publication volume, consistent with seminal works establishing the field’s conceptual and measurement foundations that later studies built upon.

Cocitation analysis

From 22,099 cited references, 63 met the minimum cocitation threshold (≥12) and were mapped into five clusters (Fig. 1). The most cocited sources reflected three interlocking knowledge bases: (1) transition theory and the early professional adaptation problem (e.g., Reality Shock and Transition Shock) [1,3,15]; (2) longitudinal and cohort evidence connecting stress and burnout with attrition risk [4]; and (3) systematic syntheses of stressors and interventions in nursing students and novices [5,21]. Measurement traditions were also prominent, supporting cross-study comparability of perceived stress and burnout constructs [9,10,22]. Resilience measurement, particularly the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), positions resilience as protective against anxiety, depression, and burnout [39]. Empirical studies and reviews consistently document high levels of stress in nursing education, with stressors including workload demands, evaluation pressure, role conflict, and clinical uncertainty; these exposures are linked with depression, anxiety, and reduced confidence [5,6,8,40,41]. Intervention studies embedded within curricula report benefits of coached coping skills, resilience training, mindfulness, and reflective practice on stress, self-efficacy, and well-being [21,42,43]. Comparative evidence suggests nursing students often report higher stress than non-nursing peers, underscoring a discipline-specific risk context [44]. Cross-cultural research indicates that stressors and coping repertoires vary across settings, emphasizing the need for context-sensitive supports [45]. Methodological guidance texts frequently cocited in this cluster reflect the importance of rigorous quantitative and qualitative approaches when measuring stress and coping and interpreting narratives of transition [46,47]. Overall, the cluster links student well-being to early-career adaptation by showing that many studies examine continuity between stress/coping during training and experiences during transition-to-practice, often in relation to burnout and retention-related outcomes [7].•Cluster 3 (blue): “Stress Appraisal, Burnout, and Measurable Drivers of Retention Risk.” This cluster consolidates constructs and instruments that operationalize stress and retention-related constructs and enable comparison across studies. It combines stress appraisal theory [38] with Bandura’s self-efficacy theory [48] to explain how perceived capability shapes coping, persistence, and learning under pressure. Stress is commonly measured using the PSS [9], while burnout is conceptualized through Maslach and Jackson’s multidimensional framework and later refinements that distinguish emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced accomplishment [22,49]. Broader burnout/engagement traditions also appear, extending measurement language beyond nursing to student populations and supporting cross-national comparisons [50]. Nursing-specific stress/burnout measurement is represented by work on stress and burnout in nursing students [10]. Empirical evidence in this cluster highlights the role of high-stakes evaluation and assessment environments in generating emotional strain and lowering confidence, and it synthesizes evidence that well-designed interventions can reduce stress during education and early transition [51]. Related work links academic and clinical performance factors with stress exposure and coping demands [52]. Longitudinal evidence shows that stress trajectories can extend beyond graduation and predict later burnout and job-related outcomes [53], consistent with cohort findings that connect baseline stress and burnout with attrition signals [4]. Taken together, the cluster provides a measurement backbone for examining commonly studied associations among educational stress, transition strain, burnout, and leaving intentions, while highlighting that coping and contextual factors remain integral to interpretation [6,41].•Cluster 4 (yellow): “Cross-Cultural Stressors, Coping Repertoires, and Evidence Standards for Attrition Prevention.” This cluster foregrounds cultural variation and methodological standards that shape what counts as credible evidence for attrition prevention. Carver’s coping taxonomy differentiates problem-focused, emotion-focused, and avoidance coping and is commonly applied when protocols require brief, standardized coping assessment [54]. The development and testing of perceived stress measures for nursing students in clinical practice, and subsequent applications in early placement contexts, support consistent measurement of clinical training stressors [55,56]. Cross-cultural studies indicate that stress patterns reflect both educational and health system constraints: Middle Eastern nursing students often report stress shaped by structural and cultural pressures [57,58], while Asian student samples highlight urban training demands, role conflict, and clinical evaluation pressures [59]. Placement-related attrition synthesis identifies antecedents such as clinical stress, academic struggles, and inadequate support [60]. Evidence synthesis standards such as the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses are also cocited, reinforcing expectations for transparent, reproducible review methods [61]. Conceptual work further locates stress and coping within broader institutional structures, illustrating that interventions must be aligned with local learning environments and workforce realities [62]. Overall, this cluster argues that retention strategies should be culturally fair and methodologically robust rather than imported uncritically across settings.•Cluster 5 (purple): “Legacy Empirical Bedrock—Early Cohort Evidence on Student Nurse Stressors and Coping.” This cluster captures early empirical work—often cohort or longitudinal in design—that identified recurring stressors in student nurses before the expansion of contemporary measurement and intervention literature. Studies described persistent exposures such as clinical evaluation pressure, workload intensity, role ambiguity, and examination burden and linked these to well-being trajectories and early attrition signals [4,14,[63], [64], [65], [66]]. Across this legacy work, maladaptive coping (e.g., avoidance) tended to correlate with poorer outcomes, while problem-focused coping and social support were consistently protective. Methodological contributions to qualitative analysis also appear in this cluster, signaling early efforts to systematize interpretation of student narratives and stress experiences [67]. Together, the cluster provides baseline empirical patterns that later intervention and transition-to-practice studies built upon, showing continuity in the literature’s recurring co-occurrence structure linking stress, coping, burnout, and attrition-related outcomes across decades.

The COVID-19 pandemic overlaid acute disruption on existing transition and workforce challenges. High-frequency terms included COVID-19, anxiety, depression, resilience, mindfulness, coping, burnout, and work engagement, reflecting intensified distress and heightened need for adaptive supports. Studies described abrupt shifts to online or hybrid learning, disrupted clinical placements, and uncertainty for students and new graduates, often alongside reduced social support [41,72]. Interventions expanded beyond traditional mentoring to include mindfulness-based programs and trauma-informed pedagogy [72,75]. Emerging work on professional identity and engagement showed that stronger identity and self-efficacy relate to better achievement and learning engagement, reinforcing the role of psychosocial resources in difficult contexts [73,74]. Peer mentoring and formal support structures continued to be recognized as protective for adaptation and well-being [68,69].

Across four decades, the thematic trajectory can be summarized as a shift from individual stress/self-efficacy toward burnout and workforce-related outcomes, then toward transition supports and, more recently, pandemic-era mental health and well-being interventions. This evolution indicates that stress, transition-to-practice, burnout, turnover intention, and other retention-related signals are repeatedly linked as an interconnected conceptual domain in the literature rather than as isolated topics. However, these mapped relationships should be interpreted as thematic and intellectual linkages, not as evidence that turnover intention, retention-risk signals, and observed turnover are equivalent endpoints or represent a validated causal sequence.

Discussion

In this discussion, we distinguish between three levels of interpretation: (1) what the cocitation and coword maps show as recurring bibliometric patterns; (2) our conceptual synthesis of those patterns; and (3) empirical findings reported in the cited primary studies and reviews represented within the mapped clusters. Cocitation and coword analyses describe intellectual linkages and thematic co-occurrence patterns; they do not establish causal direction or empirically test developmental sequences among constructs. From an OSH perspective, the mapped themes can be interpreted as a work-related psychosocial hazard domain in which early-career stress, transition-to-practice strain, and burnout represent exposure conditions arising from job demands and organizational contexts, while turnover intention functions as a leading indicator of staffing instability with potential downstream implications for patient safety and organizational safety culture [11,13]. Across 1981–2025, the mapped intellectual base shows that nursing-led theories and measures anchor this domain. Reality Shock [1], Novice to Expert [2,23], and Transition Shock [3,15] consistently cocite with longitudinal evidence that examines educational stress and early-career burnout in relation to leaving intentions and attrition-related signals [4,26,27,53]. Taken together, these patterns indicate that the literature is organized around a recurring set of co-occurring constructs that connect education and early practice (stress during training, transition-to-practice strain, burnout, and retention-related outcomes). Because cocitation and coword maps are descriptive, this recurring structure is interpreted as thematic convergence and cross-referencing, not as evidence of a causal, developmental, or temporally ordered mechanism. Accordingly, the cocitation and coword maps indicate that the literature frequently examines educational stress alongside transition-to-practice strain, and these topics often co-occur with burnout, turnover intention, and (less often) observed turnover outcomes—particularly in studies that also examine workload conditions and organizational supports.

At the level of bibliometric interpretation, the maps converge on three broad implications. First, the field is measurement ready. Widely used tools such as the PSS and nursing-specific stress/burnout measures enable cross-study comparison of stress and burnout constructs across settings [9,10,22]. However, many studies operationalize retention primarily through turnover intention measures, and fewer link program exposure to observed turnover beyond 12 months, which constrains inference even when prospective associations are reported in primary studies [27,13]. For OSH-relevant interpretation, this reliance on intention measures should be read as a limitation in estimating realized workforce risk, even if intention remains a useful early warning signal of potential staffing instability [13].

Second, within the underlying empirical literature represented in the mapped clusters, educational stress is repeatedly described as a measurable and commonly targeted concern. Nursing education is repeatedly characterized by workload intensity, clinical evaluation pressure, and emotional demands that co-occur with depression, anxiety, and reduced confidence across studies [5,6,8]. Empirical reviews and intervention studies represented within the mapped literature indicate that resilience and coping skills programs, mindfulness-based approaches, and coached coping supports are commonly evaluated and are frequently associated with improvements in stress, self-efficacy, and well-being during training [21,42,43,51]. These associations derive from the cited empirical literature rather than from the bibliometric maps themselves.

Third, the bibliometric maps repeatedly position organizational context as a central correlational theme, while the cited empirical studies provide the basis for more specific association claims. Empowering environments and supportive leadership show consistent associations with lower burnout and stronger intent to stay [11,12], while professional self-concept and confidence are commonly examined as psychological resources [24,25]. Transition programs are repeatedly presented as system responses, but reported outcomes vary with program design, preceptor preparation, and protected workload time [[29], [30], [31], [32], [33]]. Empirical studies also frequently report that work-related stress is associated with higher turnover intention among newly graduated nurses, underscoring the importance of aligning transition supports with unit realities [37,70,71].

Based on these recurrent thematic linkages, we propose a three-pillar heuristic framework as an interpretive synthesis informed by recurrent bibliometric patterns and the empirical literature cited within those patterns. The three-pillar framework should be understood as an interpretive synthesis informed by convergent bibliometric structures and the empirical studies most central within those structures; it is not a direct algorithmic output of the clustering procedure and should not be interpreted as a validated intervention model. Pillar 1 (structured residency/preceptorship with protected preceptor time) is most directly supported by the thematic emphasis on transition supports in coword cluster 1 (Education-to-Workforce Transition and Retention Supports) and the early-career outcome focus in coword cluster 3 (Burnout, Job Satisfaction, and Retention Predictors), and is anchored intellectually by cocitation cluster 1 (Foundations of Stress–Transition–Retention), where transition theories and program/organizational determinants converge. In the underlying empirical and review literature represented within the mapped clusters, structured residency or preceptorship with protected preceptor time has been linked with improved role clarity, satisfaction, and early adaptation; however, the present bibliometric mapping does not test effectiveness or retention effects directly [28,33]. Pillar 2 (longitudinal stress and burnout screening using validated tools) aligns most strongly with the measurement and construct foundations in cocitation cluster 3 (Stress Appraisal, Burnout, and Measurable Drivers of Retention Risk) and the longitudinal baseline evidence concentrated in cocitation cluster 5 (Legacy Empirical Bedrock), with thematic reinforcement from student mental health and well-being terms in coword cluster 2 (Stress, Mental Health, and Coping Resources in Nursing Education) and coword cluster 4 (Well-being, Self-Efficacy, and Engagement Strategies). In prospective studies represented in the mapped literature, longitudinal screening using validated stress and burnout tools has been discussed as a possible approach for monitoring higher-risk periods and individuals and for informing targeted support; however, this implication derives from the underlying empirical literature rather than from the bibliometric maps themselves [4,53]. Pillar 3 (psychologically safe leadership with role clarity and constructive feedback) is supported by the organizational determinants and leadership-related anchors in cocitation cluster 1 and is echoed in the organizational support/satisfaction/turnover-intention emphasis within coword cluster 3, underscoring leadership climate as a contextual condition shaping whether programs and psychosocial supports function effectively. Prior empirical studies represented in the mapped clusters have reported associations between psychologically safe leadership, role clarity, constructive feedback, and satisfaction or intent to stay; these associations reflect the underlying literature and are not effects demonstrated by the present mapping [11,12].

Methodologically, the mapping highlights a persistent design gap. The field remains survey-heavy, and implementation details (fidelity, workload protection, and leadership involvement) are often under-reported. Mixed-methods and qualitative work—grounded in established analytic standards—can help explain why interventions appear to work in some settings and not others [34,47]. Thus, the bibliometric results primarily show that stress, transition-to-practice strain, burnout, and retention-related outcomes form an interconnected conceptual domain across the literature, whereas the cited empirical studies provide the more specific evidence on associations, interventions, and contextual influences. Overall, the literature has expanded from describing stress as a recurring challenge to mapping an interconnected conceptual domain spanning education, transition-to-practice, burnout, and retention-risk outcomes, which supports co-ordinated attention across education and practice while maintaining appropriate interpretive boundaries for bibliometric evidence.

Future studies could strengthen this evidence base by following cohorts through at least the first 24 months, reporting program fidelity and preceptor workload protection, and linking proximal outcomes (stress, satisfaction, and self-efficacy) to harder endpoints such as retention and turnover rates. Reporting cost and staffing implications alongside outcomes may improve the policy relevance of future studies and help interpret turnover rates more meaningfully across diverse practice settings globally [13].

Strength and limitations

A key interpretive limitation is that a substantial proportion of the mapped corpus operationalizes retention risk using turnover intention (e.g., intention to leave/stay) rather than observed turnover or retention. Intention is an informative but imperfect proxy, and the intention-to-behavior relationship can vary across labor market conditions, contractual constraints, and organizational contexts. Consequently, the present bibliometric findings describe how the literature most commonly frames and links retention risk, but they cannot quantify actual turnover rates or infer the effect of stress/transition exposures on realized turnover outcomes. This limitation is particularly important for occupational health and workforce policy interpretation and underscores the need for future longitudinal studies that link stress, transition experiences, and burnout trajectories to observed retention/turnover outcomes and cost implications.

This systematized bibliometric review provides a transparent mapping of the knowledge base on stress, transition, and retention in nursing by integrating performance indicators with cocitation and coword science mapping. Using VOSviewer enabled clear visualization of intellectual and thematic structures, while consistent keyword cleaning and threshold rules improved interpretability and reproducibility of the networks. Focusing on journal articles indexed in WoS as document-type “Article” and applying a replicable topic-search strategy within a curated database strengthened meta-data consistency and the reliability of citation links [19]. However, relying on a single database and English-language articles may exclude regionally indexed or practice-focused work and may under-represent non-English contexts [19]. This trade-off was accepted because the study’s objective was to map the field’s intellectual and thematic structure using consistent citation meta-data suitable for cocitation and science-mapping analyses, rather than to exhaustively retrieve all potentially relevant evidence across databases. Citation-based indicators favor older publications, producing known “age” effects in influence metrics [16]. Coword results depend on authors’ keyword choices, the inclusion of Keywords Plus, and on cleaning decisions; thresholds can also exclude emerging niche topics, even when conceptually important. Because Keywords Plus are algorithmically generated by WoS rather than assigned by the author, they may not always perfectly reflect the article’s intended conceptual emphasis. Because bibliometric methods describe associations rather than evaluate study quality, findings should be interpreted as structural signals rather than graded evidence, consistent with bibliometric guidance [16].

Additional considerations include time coverage and analytic scope. The dataset ended at 31 August 2025; therefore, publications after this date are not represented, and 2025 is treated as a partial year. Because this review prioritized knowledge structures, collaboration patterns (coauthorship or institutional networks) were not analyzed, and the mapping cannot identify which teams or regions drive specific themes. Threshold selection improves map readability but introduces sensitivity to parameter choices; although exploratory runs were used to balance density and interpretability, alternative thresholds or tools could yield modestly different cluster boundaries [16]. A future sensitivity analysis using a broader database could test the robustness of thematic structures, given known coverage differences between sources [19] and include non-English records where feasible.

Conclusion

Across 1981–2025 (with 2025 as a partial year up to the export date of 31 August 2025), the mapped literature shows a recurring co-occurrence structure in which stress during training, transition-to-practice difficulties, burnout, and turnover intention are frequently examined together. Because bibliometric science-mapping is descriptive, these patterns should be interpreted as evidence of how the field is conceptually clustered and linked in the literature, not as confirmation of causal effects, temporal sequence, or fixed ordering. More specifically, the mapped results indicate thematic and intellectual linkages among stress, transition-to-practice strain, burnout, turnover intention, and related retention-risk signals; they neither demonstrate equivalence between turnover intention and observed turnover nor establish the effectiveness of any specific intervention or policy response. Consistent with these mapped themes, the broader empirical literature represented within the central clusters frequently discusses three domains of possible response: (1) structured residency or preceptorship with protected preceptor time; (2) longitudinal screening using validated stress, resilience, and burnout tools at key training–transition points to monitor elevated risk and inform timely support; and (3) psychologically safe leadership practices characterized by role clarity and constructive feedback. This framework should be understood as an interpretive synthesis informed by the mapped themes and the empirical studies most central within those structures; it is not a direct algorithmic output of the clustering procedure and should not be interpreted as a validated intervention model. Notably, much of the underlying literature operationalizes retention risk through turnover intention and related survey-based indicators rather than observed turnover; accordingly, the implications of this review are framed in terms of mapped retention-risk signals and interpretive implications from the broader literature, rather than demonstrated effects on realized retention or turnover outcomes. Future studies should prioritize longer follow-up (12–24 months), transparent implementation reporting (e.g., fidelity and workload protection), and endpoints beyond intention, including observed retention/turnover and cost implications.
