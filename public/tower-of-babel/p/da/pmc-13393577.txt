Onset and Risk Period of Functional Sound Tooth Loss: A Hyperbolic Tangent Model
Authors: Jaesung Lee, Jeong Woo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesExisting percentile analyses describe age-related tooth loss trajectories but do not quantify when accelerated loss begins or how long the high-risk period lasts. This study aimed to apply a hyperbolic tangent model to functional sound tooth loss data to derive clinically interpretable transition parameters.MethodsA hyperbolic tangent regression was fitted to normalised functional sound tooth loss data from 27,215 adults across 5 cycles of the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2010-2015), covering the 10th, 25th, 50th, and 75th percentile groups. Goodness of fit was compared with linear, polynomial, and logistic alternatives using the coefficient of determination (R2) and the Akaike information criterion. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals for all model parameters were derived from the Hessian of the least squares objective function.ResultsThe hyperbolic tangent model achieved the highest goodness of fit across all percentile groups (R2 = 0.963-0.996), outperforming all 3 alternatives. The peak age b (age of maximum tooth loss velocity, 50% cumulative loss) was 57.3, 64.9, 74.9, and 85.9 years, and the spreading coefficient c (width of the high-risk period) was 9.8, 12.8, 17.2, and 17.6 years for the 10th through 75th percentile groups, respectively. The onset age (b − c) was 47.4, 52.2, 57.6, and 68.3 years, with narrow 95% confidence intervals across all groups.ConclusionsThe hyperbolic tangent model provides a parsimonious 2-parameter characterisation of the functional tooth loss trajectory. The onset age and the high-risk duration (risky term, 2c) support percentile-specific risk stratification for preventive dental care and population-level oral health planning. Findings should be interpreted as population-level estimates from a cross-sectional design.

Introduction

Tooth loss is a major public health concern in ageing populations worldwide. Poor oral health resulting in tooth loss significantly increases total mortality and the risk of death from gastrointestinal cancer, heart disease, and stroke1 and is associated with adverse prognostic biomarkers for cardiovascular events.2 Beyond systemic consequences, tooth loss impairs dietary intake, self-esteem, and psychological well-being3,4 and is directly linked to dysphagia and increased mortality.5 Despite sustained efforts to reduce the incidence of oral disease, the age-standardised rate of global tooth loss declined by only 0.2% between 2005 and 2015,6 and the number of remaining teeth has emerged as an independent predictor of longevity in older adults.7 Active management of tooth loss is therefore becoming an increasingly important component of healthy ageing policy.8

Population-based oral health surveys provide the epidemiologic foundation for understanding tooth loss trajectories. National surveys in Korea, Japan, the United Kingdom, and Sweden have consistently demonstrated that the number of functional sound teeth declines with age in a characteristic pattern.9, 10, 11 Percentile curves—graphical representations of tooth counts stratified by age and population subgroup—have been developed as practical tools for patient education and clinical benchmarking, and they have been shown to help elderly patients understand their oral health status relative to their peers.12,13 However, existing percentile-based analyses have been largely descriptive: they characterise the general shape of tooth loss trajectories but do not extract quantitative parameters that identify when accelerated tooth loss begins for a given patient or how long the high-risk period lasts. Without such parameters, clinicians lack a data-driven basis for deciding at what age to intensify preventive care for individual patients.

The limitation of existing approaches becomes apparent when examining the shape of the percentile curves themselves. The normalised tooth loss rate, plotted against age, consistently displays 3 structurally distinct zones: a stable plateau of retention in young adulthood, a period of accelerated loss concentrated in a relatively narrow age window, and a plateau of depletion in the oldest age groups. This S-shaped pattern contains 2 clinically critical pieces of information—the age at which rapid loss begins and the duration of the high-risk episode—that descriptive statistics and polynomial curve-fitting cannot capture directly.14,15

The characteristic S-shape closely resembles the curve of the hyperbolic tangent function tanh(x), which transitions smoothly between 2 asymptotic plateaus. This visual correspondence motivated us to apply tanh(x) as a regression model for the age-specific percentile data. Unlike linear or polynomial models, which cannot reproduce flat plateaus, and unlike the logistic function, which approaches its asymptotes more slowly than the observed data, the hyperbolic tangent function captures all 3 zones within a parsimonious 2-parameter formulation. The 2 parameters carry direct clinical meaning: the peak age b identifies the age of maximum tooth loss velocity and the age at which cumulative loss reaches 50%, while the spreading coefficient c quantifies the width of the transition zone in units structurally analogous to the standard deviation of the normal distribution. This analogy motivates defining the period of accelerated loss as the interval b − c to b + c, within which approximately 76% of lifetime tooth loss is concentrated, and the initiating age (b − c) as the onset of the high-risk period. We further introduce the risky term (2c), defined as the width of this high-risk window, as a parsimonious quantitative descriptor of the compactness of accelerated tooth loss in a given population subgroup. To our knowledge, this is the first application of a hyperbolic tangent function to the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) data for the quantitative characterisation of tooth loss trajectories.

The purpose of this study was therefore 2-fold: first, to demonstrate that the hyperbolic tangent model provides a statistically superior fit to normalised tooth loss percentile curves compared with linear, polynomial, and logistic alternatives and, second, to derive percentile-specific estimates of b, c, the initiating age (b − c), and the risky term (2c), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and to discuss their implications for preventive care and oral health policy.

Methods

Data collection

KNHANES is a nationally representative, cross-sectional health survey16 administered in South Korea by the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) under Article 16 of the National Health Promotion Act. Data from the fifth KNHANES (2010 and 2012) and the sixth KNHANES (2013-2015) were used, covering 5 survey cycles. Data from 2011 were excluded because the KDCA subsequently reported low interexaminer agreement in the oral examination component for that cycle, rendering the dental data unsuitable for integration with other cycles.17

To ensure representativeness of the noninstitutionalised civilian population of South Korea, all statistical analyses incorporated the complex sample design elements of KNHANES, including stratification variables, clustering variables, and sampling weights. Oral examination–specific weights (wt_oe) were used as the primary weighting factor. For integration across the 5 cycles, individual weights were adjusted by dividing each weight by the number of included survey years, following KDCA statistical guidelines.16

Ethics statement

The KNHANES surveys included in this study were approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of the KDCA with the following approval numbers: 2010-02CON-21-C (2010), 2012-01EXP-01-2C (2012), 2013-07CON-03-4C and 2013-12EXP-03-5C (2013), and 2014-12EXP-04-5C (2014). For the 2015 cycle, formal IRB review was not required under the Bioethics and Safety Act (Article 2, Item 1), as the survey was classified as a state-conducted public welfare research project. This study constitutes a secondary analysis of publicly available, deidentified data; no additional ethical approval was required. All participants in the original KNHANES surveys provided written informed consent, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data definition and selection

Functional teeth (FT; all surfaces functioning normally) and sound teeth (ST; all surfaces healthy) were enumerated separately, and their sum (FT + ST) yielded the functional sound tooth (FST) count used throughout this study. In the KNHANES oral examination protocol, every tooth is assigned a single mutually exclusive status code by a trained dentist.17 ST are defined as natural teeth with no caries, no restorations, and no clinically detectable defects on any surface, while FT are defined as teeth that have undergone restorative treatment (filling or crown) and currently retain normal masticatory function. Teeth with active caries, prosthetic replacements other than crowns, or extracted teeth receive distinct codes and are excluded from both categories. Because ST and FT are disjoint at the per-tooth level, the sum FT + ST counts each tooth exactly once and represents the total number of teeth retaining full masticatory function in a given individual. The reliability of clinical dental measurements is known to depend on examiner training, calibration, and the specific operating conditions under which observations are made18; the KDCA quality control report identifying low interexaminer agreement in 2011 and the consequent exclusion of that cycle (see the Data collection subsection) reflects this general principle and supports the integration of the remaining cycles used here. Wisdom teeth (third molars) were excluded because they do not consistently erupt in all individuals and are frequently removed for orthodontic reasons unrelated to the ageing-related tooth loss process.

Records from adults aged ≥20 years were analysed. Of the 39,964 individuals surveyed across the 5 cycles, 4471 were excluded owing to missing values in the general examination, and a further 8278 were excluded because they were aged under 20 years (Figure 1). The final analytic sample comprised 27,215 participants. Demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. Regression analysis with tanh(x)

Rationale for model selection

Examination of the normalised tooth loss rate across all age groups revealed 3 structurally distinct zones consistently present in every percentile group (Figure 4): (1) a stable plateau of retention in young adulthood, (2) a period of accelerated loss concentrated in a narrow age window, and (3) a plateau of depletion in the oldest age groups. Four candidate functions were compared, with 3 additional sigmoidal candidates added in revision (see below): linear, polynomial (quadratic and cubic), logistic, and hyperbolic tangent. A linear model cannot reproduce flat plateaus. Polynomial models approximate the central transition but diverge outside the observed age range. The logistic function 1/[1 + e−(x−b)/c] yields an S-curve, but its asymptotic plateau is approached more slowly than observed. Three additional 2-parameter sigmoidal candidates were included to address specific empirical questions about the choice of functional form. The arctangent y(x) = (1/2) + (1/π) arctan((x − b)/c) was included to test whether the asymptotic plateaus are approached algebraically (decaying as 1/x) or exponentially. The Gaussian error function y(x) = (1/2)[1 + erf((x − b)/(c√2))], the cumulative distribution function of the normal distribution, was included as the natural empirical test of the structural analogy between the spreading coefficient c and the Gaussian standard deviation σ discussed below. The Gompertz function y(x) = exp[−ln 2 · exp(−(x − b)/c)], reparameterised so that y(b) = 0.5 and b is therefore directly comparable across all sigmoid candidates, was included as the most natural asymmetric 2-parameter alternative, allowing the symmetry assumption of the tanh model to be tested empirically. In contrast, tanh[(x − b)/c] produces a symmetric sigmoid with well-defined asymptotic plateaus that map exactly to the limits of the normalised loss rate (0 and 1) through the linear transformation in equation (3). The hyperbolic tangent achieved the highest R2 and lowest Akaike information criterion (AIC) across the 10th, 25th, and 50th percentile groups (Supplementary Table S2). The Gaussian error function performed equivalently to tanh on the lower percentile groups (ΔAIC ≤4 for the 10th and 25th percentiles) and slightly outperformed tanh on the 75th percentile group (ΔAIC = 3.79), providing quantitative support for the structural analogy between c and σ. The arctangent and Gompertz alternatives were rejected on the 10th, 25th, and 50th percentile groups by ΔAIC >30, demonstrating, respectively, that the asymptotic decay of the trajectory is exponential rather than algebraic and that the trajectory is approximately symmetric around the inflection point within the observed age range. Fitting to the 4 main percentile groups yielded R2 = 0.963 to 0.996 for the tanh model, systematically superior to the linear, polynomial, logistic, arctan, and Gompertz alternatives (Table 3, Supplementary Table S2). Mathematical formulation

Given n observations (x1, y1), …, (xn, yn), where yi is the FST count at age xi, we express the general tanh-based fitting function as(1)y(x)=c1+c2tanh(b−xc)where x = b is the inflection point and c1 ± c2 are the 2 asymptotic limits. Because the FST count decreases monotonically with age, we define a normalised tooth loss rate mapping the data onto [0, 1]:(2)wi=ymax−yiymaxwhere ymax is the FST count at the youngest age for the percentile group. By construction wi = 0 at the initial age and wi → 1 as yi → 0, fixing c1 = 0.5 and c2 = −0.5, so equation (1) simplifies to(3)y(x)=12[1−tanh(b−xc)]

At x = b, y(b) = 0.5: the peak age b is therefore the age at which exactly 50% of functional sound teeth have been lost, and tooth loss velocity is at its maximum. The spreading coefficient c governs the width of the S-shaped transition (Figure 3a) and plays a role analogous to the standard deviation σ of the normal distribution: the interval b ± c encompasses 76.2% of the total change (Figure 3b; Table 2), compared with 68.1% for μ ± σ. The 76.2% figure is a mathematical property of the hyperbolic tangent function itself, obtained analytically as tanh(1) ≈ 0.7616, and is therefore independent of the data. This structural parallel motivates defining the period of accelerated loss (also referred to as the high-risk window) as the interval b − c < x < b + c, within which 76.2% of lifetime functional sound tooth loss is concentrated, with onset at the initiating age (b − c). We further propose a quantitative descriptor of the compactness of the high-risk window, the risky term, defined as its width 2c. The risky term is introduced here as a parsimonious, percentile-specific scalar metric derived directly from the model parameters, complementing rather than replacing the descriptive terminology above.Figure 3Illustrative examples of the hyperbolic tangent function. The least squares error minimisation with respect to b and c uses the Newton-Raphson algorithm; the derivation and the Hessian-based 95% CIs are given in Appendix A.

All numerical computations, including the Newton-Raphson optimisation, the Hessian-based CIs, and the figures, were performed in MATLAB R2023a (MathWorks). Final figure panels were composed using Python 3.11 with the Pillow library. Ordinary least squares estimation is the maximum likelihood estimator under independent and identically distributed Gaussian residuals; while strict Gaussianity cannot be guaranteed for residuals on the bounded interval [0, 1], the close agreement between the nonlinear and linearised fits (Supplementary Table S3) and the unstructured residual patterns reported in Supplementary Table S2 indicate that the Gaussian noise approximation is adequate for the age-aggregated percentile data analysed here.

Results

Model validation

Compared with linear, polynomial, arctangent, Gaussian error function, Gompertz, and logistic alternatives, the hyperbolic tangent model consistently yielded the highest R2 and the lowest AIC across all 4 percentile groups (Table 3; full comparison in Supplementary Table S2). R2 values ranged from 0.963 (75th percentile) to 0.996 (10th percentile), indicating that the model captures at least 96% of the variance in the normalised tooth loss rate for most groups. The linear model systematically underestimated tooth loss at both extremes of the age range, the polynomial model showed comparable central-zone fit but diverged outside the observed age range, and the logistic model performed similarly to the tanh model for the lower percentile groups but showed marginally lower R2 for the 50th and 75th percentiles, where the transition zone is broader and more gradual. The arctangent, which differs from tanh only in the rate at which the asymptotes are approached, was rejected by ΔAIC values of 73.7, 69.5, 33.7, and 80.4 across the 4 percentile groups; the Gompertz function was rejected by ΔAIC values of 55.7, 53.5, 37.1, and 2.1. The Gaussian error function was nearly indistinguishable from tanh on the lower percentile groups (ΔAIC of 1.5 and 4.1) but slightly outperformed it on the 75th percentile (ΔAIC = 3.79 for tanh as second). Residual statistics (maximum absolute residual, mean absolute residual, and mean signed residual) for the 4 sigmoid models are reported in Supplementary Table S2 and confirm the same rank ordering: tanh and erf show the smallest residuals across all 4 percentile groups, while the larger residuals from arctan reflect its slow algebraic approach to the asymptotes, and those from Gompertz reflect forced asymmetry against approximately symmetric data. A representative visual comparison of the 4 candidate sigmoids fitted to the 10th percentile group is shown in Figure 5. Piecewise or compound functions could in principle be fitted to different age segments but were not pursued because the single tanh model already achieves R2 ≥ 0.96 across all percentile groups, and a piecewise approach would substantially increase the parameter count, sacrifice the parsimony that gives b and c their direct clinical interpretation, and incur a corresponding AIC penalty.Figure 5Four candidate sigmoid functions—hyperbolic tangent (solid), arctangent (long dash), Gaussian error function (dash-dot), and Gompertz (dotted)—fitted to the normalised tooth loss rate wi for the 10th percentile group. The hyperbolic tangent and Gaussian error function curves are essentially indistinguishable across most of the age range and follow the observed data closely; the arctangent curve approaches the upper asymptote substantially more slowly than the data, reflecting its algebraic (1/x) rather than exponential decay; the Gompertz curve shows a characteristic asymmetric departure from the observations on either side of the inflection point. Residual statistics for all 4 candidates across the 4 percentile groups are reported in Supplementary Table S2.

Percentile curves and normalised tooth loss rate

Figure 2 shows the raw FST counts stratified by age and percentile group. Because these data were drawn from a cross-sectional survey, each point represents a different birth cohort; the curves therefore describe the population-level distribution of tooth counts at each age, not the longitudinal trajectory of any single individual.

Figure 4 presents the normalised tooth loss rate wi for the 10th, 25th, 50th, and 75th percentile groups over ages 20 to 80 years, together with fitted tanh curves and the critical age markers b − c, b, and b + c. All 4 curves share the 3-zone S-shaped pattern described in the Rationale for model selection subsection. The steepness of the transition zone decreases progressively from the 10th to the 75th percentile: individuals in lower percentile groups experience a more abrupt tooth loss episode than those in higher percentile groups. The 90th percentile group was excluded from curve fitting because insufficient data points in the transition zone (fewer than 5 nonzero observations) prevented stable convergence of the Newton-Raphson algorithm. Substantively, this group represents an ideal trajectory in which cumulative tooth loss does not reach the 50% threshold within the observed human lifespan; the absence of an inflection point in the data range is therefore not a limitation of the model but a feature of the population this group describes.

Parameter estimates and confidence intervals

The estimated parameters and their 95% CIs are summarised in Table 3.

The peak age b increases monotonically across percentile groups: 57.3, 64.9, 74.9, and 85.9 years for the 10th, 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles, respectively. The narrow 95% CIs for b (eg, [57.1-57.5] years for the 10th percentile) reflect the high precision of the tanh fit.

The spreading coefficient c also increases across groups: 9.8, 12.8, 17.2, and 17.6 years. This progressive widening indicates that individuals in higher percentile groups lose their teeth more gradually over a longer period, whereas those in lower percentile groups experience a more concentrated and abrupt episode.

Period of accelerated loss and initiating age

The interval b − c < x < b + c (period of accelerated loss, also denoted high-risk window; risky term = 2c) contains 76.2% of lifetime functional sound tooth loss (Table 2). The lower boundary b − c (initiating age) marks the onset of the high-risk period.

For the 10th percentile group, the risky term is 19.7 years (47.4 < x < 67.1; 95% CI for initiating age, 47.0-47.9): patients in this group begin losing teeth rapidly from approximately age 47, with 76% of their total tooth loss occurring before age 67. For the 75th percentile group, the risky term is considerably longer at 35.2 years (68.3 < x < 103.5; 95% CI for initiating age, 67.9-68.8): tooth loss begins later but progresses more slowly, extending beyond the typical human lifespan.

These findings highlight 2 clinically distinct risk profiles. Patients in lower percentile groups face an earlier and more abrupt tooth loss episode requiring earlier, more aggressive preventive intervention. Patients in higher percentile groups have a later initiating age but a more protracted high-risk period, demanding sustained preventive care once the risky period has begun.

Interpretation note. All percentile curves derive from cross-sectional data; each age group represents a different cohort. The parameters should therefore be interpreted as estimates of the population distribution of tooth loss at each age, not as the predicted trajectory of any individual over time.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study demonstrates that the normalised functional sound tooth loss rate across the adult lifespan is described with high precision (R2 > 0.96) by a hyperbolic tangent function fitted to KNHANES cross-sectional data. The 2 model parameters—peak age b and spreading coefficient c—define the initiating age (b − c) and the risky term (b − c to b + c), within which approximately 76% of lifetime tooth loss is concentrated. Both parameters increase systematically across percentile groups (Table 3), with the 10th percentile group exhibiting an earlier initiating age (47.4 years) and shorter risky term (19.7 years) compared with the 75th percentile group (68.3 years; 35.2 years).

Interpretation of model parameters

The hyperbolic tangent model provides distinct advantages over descriptive approaches. First, it converts the complex S-shaped tooth loss trajectory into 2 scalar quantities that can be communicated readily to patients and clinicians. Second, percentile-specific parameters enable personalised risk stratification: a 45-year-old patient whose tooth loss pattern corresponds to the 10th percentile can be identified as approaching the initiating age of 47.4 years (95% CI, 47.0-47.9), enabling proactive intervention. Third, the framework supports population-level projections for evidence-based resource allocation.

The analogy between c and the standard deviation σ of the normal distribution (Table 2) is a structural parallel, not a statistical equivalence: ±c captures 76.2% of the tooth loss trajectory versus 68.1% for ±σ, a difference of approximately 8 percentage points. This parallel nonetheless provides an intuitive clinical heuristic and justifies the risky-term definition.

The empirical near-equivalence of the hyperbolic tangent and Gaussian error function fits across the lower percentile groups (ΔAIC = 1.46, 4.09, and 13.52 for the 10th, 25th, and 50th percentiles, respectively; Supplementary Table S2) provides quantitative support for this structural c ∼ σ analogy: the data could almost equally well be described as the cumulative distribution function of a Gaussian centred at b with standard deviation c. The tanh formulation is preferred for its closed-form symmetric expression, the cleaner derivation of the 76.2% interval that defines the risky term, and its slightly tighter fit on the broader percentile groups.

FST as an integrative endpoint and its clinical drivers

The functional sound tooth count is, by construction, an integrative endpoint that aggregates the cumulative impact of caries, periodontal attachment loss, restoration failure, and dental care utilisation patterns into a single tooth-level outcome. The hyperbolic tangent model presented here is therefore intentionally aetiology-agnostic: it characterises the population-level trajectory of tooth retention, regardless of the specific mechanism by which individual teeth are lost. The broad consistency of the model fit between the normoglycaemic and diabetes mellitus subgroups, as reported in Table 3, demonstrates that the model framework is portable across at least 1 major systemic risk factor stratification without loss of structural fit, supporting this integrative-endpoint framing.

The clinical drivers of the transitions identified by the model are nonetheless of interpretive value. The initiating age b − c falls between approximately 47 and 68 years across the 4 percentile groups, a window that coincides with the midlife acceleration of periodontal attachment loss, the cumulative onset of restoration failure in heavily restored dentitions, age-related changes in salivary flow, and the progressive impact of polypharmacy on oral health.19,20 The end of the high-risk window (b + c) corresponds to the population-level depletion of teeth that have remained vulnerable throughout the accelerated-loss phase; in lower percentile groups, it marks the approach to edentulism, whereas in higher percentile groups, it lies beyond the typical observed age range and reflects the gradual depletion of an increasingly resilient subset of the dentition.

Several modifiable factors have been shown in prior studies to shorten the high-risk window or shift its onset to later ages, and the present framework provides an explicit quantitative target on which such interventions can act. Regular dental attendance has been shown to slow tooth loss progression, even after the accelerated phase has begun.21,22 In addition, smoking cessation, glycaemic control in patients with diabetes, and structured periodontal maintenance therapy all help modify the underlying disease processes that drive tooth loss in this age window.23,24 Furthermore, lifelong fluoride exposure, together with socioeconomic improvements in dental care access, reshapes the population-level distribution of tooth loss trajectories from which b and c are estimated.8 Aetiology-stratified analyses (eg, separate fits for caries burden and periodontal status subgroups) using the present model framework are an important next step and are reserved for a follow-up study with access to expanded covariate data.

Clinical implications

Our results are consistent with previous studies emphasising the importance of early and sustained dental care. Regular dental attendance has been shown to slow tooth loss progression, even when initiated after accelerated loss has begun.21 The initiating ages identified here—47.4, 52.2, 57.6, and 68.3 years—provide concrete age targets for intensified preventive care across all risk strata.24

Individuals with fewer than 20 to 25 functional teeth, corresponding approximately to the b − c threshold, tend to modify their dietary behaviour in ways that further accelerate tooth loss.25,26 The tooth loss rate among those aged over 50 years is nearly 6 times that of those under 30 years,27 underscoring the importance of active midlife surveillance.22 The quantitative thresholds derived from the tanh model can support clinicians in communicating these risks through age-specific percentile charts analogous to growth charts in paediatric medicine.

Systemic health and quality of life

The clinical significance of the risky term extends beyond oral health. Reduced functional tooth count leads to dietary restriction and altered nutrient intake associated with adverse haematologic changes and elevated risk of atherosclerosis and gastrointestinal cancer.19 Maintenance of at least 20 teeth with 4 posterior occluding pairs is associated with preserved oral health–related quality of life.28 Tooth loss and dysphagia are independently associated with increased mortality,5 suggesting the risky term also marks a critical window for preventing systemic health deterioration.

Dental professionals are therefore encouraged to regard the initiating age not merely as a dental milestone but also as an early indicator of broader systemic risk, warranting comanagement with physicians responsible for noncommunicable diseases. The World Health Organization identifies oral disease as a contributor to several leading global health threats,29 and collaborative care models integrating oral and general health have been advocated.20,30

Public health implications

The percentile-specific parameters enable more targeted public health planning than previously possible. The initiating age provides a data-driven basis for optimal timing of population-level oral health screening programmes. Differences in the risky term across percentile groups suggest that preventive strategies should be stratified by risk profile rather than applied uniformly across age groups. The model’s ability to generate patient-specific risk profiles from a brief tooth count has potential applications in primary care settings, where specialist dental access is limited. The hyperbolic tangent framework complements emerging data-driven approaches to dental risk stratification, including machine learning models for the early identification of dental pain in adolescents31: where machine learning approaches excel at integrating heterogeneous individual-level predictors, the present model provides a parsimonious population-level summary that can serve as a calibration reference for risk prediction tools and as a benchmark against which individual trajectories can be interpreted.

Limitations

Cross-sectional design. The most fundamental limitation is the cross-sectional design of KNHANES. Each age-specific percentile value in Supplementary Table S1 represents a different birth cohort rather than the same individuals followed longitudinally. The curves therefore describe the population distribution of tooth counts at each age, not the individual tooth loss trajectory of any person ageing through time. Potential cohort effects cannot be excluded: older participants grew up in an era of substantially lower dental care utilisation and fluoride exposure, so their comparatively lower tooth counts may partly reflect historical differences in access rather than the inevitable consequence of biological ageing. This consideration applies most strongly to the 10th percentile group: the earlier and more abrupt tooth loss episode observed in this group may be partly exacerbated by the historically more limited access to systemic fluoridation, preventive dental services, and oral health education experienced by the older birth cohorts that contribute most to the lower percentiles of the tooth loss distribution.8 The persistence and shape of the tooth loss trajectory in younger birth cohorts who grew up with substantially expanded preventive infrastructure may therefore differ from the cross-sectional pattern reported here. Future longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether the model parameters b and c remain stable across successive cohorts.

Unmeasured confounders. Tooth loss is influenced by multiple individual-level factors, including educational attainment, income, sex, smoking, and access to dental care.32,33 The present analysis did not perform separate regression analyses for individual confounders. Our results are therefore susceptible to a degree of ecological fallacy: the percentile groups aggregate individuals with heterogeneous risk profiles. The broadly consistent results for the normoglycaemic and diabetes mellitus (DM) subgroups in Table 3 suggest that the overall model structure is robust to at least 1 major systemic risk factor, but the lower R2 values for the DM group (0.739-0.860) and wider confidence intervals indicate greater heterogeneity, warranting dedicated analysis. The peak age b in the DM subgroup is shifted earlier than in the normoglycaemic subgroup across all percentile groups (Table 3), consistent with the known acceleration of periodontal attachment loss and tooth loss in diabetic patients reported in the systemic health literature.23,33

Model-fitting uncertainty. The 95% CIs in Table 3 reflect the precision of model fitting to the n = 60 age-specific percentile values and were derived from the Hessian of the least squares objective function. They do not incorporate the sampling uncertainty of each percentile estimate from KNHANES. The true uncertainty in the population-level parameters b and c is therefore likely to be larger than the intervals suggest.

Model symmetry and 75th percentile extrapolation. Two model-related caveats deserve emphasis. First, the hyperbolic tangent function imposes symmetry around the inflection point b. The Gompertz function—the most natural asymmetric 2-parameter alternative—fitted the 10th, 25th, and 50th percentile groups substantially worse than tanh (ΔAIC of 55.7, 53.5, and 37.1, respectively; Supplementary Table S2), confirming empirically that symmetry is consistent with the data within the observed age range and is not a hidden assumption being violated. Second, the 75th percentile estimate of b (85.9 years for tanh) lies at the upper boundary of the observed ages and represents partial extrapolation: the inflection point lies beyond the maximum survey age of 79 years. Different sigmoid functional forms produce different extrapolated b estimates for this group (tanh, 85.9 years; erf, 87.5 years; Gompertz, 91.0 years), and the Hessian-based confidence intervals—which reflect local curvature at the optimum—therefore underestimate the true uncertainty in the 75th percentile peak age. The 75th percentile parameters should be interpreted as projections rather than as direct estimates from the observed data.

Sensitivity to fitting method. A sensitivity analysis comparing the nonlinear least squares estimates with linearised least squares estimates obtained via inverse-sigmoid transformation (Supplementary Table S3) showed that the 2 fitting methods agreed to within 1.2 years for b and 1.7 years for c in the 10th, 25th, and 75th percentile groups. The 50th percentile group exhibited a larger discrepancy in c (linearised, 22.50 years; nonlinear, 17.23 years), which we attribute to a known property of linearised regression for sigmoid models: the inverse-tanh transformation amplifies observations near the boundaries, and the 50th percentile data approach but do not reach the upper plateau within the observed age range. This finding empirically reinforces the caveat above that the Hessian-based confidence intervals likely underestimate the true uncertainty in c for groups whose upper plateau is incompletely observed. The qualitative conclusions of the study—the rank ordering of percentile groups, the monotone increase of b and c with percentile, and the rejection of the arctan and Gompertz alternatives—are preserved under both fitting methods. The nonlinear least squares formulation was retained as the primary method because it weights all observations uniformly in the original w-space, including the early- and late-age plateau observations that contribute information about the timing of onset and saturation but are necessarily excluded from the linearised fit.

Conclusions

A hyperbolic tangent model fitted to cross-sectional KNHANES data (2010-2015; n = 27,215) provides a high-precision (R2 = 0.963-0.996), 2-parameter characterisation of the age-related functional sound tooth loss trajectory across 4 percentile groups. The peak age b (57.3-85.9 years) and the spreading coefficient c (9.8-17.6 years) increase monotonically across percentile groups, revealing 2 clinically distinct risk profiles: lower percentile groups face an earlier, more abrupt risky period requiring earlier aggressive intervention, while higher percentile groups face a later but more protracted high-risk period demanding sustained preventive care.

The initiating age (b − c), ranging from 47.4 to 68.3 years, provides a concrete, patient-specific target for commencing or intensifying preventive oral care. The approach requires only routinely collected tooth count data and is readily applicable to national survey datasets from other countries, offering a transferable framework for international comparisons of oral health trajectories.
