Single-port robotic nephropexy for symptomatic nephroptosis in a young female: A case report
Authors: Kerri A. Kendrick, Eric Umbreit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 21-year-old woman with Ehlers-Danlos syndrome presented with symptomatic right renal mobility. Standing IV pyelogram demonstrated a 7 cm descent of the right kidney without hydronephrosis. She underwent a retroperitoneal single-port nephropexy without complications. At three months, imaging confirmed stable renal position with complete symptom resolution. This case highlights single-port robotic nephropexy as a safe and effective minimally invasive treatment with favorable short-term outcomes.

Introduction

Nephroptosis is a rare condition in which the kidney descends more than 5 cm—or the height of two vertebral bodies—when transitioning from a supine to a standing position.1 It is most often observed in young, thin women2 and may be asymptomatic or cause chronic flank pain, nausea, or hematuria.3 Symptomatic nephroptosis warrants intervention, often via surgical nephropexy to secure the kidney in its proper anatomic position. Robotic-assisted techniques have allowed for minimally invasive approaches to nephropexy, including the emerging single-port platform4,5.

We present the case of a 21-year-old woman with symptomatic right-sided nephroptosis and underlying Ehlers-Danos syndrome (EDS), successfully treated with a single-port robotic nephropexy (SPRN).

Case presentation

A 21-year-old female presented to the urology clinic with a one-year history of intermittent right flank pain worsened by standing, movement, or bending. She described a sensation of her kidney “dropping” when she stood up and reported a sharp, pinching pain radiating downward. Pain occurred approximately 25% of the time during movement. She denied urinary symptoms or left-sided pain.

Her medical history was notable for EDS, which manifested as generalized joint hypermobility and frequent joint subluxations.6

Standing and supine IV Pyelogram (IVP) revealed a 7 cm descent of the right kidney when standing, and a 3 cm descent of the left kidney, without hydronephrosis (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Surgical procedure

Under general anesthesia, the patient was positioned supine. A low anterior retroperitoneal access was obtained in the right lower quadrant via blunt dissection through the external and internal oblique muscles. A small peritoneal defect was closed with 3-0 Vicryl. The retroperitoneal space was developed over the psoas muscle. The single-port robotic system was docked, and insufflation was maintained at 10 mmHg with a 20 L/min air seal flow. The quadratus lumborum and psoas muscles were identified. Dissection proceeded superiorly along the psoas and inferior vena cava to identify the posterior aspect of the right kidney. The kidney was completely mobilized and fixated to the psoas using three 2-0 Vicryl sutures with Hem-o-lok clips, placed from the upper to the interpolar region. The fascia was closed in two layers, and the skin was closed subcuticularly with 4-0 Monocryl and Dermabond. Estimated blood loss was less than 5 mL. There were no intraoperative complications.

Outcome

At a three-month postoperative follow-up, repeat IVP in standing and supine positions revealed no displacement of the right kidney (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Discussion

Nephroptosis remains a rare and underdiagnosed cause of positional flank pain, especially in young women. The diagnosis relies on dynamic imaging studies, such as IVP or nuclear renography, performed in both standing and supine positions.

This case is further complicated by the patient's history of EDS, a connective tissue disorder that may contribute to her renal mobility due to ligamentous laxity7 and muscle weakness.8 Semyachkina et al.8 described five cases of FKBP14-related EDS, three of which were associated with nephroptosis; the renal mobility was attributed to underlying muscular weakness. Amato et al.7 similarly suggested that connective tissue abnormalities in EDS may contribute to increased visceral mobility, potentially predisposing patient to nephroptosis.

Surgical nephropexy has evolved from open approaches to laparoscopic and robotic-assisted techniques. SPRN offers advantages including fewer incisions, reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, and improved cosmetic outcomes. Tsai et al.4 first described SPRN, and Khoder et al.5 demonstrated that SPRN was feasible, safe, and comparable to conventional laparoscopic approaches.

This case reinforces the safety and efficacy of the single-port robotic approach, which allowed for precise kidney mobilization and fixation, resulting in durable symptomatic relief.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that SPRN is a safe, effective, and minimally invasive surgical option for patients with symptomatic nephroptosis. In appropriately selected patients, particularly those with connective tissue disorders like EDS, this approach may offer excellent anatomic and clinical outcomes.
