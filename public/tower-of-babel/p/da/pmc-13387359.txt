Effects of rose pollen and nectar on body size and ovarian development of Frankliniella occidentalis (Thysanoptera: Thripidae)
Authors: Xin Lei Fang, Jun Rui Zhi, Wen Bo Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWestern flower thrips, Frankliniella occidentalis (Pergande), is an omnivorous pest feeding on plant tissues and floral resources, including pollen and nectar, and can consume mite eggs. Its growth and reproduction are strongly influenced by diet quality. Compared with rose petals alone, a standardized whole-flower diet contains reproductive organs and therefore provides additional floral resources. In this study, a standardized whole-flower diet, rose petals, and kidney bean leaves as a host-plant control were used to investigate whether differences in floral resource availability affect thrips development and reproduction. After three consecutive generations, multiple parameters were measured, including body size (length and width), ovarian development (ovariole length and number of mature eggs), juvenile hormone (JH) content, vitellogenin (Vg) content, and expression of Kr-h1, br, JHEH, and Vg. Relative to the kidney bean leaf control, thrips on the whole-flower diet exhibited significantly greater body length and width, whereas those on rose petals had shorter length and similar width. Reproductive traits varied with adult age, and females reared on the whole-flower diet generally had longer ovarioles than those reared on rose petals and significantly greater numbers of mature eggs than the kidney bean leaf control at each sampled time point from Days 2 to 9 after adult emergence. JH and Vg contents and gene expression also varied significantly with diet, adult age, and their interaction. Together, these findings indicate that floral resources in the whole-flower diet enhance the growth and reproductive development of F. occidentalis and may favor its performance on flowering host plants.

Introduction

Frankliniella occidentalis (Pergande) is a globally invasive agricultural pest originating from North America. It has become one of the most economically destructive pest species (Wu et al. 2018). F. occidentalis causes extensive damage, including leaf curling, petal discoloration, fruit deformation, and tissue necrosis, thereby reducing photosynthetic efficiency, crop yield, and market value (Hao et al. 2002, Kindt et al. 2003). As a polyphagous insect, F. occidentalis has a broad host range, spanning hundreds of plant species (Reitz 2009), with up to 600 species reported (Hu et al. 2021). In addition to feeding on plant leaves, it can utilize alternative food resources such as pollen, nectar, and mite eggs (Kirk 1985, Trichilo and Leigh 1986, Ogita et al. 2021). This feeding flexibility likely contributes to its rapid spread and strong adaptability across diverse host environments.

Pollen and nectar are rich in amino acids, sugars, lipids, and diverse micronutrients, making them critical nutrient sources that significantly enhance physiological reserves and reproductive system development (Dong et al. 2008, Xuan et al. 2017, Yang et al. 2025). Zhang et al. (2016) demonstrated that cotton flowers significantly promote ovarian development and increase ovariole length and follicle number in Apolygus lucorum. Yang et al. (2025) found that fructose supplementation led to maximum fecundity and earlier peak egg production in Spodoptera frugiperda.

Similarly, the effects of pollen and nectar on F. occidentalis have been widely reported. Zhi et al. (2005) demonstrated that supplementation with apple pollen significantly reduced larval development time and increased both the net reproductive rate and the intrinsic rate of increase in F. occidentalis. More recently, Tian et al. (2025) found that adding pine pollen to kidney bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) leaves significantly enhanced fecundity in F. occidentalis, accompanied by increased vitellogenin (Vg) expression and vitellogenin (Vg) content, providing direct evidence for the role of pollen in promoting reproductive output.

Roses are valued as ornamental plants for their economic and cultural importance. Importantly, F. occidentalis commonly infests roses during the flowering stage, causing visible injuries such as streaking, discoloration, and deformation, which reduce their ornamental value (Park et al. 2002, Avellaneda et al. 2022). Although rose petals contain carbohydrates and proteins (Hegde et al. 2022, Liu et al. 2024), whole rose flowers provide more diverse nutrition, as their reproductive structures offer richer sources of pollen and nectar (Li et al. 2023, Yue et al. 2023). Yue (2025) showed that the contents of amino acids, free fatty acids, and total sugar in the stamens and pistils of roses were higher than those in the rose petals. Zhou et al. (2021) reported higher protein, soluble sugar, and lipid contents in F. occidentalis adults fed whole rose flowers than in those fed petals alone. Zhou et al. (2023) further demonstrated that whole rose flowers provide superior nutrition compared with petals alone, thereby significantly influencing thrips performance. Furthermore, Yue et al. (2023) showed that differences in nutrient composition between rose petals and whole flowers resulted in corresponding changes in nutrient content and digestive enzyme activities in F. occidentalis. Similarly, Li et al. (2023) found that different floral parts significantly affected F. occidentalis development, with nectar and pollen exerting positive effects on population growth and reproduction; these effects remained stable through the F3 generation.

Insect body size is closely related to developmental growth regulation and can be affected by environmental and nutritional conditions (Nijhout et al. 2014). Therefore, body size provides a useful morphological indicator for evaluating growth performance under different diets. Ovarian development is also an important indicator of female reproductive maturation and physiological status, because oogenesis, vitellogenesis, and oocyte maturation are directly linked to the reproductive capacity of adult females (Roy et al. 2018). At the endocrine level, juvenile hormone (JH) is a major regulator of insect development and female reproduction, and it is closely associated with vitellogenesis and oogenesis in many insects (Roy et al. 2018). Vitellogenin (Vg), encoded by the Vg gene, is the major yolk protein precursor synthesized during vitellogenesis and deposited into developing oocytes, making it a direct indicator of yolk formation and ovarian maturation (Tufail and Takeda 2008, Roy et al. 2018). Several JH-related genes are also involved in hormone-regulated developmental and reproductive processes. Juvenile hormone epoxide hydrolase (JHEH) participates in JH degradation and contributes to the regulation of JH homeostasis (Tusun et al. 2017). Krüppel homolog 1 (Kr-h1) encodes an early JH-response transcription factor that mediates JH signaling and has been associated with vitellogenesis, oocyte maturation, and ovarian development (Song et al. 2014, Jiang et al. 2017). In addition, broad (br) encodes a hormone-responsive transcription factor involved in the crosstalk between JH and ecdysone signaling and is associated with developmental transition and ovarian development (Jiang et al. 2017).

Despite this evidence, current research has largely emphasized phenomenological descriptions of food source effects on development and population parameters, while the physiological and molecular responses associated with these effects remain insufficiently characterized. Therefore, the present study aimed to address this knowledge gap by systematically comparing body size as an indicator of somatic growth, ovarian development as an indicator of female reproductive maturation, and JH content, Vg content, and the expression levels of Vg and JH-related genes as indicators of reproductive endocrine-related responses in F3-generation F. occidentalis females obtained after three generations of rearing on rose petals, whole rose flowers, or kidney bean leaves. This study documents changes in reproductive traits, hormone levels, and gene expression under different dietary regimes and may provide a basis for further investigation of diet-associated responses in F. occidentalis.

Materials and Methods

Insect and Plant Sources

F. occidentalis individuals were collected from legume vegetable crops in Huaxi District, Guiyang City, Guizhou Province, China. The colony was maintained for more than 50 generations on kidney bean pods in RXZ-series multistage programmable intelligent climate chambers (Jiangnan Ningbo Instrument Factory, China). Rearing conditions were 25 °C ± 1 °C, 70% ± 5% relative humidity, and a 14 h light:10 h dark photoperiod. For all experiments, newly emerged adults (<24 h old) from the same cohort were selected as experimental insects.

Rose plants (cultivar “Mohong”) were purchased from Guizhou Tashi Agricultural Co., Ltd. (Jianzhong Town, Weng’an County, Guizhou Province, China). Plants were grown outdoors under natural conditions and were not treated with any pesticides during the growth period. Petals used as a food source were obtained from rose flowers at a uniform flowering stage (open at 3° to 5°). For the rose petal diet, the outermost petals and the small inner petals adjacent to the reproductive organs were removed, and the remaining petals were used as the food source.

For the whole-flower treatment, intact flowers were not used directly because individual blossoms varied in flower size, petal mass, anther dehiscence, and pollen or nectar availability. Standardized reconstituted whole-flower diets were prepared. Fully opened rose flowers were dissected into petals and reproductive organs (stamens and pistils), which were then recombined at a fresh weight ratio of 4:1 (petals: reproductive organs). This diet included reproductive organs and therefore provided pollen- and nectar-associated floral resources, and is hereafter referred to as whole rose flowers.

Kidney bean leaves (cultivar “Jinshulu”) were established as a host-plant control. The seeds were purchased from Shennong Seed Industry (Xinji, China) and placed into plastic pots containing sterilized nutrient mix, with two seeds per pot. These pots were kept inside a growth chamber under the same conditions as described above. Once the seedlings had developed three or four true leaves—typically around 13 days after germination—those showing uniform growth were chosen as experimental material.

Sampling of F3-Generation F. occidentalis Under Different Dietary Treatments

The F3 generation of F. occidentalis from rose petals or whole rose flowers: Adult female F. occidentalis reared on kidney bean pods were allowed to mate for 3 days after adult emergence and were then used to establish the experimental dietary treatments. For each dietary treatment, 20 rearing boxes (20 cm × 15 cm × 5 cm) were prepared, and 400 mated adult females were introduced into each rearing box containing either rose petals or whole rose flowers. The same fresh food was replaced every 24 h. Petals or flowers containing eggs laid within plant tissues were transferred to new rearing boxes, where the eggs hatched and developed into adults. These adults were designated as the F1 generation. F1 adults were maintained on their respective diets to produce the F2 generation, which was allowed to develop into adults; this process was continued until the F3 generation was obtained. Thrips from the F3 generation were used as experimental subjects. The F3 generation was selected because preliminary observations indicated that growth and reproductive traits of F. occidentalis under the three dietary treatments had stabilized by the third generation, allowing assessment of relatively stable diet-associated responses.

The F3 generation of F. occidentalis from kidney bean leaves: The stems of kidney bean plants were cut just above the roots, and the leaf-bearing stem segments were placed into containers (30 cm × 20 cm × 20 cm; Chinese patent CN 218302954 U) for rearing thrips following the protocol described by Qiu et al. (2024). A total of 20 such containers were used, and each received two bean plants. The F3 generation of F. occidentalis was obtained using the same treatment method applied to rose petals and whole rose flowers, except that the kidney bean leaves were used as the host.

Measurement of Body Length and Width in Female F. occidentalis

For each diet, two newly emerged female adults were randomly collected from each of the 20 rearing boxes, giving a total of 40 individuals per diet, with each individual considered one biological replicate. Individuals were placed in a − 20 °C freezer for approximately 30 s to achieve rapid immobilization and posture fixation without affecting morphological structures. After freezing, samples were immediately removed and gently adjusted to an appropriate measuring posture. Measurements were conducted under a stereomicroscope (J-X5, Newheyer Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) at 20× magnification. Body length was defined as the linear distance from the anterior margin of the head to the posterior end of the abdomen, and body width was measured at the widest point of the pronotum (Fig. 1).

Effects of Different Diets on Ovarian Development

After pupation of F3-generation larvae, female pupae were identified based on external morphology and transferred to new rearing containers. The emergence of each female pupa was monitored every 30 min. Newly emerged female adults were individually placed into rearing cups (inner diameter: 2.9 cm; outer diameter: 3.7 cm; height: 3.3 cm) and paired with a newly emerged male for mating. Ten adult female F. occidentalis individuals were collected at 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 9 days after emergence for each food type and placed into 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes containing phosphate-buffered saline. Ovaries were dissected under a stereomicroscope (SZX16, Olympus Industrial Co., Ltd., Japan) at 20× to 40× magnification. During dissection, the thoracic notum was gently pressed with one dissecting needle, while the external genitalia were carefully pulled from the posterior end of the abdomen with another needle. The digestive tract, fat body, and associated tissues were removed, leaving the reproductive system intact. Ovarian morphology and structure were examined under a stereomicroscope (J-X5, Newheyer Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Each female has eight ovarioles, and all intact ovarioles were measured whenever possible. Thus, up to 80 ovarioles were measured for each food type at each sampling time point. In rare cases, one ovariole was damaged during dissection and was excluded from measurement. Ovariole length was measured using Newheyer measurement software, and ovarian morphology was photographed (Fig. 2). Ovariole length data are presented as mean ± SE based on the 10 dissected females within each treatment and sampling time point. Ovarian development was evaluated quantitatively based on ovariole length and the number of mature eggs (Xie 2022).

Measurement of ovariole length in female F. occidentalis. The numerical value shown in the image represents the measured ovariole length of the dissected individual and was used for subsequent quantitative analysis.

Determination of Vg and JH-Related Gene Expression Levels

Following the method described by Zheng et al. (2014), EF-1a (GenBank accession no. AB277244.1) was used as the reference gene because it has been reported to be stably expressed across F. occidentalis developmental stages. The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table 1. Primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was performed using the FastStart Essential DNA Green Master (Roche) kit. RT-qPCR analysis of target genes was conducted with reference to Qiu et al. (2024), with sampling time points matching those used in the ovarian development study. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with the kidney bean leaves diet on Day 1 after adult emergence as the calibrator (Supplementary Table S1). The raw Ct values of the target genes and corresponding EF-1a reference genes are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1. For each dietary treatment, samples were collected at 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 9 days after adult emergence. For each gene, three biological replicates were prepared for each dietary treatment at each time point, with each replicate consisting of 60 thrips.

Determination of Vg and JH Contents

For each dietary treatment, samples were collected at 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 9 days after adult emergence, matching the time points used in the ovarian development study. At each time point, four biological replicates were prepared for each treatment, and each replicate consisted of 60 adult female F. occidentalis. Thrips were collected, placed into 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Vg and JH contents were quantified using a commercial insect vitellogenin (VTG) enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit and an insect JH ELISA kit (Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), respectively. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Tecan microplate reader. Vg and JH concentrations were calculated from the corresponding ELISA standard curves according to the manufacturer′s instructions, and the final values were corrected by the dilution factor.

Data Analysis

Raw data were recorded and organized using Microsoft Excel 2019. Except for the Spearman correlation analyses, all statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) (IBM, Corp 2019). Data were examined for normality and homogeneity of variance before parametric analyses. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the effects of dietary treatment, adult age, and their interaction on ovariole length, mature egg number, juvenile hormone (JH) content, vitellogenin (Vg) content, and the expression levels of Kr-h1, br, JHEH, and Vg. For comparisons among adult ages within the same dietary treatment or among dietary treatments at the same adult age, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used when the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were met. When homogeneity of variance was not satisfied but parametric analysis remained appropriate, Welch’s ANOVA followed by the Games–Howell post hoc test was applied. When the assumptions required for parametric analysis were not met, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests was used. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and P < 0.01 was considered highly significant where applicable. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 10.6 (GraphPad Software 2025).

To further explore relationships among reproductive traits, hormone/protein contents, and gene-expression responses, Spearman correlation analyses were performed using R version 4.5.3 in RStudio version 2026.01.2 (Build 418) (Posit Team 2025; R Core Team 2026). Correlation analyses were conducted separately for each dietary treatment using treatment-age mean values for ovariole length, mature egg number, JH content, Vg content, and the expression levels of Kr-h1, br, JHEH, and Vg. Because these analyses were based on mean values for each treatment-age combination rather than on individual-level paired measurements, the resulting correlations were interpreted as exploratory associations and were not used to infer direct regulatory relationships.

For supplementary quality-control assessment of the qPCR data, raw Ct values of the target genes and the EF-1α reference gene were summarized and visualized. These analyses were used to describe the distribution of amplification signals and to examine the consistency of EF-1α Ct values across dietary treatments and adult ages. The raw Ct data were presented as supplementary information and were not used independently to infer differential gene expression, which was evaluated using normalized expression data.

Results

Effects of Rose Petals and Whole Rose Flowers on Body Length and Width of Female F. occidentalis

Compared with thrips fed on kidney bean leaves (control), thrips fed on whole rose flowers exhibited significantly greater body length (F = 22.725, df = 2, 117, P < 0.001), whereas those fed on rose petals were shorter. Body width differed significantly among dietary treatments (F = 18.036, df = 2, 76.621, P < 0.001; Table 2). Body width did not change significantly in thrips fed on rose petals compared with the control, but thrips fed on whole rose flowers had significantly greater body width.

Two-Way ANOVA of Diet- and Age-Dependent Effects on Reproductive Traits, Hormone Content, and Gene Expression

Two-way ANOVA revealed significant effects of food type, adult age, and their interaction on ovariole length, mature egg number, JH content, Vg content, and the expression levels of Kr-h1, br, JHEH, and Vg in F3 female F. occidentalis for all main effects (P < 0.001) and interaction terms (P < 0.001; Table 3). The significant main effect of food type indicates that thrips fed on rose petals, whole rose flowers, and kidney bean leaves differed in reproductive development, hormone accumulation, and gene expression responses. The significant main effect of adult age indicates that these reproductive, physiological, and molecular variables changed markedly during adult development. The significant interaction terms indicate that the temporal patterns of these reproductive, physiological, and gene-expression variables differed among thrips fed on rose petals, whole rose flowers, and kidney bean leaves. These results further suggest that the effects of diet were age-dependent rather than constant across the adult stage, with different diets producing distinct temporal response patterns in F3 female F. occidentalis.

Effects of Rose Petals and Whole Rose Flowers on Ovariole Length

In thrips, ovariole length varied significantly depending on diet and adult age (Table 4 and Fig. 3). A consistent temporal pattern was observed: ovariole length increased to a peak and subsequently decreased (kidney bean leaves: F = 117.984, df = 5, 24.544, P < 0.001; rose petals: F = 23.388, df = 5, 54, P < 0.001; whole rose flowers: F = 86.042, df = 5, 54, P < 0.001). However, the timing of this peak differed among three dietary treatments: ovariole length peaked at 3 days in thrips fed on kidney bean leaves or whole rose flowers, but at 7 days in those fed on rose petals. Furthermore, significant differences in ovariole length among the three dietary groups were observed at all time points. Compared with the control, ovariole length on Day 1 (F = 12.278, df = 2, 27, P < 0.001) increased with both rose diets, and no significant difference was detected between rose petals and whole rose flowers. On Days 2 and 5, rose petals caused no significant change compared with the control (Day 2: F = 43.429, df = 2, 27, P < 0.001; Day 5: F = 103.398, df = 2, 7, P < 0.001), whereas whole rose flowers led to an increase. On Day 3, rose petals decreased ovariole length compared with the control (F = 100.040, df = 2, 27, P < 0.001), but whole rose flowers had no significant effect compared with the control. On Days 7 and 9, both rose diets increased ovariole length compared with the control (Day 7: F = 96.977, df = 2, 27, P < 0.001; Day 9: F = 68.062, df = 2, 14.745, P < 0.001), with whole rose flowers producing a stronger effect than rose petals. Overall, except on Day 1, thrips fed on whole rose flowers exhibited significantly greater ovariole length than those fed on rose petals.

Effects of Food Type on Mature Egg Numbers

As shown in Table 4 and Fig. 3, food type influenced the reproductive output of F. occidentalis. Both the temporal pattern and magnitude of egg production differed among treatments. Thrips fed on rose petals exhibited a delayed and relatively low mature egg accumulation. Mature eggs were first detected on Day 3 (1.1 ± 0.3 eggs), and although egg numbers increased over time, the changes were not statistically significant across the measured time points (F = 1.074, df = 3, 36, P = 0.374).

In contrast, thrips fed on whole rose flowers showed a more vigorous and dynamic reproductive response (F = 4.962, df = 4, 45, P = 0.002). Oviposition began earlier, on Day 2 (2.5 ± 0.2 eggs), and fecundity increased to its highest value on Day 5 (4.1 ± 0.2 eggs), followed by a slight decline to 3.3 ± 0.2 eggs by Day 9. No eggs were deposited on Day 1 regardless of diet. On Day 2, 3, 5, and 7, feeding on rose petals did not significantly alter mature egg numbers relative to the control (Day 2: P = 0.146; Day 3: P = 0.085; Day 5: P = 0.866; Day 7: P = 0.635), while feeding on whole rose flowers resulted in a significant increase (Day 2: P < 0.001; Day 3: P = 0.012; Day 5: P < 0.001; Day 7: P = 0.002). On Day 9, both rose diets elevated mature egg numbers compared with the control (rose petals: P = 0.013; whole rose flowers: P < 0.001), with whole rose flowers producing a larger increase than rose petals.

Effects of Rose Petals and Whole Rose Flowers on JH Content and Related Gene Expression in F. occidentalis

During feeding on rose petals, JH content exhibited a fluctuating pattern (Table 4): it initially increased, then decreased, increased again, decreased once more, and subsequently rose (F = 157.412, df = 5, 18, P < 0.001). Overall, JH content reached its peak on Day 5. In contrast, during feeding on whole rose flowers (F = 302.771, df = 5, 18, P < 0.001), JH content was lowest on Days 1 and 2, increased slightly on Day 3, and then decreased again by Day 5. Thereafter, it increased continuously, reaching its highest value on Day 9. Compared with the control (thrips fed on kidney bean leaves), JH content in thrips fed on rose parts varied by day as follows. On Days 1 and 5, JH content decreased in thrips fed on both rose petals and whole rose flowers, with a greater decrease observed in those fed on whole rose flowers (Day 1: F = 793.941, df = 2, 9, P < 0.001; Day 5: F = 560.987, df = 2, 9, P < 0.001). On Day 2, JH content in thrips fed on rose petals was comparable to the control (F = 218.919, df = 2, 9, P < 0.001) and significantly higher than in those fed on whole rose flowers. On Day 3, JH content decreased in thrips fed on both rose diets (F = 544.679, df = 2, 9, P < 0.001), and no significant difference was observed between the two rose diets. On Day 7, JH content was reduced in thrips fed on both rose petals and whole rose flowers (F = 1093.971, df = 2, 9, P < 0.001), and the reduction was more pronounced with rose petals. On Day 9, JH content decreased with rose petals but increased with whole rose flowers (F = 250.429, df = 2, 9, P < 0.001). As to the JH content between thrips fed on whole rose flowers and rose petals, it could be found that thrips fed on whole rose flowers exhibited significantly higher JH content than those fed on rose petals on Days 7 and 9, but the opposite pattern was observed on Days 1, 2, and 5.

Feeding on rose petals resulted in a fluctuating pattern of Kr-h1 expression (F = 473.725, df = 5, 5.454, P < 0.001; Fig. 4), with an initial increase followed by a decrease, a subsequent continuous rise, and a final decrease, with relatively high expression observed on Days 2 and 7. In contrast, in thrips fed on whole rose flowers, Kr-h1 expression was highest on Days 1 and 9 and reached its lowest levels on Days 5 and 7 (F = 215.244, df = 5, 5.322, P < 0.001). Compared with the control, Kr-h1 expression on Days 1 and 9 was significantly lower in thrips fed on rose petals, but remained at control levels in those fed on whole rose flowers (Day 1: F = 670.255, df = 2, 6, P < 0.001; Day 9: F = 101.703, df = 2, 6, P < 0.001). From Day 2 to Day 7, the control consistently exhibited the highest expression (Day 2: F = 180.380, df = 2, 2.702, P = 0.001; Day 3: F = 367.522, df = 2, 3.053, P < 0.001; Day 5: F = 413.549, df = 2, 3.368, P < 0.001; Day 7: F = 713.442, df = 2, 6, P < 0.001). Among the rose diets, expression was lower in thrips fed on rose petals than in those fed on whole rose flowers on Days 2 and 3, whereas the pattern reversed on Days 5 and 7, with lower expression observed in thrips fed on whole rose flowers.

Effects of different foods and adult age on the expression levels of Kr-h1, br, JHEH, and Vg in F. occidentalis.

The expression level of br peaked on Day 1 in thrips fed on rose petals (F = 627.071, df = 5, 5.281, P < 0.001); in contrast, br expression reached its maximum on Day 9 in thrips fed on whole rose flowers (F = 346.628, df = 5, 12, P < 0.001; Fig. 4). Compared with the control, br expression showed no significant change in thrips fed on rose petals, but it increased significantly in thrips fed on whole rose flowers on Day 1 (F = 71.714, df = 2, 6, P < 0.001). On Day 2, br expression decreased significantly in thrips fed on rose petals, while it increased significantly in those fed on whole rose flowers (F = 225.134, df = 2, 6, P < 0.001). On Day 3, br expression decreased in thrips fed on both rose petals and whole rose flowers, with a greater decrease observed in the rose petals group (F = 698.281, df = 2, 6, P < 0.001). On Day 5, br expression increased significantly in thrips fed on rose petals, but decreased significantly in those fed on whole rose flowers (F = 306.240, df = 2, 6, P < 0.001). On Day 7, br expression increased in thrips fed on rose petals (F = 13.767, df = 2, 6, P = 0.006), while no significant change was detected in those fed on whole rose flowers. On Day 9, br expression increased in thrips fed on both rose petals and whole rose flowers (F = 641.045, df = 2, 6, P < 0.001), with a more pronounced effect in the whole rose flowers group.

In the two rose-diet treatments, the highest JHEH expression level was observed on Day 9, whereas this pattern was not observed in the control group (Fig. 4). In thrips fed on rose petals, JHEH expression on Day 9 was significantly higher than Days 1, 2, 3, 5, and 7 (F = 359.363, df = 5, 5.221, P < 0.001). In thrips fed on whole rose flowers, Day 9 was also significantly higher than Days 1, 2, 3, 5, and 7 (F = 486.587, df = 5, 5.369, P < 0.001). In thrips fed on rose petals, the lowest mean JHEH expression level was observed on Day 5, although it did not differ significantly from that on Day 1. In contrast, in thrips fed on whole rose flowers, the lowest expression levels occurred on Days 1 and 5, with no significant difference between these two time points. Compared with the control, JHEH expression increased in thrips fed on either rose petals or whole rose flowers on Day 1 (F = 143.536, df = 2, 3.333, P < 0.001), and there was no significant difference between the two rose parts. On Days 2 and 9, JHEH expression increased in thrips fed on both rose petals and whole rose flowers (Day 2: F = 3838.697, df = 2, 2.676, P < 0.001; Day 9: F = 1546.615, df = 2, 6, P < 0.001), with a more pronounced effect observed in those fed on whole rose flowers. On Day 3, JHEH expression decreased significantly in thrips fed on rose petals (F = 447.815, df = 2, 2.832, P < 0.001) but increased significantly in those fed on whole rose flowers. On Day 5, JHEH expression decreased in thrips fed on both rose petals and whole rose flowers (F = 297.403, df = 2, 3.394, P < 0.001), with a greater decrease observed in the rose petals group. On Day 7, JHEH expression showed no significant change in thrips fed on rose petals, while it increased significantly in thrips fed on whole rose flowers (F = 278.324, df = 2, 6, P < 0.001).

Overall, whole rose flowers tended to induce stronger changes in JH content and the expression of Kr-h1, br, and JHEH than rose petals, although the response patterns varied among genes and adult ages.

Effects of Rose Petals and Whole Rose Flowers on Vg Content and Related Gene Expression in F. occidentalis

The dynamics of Vg content over the 9-day experimental period were strongly influenced by diet, with each food source producing a distinct pattern (Table 4). In thrips fed on rose petals, Vg content fluctuated considerably over time: it peaked on Day 2, dropped sharply to a minimum on Day 3, and then varied thereafter (F = 188.641, df = 5, 18, P < 0.001). In contrast, thrips fed on whole rose flowers exhibited a different trajectory (F = 118.398, df = 5, 18, P < 0.001). After an initial decline from Day 1 to Day 2, Vg content increased to a maximum on Day 5, which was significantly higher than that on Days 1, 2, 3, 7, and 9, followed by a decrease. Compared with the control, Vg content increased in thrips fed on both rose petals and whole rose flowers on Days 1 and 9, with a more pronounced effect in rose petals than in whole rose flowers (Day 1: F = 389.358, df = 2, 9, P < 0.001; Day 9:, F = 89.663, df = 2, 9, P < 0.001). On Day 2, Vg content increased significantly in thrips fed on rose petals, but decreased significantly in those fed on whole rose flowers compared with the control, and Vg content was significantly higher in rose petals than in whole rose flowers (F = 968.244, df = 2, 9, P < 0.001). On Day 3, Vg content showed no significant change in thrips fed on rose petals compared with the control, but it increased significantly in thrips fed on whole rose flowers, with whole rose flowers resulting in higher Vg content than rose petals (F = 12.821, df = 2, 9, P = 0.002). On Day 5, Vg content increased in thrips fed on both rose petals and whole rose flowers compared with the control (F = 182.569, df = 2, 9, P < 0.001), and no significant difference was detected between the two rose diets. On Day 7, Vg content increased significantly in thrips fed on rose petals compared with the control (F = 91.523, df = 2, 9, P < 0.001), but it showed no significant change in thrips fed on whole rose flowers, with Vg content being significantly higher in rose petals than in whole rose flowers.

In thrips fed on rose petals, Vg expression decreased from Day 1 to Day 3, although differences between Days 1 and 2 and between Days 2 and 3 were not significant, followed by a fluctuating pattern, eventually reaching its highest level on Day 9, which was significantly higher than all other adult ages (Fig. 4; F = 218.028, df = 5, 12, P < 0.001). In thrips fed on whole rose flowers, no significant difference in Vg expression was observed between Days 1 and 2; expression then increased to a peak on Day 3, which was significantly higher than all other adult ages, and then gradually decreased (F = 93.094, df = 5, 12, P < 0.001). Compared with the control, feeding on rose petals reduced Vg expression at all time points (Day 1: F = 1736.336, df = 2, 6, P < 0.001; Day 2: F = 9357.149, df = 2, 6, P < 0.001; Day 3: F = 467.735, df = 2, 2.667, P < 0.001; Day 5: F = 11.394, df = 2, 6, P = 0.009; Day 7: F = 295.273, df = 2, 3.215, P < 0.001; Day 9: F = 83.639, df = 2, 6, P < 0.001), whereas feeding on whole rose flowers reduced Vg expression on Days 1, 2, 3, 7, and 9, but not on Day 5. Vg expression was significantly higher in thrips fed on whole rose flowers than in those fed on rose petals on Days 1, 2, 3, and 5, with no significant change observed on Day 7, whereas it was significantly lower in thrips fed on whole rose flowers than in those fed on rose petals on Day 9.

Diet-Dependent Correlations among JH, Vg, Gene Expression, and Reproductive Traits

To further examine whether qPCR-based transcript levels were associated with immunoassay-based JH and Vg contents and reproductive traits, Spearman correlation analyses were performed using treatment-age mean values (Fig. 5). The correlation patterns varied markedly among females fed kidney bean leaves, rose petals, and whole rose flowers, indicating diet-dependent relationships among endocrine signals, gene expression, and reproductive traits. In the kidney bean leaf-fed control group, JH content was significantly and positively correlated with JHEH expression, whereas the other correlations were not statistically significant, although several positive associations were observed between reproductive traits and the expression of Kr-h1, JHEH, and Vg. In the rose petal-fed group, moderate to strong correlations were observed among several variables, including a positive association between Vg content and Kr-h1 expression and negative associations between Vg content and egg number or ovariole length; however, these relationships did not reach statistical significance. In the whole rose flower-fed group, JH content was again positively correlated with JHEH expression. In addition, Kr-h1 expression was strongly and positively correlated with br expression, whereas both Kr-h1 and br expression levels were negatively correlated with ovariole length. Overall, the relationships between transcript abundance, hormone/protein contents, and reproductive traits were not consistent across diets, suggesting that these associations were strongly influenced by dietary treatment and adult age rather than reflecting uniform regulatory relationships under all feeding conditions.

(B) Rose petals. (C) Whole rose flowers. Correlation analyses were performed based on treatment-age mean values rather than individual-level paired measurements. Discussion

Food resources, as the primary source of energy and nutrients for insects, continuously influence their growth, development, and reproductive capacity throughout their life cycle (Ovchinnikov et al. 2023, Archer and Weldon 2024, Mokkapati et al. 2025). Previous studies have shown that resource acquisition can affect body size, reproductive potential, and physiological state during the adult stage (Leyria et al. 2025, Rho and Lee 2025). In the present study, the three diets were associated with differences in body size, ovarian development, egg production, and several reproductive physiological and molecular indicators in F. occidentalis. These findings are consistent with recent research on the relationship between nutritional quality, insect body size development, and reproductive output (Cirino et al. 2022, Kreitman et al. 2022, Holmes et al. 2023). In the present study, the inclusion of kidney bean leaves as a host-plant control allowed us to evaluate whether rose petals and whole rose flowers enhanced or reduced the performance of F. occidentalis relative to a conventional host-plant baseline. Compared with females fed on kidney bean leaves, females fed on whole rose flowers showed significantly greater body length and body width, whereas those fed on rose petals showed reduced body length and unchanged body width. These results indicate that whole rose flowers provided a more favorable dietary condition for somatic growth than rose petals, while petals alone did not consistently improve body-size development relative to the control.

Compared with thrips fed whole rose flowers, thrips fed rose petals showed a later peak in egg production and mature eggs. This result is consistent with previous studies (Gillespie and McGregor 2000, Wittmeyer et al. 2001), which showed that mixed or insect-containing diets promote ovarian development and fecundity in predatory bugs compared with single or artificial diets. In general, larger individuals may have greater resource availability for reproductive system development while maintaining metabolism. Furthermore, ovarian development depends heavily on nutritional intake, with well-nourished individuals exhibiting more complete ovarian structures and higher reproductive potential (Boggs and Ross 1993, Berger et al. 2008, Mirth et al. 2019, Zhu et al. 2024). In addition, the supplementary kidney bean leaf-fed control provided a host-plant baseline for evaluating the two rose-derived diets. This comparison further supported that the reproductive advantage of the whole flower diet was mainly reflected in more sustained ovariole development and greater mature egg production, consistent with previous evidence that diet quality and host-plant resources can shape ovarian development and fecundity in insects (Qi et al. 2022, Li et al. 2023, Yue et al. 2023).

Juvenile hormone (JH) plays a key role in insect reproduction by precisely regulating its dynamic levels within the body, thereby significantly influencing ovarian development and oogenesis (Bellés et al. 2005, Bellés and Piulachs 2015). Food quality can influence JH synthesis and signaling in insects, although the direction and magnitude of this effect may vary among species, developmental stages, and dietary contexts (Riddiford 2012, Zhang et al. 2024). For example, the addition of pollen to kidney bean leaves increased JH content and the expression of br and Kr-h1 in F. occidentalis (Qiu et al. 2024, Tian et al. 2025), suggesting that nutrient supplementation may enhance JH-related endocrine activity in this species. However, studies in other insects indicate that nutritionally unfavorable diets do not necessarily suppress JH activity. In the brown planthopper, Nilaparvata lugens, a low-quality diet increased JH levels during the adult stage, suggesting that poor nutritional conditions may also induce compensatory or stress-related endocrine responses (Kang et al. 2021). In the present study, JH content and the expression of JH-related genes also showed age-dependent responses to rose-derived diets. Specifically, neither rose petals nor whole rose flowers produced consistently higher JH levels or uniformly upregulated JH-related genes across all adult ages. Instead, the direction and magnitude of dietary effects changed over time, indicating that the JH response of female F. occidentalis to floral diets was temporally dynamic rather than linear. Similar stage-dependent reproductive regulation has been reported in Tribolium castaneum, in which JH was required for vitellogenin synthesis and reproductive maturation during early adult development (Parthasarathy et al. 2010).

Vitellogenin (Vg) is fundamental to insect reproduction, serving as the primary nutritional resource for embryonic development (Wu et al. 2020) and as the molecular basis for oocyte maturation, thereby directly reflecting reproductive potential (Bitondi and Simões 1996). The synthesis of this protein is highly dependent on nutritional supply and energy metabolism and is tightly regulated by a coordinated endocrine system (Messan et al. 2018, Bryś et al. 2021). In the present study, Vg expression was higher at most time points in insects fed whole rose flowers than in those fed rose petals. This finding was consistent with Zhao et al. (2018), who reported that in S. exigua, feeding on a more nutritious host plant (lettuce) resulted in significantly higher SeVg and SeVgR expression than feeding on tomato or celery. Furthermore, their study revealed a significant linear relationship between gene expression levels, pupal weight, and fecundity. By contrast, Vg content in F. occidentalis showed a different temporal pattern from Vg expression: it was significantly higher in thrips fed whole rose flowers only on Day 3, not significantly different on Day 5, and lower on the remaining days. This temporal mismatch suggests that transcript abundance and protein content did not vary in parallel across all time points. Consistently, correlation analysis based on treatment-age mean values showed that RNA-protein/hormone relationships were diet- and stage-dependent, and should be interpreted as exploratory correlative evidence rather than individual-level regulatory evidence.

Vitellogenesis is controlled by coordinated nutritional and endocrine signals rather than by a single hormonal or transcriptional factor (Lu et al. 2016, Wu et al. 2020). The correlation analyses further support a cautious interpretation of the relationships among endocrine signals, gene expression, protein accumulation, and reproductive traits. Because these correlations were calculated using treatment-age mean values rather than individual-level paired measurements, they should be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than direct evidence of individual-level regulatory relationships. In addition, transcript abundance does not always directly predict protein abundance, because translation, protein degradation, transport, and tissue-specific accumulation can decouple mRNA and protein levels (Liu et al. 2016). The associations among JH content, Vg content, JH- and reproduction-related gene expression, ovariole length, and mature egg number were not consistent across dietary treatments. In both the control and whole rose flower-fed groups, JH content was positively correlated with JHEH expression, whereas other relationships differed among dietary treatments. In the whole rose flower-fed group, Kr-h1 expression was positively correlated with br expression, but both genes were negatively correlated with ovariole length. These results indicated that the relationships among endocrine signals, transcript levels, protein accumulation, and reproductive traits were strongly influenced by both diet and adult age. Therefore, they did not support a single uniform regulatory pathway across all food treatments.

In conclusion, the addition of kidney bean leaves as a host-plant control clarified the biological significance of the two rose-derived diets. Rose petals alone did not consistently enhance somatic growth or reproductive development relative to the control and, in some cases, produced delayed or weaker responses. In contrast, whole rose flowers increased female body size, promoted more sustained ovariole development, and enhanced mature egg production in F. occidentalis. However, these reproductive advantages were not accompanied by uniformly higher JH content or Vg expression, indicating that diet-associated reproductive changes involved complex, age-dependent physiological and molecular responses. Future studies should compare larval and adult responses to different diets and examine upstream nutrient-sensing pathways, particularly insulin/insulin-like peptide signaling, together with measurements of nutritional composition and feeding intake. This would provide a broader framework for understanding how dietary differences are associated with stage-specific physiological responses in F. occidentalis.
