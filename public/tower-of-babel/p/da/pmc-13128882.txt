Biostimulant-driven improvement in yield, seed quality, and soil health of peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) cultivated in arid sandy environment
Authors: Lamy Mamdoh Mohamed Hamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo field experiments were conducted during the 2023 and 2024 summer seasons to evaluate the effects of commercial biostimulants on peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) productivity and soil health under arid sandy soil conditions along the Alexandria Desert Road, Egypt. A randomized complete block design with four treatments control, Humic King (humic acid), Biofertile (microbial inoculant; Azospirillum brasilense and Bacillus megaterium), and Kelpak (seaweed extract) was implemented with three replicates. Biostimulant applications enhanced several agronomic and quality parameters compared with the control. Total dry weight increased by up to 10.2%, while pod yield improved from 1.93 to 1.99 t ha⁻¹ in the control to a maximum of 3.41 t ha⁻¹ under humic acid treatment. Harvest index increased significantly from approximately 30% in untreated plants to 35.7% under humic acid application (p ≤ 0.05). Seed oil content reached 43.2% under humic acid compared with 41.4% in the control, whereas seaweed extract increased seed protein content by up to 14.4%. Post-harvest soil analyses indicated improvements in organic matter, available nitrogen, and reductions in salinity-related parameters, particularly under humic acid and microbial treatments. Overall, Humic King demonstrated the most consistent agronomic and soil benefits, supporting its recommendation for peanut cultivation in nutrient-deficient sandy soils under arid conditions.Keywords: Microbial inoculants, Sustainable agriculture, Quality, Soil fertility, Arachis hypogaea, GrowthSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) is a vital legume crop grown extensively in arid and semi-arid regions due to its economic importance and nutritional value as a source of oil and protein1. In Egypt, peanut cultivation has expanded into newly reclaimed and sandy soils, especially along desert roads and marginal lands, where soil fertility and water retention are inherently low and conventional cereal crops often exhibit poor performance2. However, these environments are typically characterized by low organic matter, poor water-holding capacity, and limited nutrient availability, which collectively constrain plant growth and yield2,3. Biostimulants are a diverse group of environmentally friendly substances and microorganisms that enhance plant growth and productivity through mechanisms independent of direct nutrient supply. They function primarily by improving nutrient use efficiency, stimulating physiological and metabolic processes, enhancing tolerance to abiotic stress, and modulating endogenous hormonal activity4,5. Biostimulants include non-microbial products such as humic substances and seaweed extracts, as well as microbial biostimulants, notably plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR).

Among non-microbial biostimulants, seaweed extracts have received considerable attention due to their richness in phytohormones, amino acids, vitamins, and trace elements. These compounds stimulate root development, enhance photosynthetic activity, and improve plant resilience under stress conditions6. In leguminous crops, seaweed-based biostimulants have been shown to promote biomass accumulation and improve seed quality through enhanced assimilate partitioning and metabolic regulation.

Humic substances, particularly humic and fulvic acids, represent another important category of biostimulants. They improve soil structure, increase cation exchange capacity, and enhance nutrient availability in sandy and low-organic-matter soils. Additionally, humic acids exert hormone-like effects that stimulate root proliferation and enzymatic activity, ultimately contributing to improved biomass production and yield performance7. Their role is especially relevant in arid soils where nutrient retention and root development are often limited.

Microbial biostimulants, including plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria such as Azospirillum brasilense and Bacillus megaterium, enhance plant performance through multiple indirect mechanisms. These include improved nutrient solubilization, phytohormone production, stimulation of root architecture, and enhanced stress tolerance5,8. In legumes, microbial biostimulants may also support nodulation efficiency and nitrogen metabolism, thereby improving both yield and seed quality traits.

Despite the documented benefits of individual biostimulant categories, comparative field-based studies evaluating commercial seaweed extracts, humic substances, and microbial biostimulants under arid sandy soil conditions remain limited, particularly for peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) cultivation in Egypt. Most available studies focus on single-product applications or controlled environments, with limited emphasis on integrated evaluation under nutrient-poor desert soils. Therefore, the present study was undertaken to evaluate the effectiveness of three commercially available biostimulant products seaweed extract, humic acid, and a microbial inoculant on peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) growth performance, yield components, seed quality attributes, and post-harvest soil properties under arid sandy soil conditions. The novelty of this work lies in its comparative field-based assessment of distinct biostimulant categories over two consecutive summer seasons (2023 and 2024) in nutrient-deficient desert soils. In contrast to previous studies conducted under more favorable agroecological conditions or focusing on single products or alternative crops, this research provides context-specific insights into the agronomic and soil responses of peanut to commercial biostimulant applications in arid environments. The findings aim to contribute practical, evidence-based recommendations for enhancing legume productivity and promoting sustainable agricultural practices in desert farming systems.

Experimental design and treatments

The experiments were arranged in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with four treatments and three replications. Each plot measured 3.5 × 5.0 m². Treatments included: T1 – Control (Plots received no biostimulant application. Plants were managed under the standard agronomic practices for peanut cultivation, including basal fertilization.); T2 – Humic Acid (Humic acid ‘Humic King’ was applied as a soil drench at a concentration of 5 g/L, applied at sowing and again at 40 days after sowing (DAS). The inoculant was prepared fresh under sterile conditions and applied directly to the seed and surrounding rhizosphere), and T4 – Seaweed Extract (A commercial seaweed extract ‘Kelpak’ was applied as a foliar spray at a concentration of 5 ml/L, administered twice: at 30 and 60 (DAS). Its rich in; natural plant hormones: Auxins (11 mg/L) and Cytokinins (0.03 mg/L), Amino acids, betaines, vitamins, and Carbohydrates and minerals (K, Ca, Fe, Zn, Mg). The peanut commercial cultivar used was Giza 6 (ARC-PN-G6), known for its adaptability to low-fertility sandy soils typical of Egyptian reclaimed desert lands and high productivity. This cultivar was released by the Oil Crops Department, Field Crops Institute, Agricultural Research Center, Egypt. Seeds were manually sown at 10 cm spacing between hills of 30 cm-spaced rows.

Data collection

At harvest, total dry weight of plant and yield traits such as pod yield, 100 seed weight, shelling %, and harvest index were recorded. Seed quality (protein and oil content) was analyzed using standard Kjeldahl and Soxhlet methods, respectively. Post-harvest’ soil samples were assessed for pH, organic matter, EC, available N and chemical properties.

Post-harvest soil analysis

After the final harvest, soil samples were collected from each treatment plot at a depth of 0–60 cm to assess changes in soil chemical properties. In addition to surface sampling (0–40 cm), subsoil samples were collected from the 40–60 cm depth to evaluate nutrient leaching and residual soil fertility. This depth was chosen based on the typical rooting depth of peanut plants and the necessity to assess nutrient movement and the lasting effects of soil amendments beyond the primary root zone, particularly in the context of highly permeable sandy soils.

Statistical analysis

Data were subjected to a factorial analysis of variance (ANOVA) using a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with two factors: treatment (four levels) and season (two levels). The statistical model included the main effects of treatment and season, their interaction (T x S), and block effects nested within season. When significant effects were detected, means were separated using the Least Significant Differences (L.S.D.) test at p ≤ 0.05. All assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were verified prior to analysis. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on three replicates (n = 3) per treatment. Where significant differences among treatments were observed, means were separated and annotated with distinct alphabetical letters to indicate statistical significance. The analytical approach followed the standard procedures described by Gomez and Gomez16 and Steel and Torrie17. Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to assess the interrelationships among key agronomic traits, including pod yield, protein content, oil content, shelling percentage, 100-seed weight, and harvest index. All statistical computations and data visualizations, including the generation of correlation heatmaps, were performed using Python 3.10, utilizing the STATS models and Pandas.

Results and discussion

The study investigated the comparative efficacy of different biostimulants on peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) productivity and seed quality over 2022 and 2023 growing season. The experimental site, characterized by sandy soil and semi-arid climate, provided a suitable context for assessing the influence of biostimulants nutrient-limited conditions. Treatments had significant impacts on the studied traits including total dry weight of plant, pod yield, shelling %, 100 seed weight, harvest index (HI), seed protein and oil content. These results are consistent with previous findings highlighting the role of biostimulants in enhancing physiological and agronomic performance under abiotic stress conditions4,18.

Total dry weight of plant

The application of biostimulants significantly influenced the total dry weight of peanut plants across both growing seasons. In both seasons, all biostimulant treatments (humic acid, microbial inoculants, and seaweed extract) resulted in higher dry weight accumulation compared to the control.

In the first season, the microbial inoculant treatment recorded the highest total dry weight (116.7 g), followed closely by seaweed extract (115.2 g) and humic acid (114.0 g), while the control treatment exhibited the lowest value (105.8 g). A similar trend was observed in the second season, where microbial inoculant led with the highest total dry matter (119.1 g), followed by humic acid (116.1 g) and seaweed extract (117.6 g), with the control producing the lowest dry weight (108.8 g) Fig. 1.

These increases in total biomass production, 10.2, 8.5 and 7,2% for Microbia inoculantsl, Seaweed and Humic acid treatments compared with control treatment, respectively, are attributed to the enhanced physiological and metabolic activities promoted by biostimulants. Microbial inoculants (e.g., Azospirillum brasilense and Bacillus megaterium) likely contributed to improved nitrogen availability and root growth, thereby supporting greater shoot biomass accumulation. Humic acid, with its chelating properties and hormone-like activity, improved nutrient uptake and soil structure. Seaweed extract, rich in natural growth hormones and trace elements, also positively influenced vegetative growth.

These results are consistent with previous findings. Humic substances are known to enhance plant growth by improving soil structure, increasing cation exchange capacity, and stimulating enzymatic and hormonal activity7,19. Seaweed extracts contain naturally occurring growth regulators such as auxins and cytokinins, which promote shoot and root development and contribute to higher biomass accumulation6,19. Microbial inoculants, particularly those containing Azospirillum and Bacillus species, facilitate nitrogen fixation and phosphorus solubilization, enhancing nutrient use efficiency and supporting vegetative growth20.

Pod yield

Pod yield was significantly improved by all biostimulants compared to the control. The microbial inoculant treatment recorded the highest yield (2.46 and 2.54 t/ha), followed by seaweed extract (3.35 and 2.45 t/ha), and humic acid (2.31 and 3.41 t/ha), while the control yielded only 1.93 and 1.99 t/ha in the first and second season, respectively (Fig. 2).

Effect of biostimulant on pod yield of peanut in 2023 and 2024 seasons, error bar for standard deviation (n = 3), lowercase letters indicate least significant difference (L.S.D) between treatment at 5%.

These findings corroborate with Canellas et al.7., who reported that humic substances stimulate root development and enhance nutrient acquisition, thereby improving biomass and yield under limited input conditions. The yield increase under microbial inoculation may be attributed to enhanced nitrogen fixation and phytohormone production by beneficial microbes such as Azospirillum and Bacillus species, which stimulate root proliferation and nutrient uptake. Also, Guanzon and Magtoto21 reported that applying Kappaphycus alvarezii extracted at 12.5 ml per plant led to a pod yield of 2.42 t/ha and seed yield of 1.71 t/ha, outperforming other treatments. Similarly, Bangi and Marajuli22 found that fermented seaweed extracted applied twice a week at a 5% concentration resulted in the highest peanut yield of 2.49 ton.ha− 1, with a return on investment of 179%, whereas, Nabillah et al.23. observed that while humic acid applied alone did not significantly affect yield parameters, its interaction with potassium fertilizer influenced the number of nonproductive gynophores and the weight of 100 seeds. The optimal combination was 75 kg/ ha of KCl with 30 kg/ha of humic acid, resulting in the best seed quality.

Shelling percentage and 100-seed weight

Shelling % increased by 7.0 and 6.5%, increased by 5.7 and 5.3% and 5.1 and 4.4% compared to control treatment for microbial inoculant, seaweed extract and humic acid treatments, in the 1st and 2nd season, respectively (Fig. 3). However, factorial ANOVA demonstrated that shelling percentage was not significantly influenced by treatment (p > 0.05), while season exerted a significant effect. This indicates that environmental variability between seasons had a greater impact on shelling performance than the applied biostimulants.

Harvest index (HI)

The HI of peanut was significantly influenced by the application of different biostimulants during both growing seasons (Fig. 5).

Across both seasons, the control treatment recorded the lowest HI values, with 30.15 and 30.61% in first and second, respectively. All biostimulant treatments significantly increased the HI compared to the control.

Among treatments, humic acid resulted in the highest HI (35.69 in 1st season and 35.32% in 2nd season), followed closely by microbial inoculants (35.11 and 35.03%) and seaweed extract (33.99 and 33.97%). These results indicate that biostimulants, especially humic acid is particularly effective in improving assimilate partitioning toward the economic yield (pods) in peanut, thereby enhancing the harvest index. This aligns with findings by Canellas et al.7, who reported that humic substances stimulate nutrient uptake, root proliferation, and enzymatic activity, all of which are conducive to increased biomass allocation to reproductive structures.

Similarly, the use of microbial inoculants, particularly plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR), may enhance carbon and nitrogen assimilation efficiency. According to Vessey8, which influence hormonal balance and root function, improving photosynthate allocation to seeds and thus the HI. In spite of, seaweed extracts, rich in natural plant hormones (auxins, cytokinins, and gibberellins), also improved HI over the control. These extracts are known to enhance photosynthesis and pod filling in legumes24 though their effect in this study was slightly lower than that of humic acid and microbial inoculants.

Application of seaweed extract treatment on peanut plants significantly increased protein content by (13.7 and 14.4%), application of microbial inoculant increased it by 8.7 and 8.5%, as well as humic acid increased seed protein content by 6.0 and 5.8% compared to control treatment in the first and second season, respectively (Fig. 6).

Higher protein levels in biostimulant treatments are attributable to better nitrogen assimilation and symbiotic microbial activity, particularly in legumes like peanut. Rouphael and Colla20 demonstrated that microbial biostimulants enhance nitrogen use efficiency, which directly contributes to improved seed protein synthesis.

Seed oil content

Oil content, a major economic trait, also increased significantly. Humic acid application resulted in the highest oil content (43.20%), followed by microbial inoculant (42.71%) and seaweed extract (42.01%) as average of the two growing seasons, whereas control plots had the lowest content (41.40%) (Fig. 7).

This increase may result from enhancing photosynthesis and lipid biosynthesis7. This metabolic stimulation is critical for oil synthesis in developing seed. Nabillah et al.23 reported significance seed quality in peanut with humic acid with potassium fertilizer. Other literature suggests humic acid enhances carbohydrate portioning toward lipid synthesis during seed filling25. Although, microbial inoculation enhances biological nitrogen fixation in legumes, leading to better protein and oil accumulation8. Also, Craigie6 reported that seaweed enhanced lipid metabolism, photosynthetic activity, and enzyme function associated with oil biosynthesis.

The standard deviation (SD) of most traits was lower in treated plots compared to the control, indicating consistent performance of biostimulants across replicates.

Trait interrelationships and advanced statistical insights

The strong positive correlations observed between pod yield, harvest index, and seed quality traits are consistent with previous findings in legumes cultivated under stress-prone environments (Fig. 8). These relationships suggest that improved source-to-sink dynamics particularly under enhanced soil fertility conditions play a central role in yield formation. Similar trends were reported by Rouphael and Colla20, who highlighted the ability of biostimulants to modulate plant metabolism and enhance assimilate partitioning toward reproductive structures. The positive association between pod yield and protein content (r = 0.81) indicates that enhanced nitrogen assimilation, likely driven by improved soil conditions and microbial activity, contributes simultaneously to higher protein accumulation and yield performance8. Conversely, the moderate negative correlation between oil and protein content (r = − 0.58) reflects a well-documented trade-off in legume seed composition, wherein metabolic allocation often favors either lipid or protein synthesis depending on genotype and environmental context6,26. These findings highlight the role of integrated soil amendments not only in boosting yield but also in influencing the balance of seed quality traits under arid, nutrient-deficient conditions.

These findings indicate that biostimulant efficacy was not only treatment-dependent but also influenced by environmental conditions across the two growing seasons. Conversely, shelling percentage and protein content exhibited weaker interaction effects, suggesting greater stability across environmental variation. Electrical conductivity and pH

Electrical conductivity (EC) exhibited a general decreasing trend with increasing soil depth across all treatments, consistent with the leaching-driven salt redistribution typical of sandy soils. The humic acid treatment consistently recorded the lowest EC values at each depth, indicating its potential role in promoting salt displacement and minimizing ion accumulation in the rhizosphere. These observed trends align with findings reported by Mostafa et al.27., who demonstrated that humic acid combined with nano-fertilizers under drip irrigation significantly reduced total dissolved solids in calcareous sandy soils. Similarly, Canellas et al.7. emphasized the contribution of humic substances to enhanced cation exchange capacity and ion mobility, improving the soil’s ability to buffer and redistribute salts.

Recent research further supports the role of biostimulants in salinity regulation. Lakhdar et al.28. reported that biostimulants, particularly those based on humic and fulvic acids, effectively reduced EC in saline soils while improving aggregate stability. Mehmood et al.29. noted that microbial inoculants enhanced ion exchange dynamics and salt mobility through enzymatic activity and organic acid secretion, facilitating greater salt leaching and electrochemical balance in root zones.

Soil pH also declined modestly with depth across treatments, reflecting the natural acidification gradient and biological interactions at the rhizosphere level. Notably, the microbial inoculant treatment resulted in the lowest pH values ranging from 8.23 in the surface layer to 8.01 in the subsoil (40–60 cm) indicating enhanced microbial respiration and production of organic acids. This trend aligns with findings by Rouphael and Colla5, who described microbial inoculants as effective agents in inducing mild rhizosphere acidification, thus increasing nutrient solubility and bioavailability.

Additional evidence from Singh et al.30. highlights the role of microbial and humic biostimulants in pH modulation through proton exudation and bicarbonate neutralization processes. These biochemical interactions are critical in regulating nutrient uptake under arid and alkaline conditions typical of sandy soils.

Although the absolute differences in EC and pH across treatments were relatively small, the trends observed here align well with the current scientific consensus that biostimulants, particularly, humic substances and microbial inoculants can contribute to improved soil ionic balance, buffering capacity, and nutrient accessibility under saline and alkaline conditions.

Organic matter and available nitrogen

Biostimulant applications, particularly humic acid and seaweed extract, contributed to modest but consistent increases in organic matter (OM) content in the upper soil layers. This improvement is attributed to the carbon-rich nature of these biostimulants and their stimulation of microbial activity, which facilitates the decomposition of organic residues and enhances carbon stabilization. Recent studies have confirmed that humic substances can improve soil microbial biomass and enzymatic activities, thereby increasing organic carbon sequestration and fertility in degraded or sandy soils31,32.

In parallel, microbial inoculants led to the highest levels of available nitrogen (N), especially in the topsoil (0–20 cm). This enhancement is likely driven by the activity of nitrogen-fixing and mineralizing bacteria such as Azospirillum and Bacillus spp., which are commonly used in microbial biostimulant formulations. These microbes enhance nitrogen availability through biological nitrogen fixation and the activation of nitrogen-cycling enzymes33,34. These trends are consistent with Soliman and Hamed19, who found that biostimulant treatments, particularly, humic acid and seaweed extract significantly improved nitrogen uptake in sandy soils, achieving levels comparable to those seen under mineral fertilization.

Collectively, these findings support the role of biostimulants as bio-enhancers that not only provide organic substrates but also activate key microbial pathways involved in nutrient cycling. The combined use of carbon-based biostimulants and microbial inoculants represents a promising strategy for improving nitrogen efficiency and organic matter restoration in low-fertility sandy soils.

Exchangeable ions and salinity management

The application of biostimulants contributed to moderate improvements in soil salinity parameters, particularly through slight reductions in the sodium adsorption ratio (SAR) and exchangeable sodium (Na⁺) levels compared to the untreated control. These effects were most apparent in the humic acid and microbial inoculant treatments, suggesting their potential role in mitigating sodium-induced soil dispersion and enhancing soil structure. Although these changes were not statistically significant, they align with previously reported mechanisms where humic substances enhance cation exchange capacity and foster the displacement of sodium ions by calcium and magnesium35,36.

Moreover, treated soils showed more balanced levels of divalent cations such as calcium (Ca²⁺) and magnesium (Mg²⁺), contributing to improved soil aggregation and permeability critical attributes in sandy soil environments28. These shifts are believed to be facilitated by the action of humic and fulvic acids and microbial biosurfactants, which can chelate ions and enhance ion mobility in the rhizosphere29,37.

Anion concentrations, particularly chloride (Cl⁻), sulfate (SO₄²⁻), and bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), were slightly reduced in all treated soils, with humic acid and seaweed extract showing the most consistent downward trends. These reductions may reflect improved nutrient uptake and water movement through the soil profile, consistent with studies highlighting the osmotic regulation and nutrient buffering effects of seaweed-based and organic biostimulants36,38.

Taken together, these findings reinforce the utility of biostimulants not only in crop enhancement but also in supporting soil remediation processes under saline and nutrient-poor conditions.

Conclusion

The evaluated commercial biostimulants Humic King (humic acid), Biofertile (microbial inoculant), and Kelpak (seaweed extract) positively influenced peanut performance under arid sandy soil conditions. Although yield differences were not statistically significant, consistent improvements were observed across key agronomic and soil parameters.

Among the tested products, Humic King demonstrated the most consistent overall performance, significantly enhancing harvest index and seed oil content while contributing to improved soil quality indicators. Biofertile improved biomass and pod yield, whereas Kelpak increased seed protein content.

Based on these findings, Humic King is recommended as the most suitable commercial biostimulant for peanut cultivation in sandy arid environments, with other products applicable depending on specific production goals.
