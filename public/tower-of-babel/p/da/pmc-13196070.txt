RSV bronchiolitis versus COVID-19: could pediatric intensive care units have done more during the pandemic?
Authors: Gregory Hansen, Tanya Holt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COronaVirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic disproportionately overwhelmed adult critical care resources. It also led to a reduction of pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admissions due to the significant attenuation of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) epidemics. We compared RSV bronchiolitis with pediatric COVID-19 admissions in the context of accepting adult ICU patients to our PICU during the pandemic.MethodsThis retrospective study compared pediatric patients admitted to the PICU with bronchiolitis and a COVID-19 infection between November 2019 to March 2023 inclusive. These two cohorts were considered with allcomers and adult patients admitted to the PICU between June 2020 and December 2021.ResultsOne hundred and twenty-six patients with RSV bronchiolitis were compared with 35 pediatric patients with COVID-19. Consequently, our vacant PICU beds were used to support adult ICU surges while maintaining below baseline admission rates. Background

The recent COronaVirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic overwhelmed health care systems across the world due to massive patient surges and demands on critical care resources. Coordinated responses spanning from global cooperations to regionalized public policies were necessary to ameliorate its devasting consequences. During its height and in its aftermath, several recommendations were proposed to guide intensive care units through future respiratory pandemics [1–5]. Although these reports addressed expanding critical care capacity, the potential facilitating role of pediatric intensive care units (PICU) was not explicitly expressed.

In general, the risk of severe disease in children and adolescents from COVID-19 was low [6, 7], with a reduction of PICU admissions across successive waves [8]. In the United States, there was also a significant reduction of total PICU admissions, mainly due to drastic decreases in respiratory conditions such as bronchiolitis [9], and more specifically respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) bronchiolitis. Similar trends were also observed throughout the Northern and Southern hemispheres [10].

The divergence of criticality between adults and pediatrics allowed for new pathways to be considered. Between June 2020 and December 2021 for example, our PICU admitted and managed 65 adult patients for a total of 437 days [11]. With admission diagnoses ranging from polytrauma to COVID-19 pneumonia and sepsis, 64.6% required conventional mechanical ventilation, and our mortality rate was 4.6%. Similar responses with favourable outcomes were also reported from American [12, 13] and European PICUs [14, 15].

For future respiratory pandemic considerations, we compared RSV bronchiolitis with pediatric COVID-19 admissions in the context of admitting adult ICU patients to our PICU.

Materials and methods

The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. The University of Saskatchewan’s Research Ethics Board, an institutional review board (IRB), approved the minimal-risk study (HREB #328) and waived informed consent. Consent was not obtained from parents, caregivers or legal guardians because data was de-identified, presented as aggregate, and retrospective in nature.

Pediatric and adult patients were identified through the PICU’s admission registry. Inclusion criteria for pediatric patients with bronchiolitis included: i) < 2 years of age with a positive viral culture; ii) admitted during four RSV endemic seasons (between November and end of March only, between November 2019 to March 2023 inclusive) shouldering Saskatchewan’s COVID-19 pandemic; iii) who failed high flow nasal cannula (HFNC) on the wards; or, iv) required either non-invasive (NIV) or conventional mechanical ventilation (CMV). Patients admitted to the PICU with bronchiolitis outside the RSV endemic season were not included. Inclusion criteria for pediatric patients with a COVID-19 infection included: (i) positive viral culture; (ii) infection as the causative diagnosis and not an incidental finding; and ii) requiring ventilatory or organ support. As published elsewhere [11], our inclusion criteria for adult patients included: > 17 years of age between June 2020 and December 2021.

Saskatchewan Context

On March 18th, 2020, a State of Emergency was declared in Saskatchewan. Provincial pediatric and adult resource allocation frameworks were rapidly created [16, 17], that protected pediatric access to critical care while eventually allowing adults to receive care in Saskatchewan’s only PICU. At first, critically ill maternal and adult patients < 40 years of age with single-system diseases were eligible, but these criteria were rapidly expanded. On February 28th, 2022, the provincial indoor mask mandate was lifted and proof of vaccination was no longer necessary, ending public health policy measures.

Variables

A pediatric intensivist (T.H.) conducted all the electronic chart reviews. Data was stored on a password protected Excel file in a secure cloud. Variables for pediatric patients included age, sex, presence of comorbidities, pediatric logistic organ dysfunction (PELOD- 2) score at admission, admission C-reactive protein (CRP), maximum ventilatory support, adjuvant supports (i.e. continuous renal replacement therapy), mortality and length of stay. The adult COVID admission data has been reported elsewhere [11].

Statistics

All statistical analyses were done using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 24. Descriptive statistics were summarized by either proportions or means with standard deviations. Differences between the pediatric patients infected with RSV or COVID-19 were calculated using difference in means and their 95% confidence interval, and two-tailed Fisher’s exact test. Differences between baseline, RSV and adult admission months were calculated using difference in means. Age, comorbidities, CRP and PELOD-2 scores were used as predictors for CMV in multiple regression analyses for patients with RSV and COVID-19. Significance level was considered at p = 0.05.

One hundred and twenty-six patients with RSV bronchiolitis were identified from our PICU registry. All electronic charts were available for review and were included in this study. Another fifty-three patients with non-RSV bronchiolitis were also identified but were not included in data analyses. Their admissions are summarized in Table 1. There were no admissions for RSV bronchiolitis during 2020-21 RSV endemic season. Thirty-five pediatric patients with COVID-19 were also identified, and all electronic charts were available. Discussion

Years of predictable RSV epidemics have allowed high performing healthcare systems to develop operational processes and resources to accommodate significant surges of respiratory illnesses. The two RSV seasons (2019-20 and 2022-23) in Saskatchewan shouldering the COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated expected epidemic patterns, with high admission peaks, substantial respiratory support requirements, low criticality and no mortality. However, consistent with others [9, 10], normal patterns of bronchiolitis were not seen during two winters of the pandemic This led to unexpected reductions of PICU admissions below baseline.

We also experienced fewer pediatric admissions for COVID-19 infections (n = 36) during the entire pandemic than the two shoulder RSV epidemics (n = 54 and 59). Consistent with reports elsewhere, patients with COVID-19 infection were younger with subsequent years and waves (mean 10.42, 4.42, 0.80 years) and had higher criticality. This translated to 25% of patients requiring CMV and an 8% mortality rate, compared to only 8% and 0% in RSV, respectively.

During the middle of the pandemic, adult ICUs in Saskatchewan were stretched to their limits while PICU beds remained vacant. To remedy, after different models of care and staff deployment were considered, it was decided to admit critically ill adults to a closed PICU [11]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, adult admissions typically occurred when our unit was below average admission volume or during a muted RSV epidemic season, leading to the question whether we could have admitted adults earlier in the pandemic? Retrospectively, our patient flow would have allowed for an earlier response. However, ensuring protection of pediatric resources and optimization of adult clinical care required jurisdictional approval, re-licensing for pediatric intensivists and the creation of admission algorithms, clinical backup support from ICU colleagues and quality assurance frameworks. This all took time and was not developed a priori.

The lessons we learned from RSV endemics and COVID-19 may have the potential to guide planning for future respiratory pandemics. First, early pandemic surveillance, its impact on children, and associations with RSV endemics may suggest infection susceptibility and need for PICU resources. If pandemics disproportionately affect adults and disrupt seasonal pediatric respiratory patterns as reported during COVID-19 [6–9], vacant PICU beds could be an asset to facilitate ICU care. Of course, the opposite scenario of increased pediatric susceptibility is also possible. Second, decisions to utilize vacant PICU beds for adult surges and addressing its ramifications could be done now, within a framework to both protect pediatric critical care resources and optimize adult clinical outcomes.

Our retrospective study has several limitations. Intensive care delivery is very nuanced with significant variability in high income nations. As such, our COVID-19 experience that will guide future decisions may not be generalizable to others. Second, future respiratory pandemics may introduce very different complexities and patterns that could render our “lessons learned” as less relevant. However, as Benjamin Franklin once quipped “By failing to prepare, you are preparing to fail,” we must continue to learn from the COVID-19 pandemic. Third, our PICU admission criterion for pediatric patients with RSV bronchiolitis and COVID-19 may not be generalizable to other centers. RSV criticality is positively skewed in our PICU, as infants with bronchiolitis are only admitted if they have failed HFNC on the ward, or require CPAP, NIV or CMV.

Conclusions

While the COVID-19 pandemic placed a disproportionate burden on adult ICU resources, it also muted the RSV epidemic for two winters. Within a very deliberate framework, our vacant PICUs beds were used to support adult surges without challenging our baseline admission rates. This suggests that our PICU could have had the capacity to absorb more ICU patients earlier in the pandemic.
