Physico-chemical properties of natural actomyosin from breast and thigh meat of fast- and slow-growing chicken: a comparative study
Authors: Ali Hamzeh, Lobdaw Saelee, Jirawat Yongsawatdigul
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysico-chemical behaviors of natural actomyosin (NAM) from slow-growing Korat chicken (KC) breast and thigh at low (0.2 M) and high (0.6 M) NaCl concentrations were evaluated and compared to those from their corresponding muscles in fast-growing broiler chicken (BC). NAM from KC breast and thigh meat showed higher solubility than their corresponding in BC (p < 0.05). Breast NAM from both chickens showed the highest solubility at 0.4 M NaCl, while thigh NAMs showed the highest solubility at 0.8 M (p < 0.05). SDS-PAGE revealed higher protein extractability for breast NAMs at low ionic strength, regardless of breed and structural protein, particularly troponin, appeared to vary within muscle and breed. NAM from KC showed higher Ca2+–ATPase activity, surface hydrophobicity, but lower total sulfhydryl groups content (p < 0.05). Particle size of NAM solutions varied with ionic strength, in which KC-NAM showed larger size than at lower ionic strength, while BC-NAM appeared to be greater at higher ionic strength. NAM from KC breast showed higher thermal stability as higher initial (T0) and maximum (Tmax) denaturation temperatures of 48.4 and 54.8°C, respectively, recorded by microdifferential scanning calorimetry. The KC-NAM, particularly from breast, exhibited lower turbidity within 40-50°C, while turbidity increased in BC samples at low ionic strength when heated at 60°C. Dynamic rheology revealed different storage modulus (G′) depending on breed, muscle type and ionic strength. Breast BC-NAM formed more elastic gel with higher end point G′ at 80°C (Ge′; p < 0.05). Ionic strength showed reverse effects on different breeds as a stronger Ge′ value achieved in KC- NAM at low ionic strength, while high ionic strength induced stronger gel in BC samples. Aggregation of NAMs began at lower temperatures at higher ionic strength and actin dissociation probably occurred in breast NAM from KC as observed by a drop of G′ at around 70°C. The results of this study revealed differences between NAM of the two breeds, by which higher gel elasticity are expected in KC at lower ionic strength.Key words: Korat chicken, natural actomyosin, slow-growing chicken, rheology, broiler

INTRODUCTION

Poultry with a forecasted production of 146 million metric tons is the leading source of global meat production and broiler with the projected production of 104 million metric tons is the main breed (FAO, 2024; Statista, 2024). This is because of the fast-growing feature of the commercial broiler chickens (BC) with high feed efficacy along with high yield (Pellattiero et al., 2020). However, some concerns have been recently raised up regarding to animal welfare. Nielsen et al. (2023) listed 19 welfare issues in broiler rearing including bone lesion, moving laziness, umbilical disorders among others. Development of indigenous and/or slow-growing breeds can be a means to overcome the issues. Slow-growing indigenous breeds have been reported to have good welfare indices (Meuser et al., 2021). Although longer rearing period of indigenous breeds can be an economical obstacle, recent studies in various regions, such as EU, US, and Brazil revealed consumers’ willingness to consider animal welfare standards even with relatively higher price than commercial broiler (Spain et al., 2018; Pinto da Rosa et al., 2021; Gorton et al., 2023).

Korat chicken (KC) is a slow-growing hybrid breed obtained by Thai indigenous male and Suranaree University of Technology (SUT 101) female (a broiler and layer chicken hybrid) chickens with unique features of lower fat and firmer texture with more chewiness compared to BC (Kubota et al., 2021; Suwanvichanee et al., 2022). The KC can be an alternative breed to develop chicken meat with animal welfare considerations. Katemala et al. (2021) revealed distinct meat quality of lower cholesterol and higher umami compound of inosine monophosphate as well as higher shear force compared to BC. However, techno-functional properties of KC muscle protein have not been systematically investigated.

Natural actomyosin (NAM) is the main part of muscle proteins contributing to techno-functional properties which are critical for meat quality and processing (Zou et al., 2018). However, protein techno-functionalities have been shown to vary with species, breeds, and muscle type. Comparative studies on muscle proteins of fish, pork, beef and chicken revealed different characteristics (Liu et al., 2011; Dara et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022). In addition, white and dark muscles in fish, longissimus thoracis and semitendinosus in cattle as well as breast and thigh in chicken exhibited different techno-functional properties (Chaijan et al., 2004; Glorieux et al., 2017; Feng et al., 2020). These variations are attributed to different isomers of actomyosin with different quantities. Chinese, Thai and Korean slow-growing native chickens as well as black-boned chickens contained higher contents of myosin and actin than commercial broiler with less susceptibility to thermal denaturation (Wattanachant et al., 2005; Lengkidworraphiphat et al., 2020). A lower myofiber diameter was observed in pectoralis muscle of Chinese native chicken (Beijing fatty chickens) and Chinese crossbred chicken (Jingxing 100) compared to BC, resulting in a lower shear force (Chen et al., 2007). In addition, Liu et al. (2012) found 16 proteins with different expressions in breast (7 proteins) and thigh (9 proteins) of Korean native chicken compared to broiler. However, these studies mostly focused on muscles quality comparison, while techno-functionalities of NAM extracted from fast- and slow-growing chickens have not been reported.

Therefore, this study was aimed to assess physiochemical behaviors and dynamic rheological properties of KC-NAM and compared to those of BC at both low and high ionic strength. The results would help to understand fundamental differences of NAM extracted from slow- and fast-growing chickens, which would explain differences in techno-functional properties of muscle proteins of between these 2 breeds.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

BC and KC at 7 and 10 wk old, respectively, were obtained from a commercial slaughterhouse (Ban-Had District, Khon Kaen, Thailand) that followed the standards of the Department of Livestock Development of Thailand. Whole eviscerated carcasses were packed filled with ice and transported to Suranaree University of Technology within 24 h postmortem. Upon arrival, samples were thoroughly washed and kept at 4°C overnight. Breast and thigh meat of 5 chickens from each breed were manually dissected for NAM extraction. Trichloroacetic Acid (TCA), Bovine serum albumin (BSA), 1-anilino-8-naphthalene-sulfonate (ANS), Tris-maleate and adenosine 5′-triphosphate disodium salt hydrate (ATP) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Other chemicals were of analytical grade.

NAM Extraction

NAM extraction was carried out according to Tang and Yongsawatdigul (2020). Fifty grams of minced breast or thigh meat were added to 250 mL of low ionic strength buffer (50 mM NaCl in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7) and homogenized at 10,000 × g for 2 min with 1-min intermittent. The mixture was centrifuged at 5000×g (CR 22 GIII, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at 4°C for 10 min. The precipitate was collected and homogenized with 250 mL of low ionic strength buffer and centrifuged at the mentioned condition. The precipitated residue was collected and added with 500 mL of a high ionic strength buffer (0.6 M NaCl in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7). The mixture was then homogenized for 2 min, and centrifuged at 10,000 × g at 4°C for 10 min. The supernatant of salt soluble proteins was collected and diluted with 3 volumes of cold distilled water (4°C). The mixture was stirred for 1 h and centrifuged at 10,000 × g at 4°C for 10 min. The white precipitate was collected and centrifuged at 12,500 × g for 15 min for water removal. The final precipitate pellet was referred to as NAM which was kept in ice and used within 3 to 5 d.

Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate-Polyacrylamide Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE)

Protein patterns of NAM were determined according to the method described by Yongsawatdigul and Sinsuwan (2018). Three grams of NAM was added 27 mL of hot 10% SDS solution. The mixed sample was homogenized for 1 min at 10,000 rpm and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was subjected to protein content measurements using Lowry method (Lowry et al., 1951) prior to addition of treatment buffer (10% SDS, 10% β-ME, 20% glycerol, 0.1% bromophenol blue, pH 6.8) at a ratio of 1:1 and heated at 90°C for 3 min. Soluble proteins (20 µg) were loaded into an acrylamide gel and run at 80 V. Thereafter, gel was stained with staining solution (50% Coomassie brilliant blue R-250, 50% methanol, and 10% acetic acid) for 15 min and de-stained in 25% ethanol and 10% acetic acid. To assess protein pattern of different NAMs at different ionic strength values, crude NAMs were solubilized at a ratio of 1:9 (w/v) in low and high ionic strength solutions of 0.2 and 0.6 M NaCl in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), respectively, prior to homogenization at 10,000 rpm for 1 min. The supernatants were then subjected to SDS-PAGE analysis as described above.

Solubility

One gram of NAM was dissolved in 19 ml of a series of NaCl concentrations of 0.0 - 0.8 M NaCl in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and shaken for 60 min at room temperature followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g at 4°C for 10 min. Protein content of supernatant was measured by Lowry method (Lowry et al., 1951) using BSA as a standard.

Ca2+-ATPase Activity

Ca2+-ATPase activity was determined according to Benjakul et al. (1997) with slight modifications. Three grams of NAM was added with 27 mL of either 0.2 or 0.6 M NaCl (in 50 mM Tris-maleate, pH 7) and homogenized for 1 min prior to centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was analyzed for protein content and adjusted to 4 mg/mL using Lowry method (Lowry et al., 1951). The diluted sample (500 µL) was added with 250 µL of 0.6 M Tris-maleate (pH 7.0), 250 µL of 0.1 M CaCl2, and 3.75 mL of deionized water and incubated at 25°C for 5 min. Subsequently, 250 µL of 20 mM ATP was added and incubated at 25°C for 8 min. The reaction was terminated using 15% (w/v) TCA and centrifuged at 3,500 × g for 5 min. The amount of released phosphate (Pi) was determined by measuring the absorbance of sample using UV/Vis spectrophotometer (GENESYS 10S, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) at 640 nm and KH2PO4 was used to be the standard solution. Ca2+ -ATPase activity of samples was expressed as µmol Pi/mg protein/min at 25°C.

Surface Hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity analysis of NAM was adapted from Tang and Yongsawatdigul (2020). NAM was dissolved in either 0.2 or 0.6 M NaCl in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.0 in a ratio of 1:9 and centrifuged at10,000 × g at 4°C for 10 min. Supernatants were diluted with respective buffers to obtain a series of protein concentrations of 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, and 1 mg/mL. Twenty µL of 8 mM ANS in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.0 was added to 4 mL of the protein solution and kept in the dark for 10 min at room temperature. Fluorescence intensity of samples was measured using a spectrofluorometer (FP-8300, JASCO, Easton, MD) at excitation and emission wavelengths of 374 and 485 nm, respectively. Surface hydrophobicity of the sample was calculated from the slope of the relative fluorescence (R) and protein concentrations (%w/v).

Where R is relative fluorescence, F is the fluorescence intensity of the sample with ANS and F0 is the fluorescence intensity of the buffer with ANS (without sample).

Total Sulfhydryl Group Content

Total sulfhydryl group content was adapted from Tang and Yongsawatdigul (2020). NAM was solubilized in 0.2 or 0.6 M sodium chloride in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and centrifuged at 10,000 × g at 4°C for 10 min. Supernatants were diluted with respective buffers to attain concentration of 4 mg/mL. Samples (500 µL) were added 4.5 mL of solubilizing buffer (8 M urea and 10 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, pH 7.0). Ellman's reagent (0.1% DTNB in 50 mM NaH2PO4 buffer, pH 7.0) of 200 µL was added to 2 mL of the mixture, and incubated at 40°C for 25 min. Subsequently, absorbance was measured at 412 nm. Total sulfhydryl group contents were calculated and expressed as mol/105 g protein using the molar extinction coefficient of 13,600 M−1 cm−1.

Turbidity

Turbidity of NAM samples at low and high ionic strength was determined according to Yongsawatdigul and Park, (1999), with slight modifications. Briefly, NAM contained in either 0.2 or 0.6 M NaCl, 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) was homogenized and centrifuged at 10,000 × g at 4°C for 10 min. The supernatant was diluted to 0.2 mg/mL with respective buffers. Three mL of diluted samples was added in a glass tube and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance of samples was measured at 320 nm at 5-min intervals during the course of incubation.

Particle Size

Particle size of NAM solution was measured as described by Tang and Yongsawatdigul (2020). NAM samples were suspended in either 0.2 or 0.6 M NaCl and centrifuged at 10,000×g at 4°C for 10 min. Supernatants were collected and adjusted to a concentration of 0.2 mg/mL, and subsequently incubated at 40, 50, and 60°C for 30 min. Samples before and after incubation at each studied temperature were measured for particle size using Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Worcestershire, UK) with a refractive index (RI) of 1.45.

Micro-Differential Scanning Calorimetry (µ-DSC)

Thermal denaturation behaviors of NAM extracted from breast and thigh meat of both chicken breeds were analyzed using a Micro-DSC7 evo (Setaram Co., Lyon, France) according to Tadpitchayangkoon et al. (2010). Naphthalene solution was used for temperature calibration, while 0.2 and 0.6 M NaCl in 20 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7 were used as reference solutions. NAM pellets were homogenized with 0.8 and 2.4 M NaCl buffers at a ratio 3:1 to attain final ionic strength of 0.2 and 0.6 M NaCl, respectively. Protein contents of homogenates prepared from KC and BC meat samples were 46.5 and 30-44.3 mg/g, respectively. Sample of 600 mg was added to the crucible and heated at 20 to 80°C using a heating rate of 1°C /min. The onset temperature (T0, ° C) and maximum temperature of denaturation (Tmax, ° C) as well as enthalpy of denaturation (J/g protein) of NAM samples were recorded.

Dynamic Rheological Measurement

Dynamic rheological properties of NAM samples was evaluated according to Li et al. (2022b). Samples were prepared as described in µ-DSC analysis. NAM paste (45 mg/g) was placed between a parallel plate with a gap of 1 mm and a diameter of 35 mm equipped with a Rheometer (HAAKE MARS 40, Thermo Scientific, Karlsruhe, Germany). The sample was heated from 20 to 80°C at 1°C /min. The applied stress and frequency were 10 Pa and 1 Hz, respectively, which were determined to be in a linear viscoelastic range of NAM samples. Storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were recorded.

Statistical Analysis

Five chickens from each breed were used for NAM extractions and each experiment was performed in triplicate. Two independent lots of chickens were used. A 2 × 2 × 2 full factorial design using general linear model (GLM) of statistical package for the social sciences software (SPSS, version 16, Chicago, IL) was applied to assess the 3 main effects, namely ionic strength (0.2 and 0.6 M NaCl), types of breed (broiler and Korat chickens) and muscle (breast and thigh) on each parameter. Mean comparison was performed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) using Duncan's multiple-range test at the significant level of p < 0.05.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Protein Patterns of NAMs

The main protein bands observed in all NAM samples included myosin heavy chain (MHC), actin, tropomyosin and myosin light chains (Figure 1A). However, troponins appeared to vary with muscle and breed types. Troponin-T was present in thigh NAMs, while it was absent or in very low intensity in NAM extracted from breast, irrespective to breed type. Troponin-T was also reported in thigh and leg of chicken, duck, and turkey (Foegeding, 1987; Ndi and Brekke, 1992; Xiong and Blanchard, 1994). Liu et al. (2012) also reported a higher expression of troponin-T in thigh of Korean native chicken when compared to commercial broilers. Regarding the breed differences, troponin-I was distinctive in KC breast and thigh. A higher expression of genes encoding troponin-I was also observed in Aseel slow-growing chicken compared to commercial broiler (Kanakachari et al., 2022). Troponin-I is one of subunits, which inhibits actomyosin ATPase, required for muscle relaxation at a decreased cytosolic Ca2+ content (Rasmussen and Jin 2021). Slow-growing chickens, particularly KC, express more activities in walking and running. Thus, distinct expression of troponin-I in KC thigh muscle would explain its role in regulating more active muscle contraction. Besides the variations within thin filament, C-protein which is part of myosin integrity is also varied. Protein components of NAM extracted from KC have not been evaluated so far. The results indicated differences between Korat and commercial broiler chickens which could lead to variations in denaturation behaviors and physico-chemical properties of muscle proteins, and ultimately techno-functional properties.

NAM Solubility

Solubility of breast NAM extracted from both breeds increased as ionic strength increased up to 0.4 M and decreased thereafter up to 0.8 M (Figure 2). Solubility of thigh NAM from both breeds continually increased up to 0.8 M. Generally, NAMs from KC breast and thigh showed higher solubility than their corresponding NAMs from BC (p < 0.05). Higher solubility of breast myofibrillar protein has been previously observed in white Leghorn hens and broiler chicken (Xiong and Brekke, 1991; Van Laack and Lane, 2000; Nahar et al., 2014), which can be due to Z-line disruption in breast proteins after rigor, whereas it does not happen in thigh (Xiong and Brekke, 1991). The presence of troponin-T in thigh NAMs (Figure 1) indicated the integrity of Z-line which contributed to lower extractability and solubility. A lower intensity of such protein band in breast NAMs could reflect the weakening of Z-line, leading to higher solubility (Ma et al., 2023). Van Laack and Lane (2000) observed a higher solubility in broiler (Arbor Acre) breast myofibrillar protein compared to Semimembranosus and Psoas major of porcine and suggested that the premier solubility of broiler breast myofibrillar protein was correlated to the high proportion of white muscle in chicken breast, while a mixed type fiber presented in pig muscles. Chicken thigh showed the least solubility in their study. A higher solubility of chicken white muscle at lower ionic strength compared to thigh was also reported by Keever (2011), in which the index was also higher than beef and pork. Stanley et al. (1994) found a positive correlation between protein solubility and the content of muscle type IIB (white muscle) in chicken and beef. Xiong et al. (2000) also reported an increased solubility of myofibrillar protein from gastrocnemius muscle of broilers with increasing ionic strength up to 0.1 M. Higher solubility of NAMs from KC compared to BC can also be due to the intrinsic property of different isomers in 2 breeds. Phongpa-Ngan et al. (2011) observed a higher solubility in breast myofibrillar protein of slow-growing broiler compared to fast-growing one, and indicated a higher expression of myosin heavy chain in slow-growing chicken breast based on proteomics analysis, whereas actin showed higher expression in fast-growing chicken breast. In this study, a lower intensity of the band corresponding to C-protein was observed in KC breast (Figure 1). This can reflect its higher extractability and solubility compared to other NAMs in this study. To evaluate the effect of ionic strength on NAMs solubility, electrophoretic analysis of soluble proteins in 0.2 and 0.6 M NaCl was carried out. NAMs extracted from breast meat of 2 breeds showed similar intensity and profiles to that of whole NAM and much higher than those of thigh at low ionic strength (Figure 1B). In contrast, high intensity of thigh NAMs appeared only at high ionic strength (Figure 1C). This confirmed the extractability of thigh NAMs at high ionic strength, whereas breast NAMs are soluble at both low and high ionic strength.

Ca2+-ATPase Activity

Ca2+-ATPase activity of NAMs from breast and thigh of BC and KC is demonstrated in Figure 3A. The factorial analysis revealed that Ca2+-ATPase activity of NAM varied with ionic strength, breed and muscle types as their interactions were significant (Table 1). The ATPase activity in the absence of Mg + 2 represents mainly myosin-ATPase, reflecting conformation of myosin globular head (Sosnicki and Cassens, 1988). NAM from BC exhibited lower Ca2+-ATPase activity compared to KC at low ionic strength (p < 0.05), reflecting a more perturbing state of BC myosin heads. Adult myosin heavy chain isoforms expressed as early as day 20 posthatch in broiler chickens, while expression in slow growing chickens, Single Comb White Leghorn, was not observed until day 33 (Lee et al., 2012). Such an early muscle development and maturation of broiler chickens could have led to a greater conformation perturbance of MHC, reflecting in lower Ca2+-ATPase of broiler chicken. Moreover, the ATPase-β, which is involved in the ATP synthase complex and catalyzes the ATP formation, showed higher expression in broiler chickens than that of Korean native chickens (Liu et al., 2012). Higher ATP accumulation is, therefore, expected in broiler chicken muscle than that of native chickens. To preserve ATP for muscle growth and other metabolic pathways in broiler chickens, ATP degradation process by myosin globular head could be lessened through genetic selection. However, relationship between Ca2+-ATPase and the rate of muscle growth in slow- and fast-growing chicken warrants further investigation.

With regard to muscle type, NAM from KC breast showed comparable activity to that of thigh (p>0.05), while thigh NAM of BC exhibited a higher activity than breast meat (p < 0.05). Hay et al. (1972) also reported a similar activity for NAM from breast and thigh muscles. The authors reported that differences in the Ca2+-ATPase activity of myosin from fast- and slow-twitch muscle fibers of chicken were less obvious than that from pigeon and rabbit. Reductions of about 28.0% Ca2+-ATPase activity were noticed in NAM extracted from KC breast and thigh, when NaCl concentration of the extraction buffer increased from 0.2 to 0.6 M. The same descending trend was also observed in BC breast (33.8%) and thigh (17.1%). Ca2+-ATPase activity is related to integrity of myosin globular head and its reduction represents the myosin conformation changes (Tang and Yongsawatdigul, 2020). Such a decline in the enzymatic activity of NAM regarding to ionic strength (NaCl) has been observed in pike eel and broiler chicken breast (Saleem et al., 2015; Si et al., 2015). This might be correlated to salting-out effect at high NaCl concentrations on myosin, by which protein depolarization and subsequently hydrophobic reactions can lead to protein aggregation (Zhao et al., 2020). NAM from BC breast meat appeared to be the most susceptible to conformational changes induced by NaCl compared to other samples. NAM extracted from broiler thigh showed the least NaCl sensitivity (p < 005). The variation in Ca2+-ATPase activity revealed different NAM conformation and NaCl sensitivity between chicken breeds and muscle types. The results of this study revealed that NAM extracted from breast and thigh meat of the slow-growing KC had a similar sensitivity toward an increase in ionic strength, which was different from BC with higher sensitivity of breast NAM.

Surface Hydrophobicity

ANS can bind to aromatic amino acid residues and reflects hydrophobic regions on protein surface. Different values for surface hydrophobicity were observed in various NAM samples (Figure 3B), in which each factor of ionic strength, breed and muscle types showed a significant effect (Table 1). Generally, KC showed greater surface hydrophobicity regardless of ionic strength and muscle type (p < 0.05). Among the muscle types, NAM extracted from thigh had higher surface hydrophobicity value than breast NAM at low ionic strength values (p < 0.05). Myosin rod α-helix is the main region of hydrophobic residues and it can lead to enhanced surface hydrophobicity upon unfolding (Wang et al., 2019). Myosin from slow-twitch muscles (type I) of chicken thigh have been characterized with higher level of α-helix rod compared to fast-twitch counterparts (type II; breast), which might contribute to higher surface hydrophobicity. A relatively higher α-helix in thigh meat of KC and BC compared to breast counterpart has been observed in previous studies (Katemala et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2023). Boyer et al. (1996) also reported a 1.5-fold higher surface hydrophobicity in the myosin of slow-twitch rabbit muscle compared to the fast-twitch one. A higher surface hydrophobicity was also observed in chicken thigh by Glorieux et al. (2017), and the authors suggested that the different isomers of actomyosins in different muscles would likely cause such variations. However, the higher exposure of hydrophobic residues made NAM more prone to form hydrophobic interactions induced by higher ionic strength as indicated by a significant reduction of surface hydrophobicity in thigh NAMs, particularly that of KC (25.4%; p < 0.05). Similar findings were also reported in porcine NAM, in which increasing NaCl concentration from 0.4 M to 0.6 M decreased surface hydrophobicity (Zhao et al., 2020). In contrary, surface hydrophobicity of tilapia NAM increased with NaCl concentrations from 0.2 to 0.6 M (Tang and Yongsawatdigul, 2020). Our study revealed that conformation of NAM from slow-growing KC was more exposed than a fast-growing BC, regardless of muscle type and ionic strength.

Total Sulfhydryl Groups

Generally, NAM of BC showed higher total SH content than KC, and breast meat exhibited higher amount than thigh counterparts (p < 0.05, Figure 3C). The variations are likely associated to the presence of various actin and myosin isomers in the system (Glorieux et al., 2017). Different muscle parts showed similar behaviors regarding to ionic strength, in which total SH contents of NAMs from both chickens reduced with increasing NaCl concentrations (p < 0.05). The findings imply significant effects of the three factors and their interactions as indicated by factorial analysis (Table 1). These results indicated that SH oxidation occurred to a greater extent at higher ionic strength, particularly in breast samples with a reduction of 23.5 and 16.1% for BC and KC, compared to those of thigh with a reduction of only 4.3% and 7.0%, respectively. Katemala et al. (2021) indicated that disulfide bonds of whole muscle are associated to chicken age, in which an older KC tended to have more disulfide bonds, particularly in thigh. Lower total SH contents in NAMs of KCs could imply higher extent of disulfide linkages, which is likely due to oxidation of thiol group. The concomitant in a reduction of total SH groups and Ca2+-ATPase activity in breast NAM likely indicated that the thiol oxidation occurred at the globular head active site of ATP hydrolysis. Similar finding has been reported in pike eel NAM upon salt exposure (Si et al., 2015). In addition, NaCl at the concentration of 0.6 M could induce formation of disulfide bonds to a greater extent, leading to lower total SH content, especially NAM from breast meat of both breeds. Our results were similar to the findings of Zhao et al. (2020), who reported a decrease in total SH groups of NAM extracted from pork longissimus dorsi upon salt exposure from 0.6 to 0.8 M. Our findings revealed that total SH groups of NAMs extracted from slow-growing KC is different from fast growing BC, which would further imply differences in disulfide interchanges during processing.

Particle Size

The particle sizes of NAMs from thigh and breast of both chicken breeds have been demonstrated in Figure 3D. Particle sizes varied with ionic strength, breed and muscle type (Table 1; p < 0.05). NAMs became larger at high ionic strength (p < 0.05), basically due to the induced repulsive effect caused by Cl¯ as well as aggregation via hydrophobic and disulfide interactions (Figures 3A–3C). NAM extracted from breast meat of both breeds possessed lower particle size than those of thighs at both ionic strength values (p < 0.05). This also correlated with lower total SH group contents in thigh NAMs of KC, which probably imply higher levels of disulfide bond formation in soluble aggregates. Higher S-S bonds in NAM from KC would likely contribute to its higher particle size than that extracted from BC at low ionic strength (p < 0.05).

Thermal Analysis

Thermal properties of NAM from breast and thigh of the two breeds at varied ionic strength conditions were expressed in Figures 4A–4C. The µ-DSC thermogram showed one peak for the NAM samples, indicating of denaturation of myosin. Different results on transitional peak were reported for chicken proteins, which might be associated to the pH, ionic strength and protein concentration (Li et al., 2019). Acton and Dick (1986) reported two transitional peaks for NAM prepared from chicken breast and thigh. Xiong and Brekke (1990) observed a single peak for salt soluble protein extracted from chicken breast and thigh at the pH of 6-6.5, while the thermogram of the breast protein showed two peaks at lower pH of 5.5, at which the authors reported that myosin become more unstable and actin become more stable. A single peak in DSC thermogram of NAM from chicken breast was attributed to higher interactions of actin and myosin during heating (Li et al., 2019). Similar findings were also observed in this study.

The onset (T0) and maximum denaturation temperature (Tmax) of myosin of all samples were lower than previously reported on BC, which might be correlated to differences in the method of analysis and applied heating rate. Kijowski and Mast (1988) assessed the effect of heating rate on the thermal properties of different broiler tissues and reported a lower thermal stability in a lower heating rate. Heating rate of 1°C/min performed in this study can explain lower value compared to the findings of Limpisophon et al. (2019) and Kovačević et al. (2011), in which a heating rate of 5°C/min was used. T0 was affected by muscle type and its interaction with breed type (Table 1), in which breast NAMs, particularly those from KC showed the higher thermal stability of 48.4°C (Figure 4A; p < 0.05). In contrary, muscle type did not effect on Tmax, while ionic strength and breed type were significant factors (Table 1). Breast AM of KC showed higher value of 54.81°C (p < 0.05). Higher thermal stability of NAM from KC is likely associated to its higher protein interactions such as hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonds. In contrast to KC, thermal properties revealed that myofibrillar protein of Thai indigenous chicken (Gallus domesticus) and myosin of spent Lohmann layers breast are more susceptible to thermal denaturation compared to broiler (Wattanachant et al., 2005; Limpisophon et al., 2019).

Enthalpy of denaturation was affected by all studied factors (Table 1). Enthalpy of BC-NAMs and Tmax of NAM from thigh meat of BC decreased as ionic strength increased (Figures 4B–4C; p < 0.05). At high ionic strength, NAM exhibited high solubility, likely in the state of partial unfolding. This can explain decreased Tmax and enthalpy of denaturation values at higher ionic strength. This reflects the effect of high NaCl concentrations on NAM conformational changes and thermostability. Li et al. (2015) reported that a reduction of denaturation enthalpy might be associated to a decrease in hydrogen bonds, which can occur through side chain depolarization by NaCl.

Turbidity

At 40°C, which is below denaturation temperature of myosin, NAMs did not show aggregations (Figure 5A). NAMs from thigh of both breeds showed higher turbidity compared to those extracted from their breast counterparts (p < 0.05), which is probably due to their larger particle size as indicated in Figure 3D. A higher turbidity for salt soluble proteins from thigh of duck and white Leghorn hen compared to their breast was also observed (Xiong and Brekke, 1990; Ndi and Brekke, 1992), in which the authors suggested a different protein-protein interaction within different muscles could cause such variations. A slight increase in turbidity was observed at 50°C which reflected initiation of aggregation, particularly in NAM extracted from breast samples at high ionic strength of both breeds (Figure 5B). However, aggregation of NAM from BC breast began in an earlier incubation time of 5 min compared to that of KC breast which initiated after 15 min. This was correlated to higher thermostability of NAM from KC breast as indicated by µ-DSC (Figures 4A–4B). The higher rate of AM aggregation occurred at 60°C, particularly in samples exposed to low ionic strength regardless to breed and muscle type (Figure 5C; p < 0.05). The higher solubility of NAMs in high ionic strength can explain this phenomenon. NAMs tended to be polymerized in filament at low ionic strength which can scatter light, while high ionic strength can induce the polymer dissociation, leading to solubilized monomer with a reduced turbidity (Li et al., 2022a). A higher turbidity at lower ionic strength has been reported on NAM extracted from chicken breast, fish and pork, among others (Liu et al., 2011; Saleem et al., 2015).

Dynamic Rheology

Dynamic rheological parameters indicate viscoelastic properties of materials. NAM is known to be one of important proteins for gelation. Storage (G′) and loss (G″) moduli represent elasticity and viscosity of NAM, respectively. Higher G′ than G″ values at 10°C observed in our study indicate the elastic behavior of NAM solution. G′ of NAMs increased with temperature and its profile varied with muscle and breed types (Figure 6, Table 1). A drastic increase in G′ values indicates the onset of gel network formation, which occurred at different temperatures referred to Ti, depending on ionic strength (Table 1; p < 0.05). Generally, aggregation of NAMs at higher ionic strength began at lower temperatures of 47.4 to 51.9°C compared to those at lower ionic strength (54.4–58.4°C; Table 2). NAM at high and low ionic strength undergo denaturation at 44.81 to 48.32 and 44.92 to 48.38°C, respectively (Figure 4A). These results indicated that denaturation of NAM took place before the onset of aggregation. At high ionic strength, NAMs exist in monomer and denatured myosin readily undergo aggregation. In contrast, actomyosins in filamentous structure at low ionic strength forming bundle (Ikemoto et al., 1968), which would require relatively higher temperatures for gel network formation compared to the monomer form of NAM at high ionic strength.

As temperature rises to 50 to 60°C, G′ values of most samples slightly decreased. A temperature at which a drop in G′ occurs is referred to Tp. Dissociation of actin from actomyosin gel networks caused a drop in G′ (Sun and Holley, 2011). The NAMs prepared at high ionic strength showed lower Tp values than their corresponding NAMs at low ionic strength (Table 2). Actin is likely to dissociate from monomer actomyosin at high ionic strength, while bundle of filamentous NAM at low ionic strength requires higher energy for dissociation. However, actin dissociation in NAM of BC at high ionic strength may occur in two steps at 58.3 and 75.3°C (Figure 6; Table 2). It should be noted that NAM extracted from breast meat of BC did not show a drop of G′ value at low ionic strength, therefore Tp was not detected (Figure 6). It could be speculated that gel networks of NAM of breast BC at 0.2 M NaCl was initiated by filamentous actomyosin without dissociation of actin. In contrast, NAM of breast KC showed distinct Tp value, implying that actin dissociated from myosin during gel network formation. This study demonstrated distinctive gel networks formation between breast NAM of fast- and slow-growing chicken at low ionic strength.

After a slight drop in G′ value, an increase in G′ was observed again at around 70°C, which was attribute to rearrangement of unfolded actin that synergistically strength up actomyosin gel networks. The NAM extracted from breast formed a more elastic gel with higher value of final storage modulus (Ge′) compared to that of thigh (Figure 6, Table 2; p < 0.05). This was in agreement with previous studies on chicken myofibrillar and salt soluble proteins as well as muscle homogenates from breast and thigh (Xiong and Brekke, 1991; Xiong and Blanchard, 1994; Lesiów and Xiong, 2003). Xiong and Blanchard (1994) reported that the unfolding and subsequently aggregation of thigh proteins during gel formation occur in a shorter time than do in breast ones, which can lead to an unordered aggregation and formation of a weaker elastic gel. In contrary, the result of our study revealed that denaturation (47°C, Figure 4A) and aggregation (47.4°C, Table 2) of breast NAM of BC occurred almost simultaneously at high ionic strength. A longer aggregation period could have part to reach the highest G′ in breast AM of BC. Wang et al., (2022) reported that aggregates of myofibrillar protein can also be crosslinked and formed a well-spanned gel network. In addition, lower total SH groups in thigh NAMs may imply higher disulfide bonds, which can lead to limited denaturation prior to gel formation (Figure 3C). Among breast NAMs, that of BC exhibited a higher Ge′ compared to that of KC at the same ionic strength (Figure 6, p < 0.05). This might be correlated to the higher intramolecular interactions such as disulfide bonds in KC as well as higher thermal stability observed in NAM of KC breast (Figure 3, Figure 4), which make them less susceptible to denaturation which is prerequisite to rearrangements of gel formation. The lower G′ values in KC samples corresponded with particle size, in which higher intramolecular interactions in KC induced a compact structure, leading to a limited expansion in a high ionic strength environment compared to BC (Figure 3D). A similar findings were observed in other sources, as a smaller particle size and lower elastic gel were reported for protein isolate prepared from lupin bean with higher disulfide bonds compared to soy protein isolate (Berghout et al., 2015). In contrary to BC, Ge′ values of KC appeared to be more sensitive to ionic strength, in which a higher Ge′ values were obtained in NAM at low ionic strength (Table 2; p < 0.05). As indicated in Table 2, thigh NAM of KC formed higher elastic gel than that of breast at low ionic strength (p < 0.05). The higher elasticity at lower ionic strength was also observed in pea and whey proteins (Sun and Arntfield, 2011; Hussain et al., 2012). This is correlated to the intrinsic differences of proteins. Chantrapornchai and McClements, (2002) reported that whey protein isolate formed a more elastic gel at ionic strength of 150 mM NaCl compared to at 250 mM. The authors observed that by increasing the ionic strength, gel structure was changed from fine strands to particulates with lower protein-protein interaction, leading to a lower gel elasticity. As the main protein contributing to techno-functional properties, NAM from slow-growing chicken has not been studied to the best of our knowledge, and this is the first study evaluating the dynamic rheology properties of the slow growing KC, indicating a different behavior compared to BC, with the ability to form a more elastic gel at lower ionic strength.

CONCLUSIONS

NAM of Korat chicken, a Thai crossbred slow-growing chicken, showed different in structural proteins, particularly troponin I, as compared to broiler chicken. In addition, different Ca2+–ATPase activity, surface hydrophobicity and total SH contents, revealed the intrinsic differences in actomyosins of the two breeds. NAM from Korat chicken breast exhibited higher thermostability and solubility at low ionic strength. In contrary to commercial broiler, NAMs from Korat chicken were more affected by ionic strength in which a stronger elastic gel was formed in low NaCl concentration, particularly in that of thigh.
