Factors associated with delayed diagnosis of fibrotic interstitial lung disease: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hyun J Kim, Surbhi Shah, Sharashchandra Shetty, Pratik Pimple, Ashwin Rai, Charlotte Petterson, Christian Lloyd, Ivana A Vaughn, Lois Lamerato, Wesley Hicks, Amy Olson, Ariel Berger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTimely diagnosis and treatment of fibrotic interstitial lung disease (ILD) is crucial to preserve lung function and limit healthcare costs. However, diagnosis is challenging and thus often delayed. Factors associated with delayed diagnosis of fibrotic ILD are poorly understood. This study assessed time to diagnosis among patients with fibrotic ILD and identified factors associated with diagnostic delay.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used previously developed algorithms to identify patients from a large integrated tertiary-care health system in the US Midwest with evidence of fibrotic ILD in 2011–2019. Adults identified by these algorithms had their ILD diagnosis and symptom onset date confirmed by chart review. Time from symptom onset to diagnosis was dichotomized as ≤ 6 and > 6 months to assign patients to timely and delayed diagnosis cohorts, respectively. Potential predictors of diagnostic delay (demographics, clinical characteristics, ILD-related symptoms and tests, healthcare use, and healthcare costs) were analyzed by stepwise backward logistic regression and by machine learning using gradient boosting, with the latter illustrated using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) plots.Results239 patients met all selection criteria; 138 (57.7%) experienced delayed diagnosis and 101 (42.3%) timely diagnosis. Mean time from symptom onset to diagnosis was 16.8 months for the overall sample, 27.5 months for patients with delayed diagnosis, and 2.2 months for patients with timely diagnosis. Compared to patients with timely diagnosis, greater proportions of those with delayed diagnosis were female (55.8% vs. 40.2%, p = 0.013) and had gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) (47.1% vs. 21.6%, p < 0.001) or rheumatic disease (21.0% vs. 10.9%, p = 0.038). In multivariate logistic regression, GERD (odds ratio [OR] 2.64, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.39–5.04) and number of complaints of cough (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.02–1.15) and shortness of breath (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.02–1.09) were associated with increased odds of delayed diagnosis. Factors associated with delayed diagnosis include GERD and repeated complaints of cough/shortness of breath. Background

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) comprises a heterogeneous group of parenchymal lung disorders with varying degrees of inflammation and fibrosis [1]. Age at onset is typically 50–70 years [2]. Fibrotic ILD is characterized by progressive scarring of the lung tissue, declining lung function, worsened quality of life, and life expectancy of 3–5 years after diagnosis [2–4]. Symptoms include shortness of breath, coughing, wheezing, and chest pain [5]. Diagnosis is confirmed by a high- or regular-resolution computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest [6] or lung biopsy [7].

Timely diagnosis of fibrotic ILD can be challenging for a number of reasons. It is rare, with an estimated age- and sex-adjusted incidence in the US of 52 per 100,000 patient-years [8]. Consequently, there may be a lack of clinician familiarity with the disease. Second, ILD-related symptoms overlap with other, more common, chronic comorbidities, including respiratory disorders (e.g., asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD]); cardiovascular diseases (e.g., angina, congestive heart failure [CHF], arrhythmias); and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) [5, 9]. Accordingly, clinicians may misattribute symptoms to these comorbidities, resulting in delayed suspicion of fibrotic ILD and subsequent diagnostic work-up. The relatively short duration of healthcare visits (in the US, evaluation and management [E/M] billing codes for established patients run in 15-minute intervals) [10] may also contribute to underdiagnosis by limiting time clinicians spend assessing their patients. Finally, diagnosing fibrotic ILD often requires a multidisciplinary team [7], which is not always available in a timely manner. For many patients with non-specific comorbidities, diagnosis may depend on a referral to a pulmonologist at a specialist center.

Early identification and treatment of fibrotic ILD is crucial in preserving lung function and quality of life; diagnostic delays may worsen clinical outcomes and increase healthcare costs [11]. Causes of diagnostic delay are not well understood. Improved understanding of factors associated with delayed diagnosis could help reduce the time between symptom onset and clinical recognition of disease, including by informing the development of remedial interventions.

Methods

Design and data source

This retrospective cohort study examined the magnitude of delayed diagnosis of fibrotic ILD, and corresponding factors that predict this unwanted outcome, using information from a US integrated delivery network (IDN). Electronic health data (claims and electronic medical records [EMR]), supplemented by information abstracted from medical charts, was obtained from the IDN’s insurance health plan, which serves ~ 500,000 persons. The IDN, a tertiary-care health system located in the Midwest region of the US, offers primary care, acute care, and specialty services. Its comprehensive EMR system contains patient demographics, ambulatory care visits, clinical results, and inpatient admissions. It also has an administrative data warehouse containing data on encounters, billing records, and outpatient prescriptions. Additionally, trained data abstractors employed by the IDN reviewed patients’ medical charts and abstracted relevant unstructured data.

The study was approved by the Henry Ford Health Institutional Review Board (approval no. 15094), which granted a waiver for collection of informed consent. All personal data was processed in accordance with applicable data privacy laws and regulations. To ensure patient confidentiality, unique IDs were created to link information abstracted from patients’ medical charts to their electronic health data.

Sample selection

Four previously developed case-selection algorithms specific to lung fibrosis, an underlying disease associated with fibrosis, or other relevant diseases that may or may not involve fibrosis [12, 13] were used to identify patients with evidence of fibrotic ILD between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2019 (Fig. 1). The sample selection period was limited to the end of 2019 to avoid potential bias in care-seeking behavior due to onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Unique encounters were required to occur on separate days within any 365-day period during the identification period. The earliest date on which a patient met criteria for any of the algorithms was deemed the “diagnosis date.”

Patients identified by at least one of the four algorithms were also required to (1) have at least one chest CT scan (high-resolution or regular) during the 2 years before, or 1 year after, the diagnosis date; and (2) be ≥ 18 years.

Case ascertainment (confirmation of fibrotic ILD via medical charts)

Patients identified via electronic data had their fibrotic ILD status confirmed via chart review. A screening form was developed to collect physician notes from all chest CT scans (high-resolution or otherwise) during the 5-year period before the diagnosis date. Each completed screening form was reviewed by a clinician author to confirm ILD status and the associated diagnosis date; a second author conducted a confirmatory review on a randomly selected 20% sample. A pulmonologist author performed adjudication on patients whose fibrotic ILD status: (1) could not be ascertained in the initial review; or (2) was not confirmed in the random check. For patients with confirmed fibrotic ILD, the date used to confirm diagnosis was designated the index date. Patients with confirmed fibrotic ILD were required to have at least 5 years of continuous enrollment in the IDN’s insurance health plan before and including the index date (i.e., “pre-index period”). The 5-year pre-diagnosis window was established based on input from clinicians and was intended to be sufficient to capture all potentially relevant symptoms of fibrotic ILD noted prior to diagnosis.

Determination of symptom onset date and diagnostic delay

For each patient with confirmed fibrotic ILD, pre-index symptom data abstracted from medical charts were integrated with electronic data for healthcare visits and admissions, laboratory tests, diagnoses, and medications. Two members of the study team then reviewed the data for symptoms possibly attributable to fibrotic ILD (dyspnea, cough, wheezing, chest pain, and reduction in physical activity). The earliest ILD-attributable symptom date in the pre-index period was designated the “symptom onset date.” Random checks and adjudication were performed using the same approach described above for case ascertainment.

The primary outcome measure was the time from symptom onset to diagnosis. Patients were stratified into two mutually exclusive cohorts based on time from symptom onset to index date: those with timely diagnosis (≤ 6 months) and those with delayed diagnosis (> 6 months). The 6-month threshold was selected based on assessment by a pulmonologist (HJK) that it encompassed a reasonable time period in which prompt and comprehensive assessment of symptom complaints should result in diagnosis (i.e., it reflected a reasonable time within the current US healthcare system between an initial complaint and ordering/performing/interpreting relevant tests and imaging, resulting in diagnosis of fibrotic ILD).

Predictors of diagnostic delay

Potential predictors of diagnostic delay were assessed, including demographics, clinical characteristics, ILD-related symptoms and tests, healthcare use, and costs before the index date. Demographic data, obtained electronically and evaluated as of the index date, included age, sex, race, ethnicity, and median household income. Clinical characteristics included body mass index, smoking status, comorbidities, family history of ILD, and symptoms possibly attributable to fibrotic ILD.

Pre-index healthcare use and associated costs, obtained from electronic data, included emergency department (ED) visits, inpatient visits, outpatient visits, and pharmacotherapies. All costs were adjusted to 2022 US dollar values using the consumer price index.

Statistical analyses

Analyses were primarily conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Algorithm performance was evaluated by calculating positive predictive value (PPV) as true positives (patients confirmed as cases based on chart review) divided by the sum of true positives and false positives (patients who met relevant algorithm criteria). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics of the aggregate sample and the timely and delayed diagnosis cohorts. For continuous variables, between-cohort differences were analyzed using Student’s t-test (variables that followed a normal distribution) or Wilcoxon rank-sum test (non-normally distributed variables). For categorical variables, chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests (as appropriate) were used to compare patients with timely vs. delayed diagnosis.

Predictors of delayed diagnosis were identified by stepwise backward multivariate logistic regression and by machine learning using the gradient boosting method. In logistic regression, univariate models were first developed to examine the relationship between diagnostic delay and individual patient characteristics. Characteristics found to be independently associated with diagnostic delay (p < 0.20) were retained in a multivariate stepwise regression model. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

Machine learning analyses were conducted using Python. Model optimization was achieved by nested cross-validation in conjunction with the GridSearchCV method, through multiple iterations involving XGBoost (a powerful and widely-used machine learning algorithm that combines multiple relatively weak predictive models to create a stronger and more accurate predictive model) and Random Forest models. XGBoost emerged as the preferred choice for model development due to its consistently better performance. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) plots were generated to identify covariates with the greatest impact on the probability of diagnostic delay. These plots provide insights into how each variable/feature contributes to the model’s predictions, helping to understand the underlying patterns and relationships in the data. Subsequently, a k-means clustering analysis was performed using the SHAP values to define patient clusters with a low or high probability of delayed ILD diagnosis based on selected patient characteristics. The aim was to help characterize patients at risk of delayed diagnosis.

Results

Patient sample

A total of 644 adult insurance health plan enrollees met selection criteria for ≥ 1 algorithms for fibrotic ILD. For 260 of these insurance health plan enrollees, fibrotic ILD was confirmed through medical chart review. PPV was 40.4% overall for all case-selection algorithms and 17.6% to 45.8% for individual algorithms (Fig. 2).

Performance of algorithms at identifying fibrotic ILD. *During the 2 years before or 1-year after the earliest relevant diagnosis. **Patients could be identified by more than one algorithm. CT, computed tomography; ILD, interstitial lung disease; PPV, positive predictive value

Of the 260 patients with confirmed fibrotic ILD, 239 (91.9%) had both ≥ 5 years of continuous insurance health plan enrollment during the pre-index period and a symptom onset date that could be ascertained and were retained for analysis.

Time from symptom onset to diagnosis

Mean time to diagnosis for the overall sample was 16.8 months (Fig. 3). A total of 138 patients (57.7% of the study sample) were diagnosed > 6 months after onset of their first ILD symptom (delayed diagnosis cohort); for these patients, mean (standard deviation [SD]) time from symptom onset to diagnosis was 27.5 (17.3) months. The other 101 patients (42.3%) were diagnosed within 6 months of their initial symptom (timely diagnosis cohort), with a mean (SD) time from symptom onset to diagnosis of 2.2 (1.4) months.

Demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with delayed and timely diagnosis

Mean (SD) age on the index date was 74.0 (10.4) years and 73.0 (11.5) years for patients with delayed and timely diagnosis, respectively (p = 0.490) (Table 1). Compared to patients with timely diagnosis, the delayed diagnosis cohort had higher proportions of females (55.8% vs. 39.6%, p = 0.013), Black or African American patients (27.5% vs. 21.8%; p = 0.230), and patients with a family history of ILD (23.9% vs. 16.8%; p = 0.184). Compared to patients with timely diagnosis, a higher proportion of patients with delayed diagnosis had GERD (47.1% vs. 21.8%, p < 0.001) and rheumatic disease (21.0% vs. 10.9%, p = 0.038); they also had nominally higher levels of chronic respiratory conditions (40.6% vs. 31.7%, p = 0.159), depression (26.1% vs. 16.8%, p = 0.089), and diabetes (37.7% vs. 32.7%, p = 0.424).

Factors associated with delayed diagnosis

In multivariate logistic regression, GERD (OR 2.64, 95% CI 1.39–5.04) and numbers of complaints of cough (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.02–1.15) and shortness of breath (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.02–1.09) were associated with an increased likelihood of delayed diagnosis (Fig. 4). An increase in healthcare costs by ≥ 30% from the first (oldest) to the last (most recent) year of the 5-year pre-index period was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of delayed diagnosis (OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.20–0.71).

The machine learning model had an accuracy of 60%, with weighted average precision of 61%. This suggested that, on average, the model correctly predicted delayed diagnosis 61% of the time. The SHAP plot indicated that a higher probability of delayed diagnosis was associated with any one of the following: age 65–70 years; increased number of complaints of shortness of breath or cough; presence of GERD or depression; and family history of ILD (Fig. 5). Patients whose healthcare costs increased by ≥ 30% during the pre-index period had a lower probability of delayed diagnosis.

Discussion

In this real-world study of factors associated with delayed diagnosis of fibrotic ILD, 58% of patients experienced delayed diagnosis, and mean time from symptom onset to diagnosis was 16.8 months. In an international survey of specialists, time from symptom onset to diagnosis was 9–12 months [14]; the corresponding estimate from a prospective cohort study in New York State was almost 2 years [15]. However, time to diagnosis in these studies was defined based on physician-estimated time from symptom onset or patient-reported onset of dyspnea, whereas our study evaluated curated EMR, unstructured EMR (through chart review), and administrative claims data to estimate diagnostic delays from a real-world perspective.

This study confirms that patients with fibrotic ILD are medically complicated, with multiple comorbidities that potentially delay diagnosis. Specifically, delayed diagnosis was associated with comorbidities with symptoms common to fibrotic ILD—notably GERD—which likely resulted (at least for patients with GERD) in misattribution of the cause of observed symptoms. The association of repeated complaints of cough and shortness of breath with delayed diagnosis may reflect patients (many of whom had comorbid pulmonary or cardiovascular conditions) repeatedly consulting with healthcare providers for persistent symptoms without their fibrotic ILD being diagnosed and treated. While somewhat surprising, this phenomenon could be related to the relatively short periods of time physicians can spend with their patients due to billing requirements (most billing codes for evaluation and management of established patients include the total amount of time spent with the patient) and full schedules. However, it also brings focus on the continued use of healthcare services in these patients without an attributed ILD diagnosis. Moreover, the observation that timely diagnosis was associated with increased costs closer to the diagnosis date may be a consequence of worsening disease severity leading to fibrotic ILD being diagnosed.

The observation that patients with delayed diagnosis were more likely to have rheumatic disease and at least one rheumatologist visit is interesting. We speculate that during the brief consultations patients have with their sometimes multiple specialists and other healthcare providers, there is insufficient time within what is allowed by payers from a billing perspective to adequately address all their symptoms and comorbidities. Moreover, specialists may be inclined to focus on their own specialty. For example, rheumatologists might focus on rheumatologic conditions already diagnosed, rather than pulmonology-related concerns. Time during an office visit may be inadequate to obtain a holistic picture of the patient’s condition and address all their symptoms.

The algorithms we used to identify patients with fibrotic ILD have the potential to identify undiagnosed patients prospectively, enabling them to be flagged for screening/specialist medical attention. This could ultimately result in better patient outcomes (and potentially reduced use and costs of healthcare services in primary care) by expediting pulmonologist referrals and/or CT scans, especially considering that algorithms can be run efficiently within existing electronic healthcare databases. While the algorithms performed modestly in identifying “true” cases of fibrotic ILD, they yielded an enriched population for subsequent screening for fibrotic ILD. Population enrichment was a necessary first step to efficiently identify patients with fibrotic ILD, as it reduced the number of patients to screen by chart review to a manageable level. While this approach would be expected to capture most patients with fibrotic ILD, any fibrotic ILD patients with just one encounter with a relevant diagnosis code would have been missed. Determining the resulting impact of any such misclassification fell beyond the scope of our study.

Although just over two-fifths of included patients were identified as being diagnosed in a timely manner, these included some who were diagnosed with fibrotic ILD on, or very shortly after, the symptom onset date. A similar phenomenon was observed in another study of patients receiving dialysis for end-stage renal disease, many of whom were hospitalized on the day that dialysis was initiated [16]—presumably because at least some of these individuals had previously undiagnosed and untreated disease and underwent emergency dialysis treatment in hospital due to complications/progression resulting from undetected renal failure. Thus, while our timely diagnosis cohort included patients whose physicians may have conducted early work-ups for fibrotic ILD, it may also include other patients whose lung function deteriorated without clinical recognition to such an extent that they ended up in the emergency room, leading to thorough examination and prompt diagnosis. Accordingly, the early diagnosis cohort may have comprised two distinct subgroups: those whose disease progressed relatively extensively without patient and/or clinician recognition, and those whose clinicians diagnosed ILD in an appropriate time frame. Further work is needed to better understand the impact of the combination of these two groups on identified predictors of delayed diagnosis.

Real-world data is needed to examine the “diagnostic journey” of patients with fibrotic ILD. Large insurance claims and EMR databases offer an efficient means to capture diverse real-world clinical practices. They are relatively inexpensive to access and typically include generalizable populations. Importantly, their size can often mitigate sample size issues with rare diseases such as fibrotic ILD. However, their usefulness depends on the ability to accurately identify relevant cohorts and to capture key potential predictors, including measures likely to be captured as structured data (rendering them readily available and analyzable) and other information likely to be captured as unstructured data (typically requiring chart review or other means of capture to render them ready for analysis). Crucially, diagnosis codes for fibrotic ILD have varying levels of specificity and sensitivity [17], and relevant symptomatology and results from diagnostic tests are not consistently available as structured data. A comprehensive data source with deep capture of patients’ health and use of healthcare services is thus required to identify factors that may be associated with delayed diagnosis of ILD. We used such a source containing claims and curated EMR data from a US IDN, supplemented by medical charts. To confirm and strengthen our findings and potentially reduce the likelihood of delayed diagnosis of fibrotic ILD, we encourage others to replicate this research in other IDNs or settings with comparable comprehensive data.

There are limitations associated with this study. First, it was based on data from a single IDN, and our findings were not confirmed using an “independent” validation dataset (i.e., a dataset not used for development of the algorithms). This was due to the relatively small number of patients with fibrotic ILD identified in the IDN and our interest in maximizing the amount of available information to inform the models. We mitigated this limitation by employing nested cross-validation within the Random Forest and XGBoost models. Nested cross-validation involves two loops: an inner loop for hyperparameter tuning and an outer loop for performance evaluation. The dataset is split into multiple subsets (or folds), where the inner loop selects the optimal model parameters, and the outer loop assesses model performance on unseen data. This ensures reliable and unbiased estimation of model performance. In small datasets, nested cross-validation is particularly advantageous, as it maximizes the use of limited data while reducing overfitting and improving the robustness and generalizability of the results. As such, nested cross-validation is a well-established technique for improving model performance and internal validity in machine learning applications [18–20]. While our use of nested cross-validation improved internal validity, the generalizability of our findings to the broader population of US patients with fibrotic ILD remains uncertain.

Another limitation is use of 6 months as the threshold between timely and delayed diagnosis. Although this was based on pulmonologist judgement, a realistic time frame for timely diagnosis of fibrotic ILD is essentially unknown. Also, while each patient’s ILD status and symptom onset date were verified by two reviewers, with a third reviewer resolving discrepancies when needed, misclassifications and incorrect symptom onset assignments may have occurred. Additionally, subjective judgments by data abstractors regarding symptoms’ attribution to ILD, alongside incomplete medical records, could have led to misinterpretations or incomplete data, potentially affecting the accuracy of symptom onset dates. While it is likely that any resulting bias would be conservative, the extent to which this occurred—and the corresponding magnitude of mis-estimation—are unknown.

While symptoms and ILD status were confirmed through review of EMR and claims data in order to evaluate time from symptom onset to ILD diagnosis, other comorbidities that might be predictors of delayed diagnosis were not confirmed in the same way. This means that some patients with ILD may have been misdiagnosed with other conditions such as GERD. Unfortunately, verification of “competing” conditions to the extent performed to verify presence of ILD was beyond the scope of our study. Further research is required to better understand the degree to which conditions such as GERD manifested as actual comorbid conditions or were a misclassification of what in retrospect should have been classified as ILD.

Our finding that relatively large numbers of complaints of cough and shortness of breath were associated with delayed diagnosis is somewhat unsurprising, given that these symptoms are hallmarks of respiratory and cardiovascular-related diseases. While there are sometimes tautologic concerns about inclusion of such data in a predictive model, we believe it appropriate in this instance, as our goal was to identify characteristics that collectively may enable clinicians to better understand the need to instigate diagnostic work-up before their patients have the “opportunity” to return with repeated complaints of cough or shortness of breath. If, on the other hand, we had other research aims (e.g., deriving an etiological model of fibrotic ILD), inclusion of such symptoms would be problematic.

The fact that cough and shortness of breath were observed across multiple visits prior to diagnosis suggests that, for at least some patients who are ultimately diagnosed with fibrotic ILD, clinicians may have misattributed the symptom(s) to pre-existing conditions. This in turn suggests that physicians should explore competing explanations for repeated complaints of relevant symptoms—especially among patients with comorbidities whose symptoms “overlap” with those of fibrotic ILD (e.g., respiratory, cardiovascular, and/or gastrointestinal comorbidities). Further research is needed to better understand the role of symptoms (and other predictors) in identifying patients in whom diagnostic work-up for fibrotic ILD is warranted. The need for diagnostic work-up should balance the clinical need for more rapid identification of an “at risk” population using the limited resources typically available at both the provider and payer levels against the risk of inducing worry in persons unlikely to ultimately have fibrotic ILD.

The symptom onset date was assigned based on information reported to healthcare providers during visits. It is likely that some patients had experienced symptoms for a considerable time before mentioning them to their healthcare provider because patients may initially attribute some symptoms to the aging process (for example, being less physically active/having quicker onset of shortness of breath compared to prior years, choosing to drive instead of walk) [5, 21]. Thus, the time between symptom onset and diagnosis may have been underestimated (i.e., our findings may be conservative). However, the purpose of this study was to identify factors associated with potentially avoidable delays in diagnosis. Accordingly, we focused on the date when fibrotic ILD symptoms were first reported to a healthcare provider to mimic that perspective. Moreover, while the data were fairly comprehensive, we were unable to capture and assess some measures that may impact time to diagnosis, such as delays in referrals, long wait times for appointments, and lack of adequate insurance coverage.

To avoid potential bias caused by changes in care-seeking behavior linked to the COVID-19 pandemic, we set the sample selection period to end in December 2019. It is interesting to speculate how the pandemic’s legacy has affected people’s care-seeking behavior in relation to respiratory symptoms. If people are relatively reluctant to see their doctor in the post-COVID era, it will only further delay diagnosis of fibrotic ILD. Conversely, patients with undiagnosed ILD who fear they may in fact have COVID (based on chest-related symptoms they are experiencing) may be more likely to go to doctor and thus may be diagnosed more quickly. While beyond the scope of our study, it would be interesting to repeat our analysis with more recent data to see if either of these scenarios (or some combination thereof) might be true.

Conclusions

In summary, delays in diagnosing fibrotic ILD are common, with the average time between presumed symptom onset and clinical identification estimated to be about 17 months. Key factors associated with delayed diagnosis include GERD and repeated complaints of cough or shortness of breath. Further research is needed to better understand how diagnostic delay impacts clinical outcomes in patients with fibrotic ILD.
