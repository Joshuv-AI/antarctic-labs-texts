Tracking of multidrug-resistant pathogen clones in Ghana: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Alex Odoom, Eric S. Donkor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) threatens effective antibiotic treatment. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial clones pose a particular challenge because they facilitate rapid resistance dissemination. Tracking dominant antibiotic-resistant clones in Ghana will inform targeted surveillance and control. This systematic review characterised prevalent MDR clones of priority pathogens isolated from humans, animals, and the environment in Ghana.MethodsA search of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases was conducted from inception to October 4, 2024, for studies reporting genetic characterisation of MDR clones from Ghana. The risk of bias in the included studies was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa scale (NOS), and data analysis involved descriptive statistics and proportional meta-analysis.ResultsTwenty-five studies met the eligibility criteria, and 10 different MDR bacterial species were identified from human, animal, and environmental sources. The pooled prevalence of MDR bacteria was 53.4% (95% CI: 39.8–66.9). The dominant E. coli clones were ST155 (38.0%), ST617 (29.1%), and ST10 (11.1%). For K. pneumoniae, ST152 and ST17 were the main clones detected, each with a prevalence of 13.7%. ST39 was also present at 9.0%. The major S. pneumoniae clones were ST802 (18.5%), ST15111 (12.3%), and ST15448 (4.8%). ST152 (27.3%), ST121 (21.3%), and ST9 (14.3%) were predominant among the S. aureus isolates. The most prevalent Acinetobacter baumannii clone was ST231 (77.3%), followed by ST2145 (13.6%).ConclusionThis systematic review provided the first comprehensive overview of MDR clones that may be circulating in Ghana. The identification of high-risk clones, such as E. coli ST155 and S. aureus ST152, highlights the need for urgent public health interventions. Continued tracking using standardised WGS methodologies across diverse sources is crucial for guiding antimicrobial resistance containment in Ghana.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-025-02161-0.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a significant threat to global public health in the 21st century. It is estimated that by 2050, antimicrobial resistance will cause 10 million annual deaths, exceeding the combined toll of heart disease, HIV, and cancer [1]. The global rise of antimicrobial resistance has been significantly exacerbated by the emergence and dissemination of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial clones [2]. MDR bacterial clones exhibit an enhanced ability to accumulate resistance determinants, remain fit, and transmit among vulnerable host populations [2]. These high-risk lineages will continue to erode the available therapeutic options and endanger public health if left unchecked [3].

Molecular techniques, such as multilocus sequence typing (MLST) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS), enable robust characterisation and real-time monitoring of clonal populations for driving resistance [4, 5]. MLST analyses sequence variations within conserved housekeeping genes to assign allelic profiles and sequence types (STs) that delineate bacterial lineages [6]. WGS offers a higher-resolution genomic perspective on the DNA sequence, structure, and content, facilitating the identification of clones and antimicrobial resistance and virulence factors [7]. These approaches have revealed the existence of international clones, which pose increasing threats globally. For example, Escherichia coli ST131 has emerged as a dominant multidrug-resistant cause of healthcare and community-onset infections worldwide owing to the successful dissemination of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase and fluoroquinolone resistance genes [8]. The Klebsiella pneumoniae strains ST258 and ST11 represent other widespread high-risk lineages that contribute to carbapenem resistance globally [9]. Similarly, widespread clones have been associated with resistance crises in other bacterial species, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) sequence type ST228 [10], extensively drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa sequence type ST235 [11], and multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii ST2 [12].

Africa is facing a disproportionately high AMR burden, emerging as a leading public health threat [13]. According to the CDC, approximately 55,000 deaths from HIV, 30,000 deaths from malaria, and a total of 255,000 deaths in Africa in 2019 were attributed to AMR [14]. Invasive infections, such as pneumonia, sepsis, and tuberculosis, already cause immense mortality, with resistance hampering frontline therapies. An increasing number of “last-resort” antibiotic classes have demonstrated reduced effectiveness against endemic pathogens like Escherichia coli, Salmonella, and Staphylococcus aureus, across the continent [13]. Major international clones, such as E. coli ST131, further propagate resistance in African countries [15].

As a West African nation of over 30 million people, Ghana exemplifies the region’s challenges with rising AMR facilitated by social determinants [16]. A recent systematic review indicated that resistance exceeds 80% for first-line antibiotics [17]. Ghana also faces significant challenges with the rising prevalence of MDR bacterial infections across various healthcare and community settings. For instance, a 2023 study conducted in a major teaching hospital in the Central Region found that 42.6% of Escherichia coli isolates were MDR, with high resistance to carbapenems, cephalosporins, and fluoroquinolones [18]. Similarly, a 2020 surveillance report from patients diagnosed with surgical site infections in a tertiary hospital in the Greater Accra Region documented that 86% of Acinetobacter baumannii and Escherichia coli isolates were MDR [19]. The situation is not limited to healthcare settings, as studies have also highlighted the alarming spread of MDR bacteria in the community. A 2024 study examining fecal carriage of Escherichia coli in healthy Ghanaian pregnant women in Accra reported that 50% of the isolates were MDR, including resistance to cephalosporins [20]. Additionally, Salmonella enterica serovars have increasingly been reported in the animal farm environment in the Ashanti Region, with 84.6% of isolates being MDR [21].

These findings underscore the urgent need for comprehensive surveillance and tracking of the predominant resistant lineages to inform targeted interventions and policy decisions aimed at curbing the spread of AMR in the country. Therefore, this systematic review aims to provide comparable baseline data on MDR clones circulating in Ghana to inform local responses and potential participation in cooperative networks geared towards long-term solutions.

Materials and methods

PRISMA guidelines

This systematic review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [22], using their checklist and flow diagram to guide record identification, screening, and evaluation.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in the PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar databases from inception to October 4, 2024, to identify relevant articles. The following search terms were combined: “Ghana”, “Greater Accra Region”, “Ashanti Region”, “Western Region”, “Eastern Region”, “Central Region”, “Northern Region”, “Upper East Region”, “Upper West Region”, “Volta Region”, “Bono Region”, “Bono East Region”, “Ahafo Region”, “Western North Region”, “Oti Region”, “Savannah Region”, “North East Region”, “antimicrobial resistance”, “multidrug resistance”, “MDR”, “genotypes”, “clonal complexes”, “sequence types”, “MLST”, “multilocus sequence typing”, “WGS”, “whole genome sequencing”, “Escherichia coli”, “Klebsiella pneumoniae”, “Pseudomonas aeruginosa”, “Acinetobacter”, “Salmonella”, “Staphylococcus aureus”, “Streptococcus pneumoniae”, “Neisseria gonorrhoea”, “Enterococcus”). The full search terms of each database are shown in Table S3. No restrictions were placed on the language or publication date. The reference lists of relevant studies were also reviewed to identify additional publications. The search results were imported into the Rayyan software [23] for the removal of duplicates and screening.

The full texts of the potentially eligible studies were retrieved and independently assessed by both reviewers according to the selection criteria. Disagreements at any screening stage were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer. Studies excluded at the full-text screening stage for reasons were documented. The study selection process was illustrated using a PRISMA flow diagram. The reference lists of all included studies were also manually searched to identify any additional relevant citations.

Data extraction

A standardised, pre-piloted data extraction form was used to extract relevant information from the included studies in Microsoft Excel. The data encompass details such as study characteristics (first author name, publication year, study location in Ghana), bacterial isolates (organisms studied, number of isolated isolates, number of MDR isolates), and molecular characterisation (genotyping techniques utilised, identified clones/sequence types, specific clones/STs identified, number of isolates from each clone/ST). One reviewer performed the initial data extraction, which was then cross-checked by a second reviewer. Discrepancies or uncertainties were resolved through a consensus discussion between the two reviewers.

Quality assessment

The Newcastle–Ottawa scale (NOS) [25] was used to assess the quality of the cross-sectional and cohort studies. The methodological qualities of the included studies were independently assessed by two reviewers using standardised criteria. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus.

For the cross-sectional study, NOS was used. The selection, comparability, and outcome domains were assessed using a star system ranging from 0 to 9. Studies achieving six or more stars were considered high quality.

Cohort and case-control studies were also assessed using the NOS. Additional aspects evaluated included description of cases/controls; ascertainment of exposure; demonstrated that outcome of interest was not present at start of study; comparability of cohorts; assessment of outcome; follow-up long enough for outcomes to occur; and adequacy of follow-up of cohorts.

Data analysis

A qualitative synthesis was conducted to compile and summarise all the MDR clones/sequence types in a single study. For quantitative synthesis, meta-analyses were carried out separately for each pathogen where clones/STs were reported in two or more studies using the ‘meta’ package in RStudio software version 4.3.3 (2024-02-29 ucrt). Forest plots were generated to present individual study results and pooled estimates. Heterogeneity between studies was assessed using the I2 statistic, with values > 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. A random-effects model that considers variations among studies was used for the meta-analysis. Pooled prevalence estimates for the clonal types were calculated along with their 95% confidence intervals. Publication bias was evaluated visually using funnel plots.

Results

Search results

The PubMed (n = 49), Scopus (n = 155), and Web of Science (n = 46) databases identified 250 articles. A manual search of the reference lists and Google Scholar identified an additional 9 articles, making 259 articles. After de-duplication (n = 133), 126 unique records remained. The titles and abstracts were screened according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Of these, 91 articles were excluded because they did not meet the eligibility criteria. The full texts of 35 potentially relevant articles were assessed. A further 10 articles were excluded after a full-text review because MDR pathogens were not identified in four cases. If MDR pathogens were identified, genotyping data were lacking in six cases. In total, 25 studies [26–50] were included in the systematic review. Study characteristics and distribution

Among the included studies, 11 (44%) focused on the Greater Accra Region, followed by six (24%) on the Ashanti Region and two (8%) on the Central Region. Six (24%) studies used a multicentre approach, covering two or more regions. No studies were conducted in the Upper East, Upper West, Eastern, or Volta Regions (Table S1).

The majority (18, 72%) identified MDR clones in antibiotic-resistant bacteria isolated from human clinical samples. These included specimens such as rectal, hand, and nasopharyngeal swabs, urine, aspirates, gastric samples, high vaginal swabs, pus, sputum, stool, wounds, and nasal swabs. Four studies (16%) reported MDR clones from animal-derived food products, including chicken eggs, livestock, raw meat, fresh milk, and dairy products. Only one study (4%) characterised MDR clones directly from animal (cloaca) samples. One study (4%) identified MDR clones in both human and animal sources, while another study (4%) reported MDR clones present in both human clinical and environmental (wastewater, sewage) samples (Table S1).

Methods for the detection and characterisation of MDR clones

Two primary methods were used for the genetic detection and characterisation of clones in MDR bacteria. Six (24%) studies combined whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and multilocus sequence typing (MLST). Eight (32%) studies used WGS alone, whereas 11 (44%) used only MLST (Table S1).

Prevalence of MDR bacteria

Ten MDR bacterial species, including Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterococcus faecium, Enterococcus faecalis, Neisseria gonorrheae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Salmonella enterica, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Escherichia coli, were identified across human, animal, and environmental sources. The pooled prevalence was 53.4% (95% CI: 39.8–66.9) (Fig. 2).

These clones included ST162, ST1662, ST13846, ST1431, ST14531, ST14536, ST14537, ST14538, ST1465, ST1466, ST1467, ST1468, ST1469, ST1470, ST1471, ST1473, ST1479, ST101, ST1158, ST117, ST12, ST124, ST127, ST1286, ST37, ST38, ST390, ST69, ST700, ST73, ST845, ST866, ST940, ST638, ST642, ST5614, ST58, ST455, ST492, ST506, ST44, ST443, ST446, ST206, ST21, ST210, ST212, ST2167, ST218, ST224, ST227, ST2461, ST2522, ST34, ST342, ST3489, ST19, ST196, ST1972, ST2, and ST202. The most prevalent E. coli MDR clone was ST155, with a prevalence of 38% (0.0–9.0), followed by ST617 (29.1% (0.0-75.9)) and ST10 (11.1% (0.0-22.8)). The pooled prevalence of the MDR clones ST648, ST542, ST450, ST410, ST354, ST205, ST1722, ST167, ST156, ST131, and ST1304 ranged from 2.8% (0.0-6.7) to 6.2% (0.0-19.3) (Fig. 3; Table 1).

Four (16%) studies [30, 32, 34, 35] identified MDR clones in Klebsiella pneumoniae. Three MDR clones of Klebsiella pneumoniae were included in the meta-analysis: ST39, ST17, and ST152. Thirteen MDR clones were excluded: ST397, ST798, ST1788, ST2171, ST2816, ST307, ST36, ST101, ST1189, ST1207, ST133, ST147, and ST15. The pooled prevalences of the MDR clones ST152 and ST17 were 13.7% (0.0-32.2) each, and the prevalence of ST39 was 9.0% (3.6–14.4) (Fig. 4; Table 1).

Four (16%) studies [39, 42, 43, 45] identified MDR clones in S. pneumoniae. Three MDR S. pneumoniae clones were included in the meta-analysis: ST802, ST15448, and ST15111. These MDR clones were excluded: ST15447, ST9733, ST1349, ST15102, ST15110, ST8437, ST847, ST9734, ST9735, ST9736, ST9759, ST983, ST15450, ST15451, ST15458, ST15459, ST15461, ST156, ST172, ST2174, ST2754, ST320, ST344, ST373, ST4194, and ST5103. The most prevalent MDR clone identified was ST802, with a prevalence of 18.5% (0.0-47.8), followed by ST15111 (12.3% (0.0-29.4)) and ST15448 (4.8% (0.0-11.2)) (Fig. 5; Table 1).

Five studies (20%) [36, 38, 44, 46, 47] identified MDR clones in S. aureus. Eleven MDR clones of S. aureus were included in the meta-analysis: ST121, ST1, ST6, ST508, ST5, ST30, ST9, ST88, ST8, ST152, and ST15. These MDR clones were excluded from the meta-analysis: ST72, ST789, ST3249, ST45, ST18, ST2021, ST239, ST250, ST133, ST101, ST97, and ST80. The most prevalent MDR clone identified was ST152, with a prevalence of 27.3% (16.6–38.0), followed by ST121 (21.3% (13.4–29.3)), ST9 (14.3% (0.0-38.2)), and ST5 (8.5% (3.4–13.6)). The prevalence of MDR clones ST1, ST6, ST508, ST30, ST88, ST8, and ST15 ranged from 2.4% (0.0-5.3) to 7.8% (1.3–14.2) (Fig. 6; Table 1).

One study (4%) identified MDR clones in Neisseria gonorrhoea [40]. The only MDR clone in Neisseria gonorrhoea identified was ST211. Of the four MDR Neisseria gonorrhoea isolates, the clone ST211 was found in one (25%).

One study (4%) identified MDR clones in P. aeruginosa [48]. The only MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolate was ST234. Of the four MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates, ST234 was found in two (50%).

Two studies (8%) identified MDR clones in Salmonella spp [37, 49]. Three MDR clones of Salmonella spp. were identified: ST1954, ST11, and ST321. None of these clones were identified in both studies; therefore, a meta-analysis was not possible. Of the eight MDR Salmonella isolates, ST11 was found in 2 (25%) and ST1954 was found in 6 (75%). Similarly, of the 19 MDR Salmonella enterica isolates, ST321 was identified in 10 (53%).

One study identified MDR clones in Enterococcus spp [41]. Twenty-five MDR clones in Enterococcus spp.: ST32, ST1052, ST1295, ST1297, ST16, ST81, ST1306, ST245, ST300, ST4, ST480, ST86, ST1442, ST2269, ST2237, ST94, ST12, ST2268, ST1216, ST158, ST1939, ST1980, ST32, ST361, and ST416. For MDR Enterococcus faecalis, of the 24 isolates, 6 (25%) were ST32 and 3 (12.5%) were ST1295 and ST1052. Similarly, for MDR Enterococcus faecium, of the 15 isolates, 2 (13.3%) were ST2269 and ST1442.

A study identified MDR clones of Acinetobacter baumannii [50]. Three MDR clones, ST2145, ST2146, and ST231, were identified in Acinetobacter baumannii. The most predominant clone was ST231, which was identified in 17 (77.3%) of the 22 isolates, followed by ST2145 in 3 (13.6%) and ST2146 in 2 (9.1%).

Quality assessment

The methodological qualities of the 25 included studies were assessed. Overall, 22 studies (88%) were deemed high-quality based on scoring ≥ 6 points on the NOS or a low risk of bias in ≥ 50% quality domains assessed. The remaining 3 (12%) studies were of moderate quality. No studies were at high risk of bias (Table S2).

Discussion

This systematic review collated the available data on MDR bacterial clones from human, animal, and environmental sources in Ghana.

We identified Sequence Type 155 (ST155) as a predominant MDR E. coli clone across human, animal, and environmental sources in Ghana, with a prevalence of 38.0%. E. coli ST155 is a high-risk clone due to its ability to persist and spread in various environments, contributing to the challenge of managing antibiotic resistance. Known for its resistance potential, the ST155 clone has successfully spread intercontinentally via mobile resistance elements such as the blaCTX-M-15 extended-spectrum β-lactamase gene and has become a dominant cause of community- and healthcare-associated infections worldwide [51]. Studies in Nigeria [52], Madagascar [53], and Tunisia [54] have also reported ST155 as a prevalent MDR lineage of E. coli.

E. coli ST155 has attained enhanced virulence and resilience within the unique socioeconomic context and antimicrobial pressure in Ghana, underscoring the need for enhanced methods to kerb further community transmission. ST617 is another clinically significant high-risk international clone of E. coli associated with blaCTX-M-15 carriage that poses an escalating global threat [55]. The 29.1% prevalence of ST617 in E. coli observed in our review indicates that it has also attained substantial propagation, likely due to similar phenotypic advantages as ST155. Our findings resonate with evidence from Tanzania, where ST617 ranked among the foremost MDR lineages of E. coli isolated from domestic farm animals [56], and Tunisia, where ST617 was identified in E. coli isolated from community patients with urinary tract infections [57].

Although the ST10 of E. coli has a more localised distribution compared to pandemic lineages like ST155, it still represents an established high-risk clone. For instance, ST10 comprises 11% of generic E. coli from food-producing animals and products of animal origin in South Africa [58]. A study in North Africa also found that E. coli isolated from sheep and goats, and harbouring ST10, carried the mcr-1 gene [59]. However, more studies are required to perform a detailed genomic comparison of E. coli lineages in Ghana to discern the resistance and virulence attributes that contribute to their sustained transmission success. Identifying adaptations that allow these major clones to thrive in Ghana will improve targeted control.

In Klebsiella pneumoniae, ST152 and ST17 had similarly high pooled prevalences of 13.7% in humans and appear to have independently disseminated and become established in Ghana. K. pneumoniae ST152 is a globally-disseminated high-risk clone known for its hypervirulence and extensive array of resistance genes, which have been implicated in numerous outbreaks worldwide. For instance, K. pneumoniae ST152 was involved in a hospital outbreak in southern Spain from 2017 to 2019 [60]. Moreover, a study in China showed that the ST152 clone of K. pneumoniae was significantly associated with carbapenem resistance, ranking among the major high-risk lineages necessitating infection control prioritisation [61]. Similarly, K. pneumoniae ST17 is a clinically significant clone that causes MDR hospital infection worldwide [62]. The high prevalence of this clone observed in this review highlights the potential influence of healthcare practices and antibiotic usage patterns, thus supporting its propagation. Hence, stringent infection control measures within healthcare facilities, includes rigorous sterilisation processes, proper hand hygiene and isolation protocols, are crucial to prevent the spread of highly resistant strains.

For S. pneumoniae, ST802 was dominant, with a prevalence of 18.5% in humans. ST802 is a globally established multidrug-resistant serotype 19 A clone that significantly contributes to antibiotic-resistant invasive pneumococcal disease [63]. The persistence of ST802 despite widespread PCV use in Ghana highlights the challenge of serotype replacement, where non-vaccine serotypes fill the ecological niche left by vaccine-targeted serotypes. This finding underscores the importance of continuous surveillance and updating vaccination strategies to address emerging and resistant strains. Expanding diagnostic approaches, including serotyping, antimicrobial susceptibility testing and WGS across enlarged population cohorts, will be key to optimising prevention given ongoing epidemiological changes from immunisation.

In S. aureus, ST152 predominated with a prevalence of 27.3% in human and animal sources, whereas ST121 had a prevalence of 21.3% in human sources. ST152 is a livestock-associated methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) clone causing human infections, suggesting possible animal origin and zoonotic transmission in Ghana. In a study of MRSA strains isolated from nasal samples of livestock in Algeria, ST152 was the most frequently recovered strain [64]. Moreover, a systematic review found that the prevalence of ST152-MRSA has increased in Africa [63]. This increase has been particularly notable in regions such as the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa [65]. The infections caused by ST152-MRSA are often severe, including skin and soft tissue infections, bloodstream infections, and pneumonia [66]. The host populations affected by this strain include both hospitalised patients and those in the community, indicating its ability to spread in diverse settings [66]. MRSA can be transmitted from animals to humans through direct contact with infected animals or contaminated items. People who work closely with animals, like veterinarians and farm workers, are at a higher risk of contracting MRSA [67], necessitating “One Health” investigations. Conversely, ST121 is a globally disseminated community MRSA lineage associated with healthcare infections [68]. ST121 is present in various regions of Africa, indicating its widespread dissemination [65]. The dominance of these resilient MRSA strains indicates an urgent need for strengthened antimicrobial stewardship and infection prevention programmes in Ghanaian hospitals and livestock systems to prevent kerb spread.

In contrast to E. coli, K. pneumoniae, S. pneumoniae and S. aureus, limited data are available on the clones of Salmonella, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter and other critical pathogens recovered from humans, animals, and environmental sources in Ghana. This knowledge gap precludes the assessment of resistance epidemiology, clinical or zoonotic risks, and inter-species transmission dynamics to optimise control strategies. Future surveillance should employ standardised WGS-based diagnostics across expanded sample sources, including animal husbandry and food systems, to bridge this significant evidence gap.

This review constitutes the first comprehensive collation of available evidence on MDR clones of priority pathogens recovered in Ghana. The identification of ST155, ST617, ST152, and ST121 as dominant regional clones emphasises the need for strengthened public health measures to interrupt the transmission of these resilient strains. Urgent interventions such as optimised antibiotic prescribing, infection prevention and veterinary oversight are crucial in Ghana to stem further resistance proliferation facilitated by these high-risk lineages.

The continued application of standardised WGS-based approaches across expanded sources is warranted for real-time genomic-informed resistance surveillance to optimise cost-effective control policies. WGS offers several specific advantages, including its ability to trace resistance transmission pathways and better understand the epidemiological dynamics [69]. By providing high-resolution data on circulating genes and phylogenetic characterisation, WGS enables precise outbreak detection and retrospective analysis [69]. This detailed genetic information aids in tracking the spread of AMR and understanding the evolution and population dynamics of resistant strains [70]. Moreover, cross-sectoral cooperation, including regional collaborations, would support the broader characterisation of resistance epidemiology and enhance regional containment. This baseline characterisation provides a platform for the ongoing monitoring of clone dynamics to evaluate the impact of interventions and guide future mitigation strategies sustaining antibiotic access in Ghana and beyond.

Limitations

While this review provides valuable insights, certain limitations must be acknowledged to aid interpretation and guide future research. First, only published literature was included, which risks publication bias. Regarding methodology, most studies employed traditional molecular typing methods like MLST rather than WGS, which restricted detailed genomic insights. WGS provided an in-depth analysis of the factors driving the transmission success of the prevalent clones. Furthermore, the included studies predominantly examined bacterial isolates from clinical settings without consideration of carriage reservoirs or environmental dissemination routes, precluding a national holistic understanding of resistance circulation dynamics. Finally, this review does not imply a direct causal relationship between the MDR clones and the clinical outcomes. Further epidemiological and functional investigations are required to verify the invasiveness and transmission potential of the dominant lineages.

Conclusions

This systematic review collates the most comprehensive data available to date on prevalent MDR bacterial clones of priority pathogens isolated in Ghana. High-risk clones, including ST155 and ST617 in E. coli and ST152 in K. pneumoniae and S. aureus, highlight urgent public health priorities. Continued application of standardised WGS-based surveillance across expanded sources is needed to comprehensively track changing resistance dynamics, understand the risk factors influencing MDR dissemination and optimise prevention over the long term. Close regional cooperation would enhance continental knowledge exchange and containment efforts against high-risk clones that transcend borders. Sustained monitoring of MDR lineages tailored to the Ghanaian context will guide life-saving strategies to ensure access to effective antibiotics for current and future generations.

Recommendations

Based on the current evidence and identified limitations, the following recommendations are made to strengthen the surveillance of MDR bacterial clones in Ghana:

Establish a national surveillance programme employing standardised whole-genome sequencing-based diagnostic approaches for comprehensive, real-time genomic tracking of changing resistance dynamics.
