Bioequivalence of Two Firsekibart Subcutaneous Formulations: A Randomized, Double‐Blind, Single‐Dose, Parallel‐Group Study in Healthy Chinese Participants
Authors: Yehua Xie, Xiao Li, Yushi Chen, Guangli Ma, Fei Gu, Yuling Lian, Xu Zhang, Xiaolan Yong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe evaluated the pharmacokinetic (PK) bioequivalence and safety of a new formulation of firsekibart (solution for injection) compared with firsekibart lyophilized powder for solution. In this randomized, double‐blind, parallel‐group study, healthy Chinese adult males received a single subcutaneous dose of 200 mg firsekibart solution for injection (test product, T) or firsekibart powder for solution (reference product, R), followed through Day 141. The primary objective was to evaluate PK bioequivalence between T and R, concluded if least‐squares geometric mean ratio (GMR) 90% confidence intervals (CIs) were within 80.00%–125.00%. Safety was also evaluated. A total of 181 participants received T (N = 90) or R (N = 91). PK profiles were comparable between T and R: least‐squares GMRs (T/R; 90% CI) for the maximum serum concentration, area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) from time zero to the last time of quantifiable concentration, and AUC from time 0 to infinity were 104.26% (98.11%, 110.80%), 103.45% (98.15%, 109.04%), and 102.14% (96.75%, 107.83%), respectively. Most treatment‐emergent adverse events were mild/moderate and generally comparable between T and R, with no treatment‐related serious adverse events reported. Firsekibart solution for injection was bioequivalent to firsekibart lyophilized powder for solution for injection in healthy Chinese males, with no new safety signals identified.Keywords: bioequivalence, firsekibart, interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), pharmacokinetics, safety

Gout is a painful crystal‐related arthropathy caused by the deposition of monosodium urate (MSU) crystals in the joints, commonly associated with cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic comorbidities, and is the most common inflammatory arthritis in adults worldwide. 1 , 2 The global prevalence of gout is projected to increase by more than 70% between 2020 and 2050, 3 with a notable burden in China, where gout incidence rose from 1.18 million cases in 1990 to 3.08 million in 2021. 4

Gout is typically classified into four phases: asymptomatic hyperuricemia, acute gout flares, intercritical period, and chronic gouty arthritis. 1 The primary goals of gout management are to treat and prevent gout flares, and to lower serum urate levels, thereby minimizing joint damage. 1 Urate‐lowering therapy (ULT) is recommended for long‐term treatment, with oral non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and colchicine and systemic glucocorticoids recommended for treatment of acute gout flares. 5 , 6 , 7 However, a substantial proportion of patients are intolerant or contraindicated to these treatment options. 8 , 9

With the pro‐inflammatory cytokine interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) recognized as a key driver of gout, 9 , 10 guidelines recommend treatment with IL‐1 inhibitors for patients intolerant, contraindicated, or unresponsive to NSAIDs, colchicine, and corticosteroids. 5 , 7 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Canakinumab is the first and currently the only IL‐1β inhibitor approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treatment of gout flare. 15 In China, however, firsekibart (formerly genakumab), a fully human monoclonal anti‐IL‐1β antibody, is the only IL‐1β inhibitor approved for gouty arthritis treatment in adult patients for whom NSAIDs and/or colchicine are contraindicated, not tolerated, or ineffective, and for whom repeated courses of corticosteroids are not suitable. 16 , 17

In a Phase 3 trial that included patients with frequent acute gout flares, firsekibart demonstrated non‐inferiority in pain relief at 72 h and superiority in flare prevention within 12 weeks when administered at 200 mg compared with compound betamethasone, with a favorable safety profile. 17 Two Phase 2 trials have also shown that firsekibart 200 mg is associated with superior prophylaxis against acute gout flares compared with colchicine in patients initiating ULT, 18 and superior relief of target joint pain compared with etoricoxib in patients with frequent acute gout flares; 19 firsekibart was well tolerated in both trials. GeneScience Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. has since developed a new liquid solution formulation of firsekibart (200 mg/vial), distinct from the lyophilized powder formulation (150 mg/vial) used in clinical trials to date. Compared with the powder for solution, firsekibart solution for injection eliminates the need for reconstitution and enables single‐dose administration, providing greater convenience in clinical practice.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the pharmacokinetic (PK) bioequivalence of firsekibart solution for injection compared with firsekibart powder for solution in healthy Chinese participants, with safety and immunogenicity also evaluated.

Methods

Study Design

This was a randomized, double‐blind, single‐dose, parallel‐group study conducted to evaluate the PK bioequivalence of firsekibart solution for injection (test product) and firsekibart lyophilized powder for solution (reference product) after a single subcutaneous injection in a homogeneous population of healthy Chinese male adults. After screening, participants were randomized 1:1 to receive a single 200 mg subcutaneous injection of test or reference product on Day 1. A double‐blind design was adopted to minimize potential bias in safety and tolerability assessments. Both the test and reference products were prepared using a standardized procedure prior to administration and administered using identical syringes to ensure consistency in dosing and facilitate blinding. Participants were admitted on Day −1, discharged on Day 6, and followed up until Day 141 (Figure 1A). Based on prior PK data, firsekibart has a median time to maximum serum concentration (Tmax) of 3.5–7.5 days and terminal elimination half‐life (t1/2) of 18.3–27.3 days after single subcutaneous administration. 20 Therefore, PK sampling was continued through Day 141 to correspond with approximately five drug half‐lives.

This study was conducted in compliance with the ethical principles originating in or derived from the Declaration of Helsinki and in compliance with all International Conference on Harmonization (ICH) Good Clinical Practice (GCP) Guidelines and regulatory requirements. All participants provided written informed consent prior to screening. The trial protocol and amendments were approved by the independent ethics committee of Chengdu Xinhua Hospital (Ethics Committee Review Drug No. 131 [2024]). The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06451848).

Study Population

Eligible participants were healthy male adults aged 18–45 years, with a body mass index (BMI) of 19.0–26.0 kg/m2 and body weight ≥50 kg. Participants were excluded if they had any known allergy to firsekibart or its components; an inability to tolerate intravenous puncture; abnormal or clinically significant findings in physical examinations, laboratory tests, and electrocardiograms (ECGs); hepatitis B, C, or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) antibodies; a history of neurological or psychiatric disorders; clinically significant systemic diseases; recent major surgery; vaccination; blood loss; use of biologics; or participation in another clinical trial. Full criteria for study inclusion and exclusion are detailed in the Supplemental Appendix.

A sample size of 178 participants (89 per group) was planned to provide 80% power to demonstrate bioequivalence within the 80.00%–125.00% equivalence margin at a significance level of 0.05, assuming a coefficient of variation of approximately 45.5%, a geometric mean ratio (GMR) of 1.05, and a 10% dropout rate.

Blood Sample Collection and Sample Assay

The bioanalytical method was developed and validated in accordance with applicable regulatory guidance, including ICH M10. Blood samples were collected within 2 h pre‐dose and at prespecified timepoints (Days 2–6, 8, 11, 13, 15, 22, 29, 43, 57, 71, 85, 113, and 141) to measure serum firsekibart concentrations using a validated sandwich enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), with a firsekibart‐specific antibody as the capture reagent and a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐labeled firsekibart‐specific antibody as the detection reagent. Optical density was measured at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 630 nm, and calibration curves were fitted using a four‐parameter logistic regression model. The validated calibration range was 195–25,000 ng/mL, with a lower limit of quantification of 195 ng/mL. The assay demonstrated acceptable performance across the validated range. Using calibration curves prepared from both the solution and lyophilized formulations, intra‐run accuracy ranged from −16.8% to 12.6%, intra‐run precision was ≤16.0%, inter‐run accuracy ranged from −7.8% to 0.5%, and inter‐run precision was ≤10.5%. Quality control samples at multiple concentration levels were included in each analytical run to ensure assay performance and reliability.

Blood samples for immunogenicity assessment were collected within 2 h prior to dosing and at prespecified post‐dose timepoints on Days 15, 29, 57, 85, and 141, as well as at the early termination visit, if applicable. Serum samples were analyzed for the presence of anti‐drug antibodies (ADAs) using a validated electrochemiluminescence (ECLU) ligand‐binding assay (ECLIA) on the MSD platform incorporating an acid dissociation (ACE) step. A multi‐tiered testing strategy was employed, including screening, confirmatory, and titer assays. The screening cut point (SCP) and confirmatory cut point (CCP) were established using a non‐parametric statistical approach based on treatment‐naïve samples. The SCP was defined as 1.17 × mean negative control (NC) ECLU signal, and CCP defined as 45.2% inhibition. The titer cut point (TCP) was defined as 1.33 × mean NC ECLU. The minimum required dilution (MRD) was established at 1:20. For confirmed ADA‐positive samples, with screening responses ≥TCP, antibody titers were determined using a serial dilution approach and calculated as the product of the MRD and the highest dilution factor at which the sample remained positive. For confirmed ADA‐positive samples with screening responses <TCP, the titer was reported as the MRD. The assay demonstrated a screening sensitivity of 7.81 ng/mL and a confirmatory sensitivity of 9.90 ng/mL, with drug tolerance of approximately 31.3–62.5 µg/mL at the low positive control level (100 ng/mL) and up to 500 µg/mL at the high positive control level (500 ng/mL).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the SAS v9.4 software.

Pharmacokinetics and Bioequivalence

PK parameters were calculated using a non‐compartmental analysis in Phoenix WinNonlin (v8.3 or later) based on actual sampling times.

The PK concentration set included all participants who received firsekibart and had at least one post‐dose concentration measurement. The PK parameter set included participants with evaluable PK parameters, and the bioequivalence set comprised those with at least one evaluable PK endpoint, which were the maximum serum concentration (Cmax), area under the concentration–time curve from time zero to the last time of quantifiable concentration (AUC0–t), and AUC from time zero to time infinity (AUC0–∞). The Tmax was compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. For Cmax, AUC0–t, and AUC0–∞, log‐transformed values were analyzed using a linear mixed‐effects model with treatment group as a fixed effect. GMRs (test/reference product) and their corresponding 90% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated and back transformed. Bioequivalence between the test and reference product could be determined and was concluded if the 90% CIs of the GMRs fell within 80.00%–125.00%.

Safety and Immunogenicity

The safety set included all participants who received firsekibart. Safety data, including adverse events (AEs), laboratory tests, vital signs, physical examinations, abdominal ultrasound, and 12‐lead ECGs, were summarized descriptively by treatment group. AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) v27.1 and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE v5.0).

The ADA set included participants with evaluable ADA results before and after dosing. ADA incidence was summarized descriptively by treatment group at each prespecified timepoint.

Results

Participant Demographics

Between June 24, 2024 and December 6, 2024, a total of 184 participants were randomized. Overall, 181 participants received either the test or reference product (90 and 91 participants, respectively), all of whom were included in the safety, PK concentration, PK parameter, and bioequivalence sets. A total of three randomized participants did not receive study drug and were therefore not included in any analysis set. After premature withdrawal of nine participants in each group, 83 in each group completed the study (Figure 1B). In the ADA set, 87 and 88 participants in the test and reference product groups, respectively, were included. Demographics and baseline characteristics were generally comparable between test and reference product groups, with a mean (standard deviation [SD]) age of 24.9 (5.65) and 24.7 (5.03) years, weight of 60.2 (4.85) and 60.5 (5.12) kg, and BMI of 20.9 (1.52) and 21.2 (1.67) kg/m2, respectively (Table 1).

Pharmacokinetics of Firsekibart

Mean serum concentration–time curves of the test and reference products are presented in Figure 2. The PK profiles of the test and reference products were similar, with an arithmetic mean (SD) Cmax of 31.8 (7.99) and 30.3 (6.81) µg/mL, respectively, AUC0–t of 31,763.5 (8,666.52) and 30,877.7 (7,417.77) h*µg/mL, and AUC0–∞ of 35,064.6 (8,459.63) and 33,943.9 (6,463.83) h*µg/mL, respectively (Table 2). The median (interquartile range [IQR]) Tmax was 7.0 (5.00, 10.00) and 7.0 (5.00, 10.04) days in the test and reference groups, respectively, with an arithmetic mean (SD) t1/2 of 31.0 (5.31) and 32.4 (5.72) days.

Safety

Safety results were generally comparable between test and reference product groups (Table 4). In total, 40.9% (74/181) of participants experienced treatment‐emergent AEs (TEAEs): 40.0% (36/90) of participants in the test product group, and 41.8% (38/91) in the reference product group. CTCAE grade ≥3 TEAEs were reported in 2.2% (4/181) of participants overall (1.1% [1/90] in the test product group; 3.3% [3/91] in the reference product group). Treatment‐related AEs (TRAEs) were experienced by 14.9% (27/181) of participants overall: 10.0% (9/90) and 19.8% (18/91) in the test and reference product groups, respectively. CTCAE grade ≥3 TRAEs were experienced by 1.1% (2/181) of participants overall, both in the reference product group. One participant overall (0.6%, in the test product group) experienced two TEAEs leading to study withdrawal: both events were SAEs (anemia and esophageal ulcer), considered unlikely related to treatment by the investigator, and were resolving or recovering after drug treatment. No other SAEs or TEAEs leading to study withdrawal occurred, and no participants experienced TEAEs leading to death.

In the test product group, common TEAEs (incidence of ≥5%) were blood uric acid increased (8.9%, 8/90), blood triglycerides increased (7.8%, 7/90), and monocyte count increased (5.6%, 5/90). No TRAEs occurred with an incidence of ≥5% in the test product group. In the reference product group, common TEAEs were blood triglycerides increased (13.2%, 12/91) and blood uric acid increased (9.9%, 9/91). Blood triglycerides increased (9.9%, 9/91) was the only TRAE occurring with an incidence of ≥5% in the reference product group.

There were no clinically significant differences in hematology, blood chemistry, urinalysis parameters, vital signs, or physical examinations, between test and reference product groups.

Immunogenicity

In the test product group, 19.5% (17/87) of participants were ADA‐positive overall: 8.0% (7/87) tested positive for ADAs pre‐dose, and 13.8% (12/87) post‐dose (Table S1). In the reference product group, 19.3% (17/88) of participants were ADA‐positive overall: 2.3% (2/88) tested positive pre‐dose, and 19.3% (17/88) post‐dose.

Discussion

In this study, the PK characteristics and safety profiles of firsekibart solution for injection and firsekibart powder for solution displayed similarity, with the least‐squares GMRs (test product/reference product) and their corresponding 90% CIs for Cmax, AUC0–t, and AUC0–∞ falling within the acceptance range for bioequivalence and showing PK bioequivalence of the two formulations. Both formulations were well tolerated, with PK and safety results consistent with those reported previously, 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 and no new safety signals identified.

Since currently recommended treatment options for acute gout flares are often ineffective, contraindicated, or associated with adverse events, 5 , 7 , 14 , 21 alternative treatment options are needed. Firsekibart was developed as a well‐tolerated injectable option for patients, and is effective in providing rapid pain relief and in prevention of recurrent flares when administered at 200 mg in patients with gout and at least two gout flares in the preceding year. 17 , 18 , 19 In the present study, we evaluated the PK bioequivalence and safety of firsekibart solution for injection, a newly developed formulation, and found that the two formulations demonstrated PK bioequivalence, with parameters consistent with those reported previously. 20 Firsekibart solution for injection therefore provides patients and clinicians with a stable and convenient treatment alternative to firsekibart for injection that does not require reconstitution, available in a 200 mg vial for immediate use.

Safety results for both formulations of firsekibart were in line with previous studies of firsekibart for injection. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 The incidence of TEAEs was comparable between the test and reference product groups, with numerically fewer TRAEs and grade ≥3 TEAEs in the test product group. In the test product group, although one participant experienced SAEs leading to the study withdrawal, these events were considered unrelated to firsekibart, recovered/resolved with concomitant medication, and were not attributed to intolerance or safety concerns. Blood triglycerides increased and blood uric acid increased were the most common TEAEs across both groups, with blood triglycerides increased (6.1% overall) the only common TRAE (incidence ≥5%). Both common TEAEs were consistent with epidemiological data among Chinese adults; the incidence of blood triglycerides increased (10.5% overall) was broadly consistent with the estimated prevalence of hypertriglyceridemia in China (13.8%–15.4%), 22 , 23 while the incidence of blood uric acid increased (9.4% overall) was comparable with national estimates in 2018–2019 (14.0%). 24 Overall, these data indicate that the safety profiles of firsekibart injection and firsekibart for injection are similar.

Study limitations were the inclusion of only participants from a single Chinese center, which may limit extrapolation of these results to the wider population, across demographics and geographical locations. The present study enrolled exclusively male participants to reduce variability associated with sex‐related physiological differences, and to ensure a more homogeneous study population; however, it remains unknown whether the PK profile of firsekibart is affected by participant sex. While the potential impact of sex cannot be fully assessed based on the present study alone, additional evidence is being generated to further evaluate this aspect, with previous clinical data including both male and female participants, 20 and a population PK analysis incorporating a broader dataset of male and female participants recently completed.

Conclusions

A single subcutaneous 200 mg dose of firsekibart injection demonstrated PK bioequivalence with firsekibart for injection among healthy Chinese male participants, with comparable, favorable safety profiles and no new safety signals identified.
