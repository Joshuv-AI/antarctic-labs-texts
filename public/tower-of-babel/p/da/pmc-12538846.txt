Mycobacterium avium-complex infection mimicking lymphoma in a miniature schnauzer
Authors: Olga Szaluś-Jordanow, Andrzej Łobaczewski, Agata Moroz-Fik, Marcin Mickiewicz, Rafał Sapierzyński, Kinga Biernacka, Tomasz Nalbert, Ewa Augustynowicz-Kopeć, Anna Zabost, Tadeusz Frymus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis case report describes an uncommon Mycobacterium avium infection mimicking lymphoma in a dog.Case presentationA 1.5-year-old female Miniature Schnauzer presented with persistent lymphadenopathy, fever, and diarrhea, which were refractory to conventional therapy. Hematological and biochemical analyses revealed anemia, thrombocytopenia, elevated liver enzymes, creatine kinase, glutamate dehydrogenase, lactate dehydrogenase, hyperkalemia, low IgG levels, and euthyroid sick syndrome. Ultrasonography confirmed generalized visceral, retroperitoneal, and peripheral lymphadenopathy with concurrent inflammatory reactions, splenomegaly, and hepatomegaly characterized by heterogeneous parenchyma and multiple foci, further supporting the suspicion of lymphoma. However, cytological examination of an enlarged lymph node showed no evidence of lymphoma but revealed the presence of numerous bacilli suggestive of mycobacterial infection. Histopathological evaluation of a surgically excised lymph node similarly failed to confirm lymphoma. Mycobacterium avium was successfully cultured from the lymph node tissue and identified via the GenoType Mycobacterium CM test. Based on antimicrobial susceptibility testing, the initial therapeutic regimen of enrofloxacin, azithromycin, and rifampicin was modified to clarithromycin. Adjunctive treatments included blood transfusion, levothyroxine, prednisolone, omeprazole, sucralfate, parapoxvirus ovis, and soy phospholipids. Despite a temporary clinical improvement, the patient’s condition progressively worsened, and the dog succumbed to the disease after three months.ConclusionsThis case underscores the importance of including Mycobacterium avium in the differential diagnosis of generalized lymphadenopathy, particularly in Miniature Schnauzers. The findings support the routine use of Ziehl-Neelsen staining during cytological examinations to prevent misdiagnosis, especially in breeds predisposed to atypical infections. It also emphasizes the crucial role of targeted antimicrobial therapy in managing such cases, although the prognosis remains guarded.

Background

Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) bacteria are part of a broader group of nontuberculous mycobacterial species, including M. avium and its four subspecies, M. intracellulare, and several other species such as M. colombiense, M. chimaera, and M. marseillense. The MAC infections are common in wild and domestic avian and mammalian species [1]. Most of these infections seem to be subclinical. In Poland, tissues of 192 free-living carnivorous animals were tested, and MAC bacilli were found in 32 mammals (mostly red foxes, some grey wolves, badgers, and a pine marten) and one bird (common buzzard). Except for the buzzard, pathological lesions were not found in any of the positive tissues [1]. However, MAC are opportunistic pathogens capable of inducing disease not only in birds but also in mammals, including humans, especially in immunocompromised hosts [2–4].

In dogs, single cases of MAC-induced diseases have been described so far [5–8]. Canine cases are rare, but the true prevalence of the infection can be underdiagnosed. Recent studies have revealed mycobacterial infections in almost 10% of dogs with lymphadenitis [9]. Misdiagnosis seems possible, as MAC infections in dogs and cats are usually chronic diseases with nonspecific signs that may involve multiple organs, thereby complicating the diagnosis. The symptoms include weight loss, lethargy, fever, gastrointestinal issues, splenomegaly, and lymphadenopathy [10]. In some patients, anemia occurs, ranging from mild to severe. Less common signs of MAC infections are spinal pain, lameness, paresis, subcutaneous swellings, and diffuse alopecia [3, 11]. Imaging studies can contribute to misdiagnosis because mycobacterial lesions tend to be interpreted as neoplasia [12–16]. Due to these reasons, diagnosing a MAC-induced disease in small animals, especially differentiation from neoplasia, remains challenging. To alert the practitioners to this potential diagnostic pitfall, we present a clinical case of canine MAC infection mimicking lymphoma in the clinical and ultrasound examination.

Case presentation

A 1.5-year-old, 6.5 kg weight, female Miniature Schnauzer had shown persistent fever, lymphadenopathy, and diarrhea for several weeks, hardly responsive to medication with antibiotics in a primary care veterinary practice. No detailed medical records regarding this treatment were available. A previous veterinarian confirmed by ultrasonography generalized lymphadenopathy involving not only superficial but also mesenteric lymph nodes. Lymphoma was suspected, and an oncological consultation was recommended. The consultation was conducted in a specialized oncology center to which the dog was referred. During this consultation, the lymphoma suspect was supported, and a fine-needle biopsy of an enlarged lymph node was performed. Chemotherapy was started in the exact center without waiting for cytological results, as treatment was introduced based on the characteristic clinical picture, and the lack of improvement after several weeks of prior antibiotic therapy. The dog felt very unwell after the initiation of chemotherapy. The cytological results obtained a few days later did not reveal lymphoma. In contrast, numerous epithelioid macrophages and some multinucleated giant cells with voluminous lace cytoplasm were present, accompanied by small lymphocytes and plasma cells (Fig. 1A). At 400x magnification, non-stained bacilli in the cytoplasm of the macrophages were seen, strongly indicating mycobacteria (Fig. 1B). The presence of Mycobacterium sp. was confirmed after Ziehl-Neelsen staining (Fig. 1C). Based on the large number of mycobacteria in the cytoplasm of the macrophages, and the lack of respiratory signs and other indications of tuberculosis, atypical mycobacteriosis was suspected. In the same oncology clinic, chemotherapy was withdrawn and replaced by the following treatment: prednisolone (Dermipred, Ceva Santé Animale, France) 1.4 mg/kg p.o. SID; enrofloxacin (Enrotron, aniMedica GmbH, Germany) 6 mg/kg p.o. SID, azithromycin (Sumamed, Teva, Poland) 6.5 mg/kg p.o. SID and parapoxvirus ovis 1 ml s.c. At this time, the owner was referred to our clinic’s department of infectious diseases for further treatment. Following the change of therapy, the dog’s condition improved, the feces became more formed, and appetite partially returned. Due to a lack of access to the abdominal ultrasound results from the previous veterinarian, the examination was repeated using a GE Logiq F (General Electric, Massachusetts, USA) ultrasound machine with a 10 − 6 MHz microconvex transducer. Generalized visceral, retroperitoneal, and peripheral lymphadenopathy was confirmed. The medial iliac lymph nodes were 12–16 mm in size, the lumbar aortic ones 9–16 mm, the splenic and mesenteric nodes 12–15 mm, the hepatic ones 13–17 mm, often in conglomerates or lobular formations. There was an inflammatory reaction around the lymph nodes and increased echogenicity of the mesentery. The nodes were predominantly hypoechoic, ranging from homogeneous to heterogeneous, rounded, with visible vascularization in the larger ones. The superficial lymph nodes had a similar structure, and the largest were the right submandibular and right retropharyngeal nodes (16 × 45 mm in size); the right popliteal lymph node (7.5 × 15.2 mm), and the inguinal lymph nodes (9–11 × 29 mm). The spleen was also enlarged, approximately 22 mm in thickness, elongated, with markedly heterogeneous parenchyma and numerous small (0.5–4 mm) focal lesions, predominantly hypoechoic, presenting a mottled pattern (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Additionally, there was band-like increased echogenicity of the spleen between the lesions. The hilar venous vessels were slightly dilated. The splenic capsule was irregular and thickened, surrounded by an inflammatory reaction (Fig. 5). The liver was moderately enlarged, with elongated lobes and rounded edges. The surface of the hepatic capsule in the accessible area was regular. The hepatic parenchyma was moderately heterogeneous, with unevenly increased echogenicity (Fig. 6). As generalized lymphadenopathy has high sensitivity to detect malignancy [17]– [18], what was confirmed by 20 years of ultrasound experience in our clinic (AŁ), the obtained scans strongly suggested lymphoma, what was also suspected by the previous ultrasound examiner in another clinic.

To exclude the coexistence of lymphoma and mycobacterial infection, the left mandibular lymph node was surgically removed for microbiological and histopathological examination. During this anesthesia, a tracheal swab was also collected, as well as a urine sample via puncture of the bladder. The PCR was performed as described earlier [19], and did not reveal the presence of M. tuberculosis complex DNA in the lymph node, tracheal swab, or urine. From the material obtained, direct smears, cultures on Löwenstein-Jensen (L-J) medium, and liquid cultures on Bactec MGIT medium (Becton Dickinson, Sparks, New Jersey, USA) were prepared. Bacterial growth was observed in the liquid medium after five days (urine, tracheal swab) and eight days (lymph node). Molecular species identification using the GenoType Mycobacterium CM test (HAIN Lifescience, Nehren, Germany) revealed that the isolates were M. avium (Fig. 7) Histopathological examination of the lymph node stained with hematoxylin-eosin and Ziehl-Neelsen method confirmed presence of mycobacteria, but no lymphoma lesions were detected (Fig. 8).

After having the final diagnosis of Mycobacterium avium-complex infection, enrofloxacin was replaced by rifampicin at a dose of 10 mg/kg p.o. SID. In addition, an attempt was made to discontinue prednisolone medication, as glucocorticoids are generally contraindicated during bacterial infections. The prednisolone dose was gradually decreased over a week. However, the dog’s condition rapidly deteriorated - the dog could hardly get up and had a completely lost appetite. As some publications indicated that corticosteroids inhibited Mycobacterium tuberculosis-induced cell death [20]– [21], prednisolone was reintroduced into the treatment regimen at the previous doses. The dog’s condition quickly improved and, for a month, remained stable. The dog had a normal appetite and started going for short walks. One month later, the antibiotic sensitivity results of the M. avium isolate were available. The isolate was sensitive to clarithromycin and amikacin, moderately sensitive to linezolid, and resistant to moxifloxacin and rifampicin. Therefore, rifampicin and azithromycin were discontinued, and clarithromycin (Klacid, Aprilia, Italy) was introduced at a dose of 10 mg/kg every second day. The dog’s condition improved, and it took longer walks. However, the improvement lasted only about two weeks, and then the condition deteriorated again. Blood tests were performed four times during the entire treatment process (approximately 3 months). Anemia, thrombocytopenia, elevated activity of liver enzymes and creatine kinase, glutamate dehydrogenase, lactate dehydrogenase, and high potassium levels were observed. The complete test results of three examinations, along with their temporal reference to the biopsy findings, are presented in Table 1. In the second blood test, the immunoglobulin G concentration was also determined. The result was 410 mg/dL, (reference range: 500–1700 mg/dL). Finally, the dog developed severe anemia, and in the fourth blood examination, the results were as follows: the total red blood cell (RBC) was 0,25 (reference range 3.04–3.39 × 10^12/L), the hematocrit (Ht) was unmeasurable. Therefore, 350 ml of whole blood was administered, and after the transfusion the RBC level rose to 3,5 mln/µl and Ht was 30,4%. The patient also exhibited apathy and reluctance to move. To exclude concurrent Addison’s disease, the ACTH stimulation test was performed, and the concentration of thyroid hormones was measured. The cortisol level before stimulation was 161 nmol/L, after two hours 290 nmol/L (reference range: 27.6-165.5). Addison’s disease was ruled out, but euthyroid sick syndrome was suspected due to the following thyroid hormone results: total thyroxine 12.8 µg/dl (reference range: 15–40), and fT4 1.3 ng/dl (reference range: 1.4-4). Thus, levotiroxine administration 10 µg/kg body weight p.o. SID (Forthyron, Bladel, Netherlands) was started.

Over one month, episodes of exacerbation marked by significant lameness and shortened stride, as well as noticeable pain in the spine and limbs were noticed. Recurrent diarrhea and vomiting were also observed. Omeprazole (Helicid, Zentiva, Czech Republik) 1 mg/kg p.o. SID and sucralfate (Ulgastran, Gedeon Richter, Poland) 100 mg/kg p.o. TID, soy phospholipids (Essentiale forte, Opella Healthcare, Warsaw, Poland) 25 mg/kg p.o. BID was also added to the therapy. In the follow-up clinical and ultrasound examinations, both superficial and abdominal lymph nodes remained enlarged, and the previously found changes in the internal organs intensified. During the entire treatment period, the patient was closely monitored for signs of adverse drug reactions. The decision to modify the treatment was based on the susceptibility profile obtained one month after initiating therapy, but also guided by clinical signs of poor drug tolerance. The treatment protocol was continuously adapted based on both laboratory parameters and clinical condition, emphasizing the importance of individualized patient monitoring when administering multi-drug therapy for systemic infections. Three months after the diagnosis of M. avium infection, the dog died.

Discussion

The presented case shows diagnostic problems encountered in small animals suffering from mycobacterial infections. These are typically chronic, debilitating conditions, producing lesions that clinically and even on diagnostic imaging can be interpreted as neoplasia. We described earlier an unusual case of M. tuberculosis infection in a dog with an intracardiac tuberculoma, where a heart tumor was wrongly suspected ante mortem. In this patient, tuberculosis (TB) remained misdiagnosed despite several visits to different veterinarians over a year [22]. In most well-developed countries, canine TB is believed to be extremely rare and therefore is hardly considered in differential diagnostics. As a result, cases most probably remain undiagnosed. A similar situation is in human medicine, where TB infections were diagnosed as a neoplasm not only based on clinical presentation and imaging studies but even during intraoperative pathology studies [23]– [24]. The TB is considered by some experts as a “master of disguise” as lesions in many organs, including the lungs, mimic neoplasia [25–27]. This appears to be similar during MAC infections in both humans and animals, as they tend to be misdiagnosed as various types of malignancies, including lymphoma [28–30], as it was with the dog described in the present publication. In some cases, like in our patient, to exclude malignancy, it is sufficient to detect acid-fast bacteria during the cytological examination. However, this method has relatively low sensitivity, and the specificity is also not very high. These values vary depending on the strain of mycobacteria and the material tested. For example, in pulmonary tuberculosis in humans the ZN staining had a sensitivity of 70% and a specificity of 97.1% [31].

Culturing of the collected specimen on Löwenstein-Jensen (L-J) medium and in Bactec MGIT liquid culture is more sensitive, but the growth of mycobacteria on these media is observed within several to a dozen days. The TB should be always excluded, but considering the potential zoonotic risk also from non-tuberculous mycobacteria, and in order to define the treatment options, the species of the found mycobacteria should be determined by molecular methods like the FluoroType MTBDR test with further species identification using the GenoType Mycobacterium CM assay or other techniques such as PCR or MALDI-TOF [3].

Generalized immune disorders are well-known risk factors for mycobacteriosis. In humans, these are primarily the result of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, immunosuppressive therapy, cancer, alcoholism, as well as congenital disorders [32]– [33]. Several reports showed a breed predisposition of Miniature Schnauzers and Basset Hounds for mycobacteriosis [5, 7]– [8, 34]. Among 43 published canine MAC cases, 16 were Miniature Schnauzers, and 10 were Basset Hounds, which means that over 60% of all published cases were in these breeds [3]. Our patient exhibited decreased IgG plasma levels, which are indicative of impaired immune function and suggest a potential reduction in immune defense mechanisms. One could speculate that there is also an age predisposition, as most of the affected dogs developed clinical signs within the first three years of age, and only rarely they were older than five years [3]. This would mean that our patient, 1.5-year-old Miniature Schnauzer, was a typical case of canine MAC-induced disease. Also typical is unsuccessful treatment of systemic mycobacteriosis in Miniature Schnauzers and Basset Hounds [3]. The mechanism of the genetic predisposition for mycobacteriosis of these breeds is unknown. In Miniature Schnauzers, a single variant in the caspase recruitment domain-containing protein 9 (CARD9) gene has been recently found [3]. CARD9 is a multi-functional signaling protein essential in innate immunity against fungi and bacteria, and is considered to play a crucial role in the host defense against M. tuberculosis [35]. Among of the offspring of one dog living in the late 1980 s, over one hundred Miniature Schnauzers developed mycobacteriosis over the past 30 years. In those of them that were homozygous for the CARD9 variant gene, M. avium-induced disease was always systemic. Therefore, genetic screening of Miniature Schnauzers used in breeding programs has been suggested to reduce the number of MAC-susceptible individuals [3].

Abnormalities in the blood test results observed in our patient were consistent with those observed in human medicine. Anemia was seen in all successive tests, which is characteristic for chronic diseases, as a typical response to prolonged infections, common in humans affected by mycobacteria. Leukocytosis with pronounced neutrophilia indicates the systemic immune response to a chronic infection. Neutrophils are the primary responders to bacterial infections [36–38]. Monocytosis suggests chronicity and ongoing recruitment of macrophages to sites of inflammation. Elevated activity of liver enzymes (ALT, ALP, GLDH) reflects hepatic involvement, which can occur in mycobacterial infections, leading to granulomatous inflammation in the liver. On the other hand, the patient was treated for a few months with several medications, including steroids, which could also affect the liver. The rise in bilirubin concentration could result either from hepatic dysfunction or hemolysis, contributing further to the anemia. Based on the results of blood tests, hepatoprotective agents and supportive therapy for anemia, including blood transfusion, were introduced into the treatment regimen.

Most published attempts to treat systemic, progressive MAC-mycobacteriosis in dogs were unsuccessful regardless of the therapeutic protocol [3, 10, 34, 39]. Localized cutaneous MAC infections such as pyogranulomatous dermatitis and panniculitis with or without systemic clinical signs can respond to prolonged (weeks to months) antimicrobial therapy, but only in dogs other than Miniature Schnauzers or Basset Hounds [3, 40–43]. As the isolation of mycobacteria and susceptibility testing are usually time-consuming, empiric antibacterial therapy should be started before obtaining the drug resistance results. A combination of rifampicin and clarithromycin, combined with new-generation fluoroquinolones, like moxifloxacin or pradofloxacin, can be an option for this [43]– [44]. In contrast, doxycycline, macrolide, or fluoroquinolone as monotherapy is usually ineffective, even in immunocompetent dogs [3]. Although the exact cause of death remains unknown due to the lack of a necropsy, the clinical course strongly suggests progressive, treatment-refractory mycobacterial disease. Over time, generalized lymphadenopathy continued to worsen, both in superficial nodes accessible to palpation and in abdominal lymph nodes confirmed on follow-up ultrasonography. Histopathological examination of the excised lymph node revealed massive infiltration by acid-fast bacilli, consistent with an advanced mycobacterial infection. Considering the progression of clinical signs despite antimicrobial therapy, and in light of published reports describing similarly aggressive and fatal courses in Miniature Schnauzers infected with M. avium, it is reasonable to assume that the animal’s death was primarily the result of disseminated, therapy-resistant infection rather than adverse drug effects.

The time, source, and route of infection in the presented case remain unknown. This is typical due to the common presence of MAC bacteria in the environment (soil and water), different transmission routes, a wide variety of hosts, a long incubation period (weeks to months), and nonspecific clinical signs. In the past, the infections were often believed to be associated with birds. However, it is well documented that a broad spectrum of mammalian species or other free-living organisms, such as amoebae, can harbor and transmit MAC bacteria [3]. Whether infected pets play a role in transmitting these agents to humans is still to be clarified. Humans are also exposed to the same fomites as contaminated soil and water as their animals. Comparison of human and swine MAC isolates to detect mutual infections gave inconclusive results [45]– [46]. However, zoonotic transmission of other mycobacterial species from animals to humans has been documented. For example, animal-to-human transmission of Mycobacterium pinnipedii has been confirmed in an individual with close contact to marine mammals [47], and a case of cat-to-human transmission of Mycobacterium bovis has been reported in the United Kingdom [48]. These findings support the plausibility that, under certain conditions, companion animals infected with nontuberculous mycobacteria could pose a zoonotic risk. Therefore, the owner of such a patient should be informed about the risk, especially if these animals are in contact with children, the elderly, or individuals with compromised immune systems, such as AIDS, immunosuppressive therapy, or other causes.

In conclusion, this case report confirms that systemic mycobacteriosis in dogs can be easily misdiagnosed as lymphoma. To avoid this typical diagnostic failure, in each canine generalized lymphadenopathy at least Ziehl-Neelsen staining should be routinely performed during cytological examination of a lymph node or other tissue especially in Miniature Schnauzers and Basset Hounds. However, as the sensitivity and specificity of this method can be not very high, PCR, culturing on special media or MALDI-TOF should be considered to obtain the definitive diagnosis.
