Economic impact of initiation of home mechanical ventilation: a budget impact analysis from the Netherlands
Authors: Ries van den Biggelaar, Marieke Duiverman, Anda Hazenberg, J F. M van Boven, Karin Vermeulen, Peter J Wijkstra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn addition to the already positive outcomes of initiating home mechanical ventilation (HMV) at home, we aimed to explore the economic impact of implementation of initiating HMV at home versus traditional clinical settings, focusing on the Dutch healthcare system.MethodsAn Excel-based cost calculator model was used adhering to International Society of Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) budget impact analysis guidelines and based on the estimated population of HMV patients from 2023 to 2027 in the Netherlands. We compared home initiation of HMV against inpatient initiation in two scenarios based on patient eligibility and assessed financial impact from both health payer and societal perspectives.ResultsSubstantial cost savings in both scenarios were observed, with significant reductions in hospital admission days and in waiting time among patients who initiated treatment at home. The main scenario (55% of patients initiated at home) indicated savings of €8.4 million for health payers and €10.0 million from a societal perspective over 5 years. The high-uptake scenario (85% of patients initiated at home) showed even greater savings: €13.8 million for health payers and €16.4 million from a societal perspective over 5 years. Tornado diagrams highlighted the low risk of unsuccessful implementation and potential for greater benefits than projected.ConclusionInitiating HMV at home yields substantial budget savings for payers and society in the Netherlands without compromising previously demonstrated clinical outcomes. These findings support broader implementation of home-based HMV initiation and may inform reimbursement reform and wider adoption of home-based HMV initiation.

Introduction

The demand for home mechanical ventilation (HMV) has markedly increased in recent years, driven by the rising prevalence of diseases leading to chronic respiratory failure and the growing recognition of its benefits in improving survival and quality of life.1 2 This trend puts pressure on already limited healthcare resources, including hospital bed capacity and the availability of trained professionals, a challenge that was further magnified during the COVID-19 pandemic.3 Although acute ventilation during pandemics differs from chronic long-term care, the crisis highlighted the strategic importance of accessible and flexible ventilatory support as part of resilient health systems.4 5

Evidence shows that non-invasive ventilation (NIV) improves survival and reduces hospital readmissions in selected patients with chronic respiratory failure, while being cost-effective when delivered in the home environment.1 2 6 Moreover, economic analyses indicate that HMV allows for comparable clinical outcomes with more efficient use of healthcare resources than hospital-based initiation.7 These findings provide a strong rationale to further develop and evaluate care models that optimise patient outcomes while ensuring economic and organisational sustainability.

One promising alternative to traditional in-hospital initiation is the direct initiation of HMV at home. Previous clinical studies evaluating this strategy reported favourable outcomes, including comparable clinical effectiveness, as reflected by gas exchange and treatment adherence, reduced healthcare utilisation, and greater satisfaction among patients and caregivers.810 Nevertheless, real-world implementation of home initiation is hampered by practical challenges, such as the need for upfront investment, uncertainties in reimbursement policies, concerns related to privacy and data security, and the availability of specialised clinical staff.11

This study aims to address these gaps by conducting a budget impact analysis (BIA) of HMV initiation at home within the Dutch healthcare system. The research questions are:

What are the projected healthcare and societal cost differences of initiating home mechanical ventilation (HMV) at home compared with in-hospital initiation in the Netherlands during 2023–2027?

By answering these questions, this study provides a comprehensive evaluation of home initiation of HMV, offering a framework that can guide healthcare decision-making in the Netherlands and potentially inform similar strategies in other countries. While the primary focus remains on chronic respiratory patients, the findings may also yield insights for broader preparedness planning since strengthening home-based care structures could relieve hospital capacity during future crises.

Methods

Study design

This budget impact analysis (BIA) follows the ISPOR good practice guidelines for BIAs and the CHEERS 2022 reporting standards.12 13 The analysis models the economic consequences of implementing home initiation of home mechanical ventilation (HMV) compared with hospital initiation in the Netherlands over a 5-year horizon (2023–2027). The perspective of both the health-payer and societal viewpoints is adopted, reflecting Dutch policy-planning standards and the national costing manual of the Healthcare Institute.14 15 Additional methodological details of the cost analysis are provided in the online supplemental Appendix.

Sample and data

The study population includes patients with chronic respiratory failure due to neuromuscular diseases (NMD), thoracic cage abnormalities, including obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) treated within the four national Dutch HMV centres. Clinical and resource-use data were derived from two previous randomised trials — the Homerun trial (NMD and thoracic cage disorders, including OHS) and the Reconsider trial (COPD) — reporting predefined outcomes on healthcare utilisation, admission days and initiation setting for home versus hospital initiation.9 10

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this study. This Budget Impact Analysis used anonymised secondary data from two previously approved studies: the nationwide Homerun trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03203577) and the Reconsider trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02652559). Both studies received ethical approval from the relevant Medical Ethics Review Committees in the Netherlands. No new patient data were collected for the present analysis and therefore no additional ethical review was required.9 10

Data analysis procedure

Costs were modelled using the ZonMw Budget Impact Analysis Excel calculator (The Netherlands Organisation for Health Research and Development, The Hague, The Netherlands) and validated against the ISPOR good practice guidelines for Budget Impact Analyses.12 16 Two implementation scenarios were analysed: (a) Main scenario: 55% of eligible patients start at home. (b) High-uptake scenario: 85% of eligible patients start at home.

Deterministic sensitivity analyses assessed uncertainty in population growth and unit-cost variability (−20% to +30%), visualised using tornado diagrams. All results are presented in 2022 euros (€) for both health-payer and societal perspectives, cumulatively over 2023–2027.

The analytic framework is summarised in figure 1, which illustrates the modelling approach comparing hospital and home initiation of home mechanical ventilation (HMV). Each pathway represents the proportion of patients initiating ventilation in either setting, expressed as market share (%) of the total eligible population. As HMV in the Netherlands is organised through four designated national centres, all patients requiring non-invasive ventilation are referred to one of these centres; hence overall coverage is assumed to be 100%. The model allocates costs per patient group — chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), neuromuscular disease (NMD), and thoracic cage disorder (including OHS) — according to these market shares and aggregates them for each year from 2023 to 2027. First-year costs for both settings were derived from the Homerun and Reconsider trials and validated against reference cost data from the Dutch Healthcare Institute.9 10 14 15 Scenario analyses then adjusted market shares to reflect gradual adoption of home initiation (main and high-uptake scenarios).

Validation and reproducibility

Input data, unit-cost assumptions and sensitivity ranges are detailed in onlinesupplemental tables 1 2 to facilitate reproducibility of the analysis. The modelling code and parameters adhere to CHEERS 2022 transparency standards.13

Results

Clinical impact

In both the 55% and 85% scenarios, clinical effectiveness was assumed to be comparable to hospital initiation, consistent with the Homerun and Reconsider trials.9 10 In the main scenario (55%), approximately 4347 patients were projected to initiate HMV at home over 5 years, increasing to 6719 patients in the high-uptake scenario (85%). This translated into a substantial reduction in hospital admission days, estimated at 21 302 days (58 years) in the main scenario and 32 921 days (90 years) in the high-uptake scenario over 5 years.

In addition, home initiation reduced waiting time — defined as the period between the clinical indication for HMV and the actual start of ventilation — from 31.2 days to 19.9 days. This corresponded to a cumulative reduction of approximately 49 125 waiting days (134 years) in the main scenario and 75 921 days (208 years) in the high-uptake scenario over 5 years.

Economic impact

From the healthcare payer’s perspective, initiating HMV at home resulted in significant cost savings over the 2023–2027 horizon. In the main scenario (55% home initiation), total savings amounted to €8.4 million, with annual savings increasing from €1.4 million in 2023 to €2.0 million in 2027. In the high-uptake scenario (85%), cumulative savings reached €13.8 million, with yearly gains rising from €2.3 million to €3.3 million during the same period (table 1).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses explored uncertainty in population size and cost assumptions for both healthcare and non-healthcare components. Across scenarios, the impact on healthcare costs ranged from a potential surplus of €2.7 million to a maximum reduction of €22.6 million. For non-healthcare costs, the estimated impact ranged from a reduction of €944,000 to €2.7 million. When combined, the overall budget impact varied from a surplus of €1.8 million to a maximum reduction of €25.4 million over 5 years (figures3 4).

Explanation of Tornado Diagrams

In both scenarios, the potential for financial benefit was greatest when the cost difference between home and hospital initiation increased. Conversely, the model indicated a risk of additional costs when home initiation costs rose and hospital costs decreased. Variations in population size had a comparatively smaller influence on financial variability, supporting the robustness of the base-case findings.

Discussion

This national budget impact analysis demonstrates that home initiation of chronic non-invasive ventilation can be implemented efficiently and sustainably within an integrated healthcare system. Over a 5-year period, the Dutch model predicts cumulative savings ranging from €8.4 million to €16.4 million depending on the adoption rate.

The magnitude of the cost savings is largely determined by the Dutch context, where all patients requiring HMV are referred to one of the four national centres. In this centralised system, hospital admission days remain the dominant cost driver, and shifting part of this process to the home environment therefore yields immediate financial returns. In contrast, countries where initiation occurs during brief outpatient titration, as reported in the OPIP trial by Murphy and colleagues, may achieve smaller relative savings since hospitalisation accounts for a lower share of total costs.17 Our results thus emphasise that the economic impact of home initiation depends not only on the technology used but also on the organisation of care—the balance between inpatient capacity, trained staff and digital infrastructure.

This BIA did not evaluate clinical safety or patient outcomes, as these were already established in earlier randomised studies (Homerun, Reconsider).9 10 In those studies, safety was an explicit subject of investigation, and patient selection proved crucial to achieving successful and safe initiation at home. The present analysis therefore assumes comparable clinical effectiveness between settings, consistent with this evidence base. In the Dutch programme, home initiation is largely organised as a nurse-led process within specialised centres, supported by telemonitoring and physician supervision. Specialised nurses coordinate home visits and monitoring, while physicians provide clinical oversight and decision-making. Within this framework, patients are telemonitored and have 24-hour on-call supervision by specialised nurses and physicians, ensuring continuity of care comparable to hospital initiation.

The consistency of cost savings across sensitivity analyses further indicates that the model is robust and that home initiation is unlikely to introduce hidden financial or organisational risks.

The analysis also identifies opportunities for additional efficiency gains. Nursing travel time emerged as a major determinant of total cost, reflecting the logistical complexity of home-based initiation. Reducing unnecessary travel through clustering of visits, hybrid digital consultations and organisational optimisation could further enhance sustainability—an important consideration given projected workforce shortages in future care. Investment in telemonitoring, combined with better central coordination and decentralised delivery across care teams, may therefore yield benefits extending beyond financial metrics, including improved continuity of care and a smaller environmental footprint. From an implementation perspective, several practical lessons emerge from this analysis. Centralised organisation of HMV services appears to facilitate economies of scale, while telemonitoring enables safe decentralisation of care delivery. In addition, workforce models that combine specialised nurses with physician oversight may support scalability while maintaining clinical expertise. These elements may represent important prerequisites for successful implementation in other healthcare systems. Viewed more broadly, home initiation of HMV represents a shift from episodic, hospital-based intervention to integrated, patient-centred chronic care. Comparable studies by Gajdoš et al. (2021) and Murphy et al. (2023) have shown that home ventilation can be cost-effective in specific disease groups; the present analysis extends this evidence to a national, system-wide scale.6 7 By modelling both the payer and societal perspectives, the study shows that home initiation can strengthen health system resilience by releasing hospital capacity and reducing dependency on inpatient resources. These findings also resonate with the observations of Coccia (2023, 2024) that decentralised ventilation capacity contributes to preparedness during respiratory pandemics, reinforcing the strategic value of home-based care.4 18

Implementation, however, requires adequate financial and organisational support. While home initiation reduces overall expenditure, the current reimbursement structure does not yet fully accommodate this model of care. In the Netherlands, HMV care is primarily reimbursed through the health insurance system, with funding historically aligned with hospital-based care pathways. As a result, reimbursement structures do not always fully reflect the organisational shift required for home initiation, particularly regarding telemonitoring and home-based service delivery. Aligning reimbursement with evolving care models will therefore be important to support broader implementation. The financing of new care pathways therefore remains complex, as reimbursement structures have traditionally been designed for hospital-based services. Ensuring affordable and sustainable reimbursement for a service that replaces hospital care is crucial to enable innovation rather than restrict it. Moreover, the availability of intensive care capacity, the redeployment of nursing staff and the organisational adjustments required to implement home initiation demand targeted investment and policy support. Without such adaptation, the diffusion of home-based ventilation may remain slow despite its demonstrated efficiency.

This study has several strengths. It represents the first national budget impact analysis evaluating home initiation of chronic non-invasive ventilation within an integrated, real-world healthcare system. By combining data from all four Dutch HMV centres, the model provides a comprehensive and representative overview of current practice, ensuring that its projections are grounded in clinical reality. In contrast to cost-effectiveness studies focusing on incremental health gains, the BIA framework quantifies the financial and organisational consequences of scaling up home initiation—information directly relevant for decision-makers and payers. The inclusion of both the health-payer and societal perspectives allows a balanced view of short-term and long-term economic effects.

As with any modelling approach, the analysis relies on assumptions regarding future costs, patient numbers and inflation. However, these assumptions were informed by empirical data from previous clinical trials (Homerun, Reconsider) and verified through sensitivity analyses to test robustness under varying scenarios.9 10 This focus illustrates the strength of the BIA framework: it enables health systems to anticipate budgetary and logistical implications before large-scale implementation using transparent and reproducible parameters that can be adapted to other contexts. Generalisability beyond the Netherlands may be limited because HMV care is organised in a uniquely centralised manner within a small number of expert centres; however, the methodological transparency of the cost calculations allows replication using local cost structures and epidemiological data in other countries. These insights may be transferable to other healthcare systems facing increasing pressure on hospital capacity and exploring decentralised models of respiratory care.

Conclusion

In conclusion, home initiation of HMV within a telemonitored network represents a clinically safe, economically advantageous and organisationally transformative strategy. By reallocating resources from inpatient to home-based care, it reduces expenditure, increases system resilience and enhances patient autonomy. Future policy should focus on developing appropriate reimbursement models and investing in organisational capacity to ensure that the savings achieved for payers translate into sustainable funding for the specialised centres that make this transition possible. These findings should be interpreted in light of the modelling assumptions and the specific organisation of HMV care in the Dutch healthcare system.
