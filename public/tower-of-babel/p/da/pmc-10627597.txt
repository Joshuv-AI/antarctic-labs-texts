Restless legs syndrome affects sleep in de novo Parkinson’s disease patients
Authors: Jae-Myung Kim, Seong-Min Choi, Soo Hyun Cho, Byeong C. Kim
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Restless legs syndrome (RLS) is common in Parkinson’s disease (PD) patients and can affect the motor symptoms and non-motor symptoms (NMSs) of PD patients. The aim of this study was to identify the clinical factors affected by RLS in patients with PD. We included 369 de novo PD patients. RLS was assessed via face-to-face interviews and the motor symptoms and NMSs of the patients were assessed using relevant scales. RLS frequency in the patients was 12.2% (45/369). PD patients with RLS (PD-RLS) exhibited a greater global Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) score than those without RLS (PD-No RLS). PD-RLS exhibited significantly greater scores in the daytime dysfunction and sleep disturbances components of the PSQI than PD-No RLS. PD-RLS exhibited a significantly greater score in the cardiovascular, sleep/fatigue, and attention/memory subdomain of the Non-Motor Symptoms Scale than PD-No RLS. The International RLS Study Group rating scale score was significantly related to PSQI components scores in the sleep disturbances, sleep latency, habitual sleep efficiency, and subjective sleep quality. RLS frequency in de novo PD patients is higher than that in the general population, and the main NMS affected by RLS in these patients is sleep disturbances. Therefore, it is necessary to manage RLS in PD patients with sleep disturbances.

1. Introduction

Restless Legs Syndrome (RLS), a common neurologic disease, is characterized by an urge to move the legs accompanying uncomfortable sensations.[1] Parkinson’s disease (PD) is one of the causes of secondary RLS.[2] and the prevalence of RLS in PD patients is greater than that in the general population.[2] Sleep disturbances are common non-motor symptoms (NMSs) in PD patients. There are various clinical presentations of sleep disturbances in PD patients, namely insomnia, excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS), rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder, and RLS.[3] PD patients with RLS may show differences in motor and non-motor symptoms compared to PD patients without RLS. PD patients preceded by RLS had lower Hoehn and Yahr stage than patients who develop RLS after diagnosis of PD.[4] PD patients with RLS had poor sleep quality than PD patients without RLS.[4]

Many studies have reported that RLS can affect the motor symptoms and NMSs of PD patients.[4,5] However, those studies included only a small group of patients or patients who were already taking dopaminergic medications. Since dopaminergic therapy can affect the RLS symptoms in PD, investigating RLS in PD prior to dopaminergic medication has the advantage of excluding drug effects. Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine RLS frequency in de novo PD patients and find clinical factors, including motor symptoms and NMSs, that are significantly affected by RLS in patients with PD.

2. Subjects and methods

The Institutional Review Board of our hospital approved this study, and all participating patients gave written informed consent.

The subjects were all de novo PD patients who presented to the hospital. None of the patients were taking dopaminergic or anti-dopaminergic medications at the time of the clinical evaluations. The clinical diagnostic criteria of the United Kingdom PD Society Brain Bank were used to diagnose PD.[6] All patients were followed up for at least 1 year to confirm that they responded to dopaminergic medications and did not have Parkinson-plus symptoms. Patients who exhibited poor responses to dopaminergic medication or had Parkinson-plus syndrome, any significant cerebral lesions on brain magnetic resonance imaging, medication-induced RLS, or any other conditions that could cause secondary RLS, namely peripheral neuropathy, rheumatologic disease, chronic renal disease, iron deficiency anemia, or spinal cord injury, were excluded from this study.

All clinical evaluations were performed prior to the initiation of anti-parkinson medication. Demographic information such as age, sex, PD duration, and age at PD symptom onset was obtained from each patient. The motor symptoms of PD were checked using the Unified PD Rating Scale (UPDRS),[7] and the modified Hoehn and Yahr (mHY) Scale.[8] The NMSs of the patients were checked using the Non-Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS),[9] Beck Depression Inventory (BDI),[10] Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE),[11] and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).[12] RLS symptoms were assessed via face-to-face interviews and the diagnosis of RLS was based on the clinical diagnostic criteria of the International RLS Study Group (IRLSSG).[1] PD patients who met the 4 RLS diagnostic criteria were diagnosed as having RLS, and PD patients with RLS (PD-RLS) were requested to complete the IRLSSG rating scale to estimate the severity of RLS symptom.[13]

SPSS Statistics 25 (IBM, Armonk, NY) was used for all statistical analyses. In the test for normal distribution using Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests, no variable showed normal distribution. Therefore, we used non-parametric tests for all statistics. Mann-Whitney test and Fisher exact test were used to compare demographic and clinical factors between PD-RLS and PD patients without RLS (PD-No RLS). Pearson’s correlation was used to analyze associations between PSQI component scores and the IRLSSG rating scale score. A P value less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

We included a total of 369 de novo PD patients, and 45 (12.2%) of whom had RLS. With respect to demographic and clinical factors, PD-RLS had greater global PSQI scores than PD-No RLS. No significant differences were found in age, sex, PD duration, age at PD symptom onset, UPDRS part III and II scores, mHY stage, NMSS total score, BDI score, or MMSE score between the 2 groups (Table 1).

4. Discussion

In this study, we observed that RLS frequency in de novo PD patients was greater than that previously reported in the general population; PD-RLS had worse sleep scores (global PSQI and NMSS sleep/fatigue domain scores) than PD-No RLS; PD-RLS had worse scores in the daytime dysfunction and sleep disturbances components of the PSQI than PD-No RLS; and RLS severity was significantly correlated with sleep disturbances, sleep latency, habitual sleep efficiency, and subjective sleep quality.

RLS is a sleep disorder associated with dysfunction of the dopaminergic system.[14] PD is one of the risk factors for RLS, and RLS prevalence in PD patients is 14% to 20%.[4,15] Since both PD and RLS are responsive to dopaminergic treatment, a dopaminergic dysfunction may play an important role in both conditions.[16] However, there is currently no evidence of a pathophysiologic or genetic relationship of the 2 diseases.[16] The pathological hallmark of PD, the Lewy body, is not related to the pathophysiology of RLS,[17] and genetic loci shared by the 2 diseases have not been identified.[18] Since dopaminergic therapy in PD patients may reduce RLS symptoms or affect RLS severity,[4] it seems important to investigate the prevalence of RLS in PD before the initiation of dopaminergic treatment. Many studies have already been conducted on RLS in PD patients. However, an advantage of our study is the inclusion of a somewhat large number of de novo PD patients.

RLS prevalence in this PD cohort (12.2%) is higher than that previously reported in the general population[19] and similar to that reported in untreated PD patients.[4,20] Moreover, RLS prevalence documented in this study is slightly lower than that previously reported in other studies involving PD patients. This correlates with previous studies in which RLS prevalence was lower in untreated PD patients than in treated PD patients[4] and the duration of anti-Parkinson treatment was found to significantly contribute to the development of RLS in PD patients.[21]

Age, sex, PD duration, and age at PD symptom onset did not differ significantly between PD-RLS and PD-No RLS, suggesting that RLS is not related to demographic factors in PD patients. Furthermore, scores related to motor symptoms and ADL were not different between the 2 groups. The association between RLS and motor symptoms in PD patients varies from study to study;[4,20,21] therefore, further research is required to elucidate this relationship.

RLS is a sleep-related movement disorder. Therefore, RLS in PD is expected to be most related to sleep disturbance among various non-motor symptoms. PD-RLS had greater global PSQI and NMSS sleep/fatigue domain scores than PD-No RLS. These findings indicate that RLS affects sleep in PD patients. These results are consistent with previous studies showing that RLS is one of the main factors aggravating sleep disturbances in PD patients.[22,23] Compared to these previous studies, our study has a number of advantages, namely that only untreated patients were included and a large number of patients were enrolled. Anti-Parkinson therapy is associated with various types of sleep disorders.[24] Based on our results, we can speculate that RLS affects daytime dysfunction and sleep disturbances in PD patients. Furthermore, RLS severity was correlated with sleep disturbances, sleep latency, habitual sleep efficiency, and subjective sleep quality. RLS is a major cause of sleep disturbances, and disturbed sleep frequently prompts sufferers to seek medical help.[25] Previous studies have shown that sleep quality was improved by RLS treatment in the general population.[26] Therefore, the diagnosis and treatment of RLS in PD patients may improve sleep quality, and a previous study of PD patients has demonstrated this.[4]

RLS has been reported to be related to various NMSs in PD patients. In a previous study, PD-RLS exhibited worse anxiety, depression, sleep quality, and cardiovascular autonomic function compared to PD-No RLS.[4] In our study, cardiovascular, sleep/fatigue, and attention/memory NMSS subdomain scores were significantly greater in PD-RLS than in PD-No RLS. No significant associations were identified between RLS and other NMSs, which may be due to the fact that our patients had suffered from PD for a relatively short time and that the NMS domain scores were too low to show significant associations between them.

There are several limitations in this study. First, we did not include a control group; hence, RLS frequency could not be directly compared between the PD patients and a control group. Second, this study was conducted retrospectively; hence, the associations observed in this study cannot be interpreted as direct causal relationship. Third, peripheral blood iron and ferritin levels may affect the development and severity of RLS,[8] but these biological parameters were not evaluated in this study. Finally, various factors, such as REM sleep behavior disorder, obstructive sleep apnea, and medications, can affect RLS or sleep in patients with PD. However, those factors were not included in this study.

5. Conclusions

In the present study, RLS frequency in de novo PD patients is higher than that in the general population, and the main NMS affected by RLS in these patients is sleep disturbances. Therefore, it is necessary to manage RLS in PD patients with sleep disturbances.
