Anthropometric measurements as surrogate for identifying low birth weight: comparison with gold standard of measuring weight among neonates at a tertiary hospital in Tanzania
Authors: Nouz-hat Salim Abdallah N, Edward Kija, Helga Naburi, Ousseine Youssoufa, Felix Muchira Nawera, Karim Manji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReducing neonatal mortality is crucial for achieving Sustainable Development Goal 3.2. In Tanzania and other low- and middle-income countries, many babies are born at home or in primary health care settings without access to weighing scales. Other anthropometric measurements apart from weight may serve as effective surrogates for identifying low birth weight (LBW) infants and prioritizing them for care.ObjectivesThis study aimed to assess the sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of four anthropometric measurements chest circumference (CHC), occipitofrontal circumference (OFC), foot length (FL), and mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) and determine the optimal cut-off values for detecting LBW infants.MethodsA hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted in the neonatal unit at Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH). Newborns admitted within 24 h of birth were enrolled. A total of 471 newborns had CHC, OFC, FL, MUAC, and birth weight (BW) measured. Pearson’s correlation coefficient assessed the relationship between these measurements and birth weight. Receiver Operating Characteristics (ROC) analysis was used to determine optimal cut-off points.ResultsOf 471 newborns, 52.2% were female. The mean birth weight was 2639 g (SD = 851), with 43.1% (203) classified as LBW. A strong positive correlation of 0.90 was found between CHC and BW (p < 0.01), followed by OFC (0.82, p < 0.01). ROC analysis showed CHC (AUC = 0.97, 95% CI 0.97–0.98) and OFC (AUC = 0.94, 95% CI 0.92–0.96) as the most reliable indicators for detecting LBW. The optimal cut-off values were ≤ 29.45 cm for CHC and ≤ 32.9 cm for OFC.ConclusionOur study demonstrated that CHC with a cutoff ≤29.45 cm and OFC with a cutoff ≤ 32.9 cm has higher sensitivity and specificity for predicting low birth weight among neonates compared to FL or MUAC, indicating greater accuracy in detecting LBW in Tanzania. Our findings apply specifically to LBW newborns; we did not classify IUGR or SGA separately, and further studies are required to validate these cut-offs in those populations.Keywords: Low birth weight (LBW), Gestational age, Anthropometry

Introduction

According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), Low birth weight (LBW) is defined as a birth weight less than 2500 g regardless of gestational age at birth. Globally, approximately one-sixth of all newborns are born with LBW, whereas 27% are born in Sub Saharan Africa (SSA) [1]. Birth weight is inversely proportion to and is a predictor of infant morbidity and mortality accounting for 60% to 80% of neonatal deaths globally [2–4].

In Tanzania, there has been a remarkable decrease in prevalence of LBW over the past decade from 13% to 7% but remains unacceptably high [5, 6]. There is overlap between preterm birth and LBW newborns, with both being important causes of neonatal mortality. LWB babies who survive the neonatal period are at risk of developing late complications like intellectual disability, learning difficulty, neurodevelopmental delay, metabolic syndrome, visual and hearing impairment among other complications [7].

Timely identification of these babies is critical in reducing neonatal mortality and to achieve Sustainable Development Goal no 3.2 of less than 12 newborn deaths in all countries by 2030.

Global effort over the past few years, focused on increasing skilled birth attendants, and health facilities, but still about half of all deliveries in Africa continue to occur at home (6). In Tanzania, while 81% of deliveries took place in hospitals in 2022, nearly a quarter of births in rural areas occur at home [8], where newborns cannot be weighed, hindering the timely identification of high-risk infants.

Several studies have showed that anthropometric measures can predicts LBW among neonates [3, 9–11]. However, anthropometric measures of newborns can be affected by sociodemographic status, genetics and ethnicity [12, 13]. For example foot length and head circumference were found to be the best surrogates for BW in Uganda and India respectively [10, 14]. To our understanding no studies have been conducted in Tanzania to assess which anthropometrics can predict LBW among neonates. Therefore, this study was designed to determine which anthropometric measures can predict LBW among neonates at Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH) neonatology unit.

Methodology

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted among neonates at Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH) in Dar es Salaam from October 2019 to February 2020. MNH is the largest tertiary care hospital in Tanzania. The hospital serves as a referral center from regional hospitals both within and outside the Dar es Salaam region and functions as a key teaching hospital. The neonatal care unit (NICU) of MNH has 100 beds and admits approximately 4,000–4,500 neonates annually, with about 120–150 at any given time and provides level III NICU services. The unit is well-equipped with appropriate instruments for anthropometric measurements and staffed by skilled doctors and nurses.

Study population

The study included newborns admitted to the neonatal unit, including preterm, term, and post-term neonates, within 24 h of birth. Newborns with major congenital malformations, birth injuries (such as scalp swelling and limb fractures), cyanosis, or those requiring respiratory support were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was calculated using the Kish–Leslie formula (n = Z²·p(1–p)/d²) with Z = 1.96, p ≈ 0.30 based on prior LMIC data, and d = 0.05, yielding n ≈ 399. Allowing 5% for potential non-response, the minimum target was 419. We ultimately enrolled 471 newborns, exceeding this requirement. Given that our primary analyses estimate diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity and specificity of anthropometric cut-offs for LBW), the achieved LBW case count (203/471 = 43.1%) provides adequate precision (~ 5–6%) for sensitivity/specificity ≥ 0.75.

Data collection

Data were collected using a questionnaire specifically designed for the purpose of this study, capturing the sociodemographic characteristics of infants (age in hours, gestational age in weeks, place of birth, sex) and mothers (age, educational level, cigarette smoking, alcohol use), as well as maternal medical conditions. Anthropometric measurements included birth weight (BW) in grams, mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), foot length (FL) (FL), occipitofrontal circumference (OFC), and chest circumference (CHC), all recorded in centimetres (cm).

Two doctors, serving as research assistants (RAs), were trained for 3 days on proper measurement techniques and supervising nurses to use standard procedures. The researcher reassessed the RAs’ performance after every 10 records to for quality assurance.

Written informed consent was obtained from the mothers 30 min post-delivery, prior to enrolment. Birth weight was measured (without clothes or diapers) within the first 60 min after delivery using a Phoenix baby scale, which was calibrated daily with a known standard weight. MUAC was measured on the left upper arm while the baby was in the dorsal decubitus position. The tips of the acromion and olecranon were identified, and the distance between these points was measured. The midpoint was then located, from which MUAC was measured using a UNICEF tri-coloured flexible measuring tape.

OFC was measured with the baby in a supine position and the head exposed. The prominent part of the occiput and the supraorbital ridges were identified as landmarks. An ultra-flexible sewing tape was then used to measure the circumference around these landmarks to the nearest 0.1 cm.

The FL was measured with the baby in a supine position, the right foot exposed and placed laterally, with the ankle held and a finger placed on the dorsum of the foot. The distance from the posterior heel to the tip of the longest toe was measured using a hard transparent plastic ruler, to the nearest 0.1 cm.

The CHC was measured with the baby in a supine position and the chest exposed. Using an ultra-flexible sewing tape, the circumference was measured at the end of expiration, from the tip of the scapula at the back to the level of the nipples, to the nearest 0.1 cm.

Gestational age (GA) was determined using a hierarchical approach: when the last normal menstrual period (LNMP) was reliable, Naegele’s formula was applied; otherwise, an ultrasound between 12 and 15 weeks was used; and when neither was available, the New Ballard Score was applied. Although early first-trimester ultrasound is the gold standard for GA determination, it was not consistently available in our setting, particularly for referred cases. This pragmatic approach reflects practice in low-resource environments.

For each newborn, FL, OFC, CHC, MUAC, and BW were measured twice by the principal investigator and assistant while observing aseptic conditions. The average of these measurements was used for analysis.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 23. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables are reported as frequencies and proportions. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationship between anthropometric measurements and birth weight, with statistical significance set at a p-value of less than 0.05. Receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC) were generated and area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used to identify the optimal cut-point for each of the anthropometric measurement. The optimal cut-off point was selected as AUC point with the highest sensitivity and specificity. Sensitivity, specificity, and both positive and negative predictive values were calculated for each cut-off and reported with 95% confidence intervals.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of MUHAS (Ref. No. DA287/298.01 A/), and permission to conduct the study was granted by the MNH administration (MNH/TRCU/Permission.2019/243). To ensure confidentiality and privacy, all study data were securely stored in locked cabinets, with access restricted to the research team. Informed consent was obtained from the mothers of eligible neonates prior to their participation in the study.

Results

A total of 1200 newborns were screened, of these 61% were excluded for various reasons, the remaining 471 eligible newborns were enrolled in the study (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart

A total of 471 newborns were enrolled in the study, with 52.2% being female. Of these, 203 (43.1%) were classified as having low birth weight (LBW). Among the LBW newborns, 163 (34.6%) weighed between 1500 g and 2499 g, 34 (7.2%) weighed less than 1500 g, and 6 (1.3%) weighed less than 1000 g. (Table 1). More than half of the participants’ mothers (68%) had completed secondary school or university education. Additionally, 65% of the mothers were either businesswomen or employed. Most of the mothers (98%) did not smoke during pregnancy, and 99% refrained from alcohol consumption during pregnancy (Table 1).

The optimal cut-off points for identifying LBW were: MUAC ≥ 9.15 cm, CHC ≥ 29.45 cm, OFC ≥ 32.9 cm, foot length ≥ 7.05 cm (Table 4). Among these, CHC showed the highest sensitivity for identifying LBW, at 94%, with a specificity of 90%, a positive predictive value (PPV) of 87%, and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 94%. This was followed by MUAC with a sensitivity of 91%, and occipitofrontal circumference (OFC) with a sensitivity of 82% (Table 4).

Discussion

Globally, around one-sixth of newborns are LBW, which present the most important underlying risk factor for neonatal death [1, 15, 16]. The burden is particularly high in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where access to birth weight measurement equipment may be limited [1, 17]. This study aimed to identify reliable anthropometric measurements that can predict LBW among neonates and their specific cut-off values.

Our findings show that all four anthropometric parameters (CC, FL, HC and MUAC) can be used for identifying LBW, with the CHC, followed by OFC showing a highest correlation. The cutoff points for predicting LBW with the best sensitivity and specificity identified were CHC ≤ 29.45 cm and OFC ≤32.9 cm, similar cut offs were reported by Kaur et al. [18] and Kapoor et al. [19] in India, Nair et al. in Nigeria [20] and Sintayehu et al. in Ethiopia [21]. While these cut off were slightly lower than other studies in Nepal, Vietnam, Nigeria, and Ethiopia [22–26], were also higher [3, 27] than in other studies done in Africa, thus highlighting CHC’s utility for identifying LBW babies across multiple countries. Furthermore, our cutoffs closely matched those (OFC, CHC, and MUAC were 33.0 cm, 30.4 cm, and 9.3 cm, respectively) of a meta-analysis of high-quality studies in developing countries [28]. The cutoff point for CHC was also in keeping with the range of 29 cm and 30 cm recommended by the WHO Collaborative Study for detection of low birth weight when using CHC [29]. Furthermore, the high correlation of CHC with birth weight together with its high sensitivity and specificity also reported by Nair et al. and Sreeramareddy et al. further confirms CHC as an anthropometric surrogate to identify LBW neonates, in Nigeria and Nepal respectively [2, 30]. Our study’s ROC analysis, with an AUC of 0.97, demonstrates that CHC provides near-perfect discrimination between LBW and normal birth weight. Thus, our findings, supported by other studies, suggest that CHC can serve as an effective screening tool for the early detection of LBW newborns within 24 h of birth. This is particularly valuable in resource-limited settings where home births are common, birth weight measurement tools are scarce, and neonatal mortality is high. Early identification of LBW neonates enables timely interventions, such as kangaroo mother care, feeding support, and infection prevention, all of which are crucial for improving neonatal survival [31].

Contrary to our study, where CHC better predicted LBW than OFC, other studies found OFC, with a similar cutoff, most predictive of birth weight, followed by CHC [23, 26, 32]. However, head circumference may be inaccurate due to moulding during delivery, also can misestimate LBW in asymmetric growth restriction, delaying necessary interventions. Although CHC measurement may risk hypothermia due to chest exposure, which can be minimized by covering the baby, its high accuracy facilitated by the clear nipple line for easy landmark identification enhances its feasibility and reliability as an anthropometric surrogate.

While other anthropometric measures, including MUAC and FL, may reduce exposure and the risk of hypothermia, they demonstrated a weak correlation with BWT in our study. However, Tiruneh et al. observed the highest significant correlation between birth weight and MUAC, followed by FL, in Ethiopia [33]. Other studies evaluating MUAC in relation to BWT also demonstrated its effectiveness in identifying LBW infants [34, 35]. Additionally, foot length has been shown to have a good predictive value for identifying LBW in Uganda, India and Papua New Guinea [10, 36–39]. Measuring MUAC and/or FL may be less practical in preterm or critically ill neonates due to positioning difficulties and potential measurement errors from tool variability. For instance, in a meta-analysis of studies done in Africa, a higher correlation between FL and birth weight was observed when the measurement was done by a hard ruler or calliper rather than non-stretchable tape [27].

In addition to variation in methodology especially tool used in measurements, differences observed across these studies may be explained by, genetic and environmental factors [40], maternal characteristics [41], social economic and sociodemographic status [42] and ethnicity [12], which can affect foetal growth and subsequently anthropometric measures in different contexts.

Despite its strengths of a large sample size this study has limitations. The study was conducted in a tertiary hospital, which may not represent rural settings where home births are more common. Additionally, factors such as maternal nutrition, genetic influences, and intrauterine growth restrictions could impact anthropometric measurements. Future studies should explore regional differences and validate these findings in community-based settings.

Another limitation is the exclusion of neonates with congenital anomalies, which may affect generalizability to all newborn populations. However, the study’s findings remain relevant for neonatal health programs, as LBW remains a major contributor to neonatal morbidity and mortality in sub-Saharan Africa.

Despite the limitations, overall, our findings highlight the need for incorporating CHC measurement into routine neonatal care protocols as a quick screening tool, especially in resource-limited settings, where a weighing scale may not be readily available. These simple, low-cost anthropometric measures can significantly improve early detection of at-risk newborns and their management with an ultimate goal of reducing neonatal mortality rate and achieving the SDG 3.2 targets.

Conclusions

Our findings apply specifically to LBW newborns; we did not classify IUGR or SGA separately, and further studies are required to validate these cut-offs in those populations.

Our study demonstrated that CHC with a cutoff ≤ 29.45 cm and OFC with a cutoff ≤32.9 cm has higher sensitivity and specificity for predicting low birth weight among neonates compared to FL or MUAC, indicating greater accuracy in detecting LBW in Tanzania.

Findings from our study recommends the use of anthropometric surrogate measures as quick screening tool in situations where functional weighing machines are not readily available to identify LBW and appropriate management must be commenced timely.
