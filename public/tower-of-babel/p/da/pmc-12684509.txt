Influence of Sex and Race or Ethnicity on Major Adverse Cardiovascular Outcomes Following Coronary Artery Bypass Surgery in a Large Integrated Health Care System
Authors: Janine Y. Yang, Douglas Stram, John Doan, Alix P. Fairman, Maqdooda Merchant, Cynthia Triplett, Ahmad Y. Sheikh, Richard V. Ha, Seema K. Pursnani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe aimed to evaluate the influence of sex and race or ethnicity on major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) following coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).MethodsThis was a retrospective study of Kaiser Permanente Northern California members who underwent CABG from 2008 to 2019, evaluating odds of MACEs (myocardial infarction, stroke, serial percutaneous intervention, repeat CABG, death) at 30 days, 1 year, and up to 12 years follow‐up using multivariable logistic and proportional hazards regression analyses. We adjusted for demographic, clinical/surgical, and socioeconomic characteristics.ResultsCohort included n=7405 with mean age 65.2 years. There were n=6082 men and n=1323 women with 2179 (35.8%) and 639 (48.3%) MACEs, respectively. Older age, higher glycated hemoglobin, diabetes, end‐stage renal disease, lower hemoglobin, higher creatinine, smoking, lack of cardiopulmonary bypass, and use of nonarterial graft were significant predictors of long‐term MACEs. Female sex was associated with an increased odds of MACE at 30 days (odds ratio, 1.62 [95% CI, 1.19–2.21]) and 1 year (hazard ratio, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.02–1.51]).ConclusionFemale, Black, and Other (Native American, multiethnic or missing) race or ethnicity groups had the greatest incidence of MACEs at up to 12 years follow‐up post CABG, with differences largely driven by increased risk factor burden. Black and Other race or ethnicity were not significantly associated with long‐term CABG risk in multivariable modeling. Further understanding is required to improve upstream preventive efforts.

Ischemic heart disease remains the leading cause of death in the United States and in the world. 1 , 2 In the treatment of ischemic heart disease, coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery is often preferred over percutaneous coronary intervention for revascularization in cases with multivessel disease or left main coronary artery disease, especially in those with complex anatomy and concurrent diabetes. 2 , 3 Although advancements in surgical techniques and medical therapy have significantly reduced CABG mortality rates, disparities in treatment access and surgical outcomes persist. 4

Prior studies have demonstrated that female and Black and Hispanic groups have worse cardiovascular outcomes following CABG. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Data from the National Inpatient Sample from 2002 to 2017 demonstrated that Black patients had the lowest odds of undergoing CABG as well as the highest adjusted mortality rate following CABG along with Hispanic patients. 13 These disparities are only partially explained by patient risk factors, hospital quality, and socioeconomic status. 14 Some of these differences may be explained by deviations in perioperative management. For example, Black and Hispanic patients are less likely to receive arterial grafts during CABG, which have been shown to have better long‐term patency as conduits compared with venous grafts. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 In Asian populations, CABG operative mortality is similar to White populations, despite South Asians carrying an increased burden and severity of cardiovascular risk factors. 19 , 20 Although South Asians are more likely to have diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia compared with other racial and ethnic groups, South Asian ethnicity itself is not associated with adverse post‐CABG outcomes in multivariable modeling. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24

Sex differences in post‐CABG outcomes also exist due to a higher burden of preoperative risk factors and variations in coronary artery disease progression. 6 , 25 , 26 Female patients undergoing CABG are typically older with a higher prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, peripheral arterial disease, and dyslipidemia compared with men. 27 , 28 , 29 Female patients are also more likely to present with decompensated heart failure, acute myocardial infarction (MI), or cardiogenic shock preceding CABG. 27 , 28 , 29 Even after adjusting for traditional risk factors, female patients undergoing CABG experience higher operative mortality and are at a greater risk of major postoperative complications such as MI and stroke. 27 , 29

Further contemporary understanding of racial, ethnic, and sex differences in baseline risk factors and outcomes in patients undergoing CABG is required to better inform clinical decision‐making and reduce health disparities. In this study, we aim to evaluate the influence of race or ethnicity and sex on short‐ and long‐term major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) following CABG in our diverse integrated health care system.

METHODS

Because of the sensitive nature of the data collected for this study, requests to access the data set from qualified researchers trained in human subject confidentiality protocols may be sent to the corresponding author.

We performed a retrospective cohort study of Kaiser Permanente Northern California members who underwent isolated CABG between 2008 and 2019. Members who lacked continuous membership at least 1 year following CABG for reason other than death were excluded. Demographic, clinical, and surgical data were extracted from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) Database and Kaiser Permanente Northern California electronic health record system. Baseline data collected included demographics (self‐reported race or ethnicity, sex, census tract education and income level, primary language), medical risk factors (smoking, body mass index, baseline glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c], hemoglobin, creatinine, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and high density lipoprotein cholesterol, diabetes, hypertension, end‐stage renal disease [ESRD], prior coronary artery disease), use of guideline‐directed medical therapy (GDMT) (beta blockers, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers, antiplatelets, statins) at any point during the follow‐up period, and lastly CABG characteristics (year of surgery, STS risk score, elective versus urgent surgical status, use of cardiopulmonary bypass, presence and number of arterial grafts). Temporal data collected during follow‐up periods included statin use, HbA1c, and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels. MACE was defined as MI, surgical or percutaneous coronary revascularization, stroke, and all‐cause mortality. In patients who had >1 of these events during the study follow‐up period, only their first MACE event was considered. MACE was evaluated at 30 days, 1 year, and up to 12 years follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

We performed bivariate analyses comparing baseline characteristics by sex, testing for statistical significance using chi‐square tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests for continuous variables. We also compared baseline characteristics by race or ethnicity, using chi‐square tests for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables. Kaplan–Meier curve estimates for MACE‐free survival were generated stratifying sex and race or ethnicity, and log‐rank tests were used to assess differences in survival between groups. Logistic regression analysis was used to identify the odds of MACE at 30 days, and proportional hazards regression analyses were used to evaluate the hazard of MACE at up to 1‐ and 12‐year follow‐up. For proportional hazards models, we evaluated the proportionality assumption by plotting Schoenfeld residuals for sex and race or ethnicity. Multivariable analyses evaluated the association of MACE with sex and race or ethnicity, adjusting for baseline clinical and demographic characteristics. To control for potential confounding, baseline factors that had an association with race, ethnicity, or sex were considered for inclusion in the regression models and were included in final models if they had a significant association with MACE at 30 days, 1 year, or 12 years or if their inclusion in the model altered odds ratios (ORs) for sex, race, or ethnicity by ≥10%. Outcomes were extracted based on documented International Classification of Diseases (ICD) and Current Procedural Terminology codes. Statistical analyses were performed in SAS 9.4 for Windows. P values were considered statistically significant at <0.05.

The study was approved by the Kaiser Permanente Northern California Institutional Review Board, and informed consent was not required nor obtained for this data‐only study.

RESULTS

The study cohort included n=7450 patients who underwent CABG between 2008 and 2019. Surgical volume varied during the study period, with an overall increase from 2008 to 2019 (Figure 1). A majority of patients were male (n=6082, 82%) versus female (n=1323, 18%). Patients self‐identified as White (n=3782, 51%), Hispanic (n=1045, 14%), Filipino (n=827, 11%), Other Asian/Pacific Islander (n=686, 10%), Black (n=396, 5%), and South Asian (n=258, 3%) (Figure 2), with an increased proportion of non‐White racial and ethnic groups over the study period (Figure 3).

Women, compared with men, were older at time of CABG (66.9 versus 64.9 years, P<0.001) with higher prevalence of prior MI (58% versus 51%), diabetes (57% versus 45%), hypertension (69% versus 61%), and ESRD (12% versus 7%), as well as higher creatinine, total cholesterol, and HbA1c values (P<0.001) (Table 1). Women had higher high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels (47.5 versus 41.4, P<0.001) and were more likely to be never smokers (57% versus 43%, P<0.0001). Women had a higher preoperative STS risk score and were more likely to undergo urgent CABG (P<0.001). Despite variation in the number of bypass grafts used, use of arterial grafts was clinically similar (men 98%, women 97%). A large majority of both men (93%) and women (92%) considered English as their primary language. Female patients lived in census tracts with a lower proportion of college graduates (42% versus 46%, P<0.001) and lower median household income ($75 565 versus $82 803, P<0.001). Women were overall less likely to receive any GDMT compared with men (96% versus 98%, P<0.001) with lower beta blocker, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitor, antiplatelet, and statin use but higher angiotensin II receptor blocker use than men.

South Asian patients were youngest at the time of CABG (61.2 years) and White patients were oldest (67.0 years) (Table 2). South Asian patients were most likely to have had prior percutaneous coronary intervention (26% versus 19% in total cohort) and most likely to be never smokers (71% versus 45% in total cohort). Black individuals had the highest body mass index at baseline (30.1 versus 28.6 in total cohort) and highest low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels (99.4 versus 94.8 in total cohort), as well as the lowest census tract median household income ($66 225 versus $81 510 in total cohort). Filipino patients had the highest baseline creatinine levels (1.7 versus 1.2 in total cohort), highest prevalence of ESRD (17% versus 8% in total cohort), hypertension (70% versus 62% in total cohort), and diabetes (60% versus 47% in total cohort). Hispanic patients had the highest baseline HbA1c (7.4% versus 6.9% in total cohort), lowest high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels (40.6 versus 42.5 in total cohort), the lowest proportion of patients who considered English as a primary language (74% versus 93% in total cohort), and the lowest census tract proportion of college graduates (37% versus 45% in total cohort). Black and Hispanic patients had the highest STS risk scores and were most likely to undergo urgent, as compared with elective, CABG surgery (69% in both groups, compared with 66% in total cohort). There were no significant differences in overall use of GDMT, beta blockers, or statins when stratified by race or ethnicity (P=0.155). However, statistically significant differences did exist in GDMT subgroups with the lowest use of angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors in Filipino and Other Asian/Pacific Islander (52%, P<0.001), angiotensin II receptor blockers in White (27%, P<0.001) and antiplatelets in Black (76%, P<0.001) patients.

In bivariate analysis, there was a strong association between MACE during 12 years of follow‐up and sex (P<0.001) and race or ethnicity (P<0.001). Although no statistically significant differences were found between type of MACE event and sex, there was a statistically significant difference in type of MACE events across racial and ethnic groups (P=0.01) (Table 3). Occurrence of any MACE was highest in Black individuals (n=186, 47%) and Other (inclusive of Native American, Multiethnic, Missing) racial or ethnic groups (n=141, 42.3%), and lowest in South Asian (n=72, 27.9%) and Other Asian/Pacific Islander (n=222, 29.1%) patients. Black patients had the highest incidence of long‐term postoperative MI (n=16, 4%) and stroke (n=92, 23.2%), and South Asian patients had the highest incidence of repeat revascularization (12.4% with subsequent percutaneous coronary intervention).

At long‐term follow‐up (up to 12 years), increased risk of MACE was significantly associated with age (HR, 1.03 [95% CI, 1.02–1.04]), increased creatinine (HR, 1.11 [95% CI, 1.06–1.15]) and increased HbA1c (HR, 1.07 [95% CI, 1.03–1.12]). Comorbid conditions such as diabetes (HR, 1.35 [95% CI, 1.17–1.55]), ESRD (HR, 1.48 [95% CI, 1.22–1.80]), and former (HR, 1.66 [95% CI, 1.41–1.95]) or current smoking status (HR, 1.23 [95% CI, 1.10–1.38]) were also associated with higher MACEs at long‐term follow‐up (Figure 6). Variables associated with decreased incidence of MACEs at end of follow‐up included Filipino ethnicity (HR, 0.72 [95% CI, 0.60–0.87]) or Other Asian/Pacific Islander race (HR, 0.71 [95% CI, 0.58–0.87]) as compared with White race, and increased hemoglobin (HR, 0.89 [95% CI, 0.86–0.92]). Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) use (HR, 0.81 [95% CI, 0.73–0.90]) and use of arterial graft were associated with lower MACEs (HR, 0.55 [95% CI, 0.41–0.74]). There were no significant differences in graft occlusion (0.11% patients without CPB versus 0.15% patients with CPB, P=0.717) or repeat CABG (0.28% patients without CPB versus 0.09% patients with CPB, P=0.054) based on CPB use. Multiarterial grafting (MAG), defined as use of ≥1 more arterial conduits, was used in 137 patients overall (1.85%) with 69 White (1.82%), 20 Hispanic (1.91%), 16 Filipino (1.93%), 5 South Asian (1.94%), 17 Other Asian/Pacific Islander (2.23%), 8 Black (2.02%), and 2 Other race or ethnicity (3.33%) patients, without a statistically significant difference in distribution (P=0.685). Overall, patients with MAG use demonstrated a lower incidence of MACEs (23 patients, 16.8%) compared with patients without MAG (2795 patients, 38.5%) (P<0.001).

DISCUSSION

In this large retrospective cohort study following a diverse set of 7405 patients up to 12 years post CABG in an integrated health care system, we observed lower long‐term MACE‐free survival in women, Black patients, and those classified as other race or ethnicity. In a multivariable regression analysis, factors associated with higher hazard of MACEs included increasing medical complexity, such as older age and diagnosis of ESRD and diabetes, as well as surgical characteristics such as lack of CPB use or arterial grafting. These findings corroborate prior studies with known associations with MACEs and comorbid cardiac conditions such as diabetes and ESRD. 30 , 31 , 32 Of note, sex and race or ethnicity were not significantly associated with higher long‐term MACEs in multivariable regression analysis, suggesting the burden of intermediary factors including comorbid medical and surgical factors, and social determinants of health to be potential explanations for the observed disparities.

Our findings regarding sex differences in long‐term post‐CABG outcomes remain consistent with existing literature, with female patients experiencing disproportionately higher MACEs than male patients. 6 , 33 , 34 This difference is largely driven by preoperative or operative characteristics such as older age with a higher cardiac risk factor burden, and surgical characteristics leading to lower use of arterial grafts, higher rate of graft failure, and lower likelihood of complete revascularization. 6 , 35 In our study, women were older with greater frequency of prior MI, diabetes, hypertension, and ESRD and higher STS score and more likely to be urgent, compared with elective, surgical status. Available census tract data also suggest differences in social determinants of health, with women less likely to be college graduates and have lower median household incomes, though limited by individual‐level data. Female patients were also less likely to be on beta blocker, statin, and antiplatelet therapy, which is consistent with prior studies demonstrating lower GDMT use among female patients in heart failure and coronary artery disease. 36 Better understanding of patient and provider‐/system‐level factors contributing to differences in prescription use, such as cost, medication adherence, and side effects, may help reduce outcome disparities.

Similar to our study findings, Black individuals have historically demonstrated worse outcomes post CABG, which can be partially explained by the presence of higher cardiac comorbidities. In the NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) survey data, Black individuals have higher rates of diabetes and exhibit a higher HR of developing ESRD relative to White individuals even after adjusting for glomerular filtration rate. 37 Black individuals also had higher rates of hypertension and heart failure, leading to higher disease burden at a younger age. 25 , 38 Studies have shown correlations between racial discrimination with higher coronary artery calcium score, high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein, and hypertension akin to chronic systemic inflammation and stress. 37 , 39 In our study cohort, Black patients represented the highest female population (34% versus 18% in total cohort) and had the greatest frequency of current and former smokers, highest body mass index, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and creatinine values and the lowest hemoglobin values. Black patients were among the higher STS scores and more likely to have an urgent, versus elective, surgical status, which has been similarly noted in other studies. 4 , 13 , 37 Understanding the upstream mechanisms for these racial disparities requires greater emphasis on social and structural factors that contribute to inequitable outcomes.

Differences in surgical characteristics such as use of CPB and number of arterial grafts were associated with differences in MACEs. Previously studies have demonstrated an advantage with better long‐term survival and longer revascularization‐free period with CPB use. 40 Lack of CPB use may be a marker of higher risk patients, potentially including those with severe large vessel atherosclerotic burden, renal failure, and right ventricular dysfunction, all of which are factors in predicting higher MACEs. 40 , 41 , 42 In our cohort, outcome differences from CPB use were not driven by graft occlusion or repeat CABG. MAG has also been associated with higher postoperative survival compared with single artery grafting, which aligns with our findings of MAG‐associated lower MACEs, though there were no significant differences in MAG across studied racial or ethnic groups. 43 , 44

Differences in social determinants such as education level, health care literacy, English proficiency, and financial stability can lead to differences in prevalence, diagnosis, and management of comorbid conditions as well as access to timely and appropriate care. Black patients are less likely to undergo CABG with lower referral rates than White patients. 4 , 13 , 37 In a national retrospective cohort study evaluating 1429 acute hospitals, Black individuals consistently had lower access to CABG regardless of health care system or hospital, which may be due to implicit biases rooted in race, socioeconomic, or education differences. 9 Furthermore, Black and Hispanic patients consistently receive less risk reduction counseling or referral to cardiac rehabilitation post CABG compared with White patients, with demonstrated poor long‐term survival rates disproportionately affecting younger Black patients. 37 Finally, although current clinical prediction algorithms include race or ethnicity and sex as independent variables to predict postcardiovascular surgery morbidity and mortality, they do not include granular race or ethnicity and social determinants of health, which are powerful prognosticators of postoperative outcomes. 45 , 46 Likewise, global gender inequalities still exist, with female patients less likely to have food and health care security, higher education, and financial independence. 37 , 42 Female patients often carry a significant burden in terms of childcare and child rearing and are more vulnerable to competing priorities in relation to their personal medical care and participation in research trials, potentially leading to underdiagnosis of relevant cardiac conditions in a timely manner. 37 , 47 Understanding upstream contributors to this lack of timely diagnosis of cardiac conditions in women requires a multifaceted approach evaluating health perceptions and disparities in care delivery.

There are several limitations to this study. As this study is retrospective, data extraction was limited to electronic health record review, and comprehensive, individual‐level social determinants of health data were unavailable. Race or ethnicity was based on self‐identification, and biologically assigned sex at birth was extracted from the medical record (gender identity was not studied). The Kaiser Permanente Northern California cohort, although representative of the racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic diversity in the state, may have overrepresentation of Asian and Other Asian/Pacific Islander patients and underrepresentation of White, Black, and Hispanic patients when compared with statistics from the US Census Bureau. 48 Furthermore, grouping of Native Americans, multiethnic groups, and missing race or ethnicity data into an “Other” group may have prevented adequate detection of potential disparities in this heterogeneous population, though data in these groups are limited by small numbers. Strengths of this study include the large cohort size, long‐term follow up based on membership, and use of a diverse integrated health care system, where variability in access to care would be theoretically limited. We also provide granular race or ethnicity data, particularly with disaggregation of a heterogeneous Asian population, to help further understand outcome disparities.

Conclusions

In this large retrospective cohort study from an integrated health care system, disparities in long‐term post‐CABG outcomes persist by sex and race or ethnicity. At Kaiser Permanente Northern California, our efforts have focused on expanding access to health education programs, rooted in lifestyle medicine, and automatic enrollment into our cardiac rehabilitation program following CABG. Additional contemporary efforts are required to reduce upstream risk factor burden in high‐risk groups in order to reduce these postoperative disparities.
