Low-value clinical practices in pressure injury management: a scoping review
Authors: Mengyao Jiang, Xuejun Wang, Yujie Wu, Yaru Xiao, Qi Xiao, Jie Xiong, Weiwei Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow-value care (LVC) is increasingly recognized as a contributor to inefficiency in healthcare. Pressure injury (PI) is a key nursing-sensitive quality indicator and involve numerous interventions. However, to date, no studies have systematically identified which practices qualify as low-value. This scoping review aims to synthesize existing evidence on LVC in PI management to inform research priorities and clinical improvements.MethodWe conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHL, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and CBM databases up to December 10, 2024, to identify eligible articles. Two reviewers independently screened the abstracts and full texts, with conflicts resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Data were extracted using a piloted data-charting form.ResultsOf the 1884 publications screened, 25 met the inclusion criteria. The focus areas of the included articles included PI prevention (n = 13), PI treatment (n = 10), and both prevention and treatment (n = 2). The identified low-value care targets were diverse. They included unrestricted practices (n = 6), dressings and topical agents (n = 4), repositioning (n = 3), support surfaces (n = 3), education (n = 2), risk assessment tools (n = 1), nutritional intervention (n = 1), electrical stimulation (n = 1), negative pressure wound therapy (n = 1), phototherapy (n = 1), electromagnetic therapy (n = 1), and therapeutic ultrasound (n = 1).ConclusionsThis scoping review found that some progress has been made in identifying low-value practices in PI management. However, significant gaps remain. Future research should focus on generating high-quality evidence and incorporating patient preferences to address these gaps.Keywords: Pressure injury, Low-value care, Value-based care, Scoping review, Nursing

Background

The nursing profession constitutes the largest proportion of the healthcare workforce, yet the shortage of nursing human resources remains a persistent challenge [1]. While previous strategies have primarily focused on nurse recruitment, reducing the workload of existing nurses is equally critical. A significant source of this workload may stem from low-value care (LVC)—clinical practices that provide no or minimal benefit, cause harm, or lack evidence-based support for patients [2, 3].

Although LVC research has largely centered on medical practices [4], studies in various healthcare settings have explored LVC in wound care [5], injury care [6, 7], trauma care [8], preoperative care [9], and critical care [10]. For example, cancer care has been shown to exhibit prevalent overuse, including excessive screening and treatment. This necessitates a systematic reduction of practices that are invalid, contradicted by evidence, supported by mixed evidence, or untested [11]. This aligns with global initiatives like the “Choosing Wisely” campaign and research on medical reversal [12, 13]. Notably, however, LVC research has historically focused on medical perspectives, with limited attention to nursing-specific practices [1].

Pressure injury (PI), previously known as pressure ulcer or bed sore, occurs when the skin or underlying tissue is damaged due to prolonged pressure on bony prominences. The global prevalence of PI was 12.8%, with a pooled hospital-acquired PI rate of 8.4%, and an incidence rate of 5.4 per 10,000 patient-days [14]. PIs are particularly common among elderly and less mobile individuals, posing significant challenges and costs for treatment. In the United States, the cost of hospital-acquired PI can exceed $26.8 billion, with approximately 59% of these costs attributed to a small proportion of Stage 3 and 4 full-thickness wounds [15]. PIs are classified into stages ranging from 1 to 4, in addition to unstageable and suspected deep tissue injury categories, with higher stage numbers corresponding to more severe tissue damage. These severe wounds consume considerable clinician time and hospital resources.

PI prevention and treatment management involve a range of interventions, including risk assessment, skin evaluation, repositioning, use of surface support, nutritional support, and education for both staff and patients [16, 17]. For critically ill patients, preventing medical device-related PI is crucial. Common strategies include the use of hyper-oxygenated fatty acids or prophylactic dressings over pressure areas, appropriate device selection, and inter-disciplinary collaboration [18–20]. PIs impose substantial financial and clinical burdens on healthcare systems [21, 22]. For examples, repositioning, defined as the act of turning individuals to alter their body position to relieve or redistribute pressure, is generally recommended by clinical guidelines for individuals at risk of PI every two hours [23]. However, while frequent repositioning effectively alleviates pressure on vulnerable areas, it can also disrupt patients’ sleep, increase pain perception, and lead to increased workload and heightened risk of musculoskeletal disorders for nursing staff, particularly in the lower back. Therefore, frequent repositioning may not always be a beneficial preventive intervention. Despite a robust literature on PI prevention and treatment, no studies have systematically identified which practices qualify as LVC in PI management. Identifying such practices is urgent to optimize nursing efficiency and patient care.

Objective

This scoping review was designed to summarize and synthesis low-value care in PI management. These findings may help to inform research gaps and offer recommendations on further studies.

Methods

Design

We performed a scoping review followed the steps outlined by Arksey and O ´Malley [24]. We reported the review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist [25].

Identification of research question

The scoping review question was “What is known from existing literature about clinical practices in PI care are considered low-value?”

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria

According to the PCC principle [26], namely participants, concept, and context, the inclusion criteria were determined as follows: (1) Participants: Individuals at risk of PI and those who have already developed pressure injuries. Articles addressing chronic wounds (diabetic ulcers, venous ulcers, PIs) were included if PI data could be isolated separately. (2) Concept: According to Li’s concept analysis of LVC [27], low-value care can be defined as care that is deemed to be without benefit after considering the effectiveness, potential harms, cost-effectiveness, alternative measures, and patient values and preferences. LVC had four defining attributes: ineffective by evidence, the harm outweighs the benefits, lack of cost-effectiveness, and misalignment with patient values and preferences. These four attributes had a hierarchical relationship as follow. First, a practice proven by evidence to have no or negligible benefit for patients was considered LVC. Second, a practice proven effective by evidence but causing harm to patients, with the risk of harm outweighing the benefits, was also considered LVC. Third, a practice proven effective and having more benefits than harms but incurring costs not proportionate to the benefits, or with equally effective and cost-effective alternatives, was considered LVC. Finally, a practice proven effective, having more benefits than harms, and being cost-effective but not aligned with patient values and preferences was also considered LVC. PI management included both PI prevention and PI treatment. (3) Context: There is no restriction. The settings can be at home, in hospitals, nursing homes, or other institutions. No date limits were applied. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Research protocols, perspectives, comments, opinions, editorials, letters to editors, books, chapters, policies, guidelines and animal studies; (2) Studies written in languages other than Chinese and English; (3) Studies for which the full text cannot be retrieved; (4) Studies with overt commercial promotional content (e.g., explicit naming and praising of specific products or manufacturers) were excluded, as this was deemed to compromise scholarly objectivity. Furthermore, to ensure a baseline quality of Chinese-language literature, the search was limited to articles published in the Chinese Science and Technology Core Journals database. This database functions as a quality filter, analogous to the Web of Science Core Collection for English literature.

Information sources and search strategy

We performed a comprehensive search using PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health (CINAHL), the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang, VIP, and the China Biomedical (CBM) Literature Database till December 10th 2024. Search terms included keywords and their synonyms relevant to two main concepts: low-value care and pressure injury. Forward and backward citation searching was performed on included articles. Search strategy

Study selection

The search results were exported to the reference management software Endnote (version X9, ClarivateTM, Philadelphia) and duplicate records were removed using the Endnote deduplication feature. After duplicate removal, title and abstracts that met the inclusion criteria were progressed to full-text screening which was independently conducted independently by two authors (JMY, WXJ). Discrepancies were resolved through discussions, when necessary, a third team member (WYJ or XYR) reviewed the discrepancy until a consensus was reached. When there are articles on the same topic, we prioritize the most representative, up-to-date, or complementary ones. Specifically, we select the most recently published articles unless earlier articles have irreplaceable unique value, and also choose articles with more rigorous methodology to avoid unnecessary duplication and ensure the representativeness and efficiency of our scoping study results.

Data extraction

Data from the included studies were extracted into a standardized data extraction form in Microsoft Excel (version 2019). Two authors (JMY, WXJ) independently charted and crosschecked data using a standard template. Disagreements were resolved by a third author (WYJ or XYR). The items of standardized data extraction form included first author, publication year, country, article type, target population, phase of PI management, category of PI practices, and type of LVC. The “Country” refers to the nationality of the first author. The four type of LVC were determined by referring to Li’s conceptual analysis of low-value care, which identifies four attributes of LVC: (1) lack of evidentiary support, (2) harm outweighing potential benefit, (3) lack of cost-effectiveness, and (4) inconsistency with patient values or preferences [27].

Synthesis of Low-Value care practices

A diverse range of interventions was identified as potentially constituting low-value care (LVC) for PI management. These interventions included a broad range of practices without specific restrictions (n = 4) [22, 28–30], repositioning (n = 3) [23, 31, 32], support surface (n = 3) [33–35], dressings and topical agents (n = 2) [36–39], nutritional intervention (n = 1) [40], electrical stimulation (n = 1) [41], negative pressure wound therapy (n = 1) [42], phototherapy (n = 1) [43], electromagnetic therapy (n = 1) [44], and therapeutic ultrasound (n = 1) [45].

A critical refinement was applied to the categorization of these practices based on the conceptual framework by Li et al. [27]. Practices were not classified as LVC solely due to low-certainty or insufficient evidence. Instead, the designation was based on one of four attributes: (1) evidence demonstrating ineffectiveness or no added benefit compared to a standard control (n = 3); (2) evidence that potential harms outweigh benefits (n = 6); (3) a lack of cost-effectiveness, particularly when evidence fails to establish superiority of a more costly intervention over a less costly alternative (n = 16); or (4) misalignment with patient values and preferences (n = 2).

Discussion

This scoping review synthesizes evidence on low-value clinical practices in PI management. We organize the findings using the PAGER framework to delineate patterns, advances, gaps, practice implications, and research priorities [46].

Patterns of evidence

The literature on low-value clinical practices in PI management is limited. Recent studies have attempted to identify effective interventions, but their findings are often inconsistent. For instance, a systematic review [47] found that prophylactic dressings on the sacrum and heels were effective in preventing PIs, while a Cochrane review [36] concluded that the evidence for the effectiveness of dressings is highly uncertain. This discrepancy highlights the need for further research to clarify the role of dressings in PI prevention. Numerous dressings are advocated for PI prevention, yet their impact on PI development remains unclear. In the intensive care unit (ICU), repositioning to relieve pressure is crucial, but the evidence base for device-related PI prevention is severely limited. Recent systematic reviews evaluated PI prevention interventions in acute hospital [48] and ICU settings [47], respectively. Their findings were markedly different: sacral and heel prophylactic dressings were effective in the ICU, while Australian medical sheepskin surfaces were more effective in acute hospital settings. However, these studies were often at high risk of bias, particularly due to attrition bias, with randomized individuals were excluded without justification.

A key challenge in synthesizing evidence for LVC is distinguishing between practices that are proven to be ineffective and those for which evidence is simply uncertain. Misclassifying “uncertain” practices as “ineffective” can stifle innovation and needed research. Conversely, failing to question the routine use of uncertain practices can perpetuate waste and potential harm. Practices with “Evidence of Ineffectiveness” should be prioritized for de-implementation. Practices with “Evidence Uncertainty” should be prioritized for high-quality definitive research, and their use in clinical practice should be highly selective and mindful of cost.

Advances

A meta-synthesis of Cochrane reviews on PI prevention and treatment [49] also highlighted the low certainty of evidence in included trials, resulting in few actionable recommendations for clinical practice. This underscores the challenge of differentiating between LVC and no-value care. LVC may include services that offer minimal benefits but are extremely cost-ineffective. Some of these services, although not directly harmful, can lead to a waste of resources and a decline in overall medical efficiency. No-value care, on the other hand, refers to services that provide no health benefits and may even be harmful, emphasizing “zero benefits” and “pure waste”. In the PI management, examples of LVC include the overuse of advanced dressings and frequent repositioning without considering individual needs. An example of no-value care in PI management is the preventive use of antibiotics for patients with PI but no signs of infection. This practice is not only ineffective but may also lead to antibiotic resistance.

The core difference between LVC and no-value care lies in “whether there is a minimal benefit.” In the PI management, over-intervention (such as the misuse of advanced dressings) and completely ineffective interventions (such as preventive antibiotics) are typical representatives. To de-implement LVC and no-value care, we can consider involving healthcare providers, patients, and their families in jointly developing PI care plans, known as shared decision-making [50]. For instance, by discussing the priority of choosing cost-effective dressings over expensive products. Clinical decision support tools [51] can also be utilized to provide risk assessment results based on electronic systems, avoiding mechanical repositioning plans. Additionally, it is essential to actively promote and implement evidence-based PI clinical practice guidelines [52].

Evidence for practice

A recent study [53] screened the recommendations in nursing guidelines and identified 66 low-value care items, five of which are related to PI: “Avoid a slouched position”, “Avoid unnecessary layers of bedding beneath the patient,” “Avoid the use of ring and donut-shaped tools,” “Do not use synthetic sheepskins,” and “Limit positions that increase pressure.” Therefore, our scoping review did not consider including guideline-type studies.

The Choosing Wisely campaign has also provided recommendations for low-value care in acute wound care, including “Do not clean the wound with saline”, “Do not soak the wound in cleanser”, “Do not use bandages on a wound closed by primary intention”, “Do not use expensive bandages when gauze suffices”, “ Do not discharge a patient without giving instructions ” [5].

A key finding of this review is that the designation of “low-value” is frequently context-dependent rather than absolute. For several interventions, particularly those involving higher-cost technology (e.g., advanced support surfaces, specialized dressings), the evidence does not condemn the technology itself but rather its undifferentiated, routine use. The LVC arises when these interventions are applied broadly to patient populations for which they offer no demonstrable incremental benefit over simpler, less costly alternatives. This distinction is crucial for clinicians and policymakers, as it advocates not for the wholesale abandonment of these technologies, but for their more targeted and evidence-informed deployment.

Gaps

However, patient preferences are often overlooked in the evaluation of LVC. Verkerk et al. [54] first proposed incorporating patient preferences into the evaluation criteria, but most studies fail to consider this dimension. Future research should prioritize patient education and shared decision-making to ensure that interventions align with patient needs and values [55, 56].

Many included reviews conclusions indicated that there were currently insufficient evidence to prove that any specific type of support surface/dressing/topical agents were more effective. In the PI international guideline, support surfaces are one of the interventions for the prevention and treatment of pressure injuries. Support surfaces can help redistribute pressure on affected areas of patients. However, conventional support surfaces are sufficient; there is no need to spend a large amount of money on more advanced low- or high-tech support surfaces, as there is not enough evidence to prove that they are more effective. It may be a case of the 80/20 rule: 20% of the cost can achieve 80% of the functionality, while the remaining 20% of the functionality would require 80% of the cost. Therefore, using high-tech support surfaces was considered LVC, not that support surfaces themselves were LVC.

According to the definition of LVC by Li et al. [27], the first attribute was that a practice proven by evidence to have no or negligible benefit for patients was considered low-value care. Of course, we cannot equate “evidence that does not prove effectiveness” with “evidence that proves harm.” The current evidence was simply limited. Moreover, the Choosing Wisely campaign for LVC advocates the principle of “less is more.” Therefore, we have included valuable research findings to provide a reference for nursing professionals. However, how to specifically define whether a practice that cannot be proven effective by evidence should be considered LVC is worth further in-depth discussion among more scholars.

Research recommendations

Future research should prioritize addressing the identified gaps in the literature. To reduce low-value care in PI management, multiple initiatives need to be launched. Planning for a cost-effectiveness analysis a priori, led by an experienced health economist, should be considered in future trials. Given the plethora of products available, future studies should engage with patients, the public, and decision-makers to determine priority interventions to test in future research. Cutting-edge technologies can also be utilized to de-implement low-value care programs for PI. For instance, machine learning algorithms can be employed to construct predictive models for PI risk. Through the electronic medical record system, patient information can be automatically retrieved, and the patient’s risk level can be calculated, thereby eliminating the need for nurses to use the Braden Scale (whether in paper or electronic format) for risk assessment. This helps to alleviate the clinical workload of nurses. Alternatively, an image analysis system can be used to analyze PI wound images, prompting nurses about the condition of the patient’s wound, the current stage of the pressure injury, and tracking changes in the patient’s wound [57]. For example, the system can compare the length, width, and depth of the wound, as well as the proportion of different tissues (such as gangrene and granulation). Smart mattresses can also assist nurses in helping patients reposition, further enhancing patient care [58].

Limitations

The study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the inclusion criteria limited the articles to those written in English or Chinese, which may have excluded relevant studies published in other languages and potentially introduced language bias. Second, the review identified a wide range of low-value care practices, but the evidence base for these practices was often inconsistent or of low certainty, making it challenging to draw definitive conclusions about their value.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while some progress has been made in identifying low-value practices in PI management, significant gaps remain. A key challenge in identifying LVC is distinguishing between evidence of ineffectiveness and mere evidence uncertainty. While uncertainty does not equate to ineffectiveness, the allocation of resources to expensive interventions with unproven superiority over cheaper alternatives can itself be considered a low-value practice. Future research should focus on generating high-quality evidence and incorporating patient preferences. Additionally, leveraging technological innovations can improve clinical practice and patient outcomes. These efforts will collectively support the development of effective de-implementation interventions to reduce low-value care practices in PI management.
