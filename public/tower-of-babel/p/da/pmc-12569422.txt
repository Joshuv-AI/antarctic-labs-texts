Effective Subcutaneous Efgartigimod Treatment in a 90-Year-Old Female With Myasthenia Gravis
Authors: Yasunobu Nosaki, Takamasa Yokoi, Katsushige Iwai
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disease that affects the neuromuscular junction. The incidence of very-late-onset MG (at age ≥65 years) has increased in recent decades,1 and diagnosing MG in patients aged ≥90 years is challenging owing to the differential diagnoses in this population, including age-related ptosis and limb weakness due to musculoskeletal disorders or stroke. Several treatments for MG are available, including the use of monoclonal antibodies that target the immune system.2 However, there are no well-established treatment strategies for elderly patients with MG, especially for those aged ≥90 years. Here we describe the case of a 90-year-old female diagnosed with MG who was treated with subcutaneous efgartigimod in addition to steroid therapy.

A 90-year-old female with a 2-week history of fatigue, right ptosis, diplopia, dysphagia, and upper limb weakness was referred to our hospital. Despite her advanced age, she had no disability in terms of performing the activities of daily living. Her cognitive function was normal (Hasegawa Dementia Scale—Revised score of 28/30). Three years prior to the referral she had experienced symptoms of left ptosis, diplopia, neck pain, and general fatigue. At that time she was diagnosed with generalized MG based on a positive antiacetylcholine receptor (anti-AChR) antibody titer (66 nmol/L; reference, <0.2 nmol/L) without thymoma. Considering her age and the potential adverse effects of steroids and immunosuppressive drugs, only an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor (pyridostigmine at 180 mg/day) was administered as first-line treatment, which eventually controlled her symptoms. At her second referral, her blood test results remained persistently positive for anti-AChR antibody (45 nmol/L), and MG exacerbation was diagnosed. To minimize the risk of multiple adverse effects, prednisolone alone at 5 mg/day was initiated. However, 4 weeks of steroid therapy proved ineffective, and so weekly subcutaneous efgartigimod was added since this can be administered oneself or by a caregiver via a short injection (typically 30–90 s). After four doses of efgartigimod her symptoms disappeared, without causing skin problems. Four weeks after treatment completion, she experienced a recurrence of MG symptoms, including general fatigue, right ptosis, diplopia, and dysphagia. These symptoms were briefly relieved by a second subcutaneous efgartigimod cycle. Her symptoms improved temporarily during each repeat of the treatment cycle. Efgartigimod is an FcRn (neonatal fragment crystallizable receptor) inhibitor that decreases the circulating levels of pathogenic IgG antibodies by increasing lysosomal degradation.3 Although clinical improvement can be observed in the present patient as early as 1 week after efgartigimod initiation and was sustained for 7 weeks, subsequent cycles were administered according to the patient’s need as long-term individualized treatment.45 Additionally, repeated cycles of efgartigimod administration consistently reduce IgG and anti-AChR antibody titers and continue to be effective, and can even lead to prolonged clinical improvement in some patients.3 Intravenous efgartigimod therapy for MG was approved in Japan in January 2022, while subcutaneous efgartigimod therapy was approved in January 2024. Both types of treatment cycle comprise once-weekly injections administered for 4 weeks. The intravenous efgartigimod dose is 10 mg/kg body weight (with a maximum of 1,200 mg for a body weight of >120 kg), while the subcutaneous efgartigimod dose is 1,000 mg regardless of the body weight. A previous study demonstrated the noninferiority, long-term safety, tolerability, and efficacy of both subcutaneous and intravenous efgartigimod for treating generalized MG.4 Intravenous efgartigimod requires no dosage adjustment in elderly patients (age ≥65 years),3 and injection-site reactions such as erythema, pruritus, pain, bruising, rash, and swelling are the primary adverse events following subcutaneous efgartigimod injections, occurring in 38.2%–42.1% of cases.4 These transient events are of mild-to-moderate severity, and since they resolve without treatment they do not require treatment discontinuation. Other side effects include headache in 12.7%–15.2% of patients. Further, conditions such as urinary tract infections (3.6%), COVID-19 (1.8%), and pharyngitis (1.8%) reportedly developed in 18.2% of patients, most of which were of mild-to-moderate severity and did not lead to treatment discontinuation.4

A previous case report underscored the effectiveness of intravenous efgartigimod treatment in patients with refractory generalized MG who received an inadequate benefit from intravenous immunoglobulin therapy (IVIG) or plasma exchange.6 A comparative study of elderly patients with generalized MG (onset at age ≥65 years) found that intravenous efgartigimod therapy was more effective and safer than IVIG.7 Moreover, successful treatment with intravenous efgartigimod as initial monotherapy in a 90-year-old female with generalized MG and underlying colon adenocarcinoma has been reported.8 The present case report has demonstrated the effectiveness and safety of repeated cycles of subcutaneous efgartigimod therapy in addition to steroid therapy in a 90-yearold female experiencing MG exacerbation. These findings indicate that subcutaneous efgartigimod treatment is effective and well tolerated in elderly patients with MG, even among those ≥90 years old.
