Clinical Outcomes of the Prospective Pakistan Registry of Echocardiographic Screening in Asymptomatic Pregnant Women
Authors: Sabha Bhatti, Sumyia Gurmani, Shakeela Naz, Haleema Yasmin, Shazia Ahmed, Kiran Zehra, Wajid Hussain, Uzma Atif, Nazli Hossain, Musa Karim, Abdul Hakeem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular disease is increasingly recognized as a leading nonobstetric cause of maternal morbidity and mortality worldwide, including low- and middle-income countries.ObjectivesThis study evaluated fetomaternal outcomes in women with and without structural heart disease (SHD).MethodsFrom a total of 25,000 patients enrolled in the Prospective Pakistan Registry of Echocardiographic Screening in Asymptomatic Pregnant Women registry between February 2023 and April 2025, 489 pregnancies with SHD were identified and compared with 510 pregnancies without SHD. Adverse fetomaternal outcomes were evaluated in both groups as a composite endpoint, comprising fetal outcomes (preterm delivery, fetal death, or low birth weight) and maternal outcomes (maternal death and pulmonary edema).ResultsThe SHD cohort had a higher mean maternal age (27.3 ± 5.7 vs 26.1 ± 5.2 years; P = 0.011) compared to the non-SHD cohort. Composite adverse maternal outcomes (3.7% vs 0.4%; P < 0.001), fetal outcomes (29.9% vs 13.3%; P < 0.001), and overall fetomaternal outcomes (30.7% vs 13.3%; P < 0.001) were significantly higher in the SHD compared to the non-SHD cohort. SHD was independently associated with adverse fetomaternal outcomes (adjusted OR: 2.67; 95% CI: 1.93-3.70; P < 0.001).ConclusionsProspective Pakistan Registry of Echocardiographic Screening in Asymptomatic Pregnant Women is the first global study linking subclinical echocardiographic abnormalities in asymptomatic pregnant women to adverse fetomaternal outcomes. In a large cohort of asymptomatic pregnant women, an abnormal echocardiogram was found to be associated with 2- to 4 time as many adverse fetomaternal outcomes compared to a normal echocardiogram. Future studies are needed to evaluate optimal timing of screening, cost-effectiveness, and applicability of broad antenatal screening in higher-income countries.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the predominant nonobstetric cause of maternal morbidity and mortality in developed countries with an increasing trend seen globally.1, 2, 3, 4), 5 This condition significantly increases the risk of adverse maternal and fetal outcomes, including preterm delivery, fetal loss, and maternal death. As most low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) undergo an obstetric transition—shifting from obstetric causes such as hemorrhage and sepsis to nonobstetric causes—CVD is emerging as a major contributor to maternal deaths, mirroring trends observed in high-income countries.4,5 In Pakistan, the maternal mortality ratio (per 100,000 live births) has declined from 432 (281-647) in 1985 to 155 (107-241) in 2023, reflecting significant public health progress.6,7 However, this ratio remains one of the highest globally, underscoring persistent challenges.PerspectivesWhat is known?CVD is increasingly recognized as a leading nonobstetric cause of maternal morbidity and mortality worldwide, including LMICs. What is known?

CVD is increasingly recognized as a leading nonobstetric cause of maternal morbidity and mortality worldwide, including LMICs. Previous studies have shown that screening during pregnancy may help identify structural heart disease.

What is new?

The findings offer compelling evidence of a significantly elevated risk of adverse fetomaternal outcomes in participants with abnormal echocardiographic findings compared to those with normal results, with risks increased by several-fold regardless of symptoms.

What is next?

Larger global multicenter studies are needed to further evaluate the role of screening in pregnancy and whether it affects outcomes. Prognostic risk scores can be developed from such data.

The initial diagnosis of heart disease during pregnancy, coupled with late pregnancy assessment, and delayed recognition of CVD, is strongly linked to poor pregnancy outcomes.8, 9, 10 Early detection of CVD is crucial as it provides opportunities for risk stratification, optimization of care, and preparation for a safe delivery. A standardized approach to CVD screening during pregnancy is thus essential to reduce fetomaternal morbidity and mortality.8, 9, 10 The Prospective Pakistan Registry of Echocardiographic Screening in Asymptomatic Pregnant Women (PRESAP) previously documented a 3.8% prevalence of structural heart disease (SHD)—including left ventricular (LV) dysfunction (LVD), valvular heart disease, and congenital heart disease—among 15,068 asymptomatic women during routine antenatal visits, establishing it as the largest reported registry to date.11 This finding builds on global data from routine echocardiographic screening programs. The prevalence of asymptomatic heart disease in LMIC ranges from 2.5% in India to 8.9% in Sub-Saharan Africa.12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Collectively, these registries encompass tens of thousands of patients across diverse regions and demonstrate the feasibility and varying prevalence of SHD detected through routine antenatal echocardiography.

Despite these extensive screening efforts, there remains a critical evidence gap as to whether subclinical echocardiographic findings in asymptomatic pregnant women, including LVD or valvular lesions, confer an increased risk of adverse fetomaternal outcomes. This lack of outcome data hinders the ability to assess the clinical and public health implications of routine screening, particularly in LMICs where rheumatic heart disease and other cardiovascular conditions are prevalent. PRESAP is unique, not only for its unparalleled sample size, but also for its focus on a resource-constrained population with high maternal mortality, making it ideally positioned to address this gap. This study presents the first outcomes data from the PRESAP registry, offering novel insights into the prognostic significance of routine echocardiographic screening in asymptomatic pregnant women.

Methods

Study design and participant enrollment

This study utilized the PRESAP, an ongoing prospective registry conducted across multiple sites in Pakistan. Initially, 15,068 asymptomatic pregnant women without known structural heart disease were screened between February 2023 and March 2024 at Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Center and the National Institute of Cardiovascular Diseases in Sindh Province, with SHD identified in 569 (3.8%) patients.11 Subsequently, Dow University of Health Sciences Civil Hospital, Karachi, was added as an enrollment site. The current report includes 25,000 patients enrolled until April 2025. Participants were screened once per pregnancy, with prior screening at antenatal visits or known CVD as exclusion criteria.

This study is based on an ongoing registry (PRESAP), in which patients with abnormal findings are followed clinically. Women with a normal screening echocardiogram did not require and, therefore, did not undergo routine cardiology follow-up.

As per the registry protocol, all patients with abnormal echocardiographic findings were followed up (either through in-person clinic visits or via telephone visits) after their expected date of delivery to document pregnancy outcomes. Patients for whom pregnancy outcome data were successfully obtained were included in the SHD cohort.

For comparison, a pool of pregnant women without SHD was randomly selected in a 1:2 ratio (accounting for an anticipated 50% nonresponse rate) and contacted using their registered phone numbers to record pregnancy outcomes.

Clinical assessment

Clinical data, including obstetric history, comorbid conditions (for example, hypertension (self-reported history), anemia), and vitals, were collected from medical records and screening interviews, entered into the registry using Research Electronic Data Capture. Antenatal details, including the diagnosis of pregnancy-induced hypertension made by the primary physician, were obtained from the patient’s records.

Echocardiographic screening protocol

Trained sonographers performed a standardized limited 2-dimensional transthoracic echocardiogram using general electric ultrasound systems and a Butterfly point-of-care device. Data from the Butterfly device were recorded on a secure cloud-based server, accessible to dedicated faculty via encrypted credentials. The protocol included parasternal long-axis, parasternal short-axis, apical 4-chamber, and apical 2-chamber views, with color Doppler across mitral and aortic valves, and continuous wave and pulse wave Doppler across the LV outflow tract to assess gradients. LV systolic function was assessed by 2 readers using visual estimation, with LV ejection fraction (EF) calculated via the biplane Simpson method; discrepancies were adjudicated by a third reader. Per American Society of Echocardiography guidelines, severe LVSD was defined as EF ≤ 30%, moderate as 30% to 40%, mild as 41% to 55%, and normal as >55%, with EF <55% considered significant. Patients with significant (more than mild) valve disease or suspected congenital heart disease underwent a comprehensive transthoracic echocardiogram for detailed assessment. It is important to note that all echocardiograms were performed during pregnancy. The patient selection criteria have been detailed previously.11

Outcomes

Pregnancy outcome data were collected by trained clinicians blinded to the echo findings during clinic and telephone visits. The primary study endpoint was adverse fetomaternal outcomes defined as a composite of adverse fetal outcomes and maternal outcomes. Composite adverse fetal outcomes included fetal death, preterm delivery (<37 weeks), or low birth weight (<2.5 kg). Composite adverse maternal outcomes included maternal death or pulmonary edema.

The secondary outcomes comprised each individual component of the primary composite outcome and a composite of delivery-related complications, including wound infection, bleeding, or any other delivery-related complication.

Management and follow-up

Patients with SHD were referred to an adjacent cardio-obstetrics clinic for management per standard guidelines and followed longitudinally.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the National Institute of Cardiovascular Diseases Institutional Review Board (IRB-32/2023).

Statistics

Data were managed and analyzed using IBM SPSS (version 21). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD for normal distributions or median (IQR) for skewed data. Patients were stratified into 2 groups based on abnormal eco presence. Associations with clinical and demographic factors were assessed using chi-square tests, t-tests, or Mann-Whitney U tests. Univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed to determine the adjusted association between SHD and adverse fetomaternal outcomes incorporating variables significant in bivariate analysis (P < 0.20) or with plausible clinical relevance from literature. Collinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor for the multivariable regression models. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Baseline hemoglobin data were missing for a small number of patients. Given the diagnostic nature of this parameter, no imputation methods were applied. Hemoglobin levels were therefore summarized using complete-case analysis, and the proportion of missing data is reported for transparency.

Discussion

This outcomes study from the PRESAP represents the first investigation to report clinical outcomes in a cohort of asymptomatic pregnant participants that underwent routine echocardiographic screening. PRESAP moves beyond identifying the prevalence of SHD in LMIC and begins to explore prognostic implications of antenatal detection. The findings offer compelling evidence of a significantly elevated risk of adverse maternal-fetal outcomes in participants with abnormal echocardiographic findings compared to those with normal results, with risks increased by several fold. Specifically, the SHD group exhibited a significant increase in preterm delivery (11.7% vs 2.7%), fetal death (13.7% vs 2.9%, P < 0.001), and maternal mortality (2.5% vs 0.4%), culminating in a 2.8-fold higher incidence of composite adverse outcomes (30.7% vs 13.3%, P < 0.001). Logistic regression further confirmed SHD, alongside hypertension, pregnancy-induced hypertension, and age, as independently associated with elevated risk of adverse fetomaternal outcome. With an event rate of 30.7% in the SHD group compared to 13.3% in the non-SHD group, the absolute risk increase is 17.4%, corresponding to a Number Needed to Harm of approximately 6 pregnancies indicating a fetomaternal event per 6 diagnosed cases of SHD.

These results underscore the clinical significance of subclinical SHD, including LVD, valvular lesions, and congenital heart disease—in asymptomatic pregnant women, a population previously considered low risk.17 The persistence of such alarming outcome disparities, even with referrals to cardio-obstetrics specialists, suggests critical gaps in current management strategies. A key contributing factor may be the timing of screening, with 70% of echocardiograms performed in the third trimester, potentially delaying risk stratification and therapeutic interventions that could optimize cardiac status before delivery. This late assessment aligns with prior evidence linking delayed recognition of structural heart disease to poor outcomes,7,8,9 emphasizing the need for earlier screening to enhance maternal and fetal prognosis.

The findings of PRESAP gain additional context when compared to the Madras Medical College Pregnancy and Cardiac (M-PAC) Registry from India, which reported a 1.9% maternal mortality and 15.2% maternal cardiac event rate in 1,029 pregnancies with known or newly diagnosed heart disease.5 Unlike PRESAP, which uniquely screened 15,068 asymptomatic women and identified a 3.8% prevalence of subclinical SHD with subsequent outcomes, M-PAC focused on a preselected cohort with established or pregnancy-diagnosed heart disease, predominantly rheumatic (42%) and complicated by pulmonary hypertension (34.2%). The M-PAC mortality (1.9% vs PRESAP 2.5% in the abnormal echo group) and adverse fetal event rate (33.7%) align with the elevated risks observed in PRESAP (33.3% composite adverse outcomes), the latter’s focus on asymptomatic women reveals a previously unstudied dimension of risk. PRESAP demonstrated a 4.7-fold increase in fetal death (13.7% vs 2.9%) and 5-fold increase in maternal mortality (2.5% vs 0.5%) in women with abnormal echocardiograms—despite their asymptomatic status—underscoring the prognostic power of routine screening. This is in contrast to M-PAC reliance on the modified World Health Organization classification (c-statistic 0.794-0.796), suggesting that PRESAP data could enhance risk stratification models for subclinical disease. Importantly, the shared challenge of late assessment (70% third-trimester screening in PRESAP, akin to M-PAC late diagnosis concerns further supports the urgent need for earlier screening, a hypothesis that a randomized trial comparing routine vs selective approaches could validate.

The global context further amplifies the relevance of PRESAP. As LMICs like Pakistan undergo an obstetric transition, with CVD emerging as a major contributor to maternal mortality, the identification of actionable risk factors through routine echocardiographic screening becomes imperative. This is particularly pertinent given that, despite substantial advancements in medical and surgical management, CVD remains responsible for over 33% of pregnancy-related deaths in the United States, with nearly half deemed preventable.1, 2, 3 The PRESAP cohort, currently of over 25,000 participants, the largest to date, provides robust evidence from a high-risk, resource-constrained setting where rheumatic heart disease predominates. This contrasts with high-income settings where congenital and ischemic heart diseases are more prevalent, suggesting that PRESAP findings may have broader applicability across diverse epidemiological profiles. However, the high adverse outcome rates despite specialist referrals raise questions about the adequacy of downstream care and whether these adverse outcome rates had been even higher without screening.

Study Limitations

Despite the strength of these findings, several limitations merit discussion. First, only approximately 50% of participants with abnormal echocardiograms had detailed follow-up. Although this is a reasonable rate for a large registry in a resource-limited LMIC, it introduces the possibility of selection bias. Second, data on management and downstream care following referral to cardio-obstetrics services were not available in all patients, precluding insights into whether adverse outcomes were driven by delayed therapy, lack of access, medication constraints, or intrinsic disease severity. Third, echocardiography was predominantly performed in the third trimester, limiting the potential for early risk stratification and timely intervention. Earlier detection, potentially in the first or second trimester, may have mitigated some of the observed risk. In addition, the LVD group included patients with a range of cardiomyopathies that could have included peripartum, hereditary or previously unrecognized LVSD, which may differ in their trajectory and treatment response. Importantly, the specific cause of maternal death was not systematically documented in PRESAP. However, given the several-fold higher maternal mortality in women with abnormal echocardiograms (2.5% vs 0.5%), it is reasonable to infer that a substantial proportion of these deaths were related—directly or indirectly—to underlying cardiac dysfunction. These may have occurred through pathways involving decompensated heart failure, arrhythmias, or through indirect consequences such as premature delivery, cesarean-related complications, or thromboembolic events, all of which are more common in structurally abnormal hearts. Finally, adverse obstetric outcomes were assessed by telephone interview at variable intervals, which may introduce recall bias, particularly among participants aware of their structural heart disease diagnosis during pregnancy.

Conclusions

The PRESAP registry outcomes study demonstrates that routine antenatal echocardiographic screening identifies a high prevalence of subclinical SHD in asymptomatic pregnant women, associated with a 2- to 4-fold increased risk of adverse fetomaternal outcomes, including maternal and fetal death. As the first to report outcomes in a screened asymptomatic cohort, these novel and provocative findings support the adoption of routine screening in high-risk settings, with a call for future research on timing and a randomized trial to validate its efficacy.
