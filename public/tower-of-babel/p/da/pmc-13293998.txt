STAT3 interference-driven nanomodulators reverse lipid metabolism-associated chemoresistance and potentiate metalloimmunotherapy in breast cancer
Authors: Zifan Pei, Yifan Yin, Nailin Yang, Shumin Sun, Nan Jiang, Fangyi Yu, Xiaofen Zhang, Jihu Nie, Youdong Chen, Longxiao Li, Jie Wu, Fei Gong, Minming Chen, Wei Liu, Liang Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryClinical evidence has suggested that persistent STAT3 activation and alteration in lipid metabolism correlate with pathological progression in patients with breast cancer, driving resistance to chemotherapy. Herein, we show that H2S, an endogenous gas signaling molecule, sensitizes breast cancer to chemotherapy by suppressing STAT3 signaling-mediated lipid metabolism and improves tumor immunogenicity. Moreover, we further identify Ni2+ as immune agonist for potent metalloimmunotherapy. The construction of an activable nanoplatform enables the controllable delivery of metal ions and H2S gas, which triggers pyroptosis and mobilizes multiple immune cells to elicit systemic anti-tumor immunity in combination with chemotherapy. Moreover, the synergy with αPD-1 inhibits the progression of both primary and metastatic tumors. Our work highlights the potential of bioactive gas transmitters and nutrient metal ions for reversing the dual dilemmas of chemoresistance and low immunogenicity in breast cancer and thus provides a nanosensitization strategy to synergize with multiple clinical treatment modalities.Keywords: breast cancer, STAT3, chemotherapy, H2S, metalloimmunotherapy, αPD-1

Introduction

During the past four decades, the incidence of breast cancer has increased alarmingly, which has become the second most widespread and fatal malignancy in females.1,2 Currently, chemotherapies based on anthracyclines and paclitaxel remain the cornerstone of clinical treatment for breast cancer.3 However, breast cancer cells develop chemoresistance via various molecular biological mechanisms, eventually leading to recurrence and metastasis, with a five-year survival rate of less than ∼30% in patients with advanced breast cancer.4,5 Despite the revolution that immunotherapy has led to contemporary breast cancer treatment, few patients with solid tumors benefit from clinical immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy due to the “cold” nature of the breast tumor microenvironment (TME), with low immunogenicity and immunosuppression.6,7 Therefore, the “battle” against chemotherapy resistance and the immunosuppressive TME of breast cancer should be enduring.

Recent evidences have demonstrated that metabolic reprogramming drives the chemoresistance of cancer cells.8,9,10,11 Generally, tumor cells acquire therapeutic resistance via the upregulation of specific metabolic signaling pathways and the accumulation of immunosuppressive metabolites.8,9,10,11 In breast cancer, alterations in lipid metabolism elicit resistance to chemotherapy, which is regulated mainly by signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signal transduction.12,13,14 The overactivation of STAT3 signaling has been identified in multiple types of cancer, dominating metabolic reprogramming, cell death resistance, and tumor invasion.15,16 Previous studies have shown that STAT3 signaling facilitates lipid deposition and fatty acid synthesis in breast cancer cells, which synergistically lead to the development of drug resistance.14 More importantly, STAT3-driven mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO) promotes the self-renewal and survival of breast cancer stem cells and induces chemoresistance and metastasis of breast tumor.17 The inhibition of STAT3 signaling blocks the expression of diverse lipid metabolism genes and sensitizes multiple cancers to chemotherapy.14,18,19,20,21 Thus, STAT3-driven lipid addiction may be a metabolic vulnerability of breast cancer and a potential therapeutic target. Although various small molecule inhibitors targeting STAT3 have been developed and entered clinical trials, it remains a great challenge to avoid off-target and side effects.22,23 Notably, the use of endogenous biocomponents enables STAT3 inhibition, in which hydrogen sulfide (H2S) gas, a gas signal transmitter, promotes STAT1 signaling via sulfhydration of selenoprotein F (Sep15) and thus antagonizes STAT3 phosphorylation to inhibit Th17 cell differentiation.24 In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), exogenous H2S directly blocks the STAT3 pathway and regulates lipid uptake and metabolism.25,26,27,28 H2S gas has also been proven to be essential for maintaining hypomethylation patterns and inhibiting methyltransferase activities, which may enhance immunogenic molecule expression and result in increased tumor immunogenicity.29,30,31,32 These findings lead to the hypothesis that exogenous H2S can be delivered to regulate STAT3 signaling and lipid metabolism to potentiate the chemosensitivity and immunogenicity of breast cancer. In addition, emerging evidence has demonstrated that nutrient metal ions, as crucial participants in life activities, exert anti-tumor effects to initiate an effective immune response, termed as “metalloimmunotherapy”.33,34,35 Transition metals, such as Mn, Zn, Cu, Fe, and V, activate innate immune pathways and regulate cell death to convert immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones.36,37,38,39,40 However, more immunological mechanisms and therapeutic paradigms of metal adjuvants remain to be investigated.

Herein, we showed that elevated STAT3 signaling and lipid metabolism correlated with pathological progression and drove resistance to chemotherapy in breast cancer. To this end, we found that the delivery of exogenous H2S regulated STAT3-mediated lipid metabolism to sensitize breast cancer to doxorubicin (DOX), a first-line anthracycline chemotherapeutic drug, and improved immunogenicity by alleviating the hypermethylation pattern post chemotherapy. To address the controllable delivery of H2S, we elaborately designed a bioactive nanoplatform based on transition metal sulfides that integrated with “thiol-polysulfide” activated H2S release and metalloimmunotherapy, in which nickel ions (Ni2+) was selected due to its pyroptosis induction and immune adjuvant-like potency. After cellular uptake, albumin (ALB) mineralized nickel sulfide nanoparticles (NiS@ALB) and responded to endogenous glutathione (GSH) cleavage to deliver Ni2+ and H2S, which synergized with chemotherapy to initiate a systemic immune response and increased the sensitivity to ICB. Thus, further combination with αPD-1 increased T cell infiltration and eradicated metastatic tumors. Our work proposed a synergistic therapeutic paradigm of metabolic-immune modulation to potentiate multiple clinical interventions of breast cancer.

Results

Persistent STAT3 signaling and lipid metabolism correlate with progression and chemoresistance of breast cancer

Breast tumors are physiologically immersed in a fat-rich microenvironment; we first conducted immunofluorescence staining of phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) and key genes involved in lipid metabolism, including CPT1A (FAO), ACACA (alias: ACC1, fatty acid synthesis), ACLY (acetyl-CoA generation), and CD36 (fatty acid uptake), in a tissue microarray containing samples from 136 breast cancer patients (Figure 1A. The fluorescence intensities of these genes were analyzed and scored to determine their correlation with STAT3 signaling and pathological grades (Figure S1A). By randomly selecting 10 visual fields of breast cancer tissues, we observed a positive correlation between lipid metabolism and pathological grades (Figures 1B and 1C). The analysis of the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage and the lipid metabolism score of the microarray confirmed a positive correlation between the score and TNM stages (Figure 1D). Moreover, we found that the degree of p-STAT3 activation was positively correlated with the lipid metabolism score, suggesting a potential regulatory interaction and the impact to dynamic progression of breast cancer and key clinical indicators (Figure 1E). Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases confirmed similar results (Figure S1B). Compared with that in normal breast tissues, the expression of genes involved in lipid synthesis and oxidation was significantly higher and indicates poor prognosis in breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA) patients. Moreover, in a retrospective analysis of single-cell and spatially resolved transcriptomic analysis of human breast cancers (NCBI: GSE176078),41 we found that genes involved in lipid metabolism are highly expressed in malignant cells, especially the genes related to fatty acid synthesis and elongation, indicating that cancer cells are active contributors to lipid metabolism in the TME (Figure S1C). Considering that anthracycline-based chemotherapy remains the first-line clinical treatment option for breast cancer and that chemoresistance frequently occurs, we next performed transcriptome sequencing of DOX-responsive (“R”) and non-responsive (“NR”) tumors (Figures 1F and 1G; Figure S1D and S1E). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) indicated significant enrichments in various lipid and fatty acid transport, storage, metabolic, and biosynthetic processes, and positive regulation of tyrosine phosphorylation of STAT protein (Figure 1H). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) confirmed negative regulation of multiple immune effector processes, suggesting the gradual formation of immunosuppressive TME in drug-resistant tumors (Figures 1H; S1F and S1G). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis further demonstrated specific lipid metabolic pathways related to arachidonic acid, linolenic acid, sphingolipid, ether lipid, and linoleic acid metabolism, including hypoxia-related HIF-1 signaling pathways, glycolysis, and gluconeogenesis (Figure 1I). These results collectively indicated that elevated STAT3 signaling and lipid metabolism promoted the progression and chemoresistance of breast cancer.

H2S regulates STAT3-mediated lipid metabolism and potentiates chemosensitivity and immunogenicity

To further elucidate the correlation between STAT3 signaling and lipid metabolism, we incubated 4T1 cells with Stattic, a STAT3 phosphorylation inhibitor, and detected reduced lipid uptake, accumulation, and FAO activity. (Figure S2A). As revealed by western blotting (WB) assay, NaSH (H2S gas donor) decreased the phosphorylation of STAT3 and increased STAT1 signaling as the concentration and time increased (Figures 1J; S2B). The inhibition of STAT3 could lead to remarkable metabolic variations. We detected downregulated expression of CD36 and FABP5, which are essential cellular fatty acid uptake proteins (Figures 1K; S2B). As a result, boron-dipyrromethene (BODIPY) 493/503 and Oil Red O staining suggested a reduction in lipid droplets (Figures 1L; S2C and S2D). Co-treatment with the STAT1 inhibitor fludarabine did not attenuate the inhibition of STAT3 phosphorylation, lipid uptake, or oxidation by H2S (Figure S2E). Considering that the chemoresistance of breast cancer is closely correlated with lipid accumulation and fatty acid metabolism, we further constructed a high-lipid model via sodium oleate induction.42 The increase in cell viabilities indicated the addiction of breast cancer cells to lipids, and the upregulation of p-STAT3 and FAO activity further weakened the cytotoxicity of DOX (Figures S2F–S2H). Sodium oleate treatment increased intracellular lipid droplets, which was reversed by co-incubation with NaSH (Figures 1L and 1M). Accordingly, perilipin 2 (PLIN2), participating in the formation and stability of intracellular lipid droplets, was also detected to have decreased (Figure 1N). The disturbance of lipid droplets may lead to reduced bioenergy. The level of acetyl-CoA, an important intermediate metabolite in energy metabolism, decreased after NaSH treatment (Figure 1O). In lipid metabolism, acetyl-CoA is a precursor for the biosynthesis of various fatty acids, catalyzed by acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC).43 The decreased activity of ACC was further measured (Figure 1P). Thereafter, fatty acids are transported into mitochondria for FAO to obtain energy.43 Carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1A (CPT1A), the rate-limiting enzyme of FAO, was downregulated after NaSH treatment (Figure 1Q). The faint blue fluorescence of the FAOBlue probe and decreased intracellular oxygen consumption also reflected decreased FAO activity and inhibited mitochondrial function, respectively (Figures 1R and 1S; Figure S2I).44 As expected, NaSH co-incubation improved the sensitivity of cancer cells to chemotherapeutic drugs (e.g., DOX and CDDP [cisplatin]), which was more significant under high-lipid conditions (Figures S2J and S2K; Figure 1T). In addition, the expression of various DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) was upregulated in breast cancer, the hypermethylation pattern was maintained, and DFNA5/GSDME expression was downregulated, resulting in decreased immunogenicity (Figure S2L). H2S has been reported to be associated with the demethylation process and enhanced immunogenicity,29,30,31,32 as evidenced by decreased DNMT activity and upregulated DFNA5/GSDME expression (Figures S2M and S2N). The downstream product of demethylation, 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5-hmc), was also elevated (Figure S2O). Thus, H2S alleviated chemotherapy-mediated hypermethylation. The expression of DNMTs was suppressed after NaSH and DOX co-treatment, and the cleaved N-terminal fragment of GSDME was detected (Figure 1U). Next, we performed an in vivo study to validate H2S-sensitized chemotherapy (Figure 1V). Weight loss was a common side effect of DOX chemotherapy in mice (Figure S2P). Although NaSH alone did not inhibit tumor growth, the combination administration significantly improved the therapeutic efficacy of DOX (Figures 1W; S2Q and S2R), which was further reflected by fewer pulmonary metastatic nodules (Figures S2S and S2T). Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Ki-67 staining revealed damaged tumor tissue and inhibited cancer cell proliferation, respectively (Figure 1X). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed upregulated STAT3 signaling and elevated lipid deposition after chemotherapy, which had improved after NaSH treatment (Figure 1Y). Above all, we demonstrated that exogenous H2S inhibited STAT3 signaling and promoted STAT1 signaling, resulting in suppressed fatty acid uptake and β-oxidation. In addition, H2S-mediated methylation inhibition improved immunogenicity, thus synergistically sensitizing breast cancer to chemotherapy (Figure 1Z).

Metal ion screening and construction of NiS@ALB

We first selected typical essential metal ions for organisms to identify potential candidates for metalloimmunotherapy, which resulted in differential cytotoxicity toward 4T1 and DC2.4 cells (Figures 2A and 2B; Figure S3A). Optical microscopy revealed pyroptosis-like cell death (cell swelling and foaming) after Co2+ and Ni2+ treatment (Figure S3B). WB assay suggested that Fe2+, Co2+, and Ni2+ activated the NLRP3 inflammasome in 4T1 cells, with the most significantly cleaved caspase-1 (C-Cas-1) and amino-terminal fragment of GSDMD (GSDMD-N) detected after Ni2+ treatment (Figure 2C). However, the Cas-3-GSDME pathway was not significantly activated and may be associated with the hypermethylation pattern of DFNA5 in breast cancer. Cu2+-induced significant Cas-3 cleavage may primarily lead to apoptosis, consistent with the observed rounding and shrinking morphology (Figures S3B and S3C). Compared with apoptosis, necroptosis, or ferroptosis inhibitors, only VX-765 (a caspase-1 inhibitor) rescued the most Ni2+-induced dying cells (Figure S3D). Additionally, Ni2+ effectively upregulated the expression of Myd88 in DC2.4 cells (Figure 2C). To validate our findings, bone-marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were extracted for incubation with metal ions. Mn2+, a potent STING agonist, first stimulated DCs maturation, but seemed toxic at high concentrations. As expected, Co2+, Ni2+, and Cu2+, all stimulated DCs maturation, and highest proportion of matured DCs was detected after Ni2+ incubation (Figures 2D; S3E). The brightest Myd88 fluorescence in the Ni2+-treated DCs was also captured (Figure 2E). These results suggested that metal ions had great potential in the positive regulation of anti-tumor immune response, in which Ni2+ could activate inflammasomes, trigger inflammatory cancer cell death, and efficiently stimulate DCs maturation via Toll-like receptor (TLR)-Myd88 axis, making them competitive candidates for metalloimmunotherapy.

Next, we intended to construct a nanoplatform integrated with both metalloimmunotherapy and stable H2S release. A simple ALB-assisted biomineralization method was selected to synthesize bioactive NiS@ALB nanoparticles. In brief, bovine serum albumin served as a template to provide binding sites and adsorbed Ni2+ ions, and then Na2S was added under alkaline conditions. After mineralization and aging at room temperature, NiS nucleated and grew in the albumin nanocages, forming NiS@ALB nanoparticles with abundant polysulfides that could respond to GSH cleavage and release H2S gas through the “thiol-polysulfide” mechanism (Figure 2F).45,46,47,48 Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed a spherical morphology of the NiS@ALB nanoparticles, where Ni and S were uniformly distributed (Figures 2G and 2H; Figure S3F). The hydrodynamic diameter of NiS@ALB was ∼140 nm in different media, and the zeta potential was −29.3 ± 1.51 mV, ensuring dispersion and stability under physiological conditions (Figure 2I). X-ray diffraction (XRD) revealed the amorphous structure of NiS@ALB (Figure S3G). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis suggested multiple chemical states of nickel. Sulfur existed mainly in three forms, including metal sulfides (∼161.7 eV, attributed to Ni–S bonds), organic sulfides (∼163.5 eV, including C–S, S–S, and polysulfides), and sulfates (∼167.8 eV) (Figures 2J and 2K; Figure S3H). Due to the susceptibility of polysulfides to be cleaved by GSH, NiS@ALB efficiently depleted GSH with increasing time and concentration (Figures 2L–2O), during which process H2S gas was released, as indicated by the lead acetate test paper and the WSP-1 probe (Figures 2P and 2Q). XPS analysis indicated that nickel was partially reduced by GSH. Notably, the decrease in the proportion of organic sulfides indicated the fragmentation of polysulfides by GSH attack (Figures 2R and 2S; Figure S3I). Raman spectroscopy revealed that a polysulfide peak located at ∼480 cm−1 was weakened after GSH treatment (Figure 2T).49 WSP-1 probes measured accelerated H2S release with increasing GSH concentration (Figures 2U; S3J). H2S release reached its peak within 1 h and remained significantly higher than under GSH-free conditions for 5 h (Figures 2V; Figures S3K and S3L). The methylene blue (MB) colorimetric method further quantified H2S yield. which increased with enhancing GSH concentration and time (Figurrs 2W and 2X; Figures S3M–S3P). Furthermore, NiS@ALB degraded into colorless and transparent solution in aqueous solution (Figure 2Y). TEM images also revealed the collapse of NiS@ALB nanostructures into ultra-small nanodots (Figure 2Z). Together, the above results demonstrated the successful construction of an activatable nanoplatform for co-delivery of bioactive gas and metal ions.

Gas-metabolic nanomodulators potentiate the chemosensitivity of breast cancer

Given the outstanding properties of NiS@ALB, the in vitro biological effects were subsequently investigated in 4T1 breast cancer cells. Intracellular H2S generation was detected via WSP-1 probes. The addition of 1 mM GSH increased the fluorescence intensity, which decreased after pre-treatment with N-ethylmaleimide (NEM, a thiol scavenger) (Figure 3A). JC-1 staining detected an increase in green fluorescence of abnormal mitochondria, suggesting the collapse of the mitochondrial membrane potential (Figures 3B; S3Q). WB assay revealed the downregulation of STAT3 phosphorylation and CD36, FABP5 expression after NiS@ALB treatment, mainly attributive to H2S release (Figure 3C). The inhibition of lipid droplets could lead to decrease in PLIN2 expression and subsequent disturbance of bioenergy. Similarly, both acetyl-CoA and ACC decreased after NiS@ALB treatment (Figures 3G–3I). In addition, acetyl-CoA is also a precursor for cholesterol biosynthesis.43 Weak blue fluorescence of cholesterol was observed as indicated by Fillipin Ⅲ staining (Figure S3R). Downregulated CPT1A and FAO activities were subsequently detected, which may also be associated with H2S-mediated mitochondrial damage (Figures 3J; S3S). These effects ultimately resulted in a reduction in the level of intracellular ATP (Figure 3K). Although sodium oleate-induction increased the number of intracellular lipid droplets and cholesterol deposition, NiS@ALB reversed these effects (Figures 3L–3N). Further investigation of lipid metabolism indicators also confirmed the potential of NiS@ALB to regulate fatty acid metabolism under high-lipid conditions (Figures 3O–3R). As expected, lower FAO activity, intracellular ATP levels, and oxygen consumption rates were detected (Figures 3S–3U; Figure S3T). At concentrations that did not cause significant cytotoxicity, NiS@ALB co-incubation increased the cytotoxicity of chemotherapeutic drugs (Figures S3U and S3V). More importantly, high-lipid models improved the resistance of 4T1 cells to DOX but became more sensitive after NiS@ALB treatment (Figure 3V). Collectively, these data suggested that NiS@ALB could serve as potent gas-metabolic nanomodulator to increase the chemosensitivity of breast cancer (Figure 3W).

Metalloimmunotherapy elicits pyroptotic cancer cell death and mobilizes immune cells

We next evaluated the potential of NiS@ALB as a metalloimmunotherapeutic agent for improving the immunogenicity of breast cancer (Figure 4A). MTT results suggested significant cytotoxicity compared with that of NaSH or Ni2+ alone (Figure 4B). Immunogenic cell death was evaluated via calreticulin (CRT) exposure, high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) release and ATP leakage (Figures 4C and 4D). To further elucidate the mechanism of cell death, swelling and foaming pyroptosis-like morphologies were captured via optical microscopy, and abundant lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release was detected (Figures S4A and S4B). Annexin V-FITC/PI staining revealed giant bubbles on the plasma membrane and propidium iodide (PI) permeability in Ni2+- and NiS@ALB-treated cells, suggesting changes in membrane permeability during inflammatory cell death (Figure 4E). WB assays detected NLRP3 inflammasomes assembly and the emergence of C-Cas-1 and GSDMD-N. IL-1β and IL-18 were matured and released (Figure 4F). Although Ni2+ upregulated the reactive oxygen species within the cells, no significant variations were detected in the lipid peroxidation (LPO) levels (Figures S4C and S4D). The co-treatment with cell death inhibitors further confirmed that pyroptosis was the dominant form of cell death (Figure S4E). Next, we explored the potential of NiS@ALB for sensitizing breast cancer cells to chemotherapy. Compared with incubation with DOX alone, co-incubation with NiS@ALB resulted in brighter CRT fluorescence (Figures 4G and 4H), and higher concentrations of HMGB1 and ATP were detected in the cell supernatant (Figures 4I and 4J). Moreover, release of H2S could mediate the demethylation process (Figure S4F). WB assay revealed that the upregulation of methyltransferases post DOX treatment was alleviated by co-incubation with NiS@ALB, indicating that chemotherapy-induced drug resistance was efficiently inhibited (Figure 4K). Therefore, increased immunogenicity enabled DOX to elicit GSDME-mediated pyroptosis, characterized by increased GSDME-N and C-Cas-3 expression (Figure 4K). To further elucidate the underlying therapeutic mechanisms, transcriptome analysis was performed to reveal mRNA variations in NiS@ALB-treated 4T1 cells (Figures 4L; S4G and S4H). Notably, key genes involved in lipid metabolism, FAO process, and methylation were significantly downregulated, while genes related to inflammatory response and demethylation process were significantly upregulated (Figure 4M). KEGG analysis of all the DEGs suggested enrichment of various pro-inflammatory signaling pathways (Figure 4N). GO enrichment indicated that genes were distributed mainly in inflammation- and metabolism-related gene sets, suggesting the disturbance of these biological processes (Figure 4O). GSEA confirmed the above results and revealed the positive regulation of various anti-tumor immune responses (Figure S4I).

Thus far, we discovered that NiS@ALB triggered a pyroptosis-mediated inflammatory response. Given that Ni2+ ions are potent immune agonists, we next investigated their ability to mobilize immune cells. As expected, both Ni2+- and NiS@ALB matured BMDCs (Figures 4P and 4Q) and promoted TNF-α and IL-12p70 secretion (Figures 4R; S4J). Further validation indicated that Ni2+ polarized RAW264.7 cells toward the M1 phenotype (Figures 4S; S4K) and elevated the levels of various pro-inflammatory cytokines in the cell supernatant (Figures 4T; S4J). Immunofluorescence revealed the mechanisms by which Ni2+ increased the expression of iNOS, Myd88, and IRF3, which subsequently led to the ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of IKBα, freeing NF-κB for nuclear translocation (Figures 4U and 4V; Figure S4l). The above evidence revealed that NiS@ALB served as a potent metallo-adjuvant to activate the TLR-Myd88-NF-κB axis to mobilize immune cells (Figure 4W).

In vivo anti-tumor efficacy and immune evaluation

After identifying NiS@ALB as multifunctional nanoplatform for modulating metabolic-immunotherapy, we performed in vivo experiments to study its therapeutic efficacy and mechanism (Figure 5A). Free Ni2+ and Stattic marginally inhibited tumor growth. In contrast, two doses of NiS@ALB injection successfully suppressed tumor progression (Figures 5B and 5C; Figures S4M and S4N). Furthermore, the imaging of tumors after NiS@ALB injection revealed that large amounts of H2S were released within 24 h and reached the baseline level after 72 h. The inhibition degree of p-STAT3 was also strongly correlated with in vivo H2S release (Figures S4O–S4Q). Immunofluorescence staining revealed suppressed p-STAT3 and lipid metabolism indicators, and increased NLRP3, C-Cas-1, and GSDMD-N after 2 doses of NiS@ALB treatment (Figures 5D; S4R). In addition, there was no significant fluctuation in the weight of the mice during the treatment period, and no obvious abnormalities in liver or kidney functions were detected (Figures S4S–S4U).

Immune evaluation was performed two days after the last administration (Figure 5E). Flow cytometry revealed significant DCs maturation in tumors after Ni2+ sensitization (Figures 5F; S5A). Notably, Stattic had certain immune activating effects, suggesting the potential of STAT3 inhibition in immunotherapy. Stimulated by antigen presentation of mature DCs, the number of CD8+ T cells nearly doubled, and the proportion of CD4+ T cells increased after treatment with NiS@ALB, and H2S also activated T cells, which was consistent with previous studies (Figures 5G and 5H; Figures S5B and S5C).50 The upregulation of IFN-γ+ T cells indicated the potent activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (Figures 5I; S5D). Both Ni2+ and NiS@ALB sensitized natural killer (NK) cells (Figures 5J; S5E), and polarized the tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) toward M1 phenotype. (Figures 5K; S5F and S5G). Accordingly, immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs) were downregulated (Figures 5L; S5H). Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) rely on STAT3 signaling to maintain their immunosuppressive function,51,52 which was relieved by the STAT3 inhibitors H2S and Stattic. However, NiS@ALB treatment recruited MDSCs, possibly to counteract the intense inflammatory response triggered by Ni2+ (Figures 5M; S5I). As previously demonstrated, upregulated cxcl2 and pyroptotic cell death could transmit recruitment signals to MDSCs to negatively regulate the immune response (Figure 4M).53,54 Thus, high levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IFN-γ, and IL-12p70 were detected within the tumor (Figures 5N; S5J). In addition, we detected higher proportions of matured lymphatic DCs and TNF-α concentrations after NiS@ALB treatment (Figure S5K), even though the levels of other cytokines did not significantly differ due to sample number limitations (Figure S5L). The combined administration of VX-765 (Belnacasan), a specific caspase-1 inhibitor, weakened the therapeutic effects (Figures 5O and 5P; Figure S5M), and fewer tumor-infiltrating matured DCs, CD8+/CD4+ T cells, and NK cells were detected (Figures 5Q–5T; Figures S5N–S5Q). Notably, VX-765 co-treatment reduced MDSCs infiltration within the tumor and downregulated the expression of Arg-1, an immunosuppressive effector marker of MDSCs, further demonstrating that NiS@ALB-induced pyroptosis was a direct trigger for MDSCs chemotaxis (Figures 5U and 5V; Figure S5R). Therefore, combining αLy6G/Ly6C (Gr-1) antibodies with NiS@ALB treatment to eliminate MDSCs achieved better efficacy and promoted more T cell infiltration (Figures 5W–5Y; Figure S5S).

NiS@ALB sensitizes breast tumors to DOX and stimulates a systemic immune response

Since chemoresistance is a major challenge in breast cancer treatment, we explored the potential of NiS@ALB to sensitize breast cancer to DOX (Figure 6A). The resistance to DOX led to negligible anti-tumor effects, while NiS@ALB merely suppressed tumor growth in the early stage. However, the combined administration inhibited tumor growth within 20 days and resulted in the lightest spleen weight (Figures 6B and 6C; Figures S6A–S6C). Furthermore, fewer pulmonary metastatic nodules were observed after the combination treatment (Figures 6D and 6E). H&E and Ki-67 staining revealed widespread cancer cell death and tumor proliferation inhibition in the combination group (Figure 6F). Immunofluorescence revealed the activation of STAT3 signaling and a subsequent increase in lipid deposition after chemotherapy, which could be the basis for the acquisition of drug resistance in breast cancer cells and was reversed post NiS@ALB treatment (Figure 6G).

We next investigated the initiation of systemic immune responses (Figure 6H). DOX could induce immunogenic cell death and possess certain immune activation properties. To our excitement, a higher proportion of matured migratory DCs (MigDCs) and resident DCs (ResDCs) were detected in the lymph nodes after combination therapy (Figures 6I and 6J; Figures S6D and S6E). Accordingly, the maturity of the DCs within the tumor increased (Figures 6K; S6F). The upregulation of antigen-presenting MigDCs and subsequent maturation of ResDCs marked the effective initiation of anti-tumor immune response. These antigen-presenting cells present tumor antigens through MHC-2 molecules to guide the recognition of helper T cells, triggering an adaptive immune response. Therefore, the percentages of both CD8+ and CD4+ T cells significantly increased (Figures 6L and 6M; Figures S6G and S6H). In addition, the ratio of M1 to M2 macrophages was elevated in combination therapy group (Figures 6N; S6I–S6K). The number of MDSCs remained high in the TME, indicating severe local inflammation (Figure S6L). Moreover, we extracted the spleen for lymphocyte analysis. Notably, the increase in the number of effector IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells indicated the activation of systemic immune response (Figures 6O; Figures S6M and S6N). More importantly, we observed an elevated proportion of effector/memory CD44+ CD8+ and CD44+ CD4+ T cells in the spleen (Figures 6P and 6Q; Figures S6O and S6P). Correspondingly, high concentrations of TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-1β both in serum and tumor were detected (Figures 6R–6T; Figure S6Q). To further demonstrate the generation of immune memory, we conducted tumor rechallenge experiments (Figure S7A). The NiS@ALB-sensitized chemotherapy group exhibited significant tumor control and T cell infiltration, with more IFN-γ+ effector CD8+ T cells being detected in the rechallenged tumors (Figures S7B–S7D). Meanwhile, both CD8+/CD4+ central memory T cells (Tcm) and effector memory T cells (Tem) were increased in peripheral blood (Figures S7E and S7F). Finally, to evaluate the limitations of our strategy, we detected increased PD-L1 expression after combined chemoimmunotherapy, which delivered negative regulatory signals and upregulated PD-1 expression (Figure 6U). The induction of PD-L1 expression may be due to therapeutic immune stimulation and could ultimately result in T cells exhaustion.55

Combination with ICB therapy effectively inhibits tumor growth and metastasis

The signals of upregulated PD-L1 and PD-1 expression encouraged us to further combine ICB therapy for better efficacy, as this suggested a potential sensitivity to PD-L1/PD1 blockade. Given that the combination of pembrolizumab (an anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody) and chemotherapies has been approved as a first-line treatment in patients with metastatic triple-negative breast cancer,56 αPD-1 was selected for subsequent combination therapy trials. We constructed bilateral 4T1 tumor models for validation of systemic anti-tumor immunity (Figure 7A). Both free DOX and αPD-1 failed to inhibit tumor growth, and combination therapy with DOX and αPD-1 also showed poor efficacy, consistent with the small proportion of clinically beneficial patients. Although NiS@ALB + DOX administration successfully suppressed primary tumor progression, it moderately inhibited distant tumor growth. However, the triple combination therapy with αPD-1 significantly controlled both primary and distant tumors and effectively inhibited pulmonary metastasis (Figures 7B–7D; Figures S7G and S7H). Due to the blockade of PD-1 expression, the numbers of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells in primary and distant tumors further increased (Figure 7E).

Given that combined therapy with αPD-1 could amplify T cell-mediated systemic immunity to overcome bilateral and metastatic tumors, we next constructed an orthotopic 4T1 tumor model to better simulate the physiological microenvironment of breast cancer, in which metastatic nodules can spontaneously form in multiple organs (Figure 7F). Through in vivo bioluminescence imaging, we observed that the mice receiving combined administration of NiS@ALB and DOX exhibited weaker bioluminescence signals than those receiving either chemotherapy or ICB therapy. Both primary and metastatic tumors were inhibited within 25 days after further combination with the αPD-1 antibody (Figures 7G–7I; Figure S7I). Bioluminescence imaging of the lungs of the mice in each group revealed fewer pulmonary metastatic nodules in the combination treatment group (Figure 7J). These results collectively demonstrated that NiS@ALB-sensitized chemotherapy plus αPD-1 immunotherapy could effectively inhibit orthotopic breast tumor progression and prevent lung metastasis.

Potent anti-tumor capacity in a drug-resistant xenograft model

The xenograft model simulates the growth environment of human tumors and reflects the sensitivity to experimental drugs. To this end, we constructed a drug-resistant xenograft model by subcutaneously inoculating human breast cancer MCF-7/ADR (adriamycin-resistant) cells into the right flank of nude mice (Figure 7K). The poor efficacy of single DOX treatment indicated the resistance of the MCF-7/ADR cells to chemotherapy. Although NiS@ALB moderately inhibited tumor growth due to an insufficient immune response in immunodeficient nude mice, the combination treatment suppressed tumor growth within 40 days (Figures 7L and 7M). Overall, the above results indicated that NiS@ALB could sensitize DOX-resistant humanized tumors to chemotherapeutic agents.

Discussion

Extensive evidence suggests that metabolic alteration, as one of the most recognized hallmark phenotypic characteristics in tumors, is largely orchestrated by STAT signaling, which simultaneously regulates the expression of multiple genes at transcriptional and epigenetic levels.14,57 In breast cancer, the excessive activation of STAT3 promotes cancer cell survival, growth, and invasion, which is also associated with progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance.58 The correlation between STAT3 signaling and reprogrammed lipid metabolism has been established.14 The STAT3 proteins increase not only lipid import by acting as transcription factors to upregulate CD36 and FABPs expression, but also lipid synthesis and mitochondrial FAO in breast tumor cells, which play crucial roles in tumorigenesis and chemotherapy resistance.14,17,59

Inspired by the bioactivity of endogenous gas signaling molecules, we demonstrated the potential of H2S in sensitizing breast cancer to chemotherapy. Although a previous study reported that H2S enhanced chemotherapeutic efficacy by promoting drug penetration,60 we aimed to establish a bridge between gas metabolism modulation and improved chemosensitivity. Exogenous H2S maintained STAT1 phosphorylation and suppressed cancer-promoting STAT3 activation to regulate lipid metabolism by reducing lipid deposition and mitochondrial FAO. Despite the competitive inhibition of STAT1 and STAT3, the addition of STAT1 inhibitor did not reverse the inhibition of STAT3 by H2S, suggesting that H2S could regulate STAT3 signaling independently in breast cancer. The direct inhibitory effect of H2S on STAT3 has been investigated in models that include but are not limited to HCC,25,26 microglial cells,61 smooth muscle cells,62 and renal tubular cells.63 The underlying mechanisms are closely related to protein persulfidation-mediated STAT3 inactivation and inhibition of IL-6-mediated inflammation, whereas endogenous H2S deficiency stimulates STAT3 phosphorylation, further suggesting the relevance of gas transmitters to STAT3 in multiple disease models.25,64,65 In addition, H2S relieved the hypermethylation pattern post DOX treatment by inhibiting the activity of DNMTs to improve tumor immunogenicity, leading to upregulated GSDME expression and cleavage. Although H2S alone did not cause direct killing of tumor cells, as the complex metabolic network enables H2S to upregulate glucose metabolic flux through energy compensation pathways,66 multiple mechanisms indicated that exposure to H2S increased the sensitivity of breast cancer cells to chemotherapeutic drugs as demonstrated both in vitro and in vivo.

Furthermore, transition metal ions are potent TLR agonists, which play critical roles in innate and adaptive immune response.33 Ni2+ and Co2+ have been identified as metal contact allergens that elicit inflammatory reactions.33,67 We selected Ni2+ ions as potent candidates for metalloimmunotherapy, which could serve as effective metallo-adjuvants to activate multiple immune cells and trigger Cas-1-GSDMD-dependent cancer cell pyroptosis. Although Ni2+-induced human TLR4 activation is species-specific because of protein conformation,68 Ni2+ could directly activate MyD88 and IL-1 receptor signaling in mice and trigger allergies,69 which are key adaptor proteins activated by upstream TLRs to bridge the downstream NF-κB pathway to promote innate immune signaling.

Based on the above findings, NiS@ALB was constructed for controllable H2S delivery to modulate STAT3 signaling and reduce lipid uptake, synthesis, and oxidation in breast cancer cells. Although ACC-mediated fatty acid synthesis competes with the mitochondrial FAO process, the co-downregulation may be a synergistic result of STAT3 intervention, since STAT3 signaling regulates both lipid synthesis and oxidation.14,17 H2S disrupted mitochondrial function, and the down-regulation of co-metabolic substrate acetyl-CoA led to the systemic collapse of intracellular energy metabolism, which synergistically sensitized breast cancer cells to DOX. In addition, Ni2+-mediated metalloimmunotherapy elicited cancer cell pyroptosis and mobilized multiple immune cells to initiate strong anti-tumor immunity. Although H2S inhibited intratumoral MDSCs infiltration, intense local inflammation post NiS@ALB treatment upregulated this cell population. The inhibition of pyroptosis attenuated tumor suppression and reduced MDSCs chemotaxis and immunosuppressive function. In vivo depletion of MDSCs improved therapeutic efficacy and T cell infiltration. Notably, H2S levels after drug injection were positively correlated with p-STAT3 inhibition in vivo. The high intratumoral gas concentrations within 12–24 h significantly inhibited STAT3 signaling, providing a “therapeutic window” for subsequent combination chemotherapy. Therefore, NiS@ALB sensitized breast tumors to chemotherapy and activated a systemic immune response to eradicate metastatic tumors. The combined chemoimmunotherapy provided immune protection in mice, strongly inhibited rechallenge tumor growth, and increased immune memory and effector T cell populations. The synergy with αPD-1 further promoted T cell infiltration, which successfully suppressed bilateral and orthotopic breast tumor progression and inhibited pulmonary metastasis.

Our study focused on metabolic rearrangement to induce chemotherapy resistance and low immunogenicity in breast cancer, and proposed a bioactive gas-metal ion strategy for metabolic-immune modulation (Figure 7N). Multiple preclinical models confirmed the robust efficacy and translational potential of NiS@ALB. These findings are critical for advancing current clinical interventions for cancer therapy.

Limitations of the study

We acknowledge certain limitations of this study. First, although we found that H2S gas transmitter could inhibit STAT3 signaling and maintain STAT1 signaling to enhance chemotherapy sensitivity in breast cancer, the specific mechanism remains unclear. Further exploration of the active sites will help to develop more targeted therapeutic strategies. Second, the in vivo primary models employed in this work, which are mainly based on 4T1 and MCF-7 cell lines, may not fully reproduce the genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity of human breast cancers. Future use of organoid models or patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models will help improve the relevance of translational research. Third, given the chemotaxis of MDSCs generated by robust immune stimulation, the possibility of multiple combination therapies should be investigated, as elimination of this population of immunosuppressive cells is crucial for the efficacy of long-term immunotherapy. Finally, as a conceptual validation, local drug administration may not fully fit the clinical treatment scenario. Future studies focusing on systemic drug delivery routes would greatly accelerate clinical translation. The tumor enrichment of nanodrugs, in vivo gas release kinetics, and the potential biosafety of metal ions are worth further investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

4T1 and luciferase-expressing 4T1 (4T1-Luc) murine breast cancer cells, and RAW264.7 murine leukemia monocyte macrophages were obtained from ATCC. DC2.4 murine bone marrow-derived dendritic cells were obtained from the Cell Bank, Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. MCF-7/ADR cells were purchased from Shanghai Fuheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All the cell lines were authenticated by STR profiling by the provider, and were routinely tested to confirm the absence of mycoplasma contamination. The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 mg/mL) in a humidified incubation chamber at 37°C and 5% CO2. DOX·HCl (500 ng/mL) was added into the culture medium of the MCF-7/ADR cells to maintain a drug-resistant phenotype. Sodium oleate (100 μM) was added into the 4T1 culture medium to simulate a high-lipid condition.

Animals and tumor model

6-8 weeks old female BALB/c mice, C57BL/6 mice, and 4–6 weeks old female nude mice were purchased from Hangzhou Qizhen Biological Technology Co. Ltd. All procedures involving mice were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Soochow University Laboratory Animal Center (Protocol Number: 202409A0811). The mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in a controlled environment (temperature: 22 ± 2°C; humidity: 50 ± 10%; 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to standard rodent chow and autoclaved water. To establish the tumor model, 4T1 cells (2 × 106) in PBS (50 μL) were subcutaneously injected into the right side of the back of each mouse. For the bilateral tumor model, 2 × 106 4T1 cells were injected into the right side of the back of each mouse on day −7 for the primary tumor, and another 1 × 106 cells were subsequently injected into the left side for the distant tumor. For the orthotopic breast tumor model, 2 × 106 4T1-Luc cells were injected into the mammary fat pad of mice. For the xenograft tumor model, 1 × 107 MCF-7/ADR cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of nude mice. The mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups after tumor cell inoculation and confirmation of palpable tumor formation.

Note on sex of animals

This study utilized female mice for tumor models. As single-sex cohorts were used for each animal model, a formal analysis of the influence of sex on the reported outcomes was not performed. This represents a limitation to the generalizability of the findings.

Construction and analysis of tissue microarray

The human sample consisted of 136 breast cancer patients (East Asian women, mean age 54.8 ± 9.1 years, range 33–76) with clinical diagnosis and informed consent. Their postoperative breast tumor tissues were pathologically confirmed and collected to construct the tissue microarray, which was purchased from Hunan Aifang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. This study was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Soochow University (Approval No. SUDA20260120H01). The human breast cancer tissue microarray was further stained with p-STAT3 (570), CPT1A (520), CD36 (690), ACLY (620), ACACA/ACC1 (480) and DAPI for immunofluorescence in Hunan Aifang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Subsequently, the lipid metabolism indicators (CPT1A, CD36, ACLY, ACACA/ACC1) and p-STAT3 were scored as negative (−), weak (+), moderate (++) and positive (+++) respectively by two experienced pathologists according to the fluorescence intensity.

Method details

Analysis of public databases

GSE176078 dataset was sourced from the GEO database from NCBI.41 The corresponding single-cell data and annotation results were downloaded in .h5 format from TISCH (BRCA_GSE176078). The R software MAESTRO and Seurat were used to process and analyze the single-cell data. The t-SNE method was used to re-cluster the cells. Lipid metabolism-related genes and prognostic information of BRCA were obtained and analyzed from Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (gepia.cancer-pku.cn) based on TCGA and GTEx databases (log2|FC| cutoff: 0.585, p value cutoff: 0.05).

Synthesis of NiS@ALB nanoparticles

NiS@ALB nanoparticles were synthesized via a simple room temperature mineralization method. First, 1 mL of Ni(CH3COO)2 aqueous solution (100 mM) was added dropwise into 6 mL of BSA solution (10 mg/mL) under magnetic stirring. After thorough mixing, the pH was slowly adjusted to 8–9 by the addition of NaOH (1 M). Then, another 2 mL of Na2S aqueous solution (100 mM) was added dropwise into the mixture. The system immediately turned into a dark black solution, indicating the successful formation of NiS precipitates. The above reaction was allowed to proceed for 12 h to mineralize and form NiS@ALB nanoparticles. To purify the synthesized NiS@ALB, the solution was transferred to an ultrafiltration tube (100,000 MWCO) and centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 30 min to remove unreacted components. After repeating three times, the NiS@ALB nanoparticles were resuspended in water and stored at 4°C for further use.

Characterization

TEM images were taken on an FEI Tecnai F20 TEM at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV. XPS measurement was conducted with a PHI Quantera SXM instrument equipped with an Al X-ray excitation source (1486.6 eV). Powder XRD patterns were collected on a PANalytical X-ray diffractometer. UV-Vis-NIR absorption spectra were obtained with a PerkinElmer UV-Vis spectrophotometer. The dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential of the nanoparticles were recorded using a MAL VERN ZEN3690. The absolute contents of nickel were measured via inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). The aqueous solution used in the experiment was prepared from deionized water from a Milli-Q water purification system.

GSH consumption

NiS@ALB nanoparticles were incubated with GSH (1 mM) in aqueous solution at 37°C. After different time s of incubation, the above system (20 μL) was injected into a PBS solution (480 μL). Then, 2.5 μL (20 mM) of DTNB was added to the mixture. The absorbance at ∼412 nm was analyzed via UV-vis-NIR spectroscopy.

Qualitative and quantitative determination of H2S release

WSP-1, a H2S-sensitive probe (10 μM), was incubated with various reaction mixture, and a NaSH standard curve was generated for 3 h at 37°C. The fluorescence was observed at Ex/Em 465/515 nm by a microplate reader. In addition, a piece of lead acetate test paper was adhered to the top of the small Petri dishes, and co-incubated with different reaction systems containing NiS@ALB or GSH at 37°C for 3 h to observe the color change of the piece of test paper. MB colorimetric method was used for the quantitative determination of H2S content. NiS@ALB and GSH aqueous solution were co-incubated for different times at 37°C, and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant (solution A). Zinc acetate (40 mM) and sodium acetate (10 mM) were mixed to form solution B. Then, 1 mL of solution A and 1 mL of solution B were mixed for 30 min. Finally, 20 μL of FeCl3 and N, N-dimethyl-p-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride (DMPD·2HCl, 0.15 mol/L), were added into the system respectively for another 30 min of incubation at 37°C to form blue MB. The absorbance at ∼664 nm was detected. A standard curve was established with different concentrations of NaSH solution to calculate the linear relationship between concentration and absorbance.

Cytotoxicity

Cell viability was evaluated via the standard MTT assay. In brief, 2 × 104 cells were seeded per well in a 96-well plate under normal or high-lipid conditions for overnight adhesion. After various treatments, the culture medium was removed, and MTT was added. After incubation for 4 h, DMSO was used to dissolve formazan. The absorbance was determined at 490 nm using a microplate reader. The cell morphology was observed with an optical microscope. To determine the synergistic killing effect of H2S and DOX, the cells were pre-treated with NaSH (250 μM) for 12 h and then co-treated with NaSH (250 μM) and DOX for another 12 h under normal and high-lipid conditions. To determine the cytotoxicity of various metal ions, 4T1 cells and DC2.4 cells were co-treated with MnCl2, FeCl2, CoCl2, NiCl2 and CuCl2 for 12 h of incubation. To determine the cytotoxicity of NiS@ALB, the cells were treated with different concentrations of NaSH, NiCl2, or NiS@ALB and incubated overnight. To determine the synergistic killing effect of NiS@ALB and DOX, NiS@ALB (100 μM) and DOX were co-treated under normal and high-lipid conditions and incubated overnight. To determine the form of cell death, 10 μM of different inhibitors, including the apoptosis inhibitor Ac-DEVD-CHO, necroptosis inhibitor Nec-1, ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1, and pyroptosis inhibitor VX-765, were co-incubated with NiCl2 (500 μM) and NiS@ALB (200 μM) for 12 h.

Transcriptome analysis

mRNA sequencing and analysis were performed on 4T1 cells from the NiS@ALB group and the untreated group (500 μM for 12 h, n = 3). Total mRNA from the cells was extracted with TRIzol reagent. High-throughput sequencing of the mRNAs was performed, and the mRNAs were analyzed at GENEWIZ Co., Ltd. Suzhou, China. The significant differential expression of genes was statistically analyzed by log2|FC| ≥ 1 and q value (FDR, p adj) ≤ 0.05. For transcriptome sequencing of tumor tissues, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with DOX (5 mg/kg) on days 0 and 3. On day 10, the DOX-responsive (“R”) and non-responsive (“NR”) 4T1 tumors (n = 3) were extracted, and cut into small pieces (2 mm × 2 mm × 2 mm), and then frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. High-throughput sequencing was performed with “NR” versus “R” at GENEWIZ Co., Ltd. Suzhou, China, and the data was analyzed via https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn, an online platform for data analysis and visualization. The significant differential expression of genes was statistically analyzed by log2|FC| ≥ 0.585 and q value (FDR, p adj) ≤ 0.05.

Western blotting analysis

4T1 cells or DC2.4 cells were seeded in 5 cm culture dishes and treated with 500 μM of MnCl2, FeCl2, CoCl2, NiCl2, CuCl2, NaSH, NiS@ALB, or Stattic (10 μM) for overnight incubation. In addition, NaSH (500 μM) or NiS@ALB (250 μM) were co-treated with DOX (100 μM) for 12 h of incubation, respectively. The proteins were collected with lysis buffer containing protease inhibitor (PMSF, 1 mM). After adding equal amounts of protein and SDS-PAGE separation, the proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes and then blocked with 5% BSA for 2 h. The membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4°C and secondary antibodies for 4 h. After washing with PBST (PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20), the electrochemiluminescence reagent was added and the membranes were exposed.

5-hmc staining

4T1 cells (2 × 105) were seeded on 12-well plate for overnight adhesion, incubated with different concentrations of NaSH for 12 h, or with 250 μM of NaSH for different times, or with 250 μM of NaSH, NiCl2, or NiS@ALB for 12 h. The cells were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. For 5-mC or 5-hmC staining, 0.1% Triton X-100-permeabilized cells were denatured with 2 N HCl for 15 min, and then neutralized with 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.5) for 10 min. After blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h. the cells were stained with a primary anti-5-hmc antibody for 2 h and an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody for another 1 h at room temperature. After the samples were washed with PBS and stained with DAPI, images were acquired via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).

Lipid and cholesterol staining

For Oil Red O and BODIPY 493/503 staining, 2 × 105 4T1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates under normal or high-lipid conditions. After overnight adhesion, the cells were treated with 250 μM of NaSH, NiCl2, NiS@ALB, and Stattic (10 μM), or with NiS@ALB (250 μM) under normal or high-lipid conditions for 12 h of incubation. NaSH (∼500 μM) was used to treat cells under normal or high-lipid conditions for 24 h, and an additional 500 μM of NaSH was supplemented in the culture medium after 12 h of incubation. In addition, different concentrations of NaSH were added for 12 or 24 h of incubation. After all the treatments, the cells were stained with a commercial Oil Red O staining kit according to the manufacturer’s protocols, and observed under an optical microscope. Subsequently, isopropanol was added to extract the Oil Red O dye from each group, and the absorbance was measured at 500 nm for quantification. For BODIPY 493/503 staining, 10 μM of the fluorescent probe was co-incubated with the treated cells for 30 min. After DAPI staining, CLSM was used for visualization. Fillipin Ⅲ was used for cholesterol staining. 12-well plate-adhered 4T1 cells were treated with 250 μM of NaSH, NiCl2, NiS@ALB, and Stattic (10 μM), or NiS@ALB (250 μM) under normal or high-lipid conditions for 12 h of incubation. The cells were stained with Fillipin Ⅲ (0.1 mg/mL, 1 h) for CLSM visualization.

JC-1 and FAO assay

JC-1 was used to evaluate the function of mitochondria. 12-well plate-adhered 4T1 cells were treated with NiS@ALB (250 μM), GSH (1 mM) or CCCP (10 μM) for 6 h. The cells were washed and stained with JC-1 (10 μM) for 30 min. The fluorescence was analyzed via CLSM and flow cytometry. To detect FAO activity, FAOBlue, an intracellular FAO fluorescent probe, was applied. 2 × 105 4T1 cells were uniformly seeded on a confocal dish for overnight adhesion and treated with 250 μM of NaSH, NiCl2, NiS@ALB, Stattic (10 μM), or NiS@ALB (250 μM) under normal or high-lipid conditions for 12 h of incubation. 500 μM of NaSH was used to treat cells under normal or high-lipid conditions for 24 h, and an additional 500 μM of NaSH was supplemented in the culture medium after 12 h of incubation. After all the treatments, the cells were washed and stained with 10 μM of FAOBlue for direct CLSM visualization at Ex/Em 405/460 nm. To detect intracellular oxygen consumption, cells from each group treated as described above were stained with the oxygen-sensitive probe [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 (10 μM) for 30 min, whose fluorescence could be quenched by intracellular oxygen. After fixation and DAPI staining, the red fluorescence was detected via CLSM.

Detection of PLIN2, acetyl-CoA, ACC, and CPT1A

4T1 cells (2 × 105) were seeded in 12-well plates under normal or high-lipid conditions. After overnight adhesion, the cells were treated with 250 μM of NaSH, NiCl2, NiS@ALB, or Stattic (10 μM), as well as NiS@ALB (250 μM) under normal or high-lipid conditions for 12 h of incubation. Cells were treated with 500 μM of NaSH under normal or high-lipid condition for 24 h, and the culture medium was supplemented with an additional 500 μM of NaSH after 12 h of incubation. Then, all the cells were lysed and the supernatant were collected for ELISA analysis of PLIN2, acetyl-CoA, ACC and CPT1A using a commercial kit.

Intracellular H2S, ROS and LPO detection

12-well-plate-adhered 4T1 cells were treated with NiS@ALB (250 μM) and GSH (1 mM) for 3 h at 37°C. Thiols in cells were scavenged by N-ethylmaleimide (NEM, 1 mM), which was used for 30 min of pretreatment of the adherent cells. A commercial H2S-sensitive fluorescent probe WSP-1 (50 μM) was used for cell staining at 37°C for 30 min. For intracellular ROS detection, the cells were treated with 250 μM of NaSH, NiCl2, or NiS@ALB for 12 h, and then stained with DCFH-DA (20 μM) for 30 min. After DAPI staining, the cells were observed via CLSM. For LPO detection, the cells were stained with Liperfluo (10 μM) for 30 min, and then were washed for flow cytometry analysis.

Pyroptosis staining

A total of 2 × 105 4T1 cells were uniformly seeded on a confocal dish for overnight adhesion and treated with 500 μM of NaSH, NiCl2, or NiS@ALB for 12 h. The cells were stained with annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) for 30 min. The morphology of the pyroptotic cells was acquired via CLSM.

Detection of CRT and HMGB1

A total of 2 × 105 4T1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured for 12 h. The cells were incubated with 250 μM of NaSH, NiCl2, or NiS@ALB for 12 h. For immunofluorescence staining, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and treated with 0.1% Triton X-100/PBS solution for CRT staining and 1% Triton X-100/PBS solution for HMGB1 staining. After being blocked with 5% BSA, the cells were treated with primary anti-calreticulin and anti-HMGB1 antibodies for 2 h and then stained with an Alexa Fluor 488/647-conjugated secondary antibody for another 1 h at room temperature. The washed cells were further stained with DAPI before CLSM visualization. For flow cytometry, the cells were treated with NiS@ALB (250 μM) and DOX (100 μM) for 8 h. The cells were washed and analyzed via flow cytometry following the above steps without paraformaldehyde fixation and DAPI staining. For detection of HMGB1 release, the cell supernatant was collected for a commercial kit testing.

Detection of ATP

For detection of intracellular ATP, 12-well plate-seeded 4T1 cells were treated with 250 μM of NaSH, NiCl2, NiS@ALB, or Stattic (10 μM) for 12 h, as well as 250 μM of NiS@ALB under normal or high-lipid conditions for 12 h. For the detection of released ATP, 12-well plate-seeded 4T1 cells were treated with 500 μM of NaSH, NiCl2 and NiS@ALB, or NiS@ALB (250 μM) and DOX (100 μM) for overnight incubation. Subsequently, the supernatant and cell lysate were both collected for ATP detection via a commercial kit following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Dendritic cell maturation and macrophage polarization

Bone marrow stem cells were obtained from the leg bones of C57BL/6 female mice aged 4–6 weeks and further stimulated with 20 ng/mL GM-CSF to differentiate into BMDCs for 6 days. Then, the DCs were plated at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and treated with different groups (various metal ions, NaSH, NiCl2, NiS@ALB and lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1000 ng/mL)) for 16 h. The supernatant was collected for ELISA analysis, and the DCs were stained with antibodies of FITC-CD11c, APC-CD80, and PE-CD86 to detect the matured DCs (CD80+ CD86+ DCs in CD11c+ DCs) via flow cytometry. In addition, RAW264.7 cells were uniformly seeded and treated with NaSH, NiCl2, NiS@ALB and LPS (1000 ng/mL) for 16 h. The supernatant was collected for ELISA analysis, and the cells were stained with antibodies of FITC-F4/80, PE-CD80, and APC-CD206 to detect M1 macrophages (CD80+ F4/80+ cells) and M2 macrophages (CD206+ F4/80+ cells) via flow cytometry.

Immunofluorescence of iNOS, Myd88, NF-κB, IKBα, and p-IRF3

RAW264.7 cells were treated with 500 μM of NaSH, NiCl2, NiS@ALB, or LPS (1000 ng/mL) for 12 h. BMDCs were treated with 500 μM of different metal ions for 12 h. For the immunofluorescence assay, 4% paraformaldehyde-fixed BMDCs or RAW264.7 cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (1% Triton X-100 for NF-κB staining) and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. The cells were then stained with primary antibodies for 2 h and an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody for another 1 h at room temperature. After DAPI staining, CLSM was used for visualization.

In vivo study

Drugs were administered when the 4T1 tumor volume reached ∼100 mm3, and the tumor size and body weight of the mice were monitored every two days. The tumor volumes were calculated via the following formula: volume = length × width2/2. For combination treatment with H2S and DOX, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5): (G1) untreated; (G2) NaSH (i.t.); (G3) DOX (i.v.); and (G4) NaSH (i.t.) + DOX (i.v.). NaSH was injected on days 0, 3, and 6 at a dose of 5 mg/kg, and DOX was injected on days 1, 4, and 7 at a dose of 5 mg/kg. The mice were randomly euthanized for histopathological and immunofluorescence staining of H&E, Ki-67, p-STAT3 and BODIPY 493/503 after the last administration. In addition, the mice were sacrificed, and their lungs were extracted for H&E staining to evaluate the number of metastatic nodules. To visualize the spatial diffusion of H2S within the tumor, the fluorescent probe Sulfidefluor-7 AM (SF7-AM) was employed. In brief, fresh tumor tissues (n = 4) were collected at key time points (pre-injection of NiS@ALB, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-injection), and immediately cut into the largest section and stained with 20 μM SF7-AM for 30 min at 37°C in a humidified chamber protected from light. Fluorescence images were acquired and quantified under identical acquisition settings with an IVIS Lumina III in vivo fluorescence imaging system. The excess tumor tissues were fixed and subjected to p-STAT3 immunofluorescence staining. To determine the anti-tumor efficacy of NiS@ALB, the mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5): (G1) untreated; (G2) NaSH (i.t.); (G3) NiCl2 (i.t.); (G4) NiS@ALB (i.t. on day 0); (G5) NiS@ALB (i.t. on days 0, 3); and (G6) Stattic (i.t.). The concentration of NiS@ALB was set to 0.5 μmol per mouse, and the molar amounts of NaSH, NiCl2 and Stattic were consistent with those of NiS@ALB. After the last administration, the mice were randomly euthanized for histopathological and immunostaining analysis of H&E, Ki-67, WSP-1, NLRP3, cleaved-caspase-1 and GSDMD-N. For the combination treatment of NiS@ALB and VX-765, the mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 5): (G1) untreated; (G2) NiS@ALB (i.t.); and (G3) NiS@ALB (i.t.) + VX-765 (i.p.). NiS@ALB and VX-765 was injected on days 0 and 3 at a dose of 0.5 μmol and 30 mg/kg per mouse, respectively. For in vivo MDSCs depletion, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5): (G1) untreated; (G2) αLy6G/Ly6C (Gr-1) (i.p.); (G3) NiS@ALB (i.t.); and (G4) NiS@ALB (i.t.) + αLy6G/Ly6C (Gr-1) (i.p.). NiS@ALB was injected on days 0 and 3 at a dose of 0.5 μmol per mouse, and αLy6G/Ly6C (Gr-1) was injected on days 1 and 4 at a dose of 200 μg per mouse. The mice were randomly euthanized for tumor immunofluorescence staining of CD8 and CD4. For the combination treatment of NiS@ALB and DOX, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5): (G1) untreated; (G2) NiS@ALB (i.t.); (G3) DOX (i.v.); and (G4) NiS@ALB (i.t.) + DOX (i.v.). NiS@ALB was injected on days 0 and 3 at a dose of 0.5 μmol per mouse, and DOX was injected on days 1 and 4 at a dose of 5 mg/kg. The mice were randomly euthanized for histopathological and immunofluorescence staining of H&E, Ki-67, p-STAT3 and BODIPY 493/503 after the last administration. After all the treatments, the mice were sacrificed and their lungs were extracted for H&E staining to evaluate the number of metastatic nodules. For the rechallenge tumor model, mice with light tumor burden after combined treatment were subcutaneously reinoculated with another 2 × 106 4T1 cells on the other side two days after the last administration. The tumor growth was monitored and tumor sections were randomly selected for CD8/CD4 staining.

Immune evaluation

To determine the immunological effects of NiS@ALB, once the 4T1 tumor volume reached ∼100 mm3, the mice were grouped and treated as described above. The mice were sacrificed on day 5 for immune evaluation. The tumors and the lymph nodes near the tumors were extracted and processed into single-cell suspensions. Samples were stained with antibodies for flow cytometry detection (DCs: FITC-CD11c, APC-CD80, and PE-CD86; T cells: FITC-CD3, APC-CD4, PE-CD8, and PerCP-CD45; IFN-γ+ T cells: FITC-CD3, PE-CD8, APC-IFN-γ, and PerCP-CD45; Tregs: FITC-CD3, APC-CD4, PerCP-CD45, and PE-Foxp3; NK cells: FITC-CD3, APC-CD49b, and PerCP-CD45; macrophages: FITC-F4/80, APC-CD206, PE-CD80, PerCP-CD45; MDSCs: FITC-CD45, APC-Gr-1, and PE-CD11b). In addition, the supernatants of the tumors and lymph nodes were collected for ELISA analysis of TNF-α, IL-1β, IFN-γ, and IL-12p70 followed by the manufacturer’s protocols. For immune evaluation of the combination treatment with VX-765, the mice were sacrificed on day 5 to collect the tumors and the lymph nodes near the tumors. The single-cell suspensions were stained for DCs, T cells, NK cells and MDSCs (FITC-CD11b, APC-Gr-1, PE-Arg-1, and PerCP-CD45) evaluation. For immune evaluation of the combination treatment of NiS@ALB and DOX, once the 4T1 tumor volume reached ∼100 mm3, the mice were grouped and administrated the same as described above. All the mice were sacrificed on day 6. The tumors, spleens, and lymph nodes near the tumors were extracted and processed into single-cell suspensions. Samples were stained with antibodies for flow cytometry detection (lymph DCs: FITC-CD11c, APC-CD86, PE-MHC-2, PerCP/Cyanine5.5-CD80; Tumor DCs: FITC-CD11c, APC-CD86, PerCP/Cyanine5.5-CD80; T cells: FITC-CD3, APC-CD4, PE-CD8, PerCP-CD45; Spleen IFN-γ+ T cells: FITC-CD3, PE-CD8, APC-IFN-γ, PerCP-CD45; Spleen CD44+ T cells: FITC-CD44, APC-CD3, PE-CD8, PerCP-CD4; macrophages: FITC-F4/80, APC-CD206, PE-CD80, PerCP-CD45; MDSCs: FITC-CD45, APC-Gr-1, PE-CD11b). The peripheral blood of the mice was centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 15 min at 4°C for serum separation. The serum and supernatants of the tumors were collected for ELISA analysis of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IFN-γ followed by the manufacturer’s protocols. For evaluation of immune memory, peripheral blood was collected from the mice two days after tumor rechallenge for immune memory T cell analysis (FITC-CD3, PE-CD8, PE-CD4, APC-CD62L, and PerCP-CD44). All mouse tumors were collected at the end for effector T cell analysis (FITC-CD3, PE-CD8, APC-IFN-γ, and PerCP-CD45). The gating strategies were presented in Figure S8.

Bilateral tumor model in combination with αPD-1 therapy

When the primary tumor reached ∼100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5): (G1) untreated; (G2) DOX (i.v.); (G3) αPD-1 (i.p.); (G4) DOX (i.v.) + αPD-1 (i.p); (G5) NiS@ALB (i.t.) + DOX (i.v.); and (G6) NiS@ALB (i.t.) + DOX (i.v.) + αPD-1 (i.p.). NiS@ALB was injected into the primary tumor on days 0 and 3 at a dose of 0.5 μmol per mouse. DOX was injected into the tail vein on days 1 and 4 at a dose of 5 mg/kg. αPD-1 was intraperitoneally injected on days 2 and 5 at a dose of 200 μg per mouse. The tumor sizes and body weights of the mice were recorded every two days. The tumor volumes were calculated via the following formula: volume = length × width2/2. After the last administration, the mice were randomly euthanized for histopathological and immunostaining analysis of CD8 and CD4. After all the treatments, the lungs were extracted for evaluation of the number of metastatic nodules.

Orthotopic breast tumor model

When the orthotopic 4T1-Luc tumor reached ∼100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5): (G1) untreated; (G2) DOX (i.v.); (G3) αPD-1 (i.p.); (G4) DOX (i.v.) + αPD-1 (i.p); (G5) NiS@ALB (i.t.) + DOX (i.v.); and (G6) NiS@ALB (i.t.) + DOX (i.v.) + αPD-1 (i.p.). NiS@ALB was injected into the tumor on days 0 and 3 at a dose of 0.5 μmol per mouse. DOX was injected into the tail vein on days 1and 4 at a dose of 5 mg/kg. αPD-1 was intraperitoneally injected on days 2 and 5 at a dose of 200 μg per mouse. The tumor sizes were recorded every two days, and the tumor volumes were calculated via the following formula: volume = length × width2/2. For bioluminescence imaging, the mice were anesthetized and intraperitoneally injected with a saline solution of D-Luciferin potassium salt (1.5 mg/mL, 200 μL per mouse). The bioluminescence images of the mice and metastatic lung nodules were obtained with an IVIS Lumina III in vivo fluorescence imaging system.

Drug-resistant xenograft tumor model

When the size of the MCF-7/ADR tumor reached ∼50 mm3, all the nude mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5): (G1) untreated; (G2) DOX (i.v.); (G3) NiS@ALB (i.t.); and (G4) NiS@ALB (i.t.) + DOX (i.v.). NiS@ALB was injected into the tumor on days 0 and 3 at a dose of 0.5 μmol per mouse. DOX was injected into the tail vein on days 1 and 4 at a dose of 5 mg/kg. The tumor sizes were recorded every four days, and the tumor volumes were calculated by the following formula: volume = length × width2/2.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The data were presented as the mean values ±SD. Data in flow cytometry were analyzed with FlowJo X , and quantified by mean fluorescence intensity (M.F.I.). Statistical analysis was conducted via GraphPad Prism software 8. Unpaired t-test, one-way ANOVA comparison test, and two-way ANOVA comparison test were used to compare the differences between groups, according to the experimental design and data structure.
