Identification of candidate genes and ceRNA networks of HPG axis in regulating sexual precocity of roosters by whole transcriptomic sequencing
Authors: Hai Xiang, Xiangkun Li, Xiaoxu Sun, Zhengfen Zhang, Jixiang Wei, Zhijie Liu, Zheng Ma, Siyu Chen, Junwei Gou, Fei Ye, Hua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrecocious puberty in chickens is an important biological trait regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis. The comb height of roosters is an indicator of precocious puberty in breeding. However, the underlying mechanisms of precocious puberty, particularly in roosters, remain underexplored.ResultThis study utilized whole-transcriptome sequencing of the hypothalamus, pituitary, and testicle to identify candidate genes and competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks associated with precocious puberty. The result showed that early sexually mature roosters exhibited more developed testicles and comb, along with elevated levels of gonadotropin -releasing hormone (GnRH), luteinizing hormone (LH), and testosterone. A total of 108 differential expressed genes (DEGs), 18 differential expressed lncRNAs (DElncRNAs), and 54 differential expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs) were identified in the hypothalamus; 190 DEGs, 27 DElncRNAs, and 45 DEmiRNAs in the pituitary; and 339 DEGs, 48 DElncRNAs, and 626 DEmiRNAs in the testicle. Notably, NMRK2 and PSMD14 were co-expressed across these tissues, with higher expression in sexually mature roosters. Furthermore, 13 genes including PCDH9, RAB3C, MYO5A, SYNE2, ATP8B5 and EIF4G1, were co-expressed in the hypothalamus and pituitary, while 17 genes including SMG7, PTPRS, C20ORF194 and RNF213 were co-expressed in the pituitary and testicle. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis identified key regulatory genes for sexual maturation, including MYO5A, NMRK2, AKT3, CREB1, and EIF4G1 in the hypothalamus; ERBB4, MYO5A, PTPRF, and CDK6 in the pituitary; and SRC, PSMD14, UBE2O, and PIK3CB in the testicle. The ceRNA network TCONS_00062798/TCONS_0008908—gga-miR-206—AKT3 in the hypothalamus regulates GnRH secretion, promoting sexual maturation via ErbB and MAPK signaling pathways. The ceRNA network circ_02349 0-TCONS_00071970- gga-miR-183/gga-miR-200a-3p/novel-m29153p—CDK6 of the pituitary may inhibit LH release by disrupting the cell cycle, reducing sexual maturation. In the testicle, the ceRNA network novel_circ_022575/novel_circ_006317/ENSGALT0 0000076697/ENSGALT00000046820—miR-16-x/gga-miR-16c-5p—SRC regulates testosterone secretion through PPAR, TGF-β, and mTOR signaling pathways, indirectly influencing GnRH and gonadotropin release.ConclusionThese findings identified candidate genes and ceRNA networks associated with sexual precocity regulation in the HPG axis of the rooster, not only providing novel mechanisms underlying rooster sexual precocity but also highlighting the complex interplay between tissues in the HPG axis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12971-w.

Introduction

Precocious puberty is defined as a pathological state in humans, often leading to early puberty. However, it is a considerable economic trait in poultry. The condition known as central precocious puberty (CPP) in humans is characterized by the hypothalamic-pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis, which results in the disruption of normal hormonal levels during pubertal development. This disruption can manifest in a variety of ways, including accelerated linear growth and early bone age [1]. The sexual maturity time of roosters is a key economic trait that affects carcass quality and reproductive performance. Sexual precocity can reduce production costs and improve production efficiency. The study of precocious puberty in poultry can facilitate the breeding process and enhance production efficiency. The comb height of roosters, as a secondary sexual characteristic, directly reflects the endogenous androgen levels and can serve as a phenotypic selection criterion for evaluating rooster sexual precocity. The process of sexual maturation is primarily regulated by reproductive hormones, which are mainly controlled by the HPG axis. Relevant studies have demonstrated that sex hormones in roosters influence the development of the comb, and the secretion of these hormones is regulated by the HPG axis [2].

The initiation of human puberty is a complex process involving the interplay between the upstream regulation of the hypothalamic Kisspeptin-Neurokinin -Dynorphin (KNDY) system and the downstream regulation of the gonadotropin -releasing hormone (GnRH) pulse generator [3]. The GnRH pulse generator is a neural network constituted by the HPG axis. The activation of GnRH neurons signifies the initiation of puberty, with the frequency pulse release of GnRH being regulated by the KNDY system [3]. However, the role of the KNDY system in avian sexual maturation has not yet been established, and related research remains to be conducted. In a similar manner, the activation of GnRH neurons marks the onset of animal sexual maturity. The increase in GnRH pulse secretion is primarily attributable to transsynaptic changes and glial input [4, 5]. The aforementioned changes consist of an increase in excitatory inputs (e.g. glutamate and kisspeptin) and a decrease in inhibitory inputs (e.g. MKRN3, GABA, opioid neurons, RFRP and GnIH). The role of glial components, encompassing growth factors and diffusible small molecules, in directly or indirectly stimulating GnRH secretion, is also a subject of interest. For instance, astrocytes and ependymal glia [6] have been shown to mediate this process through the ErbB signaling pathway, thereby released luteinizing hormone (LH) by the pituitary [7].

In the context of vertebrates, the hypothalamic–pituitary–testicular (HPT) axis establishes a continuous signal hierarchy, thereby ensuring the maintenance of optimal circulating levels of sex hormones. This regulatory mechanism is pivotal in the orchestration of gonadal activity, steroid secretion, and coordinating with environmental factors [8]. The sexual maturity of roosters has been suggested to be regulated by three levels of hormones in the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, and testicle. As reported by Woods et al. [9], the HPG axis commences function at 13.5 days for males and 14.5 days for females during embryonic development. Prior to this, the hypothalamus, adenohypophysis, and gonads have been observed to function independently. In hens, the HPG axis exerts a dominant influence over the reproductive system, directly regulating ovulation, and consequently egg production [10]. The HPG axis in poultry exhibits plasticity, regulating immunity and reproduction through the synthesis and release of hormones [11, 12].

The ceRNA network plays a crucial role in the regulation of the HPG axis and is a core molecular mechanism mediating upstream environmental signals and downstream reproductive endocrine functions [13]. Currently, most of the research on the HPG axis via the ceRNA network focuses on single-organ combined studies involving the gonads [14, 15], while research on the initiation mechanism of sexual maturation in chickens based on the HPG axis has predominantly focused on female chicken [16, 17]. Research on the initiation mechanism of male sexual precocity mainly focuses on the identification of key genes and regulatory pathways. To date, no research has been conducted to systematically explore the mechanism of male chicken sexual precocity from the dual perspectives of the HPG axis and the ceRNA network through multi-organ joint analysis. The present study aims to perform whole transcriptome sequencing on the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, and testicle of roosters at high and low sexual precocity levels to find differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially expressed non-coding RNAs (DEncRNAs) in different tissues. And by deploying functional enrichment as well as construction of PPI protein interaction network and the ceRNA regulatory network, this work will provide a preliminary exploration of the molecular markers and regulatory mechanisms underlying the HPG axis-mediated development of rooster sexual precocity.

Materials and methods

Animals and sample collection

A total of 300 Qingyuan partridge roosters were selected for this study from the routine breeding and management flocks of Guangdong Tinoo’s Foods Group Co., Ltd. All roosters were subjected to standardized routine feeding and management under identical conditions. At 42 days of age, 100 roosters with the comb heights of 22–29 mm were designated as the high comb group, and 100 roosters with the comb heights of 10–17 mm were classified as the low comb group [18]. The roosters were maintained until they reached 77 days of age. Fifteen roosters from the high comb group (H) and fifteen roosters from the low comb group (L) were selected for the experiment at random. Approximately 1 mL of blood was collected from the wing vein of 10 roosters from each group. During the slaughtering process, the following phenotypic traits were measured for all 30 roosters: body weight, comb height, comb length, comb base thickness, comb weight, testicle long axis, testicle short axis, and testicle weight. Subsequently, the hypothalamus, pituitary, and testicles were collected and immediately stored in liquid nitrogen.

Serum separation and hormone determination

Ten blood samples from each of the high and low comb groups were placed in sterile tubes, centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatants were frozen at −20 °C, avoiding repeated freezing and thawing. After that, the concentrations of GnRH, LH, and testosterone were measured using double-antibody sandwich ELISA by Chicken GnRH ELISA KIT (Ruixin Biotech,China), Chicken LH ELISA KIT (Ruixin Biotech,China) and Chicken Testic ELISA KIT (Ruixin Biotech,China). All experimental procedures were conducted precisely following the kit manuals' manufacturer's instructions.

RNA extraction, libraries construction, and RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the hypothalamus, pituitary, and testicles of both high comb group (n = 3) and low comb group (n = 3), following the manufacturer's protocol (Invitrogen). Genomic DNA was removed in order to retain mRNAs and ncRNAs, using a DNase I kit (TaKara). RNA quality was assessed with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyser, and quantity was measured using the NanoDrop ND-2000. Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. performed library construction and sequencing using the Illumina HiSeq 6000 or Xten platform.

Read mapping and transcriptome assembly

The raw reads obtained from the process of sequencing were processed in order to remove primer/adaptor contamination, low-quality bases and undetermined bases using Cutadapt [19]. The quality of the sequence was then verified using the FastQC tool (Table S1). Utilising Bowtie2 [20], TopHat2 [21] and Cufflinks [22], the reference genome was used to aligne the reads.

Identification of differentially expressed mRNA and bioinformatics analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between groups were identified using the edgeR package [23], applying criteria of |log2(fold change)|> 1 and FDR < 0.05. DEGs were then mapped to Gene Ontology (GO) terms. A hypergeometric test (P < 0.05) was used to define the significantly enriched GO terms compared to the genome background. The KEGG pathway database was used to identify significantly enriched pathways with a P < 0.05. The Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) database was used to obtain protein–protein interaction (PPI) data, with a confidence score > 0.9 defined as significant. The PPI network was visualized by Cytoscape [24]. The CytoHubba and Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) [25] application in Cytoscape was used to find the hub genes [26].

Identification and analysis of LncRNAs

Bowtie2 and TopHat2 were employed for the alignment of reads to the reference genome. Subsequently, Cufflinks and TopHat2 were utilized to reconstruct the transcription. The identification of novel transcripts, characterized by a length exceeding 200 bp and comprising more than two exons, was facilitated by Cuffcompare. The protein-coding potential of these novel transcripts was then determined using CNCI [27] and CPC software [28]. The RSEM software [29] was then deployed to quantitatively analyze the abundance of lncRNA transcripts, and normalized the transcriptional expression level with FPKM. The selection of differentially expressed lncRNAs (DElncRNAs) was conducted utilizing the criteria of a |log2(fold change)|> 1 and FDR < 0.05 by using edgeR package. The correlation of expression between these lncRNAs and protein-coding genes was found to exceed 0.9, thus identifying them as target genes of the DElncRNAs.

Identification and analysis of CircRNAs

The differential expression of circRNAs (DEcircRNAs) were identified by employing the edgeR packet with |log2(fold change)|> 1 and P < 0.05. The circRNAs were annotated in the circBase database, and the annotated circRNAs were designated as existing circRNAs, while the unannotated circRNAs were defined as novel predicted circRNAs. Starbase was used to predict targeting relationships with miRNAs of circRNAs that had been annotated in circBase. Targeting relationships of novel circRNAs were predicted using Mireap, miRANDA and TargetScan.

Identification and analysis of miRNAs

Clean tags were matched against small RNAs in the Genebank and RFAM databases to identify and exclude rRNA, scRNA, snoRNA, snRNA, and tRNA. All clean tags were aligned to the reference genome and compared with the miRbase database to identify existing and known miRNAs. Then the MIREAP software was used to identify the new candidate miRNAs. The expression levels of total miRNA (existing miRNAs, known miRNAs and novel miRNAs) were calculated and normalized to TPM to calculate the expression profiles of all miRNAs. The edgeR package was used to identify differentially expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs) with |log2(fold change)|> 1 and P < 0.05. Predictions of the target genes of total miRNAs were performed by RNAHybrid, Svm_Light, Miranda and TargetScan, and the intersection of their results was the candidate target gene.

Construction and analysis of ceRNA regulatory network

The ceRNA regulatory network of hypothalamus, pituitary and testicle was constructed independently. The Spearman Rank correlation coefficient (SCC) was used to assess expression correlations between mRNA-miRNA, lncRNA-miRNA, and circRNA-miRNA. Pairs with a correlation coefficient < −0.7 were identified as negatively co-expressed lncRNA-miRNA, mRNA-miRNA, or circRNA-miRNA pairs. MRNA, circRNA, and lncRNA were identified as miRNA target genes, with all RNAs exhibiting differential expression. The Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) was used to assess the expression correlation between lncRNA-mRNA or circRNA-mRNA. Pairs with PCC > 0.9 were selected as co-expressed pairs of lncRNA-mRNA and circRNA-mRNA. In these pairs, mRNA, circRNA, and lncRNA were negatively co-expressed and targeted by a shared miRNA. The significance of shared miRNA sponges between the two genes was assess using the hypergeometric cumulative distribution function test. Gene pairs with P < 0.05 were chosen. The construction of the lncRNA-miRNA-circRNA-mRNA network involved the assembly of all co-expression competing quadruplets that had been identified above, and this was then visualized using Cytoscape. In the ceRNA network, RNA connectivity is determined by the count of co-expressed target miRNAs. Hub genes were identified as the ceRNAs with the greatest connectivity.

Real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) validation

To investigate whether the sequencing results have a similar effect in other rooster populations, according random selection, the expression of 8 key DEGs, 5 DElncRNAs, 5 DEcircRNAs and 8 DEmiRNAs in hypothalamus, pituitary and testicle tissues were detected by qRT-PCR experiments. Samples were collected from six high and six low sexual precocity 77-day-old roosters, respectively. After RNA extraction, first-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) of mRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs was synthesized using HiScript@ III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd. Nanjing, China) and First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) of miRNA was synthesized using miRNA First Strand cDNA Synthesis (Tailing Reaction or Stem-loop Method) (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The detailed RNA primer sequence information was presented in Table S2. The primer efficiency was determined by temperature gradient PCR and the concentration of primers was 10 µM. QRT-PCR was conducted using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd Nanjing, China). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as a reference gene for normalization. Each amplification was conducted with three replicates for both the control and case groups. Using the 2−△△CT method for relative gene expression calculation, the amplification efficiencies were nearly 100%.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0) and R packages as appropriate. Phenotypic data (e.g., comb and testis traits) were compared between high and low comb groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s post‑hoc test. For ceRNA network construction, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (SCC ≤ –0.7) was used to screen miRNA–target pairs, while Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC > 0.9) was applied to identify ceRNA pairs. The reliability of RNA‑seq data was validated by qRT‑PCR on additional biological samples, and the consistency between the two platforms was assessed using Pearson’s correlation analysis of log₂(fold change) values.

Enrichment and PPI network of DEGs

The DEGs in the hypothalamus were enriched to 181 GO terms (P < 0.05) (Table S3). Among them, 17 biological process and 1 molecular function GO terms were the most enriched (P-adjust < 0.05). These GO terms were primarily associated with cholesterol/sterol metabolism and transport, and the regulation of glycoprotein and protein-lipid complex biosynthesis. The DEGs in the pituitary were enriched to 242 GO terms (Table S4), among which 34 molecular function GO terms were mostly enriched (P-adjust < 0.05). The list of key genes in the HPG axis

The differential expressed ncRNA to sexual precocity

A total of 51,440 lncRNA transcripts were identified from 17 lncRNA sequencing libraries, including 16,130 known and 35,310 novel lncRNA transcripts. In summary, 18 (8 up-regulated and 10 down-regulated), 27 (15 up-regulated and 12 down-regulated), and 48 (22 up-regulated and 26 down-regulated) DElncRNAs were detected in the hypothalamus, pituitary, and testicle, respectively. Additionally, 151 (78 up-regulated and 73 down-regulated), 37 (23 up-regulated and 14 down-regulated), and 633 (295 up-regulated and 338 down-regulated) DEcircRNAs were detected in the same respective tissues (Figure S2). Furthermore, the known and novel miRNAs, along with their respective target genes, were predicted for the hypothalamus, pituitary, and testicle. The differential expression analysis revealed 54 DEmiRNAs in the hypothalamus (19 of which were up-regulated, and 35 were down-regulated), 45 DEmiRNAs in the pituitary (all of which were down-regulated), and 626 DEmiRNAs in the testicle (539 of which were up-regulated, and 87 were down-regulated) (Figure S2). The number of novel miRNAs and DEmiRNAs in the testicle was greater than that observed in the hypothalamus and pituitary..

CeRNA regulatory network in response to sexual precocity

The ceRNA network of the hypothalamus consisted of 13 nodes and 17 interaction pairs (Fig. 5A), including 3 mRNAs (CREB1, AKT3 and SYNE1), 2 lncRNAs (TCONS_00062798 and TCONS_00089080), 2 circRNAs (novel_circ_025263 and novel_circ_005104) and 6 miRNAs (gga-miR-10a-5p, gga-miR-6546-3p, miR-10-x, gga-miR-206, novel-m2809-5p and miR-135-y). Two lncRNAs regulated AKT3 expression by competitively binding gga-miR-206, while novel_circ_005104 competitively bind miR-135-y and novel-m2809-5p to regulate CREB1 expression; novel_circ_025263, on the other hand, regulated SYNE1 expression by competitively binding gga-miR-10a-5p, gga-miR-6546-3p and miR-10-x. Novel_circ_023490 and TCONS_00071970 together regulated the expression of microRNAs by competitive binding to gga-miR-200a-3p. The binding of gga-miR-183, gga-miR-200a-3p and novel m2915-3p to CDK6 expression was subject to competitive binding, while their joint regulation of PTPRF expression was achieved through competitive binding to gga-miR-200a-3p. QRT-PCR validated expression

A total of 8 key DEGs, 5 DElncRNAs, 5 DEcircRNAs and 8 DEmiRNAs were validated by qRT-PCR in hypothalamus, pituitary and testicle tissues of divergent comb height cocks. The results showed that the Pearson correlation coefficients between qRT-PCR and RNA-seq expression levels were 0.503 (P = 0.088), 0.891 (P < 0.01) and 0.760 (P < 0.05) for hypothalamus, pituitary and testicle, respectively (Figure S3). The correlation between qRT-PCR and RNA-seq results in hypothalamic tissue did not reach statistical significance. This phenomenon may stem from the methodological differences between qRT-PCR and high-throughput RNA-seq, along with the fact that the hypothalamus exhibits greater biological complexity and heterogeneity than the pituitary gland and testes. Although the correlation lacked statistical significance, the expression patterns of key transcripts were still consistent with the trends observed in RNA-seq in terms of the direction of expression change. Moreover, highly significant and robust correlations were identified in the other two tissues (pituitary gland and testes), which demonstrates the reliability of the sequencing results.

Discussion

Compared with mammals, there are significant differences in the upstream regulation of the reproductive axis in avian species. The most prominent characteristic is that the hypothalamus of birds lacks the conserved KNDy neuron system found in mammals (a neuronal population expressing kisspeptin, neurokinin B, and dynorphin). The absence of this critical system indicates that the initiation and pulsatile release of GnRH in avian species depend on other avian-specific neuropeptides or regulatory mechanisms. The development of avian sex organs, reproductive behavior and initiation of sexual maturation are regulated by the HPG axis [8, 30]. Our study found that sexual precocity roosters exhibited significantly more developed testis and combs, along with elevated levels of GnRH, LH and testosterone. This supports the hypothesis that high comb height correlates with high sexual maturity and is consistent with hormonal patterns during sexual maturation [31].

The hypothalamus, a key neural center modulated by the HPG axis [3], secretes GnRH in response to excitatory signals from other brain regions. Activated GnRH is transported to the anterior pituitary via the portal venous system through ErbB and calcium ion signaling pathways, binding to receptors on gonadotropin (GTH) cells to stimulate the synthesis and secretion of GTH (including FSH and LH) [31]. Then GTH is transported to the gonadal tissue via the bloodstream, where LH activates the cAMP-PKA and cGMP-PKG signal pathways in testicular stromal cells, enhancing the activity of enzymes involved in steroidogenesis [32]. GnRH also directly interacts with gonadal organs to stimulate sex hormone synthesis and secretion [31]. The testicle processes hormonal signals via pathways like Wnt and TGF-β, which control testosterone production and germ cell growth and differentiation, facilitating secondary sexual characteristic development and sexual maturation in chickens [33]. The pituitary and gonadal hormones have corresponding receptors in the hypothalamus, forming a negative feedback loop to maintain endocrine balance.

This study identified inter-tissue co-expression of functional genes in the HPG axis that may be associated with sexual maturation. Notably, NMRK2 and PSMD14 were co-expressed across the hypothalamus, pituitary, and testicle. NMRK2 activates the nicotinamide riboside kinase pathway, regulating cell growth and death, and is involved in neurotransmission and signal transduction during sexual development [34, 35], which was up-regulated in three tissues of high comb group. PSMD14, which has been demonstrated to promote cell regeneration through ubiquitination [36, 37], exhibited higher expression in hypothalamus, pituitary, and testicle of the high comb group, consistent with its role in sexual maturation. Among the six DEGs co-expressed in the hypothalamus and pituitary with the same expression trend, RAB3C and AGPAT3 showed higher expression in the high comb group. These genes were enriched in cytoskeleton proteins, MAPK signaling pathways, and transcription factors, thus suggesting their involvement in neural signal transduction regulating sexual precocity. Furthermore, among the nine DEGs co-expressed in the pituitary and testicle, SMG7 was higher in the high comb group, while USP46 and CPT1A were higher in the low comb group. The functional annotations indicate that SMG7, USP46, and CPT1A play roles in cell growth and energy metabolism, highlighting the importance of these genes in sexual development [38–40]. Additionally, the PPI network analysis revealed that the testicle, as a downstream target organ, possesses a greater number of regulatory genes, indicating that sexual precocity depends on coordinated regulation between upstream and downstream tissues. These findings underscore the critical role of signal transduction and energy metabolism pathways in the HPG axis for the coordination and regulation of sexual precocity.

The ceRNA network revealed the expression of AKT3, CREB1 and SYNE1, along with several ncRNAs in the hypothalamus associated with rooster sexual precocity. Previous studies have reported that AKT3 is mainly expressed in the hypothalamus and testicle [41]. The study identifies AKT3 as significantly enriched in the regulation of phosphate metabolism, MAPK signaling pathway and ErbB signaling pathways. Notably, the ErbB pathway plays a defined role in glia-mediated control of female sexual development [5, 6]. Together, these findings indicate that AKT3, as a component of these conserved signaling pathways, likely contributes to the process of male sexual maturation. In this study, the expression of gga-miR-206 was found to be down-regulated in the hypothalamus, while the related gene AKT3, and the related lncRNAs TCONS_00062798 and TCONS_00089080 were found to be up-regulated. This suggests that TCONS_00062798 and TCONS_00089080, as ceRNAs, may competitively bind to silence gga-miR-206 expression, allowing the AKT3 to resemble a dual stimulatory and inhibitory regulatory system in the hypothalamus that regulates sexual maturation and development by fully participating in hormone secretion. CREB1 encodes a transcription factor from the leucine zipper family of DNA-binding proteins, which is phosphorylated by various protein kinases. It induces transcription of genes in response to hormonal stimulation of the cyclic adinosine monophosphate (cAMP) pathway [42]. It has been demonstrated that miRNA-135 has a targeting relationship with CREB1 [43]. In the present study, the hypothalamus of highly sexually mature roosters exhibited elevated levels of miR-135-y expression, while CREB1 and novel_circ_005104 expression levels were reduced. These findings suggest that novel_circ_005104 may function as a sponge for miR-135-y, thereby modulating the role of CREB1 in the hypothalamus during sexual maturation. SYNE1 is physically close to ESR1, and has been reported play a role in sex determining [44], sex steroid hormone signaling and function [45] and ovarian cancer [46]. The present study shows that SYNE1 is responsible for the rooster sex maturity and is regulated by the competing binding of novel_circ_025263 to gga-miR-10a-5p, gga-miR-6546-3p and miR-10-x in the hypothalamus.

The novel_circ_023490 and TCONS_00071970 compete to bind to gga-miR-183, gga-miR-200a-3p, and novel- m2915-3p in the pituitary, thereby regulating the expression of CDK6, which is a key kinase involved in the cell cycle [47], Its high expression in the low maturity chicken and its significant enrichment in negative regulation of protein serine/threonine kinase activity and p53 signaling pathway suggest that it might reduce male sexual maturation. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that novel_circ_023490 and TCONS_00071970 compete with gga-miR-200a-3p to regulate the expression of PTPRF, which is a negative regulator of insulin signaling. The elevated expression of PTPRF in the low maturity chicken and its significant enrichment in protein phosphatase, cell adhesion molecules, insulin signaling pathway and adheres junction, suggest a close association between sexual precocity of roosters and energy metabolism.

The present study revealed that the testicle not only serves as a target organ for hormone regulation in the hypothalamus and pituitary but may also be a target organ for non-coding RNA regulation of gene expression. As the primary sexual characteristic, the development of the testicle affects the growth of the second sexual characteristic [48], and provides the energy and site required for gametogenesis and maturation [49]. The testicle is composed of spermatogenic cells, supporting cells and mesenchymal cells. The production of animal testosterone is primarily facilitated by interstitial cells and adrenal cortex reticulum of testicle, and is consequently regulated and secreted by the HPG axis [50]. This study identified that testicular ceRNA hub genes were predominantly enriched in pathways associated with sexual maturation, including GnRH signaling pathway, VEGF signaling pathway, TGF-β signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway, and Wnt signaling pathway. The TGF-β signaling pathway has been the focus of numerous studies in the context of chicken gonadal development [9, 51, 52], and its role in regulating the growth and differentiation of germ cells in the chicken testicle has been well documented [53]. The Wnt signaling pathway has been demonstrated to be closely related to the secretion of testosterone by testicular stromal cells in male animals and the regulation of follicle development in female animals [54]. Furthermore, mTOR has been found to affect the proliferation and differentiation of male mammalian germ cells [55]. In particular, the ceRNA network showed that ENSGALT00000076697, ENSGALT00000046820, novel_circ_022575 and novel_circ_006317 could all compete to bind miR-16-x and gga-miR-16c-5p to regulate the expression of SRC. The SRC family comprises three distinct transcriptional activators, namely SRC-1, SRC-2, and SRC-3 [56]. It has been demonstrated that the absence of SRC-3 result in diminished secretion of gonadal steroid hormones, including estrogen and androgens, consequently leading to growth retardation, delayed puberty, diminished reproductive function and stunted breast development in mice [56]. In addition, at the onset of puberty in female rats, the expression of SRC-1 in the Medial Preoptic Area (MPOA) region of the hypothalamus reached its peak, suggesting that it plays a role in establishing a positive feedback fluctuation mechanism responsible for ovulation and periodic secretion of estradiol and gonadotropins [57]. This study constructs a ceRNA regulatory network to elucidate the interaction relationships between genes and non-coding RNAs. Through functional enrichment analysis of the target genes of ceRNAs, it preliminarily concludes that the rooster HPG axis primarily regulates the development of sexual precocity at the genetic level by controlling hormone secretion and energy metabolism. In the future, investigating the specific coordination mechanisms between HPG axis tissues will be the direction of deeper research.

Conclusions

This study identified the key ceRNA networks in the hypothalamus as TCONS_00062798/TCONS_0008908-gga-miR-206-AKT3 and novel_circ_ 005104-miR-135-y- CREB1. The key ceRNA networks in the pituitary were novel_circ_023490-TCONS_00071970-gga-miR-183/gga-miR-200a-3p/novel-m29153p-CDK6 and novel_circ_023490- TCONS_00071970-gga-miR-200a −3p-PTPRF. The key ceRNA networks in the testes were novel_circ_ 022575/novel_circ_006317/ENSGALT00000076697/ENSGALT00000046820-miR-16-x/gga-miR-16c-5p-SRC. The hub genes of ceRNA network in HPG axis were mainly enriched in adrenergic signaling in GnRH signaling pathway, VEGF signaling pathway, TGF-β signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, and glutamate metabolism. This study preliminarily explored the expression characteristics of genes and ceRNAs in the HPG axis of roosters, providing a molecular basis for further scientific interpretation of the regulatory mechanisms underlying sexual maturation development in the rooster HPG axis.
