Incidental finding of polyorchidism during elective orchidopexy in a child: a case report
Authors: Eiman Yassir Musa Hussain, Khadidja Amedjoudj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolyorchidism is a rare congenital anomaly characterized by the presence of more than two testes. It is commonly discovered incidentally during surgical exploration. We report a case of a 7-year-old male with Polyorchidism diagnosed during elective surgery for undescended left testis. An intra-scrotal supernumerary testis was found with a cryptorchidic testis located at the inguinal canal. We opted for Orchidectomy of the intrascrotal atrophic testis, and orchidopexy of the healthy testis. Histopathology confirmed the diagnosis. Postoperative recovery and follow-up were uneventful. This case highlights the importance of polyorchidism management based on anatomical and functional findings intraoperatively.

Introduction

Polyorchidism is a rare congenital urologenital anomaly defined by the presence of more than two testes. Polyorchidism is the most commonly reported variant, with fewer than 250 cases described in the literature.1,2 The etiology is attributed to abnormal division of the genital ridge during embryogenesis.3,4 Although often asymptomatic, polyorchidism is frequently associated with other urogenital anomalies, including cryptorchidism,5, 6, 7 inguinal hernia, and testicular torsion.8,9

Diagnosis is often made intraoperatively during elective surgery. The management depends on the location, morphology, and viability of the testes. This report presents a rare case of polyorchidism diagnosed during surgery for presumed cryptorchidism.

Case report

A 5-year-old male with no significant past medical history was referred for evaluation of a non-palpable left testis. Physical examination revealed an empty left hemiscrotum and a palpable mass in the left inguinal region. The intrascrotal supernumerary testis was markedly atrophic and fibrotic, which explains why it was not clinically palpable preoperatively. We opted for orchidectomy of this atrophic testis, while the inguinal testis with a normal epididymis and separate vas deferens was preserved and orchidopexy performed.The right testis was normally positioned and of normal volume. Since the cryptorchidic testis was palpable, ultrasound was not done and a preliminary diagnosis of left cryptorchidism was made, the patient was scheduled for orchidopexy. Surgical exploration via a left inguinal incision revealed two distinct testicular structures; the first was of normal volume, well-vascularized located at the superficial inguinal canal (Fig. 1), and the second was located within the left hemiscrotum, appearing atrophic with poor vascularity and consistency, each one owns their own vas defferns (Fig. 2). An orchidectomy was performed for the atrophic intrascrotal testis. The viable inguinal testis was mobilized and successfully brought down to the scrotum with orchidopexy. The right testis was not surgically explored as it was clinically normal.

Histopathological examination confirmed the presence of atrophic testicular tissue and completely fibrosed seminiferous tubules with no signs of malignancy. No epididymal tissue was identified in the atrophic testis, while the inguinal testis demonstrated a normal epididymis intraoperatively. The postoperative follow-up was uneventful, and follow-up ultrasonography at the first and 6th month confirmed normal vascularity and scrotal positioning of the preserved testis, with no anomaly on the contralateral side.

Discussion

Polyorchidism, especially when associated with cryptorchidism, is a rare but important differential diagnosis during surgical management of non-palpable testes. In most cases, the supernumerary testis is located in the scrotum or inguinal canal.10,11 Embryologically, the anomaly is thought to result from longitudinal or transverse division of the genital ridge and mesonephric duct.12

Leung classification of polyorchidism is based on testis embryology,13 there are 4 types of which are described as follows: Type 1-Supernumerary testis that lacks an epididymis or vas and has got no attachment to the usual testis. Type 2- the supernumerary testis draining into the epididymis of usual testis and sharing a common vas. Type 3- the supernumerary testis has its own epididymis and both epididymis of ipsilateral testis drains into common vas. Type 4- complete duplication of testis, epididymis and vas. Type 2 is considered to be most common and together with type 3, they compromise 90 % of polyorchidism. In our case, the patient had type 4 polyorchidism, as both testes possessed their own vas deferens, consistent with complete duplication.

In such cases, preservation is generally recommended unless the testis is nonviable or suspicious for malignancy.

The followers of conservative approach advance that the incidence of infertility is higher in polyorchidism and if the supernumerary testis is functional, it should be conserved to improve the spermatogenesis. This approach has to be balanced against the risk of malignancy which is 4–7 % in polyorchidism.13

In our case we performed an orchidectomy of the atrophic testis and preservation and orchidopexy of the healthy one, and this decision aligns with current best practices. This case emphasizes the importance of anatomical classification and reinforces the need for detailed intraoperative evaluation and individualized treatment strategies in the setting of polyorchidism and cryptorchidism.

Conclusion

Polyorchidism is a rare urogenital anomaly usually discovered incidentally during exploration of other conditions such as cryptorchidism, testicular torsion or inguinal hernia. Imaging and surgical exploration remains the mainstay for diagnosis and management. Preservation of viable testis with orchidopexy and orchidectomy of atrophic or nonfunctional tissue is recommended to optimize outcomes and minimize future risk.
