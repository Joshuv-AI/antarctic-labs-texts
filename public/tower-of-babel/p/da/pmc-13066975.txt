Low LAMA2 expression may affect drug resistance and prognosis in ovarian cancer via positive LGR5 coexpression
Authors: Zhe Du, Yuting Su, Lianping Xia, Shunmei Lei, Yao Ke, Xiaoying Chen, Xia Liu, Fuqiang Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrug resistance often occurs during ovarian cancer (OC) treatment, and factors related to drug resistance often contribute to poor prognosis. Laminin subunit alpha 2 (LAMA2) can affect cancer progression, but its association with OC has rarely been studied. This study aimed to preliminarily investigate the expression of LAMA2 in OC and its ability to predict chemoresistance and patient prognosis using open-access big data and bioinformatics analysis.MethodsData retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), the Genotype-Tissue Expression Project (GTEx), and the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) were used to analyze messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) and protein expression. Protein expression in OC tissues was detected using hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Up to 10,000 samples from 33 different cancer types and bivariate correlations were used to investigate the coexpression of the two genes. Gene expression with respect to tumor immune infiltration was determined using the R software package and TCGA data.ResultsLikely modified by deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) methylation, LAMA2 expression was significantly downregulated at the mRNA and protein levels in OC tissues and was consistently downregulated in 19 different tumors. Furthermore, LAMA2 expression was decreased in 90 platinum-resistant and cisplatin-resistant A2780 cells. A low level of LAMA2 predicted short overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in 149 OC patients and was related to low immune infiltration and high tumor grade. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis of the genes coexpressed with LAMA2 revealed that Rat sarcoma (Ras) protein signaling and other oncogene-related pathways were involved. LAMA2 was positively coexpressed with leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein-coupled receptor 5 (LGR5), aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family member A1 (ALDH1A1), and von Willebrand factor (VWF) in 558 OC and 90 drug-resistant tissues and was coexpressed with these three genes in 19, 17, and 25 different tumors, respectively. Moreover, low LGR5 expression predicted short OS, and a combination of genes with LAMA2 could better predict short OS.ConclusionsLow LAMA2 expression is significantly associated with chemoresistance and poor prognosis in OC. This association may involve crosstalk with LGR5 and Ras signaling pathways. Thus, LAMA2 may represent a candidate biomarker for predicting treatment response and survival outcomes, warranting further preclinical and clinical investigation.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Our study found low laminin subunit alpha 2 (LAMA2) expression is a potential risk factor for ovarian cancer (OC) drug resistance and short survival, possibly via interactions with leucine rich repeat containing G-protein-coupled receptor 5 (LGR5) and Rat sarcoma (Ras) protein signaling.

What is known and what is new?

• Previous studies have shown that LAMA2 expression is downregulated in drug-resistant OC cell lines.

• The study employed open-access big data and bioinformatics analysis to investigate the relationships among LAMA2, chemotherapy resistance, and the prognosis of OC patients. We found low levels of LAMA2 are likely to adversely affect OC progression and therapeutic resistance. And LAMA2 may contribute to OC progression and drug resistance by modulating LGR5 and Ras signaling and its downstream pathways.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• LAMA2 may serve as a novel therapeutic target and a potential prognostic biomarker for OC. Further research is required to elucidate the specific functions and mechanisms of LAMA2 via interactions with LGR5 and Ras signaling in OC.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is the fifth leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally and one of the leading causes of gynecological cancer worldwide (1). Most OC patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage due to the atypia of early symptoms and limitations of screening methods (2), and the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate for these advanced-stage patients is only approximately 46% (3). The clinical treatment for advanced OC is cytoreduction followed by platinum-taxane-based chemotherapy (4), but many patients develop drug resistance, ultimately leading to treatment failure (5). The development of drug resistance in OC is complex and involves processes such as deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage repair, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), the cell cycle, apoptosis, autophagy, and abnormal signaling pathways, which are regulated by multiple genes and influenced by multiple factors (6). Therefore, the identification of factors that affect chemoresistance and prognosis is important for the clinical management of OC patients (7).

Laminins are macromolecular glycoproteins that consist of five α, four β, and three γ chains (8). In particular, by attaching to the basement membrane, laminins can connect the basement membrane to adjacent cells; transmit important signals for related cell behaviors, including cell adhesion, migration, and differentiation (9); and determine the physiological functions of the basement membrane (10). Many recent studies have shown that laminins are differentially expressed in many tumors and affect tumor growth (11), migration and invasion (12), angiogenesis (13), and chemotherapy resistance (14). Additionally, laminins have prognostic value and can be used as potential biomarkers for early tumor diagnosis (15).

Laminin subunit alpha 2 (LAMA2) has been identified as a molecular target that directly or indirectly interacts with oncogene-related signaling pathways to affect cancer progression (16,17); in addition, this gene could serve as a biomarker to predict the prognosis of patients with colorectal cancer (18), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) (19), or other cancers. Despite several studies, LAMA2 has not been extensively investigated in cancer, particularly its relationship with drug resistance and OC prognosis. While several studies have shown that LAMA2 expression is downregulated in drug-resistant OC cell lines (20,21), its precise role in OC progression and chemoresistance remains poorly understood. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to preliminarily investigate the expression of LAMA2 in OC and its ability to predict chemoresistance and patient prognosis using open-access big data and bioinformatics analysis. We present this article in accordance with the REMARK and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-aw-2192/rc).

Methods

Data acquisition

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Laminin messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) expression data for OC tissues (n=426) and normal controls (n=88) and mRNA expression data for 33 different tumors and normal controls were retrieved from Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA; http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) (22) and integrated with data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (23) and Genotype-Tissue Expression Project (GTEx) (24). Laminin protein expression data for OC tissues (n=100) and normal controls (n=25) were retrieved from University of ALabama at Birmingham CANcer data analysis portal (UALCAN; http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) (25) and integrated with data from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) (26). Gene expression data for A2780 epithelial OC cells with acquired platinum resistance was derived from a microarray (GDS3754), which was deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) (27).

The mRNA expression and OS data of 1,656 OC patients were retrieved from the Kaplan-Meier plotter (http://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php) (28) and integrated with data from GEO (29) (GSE15622, GSE26193, GSE26712, GSE9891, GSE63885, GSE27651, GSE3149, GSE51373, GSE65986, GSE18520, GSE14764, GSE19829, GSE30161, and GSE23554) and the TCGA ovarian cohort.

Genes coexpressed with LAMA2 in 558 OC samples from the TCGA ovarian cohort were downloaded from cBioPortal (http://www.cbioportal.org), and the significance of two coexpressed genes was determined using Spearman’s correlation and the q value (30). DNA methylation, mRNA expression, and clinical data for 489 OC tissues were downloaded; among the 489 tissues, 197 were chemotherapy-sensitive, and 90 were chemotherapy-resistant. RNA-sequencing expression (level 3) profiles and corresponding clinical information for tumor immunity analysis were downloaded from a TCGA dataset (https://portal.gdc.com). Intersections of multiple sets of genes were performed using Venn software (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/).

Coremine Medical (http://www.coremine.com/medical/) (31) was utilized for the text mining of OC drug-resistant and LAMA2-related genes, using the keywords ‘LAMA2’ (gene/protein, Human) (6,893 connections), ‘Malignant neoplasm of ovary (alias Ovarian Cancer)’ (disease) (51,425 connections), and ‘drug resistance’ (mesh) (61,734 connections) (P<0.05).

Cell lines and cell culture

Human OC cell lines were cultured in our laboratory. The cell lines HeyA8 (H) was kind gifts from Prof. Fengxia Xue of Tianjin Medical University. Carboplatin-resistant OC cells (A2780-CBP) was established from parental cell lines A2780 by gradual exposure to increasing concentrations of carboplatin and cisplatin. The two OC cell lines were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium (Wisent Corporation, Nanjing, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10099141; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Thornton, NSW, Australia) at a constant temperature of 37 ℃.

Gene expression analysis

Box plots were generated to display the differential expression of laminins at the mRNA level in tumor and normal tissues using GEPIA. The settings were as follows: |log2fold change (FC)| cutoff =1; P value cutoff =0.01; dataset selection, “ovarian cancer (OV)”; and “matched TCGA normal and GTEx data”. Box plots of the differential expression of LAMA2, laminin subunit alpha 4 (LAMA4), laminin subunit beta 1 (LAMB1), and laminin subunit beta 1 (LAMB2) in 33 different tumor and normal tissues were also generated. Using UALCAN, we explored the differential expression of laminins at the protein level in primary tumors and normal tissues. The main settings included “Enter gene names”, the CPTAC dataset “ovarian cancer” and “sample types”.

Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

An ovarian tissue microarray (HOvaC151Su02) was purchased from Shanghai Outdo Biotech Co., Ltd. and included 149 OC tissues and 2 normal ovarian tissues adjacent to the cancer. The primary antibody used was a mouse monoclonal antibody against human LAMA2 (dilution 1:100; AMab91166; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The results were evaluated according to the Remmele and Stegner immunoreactive score (IRS), which combines the percentage of positive cells and the staining intensity. All slides were evaluated independently by two pathologists. The staining intensity scores ranged from 0 to 3: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), and 3 (strong). The staining percentages were graded as follows: 0 (0–25%), 1 (26–50%), 2 (51–75%), and 3 (76–100%). The intensity of the staining score multiplied by the percentage of positive cells was the IRS (range, 0–9): IRS <5 was considered low expression of the LAMA2 protein, and IRS ≥5 was considered high expression of the LAMA2 protein.

OS analysis

The Kaplan-Meier method was used for survival analysis, in which the automatically selected optimal cutoff value was used to categorize gene expression as low or high. The effect of combined gene expression on patient prognosis was examined in accordance with the results of a previous study (32). Samples with high expression of two genes were assigned to the high expression of combined genes group, and samples with low expression of the two genes were assigned to the low expression of combined genes group. These two groups were compared via Kaplan-Meier curves to assess the associations of the expression of the combined genes with prognosis. The probes used were 213519_s_at-LAMA2, 210989_at-LAMA4, 216264_s_at-LAMB2, and 210393_at-LGR5 (leucine rich repeat containing G protein-coupled receptor 5). Genes with prognostic significance (P<0.05) were identified as predictors of OS using univariate survival analysis.

Western blot

Protein extraction was carried out using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. The total protein concentration was measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE, 10% gel) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes using a semidry transfer system at 90 V for 2 hours. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20) for 1 hour at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with the following primary antibodies: anti-LAMA2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific #CL3450) and β-Tubulin (CST #2128). After being washed with TBST, the membranes were incubated with the corresponding horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (CST #7074) for 1 hour at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (ECL, 34580, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Tumor immunity analysis

The expression score and abundance of tumor-infiltrating immune cells were assessed with “immunedeconv”, which is an R software package that integrates 6 algorithms (33). CD274 molecule (CD274), cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA4), hepatitis A virus cellular receptor 2 (HAVCR2), lymphocyte-activating 3 (LAG3), programmed cell death 1 (PDCD1), programmed cell death 1 ligand 2 (PDCD1LG2), T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT), and sialic acid binding Ig-like lectin 15 (SIGLEC15) were selected as the immune checkpoint-relevant transcripts. The potential immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) response was predicted with the tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) algorithm (34). All the above analyses were implemented using the R foundation for statistical computing [2020] version 4.0.3 with the “ggplot2” and “heatmap” packages. The Wilcoxon test was performed to assess significant differences in immune-related cells, immune checkpoint-related genes, and TIDE between the LAMA2 low- and high-expression groups.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis

KEGG pathway analysis (35) and functional clustering were performed using KEGG Orthology Based Annotation System (KOBAS; http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/kobas3/?t=1) (36). The q value was adjusted for the P value using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, and a pathway was considered significantly enriched when the q value was less than 0.05.

Statistical analysis

SPSS Statistics (version 26.0) was used for the statistical analysis. The differential expression of genes in drug-resistant and drug-sensitive OC cells or tissues was compared via t-tests. The correlation between gene expression and clinicopathological characteristics was evaluated using Pearson’s χ2 test (two-sided). Correlations between DNA methylation and the mRNA expression of two genes were determined using bivariate correlations (Spearman and Pearson). Differences were considered statistically significant at P<0.05.

Results

LAMA2 expression is significantly downregulated in OC tissues, and low LAMA2 expression is associated with poor prognosis

The mRNA and protein expression levels of laminins in OC tissues were assessed using TCGA, GTEx, and CPTAC data. As shown in Figure 1, compared with the mRNA expression in 88 normal tissues, laminin subunit alpha 1 (LAMA1), LAMA2, LAMA4, LAMB1, LAMB2, and laminin subunit gamma 3 (LAMC3) expression was downregulated in 426 tumor tissue samples (Figure 1A). At the protein level, LAMA2, laminin subunit alpha 3 (LAMA3), LAMA4, laminin subunit alpha 5 (LAMA5), LAMB1, LAMB2, laminin subunit beta 3 (LAMB3), laminin subunit gamma 1(LAMC1), and LAMC3 levels were lower in tumor tissues (n=100) than in normal tissues (n=25) (Figure 1B). Among the differentially expressed laminins, LAMA2, LAMA4, LAMB1, and LAMB2 expression was consistently downregulated in OC tissues at both the mRNA and protein levels.

Consistent with its decreased expression in OC tissues, LAMA2 expression was significantly downregulated in 19 other tumors (Figure 2A), whereas LAMA4 and LAMB2 expression was downregulated in 11 and 12 tumors (Figure 2B,2D), respectively. The associations of LAMA2, LAMA4, and LAMB2 with OS in OC were subsequently determined using 1,656 OC samples. As shown in Figure 3, low expression levels of LAMA2 and LAMA4 were significantly correlated with poor OS in OC patients, whereas low levels of LAMB2 were associated with good OS. We further investigated the relationship between low LAMA2 expression and prognosis via a microarray analysis of 149 OC tissue samples. As shown in Figure 4, IHC results revealed that the LAMA2 protein was positively expressed in the cytoplasm and membrane of OC tissues. Representative images of LAMA2-positive and LAMA2-negative OC samples are shown in Figure 4A. The survival analysis results revealed that low LAMA2 protein expression was associated with poor OS, although the difference was not statistically significant in patients with OC (Figure 4B); this result is consistent with a large sample analysis (Figure 3A), reinforcing the potential prognostic value of LAMA2 in OC. Furthermore, compared with high LAMA2 expression, low LAMA2 expression was associated with a worse disease-free survival (DFS) for patients with OC (Figure 4B). In addition, we explored LAMA2 expression in relation to clinical and pathological parameters, including tumor stage, grade, and other relevant factors (Figure 4C). Multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed that LAMA2 expression was an independent predictor of DFS [hazard ratio (HR): 0.634, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.421–0.956, P=0.03] but not OS (HR: 0.736, 95% CI: 0.434–1.248, P=0.26). Other factors, such as tumor size and M stage, were significant predictors of both OS and DFS, highlighting their crucial role in patient prognosis.

Low LAMA2 expression is potentially related to OC drug resistance and high tumor grade

The transcript levels of LAMA2 and LAMA4 in drug-resistant OC cells and tissues were measured. As shown in Figure 5, compared with that in parent cells, the expression of LAMA2 and LAMA4 was significantly lower in cisplatin-resistant A2780 OC cells (Figure 5A), and, consistent with its expression in cells, LAMA2 expression was significantly lower in 90 drug-resistant OC tissues than in 197 drug-sensitive tissues (Figure 5B). In addition, to validate these bioinformatic findings, we performed experimental analyses using OC cell lines. Western blot analysis confirmed that LAMA2 expression was significantly lower in carboplatin-resistant HeyA8-CBP (H-C) cells than in HeyA8 (H) parental cells (Figure 5C). These experimental results provide additional evidence supporting the bioinformatic data. The chi-square test indicated that the percentage of drug-resistant OC tissues with low levels of LAMA2 was 62.2% (56/90 cases), whereas the percentage of drug-sensitive tissues with low LAMA2 was 48.2% (95/197 cases) (P=0.03). Moreover, the percentage of histological grade 2 tumors with low levels of LAMA2 was 24.6% (14/57 cases), whereas the percentage of histological grade 3 tumors with low levels of LAMA2 was 53.6% (225/420 cases) (P<0.001) (Table 1). There was no significant correlation between LAMA2 levels and primary therapy outcomes (P=0.53) or tumor stage (P=0.36).

LAMA2 potentially affects drug resistance and prognosis via coexpression with LGR5

A total of 249 genes that potentially interact with LAMA2 and are implicated in the regulation of OC drug resistance were text-mined with three terms: ‘LAMA2’ (gene/protein, Human), ‘Drug Resistance’ (mesh) and ‘Malignant neoplasm of ovary (alias Ovarian Cancer)’ (disease) (P<0.05). As shown in Figure 6, the 249 genes and 447 genes that were differentially expressed in 90 drug-resistant OC tissues were intersected, and seven overlapping genes, LGR5, aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family member A1 (ALDH1A1), von Willebrand factor (VWF), vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), BCL2 binding component 3 (BBC3), telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), and vitronectin (VTN), were obtained (Figure 6A). Among these genes, LGR5, ALDH1A1, VWF, and VEGFA expression was significantly downregulated in 90 drug-resistant OC tissues, whereas BBC3, TERT, and VTN expression was upregulated (Figure 6B). Consistent with the expression results (Figures 5B,6B), LAMA2 was positively coexpressed with LGR5, ALDH1A1, and VWF in 558 OC samples and 90 drug-resistant OC tissues in the TCGA cohort and negatively coexpressed with VTN (Figure 6C,6D); the results for VEGFA, BBC3, and TERT were inconsistent.

Given the strong coexpression of LGR5, ALDH1A1, and VWF with LAMA2 in OC and the close relationship of LAMA2 with OS (Figure 3A), the associations of these three genes with OS were also determined via Kaplan-Meier plot analysis of 1,656 OC samples. As shown in Figure 3B, LGR5 expression was related to OS in OC. As expected, a low level of LGR5 was significantly associated with poor OS, and the combination of LAMA2 and LGR5 provided better prognostic potential for OS in patients with OC.

Low LAMA2 expression is positively correlated with tumor immune infiltration

The correlation between LAMA2 expression and tumor immunity in OC patients was investigated. As shown in Figure 10, the number of immune-associated cells, such as endothelial cells (P<0.001), CD4+ T cells (P<0.01), and CD8+ T cells (P<0.05), was lower in OC tissues with low LAMA2 expression than in those with high LAMA2 expression; however, the number of natural killer cells (P<0.001) was greater. Immune checkpoint analyses revealed that the expression of immune checkpoint molecules CTLA4 and TIGIT were significantly upregulated in OC tissues with low levels of LAMA2 (P<0.05); however, SIGLEC15 expression was significantly downregulated (P<0.01) (Figure 10C). Subsequently, TIDE analysis revealed that for OC tissues with low levels of LAMA2, treatment was effective for 69 cases and ineffective for 118 cases. For OC tissues with high levels of LAMA2, treatment was effective for 56 cases and ineffective 131 cases were ineffective. Thus, the TIDE score was significantly lower in OC tissues with low levels of LAMA2 (P<0.01; Figure 10D).

LAMA2 potentially regulates OC progression and drug resistance through interactions with Ras signaling

KEGG pathway analysis of genes coexpressed with LAMA2 was used to elucidate the potential mechanisms of LAMA2 in OC regulation. On the basis of Spearman’s correlation analysis, 7,406 genes were significantly coexpressed with LAMA2 in 558 OC samples (q<0.05), of which 2,536 (q<0.0001) were subjected to KEGG pathway analysis and functional clustering. As shown in Figure 11, among the clusters of enriched pathways, several clusters, especially the top three clusters (C1–C3), are known to regulate cancer progression in OC (Figure 11A). Specifically, the first cluster (C1) included the Rat sarcoma (Ras), Ras-related protein 1 (Rap1), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase-protein kinase B (PI3K-AKT) signaling pathways, all of which are oncogene related.

The possible molecular mechanism by which LAMA2 influences OC drug resistance was further analyzed via KEGG pathway analysis of genes coexpressed with LAMA2 in drug-resistant tissues. Among the 7,406 genes that were significantly coexpressed with LAMA2 in 558 OC samples (q<0.05), 3,547 (q<0.001) were subjected to differential expression analysis: 447 genes were significantly dysregulated between 90 drug-resistant tissues and 197 drug-sensitive tissues. KEGG pathway analysis of the 447 genes was performed, and some chemoresistance-related pathways were enriched and clustered (Figure 11B), including the Hippo signaling pathway, signaling pathways regulating the pluripotency of stem cells, and the Ras signaling pathway.

Discussion

Currently, chemotherapy resistance in both primary and recurrent OC leads to poor prognosis and high mortality; however, alleviating multidrug resistance can prolong OC patient survival (39). Therefore, new biomarkers are needed to predict chemotherapy resistance and develop treatment strategies to overcome this resistance. LAMA2 has been reported to be associated with tumors (40), including OC. Previous studies have explored the role of LAMA2 in OC progression, the associations of LAMA2 with chemoresistance, and prognostic relevance of LAMA2; however, the exact mechanisms remain unclear. For example, Diao and Yang systematically analyzed the expression and prognosis of laminin family genes in OC (41), and Hao et al. delineated the functional contributions of laminin components in human ovarian tissue cultures (42). In this study, we used open-access big data and bioinformatics analysis to investigate the relationships among LAMA2, chemotherapy resistance, and the prognosis of OC patients.

LAMA2 is the major component of the laminin basal laminae-α subunit of laminin (43). On the basis of existing evidence, LAMA2 can cause laminin α2-related muscular dystrophy (44). In recent years, several studies have shown that LAMA2 expression is downregulated in LUAD (16), invasive pituitary adenoma (45), and colorectal cancer (18) and plays diverse roles in tumor progression. In our study, we found that LAMA2 expression was significantly downregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels in OC (Figure 1A,1B). Moreover, LAMA2 gene expression was also lower in cisplatin-resistant A2780 OC cells and 90 platinum-resistant tissues than in their respective controls (Figure 5A,5B). The expression level of LAMA2 protein was significantly lower in platinum-resistant OC cells H-C and S-C compared to the sensitive OC cells H and S (Figure 5C). Consistent with our results, Januchowski et al. (46) reported that LAMA2 expression was decreased in cisplatin-resistant A2780CR2 and paclitaxel-resistant A2780PR1 OC cells. Furthermore, low LAMA2 expression was associated with a high tumor grade and low level of immune infiltration (Table 1; Figure 10) and predicted poor OS in a large sample of 1,656 OC patients (Figure 3A). Validation in an independent cohort of 149 OC tissues further confirmed that the downregulation of LAMA2 expression predicted worse OS and DFS (Figure 4). Collectively, these data provide preliminary evidence that low levels of LAMA2 are likely to adversely affect OC progression and therapeutic resistance.

This study confirms that LAMA2 is downregulated in OC, which is consistent with the findings of Diao et al., who also reported lower levels of LAMA2 mRNA in OC tissues compared to normal levels (41). Although Diao et al. did not delve into the role of LAMA2, our findings resonate with reports on other members of the laminin family (such as LAMC3) influencing chemotherapy sensitivity by regulating key pathways like the cell cycle and autophagy (47). This suggests that different laminin subunits may participate in the adaptive response of tumor cells to therapeutic stress by modulating shared core pathways. Secondly, this study observed that low LAMA2 expression is associated with poor prognosis, complementing the findings of Diao et al. in OC. Their research showed that high LAMA2 expression correlates with better progression-free survival (PFS), particularly in early-stage OC patients (41). Low LAMA2 expression may promote tumor invasion, immune evasion, and the evolution of drug resistance by compromising basement membrane integrity. Additionally, this study suggests that LAMA2 may further modulate disease progression and treatment responses by influencing the immune microenvironment. Although current research on the function of LAMA2 in cancer remains relatively limited, this study significantly deepens the understanding of the functional complexity of LAMA2 in OC by integrating drug-resistant phenotypes, immune infiltration characteristics, and multi-omics survival analyses. In contrast to widely reported oncogenic members of the same family (such as LAMC2) (41), low LAMA2 expression promotes tumor progression, fosters an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and contributes to chemotherapy resistance.

LAMA2 may affect OC progression and drug resistance via interactions with Ras signaling. KEGG pathway analysis of the 2,536 genes coexpressed with LAMA2 in 558 OC tissues revealed that LAMA2 potentially regulated OC progression via the Ras, Rap1, MAPK, and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways (Figure 11A), which are all oncogene-related pathways (48). Notably, Ras signaling enrichment was observed for 447 genes that were coexpressed with LAMA2 in 558 OC tissues and were differentially expressed in 90 drug-resistant tissues (Figure 11B). Ras signaling, an upstream pathway for the Rap1, MAPK, and PI3K-AKT pathways, is involved in several cancer processes (49). The inhibition of the Ras-MAPK pathway increases the drug sensitivity of OC cells (50). Additionally, LAMA2 has been shown to interact with Ras signaling in other cancers, such as LUAD, where it promotes metastasis via PI3K/AKT activation (16). Taken together, these findings indicate that LAMA2 may contribute to OC progression and drug resistance by modulating Ras signaling and its downstream pathways. While these associations are intriguing, the mechanistic relationship between LAMA2 and Ras signaling remains correlative, and thus, future studies should employ functional experiments.

Moreover, we focused on the relationship between LAMA2 and LGR5 in OC, as the coexpression of these two genes has promising potential for predicting drug resistance and prognosis. Specifically, we observed that LAMA2 was positively coexpressed with LGR5 at the mRNA level in 558 OC and 90 drug-resistant tissues (Figure 6C,6D). Additionally, this positive coexpression pattern was consistent across multiple tumor types, with stable coexpression observed in 10 different cancers (Figures 7-9). Compared with that in 197 sensitive tissues, the combined expression of LAMA2 and LGR5 was significantly lower in 90 drug-resistant OC tissues (Figure 6B). Importantly, low LAMA2 and LGR5 expression was significantly associated with poor OS in 1,656 OC patients, and the combination of these two genes provided a better prognostic potential for OS in OC patients (Figure 3B). LGR5, a well-established stem cell marker, has been implicated in cancer progression and therapy resistance. For example, the knockdown of LGR5 inhibited drug resistance to docetaxel in breast cancer cells (51), whereas forced expression of the gene increased chemoresistance in cervical cancer (52). Mechanistically, LGR5 drives glioma metastasis via PI3K/AKT signaling (53), a pathway enriched in our coexpression network analysis (Figure 11B). Strikingly, KEGG enrichment of LAMA2-coexpressed genes revealed RAS signaling as one of the most significantly activated pathways (Figure 11), alongside PI3K-AKT, Hippo, and stemness-related networks. This finding is highly relevant because LGR5 has been widely reported to be closely associated with the regulation of multiple signaling pathways involved in tumor progression. Studies have demonstrated that LGR5 directly regulates both pro-survival mitogen-activated extracellular signal-regulated kinase (MEK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and proliferative Wingless-type MMTV integration site family (Wnt)/β-catenin signaling in neuroblastoma, driving tumor cell survival and expansion (54). Given that MEK/ERK is the canonical downstream effector of RAS activation, this finding provides direct mechanistic evidence linking LGR5 to RAS pathway output—even in non-gastrointestinal malignancies. In parallel, we found that YAP-dependent activation of LGR5 stem cells can drive tumorigenesis upon dysregulation of the Hippo pathway (55). LGR5 cancer stem cells have been functionally validated as key drivers of tumor initiation, chemotherapy resistance, and recurrence in colorectal cancer (56), underscoring their pro-malignant role across different tissue contexts. This phenotypic overlap further supports LGR5 as a plausible downstream effector. LGR5 intestinal stem cells rely on integrated PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin signaling for stress adaptation (57). Therefore, we hypothesize that low LAMA2 expression may disrupt signaling pathways such as RAS, Hippo and PI3K/AKT, ultimately leading to increased expression or activity of LGR5. As a core downstream effector, LGR5 directly mediates the resulting aggressive biological behaviors, including tumor proliferation and therapy resistance. Our study extends these findings by suggesting that LAMA2 may be coexpressed with LGR5 to modulate chemoresistance and prognosis in OC. Furthermore, our methylation analysis revealed a significant inverse correlation between LAMA2 DNA methylation and LAMA2 transcript levels in 489 OC and 90 drug-resistant tissues (Figure 12), suggesting that epigenetic silencing is a potential driver of the downregulation of LAMA2 expression in chemoresistance. Moreover, the methylation of LAMA2 is potentially related to Ras and PI3K-AK. Although direct evidence linking LGR5 methylation to OC was not explored herein, prior colorectal cancer studies revealed that LGR5 promoter hypomethylation is correlated with LGR5 overexpression and adverse outcomes (58). Given the shared PI3K/AKT pathway association and coexpression patterns, we hypothesize that LAMA2 methylation may indirectly disrupt LGR5-mediated signaling, thereby exacerbating chemoresistance. Future studies should validate this interplay, such as by assessing LGR5 expression under LAMA2 epigenetic modulation or exploring comethylation patterns.

Increasing evidence indicates that immune cell infiltration can affect the progression and prognosis of cancers (59,60). Laminins have a significant effect on immune cells (61). For example, LAMA4 is strongly and favorably associated with the infiltration of macrophages in gastric cancer (62), and LAMC2 promotes macrophage infiltration and extracellular matrix remodeling and is correlated with poor outcomes in non-small cell lung cancer patients (63). Moreover, as an immune-related gene (61), LAMA2 is associated with a poor prognosis in patients with PAAD (64) and mediates the activation of LCK-dependent T cells by staphylococcal enterotoxin E (19). Our results demonstrate that LAMA2 might be an immune-related gene in OC. Compared with that in OC tissues with high LAMA2 expression, the number of immune-associated cells (endothelial cells, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells) was lower in OC tissues with low LAMA2 expression (Figure 10A), and the expression of the immune checkpoint molecules CTLA4 and TIGIT was significantly upregulated (Figure 10C). In addition, the TIDE score was significantly lower in OC tissues with low LAMA2 expression (Figure 10D). This suggests a higher likelihood of immune evasion and a poorer response to immunotherapy. These results demonstrate that the reduction in effector cells accompanied by the amplification of inhibitory checkpoints indicates that low LAMA2 expression fosters an immunosuppressive or “immune-cold” TIME, leading to enhanced tumor immune escape. KEGG pathway analysis (Figure 11) revealed that genes co-expressed with LAMA2 are significantly enriched in the Ras, Rap1, MAPK, PI3K-AKT, Hippo, and stem cell pluripotency signaling cascades. These pathways are not only pivotal drivers of tumor progression and chemoresistance but also established upstream regulators of the immune microenvironment, governing immune cell recruitment and effector function. This finding confirms that low LAMA2 expression is a risk factor for OC. This observation resonates with recent findings pertaining to tubulin alpha 1b (TUBA1B), a tubulin subtype whose overexpression is correlated with poor prognosis and immune evasion in multiple cancers, including breast cancer (65). Both studies highlight the critical role of immune-related biomarkers in shaping the tumor microenvironment, suggesting that targeting such molecules could improve therapeutic strategies.

The co-expression of LAMA2 and LGR5 may synergistically reshape the tumor immune microenvironment, driving tumor progression and immune evasion. Our study indicates that in OC, low expression of LAMA2 is associated with reduced immune cell infiltration, decreased TIDE scores, and elevated immune checkpoint molecules (Figure 10), suggesting its involvement in immune regulation. In bladder cancer, LAMA2 may contribute to the formation of a dense extracellular matrix (ECM) barrier and activate cancer-associated fibroblasts to secrete TGF-β/IL-10, thereby physically hindering CD8+ T cell infiltration (66). Meanwhile, LGR5+ tumor cells, through high secretion of granulocyte macrophage-colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF), activate the p-STAT3 pathway in myeloid-derived suppressor cells and drive IL-10 release, functionally suppressing CD8+ T cell activity (67). The synergy between these two mechanisms may establish an “immunosuppressive-drug resistant” collaborative network characterized by both “physical exclusion” and “functional inhibition”. Notably, this collaborative network may be co-regulated by the Ras signaling pathway. KEGG enrichment analysis (Figure 11) shows that gene sets co-expressed with LAMA2 are significantly enriched in the Ras signaling pathway. We speculate that LAMA2, as an extracellular matrix protein, may activate the Ras/MAPK pathway through receptors such as integrins, thereby providing positive feedback regulation of LGR5 expression. This process likely consolidates and amplifies the formation of the aforementioned immunosuppressive microenvironment, ultimately enhancing the immune evasion capabilities and treatment resistance of tumor cells.

The discovery of reliable biomarkers has played a crucial role in cancer treatment (68). Decreased LAMA2 expression has been shown to be a potential biomarker in various cancers, including colorectal cancer, nonfunctioning pituitary adenomas, bladder cancer (69), and hepatocellular carcinoma (70). For example, low LAMA2 expression was associated with a worse prognosis in bladder cancer patients (69) and predicted poorer OS as well as greater cancer recurrence in hepatocellular carcinoma patients (70). In this study, we revealed that low LAMA2 expression significantly predicted poor OS in patients with OC. In addition, low LAMA4 and LGR5 expression was associated with poor OS, and a combination of these two genes with LAMA2 could better predict short OS (Figure 3A). LAMA4 is a possible biomarker of disease progression and poor prognosis in MESO patients (71), and low LGR5 expression is related to poor OS in patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (72). Our previous study revealed that the combination of neurocalcin delta (NCALD) and C-X3-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CX3CL1) was more effective in predicting OS than either of these genes alone (73). All the above studies indicate that low levels of LAMA2, LAMA4, and LGR5, particularly the combination of LAMA2 and the other two genes, might be used as prognostic biomarkers in the management of OC. Like LAMA2, cytokine induced apoptosis inhibitor 1 (CIAPIN1), a cytokine-induced apoptosis inhibitor, has recently been identified as a prognostic marker in invasive breast cancer, where its expression is linked to immune infiltration and poor survival and therapy resistance (74). Notably, emerging technologies such as surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), initially applied for rapid pathogen detection (75,76), hold promise for advancing cancer diagnostics. By adapting SERS-based platforms, future studies could simultaneously profile LAMA2, LGR5, and other biomarkers in OC patients, enabling the real-time monitoring of chemoresistance and immune status.

In addition, laminins are among the main constituents of the basement membrane and play crucial roles in the regulation of tumor progression and development. For example, LAMA3 affects cell proliferation, invasion, migration, and drug resistance in LUAD by interacting with LINC00628 (77), and LAMC2 functions as an oncogene by upregulating p38 expression under the regulation of miR-125a-5p in OC (78). In this study, in addition to that of LAMA2, the expression of LAMA4, LAMB1, and LAMB2 was downregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels in OC (Figure 1A,1B) and consistently downregulated in at least six different tumors (Figure 2B-2D). These results indicate that LAMA4, LAMB1, and LAMB2 may play roles in the regulation of OC and other cancers. All of our findings need to be validated in clinical samples and cell experiments.

Conclusions

In summary, the results of this study suggest that low LAMA2 expression is likely an adverse factor affecting OC drug resistance and prognosis, probably via interactions with LGR5 and Ras signaling.
