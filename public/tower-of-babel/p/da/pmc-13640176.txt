Glycyrrhiza polysaccharide inhibits feline infectious peritonitis virus infection screened by a recombinant virus expressing eGFP protein
Authors: Yuan Yang, Haixia Fu, Mengyu Chang, Mengjia Zhang, Jiaru Zhou, Kangkai Xu, Mingjun Yang, Wentao Li, Hongmei Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFeline infectious peritonitis (FIP) is a fatal infectious disease caused by feline infectious peritonitis virus (FIPV) which causes death of young cats less than 2 years old. Until now, few drugs are available for treatment of FIP. Here, we developed a modified FIPV expressing eGFP reporter gene by reverse genetics to evaluate the ability of Chinese herbal polysaccharides on inhibition of FIPV infection in Crandell-Rees feline kidney (CRFK) cells.ResultsThe results demonstrated that the recombinant FIPV could express eGFP reporter gene, replicated in the CRFK cells and grow in a similar way with wild-type FIPV. By using this recombinant FIPV, we found that glycyrrhiza polysaccharide (GPS) effectively inhibited FIPV when added at the same time with virus or post-infection in CRFK cells. Further studies revealed that GPS inhibited FIPV adsorption and replication, but did not affect FIPV internalization in CRFK cells.ConclusionsIn the current study, we successfully established a modified FIPV platform which supports high-throughput drug screening in vitro, and we found that GPS is a promising candidate for treating FIP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05679-2.Keywords: Feline infectious peritonitis virus, eGFP reporter gene, Glycyrrhiza polysaccharide, Virus infection, CRFK cells

Background

Feline infectious peritonitis (FIP) caused around 0.3% to 1.4% of feline death, and is caused by feline infectious peritonitis virus (FIPV) which is a mutant strain of feline coronavirus (FCoV) [1]. FCoV is α-coronavirus with an envelope structure and a continuous single-stranded RNA genome [2]. It contains two genotypes: FCoV I and FCoV II [3]. FCoV I exhibits marked tropism for macrophages, replicating efficiently and producing viral particles exclusively in this cell type. It demonstrates weak serological cross-reactivity with canine coronavirus (CCV). Currently, identified strains of FCoV I includes FECV-UCD, FIPV-I-UCD1, FIPV-I-UCD3, FIPV-I-UCD4, and FIPV-I-TN406 [4, 5]. FCoV II exhibits broad cellular tropism, enabling efficient replication in diverse feline cell lines. It demonstrates strong serological cross-reactivity with CCV and originated through homologous recombination between FCoV I and the spike (S) protein gene of CCV [6]. Representative strains of FCoV II include FIPV-II-NOR15 [7], FIPV-II-1683, and FIPV-II-1146 [8]. In natural infections, approximately 80% to 95% of FIP cases are attributed to FCoV I [9]. Both FCoV I and FCoV II can be classified into two biotypes: feline enteric coronavirus (FECV) and FIPV [10]. FECV primarily infects mature intestinal epithelial cells, typically causing mild signs gastroenteritis. In contrast, FIPV loses its tropism for intestinal epithelium and instead exhibits broad cellular tropism, infecting macrophages, monocytes, plasma cells and lymphocytes. FIPV spreads systemically throughout the host within 1–3 weeks after infection, leading to severe infectious peritonitis and multisystemic disease [11, 12].

GS-441,524 has demonstrated safety and efficacy in treating cats naturally infected with FIPV [13]. However, its high cost and lack of official regulatory approval in most countries have substantially limited its widespread clinical adoption. Polysaccharides represent a key class of bioactive constituents in traditional Chinese medicine. These macromolecules consist of multiple monosaccharide units linked by glycosidic bonds and exhibit significant biological activities, including antitumour, antiviral, antioxidant, immunomodulatory, antidiabetic, and lipid-lowering effects [14–18]. However, the therapeutic potential of Chinese herbal polysaccharides against FIPV and their underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

Here, we first constructed a modified reporter virus, FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP, by replacing the ORF3abc gene with and eGFP cassette in the FIPV79-1146 cDNA backbone. We then evaluated the inhibitory effects of severe Chinese herbal polysaccharides against FIPV infection in Crandell-Rees feline kidney (CRFK) cells. These study offers a high-throughput platform for screening therapeutic drugs for treating FIP cats.

Results

Generation of the recombinant mFIPV

To construct a recombinant FIPV that containing the eGFP reporter gene, we used the homologous recombination strategy based on FIPV-1146 virus (Fig. 1). 5’UTR-ORF1a (734 bp), ORF1b-3’UTR (9,284 bp), linearized pUC57 vector (4,242 bp), MHV-S (3,798 bp), and pUC57-5’UTR-1a-1b-FIPVΔS-3’UTR (10,033 bp) were obtained by PCR amplification (Fig. 2A). Then, pUC57-5’UTR-1a-1b-FIPVΔS-3’UTR was ligated with the eGFP fragment to construct the plasmid p-mFIPV. The p-mFIPV plasmid was linearized by NotⅠ digestion, yielding a single 13,817 bp fragment (Fig. 2A). This confirmed successful p-mFIPV construction.

To generate an FIPV recombinant virus carrying the chimeric MHV-S gene, donor RNA transcribed from p-mFIPV was transfected into CRFK cells which were pre-infected with FIPV79-1146. Then, CRFK cells were co-cultured with murine LR7 cells for 48 h. The observation of syncytium formation, a hallmark cytopathic effects (CPE) characteristic of MHV infection in LR7 cells confirmed successful rescue of the recombinant virus mFIPV. The mFIPV acquired the ability to infect murine cells due to the replacement of the FIPV-S protein with the MHV-S protein (Fig. 2B). Full-length S gene sequencing confirmed 100% identity with MHV-A59-S, verifying complete replacement of the FIPV79-1146 S. Indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) of mFIPV-infected LR7 cells demonstrated detectable MHV-S and FCoV-nucleocapsid protein (FCoV-N) expression, while FCoV spike protein (FCoV-S) signals were absent (Fig. 2C).

Construction of the recombinant FIPV expressing eGFP

The RNA transcribed from NotI-linearized p-FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP (Fig. 3A) was electroporated into mFIPV-infected LR7 cells. Following co-cultured with CRFK cells, characteristic CPE-including cell rounding, detachment and syncytial formation-were observed. These morphological changes mirrored wild-type FIPV79-1146 infection and coincided with eGFP fluorescence at 48 h post-electroporation (Fig. 3B). These may indicated successful engineering of a recombinant reporter virus expressing the eGFP gene (FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP) by reverse genetics. Further IFA assay verified that the FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP-infected CRFK cells exhibited FCoV-N and FCoV-S proteins expression as wild-type FIPV, and showed specific eGFP fluorescence (Fig. 3C). Critically, MHV-S protein expression was absent, confirming successful reversion of the MHV-S gene to the native FIPV-S in the genomic backbone (Fig. 3C). Western blot analysis also confirmed FCoV-N protein expression in the recombinant FIPV-infected cells (Fig. 3D). Moreover, growth kinetics analysis revealed no significant difference in proliferation capacity between FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP and its parental virus (Fig. 3E). No statistically significant decline of the mean fluorescence intensity occurred between multiple passages from P1-P10 (Fig. S1A). Replication kinetics were consistent across passages, with no attenuation observed in peak viral titers (P1 vs. P5 vs. P10, Fig. S1B). Specific PCR targeting the inserted eGFP gene and sequencing confirmed intact insertion from P1 to P10 (supplementary Fig.S1CD in File1 and File 2).

Development of antiviral assays utilizing FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP

We administered 250–4000 µg/mL Lentinan, 250–5000 µg/mL Lycium barbarum polysaccharide, 250–5000 µg/mL Poria cocos polysaccharide, 250–3000 µg/mL GPS, and 125–2000 µg/mL Flos lonicera polysaccharide on CRFK cells for 24 h and 48 h. The cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay demonstrated that the maximum non-toxic concentrations (MNTC) for these five polysaccharides were 4000 µg/mL (Fig. 4A), 3000 µg/mL (Fig. 4B), 3000 µg/mL (Fig. 4C), 3000 µg/mL (Fig. 4D), and 1000 µg/mL (Fig. 4E), respectively at 48 h.

Antiviral efficacy of these five polysaccharides against FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP was evaluated through fluorescence intensity in CRFK cells. The results determined the half-maximal effective concentrations (EC50) as follows: 5984 µg/mL for Lentinan (Fig. 4A), 1337 µg/mL for Lycium barbarum polysaccharide (Fig. 4B), 358 µg/mL for Poria cocos polysaccharide (Fig. 4C), 1388 µg/mL for GPS (Fig. 4D), and 899 µg/mL for Flos lonicera polysaccharide (Fig. 4E). Notably, 3000 µg/mL GPS exhibited 99.8% inhibition of the reporter virus, which showed better antivirus efficacy than other agents (P < 0.001).

GPS displays anti-FIPV activity

The CRFK cells were pretreated with GPS for 2 h, followed by infection with FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP (MOI = 0.1). After 2 h, cells were washed with PBS and cultured with fresh medium and GPS for 24 h (Fig. 5A). Immunofluorescence assay revealed a dose-dependent reduction in eGFP fluorescence in GPS-treated groups, and 3000 µg/mL GPS showed a 99% inhibition rate to virus (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5B-C). TCID50 and qPCR indicated that the viral titers and viral copy numbers in cell supernatants were reduced by GPS-treated cells compared with GPS-untreated cells (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5D-E). Moreover, no difference was observed on the inhibition rates between 3000 µg/mL GPS and the positive control of GS-441,524 (50 µM) (Fig. 5B-C).

Besides, pre-treatment (2-hour drug administration prior to infection), co-treatment (simultaneous application of GPS with virus), and post-treatment (drug administration post-infection) strategies were employed to determine which stage GPS interferred during virus growth in the CRFK cells (See Methods in Additional File 1). The results suggest that GPS mainly exert its antiviral effects during or after viral entry (See Figures S2 ~ S4 in Additional File1).

GPS inhibited FIPV binding

Binding to the cell surface is the first step in the early stage of virus infection. Therefore, CRFK cells were incubated with FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP (MOI = 0.1) and GPS at 4 °C for 2 h. The infected cells were then washed and cultured in DMEM at 37 °C for 24 h (Fig. 6A). The IFA revealed that 3,000 µg/mL GPS inhibited FIPV infection compared with control group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6B). The TCID50 and qPCR showed the inhibition rates of approximately 9.4% and 39.3%, respectively, based on the virus titer and virus copy number, after treated with 3,000 µg/mL GPS (Fig. 6C-D). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that the virus N protein in the 3,000 µg/mL GPS-treated cells decreased compared with none GPS treated cells (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6E).

GPS did not affect the internalization of FIPV

The CRFK cells were incubated with FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP (MOI = 0.1) at 4 °C for 2 h, then the cells were treated with GPS at 37 °C for 2 h, and were finally washed and cultured in DMEM for 24 h (Fig. 7A). IFA revealed no significant difference in fluorescence intensity between the GPS-treated and untreated groups (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, both TCID50 and qPCR assays demonstrated comparable viral titers and viral genome copy numbers, respectively, in GPS-treated versus untreated cells (Fig. 7C-D). These results demonstrate that GPS did not affect virus internalization into CRFK cells.

GPS affected FIPV replication

CRFK cells were first infected with FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP (MOI = 0.1) at 37 °C for 2 h, followed by washing with PBS, and then incubated with the corresponding concentrations of GPS at 37 °C for 24 h (Fig. 8A). IFA showed dose-dependent inhibition of FIPV infection when GPS concentrations increased from 0 to 3,000 µg/mL, and 3,000 µg/mL GPS completely inhibited FIPV infection compared with the untreated group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 8B). The TCID50 and qPCR results showed inhibition rates of approximately 20.7% and 94.3%, respectively, following treatment with 3,000 µg/mL GPS (Fig. 8C-D). Western blot analysis revealed that 3,000 µg/mL GPS significantly inhibited the FIPV-N protein expression in CRFK cells (P < 0.001) (Fig. 8E). These results indicated that GPS significantly inhibited FIPV replication.

Discussion

Despite the development of multiple vaccines against FIP, the phenomenon of antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) prevents these vaccines from providing sufficient immune protection and may even enhance the infectivity of FIPV [19, 20]. Therefore, developing potential anti-FIP drugs has become an effective strategy for controlling FIP. Reverse genetics systems are important tools for studying RNA viruses and developing antiviral drugs. Currently, methods for constructing reverse genetics systems for FCoV include targeted RNA recombination [21], BAC vectors [22], vaccinia virus vectors [23], yeast TAR technology [24], and circular polymerase extension reaction (CPER) [25]. Targeted RNA recombination demonstrates significantly higher efficiency than CPER in multi-fragment replacement, making it particularly suitable for constructing complex chimeric viruses. Previous research reported that deletions in the ORF3abc and ORF7ab genes of FIPV are crucial for viral replication and virulence in vivo but do not affect its replication in vitro [22, 26], making these regions ideal insertion sites for foreign genes. In the current study, the chimeric strain mFIPV, generated by swapping the FIPV S protein for the MHV S protein, acquired the ability to infect murine receptor-expressing LR7 cells. This adaption not only expands experimental tractability but also enables future evaluation of antiviral strategies in murine models, offering a path to bridge in vitro finding with in vivo efficacy. Furthermore, our successful replacement of ORF3abc with eGFP via RNA recombination technology resulted in a recombinant FIPV strain that retained stable passage capability and parental-like biological properties. This engineered virus provides a reliable and quantifiable platform for high-throughput screening of anti-FIPV therapeutics, addressing a critical need in FIP drug development.

Despite reported antiviral effects of lentinan [27], Lycium barbarum polysaccharide [28], poria cocos polysaccharide [29] and Flos lonicerae polysaccharide on diverse viruses via entry or replication blockade, no significant anti-FIPV activity in CRFK cells was observed for these compounds. This indicates virus-specific divergence in the antiviral effects of plant-derived polysaccharides, which may stem from variations in viral targets, host-pathogen interaction mechanisms, or structural features of polysaccharides themselves. Previous studies demonstrated that 40 µg/mL GPS achieves maximum inhibition of porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) during early infection stages [18]. In contrast, 3,000 µg/mL GPS achieves maximum suppression of FIPV replication in CRFK cells in vitro. The reason for the significant difference in effective concentrations may be due to the GPS purity and botanical origin. Nevertheless, the appropriate concentration of GPS for treating FIPV cats in vivo need further exploration.

Virus infection involves four stages: viral adsorption to the cell surface by binding to specific receptors or cytokines [30], internalization into the host cell, genome replication, and viral release. Each stage can serve as a potential target for antiviral drugs. Antiviral drugs can be classified into virus-targeting drugs and host-targeting drugs based on their target of action [31, 32]. In this study, pre-treating cells with GPS did not reduce FIPV infectivity, possibly due to the lack of effect of GPS on host cell receptors or cytokines. However, when GPS and FIPV were incubated together and then added to the cells, or when GPS was added after FIPV infection, GPS significantly inhibited FIPV infection, indicating its inhibitory effects in both the early and replication stages of FIPV infection. These findings suggest that the antiviral effects of GPS are primarily due to targeting FIPV. Besides, studies on the FIPV lifecycle further verified GPS impacts FIPV adsorption and replication, but not internalization in CRFK cells. However, the inhibition of viral adsorption is less pronounced than that of viral replication. This may be because co-treatment and post-infection treatment with GPS primarily target viral genes related to replication. However, the mechanisms by which GPS inhibits FIPV adsorption and replication require further investigation. Moreover, we found the apparent discrepancy in inhibition percentages observed at 3000 µg/mL GPS between the qPCR (39.3%, 94.3%) and TCID50 (9.4%, 20.7%) during virus infection in Figs. 6 and 8, respectively. This may firstly due to the intrinsic distinction between qPCR and TCID50: qPCR quantifies total viral RNA includes successfully attached and entered, initiated replication, and produced new genomic RNA, while TCID50 specifically quantifies infections virus particles capable of completing the full replication cycle and producing cytopathic effect (CPE) in new target cells. Thus, TCID50 provides a more stringent measure of successful infection establishment than qPCR. Secondly, GPS primarily acts by inhibiting viral attachment and replication, qPCR inhibition (e.g.,39.3%) reflects a reduction in this RNA load compared to untreated groups. However, the TCID50 assay measures the new infections progeny which produced by a small fraction of cells and might not be proportionally reduced if the replication cycle proceeds efficiently within the infected cells. Thus, this could lead to a less pronounced inhibition observed by TCID50 (e.g.,9.4%) compared to the induction in initial infection events measured by qPCR. However, the factors sampling time, more stringent washing, RNase treatment, and residual compound removing which should be strictly controlled may also lead to this discrepancy.

Though FCoV type I predominates in natural FIP cases, but it is challenging to propagate in cell culture, whereas type II strains grow efficiently across cell lines of CRFK. The FIPV strain 79-1146 has been served as a foundational model for reverse genetics and FIPV drug screening, providing a stable and well-characterized platform for genetic manipulation [33]. Epidemiological surveys have also shown that type II strains are detectable in clinical FIP cases, confirming the mixed infection of FCoV type I and II in FIP cats [9]. Genomic analyses indicated that FCoV type II arose via recombination with type I backgrounds which retaining significant similarity in replication-related genomic regions that supports the use of type II for initial antiviral screening [6]. For instance, though GS-441,524 was initially screened using the type II strain 79-1146 in vitro, it has proven highly effective against FCoV type I infected cats [13]. However, in the current study, we primarily utilized the FCoV type II strain 79-1146 to evaluate the drug efficiency. GPS has not been evaluated against type I-related strains or clinical isolates, the efficacy of GPS on against type I clinical isolates remains to be established in future study.

The primary monocyte or macrophage are idea cell model to perform research on FIPV in vitro. However, these cells are prone to apoptosis and it is necessary to shorten the experimental period and avoid excessive passaging when exploration. Now, CRFK cells are a widely accepted and well-validated model for FIPV infection in vitro (particularly for strain 79-1146) and antiviral drug screening. Van Hamme find that key infection processes including viral attachment and internalization in CRFK cells are highly consistent with those in feline monocytes [34]. Bálint confirmed that CRFK cells support stable replication of FIPV even after ORF3abc deletion, enabling reliable application of recombinant reporter viruses used in this study [22]. Murphy employed CRFK cells to evaluate GS-441,524 (a gold-standard anti-FIP agent) and also reported the therapeutic efficacy of GS-441,524 in vivo [13]. However, though CRFK cells served as a practical initial screen for antiviral activity and drug selection. Limitations arise from their inability to model macrophage-specific processes, particularly ADE and immunopathology. Our data on GPS effect on FIPV is a proof-of-concept in a simplified system, with clinical relevance contingent upon validation in primary feline macrophages. Validation in feline monocytes/macrophages or dosing studies in FIP animal models is essential to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

In this study, we first established an effective recombinant virus for anti-FIPV 79-1146 drug screening and demonstrated the significant anti-virus activity of GPS which interfered virus adsorption and replication in CRFK cells. These results identified GPS as a potential therapeutic drug against FCoV type II strain infection.

Materials and methods

Cells, virus, and drugs

CRFK cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The feline infectious peritonitis virus strain 79-1146 (FIPV 79-1146) and mouse hepatitis virus strain A59 (MHV-A59) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained in our laboratory. GPS (≥ 95%) was purchased from Ci Yuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shaanxi, China) and was diluted to 10 mg/mL in serum-free DMEM.

Construction of recombinant plasmids

The 5’-UTR-ORF1a fragment and ORF1b-3’-UTR fragment were amplified by PCR from FIPV79-1146 cDNA using A-F1/R1 and A-F2/R2 primers shown in Table 1. Primer A-F1 was designed to incorporate a T7 promoter and an XhoI restriction site, while Primer A-R1 contained a SacI restriction site. The fragments were then cloned into pUC57 vector, generating pUC57-5ʹUTR-1a-1b-FIPV-3ʹUTR. For chimeric plasmid construction, the full-length S gene of MHV-A59 were amplified from cDNA using primers B-F1/R1 (Table 1). The S gene-deleted backbone (pUC57-5’UTR-1a-1b-FIPVΔS-3’UTR) was generated by PCR from linearized pUC57-5’UTR-1a-1b-FIPV-3’UTR using primers B-F2/R2. The purified MHV-A59-S fragment was then inserted into the predefined deletion site via homologous recombination, yielding chimeric plasmid p-mFIPV. Given that deletion of the ORF3abc genes does not impair viral growth in vitro [27], we constructed a recombinant plasmid by replacing ORF3abc with an eGFP reporter gene. Thus, the ORF3abc-deleted genomic fragment was amplified from pUC57-5ʹUTR-1a-1b-FIPV-3ʹUTR using primers A-F3/R3 (Table 1). The eGFP gene was amplified from pEGFP-N1 with primers eGFP-F/R (Table 1), and was recombined to ORF3abc-deleted genomic fragment through Gibson assembly, yielding pFIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP.

Targeted RNA recombination

The chimeric MHV-FIPV S gene was precisely integrated into the FIPV genome viatargeted homologous RNA recombination between donor RNA derived from p-mFIPV and the recipient FIPV 79-1146 genome. NotI-linearized p-mFIPV was transcribed in vitro using the T7 RNA polymerase kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to generate viral genomic RNA. Donor RNA was electroporated into CRFK cells preinfected with FIPV for 4 h at 450 V, 50 µF, with 3 pulses. Cells were then seeded onto LR7 cell monolayers for 48 h. Progeny virus was harvested from the supernatant upon observation of syncytium formation in LR7 cells, yielding the intermediate virus mFIPV.

To generate recombinant FIPV with restored native S gene and reporter gene, NotI-linearized p-FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP was transcribed in vitro using T7 RNA polymerase kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to produce viral genomic RNA. Donor RNA was electroporated into LR7 cells that were pre-infected with mFIPV for 4 h. Cells were then co-cultured with CRFK cells. Progeny virus was collected from the supernatant when CRFK cells exhibited typical CPE including cell rounding, detachment, and syncytium formation with eGFP fluorescence. Finally, the recombinant FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP was isolated through two rounds of plaque purification. The eGFP stability was determined after the recombinant virus passaging from P1 to P10 in CRFK cells and was sent to sequencing at P10 to prevention mutations in eGFP fragment (Supplementary methods in file 1).

CCK-8 assay

Cytotoxicity was evaluated in CRFK cells using the CCK-8 (Biosharp, Anhui, China). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated until approximately 80% confluent. Treatments included GPS (0, 250, 500, 1,000, 2,000, or 3,000 µg/mL) or other drugs. After 48 h, the cells were washed with PBS, and incubated with 10 µL CCK-8 solution for 1 h. Absorbance (OD value) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (PerkinElmer EnSpire, USA). Cell viability was calculated using the following formula: cell viability = [OD (with drug) – OD (blank)] / [OD (without drug) - OD (blank)] × 100%. IFA assay

CRFK cells at 90% confluence were infected with virus for 2 h. Cells were then washed with PBS and cultured in DMEM containing 2% FBS for 24 h. After washing with sterile PBS, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilizated with 0.03% Triton X-100, and were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 45 min. Primary antibodies included: mouse monoclonal anti- FCoV-N, mouse polyclonal anti-FCoV-S, and mouse monoclonal anti-MHV–S. All antibodies were prepared in our laboratory and the working concentration was 2 µg/mL. After washing with PBST (PBS with 0.1% Tween-20), the cells were incubated with secondary antibody of Donkey anti-Mouse IgG (H + L), Alexa Fluor™ 790 (Antgene, Wuhan) and Rabbit anti-Human IgG (H + L), or Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Abclonal, Wuhan) respectively, at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, nuclei were stained with DAPI. Immunofluorescence imaging was performed at the National Key Laboratory of Agricultural Microbiology Core Facility.

Viral adsorption, internalization, and replication assays

All assays were performed when CRFK cells reached 80–90% confluence. For viral adsorption assay, CRFK cells were incubated with FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP (MOI = 0.1) and GPS (0, 500, 1,000, 2,000, or 3,000 µg/mL) at 4 °C for 2 h. The infected cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and cultured in DMEM containing 2% FBS at 37 °C for 24 h. For the viral internalization assay, CRFK cells were infected with FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP (MOI = 0.1) at 4 °C for 2 h, washed with chilled PBS, and then incubated with GPS (0, 500, 1,000, 2,000, or 3,000 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 2 h. Thereafter, the cells were washed with PBS and cultured in DMEM containing 2% FBS at 37 °C for 24 h. For the viral replication assay, CRFK cells were infected with FIPV-ΔORF3abc-eGFP (MOI = 0.1) at 37 °C for 2 h, washed with PBS, and incubated with GPS (0, 500, 1000, 2000, or 3000 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 24 h. All assays included a control group that was neither treated with GPS nor infected with virus. Cell suspension or cell lysates were collected for TCID50, qPCR and Western blot analyses.

Viral titer assay

The viral titers were assessed by the half tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assay. After 24 h of infection, the virus solution in the 24-well plate was repeatedly freeze-thawed at -80 °C three times, after which the mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min at 4,000 rpm to collect the viral supernatant. CRFK cells were seeded in 96-well plates in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. When cell density reached the confluence of 80%-90%, viral samples were inoculated into CRFK cells using 10-fold serial dilutions ranging from 10− 1 to 10− 8. For each dilution, 100 µL of viral solution was mixed with 900 µL of DMEM containing 2% FBS, and eight replicates were prepared for each dilution. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 3–4 days. Fluorescence intensity was monitored with a fluorescence microscope until no fluorescence was observed in cells within the highest dilution wells. The TCID50 was calculated according to the Reed-Muench method.

Western blot

CRFK cells were washed with PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) to collect the protein The protein samples were separated by 12% SDS‒PAGE and subsequently transferred on to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked in 5% skim milk at room temperature for 2 h. Following this, the membrane was washed with TBST and incubated at 4 °C overnight with either a mouse monoclonal anti-FIPV antibody (LS Bio, USA) or a mouse monoclonal anti-β-actin antibody (ABclonal, Wuhan, China). Then, the membrane was washed with TBST and incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000) at room temperature for 45 min. Finally, after washing with TBST, bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Bio-Rad, Calif. USA).

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Viral RNA was extracted from the cells or cell supernatants by trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). One-strand cDNA was synthesized from the total RNA using the HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qPCR was performed using a SYBR® Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Tracking Kit (Accurate Biology, China). A standard curve of each experiment was constructed with FIPV79-1146 N gene standard plasmids. The following primers were used according to previous research [35]: Forward primer: 5′ -TGGCCACACAGGGACAAC-3′ and reverse primer: 5′- AGAACGACCACGTCTT TTGGAA-3′. Absolute quantification was performed, and the viral genome copy number was calculated based on the Ct value of each sample and the standard curve.

Statistical analysis

All the results were obtained from at least three independent experiments and are presented as the means ± SD. All the experimental groups were compared to the 0 µg/mL GPS treated cells. Data were analyzed via one-way ANOVA using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. The significance is indicated as follows: ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001.
