Development and Validation of a Health Literacy Scale for Patients With Cardiovascular Disease (HLS‐CVD)
Authors: Jeongeun Sim, Seon Young Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAssessing health literacy has become important as a component of strategies to increase self‐care behaviour for patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD).AimThis study aimed to develop a scale for measuring health literacy in patients with CVD and to evaluate its reliability and validity.MethodsThis is an instrument development study with a psychometric analysis. The initial questions were based on the components identified in a concept analysis, and the reliability and validity were evaluated with 391 patients visiting outpatient hospitals due to CVD such as myocardial infarction, arrhythmia and heart failure.ResultsThe final scale consisted of 22 items across four factors: ‘Understanding and searching for health information’, ‘Actively selecting and evaluating health information’, ‘Utilization of health information resources’ and ‘Interacting with healthcare providers’. The four‐factor structure demonstrated acceptable model fit in confirmatory factor analysis. Convergent and criterion validity were supported by significant correlations with established health literacy tools. Internal consistency was high (Cronbach's α = 0.891), and test–retest reliability was satisfactory.ConclusionThis health literacy scale for patients with CVD can be utilized in future surveys or intervention studies to enhance the self‐care behaviour of individuals with CVD. Its psychometric analysis should be repeated with CVD patients in other countries or hospital settings.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death globally, with approximately 20.5 million deaths reported in 2021 (Lindstrom et al. 2022). As the prevalence of CVD continues to rise, so does its associated socioeconomic burden. The American Heart Association (AHA) projects that by 2035, CVD will surpass diabetes and Alzheimer's disease as the most burdensome health condition in terms of cost and impact (AHA 2023). In particular, to prevent the progression to heart failure following primary cardiac events such as myocardial infarction, it is essential to conduct continuous nursing research that supports long‐term disease management and promotes sustained health behaviour change. Among the factors influencing self‐care behaviour, health literacy has emerged as a key determinant.

Health literacy is defined as the cognitive and social ability to access, understand, evaluate, and apply health‐related information (World Health Organization [WHO] 1998). Previous studies have shown that lower health literacy in patients with heart failure or CVD is associated with reduced disease knowledge, poor medication adherence, and lower self‐efficacy for dietary and exercise behaviours (Rezaie et al. 2022; Shin et al. 2016). Furthermore, health literacy has been identified as a predictor of quality of life (Kanejima et al. 2022) and a significant factor influencing self‐care compliance, particularly in older adults (Jo et al. 2020). In line with this, the AHA has recommended assessing patients' health literacy when designing educational interventions aimed at improving self‐care behaviours (Magnani et al. 2018). As such, health literacy serves not only as a target of intervention but also as an important indicator for evaluating the effectiveness of those interventions in the fields of cardiovascular health and nursing.

However, the tools currently used to measure health literacy in CVD patients were largely developed for the general adult population and often fail to reflect the disease‐specific needs of individuals with CVD. For example, the Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine (REALM; Davis et al. 1993) and the Test of Functional Health Literacy in Adults (TOFHLA; Parker et al. 1995) primarily assess functional literacy such as word recognition and numeracy (Kang and Lee 2015), and may be insufficient for evaluating the complex informational and behavioural demands faced by CVD patients. Similarly, generic tools such as the European Health Literacy Survey Questionnaire (HLS‐EU; Pelikan and Ganahl 2017) are useful for population‐level comparisons but may lack sensitivity to the emotional, social and practical challenges faced by individuals managing chronic diseases. To address these limitations, Matsuoka et al. (2016) developed a health literacy scale specific to heart failure patients, focusing on functional, communicative and critical domains. While their tool contributes meaningfully to the assessment of individual capacities, it does not capture sociocultural dimensions—such as the use of social support and informal caregiving—which are particularly relevant for older CVD patients who often rely on such networks. Thus, there is a need for a more comprehensive and disease‐specific instrument that incorporates both individual and sociocultural components of health literacy.

The concept of health literacy itself has evolved over time. Initially regarded as basic reading and writing ability within a clinical context (Parker et al. 1995), health literacy is now recognized as a dynamic, multidimensional construct that encompasses information‐seeking, communication with healthcare providers, critical evaluation of health content and real‐world application (WHO 2024). A hybrid concept analysis of health literacy among patients with CVD, including ischemic heart disease, arrhythmia and heart failure, identified not only individual self‐care attributes but also sociocultural aspects such as the evaluation and application of information and interactions with healthcare providers (Sim and Hwang 2019).

Therefore, the aim of this study was to develop and validate a measurement tool that captures the multidimensional and context‐specific attributes of health literacy in patients with CVD. From a nursing perspective, health literacy is fundamental to effective patient education, self‐care support and long‐term disease management, with nurses playing a central role in assessment and intervention. This tool is expected to support the development and evaluation of nurse‐led interventions and to serve as a useful resource for future research aimed at improving self‐care behaviours and intervention effectiveness among patients with CVD.

Methods

Design

This study employed an instrument development design to develop a scale for measuring health literacy in patients with CVD and subsequently evaluated its reliability and validity.

Scale Development Process

Development Stage

The scale development and evaluation processes were conducted according to the scale development procedure suggested by DeVellis (2017). First, the dimensions and attributes of the concept were identified based on the results of a previous study (Sim and Hwang 2019) that performed a concept analysis of the health literacy of patients with CVD. In‐depth interviews were conducted with 13 patients of various ages and education levels who were diagnosed with myocardial infarction, angina pectoris, arrhythmia, and heart failure and who were visiting the hospital. The research results were classified into two dimensions: the individual functional dimension and the inter‐relational dimension. In the individual functional dimension, three attributes were identified: the ability to understand written health information, the ability to retrieve health information, and the ability to utilize health information. In the inter‐relational dimension, two attributes were identified: interacting with healthcare providers and utilizing health information support resources (Sim and Hwang 2019). The elements and initial questions for scale development were composed based on the two derived dimensions and five attributes.

Preliminary Question Composition

The questionnaire items were developed based on the attributes identified during the concept analysis phase, resulting in an initial pool of 47 items derived from literature review and interview data. However, a large number of initial items may reduce participant concentration and increase the likelihood of missing responses. Following the rationale by Ji and Kim (2015), which suggests that a smaller number of items is preferable as long as the construct is adequately captured, two researchers reviewed and consolidated overlapping or similar items to refine the initial item pool. The initial questions included sub‐dimensions of ability to understand health information (3 questions), ability to search for health information (6 questions), ability to utilize health information (10 questions), communication with healthcare providers (5 questions) and utilization of health information support resources (5 questions). The initial questions of the scale were derived as a total of 29 questions.

Content Validity Verification

The initial item pool consisted of 47 items. After removing conceptually redundant or overlapping items, 29 items remained for content validity evaluation. Content validity of these 29 items was assessed by a panel of experts, including two cardiologists with over 10 years of clinical experience, three nursing professors specializing in CVD research and four clinical nurses working in cardiology. The scale‐level Content Validity Index (S‐CVI) was 0.93, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.90 (Lynn 1986; Polit et al. 2007). As a result of the expert review, 5 items that did not meet the I‐CVI criterion were deleted, and 9 items were revised based on expert feedback. Consequently, a total of 24 preliminary items were finalized for psychometric testing. Each item was rated using a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ (1) to ‘strongly agree’ (4).

Preliminary Investigation

In accordance with the criteria appropriate to conduct a preliminary survey (DeVellis 2017), we conducted a preliminary survey of 30 patients with CVD at a medical institution in Seoul, Korea. The response time was 7–10 min, and as a result of the preliminary survey, no items were corrected. After reviewing the items, a professor majoring in Korean literature conducted proofreading to identify the items used in the final questionnaire.

Scale Evaluation Phase: Reliability and Validity

Study Participants

The participants of this study were adults aged 18 years or older who visited the outpatient departments of two tertiary‐care hospitals located in Seoul and G Province, Korea, and had been medically diagnosed with previous myocardial infarction, angina, heart failure and arrhythmia. This criterion was chosen as the scale was designed for general applicability across major CVD conditions rather than for a single disease category to ensure broader clinical relevance.

For a stable correlation between items during scale development, the appropriate sample number is 150 or more for Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and 200 or more for Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) (Yu 2012). Of the 420 questionnaires collected, 391 were included in the final analysis. A total of 29 responses were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) substantial missing data, (2) inconsistent responses to reverse‐coded items and (3) uniform response patterns suggesting low engagement (e.g., selecting the same option throughout). To prevent inflation of model fit and construct validity, the total sample of 391 responses was randomly split using the SPSS case selection function. Of these, 201 participants were randomly allocated for EFA, and the remaining 190 participants were used for CFA.

Ethical Consideration

This study was conducted following approval from the Institutional Review Board of Kangbuk Samsung Medical Center in Seoul (KBSMC 2020‐08‐018‐002). Prior to data collection, the first author provided potential participants with detailed information regarding the purpose of the study, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality and protection of personal information, compensation for participation, and the right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the survey was subsequently administered to those who voluntarily agreed to participate.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS/WIN 24.0 and AMOS/WIN 24.0. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean and standard deviation, were used to summarize participants' general characteristics. The content validity of the preliminary scale was evaluated using the CVI. For construct validity, both EFA and CFA were conducted. EFA was performed using Principal Axis Factoring with direct Oblimin rotation. The number of factors was determined based on eigenvalues greater than 1.0, scree plot inspection and cumulative variance explained, following Lee (2010). CFA was conducted on a separate subsample using the maximum likelihood estimation method. Model fit was assessed using multiple indices: Chi‐square (χ 2), Chi‐square minimum divided by degrees of freedom (CMIN/df), Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Standardized Root Mean Residual (SRMR), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Incremental Fit Index (IFI).

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's alpha, with values ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable. Test–retest reliability was evaluated in a subsample of 50 participants who completed the scale twice, 2 weeks apart, using Pearson's correlation coefficients and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). Convergent validity was examined through critical ratios (C.R.), average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR).

In addition, convergent validity was further evaluated by examining the Pearson correlation coefficient between the total score of the newly developed 22‐item scale and the Set of Brief Screening Questions modified by Kim (2010). This instrument was selected because it is widely used as a brief measure of health literacy in Korean clinical settings and has demonstrated acceptable reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.91) and validity in previous studies (Kim 2010). Therefore, it provides an appropriate external criterion for assessing the convergent validity of the newly developed scale.

Criterion validity was assessed by examining the Pearson correlation coefficient between the newly developed 22‐item scale and the European Health Literacy Survey Questionnaire–Short Form (HLS‐EU‐Q16) (Pelikan and Ganahl 2017). The HLS‐EU was selected as the external criterion because it is one of the most widely used and validated instruments for measuring health literacy internationally, with substantial evidence supporting its reliability and construct validity. Therefore, it served as an appropriate gold‐standard measure for evaluating the criterion validity of this newly developed scale.

Results

General Characteristics of Participants for Instrumental Evaluation

The average age of the participants was 63.5 ± 10.9 years, with those in their 60s or older accounting for 68.3%, followed by those in their 50s (22.3%) and those under 40 (9.5%). Of the 391 patients, 132 (33.8%) were female and 259 (66.2%) were male. Married was the most common marital status (77.2%), followed by living with a spouse and children (49.9%) and living alone (14.8%). Myocardial infarction was the most common condition (33.1%), followed by angina (29.6%), arrhythmia (17.5%) and heart failure (12.3%) (Table 1).

Item Analysis

The content validity of the scale was confirmed through expert evaluation. The item‐level CVI values ranged from 0.80 to 1.00, and the scale‐level CVI was 0.92, indicating excellent content validity. As a result of analysing each item developed in this study, the average score of each item was 2.30 to 3.65, and there were no items outside the standard values of skewness ±3 and kurtosis ±7 (Yu 2012). Item analysis was used for evaluating the correlation coefficients between items and the total score. Consequently, it was confirmed that the correlation coefficient was less than 0.40 in this study (Field 2009), the correlation coefficient between items and total score was 0.41 to 0.74, and all items were 0.40 or higher.

Validity Analysis

Construct Validity

Exploratory Factor Analysis

The EFA was conducted on 190 of 391 participants selected using the case random sampling method with the final developed 24‐item version of the scale. Kaiser‐Meyer‐Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett's sphericity tests were performed to check whether the collected data were suitable for factor analysis. In this study, the KMO measure value was 0.867, which means that each variable was predicted by other variables and was suitable for factor analysis (Heo 2013). As a result of Bartlett's sphericity test, the approximate χ 2 value was 2743.07 (p < 0.001), indicating that the correlation between variables was significant (Heo 2013).

Factors were extracted using the principal factor method, and to determine the number of factors, four factors greater than one among the eigenvalues of the sample correlation matrix were determined based on Kaiser's rule (Heo 2013). Factor rotation was performed directly using the Oblimin method based on the assumption that there is a correlation between the subfactors of the health information comprehension ability of patients with CVD (Heo 2013). Factor loading was used to identify the factor structure, and the value was the correlation coefficient between each variable and the corresponding factor, and items with a factor loading value of 0.40 or higher were selected (Hair et al. 2010).

As a result of the EFA, two items were deleted in the first EFA due to factor loadings < 0.40. A second EFA was then conducted on the remaining 22 items (Hair et al. 2010). To construct an item that can express the meaning of each factor well, it is recommended that the item be removed if the commonality is less than 0.30 (Field 2009). The cumulative variance explained by the four extracted factors was 62.9%. The variance contribution of each factor was 20.8%, 16.3%, 13.1% and 12.8%, respectively (Table 2).

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

A CFA was performed to determine whether the internal structure identified by the EFA was replicated in a separate sample. A CFA was performed to verify whether the factor structure identified by EFA was replicated in an independent sample. Of the total sample of 391 participants, 201 were randomly assigned to CFA. The analysis was conducted using the maximum likelihood estimation method, and each latent factor consisted of at least three observed variables, satisfying the prerequisite for CFA (Yu 2012).

The CFA was conducted on 22 items representing four sub‐factors. All factor loadings exceeded 0.50, and squared multiple correlations (SMC) were above 0.20. C.R. for all items were ≥ 1.96 with p < 0.05, meeting the statistical significance criteria (Yu 2012).

Model fit indices indicated good fit: χ 2 = 365.80 (df = 203, p < 0.001), CMIN/df = 1.80, GFI = 0.90, SRMR = 0.06, RMSEA = 0.06, CFI = 0.96, IFI = 0.96. Discriminant validity was confirmed, as the squared correlations between factors (0.08–0.27) were smaller than the AVE values (0.58–0.72) (Figure 1).

Convergence Validity Verification

To verify the convergent validity, the Pearson correlation coefficient between the 22‐item scale developed in this study and the Set of Brief Screening Questions as modified by Kim (2010) was analysed. The analysis revealed a positive correlation (r = 0.38, p < 0.001), confirming the scale's convergent validity.

Criterion Validity Verification

Concurrent validity, a type of criterion validity, assesses the validity of a measurement tool by examining its correlation with an established instrument that has been successfully used in related fields (DeVellis 2017). According to Smith (1976), the external criterion should possess validity, reliability, and practical acceptability. In this study, an existing health literacy scale, previously validated for reliability and validity and widely accepted in clinical practice, was selected as the external criterion for evaluating concurrent validity. The criterion validity of the newly developed scale was assessed by calculating the correlation coefficient between its scores and those of the established health literacy scale.

To verify criterion validity, the Pearson correlation coefficient between the scores of this scale (22 items) and the HLS‐EU scale (Pelikan and Ganahl 2017) was checked. As a result of the analysis, the correlation coefficient was 0.45 (p < 0.001), indicating a positive correlation; thus, the validity of the correlation was secured.

Reliability Analysis

Stability and Reliability

Test–retest reliability was evaluated using a two‐week interval. The Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.87 (p < 0.001), and the ICC was 0.843, indicating high stability and reliability of the scale.

Homogeneity Reliability

This was verified using the most representative Cronbach's coefficient for internal consistency reliability. Consequently, Cronbach's α = 0.891 for all 22 questions; active health information selection and evaluation by factor, 0.92; utilization of health information resources, 0.89; interaction with healthcare providers, 0.91; and understanding and exploring health information, 0.81, indicating the reliability of this scale.

Final Development of a Health Literacy Scale for Patients With CVD

The validity and reliability of the initial questions for measuring the Health Literacy Scale of patients with CVD (HLS‐CVD) were verified using various methods, and a measurement scale was developed. This self‐report questionnaire uses a 4‐point Likert scale with four sub‐factors: ‘Understanding and searching for health information’, ‘Actively selecting and evaluating health information’, ‘Utilization of health information resources’, ‘Interacting with healthcare providers’. Ultimately, it was developed into 22 items, with 1 point being given for ‘not at all’ and 4 points being given for ‘strongly agree’, with a total score ranging from 22 to 88.

Discussion

In this study, the initial items were developed based on the attributes identified in a prior concept analysis of health literacy in patients with CVD by Sim and Hwang (2019), and the scale was subsequently tested for validity and reliability. The discussion is organized around the four domains finalized through this development process.

The domain of ‘Understanding and searching for health information’ consists of items that assess comprehension of commonly encountered materials in hospital settings, such as examination instructions, consent forms and medication information. These items go beyond simple word recognition and aim to measure the patient's ability to understand and apply health information in real‐life medical contexts. Unlike conventional tools such as the TOFHLA (Parker et al. 1995) and REALM (Davis et al. 1993), which focus on basic reading skills, this instrument improves ecological validity by incorporating items grounded in real clinical scenarios.

The ‘Interacting with healthcare providers’ domain includes four items that evaluate the patient's ability to explain their medical history and current symptoms to providers and to comprehend cardiovascular care plans communicated by healthcare professionals. This is particularly important for CVD patients, as atypical symptom presentations, such as epigastric pain, fatigue or dyspnea, can lead to delayed recognition and impact survival outcomes (AHA 2023). This domain emphasizes dialogical health literacy, assessing the patient's capacity for two‐way communication with healthcare providers. In contrast to tools that focus solely on the comprehension of printed materials, this scale captures a more dynamic aspect of communication within clinical interactions.

The ‘Use of health information support resources’ domain contains six items and evaluates the patient's ability to utilize informal support systems, such as spouses, children or cohabitants, to understand and apply health information. This is especially relevant for older adults or individuals with limited health literacy, who often rely on family members to interpret medical instructions, ask questions on their behalf or reinforce treatment compliance. These findings align with the view that health literacy encompasses not only individual abilities but also the effective mobilization of sociocultural resources (Sim and Hwang 2019). While tools such as the HLS‐EU (Pelikan and Ganahl 2017) address the concept of social support, they do not provide item‐level assessments of how patients engage family or caregivers in decision‐making processes. The present scale addresses this gap by operationalizing sociocultural health literacy as an integral component of CVD patients' overall health literacy.

The ‘Actively selecting and evaluating health information’ domain consists of eight items and assesses whether patients can critically evaluate and select relevant information for managing their cardiovascular health. The items reflect real‐world decision‐making situations, such as interpreting online health content, making medication‐related decisions, and understanding physician guidance. While this domain shares conceptual similarities with the HLS‐EU (Pelikan and Ganahl 2017), which evaluates access, comprehension, appraisal, and application of health information, the present scale is distinct in its disease‐specific focus and context‐driven item construction. In comparison, Matsuoka et al. (2016) developed a health literacy tool specifically for heart failure patients, structured around functional, communicative, and critical literacy domains. While their instrument provides a useful framework for evaluating medication adherence and disease understanding, it does not incorporate aspects related to sociocultural resource utilization or real‐life clinical interaction. In contrast, the current scale reflects the actual lived experiences of CVD patients and includes both individual and contextual factors, allowing for a more multidimensional assessment of health literacy.

This 22‐item scale was developed based on qualitative interviews with patients with CVD and validated through rigorous psychometric testing. The pilot survey showed that the average completion time was 7–10 min, suggesting high feasibility in busy clinical settings. One of the strengths of this scale is its applicability as a health literacy assessment tool for patients with chronic cardiovascular conditions such as arrhythmia and heart failure. Developing a shortened version that retains its reliability and validity may further enhance its utility in clinical practice.

Limitations

As this study was conducted in Korea using a convenience sample of patients with CVD from two tertiary‐care hospitals in Seoul and G Province, caution is necessary when generalizing the results to the broader population of CVD patients in Korea or internationally. Furthermore, as this study took place during the coronavirus pandemic, the selection of medical institutions for participant sampling was restricted, and access to patients in the acute stage of their disease during hospitalization was not feasible. Therefore, future validation studies on the psychometric properties in various institutions and countries are needed.

Conclusion

We developed the Health Literacy Scale for Cardiovascular Disease (HLS‐CVD), a 22‐item tool specifically designed for patients with CVD, by addressing the limitations of existing generic scales and incorporating key concepts such as the use of health information and interaction with healthcare providers. As the aging population increases and the prevention of heart failure progression becomes a growing concern, this scale offers a timely and practical tool to facilitate both descriptive and intervention research on health literacy, an essential factor influencing self‐care behaviour in patients with CVD. In nursing practice, the HLS‐CVD may assist nurses in identifying patients with limited health literacy and tailoring education strategies accordingly. At the policy level, the scale may support the development and evaluation of CVD management programs. In nursing education, integrating health literacy assessment may enhance nurses' competencies in patient‐centred communication. In research, the HLS‐CVD provides a validated tool for intervention studies and comparative analyses. Future studies should evaluate the scale's validity and generalizability across diverse populations, including patients with acute and chronic forms of CVD in different cultural and healthcare contexts.
