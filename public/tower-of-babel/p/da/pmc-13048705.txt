Unresolved questions in atrial septal defect pathophysiology: Shunt dynamics, exercise physiology, and pulmonary vascular disease risk
Authors: Ashish H. Shah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite advancements in the diagnosis and management of atrial septal defect (ASD), key hemodynamic aspects remain poorly understood. This review explores limitations of the pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio (Qp:Qs), the impact of exercise on shunt volume, and predictors of pulmonary arterial hypertension. We highlight that Qp:Qs may not reflect true pulmonary blood flow, particularly during exertion, and that exercise-induced augmentation of shunt flow could predispose to right heart overload. Future studies integrating advanced imaging and hemodynamic measures are essential to refine risk stratification and management in patients with ASD.

INTRODUCTION

Atrial septal defect (ASD), particularly the secundum subtype, is a common congenital heart lesion resulting in a left-to-right interatrial shunt. Transcatheter or surgical closure of such defects has demonstrated right atrial-ventricular remodeling (generally inversely related to the patients’ age),[1] improved exercise capacity, and survival benefit.[23] While the diagnosis and management of ASD have significantly advanced, several physiological and pathophysiological mechanisms remain poorly understood. The clinical decision to pursue defect closure is often guided by the pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio (Qp:Qs), as well as parameters such as systolic pulmonary artery pressure relative to systemic pressure and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR).[4] However, these surrogate markers may not accurately capture the true physiological burden imposed by left-to-right shunting. Furthermore, the interaction between physical activity, shunt volume dynamics, and long-term pulmonary vascular sequelae remains incompletely elucidated. This review highlights key knowledge gaps in ASD pathophysiology, including (1) limitations of Qp:Qs as a hemodynamic metric; (2) effects of exercise on absolute pulmonary arterial flow, shunt fraction, and right heart volume load; and (3) predictors of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) in patients with ASD.

LIMITATIONS OF QP:QS AND THE NEED FOR ABSOLUTE SHUNT VOLUME METRICS

The Qp:Qs ratio, along with comparisons of pulmonary artery pressure and PVR with their systemic counterparts, has long been a fundamental criterion for guiding decisions regarding ASD closure.[4] A ratio ≥1.5:1, a value influenced by the ASD size and compliance of the ventricles, is commonly used to justify the defect as large enough to have hemodynamic consequences and warrant closure in adults.[4] However, this relative index does not capture the absolute pulmonary blood volume traversing the pulmonary vasculature, which may be of greater pathophysiological relevance.

For example, two patients may each have a Qp:Qs of 1.8:1, yet one may have a resting cardiac output of 4 L/min and another of 6 L/min, resulting in vastly different pulmonary blood flow burdens (7.2 L/min vs. 10.8 L/min, respectively). Increased flow augments pulmonary arterial pressure, albeit with a variable response.[5] The lack of integration of absolute flow volumes may underappreciate the cumulative volume overload imposed on the right heart and pulmonary vasculature, especially in a tall, athletic person with hyperdynamic circulation. Moreover, the use of Qp:Qs assumes stable systemic and PVRs and does not account for physiological fluctuations during physical activity or neurohumoral modulation, limiting its utility in dynamic states.

EXERCISE-INDUCED AUGMENTATION IN PULMONARY FLOW AND MODULATION OF QP:QS: UNDERAPPRECIATED OR OVERLOOKED?

Another underexplored area is the change in pulmonary arterial flow and the behavior of left-to-right shunting through ASD during exercise. Normal cardiac output ranges from 5 to 6 l/min, which can increase markedly, especially in athletes, who can augment cardiac output to more than 30 l/min. In patients with normal biventricular circulation, augmentation in pulmonary arterial flow is in par with the change in systemic circulation. Left atrial pressure increases more than right atrial pressure during activity, and it is described to be stiffer in patients with ASD.[6] Furthermore, exercise-induced changes in right and left atrial compliance, PVR-compliance, and systemic arterial pressure could alter trans-atrial pressure gradients, dynamically influencing shunt volume. Such alterations may also be influenced by age, cardiovascular risk factors, and atrioventricular compliance. Based upon these details, one may hypothesize that exercise augments left-to-right shunting, yet quantitative data on this phenomenon are scarce.

The assumption that Qp:Qs remains stable at rest and exercise is largely unvalidated. Few studies evaluating a small number of participants with variable ages have reported a decrease in Qp:Qs during exercise; however, responses in absolute shunt volume have been variable.[789] During the stress test, Bay et al. demonstrated increased absolute shunt volume,[7] Nielsen and Fabricius found decreased shunt volume,[8] and Stephensen et al. (dobutamine and atropine stress) found no change in shunt volume with exercise.[9] Two key points merit emphasis: first, not all studies directly assessed the impact of exercise; some instead relied on surrogate stressors. Second, while these studies reported average trends in physiological responses, the findings were not consistent across all patients. In fact, some individuals exhibited responses that contradicted the overall trajectory, highlighting underlying heterogeneity in this population. Importantly, an altered Qp:Qs ratio during activity does not necessarily imply a proportional increase in absolute shunt volume. In fact, with increasing systemic cardiac output, a stable or even declining Qp:Qs ratio could still signify a net increase in pulmonary flow, underscoring the limitations of the ratio-based approach. Hence, the true impact of exercise on ASD pathophysiology across the age range remains unclear.

Although real-time four-dimensional flow magnetic resonance imaging or invasive exercise hemodynamics may allow for more sophisticated assessment of dynamic shunt behavior, these tools are not routinely used in clinical practice. The increase in pulmonary blood flow during exercise may exacerbate right heart dilation and potentially trigger maladaptive pulmonary vascular remodeling in susceptible individuals. This raises a critical clinical question: Should patients with an unrepaired ASD limit physical activity, particularly endurance sports, to avoid episodic or chronic right heart volume overload? Current guidelines do not provide clear directives, and the long-term consequences of exercise-induced augmentation of shunt flow and its effect on pulmonary arterial remodeling remain speculative.

PREDICTORS OF PULMONARY ARTERIAL HYPERTENSION IN ATRIAL SEPTAL DEFECT: WHO IS AT RISK?

While many patients with unrepaired ASDs tolerate the lesion well for decades, a subset develops PAH either before or even after closure. Predicting which patients will experience this complication remains an area of clinical uncertainty. Eisenmenger syndrome, characterized by reversal of the shunt due to elevated PVR, is well described but fortunately rare in secundum ASDs. More frequently, patients may develop late-onset PAH after ASD closure, sometimes years or decades after the intervention.

Furthermore, the long-held belief that closure of an ASD reduces pulmonary pressures in all patients is overly simplistic. Therefore, preclosure hemodynamic assessment, including vasoreactivity testing and PVR quantification, remains essential, particularly in older adults or those with symptoms suggestive of pulmonary vascular disease. An abnormal rise in pulmonary arterial pressure in response to augmented blood flow (exercise or medication-mediated) either before or even after ASD closure may identify and potentially help in risk-stratifying patients who remain at higher risk of developing PAH.[5] Interestingly, some patients develop PAH despite early closure, suggesting that nonhemodynamic factors such as endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, or in situ thrombosis may contribute to the pathogenesis.

CONCLUSIONS

Despite decades of clinical experience, ASD remains incompletely understood. Although relevant in a small number of patients with ASD, overreliance on the Qp:Qs ratio and underappreciation of dynamic changes in shunt flow during exercise may obscure important risks related to volume overload and pulmonary vascular remodeling. Furthermore, the development of pulmonary arterial hypertension in select patients with or without ASD closure highlights the need for more personalized approaches to risk stratification. As noninvasive imaging and hemodynamic tools continue to evolve, a deeper understanding of ASD pathophysiology will be critical in optimizing management and improving outcomes.
