Accelerating Non-Phase-Change Photochromism and Upconversion Modulation in Double Perovskite Ba2LaNbO6 via Ag+ Doping
Authors: Zan Xu, Chenchen Yang, Rongbao Feng, Xue Bai, Yingzhu Zi, Xinhao Cai, Yueteng Zhang, Wuxi zhang, Weiyu Lin, Junxiao Wu, Lingjia Weng, Yangke Cun, Yue Liu, Jianbei Qiu, Zhiguo Song, Jiayan Liao, Anjun Huang, Zhengwen Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Double perovskites (DPs) are the latest addition to inorganic photochromic materials and show enormous potential with their fascinating optoelectronic properties and lead-free environmental friendliness. However, real-time reversible optical modulation combining the excellent optoelectronic properties of DPs and photochromism has various limitations, including how to achieve photochromism and acceleration of the response rate. Herein, we have developed a type of rare-earth-doped Ba2LaNbO6: Yb3+, Er3+, Ag+ (BLNO) phosphor, which demonstrates a remarkably fast and obvious photochromic response within 1 s. This work accelerates nearly 2 orders of magnitude of response speed of non-phase-change category photochromism in DP oxides over the previous studies. Furthermore, through experimental characterization, we revealed Ag+ effectively increases the two main elements for photochromismdefects and carriers. The mechanism of defect formation energy reduction as well as the creation of new deep traps is also discussed through the theoretical calculation method. Finally, we demonstrate the high-speed dual-mode optical information storage based on BLNO flexible thin film through mask patterning and handwriting.

Introduction

Photochromic materials (PCMs) enable optical modulation by exhibiting at least one new absorption band when stimulated by external electromagnetic waves. , The merits of the inorganic PCM-based modulation lie in its reversibility and real-time nature, which has attracted increasing attention as programmable smart platform. − Double perovskites (DPs) are the newest subdivision to the inorganic photochromic phosphor kit, and have triggered intensive study due to their pronounced optoelectronic properties while avoiding lead toxicity, compared to CsPbX3 series. However, despite the superior thermal stability and wider bandgap compared to DP halides, achieving remarkable photochromism in DP oxides remains an open issue. The most pressing challenge pertains to the inherent photochromism exhibited by pure DP hosts, which manifests as either slight color contrast or invisibility. The underlying cause of this challenge remains to be definitively determined; however, Yang et al. have postulated a paradoxical relationship with the properties of the pure DP structure and the dynamics of the specific category of photochromic mechanism. More specifically, the high defect tolerance (Goldschmidt tolerance factor) and the low carrier effective mass oppose the F-center mechanism of photochromism.

Intriguingly, distinct categories of photochromism also show different applicability due to their different mechanisms. Inorganic PCMs can be divided into two main categories according to their chemical and structural phases before and after color change: phase-change PCMs and non-phase-change PCMs. Phase-transformable PCMs (e.g., KNN, BaTiO3, etc.) − follow the Ising model (a statistical model used to describe phase transitions), exhibit nonlinear optical property curves distinguished by critical point. This makes them suitable for applications like optical switching and binary storage. In contrast, non-phase-change PCMs (e.g., BaMgSiO4, Ba2MgSi2O8, etc.) − have approximately linear optical property curves, making them suitable for precision detection and modulation.

The development of photochromic DP oxides is illustrated in Figure a. In 2023, Yang et. al first reported the photochromism in DP oxide Ba2YTaO6 by leveraging the Co2+ as a colorant. Later, Wang et al. investigated photochromism in La2MgSnO6 employing Fe3+ ions as colorants. In 2024, Lin and Wang et al. reported a fast photochromic response in a ferroelectric twin perovskite Ba8Ti3Nb4O24, achieved by doping Pr3+ as both colorant and luminescent center. This ferroelectric twin perovskite stands out due to its host material falling within the ferroelectric phase-change category, distinguishing it from other DP hosts. More recently, photochromism using X-rays as a source of stimulation has been found in La2MgZrO6 and Ba2LaNbO6 using Pr3+ and Bi3+ as colorants, respectively. , In particular, Ba8Ti3Nb4O24: Pr3+ is endowed with an appreciable color change response speed (0.6 s, 65.2% reflectivity contrast, reflecting color difference per time) due to its unique phase-change initiation properties. However, non-phase-change photochromism has been limited in the rate of coloration in DP oxides. In hosts using 254 nm ultraviolet (UV) as a stimulating light source, Ba2YTaO6: Co2+ required up to 20 min for 18.3% of maximum reflectivity difference, while the faster La2MgSnO6: Fe3+ also required 60 s for reaching the saturation reflectivity contrast value of 23%. The coloration of Ba2YTaO6: Co2+ in DP oxides has been limited by the use of X-rays as a stimulating light source. Hosts using higher energy X-rays as a stimulus source should have been expected to respond faster, yet La2MgZrO6: Pr3+ took up to 30 min to saturate 32% of reflectivity contrast, while the faster Ba2LaNbO6: Bi3+ also took 10 min to achieve 23.5% reflectivity contrast. Furthermore, we have statistically analyzed the change rate of color difference per unit time (ΔOD/s) and the modulation rate of luminescence per unit time based on photochromism (ΔRn/s) of the reported DP oxides, which are presented in Figure b,c, respectively. The research indicates that non-phase-change photochromism in DP oxides currently lacks suitable hosts capable of rapid response and achieving significant color changes, thereby constraining their wider utility.

Herein, we present a strategy using Ag+ ions to enable accelerated photochromism of Ba2LaNbO6: Yb3+, Er3+ (BLNO). BLNO exhibits a remarkable 1-s response white-pink reversible photochromism under alternating 365 nm UV coloration light and 470 nm bleaching light irradiation. The upconversion luminescence (UCL) of the Yb3+-Er3+ luminescent center was modulated in a real-time reversible manner through this photochromism and the UCL modulation ratio reached 47%. Compared to the best reports of photochromic DP oxides of the same type, this result improves the response speed by nearly 2 orders of magnitude (90 times). By leveraging experimental characterizations and theoretical calculations, we discuss the key role of Ag+ as a colorant in deepening the color contrast of photochromism and accelerating photochromic kinetics. Based on flexible films prepared with BLNO phosphors and Poly­(dimethylsiloxane) (PDMS) solvents, we demonstrate optically patterned information storage for mask writing as well as handwritten direct information writing that is more dependent on fast photochromic reaction speed.

Experimental Section and Calculation Method

BLNO Phosphor Synthesis

Pure Ba2LaNbO6 and Ba2LaNbO6: Yb3+, Er3+, Ag+ powders were synthesized via a high-temperature solid-state reaction. BaCO3 (99.95%, Aladdin), La2O3 (99.99%, Aladdin), Nb2O5 (99.9%, Aladdin), Yb2O3 (99.99%, Aladdin), Er2O3 (99.99%, Aladdin), and Ag2O (99.0%, Aladdin) served as raw materials. These materials were weighed stoichiometrically and mixed in an agate mortar. To facilitate sintering, 5 wt % H3BO3 (99%, Sigma-Aldrich) was added as a flux. The mixture, combined with several drops of alcohol, was ground until the alcohol volatilized completely. The resulting powder was transferred to an alumina crucible and sintered in air at 1350 °C for 6 h. Afterward, the samples cooled naturally to room temperature and were regrounded to obtain the final products.

Characterization

The phase of powders was measured by an X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 ADVANCE). The morphology and element distribution of BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+, Ag+ phosphors were observed by a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, ZEISS Gemini SEM 300). The UCL spectra of BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+, Ag+, and BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+, Ag+ phosphors are collected by spectrophotometer (HITACHIU-F-7000) under 980 nm laser excitation. The diffuse reflectance spectra of photochromic powders were measured on a spectrophotometer (U-4100) equipped with an integrating sphere, and BaSO4 was used as a reference sample. The valence states of elements were determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy with the type of PHI5000 Versaprobe-II, using Al as target. The electron paramagnetic resonance spectra were obtained by a paramagnetic resonance spectrometer (Bruker EMXplus-6/1). Hall measurements were determined using an Ecopia Hall effect tester (HMS-7000) at room temperature. The thermoluminescence spectra of BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+, Ag+ powders were measured by a thermal luminescence (TL) meter (FJ-427A1), and the heating rate is 1 °C s–1. For all TL measurements, the samples have undergone uniform irradiation to achieve saturation filling by 365 nm UV light.

Calculation Method

Calculations were carried out using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP). A plane-wave cutoff energy of 520 eV was used, with convergence criteria for self-consistent total energy differences and atomic forces set to 10–6 eV/atom and 0.01 eV/Å, respectively. The ideal lattice structure of the BLNO host was constructed with a 2 × 2 × 1 supercell containing 80 atoms. The band structure and density of states were obtained using the PBE + U method, with the Hubbard U value set to 6.0 eV, applied specifically to the La f-orbitals. The spin–orbit coupling (SOC) was not considered in these calculations. Structural optimizations were performed using a 2 × 2 × 3 Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh.

UCL of Yb–Er combinations is frequently employed as a nondestructive reading method for optical information. , As previously reported, since the B’-lattice position in the BLNO lattice is La belonging to the lanthanide series, Yb3+, Er3+ dopant ions belonging to the same series preferentially occupy the La sites. , The XRD patterns of different concentrations of Yb3+ and Er3+ ions doping are shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information), and the XRD patterns showed that the diffraction peaks of different concentrations of Yb3+ and Er3+ ions doping were all in accordance with the standard card of (JCPDS: 37–0856). To investigate the optimal concentration of Er3+ and Yb3+ doping, a mol % Yb3+ (a = 5,10,15,20,25) and b mol % Er3+ (b = 2, 4, 6, 8, 10) were doped into BLNO, respectively. As the UCL spectra of different concentrations of Yb3+ and Er3+ ions doping shown in Figure S2, we first fixed the doping concentration of Er3+ as 2 mol %, and determined that the optimal value of Yb3+ in the range of 5–25 mol % was 10 mol % doping; Second, we fixed the concentration of Yb3+ as 10 mol % and verified that the optimal value of Er3+ doping concentration in the range of 2–10 mol % was 6 mol %; Finally, the optimal rare earth doping concentration of BLNO was established as BLNO: 10 mol % Yb3+, 6 mol % Er3+ (denoted as BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+).

Ag+ as a colorant in BLNO photochromism plays a role in substantially increasing the chromatic contrast per unit time as well as enhancing the maximum optical density difference (ΔOD), which can be expressed by the following equation: 1 ΔOD=T0−T1T0×100% where T 0 and T 1 represent the reflectances monitored at 564 nm before and after coloration, respectively. This ΔOD is a key metric to visualize the difference in material color contrast before and after photochromism, and is characterized in opaque materials (e.g., polycrystalline powders) using the diffuse reflectance index, and in transparent materials (e.g., single crystals or glass) using the transmittance coefficient. Figure c shows the XRD patterns of BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+, z mol % Ag+ (z = 0,1,2,4,5), and all match well with that of the standard card of BLNO (JCPDS: 37–0856). Notably, it exhibited no discontinuous trend of red-shifting of the XRD diffraction peaks, which indicates that the actual doping lattice of Ag+ may be more complex. The ionic radii of Ba2+ (coordination number, CN = 12), La3+ (CN = 6), and Nb5+ (CN = 6) ions were 1.42, 1.032, and 0.64 Å, respectively, which, according to the Ag+ ions (CN = 6, R = 1.15 Å). From the above information, we can tentatively speculate that Ag+ preferentially occupies the Ba2+ lattice but may also simultaneously displace other elements in the lattice or enter the interstitial lattice. Moreover, in Figure d,e, we depict the electronic band structure of a pure BLNO lattice and the density of projected states accurate to the individual orbitals of the individual elements, calculated on the basis of density-functional theory (DFT). So far, we have established that the optimal rare earth and colorant doping concentrations for BLNO phosphors are BLNO: 10 mol % Yb3+, 6 mol % Er3+, and 3 mol % Ag+ (denoted as BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+, Ag+). The morphology of BLNO: Yb3+/Er3+/Ag+ phosphors was carried out by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) images and the corresponding energy spectrometry (EDS) mapping images, as shown in Figure g. The results showed that the particle diameter distributions were in the range of 1.5–3.5 μm, and the elements were uniformly distributed.

UCL Modulation of Fast Photochromism Based on BLNO: Ag+, Yb3+, Er3+ Phosphor

Photochromism refers to a real-time, reversible color change process triggered by an external light stimulus. The observed color change corresponds to an enhancement of the absorption band: variations in the visible region are directly perceived by the naked eye, whereas those occurring in the nonvisible region are generally categorized as generalized photochromism. , Therefore, when the absorption band coincides with the luminescence band, the luminescence energy will be absorbed by the substrate itself, thus realizing the real-time reversible modulation of luminescence. The UCL modulation ratio (ΔRn) can be given by the following equation: 2 ΔRn=I0−I0I0×100% where I 0 and I 0 represent the UCL intensities monitored at 564 nm before and after coloration, respectively.

It is observed that the Yb–Er system exhibits three main luminescence peaks at 524, 564, and 640 nm in the BLNO host. In Figure S3, we demonstrate the photon numbers of each peak and the corresponding transitions in Yb3+ and Er3+ 4f-4f energy level diagram. The 524, 564, and 640 nm peaks are created by the 2H11/2 → 4I15/2, 4S3/2 → 4I15/2, and 4F9/2 → 4I15/2 transitions with two-photon upconversion. The ΔOD reaches 15.2% (80% maximum limit), and only a small increase can be achieved by extending the irradiation time, suggesting that the phosphor is nearly saturated within 1 s. The corresponding ΔRn of the main UCL peak at 564 nm also reaches 34.1% within 1 s, and further extended irradiation brings it to 47.5%. The Effect of Ag+ Ions on the Photochromic Properties of BLNO Host and BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+ Host

In discussing the role of Ag+ ions in promoting photochromism, it is crucial to differentiate the contributions of various influencing factors. For example, Zheng et al. have reported that codoping with Mn2+ and Nd3+ in Cs2AgInCl6 (CAIC) DP halides enhances photochromic response. Similarly, Lin et al. demonstrated that codoping Mn2+ and Er3+ in CAIC DP halides significantly promotes photochromism. In both DP halide and oxide-based PCMs, multiple doping strategies are often employed, including single doping of rare earth elements, single doping of transition metals, and codoping of REEs and transition metals. Rare earth ions, such as Pr3+ in La2MgZrO6: Pr3+, not only serve as luminescent centers but also act as coloring agents, facilitating photochromism. ,, Transition metals, traditionally used as coloring agents, have also been reported to function as luminescent centers or as inducing factors for self-trapped excitons (STE), with examples including Fe, Ni, and Sb. −

To investigate the roles of Ag+ ions and rare earth elements in the photochromic behavior of BLNO hosts, we performed a series of comparative experiments, as illustrated in Figure . By controlling the doping concentrations, we systematically examined the photochromic properties of pure BLNO and BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+ samples.

First, we conducted saturation photochromic testing on pure BLNO samples, shown in the first diffuse reflectance spectra of Figure a. The results reveal that BLNO matrices without either Ag+ or rare earth ion doping exhibit limited photochromic properties, with a maximum optical density change (ΔOD) of only 4.83%, which is insufficient for practical photochromic applications. Interestingly, upon doping with Ag+ ions at varying concentrations (1 to 5 mol %), we observed a significant optimization in photochromic performance. The photochromic contrast first increased and then decreased as the doping concentration was adjusted, with the optimal concentration identified at 3 mol % Ag+, where a maximum ΔOD of 17.50% was achieved.

Second, we examined the photochromic properties of BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+ hosts with incremental concentrations of Ag+ doping, as shown in Figure b. The diffuse reflectance spectra of these samples reveal new absorption peaks corresponding to the 4f-4f transitions of the rare earth ions Yb3+and Er3+. These transitions include the 4F7/2, 2H11/2, 4F9/2, 4I9/2, and 4I11/2 states of the respective energy levels. As in the undoped host, the saturation photochromic contrast of the rare earth-doped samples also showed an initial increase followed by a decrease with increasing Ag+ concentration. The optimal doping concentration for these samples was also found to be 3 mol % Ag+, with a maximum ΔOD of 19.63%.

By comparing the first diffuse reflectance spectra in Figure a with those in Figure b, we were able to isolate the effects of Ag+ doping on the photochromism. This comparison enabled us to focus solely on the impact of the rare earth ions, Yb3+ and Er3+, on photochromic behavior. Under conditions where no Ag+ ions were present, BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+ exhibited only marginally improved photochromic properties compared to the pure BLNO sample, with a ΔOD of 6.66%. This suggests that while Yb3+ and Er3+ ions provide a slight enhancement to the photochromic contrast, the primary photochromic enhancement is driven by the Ag+ dopants.

To further validate these findings, we present a comparison of the saturation photochromic behavior of BLNO and BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+ samples across different Ag+ doping concentrations in Figure c. The accompanying photographs illustrate the maximum photochromic contrasts achieved at each doping level. The results indicate that while the addition of rare earth elements enhances the photochromic properties slightly, the trend of initial enhancement followed by attenuation with increasing Ag+ concentration remains consistent.

In conclusion, it is clear that Ag+ ions play a dominant role in enhancing the photochromic contrast over time and significantly improving the maximum photochromic response. In contrast, rare earth doping has a modest supplementary effect, with Yb3+ and Er3+ ions only slightly improving the overall performance.

F-Center Photochromism of Ag+ Colorants with Non-Phase-Change Categories

As shown in Figure c, we have identified that 3 mol % Ag+ is the optimal doping concentration. Obviously, Ag+ as a colorant can significantly increase ΔOD to achieve higher ΔRn. However, determining the mechanism of BLNO-based photochromism is a prerequisite for determining the specific mechanism of action of the Ag+ colorant. The mechanisms of photochromism in inorganic phosphors include: (1) defect capture of carriers to form a color center; (2) phase transition or chromophore valence change. The mechanisms of photochromism can be ruled out by first excluding the chemical and ferroelectric phase transition, which has been studied in Figure b. Second, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterization allows us to explore whether there is a valence change before and after photochromism. The high-resolution XPS spectra of BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+ phosphors before and after irradiation by UV lamp are demonstrated in Figure S5, including Ba 3d orbit, La 3d orbit, Nb 3d orbit, Yb/Er 4d orbit. Compared with the original sample, the XPS peaks before and after photochromism did not change in contour or position, indicating that the photochromic behavior of BLNO phosphor was independent of valence change.

At this point, we can already preliminarily deduce that this BLNO-based photochromism should belong to defect-induced color center type. The color centers are divided into various types, in which the color center formed by the capture of positively charged holes by cation defects is the V-center, and the absorption bands it forms are mostly located in the ultraviolet wavelength band; while the color center formed by the capture of negatively charged electrons by anionic vacancies is the F-center, and the absorption bands it forms are mostly located in the visible-near-infrared wavelength band.

In this work, the absorption bands are generated in the blue-green light band of visible light, so the color change is white-red discoloration. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that this photochromic change is due to the formation of F-centers by oxygen vacancy capture carriers. However, whether Ag+ ions would cause intrinsic absorption in pure BLNO and whether they would affect the absorption peak of F-centers require additional DFT calculations for verification. Thus, we perform the DFT-calculated absorption spectra of the perfect lattice and lattice with oxygen vacancy in pure BLNO system in Figure S6; DFT-calculated absorption spectra of the perfect lattice and lattice with oxygen vacancy in BLNO_AgBa system in Figure S7; DFT-calculated absorption spectra of the perfect lattice and lattice with oxygen vacancy in BLNO_AgLa system in Figure S8; and DFT-calculated absorption spectra of the perfect lattice and lattice with oxygen vacancy in BLNO_AgNb system in Figure S9. The results show that the formation of the added absorption bands and the defective bands coincide with the experimentally measured ones. The results also show that the doping of Ag+ ions did not significantly alter the intrinsic absorption in AgBa sites. However, upon the replacement of the AgLa site, which leads to the formation of oxygen vacancies in the vicinity, an overall increase in the absorption coefficient was noted. This phenomenon can potentially be attributed to the fact that Ag+ ions, acting as identical defect centers, also capture electrons and form the Ag0 state, thus giving rise to the observed absorption. The substitution of the AgNb site has a relatively lower precedence. In conclusion, from the perspective of the experimental absorption/reflection spectra before and after the photochromic effect, AgBa remains dominant and does not exert a significant influence on the photochromic effect induced by the F-center. Thus, we can characterize its photochromism as an oxygen vacancy-induced F-center-like photochromism.

Mechanistic Insights into Ag+-Induced Enhancement and Kinetic Acceleration of Photochromism

Photochromism Promoted by Ag+ Colorants with Deep Traps

The discussion of the role of Ag+ colorants around F-center photochromism should begin with a ranking of the factors that promote the photosensitive reaction to be performed. To further substantiate the above conclusion, the Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) signal is shown in Figure a. Among the samples of BLNO:10 mol % Yb3+, 6 mol % Er3+, and z mol % Ag+ (z = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5), it can be observed that the 3 mol % sample exhibits the strongest signal characteristics, which is in line with the experimentally observed phenomenon that samples are the darkest in color at this concentration. In addition, the center of the signal peak corresponds to a g-value of 2.03, which is consistent with the signal characteristics of unpaired electrons captured by oxygen defects in the BLNO crystal environment. The link between defects and photochromism is further revealed by thermoluminescence spectroscopy (TL), as shown in Figure b. Obviously, the overall peak position of the TL of the BLNO:10 mol % Yb3+, 6 mol % Er3+, and z mol % Ag+ (z = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5) samples was significantly shifted to higher temperatures. These results reveal the significant role of Ag+ colorants in deepening the trap depth. Interestingly, as the gradient of Ag+ concentration increases, it can be observed that the trend of the TL curves can be summarized in two trends: (1) the peak center maintains a consistent tendency to move to higher temperatures (deeper traps) and tends to saturate after reaching 3 mol %; (2) the integral area and the maximum height of the TL increase and then decrease with the increase of Ag+ concentration. However, there is another questionable point, i.e., why the intensity of the TL curve decreases instead after the Ag+ concentration is elevated above a certain threshold. How Ag+ Promotes F-Center Formation and Acceleration of High Color Contrast Photochromism

The change in trap depth alone is far from sufficient to illustrate the specific role of Ag+ as a colorant. As the subject of the F-center is composed of two elements, the positively charged anionic (oxygen) vacancies on the one hand, and the negatively charged electrons used to fill its defective centers on the other hand.

We tested the spectra of the oxygen element O 1s of BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+ and BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+, Ag+ separately by XPS (Figure a,b). The O 1s peak was fitted into two peaks, lattice oxygen and vacancy oxygen, and the proportion of defective oxygen (integral area ratio) increased from 20.89 to 27.45% with Ag+ doping, while the proportion of lattice oxygen decreased from 79.11 to 72.55%. This indicates that the doping of the colorant Ag+ successfully introduced more oxygen vacancies, which is one of the prerequisites for the formation of F-center.

The information about the effect of Ag+ colorants on the nature of defects and the electron space population can be further explored by using DFT methods. In Figure S10, the band structure and density of states (DOS) diagram of the defect bands formed by a single oxygen vacancy in the pure BLNO lattice are demonstrated. From the DOS on the right, we can confirm that the electronic density of states is contributed by the P-orbital of the oxygen atom. Furthermore, we calculated the electronic properties of different Ag-occupied site scenarios in Figure S11. The specific distribution of oxygen vacancy defect band structure diagrams in the corresponding modified lattice for the three occupied lattice scenarios of AgBa, AgLa, and AgNb, respectively (Figure S11a–c). Obviously, no matter which Ag lattice position, its results show a significant effect of deepening the trap. This confirms that the filling priority of the shallow trap is higher than that of the deep trap, and the electron escape of the deep trap is slower than that of the shallow trap, which also reflects the role of the deep trap in coloration. More specifically, (1) the addition of Ag+ colorant deepens the trap depth (expanding the defect capacity within the appropriate concentration) and also increases the chromatic contrast of photochromism at the same time; (2) for the colored samples, increasing the temperature allows the trapped electrons in the color core to thermally escape, and the higher the temperature, the more complete the bleaching; (3) the shallow traps have a low electron thermal escape temperature, and the deep traps have a high electron thermal escape temperature. Based on the above three points, the practical relationship between deepening the trap depth of Ag+ colorants and deepening the contrast of the photochromic color difference of Ag+ colorants is revealed. It should be noted that this discovery shares the same uneven distribution of traps in many multianion-based multicolor inorganic PCMs, yet it clearly exhibits only a single optical absorption peak. − We speculate that this is related to the single anion and high symmetry of BLNO. Additionally, the TL trap analysis in this work provides some reference for the yet-to-be-concluded multicolor photochromic mechanism of composite anions.

Correlation Analysis between Ag+ Doping Concentration and Photochromic Properties

In our initial observations, as shown in Figures f and , we noted a characteristic phenomenon: both the BLNO host and the BLNO doped with Yb3+ and Er3+ exhibited a correlation between photochromism and Ag+ concentration, which initially increased and then decreased. Specifically, at lower Ag+ concentrations (0–3 mol %), the photochromic contrast (ΔOD) exhibited a positive correlation with the Ag+ concentration, peaking at 3 mol %. However, at higher Ag+ concentrations (3–5 mol %), a negative correlation between ΔOD and Ag+ concentration was observed. A similar trend was also evident in the EPR spectra (Figure a) and thermoluminescence peaks (Figure b).

To explain this nonlinear trend, several factors must be considered, including the defect chemistry induced by Ag incorporation, the formation energy of oxygen vacancies, and the migration of Ag+ ions within the DP structure. The interplay of these factors ultimately dictates the optimal doping concentration for achieving the maximum photochromic response.

At low Ag+ concentrations, substitution of BLNO lattice ions by Ag+ promotes the generation of oxygen vacancies for charge compensation. In Figure c, we present the calculated defect formation energies and their corresponding priorities for various lattice sites. When Ag+ substitutes for Ba2+ or La3+, local charge imbalances spontaneously trigger charge-balancing mechanisms. Based on the computed oxygen vacancy formation energy (Figure c), we can reasonably deduce that the increase in oxygen vacancies is primarily driven by this mechanism. These oxygen vacancies are key contributors to F-center-type photochromism. Under 365 nm illumination, the trapped charge carriers in the oxygen vacancies induce changes in the absorption band, resulting in a shift in color. Thus, as the Ag+ doping concentration increases from 1 to 3 mol %, the observed increase in ΔOD directly reflects the growing number of isolated oxygen vacancies generated through this charge compensation process.

However, once the Ag+ concentration exceeds 3 mol %, this beneficial process reaches saturation, and the system exhibits a reversal in behavior. At this point, excess Ag+ no longer contributes to an increase in vacancy formation but instead begins to suppress the creation of photochemically active vacancies. This suppression can be attributed to two factors: defect association and the reduced thermodynamic driving force for vacancy formation at higher doping levels. At high concentrations, Ag+ ions tend to form neutral complexes with nearby oxygen vacancies, such as Ag_Ba’-V O•. These complexes effectively isolate the vacancies in an optically inert form, thereby diminishing their contribution to the photochromic effect. Furthermore, as the doping level increases, the likelihood of Ag+ ions occupying interstitial sites rises, introducing alternative compensation mechanisms that do not require the formation of oxygen vacancies. The cumulative result is a decrease in the density of isolated, optically active vacancies, which macroscopically manifests as a reduction in ΔOD above the 3 mol % threshold.

Thermodynamic analysis of defect formation further supports this interpretation. The chemical potential for oxygen vacancy formation increases gradually with Ag+ concentration. At low doping levels, the energy cost for vacancy formation is relatively low, as the lattice can easily accommodate the creation of vacancies to compensate for charge substitution. However, as the Ag+ content increases, the incremental energy required for additional vacancy formation becomes larger, reflecting both the saturation of easily replaceable sites and the increased electrostatic cost of introducing more vacancies into an already defect-rich environment. This increase in chemical potential effectively raises the energy barrier for oxygen vacancy formation, thereby suppressing the emergence of new photochemically active centers at higher Ag+ concentrations. Therefore, the peak in ΔOD observed at 3 mol % corresponds to the point where vacancy formation remains energetically favorable, but further increases in doping concentration shift the system toward the formation of defect associations and compensation pathways that no longer contribute to optical contrast.

In addition to these thermodynamic considerations, the inherent mobility of Ag+ ions plays a significant role in shaping the defective landscape. Ag+ ions are known to be highly mobile within double-perovskite lattices, a property extensively reported in studies. − This mobility facilitates the redistribution of Ag ions upon incorporation. At moderate doping levels, this mobility can stabilize the lattice by allowing Ag+ ions to occupy energetically favorable substitutional sites, thus promoting vacancy formation through effective charge compensation. However, when the doping concentration exceeds the optimal range, the same mobility causes Ag+ ions to aggregate, leading to the formation of defect complexes. Migrating Ag+ ions readily combine with existing oxygen vacancies to form charge-neutral defect pairs, which contribute minimally to the photochromic effect. Furthermore, at high concentrations, Ag+ ions tend to migrate to interstitial sites, further reducing their role in the photochromic process.

The phenomenon of TL spectra intensity decreasing with increasing Ag concentration in Figure b also supports this conclusion from another perspective. Thermoluminescence (TL) is a widely used characterization technique in which charge carriers, previously trapped at defects in the material, are released upon heating. These carriers subsequently recombine in a nonradiative transition manner and give rise to measurable optical emission. The TL signal thus provides two main pieces of information: the depth of the trap, which can be inferred from the peak position using eq , and the relative trap capacity which can be inferred from the integral area ratio. In previous chapters, we have already provided the band structure and DOS of the BLNO host with oxygen vacancies, as well as those of BLNO doped with Ag at various sites (Figures S10 and S11). From these results, it is clear that Ag doping deepens the trap level compared with the pristine host. As we discussed earlier, Ag also promotes the formation of oxygen vacancies by charge compensation, thereby enhancing the trap concentration. However, when the Ag content exceeds 3 mol %, the TL capacity starts to decrease. This phenomenon can be understood in two ways: at high Ag mol % levels, the formation of oxygen vacancies is suppressed, leading to a reduced TL peak intensity; in addition, Ag located at interstitial sites acts as a competing electron capture center. In this case, the recombination of carriers is more likely to proceed via radiative transition channels, rather than through nonradiative transition TL emission, which explains the disappearance of the TL peak at 5 mol % Ag. The BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+, Ag+ phosphors were mixed with the PDMS gel and dried to obtain a flexible film with a length and width of 5 cm and a thickness of only 1.5 mm, as shown in Figure a. The film has good foldability and flexibility, as shown in the top right of Figure a, it can be folded like paper, reflecting its advantages in flexible applications. Figure b schematically shows the BLNO flexible film in the process of writing, reading, and erasing optical information. Similar to the rest of the photochromic oxides, the film can be custom-patterned with a sheet light source and a mask (Figure c), and can be used for multiple functions such as anticounterfeiting and optical information storage. , Here, we have written “TSUK” using binary ASCII code, which can be read out nondestructively in both bright-field and dark-field compatible environments. Unlike other non-phase-changing photochromic DP oxides, BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+, Ag+ enables handwritten information input based on a point light source. It should be emphasized that this relies on its near 2 orders of magnitude or even more photochromic response speed. It is worth noting that heating can also quickly bleach the pattern, and heating at 200 °C for about 20 s can fully restore it to its original state. The handwritten information input guarantees “characteristic signature” and “customized content customization” based on the user’s habit, which is not available in mask patterning, and has high information encryption qualities. These applications broaden the field of optical information based on inorganic PCMs and give new highlights not available in previous research by using their fast photochromism.

Conclusions

We report a fast photochromic effect in a double perovskite (DP) Ba2LaNbO6:10 mol % Yb3+, 6 mol % Er3+, 3 mol % Ag+ (BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+, Ag+) phosphor. Upon exposure to 365 nm ultraviolet light for 1 s, the BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+, Ag+ powder exhibited significant photochromic color change (approaching saturated color difference contrast) and effective optical modulation. It accelerated the coloration rate of non-phase-change photochromism in DPs by nearly 2 orders of magnitude. In addition, we verified that this material can be bleached back to its original state under 470 nm light and has good reversibility and cycle fatigue resistance. Through experimental characterization and theoretical calculations, the key factor in accelerating photochromism is attributed to the Ag+ ions as colorants, where the pure BLNO host without Ag+ colorants exhibits negligible photochromism. More specifically, Ag+ deepens the color of photochromism (color difference contrast) and promotes the occurrence of photochromic reactions simultaneously, resulting in rapid and obvious photochromism. The results show that the deep-color photochromism in this work comes from the F color center formed by deep traps. And an appropriate concentration of Ag+ can simultaneously increase the overall trap depth and the capacity of the deep trap. Furthermore, due to the doping of Ag+ colorants, the phosphor also exhibits a significant increase in oxygen vacancy content (+31.4%) and carrier concentration (+35.5%). These two factors are precisely the key conditions for the formation of F-centers (oxygen vacancies capturing carriers), thus verifying the photochromic acceleration effect of Ag+. The range of Ag+ doping concentration exceeding 3 mol % will suppress the photochromic performance due to Ag entering interstitial sites and migration. Finally, we prepared flexible films of BLNO: Yb3+, Er3+, Ag+ using PDMS gel and demonstrated optical information storage applications based on mask patterning and handwritten pattern input requiring rapid photoconversion. This work contributes a positive case study to the exploration of the photoconversion kinetic mechanisms of DP inorganic PCMs and provides a new strategy for ultrafast photoconversion.
