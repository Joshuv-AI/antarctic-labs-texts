Choosing Virtual Versus In‐Person Mental Health Crisis Care: A Theoretical Framework for the Decision‐Making Process From the Patient's Perspective
Authors: Angela Buchel, Jocelyne Lemoine, Danielle Carignan Svenne, James M Bolton, Jennifer M Hensel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDecisions around receiving hospital‐level care are complex; the addition of a virtual option introduces additional considerations.AimTo develop a theoretical framework for the decision‐making process regarding in‐person or virtual‐based crisis stabilisation support.MethodWeb‐based surveys emailed to all individuals admitted to in‐person and virtual Crisis Stabilisation Units between March 2022 and May 2023. Purposive sampling identified interview candidates based on use of the virtual service. A constructivist grounded theory approach started with analysis of the interviews and triangulated with survey data from in‐person service users.ResultsSurveys (N = 76 in‐person users) and interviews (N = 21 virtual users) were analysed. The decision‐making framework highlighted clinical and safety risk, technological, environmental and systems factors within the clinical recommendation. The recommendation was either congruent or incongruent with the patient's desires leading to agreement or compromise through negotiation. Many patients viewed the virtual service as a good first step in care, while some continued to fear forced confinement.DiscussionMany patients are reluctant to accept in‐person crisis stabilisation, and the virtual option provides an acceptable alternative. Clinical recommendations should consider patient, environmental, and technological factors.Implications for PracticeClinicians must be willing to consider when virtual care may be an acceptable patient‐centred option.Keywords: crisis, emergency, health services, inpatient, mental health, patient‐centred, virtual care

Introduction

Virtual care has now become an integral part of healthcare delivery; catalysed by the necessity for alternative modes of contact during the COVID‐19 pandemic [1]. Virtual care has broadly been defined as the use of synchronous and asynchronous communication or information technologies, including video, telephone, text, and email, for the purpose of patient care [2]. Pre‐pandemic, virtual mental health care was already rising for consultation, psychotherapy, and medication management [3]; with a significant increase occurring alongside eased regulatory constraints in virtual service provision and limited alternatives, leading mental health professionals to adapt delivery of care to a variety of virtual mediums [4]. This resulted in a number of novel adaptations of mental health services to ensure continuity of care which aided in the growing awareness of the benefits of virtual care for both the patient and provider, such as increased accessibility and reduced transportation barriers and cost [5, 6, 7, 8], as well as fewer missed or cancelled appointments [8, 9, 10]. With the resolution of pandemic‐related restrictions, and resumption of usual care practices, there is a need to build evidence to support the continued delivery of virtual mental health care to capitalise on these benefits.

In contrast to elective outpatient care, the use of virtual alternatives for the assessment and management of acute mental health crisis intervention has received less attention. Some novel virtual adaptations of acute mental health care delivery have included comprehensive virtual crisis assessment and counselling, virtual partial hospitalisation programs (PHP), and virtual wards or day programs as an alternative to inpatient admission [10, 11, 12, 13]. Studies that have investigated programs with both in‐person and virtual options have found that both result in symptom reduction and increased quality of life [14] and are equally effective and satisfactory in comparison to usual care across a range of patient populations [12, 13, 14].

While there is some initial evidence supporting the efficacy and acceptability of remote care during a mental health crisis, there are some potential challenges that equate to it not being the right option for every individual, particularly as a comprehensive intervention for acute mental health crisis stabilisation. A major concern is the potential difficulty in managing high acuity mental health crises virtually, specifically the challenge of maintaining patient safety in cases where patients are presenting with suicidal ideation [12, 15]. There have also been concerns discussed regarding the potential for a diminished therapeutic relationship [3, 5, 6, 9, 16], concerns over privacy and security [3, 5], technological difficulties [16], and accessibility issues which may exacerbate existing mental health disparities due to a lack of necessary high‐speed internet [3, 6, 17] and housing instability [3, 5, 18]. While initial work supports that virtual wards are feasible for patients experiencing acute mental health crises [12, 13, 19], the research is still quite limited with little exploration of the dynamic factors that lead to an individual being referred to virtual vs. in‐person services. Research examining whether individuals in mental health crisis are offered hospital admission or not, in the absence of a virtual alternative, indicates that this decision is highly variable and subject to clinical factors (patient's clinical history), threat/fear factors (risk of suicide or violence), interpersonal dynamics between the client and physician, and contextual factors (inpatient service capacity) [20]. When a virtual alternative is added as a disposition option, this may introduce additional considerations. Comparative research on patients with heart failure identified a range of themes concerning the factors that lead to an inpatient versus virtual treatment plan, including the perceived usefulness of the technology offered in the virtual program, the availability of clinical/technical support, and the opinion of other individuals important to the patient [21].

The current mixed methods research aims to examine the experiences of patients who utilised a virtual form of a crisis unit (virtual Crisis stabilisation Unit; vCSU) offered in Manitoba, Canada, specifically focused on the decision to utilise virtual versus facility‐based crisis care. The crisis unit is a 16‐bed voluntary facility that functions as an alternative to hospital admission for individuals experiencing lower acuity mental health and addiction crises. The virtual unit was launched in April 2020 during COVID‐19 as a means to continue access to intensive crisis stabilisation support in community in response to reduction of in‐person beds due to physical distancing requirements [19]. The virtual crisis unit is a 6‐‘bed’ unit providing access to the same multidisciplinary team and core interventions that those admitted to the in‐person unit receive (e.g., individual therapeutic sessions, medication reminders and review, group therapy sessions), however the care is delivered virtually by a combination of phone, video‐conferencing (Zoom or Microsoft Teams), email, and text. As a result of high demand, the programme sustained after re‐opening of the physical beds, and obtained government funding to continue as its own entity. Understanding the factors that affect an individual's decision to pursue virtual crisis stabilisation will provide important information for program improvement, as well as design of similar programs and system planning as it relates to virtual crisis care delivery.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This concurrent mixed methods study utilised surveys and individual interviews for data collection and an integrated data analysis in keeping with a constructivist grounded theory approach [22]. All individuals accessing the in‐person or virtual crisis unit between March 2022 and May 2023 were invited to complete a web‐based survey. Interview candidates were purposively sampled from survey respondents who had utilised the virtual service, inclusive of those completing the in‐person CSU survey who indicated a prior experience with the virtual program (see Figure 1 for schematic of data collection and analysis).

Schematic of data collection and analysis procedures.

The study was based in Manitoba, a province in Canada. Winnipeg is the largest city in Manitoba, home to nearly half of the province's population. Patients presenting to any of the emergency departments or urgent care centres, or the 24/7 walk‐in mental health Crisis Response Centre may be referred to either the in‐person or virtual crisis unit if they are voluntary and presenting with a subacute mental health crisis not requiring psychiatric hospitalisation. The referral decision is based on a Mental Health Assessment (MHA) conducted by a mental health professional who assesses the patient's current crisis and needs. Individuals who are deemed at imminent high risk of harm to themselves or others, experiencing more severe symptoms, or individuals who are not medically stable are not referred to the crisis unit and are instead directed towards inpatient hospital resources [19]. Referral criteria for the virtual unit are the same, with an additional requirement that the individual needs to complete suitable safety planning to return to the community and have a telephone or internet‐enabled device on which the virtual team can make contact. The target length of stay for both units is between 3 and 5 days with rapid connections made to post‐crisis follow‐up services when applicable.

Research Ethics

All participants gave their informed consent before participating in the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the University of Manitoba's Health Research Ethics Board (HREB), and the protocol was approved by the HREB (HS25298(H2021:435)).

Data Collection

Surveys

A web‐based quality improvement (QI) survey was created in collaboration with leadership for the program to collect feedback on overall experience of care. A separate optional research survey link was embedded in the QI survey. The QI survey link was distributed by email to all clients who had utilised either the inpatient crisis unit or the virtual unit between March 2022 and May 2023 using the REDCap platform. As well, a poster containing the QR code for the survey was posted at the in‐person crisis unit facility, and the link was separately included on virtual patients' discharge communications (via email). Upon completion of the QI survey, individuals were directed to continue to the research study if willing. The message included mention of an incentive for completing the research study (entry into a monthly draw for a $50 electronic gift card). Consenting individuals were informed that their QI survey responses would be linked with their research survey responses for use in data analysis.

Two different surveys were developed: one for the virtual unit and one for the in‐person unit. Both QI surveys collected demographic and service utilisation information including: age, gender, ethnicity, and prior crisis service use. Additional quality indicators were included such as satisfaction ratings and open‐ended feedback. The in‐person unit research survey contained 2 questions that assessed (1) the client's awareness of the virtual unit as an option and their willingness to access it in the future, and (2) the factors that would lead them to consider it or not. The virtual unit research survey collected additional data on the experience of virtual care which is not reported in this manuscript.

Interviews

Individuals who had completed the virtual unit survey and those who completed the in‐person unit survey and indicated having utilised the virtual unit in the past, and who were willing to be considered for an interview, were contacted by a research team member to discuss the interview portion of the study and obtain informed consent. As data collection and analysis progressed, additional interview candidates were purposively identified with clinicians who provided additional encouragement and information about the study and offered to connect them to a research team member to support survey completion. Interview participants were offered a $25 electronic gift card to compensate their time and contribution. Interviews were conducted virtually over Zoom or telephone by a member of the research team (JL), and participants had the option of interviewing with a support person. The semi‐structured interview guide consisted of questions which explored patients' personal experiences of receiving care for mental health crises virtually and, if a support person was present at the interview, the personal experiences of providing support while the treatment occurred virtually.

All interviews were audio recorded using Zoom or, in the case of telephone interviews, a hand‐held digital voice recorder. The audio recordings were stored securely on an encrypted USB and assigned a unique study ID. The deidentified audio files were submitted to a professional transcription service through a secure portal. Once the transcripts were verified by the research team, the audio files were deleted and the coded, de‐identified transcripts were uploaded to Nvivo 12 for data analysis.

Data Analysis

Respondent characteristics and quantitative survey responses were analysed descriptively. Interview data and open‐ended survey questions were analysed thematically. The overall approach to analysis followed Constructivist Grounded Theory [22]. This approach emphasises that reality is subjective and varies between individuals, including researchers who interpret the data [22]. In general, grounded theory approaches are appropriate for process‐related research questions in which participants describe experiences that change over time or that have stages and phases [23].

Through early analysis of the interview data, the theme related to choosing the virtual stay emerged and was subsequently explored more deeply through theoretical sampling [22] of interview participants (e.g., those who had experienced both forms of care), as well as triangulation [24] with open ended survey responses obtained from the in‐person unit users. Because the latter responses were often quite brief, a qualitative content analysis was conducted [25], moving from inductive to deductive in comparison to the themes identified from analysis of the interview transcripts. Analysis of the interview transcripts was inductive and iterative using a constant comparative method, and concurrent with ongoing data collection. Analysis began with open coding processes [26] which consists of reviewing qualitative data line by line and splitting the data into segments based on meaning (i.e., codes). This is followed by selective coding processes for theory‐generation which consists of comparing and re‐organising codes which “cluster” together into themes (i.e., categories) [26]. Constructive Grounded Theory results in a dominant theme referred to as the ‘core category’ which encompasses other themes referred to as ‘sub‐categories’ [27]. After the transcription of early interviews, the 2 interview coders met on 3 occasions to compare preliminary themes after independently coding the same 5 interviews. Preliminary themes and their definitions were discussed and debated until intercoder agreement could be reached. The authors divided the remainder of transcripts and continued having regular meetings to discuss emergent themes and their relation to each other using concept maps [28]. After coding the first 10 transcripts, theoretical sampling [22] was used to identify eligible patients with characteristics which might further develop the emerging themes and theory.

At several meetings towards the end of the analysis phase, when the framework was taking shape, the themes generated from the interviews were compared to those generated from open‐ended survey responses for triangulation purposes to increase validity and to further inform the development of the theoretical framework [24]. Finally, a theoretical framework of the decision‐making process for in‐person or virtual mental health crisis care services was developed from the concept maps. Three team members were involved in data analysis (AB, JL, and JH). AB and JL completed coding of survey responses; JL and JH completed coding of interview data. All three authors met together to discuss the integration of all data and the development of the overall framework.

Reflexivity Statement

The research team included individuals with backgrounds in mixed methods research as well as clinical service providers (medical doctors and trainees). The researchers conducting the analysis engaged in memo writing throughout the data interpretation process to document their subjectivity (i.e., thoughts, assumptions, beliefs, and decisions). This reflexivity practice provides a record with which the evaluation of knowledge co‐construction is possible [29]. Project collaborators bringing broader expertise in crisis care and research methods were frequently consulted to review the findings and collect additional perspectives on findings from the data.

Results

A total of 691 survey invitations were sent to individuals discharged from the in‐person unit. From these, 109 surveys were completed, of which 76 (69.7%) included responses to the research portion of the survey. A total of 345 survey invitations were sent to individuals discharged from the virtual unit. Fifty‐eight surveys were completed, of which 28 (48.3%) included responses to the research portion of the survey. A total of 26 individuals (17 from virtual unit survey respondents and 9 from in‐person unit survey respondents) were contacted about the interview portion of the study and a total of 21 agreed (15 from virtual unit survey respondents and 6 from in‐person unit survey respondents). One interview participant attended with a caregiver.

Both groups had high rates of prior crisis service use, including prior in‐person unit admissions (44.7% of in‐person crisis unit survey respondents and 33.3% of interview participants, respectively), and prior psychiatric hospital admissions (40.8% and 38.1%, respectively). Average age between the groups was comparable at 36.9 years for in‐person crisis unit survey respondents and 38.2 years for interview participants. In both groups, the majority of participants identified as female and of white ethnicity.

Of the patients who responded to the in‐person unit survey, only 28.9% were previously aware of the availability of the virtual unit. When asked what they would do in the future if they presented with another acute mental health crisis, 76.3% indicated they would use in‐person crisis unit services, 2.6% indicated they would use virtual services, and 21.1% indicated they were unsure. The themes and sub‐themes identified from the in‐person crisis unit survey question regarding why the individual would consider the virtual in the future or not are summarised in the Table S1. A total of 63 (out of 76) respondents provided answers to this question. The most prominent themes that arose in these responses included virtual care being insufficient for patient needs, clinical and safety risks, environmental factors, preferred care setting, a desire for an in‐person relationship with mental health clinicians, and concerns over the virtual modalities used. Among the 2 respondents who indicated they would utilise virtual services in the future, themes illustrated various positives about virtual care, as well as a belief that the virtual option offers a sufficient amount of care. For those who were unsure, themes included concerns about the crisis unit program in general, concerns about the virtual service, unpleasant prior experiences with in‐person services, and most commonly, an indication that the decision would be dependent on situational factors.

Core Category: Recommendation to vCSU, CSU, or Option Given

The core category of the framework is labelled Recommendation. This theme refers to the clinician's recommendation (as perceived by the patient), or the clinician presented options to the patient and the patient was given a choice about the referral outcome. Of the 21 participants interviewed for the study, 3 (14.3%) reported the clinician suggested the virtual unit, 4 (19.0%) reported the clinician suggested the in‐person unit, 8 (38.1%) reported the clinician told them about the available options and were given the choice of the virtual or in‐person unit, and for 6 (28.6%) it was unclear which option the clinician recommended.

Sub‐Categories: Environmental, Technological, Systems, and Clinical or Safety Risk

The factors influencing the recommendation are grouped into sub‐categories (or sub‐themes) which are labelled Environmental, Technological, Systems, and Clinical or Safety Risk.

Each of these sub‐categories is further grouped into codes. For example, the sub‐category Environmental is further grouped into two codes labelled Inadequate Support in the Community or Caregiver Burnout and Negative Influences in the Community. The sub‐category Clinical or Safety Risk is further grouped into three codes labelled Hospital Not Recommended, Lack of Self‐Awareness or Self‐Management Skills, and Severity. The sub‐category Systems is further grouped into codes labelled Availability of Beds and COVID‐19 Protocols. These sub‐categories and codes are defined in Table 2. Sub‐Categories: Agreement and Compromise

The core category, Recommendation, leads to the sub‐categories labelled Agreement and Compromise. These sub‐categories refer to the decision to admit the patient to the vCSU which is based on weighing clinician and patient considerations. In the situation of Agreement, the referral is congruent with the patient's desired outcome for service. Compromise refers to the situation where the referral is incongruent with the patient's desired outcome for service. As the framework illustrates, the decision to be referred for care in the virtual unit was a complex interplay of factors. Ultimately, the virtual option was in line with expectations for some, and for others it was either less or more than they had in mind when presenting for crisis support. A negotiation often occurred when the clinician felt that the individual's needs required a different level of intervention than the individual wanted; in most cases discussed by the interview participants, this occurred when the clinician recommended in‐person care and the individual did not wish to pursue this, both agreeing that the virtual support is a good first step.

Discussion

This study investigated the different factors that led to receipt of virtual services versus in‐person services for crisis stabilisation. The decision to receive virtual vs. in‐person care for intensive crisis support resulted from various influences arising from the clinical scenario, experience of the crisis assessment, environmental factors and supports, comfort with virtual care and personal preferences. Often this was not straightforward and involved negotiation and compromise between the clinician and patient. This is the first study to examine this process with respect to a decision to accept in‐person versus virtual acute mental crisis care. Some of the same contextual factors identified in a study focusing on the decision to admit an individual in a mental health crisis to hospital or not [20], were identified in this study. Additionally, factors that were identified in a study evaluating uptake of a virtual program to manage congestive heart failure were also found to be relevant in our study, including the perceived usefulness of the service and the availability and ease of the necessary technology [21]. What is unique in this study is the introduction of an additional option for care for those whom hospital is considered less ideal due to lower acuity of need and/or personal desires. Previous studies from other sectors of medicine have shown that if a patient is inappropriately discharged, it could lead to clinical deterioration, a delayed admission, or even death [30]. Inappropriately admitting too many patients to inpatient medical wards increases the risk of in‐hospital adverse events and worsens system strain [30]. Admission to a psychiatric unit is associated with stigma and may lead to an increased risk of suicidal ideation if the patient does not want inpatient admission [31].

We found that while the addition of a virtual option is viewed as positive, the decision‐making process regarding admission to an in‐person versus a virtual care modality is complex and several often conflicting factors must be considered. The decision must balance the patient's wishes with the clinician's judgement about the appropriateness of either service as well as clinician efforts to reduce burden on inpatient units. For appropriate candidates, the virtual service can act as a compromise between the clinician and the patient, whereby the patient is able to avoid an inpatient admission and stigmatisation is reduced, while the clinician can ensure that the patient is still receiving some form of follow‐up care and monitoring.

What is clear from this study is that virtual crisis stabilisation care is not a full replacement for in‐person care, but rather meets the needs of a certain patient population that does not always require in‐person services and can receive the same or better care delivered to them in the physical environment of their choosing. There are many situational and personal factors that make virtual care suboptimal for a client, and in these situations, in‐person care is still the most appropriate course of action [32]. In particular, patients who are experiencing crisis as a result of environmental factors may require time away from those triggers (e.g., substance use, volatile relationships) [32], in order for the crisis to stabilise. Self‐management is an important consideration in recommending virtual care, not only in terms of the resulting emphasis on self‐management in one's natural environment but also the need for an individual to have some capacity to self‐manage and be a reliable participant in their care for them to be good virtual candidate. Several individuals interviewed for this study remarked that they were interested in the virtual option but found it difficult due to their lack of self‐awareness and ability to self‐manage. Self‐management and self‐efficacy have been previously shown to be associated with better recovery outcomes [33], highlighting the need to build these skills during and between crises. Access to the technology required to receive virtual care is an important consideration that is modifiable by increased infrastructure that makes reliable internet available in all areas, and provision of devices to those who need them. Even with universal access to technology, virtual will not be the preference of those who have concerns about the viability of virtual interactions [32], concerns over privacy and confidentiality [3, 5], or lack of comfort interacting over a virtual medium. These factors may be modifiable with education and technical support, but it is unlikely that virtual will be fully accepted by all. Offering virtual options can reduce the burden on inpatient resources and make those options more accessible to those who need and prefer them [34].

Implications for Clinical Practice

As virtual care becomes more mainstream and services expand offerings to add alternatives and reduce rising demands on inpatient facilities, the decision over whether or not a patient is referred to virtual or inpatient services will need to be made. The current research provides a framework for that decision‐making, illustrating factors that are more likely to result in a successful virtual admission.

An implication of this research is that clinicians, who have traditionally delivered care to patients experiencing mental health crises in inpatient settings, may need to increase their tolerance for home‐based care. While many clinicians recommended the traditional in‐person care, they made compromises to refer to virtual when the individual was hesitant about the in‐person milieu. While most mental health clinicians are generally supportive of virtual modalities for delivering care [5, 16], the concept of delivering acute crisis care virtually is still novel, and many clinicians may be reluctant to adopt it into their practice. While clinicians may be more comfortable delivering care in‐person, the benefits of virtual care for patients (increased accessibility, reduced time and cost, and satisfaction with the service) illustrate that virtual care is a viable option in these situations and something that can greatly enhance person‐centred care delivery.

Implications for Service Users

This research has significant implications for service users because the results highlighted the fact that the decision‐making process is a shared one that involves collaboration and compromise between the healthcare practitioner and the patient. When options are available, patients should be involved in the process of deciding where they should be admitted, whether it be virtual or in‐person.

Additionally, increased usage of virtual care modalities for the delivery of acute mental health crisis care has implications for individuals who struggle with receiving care in inpatient facilities due to increased exposure to harmful hospital triggers or due to negative effects on their behaviour and wellbeing. By allowing patients who do not do well with inpatient care to receive their care virtually, there will be a larger willingness to seek treatment, and thus more patient needs can be met.

Limitations

There are several limitations of the study. Surveys were voluntary, although respondents were representative of the typical population accessing these services [19, 35]. Surveys were web‐based, and although hard copies were made available, no responses were submitted this way, and this may represent a sampling bias against people who did not have an email address. We used data from individuals who accessed in‐person crisis unit services to triangulate our framework; however, many of them were unfamiliar with the virtual alternative, and we did not specifically identify input from individuals who may have been offered the virtual format and declined. Although the results were informally discussed with clinical team members, we did not explicitly incorporate the perspectives of the clinicians as it relates to the decision‐making process. This study focuses on the in‐person unit and a virtual alternative, which are subacute services, supporting individuals who do not require inpatient psychiatric admission. Further work could investigate the decision‐making process along this continuum of care. Lastly, the virtual unit is situated within unique contextual factors (i.e., health service organisation in Canada) and may not be generalisable to other jurisdictions that do not have an established virtual care infrastructure. That said, this article presents concepts that are transferable to subacute mental health virtual care generally such as the factors and patient considerations that influence the appropriateness for a recommendation to receive virtual care.

Conclusions

Virtual crisis care is an acceptable alternative for many requiring intensive support for crisis stabilisation and creates options for those who do not wish to pursue facility‐based care. Virtual care options will likely continue to advance with health systems exploring person‐centred and cost‐effective solutions such that a good understanding of which patients are appropriate will be vital. This research demonstrates how the decision to use virtual versus in‐person crisis care services is a complex and multifactorial one. Importantly, the decision encompasses negotiation and compromise, and the awareness that varying situational factors will make either in‐person care or virtual care more appropriate for each individual patient. These findings suggest that infrastructure to support the delivery of high‐quality care can virtually increase access and optimise human healthcare resources. Additionally, they imply that virtual crisis care services are best delivered alongside seamless integration with usual facility‐based care so that care can be best matched to individual needs and clinical teams are supported in caring remotely for individuals who may pose elevated risk and ultimately require more intensive intervention. Future research should further investigate virtual care outcomes in comparison to and along a spectrum with usual in‐person care.
