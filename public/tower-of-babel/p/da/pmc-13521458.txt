Hemopexin Purification From Human Cohn Fraction IV Paste and Its Biophysical Characterization and Functional Evaluation in Sickle Cell Disease Mice
Authors: Shuwei Lu, Saini Setua, Mohd A. Khan, Aaradhya Bansal, Quintin O'Boyle, Pedro Cabrales, David C. Irwin, Paul W. Buehler, Andre F. Palmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHemopexin (Hpx) is an acute phase plasma protein that is responsible for sequestration and removal of cell‐free heme with very high affinity (Kd < 1 pM). Hpx expression in liver cells is induced following an inflammatory event such as severe hemolysis. Therefore, plasma Hpx has potential clinical relevance due to its' ability to bind free heme, thus reducing lipid peroxidation and activation of inflammatory pathways in genetic anemias, such as sickle cell disease (SCD). This provides the rationale for purifying Hpx at high purity and at large scale. Starting from human Cohn fraction IV, we purified a protein cocktail using tangential flow filtration for use in this study as the starting material for purification of Hpx. Hpx was purified from the protein cocktail using immobilized metal ion affinity chromatography. SDS‐PAGE and densitometric analysis showed that Hpx was purified to homogeneity and was approximately 99% pure. To gain further insight into Hpx activity and its' ligand binding properties, we employed comparative biophysical techniques such as UV‐visible, circular dichroism, electron paramagnetic resonance and stopped flow spectroscopy. Finally we evaluated Hpx in a mouse model of SCD to test the ability of the novel material to attenuate tissue iron accumulation.

Introduction

Hemopexin (Hpx) is an acute phase plasma protein (~60 kDa), whose primary function is the binding and removal of cell‐free heme in an equimolar ratio (1:1 Hpx:heme) (MULLER‐EBERHARD et al. 1968; Davies et al. 1979). Hpx is produced in the liver and consists of a single polypeptide chain with 439 amino acid residues (Altruda et al. 1985). Hpx binds heme with the highest affinity of any protein (Kd < 1 pM) to form a low spin bis‐histidyl heme complex inside the heme pocket surrounded by 8 hydrophobic residues (Baker et al. 1999; Morgan et al. 1976; Hrkal et al. 1974). Once heme is bound to the heme pocket of Hpx, removal of heme from the plasma is mediated by liver macrophages through receptor‐mediated endocytosis of the Hpx‐heme complex (holo‐Hpx) to the LDL receptor‐related protein 1 (LRP1), which facilitates recycling of Hpx (Hvidberg et al. 2005). Binding and clearance of heme attenuates responses such as lipid peroxidation, and activation of inflammatory pathways (Vincent et al. 1988; Yalamanoglu et al. 2018; Ofori‐Acquah et al. 2020). Hpx is present in plasma at a concentration of 0.4 – 1.5 mg/mL (MULLER‐EBERHARD et al. 1968; Weeke and Krasilnikoff 1972; Hanstein 1968). Under clinical conditions characterized by hemolysis such as sickle cell disease (SCD), heme is first sequestered by human serum albumin (HSA) in the plasma before binding to Hpx (Morgan et al. 1976). Consistently high heme stress causes a reduction in the plasma levels of Hpx, which associates with severity of SCD patients (Yalamanoglu et al. 2018; Balla et al. 1993).

Previously reported Hpx purification methods that start with a basis of plasma or plasma fractions include heme‐agarose affinity chromatography, (Takahashi et al. 1985a; Olsen 1980; Štrop et al. 1981) lectin chromatography, (Hrkal and Rejnková 1982; Vretblad and Hjorth 1977) anion exchange chromatography (Vretblad and Hjorth 1977) and utilizing various forms of chemical treatment such as ammonium sulfate and rivanol precipitation (Heide et al. 1964). In addition to these purification techniques, purification based on the strong interactions between Hpx and metal ions is the newest and perhaps the most cost effective and efficient approach for removing contaminants (Mauk et al. 2005). Metal affinity chromatography together with size exclusion chromatography is often used in tandem to purify Hpx (Mauk et al. 2005; Karnaukhova et al. 2021).

The goal of this study is to develop an efficient single step nickel (Ni2+) affinity chromatography purification process to obtain highly pure Hpx from a protein cocktail obtained from tangential flow filtration (TFF) purification of human Cohn fraction IV. Purified Hpx will be assessed for its' purity, structural and functional integrity by various analytical techniques.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Sodium phosphate monobasic anhydrous (NaH2PO4), sodium phosphate dibasic anhydrous (Na2HPO4), sodium chloride (NaCl), nickel sulfate heptahydrate (NiSO4 ∙ 7H2O), and imidazole (C3H2N4) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO), and 0.2 µm polyethersulfone syringe filters were purchased from ThermoFisher (Waltham, MA). Human Cohn fraction IV paste was purchased from Seraplex (Pasadena, CA). Protein purifications were performed using prepacked columns purchased from Cytiva (Marlborough, MA) on an NGC Quest 100 chromatography system (BioRad, Hercules, CA).

Hpx Purification

Human Cohn fraction IV (FIV) paste was suspended in 3.75 mM phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at 4°C, and fractionated using tangential flow filtration as previously described by our group to yield a protein cocktail with proteins bracketed between 50 and 100 kDa in molecular weight (MW) (Pires et al. 2021). The protein cocktail was used as the starting material for subsequent purification of Hpx using Ni affinity chromatography on a 20 mL HisPrep FF prepacked column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA). The binding and elution buffers used were 20 mM phosphate buffer (PB) with 250 mM NaCl pH 7.40 (buffer A), and 20 mM PB with 250 mM NaCl and 100 mM imidazole pH 7.4 (buffer B) respectively. The protein cocktail was first diluted to a concentration of 20 mg/mL using buffer A and filtered through A 0.2 µm dead end filter prior to column loading at 0.5 mL/min. Post column loading of the protein cocktail, the column was washed with 5% buffer B for 5 column volumes (CVs) at 5 mL/min. Protein elution was performed with increasing amounts of buffer B in isocratic steps at 25% for 10 CVs and 50% for 10 CVs at 5 mL/min. Columns were regenerated after each run by first chelating the Ni2+ ions with 5 CVs of 50 mM EDTA and finally loaded with 3 CVs of 100 mM NiSO4 heptahydrate. Five CVs of deionized (DI) water was used to wash the column between each regeneration step and post Ni2+ loading. Fractions collected with high Hpx purity were pooled and buffer exchanged to 50 mM PB pH 7.4, using 10 kDa Amicon ultracentrifuge filters (Millipore Sigma, St. Louis, MO) and stored at −20°C until further use.

SDS‐PAGE Analysis

Pre‐cast 4%–20% Novex tris‐glycine mini protein gels (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) were used with the Novex mini gel tank (Waltham, MA) for SDS‐PAGE analysis. Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) running buffer (10×) was purchased from Invitrogen (Waltham, MA). Samples were diluted to 1 mg/mL before addition of SDS in a 1:1 ratio and heated at 85°C for 10 min. 20 µL of the sample were then loaded onto the gels and ran at 220 V for 40 min. The gel was stained with Coomassie blue (Thermofisher, Waltham, MA) for 40 min and destained overnight using a destaining solution (60% deionized water, 30% methanol, 10% acetic acid). Densitometric analysis was performed with GelQuantNet software provided by biochemlabsolutions. com to determine protein purity.

Total Protein Concentration

The total protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay (Bradford 1976).

MALDI‐TOF Mass Spectrometry

Samples were diluted to 1 mg/mL on a protein basis in 50 mM phosphate buffer (PB) (pH 7.4). A saturated solution of sinapic acid was prepared for use as the matrix by adding 10 mg of sinapic acid to 1 mL of 50% v/v acetonitrile with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. The sample was prepared by mixing protein with the matrix in a 1:1 ratio, and 1 µL of sample was loaded onto a matrix assisted laser desorption (MALDI) plate. The MALDI plate was then analyzed on a Bruker Microflex MALDI time of flight (MALDI‐TOF) mass spectrometry system (Bruker, Billerica, MA). The data was analyzed using the Bruker Flex Analysis software (Bruker, Billerica, MA).

Circular Dichroism Spectrometry

The circular dichroism (CD) spectra in the far ultraviolet region between 190 and 260 nm in a 0.1 cm path length quartz cuvette was measured on purified Hpx samples and Hpx samples treated with heme using a JASCO J‐815 CD spectrometer (Jasco, Easton, Maryland). Samples were diluted to 9 µM (protein basis) in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) (pH 7.4). Heme solutions were prepared with 5 mg hemin chloride (Milipore Sigma, Burlington, MA) in 1 mL 100% DMSO. Concentrations were measured spectrophotometrically using an extinction coefficient of 120,000 M−1 s−1 at 280 nm (Seery et al. 1972). Samples treated with heme were diluted to 9 µM (protein basis) along with 9 µM heme. Baseline subtraction was performed using PBS, and the protein CD spectra consisted of an average of three scans. Data analysis was performed using Spectra ManagerTM (Jasco, Easton, Maryland).

Thermal stability was analyzed by monitoring the change in molar ellipticity at 231 nm. Purified apo‐Hpx and holo‐Hpx samples were subjected to heating at a ramp of 2°C/min in the temperature range between 20°C and 90°C in a 0.1 cm path length quartz cuvette. Heme was prepared as described previously in DMSO. Samples were diluted to 9 μM (protein basis), with 9 μM heme added. Data analysis was performed using Spectra ManagerTM.

Heme Titration

Titration of Hpx with heme was measured spectrophotometrically using a plate reader at 414 nm. Samples were prepared at a 9 µM protein basis. Heme was prepared in DMSO as described above and concentrations were determined spectroscopically. Ten ligand (L): protein (P) ratios between 0.1 L/P to 2.0 L/P were measured in triplicate and the data was regressed to a linear fit in R Studio (Posit, Boston, MA) to determine Hpx saturation with heme.

Stopped Flow Kinetics

The kinetics of apo‐Hpx binding to free heme was performed using a SX‐20 stopped flow spectrophotometer (Applied Photophysics, Leatherhead, U.K.). 5 mL of hemin chloride (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was solubilized in 1 ml 0.1 M NaOH and the concentration was determined using UV‐visible spectrometry with an extinction coefficient of 120,000 M−1cm−1 (Seery et al. 1972). Purified apo‐Hpx samples were diluted to 5 µM, with heme concentrations of 10, 20, 30, and 40 µM in 2 mM Tris buffer (pH 7.4). Stopped flow readings were measured in absorbance mode at 414 nm at room temperature. All experiments were performed in triplicate and the data was regressed to a double exponential in R Studio (Posit, Boston, MA).

Hydrogen Peroxide Mediated Oxidation

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) mediated oxidation of holo‐Hpx was monitored in a photodiode array spectrophotometer following procedures outlined in the literature (Karnaukhova et al. 2021). Briefly, a sample was prepared with 19 μM heme:holo‐Hpx at a L/P molar ratio of 0.8 to yield eightfold molar excess (H2O2) over the Hpx‐heme complex and a reference sample with free heme (19 μM) in PBS with eightfold molar excess H2O2. The absorbance change at 414 nm versus time was monitored immediately after addition of H2O2 for 10 min with measurements every 30 s at room temperature in a 10 mm pathlength quartz cuvette.

Electron Paramagnetic Resonance Spectroscopy

Hpx samples at a concentration of 200 μM were incubated with heme at a 1:0.9 molar ratio at room temperature in 50 mM PB while shaking for 1 h prior to experiments. Four millimeter medium wall Suprasil EPR sample tubes 250 mm L (Wilmad Life Sciences, Vineland, NJ) were used for loading the samples. EPR measurements were performed at 10 K using an X‐Band (9.37 GHz) EMXPlus (Bruker, Billerica, MA). Temperature control was maintained using a MercuryITC (Oxford Instruments, Oxfordshire, U.K.). The following settings were used for sample analysis: microwave power 0.2 mW, power attenuation 30 dB, modulation amplitude 10 G, modulation frequency 100 kHz. The sample was measured at center 3300 G with a sweep width of 2800 G. Thirty measurements were averaged to reduce noise in the spectra. Data analysis was performed using Xenon (Bruker, Billerica, MA) and EasySpin Documentation on the EasySpin.org website.

Plasma Concentration of Hemopexin and Tissue Iron Distribution in Sickle Cell Disease Mice

Experimental Design: To test if Hpx obtained from the novel purification method could limit tissue distribution of iron originating from heme, Berk homozygous SCD (Berk‐SS) mice were dosed subcutaneous with 300 mg/kg/day of Hpx for 5 weeks. This dose was chosen based on the demonstration of therapeutic effectiveness of Hpx treatment on cardiopulmonary function in Berk‐SS mice (Buehler et al. 2021; Lucero et al. 2025).

Ethical Approval and Animal Care: Berk‐SS mice (10‐12 weeks old; ∼20–25 G) were obtained from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). Mice were housed and bred in an AAALAC accredited animal facility at the University of Maryland Baltimore Medical School. Female heterozygous Berk‐SS mice were bred with male homozygous Berk‐SS mice to generate homozygous offspring. Specifically, Berk‐SS mice with genotype Tg (Hu‐miniLCR α1 Gγ Aγ δ βs) Hba0/0 Hbb0/0 and the hemizygous with genotype Tg(Hu‐miniLCR α1 Gγ Aγ δ βs) Hba0/0 Hbb0 Hbb+ were littermates. All experimental procedures were conducted under the guidelines required by the National Institutes of Health. The use of animals in the experiments was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Maryland Baltimore.

Hemopexin Quantification: Human Hpx levels in Berk‐SS plasma were measured by using a commercial ELISA kit. The following human Hpx kit was used (ab108859, abcam, Waltham, MA). All assays were performed by following the manufacturer's recommendations with additional plasma dilution made as required with the kit provided dilution buffer.

Perls Iron Staining: Tissue sections were dewaxed‐hydrated, incubated (45 min., RT) in 0.3 M HCl (ACROS Organics, Geel, Belgium) and 2.5% (w/v) potassium ferrocyanide trihydrate (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA), and washed with distilled water. Sections were then incubated (30 min., RT) in methanol containing 0.3% H2O2 (LabChem, Zelienople, PA, USA) and 0.01 M NaN3 (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Sections were washed in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and incubated with 3,3’‐diaminobenzidine and H2O2 (SIGMAFAST ™, Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 3 min. at RT. Sections were then counterstained in hematoxylin (Gil no. 2, Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A DM4 B microscopy system was used for image analysis at 4×, 20× and 63× magnification (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany). All tissue images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Results and Discussion

NI‐NTA Affinity Chromatography Purification of Hpx and SDS‐PAGE Analysis

The protein cocktail containing proteins bracketed between (50 – 100 kDa) that was purified from Cohn Fraction IV via TFF as described in the literature was used as the basis for Hpx purification in this study (Pires et al. 2021). Preliminary studies were performed using immobilized metal ion affinity chromatography (IMAC) with 1 mL HiTrap columns, saturated with different metal ions (Ni2+, Zn2+ and Cu2+) (data not shown). Purification using immobilized Ni2+ showed promising results for separation of Hpx, which was corroborated by previous studies in the literature (Mauk et al. 2005). For initial column screening studies, the column volume was scaled up to 5 mL HiTrap columns before ultimately using 20 mL HisPrep columns.

A single step Hpx purification profile using immobilized metal ion (Ni2+) affinity chromatography is shown in Figure 1A. The protein cocktail was diluted 10×, and loaded onto the Ni‐NTA column, and unbound proteins, primarily HSA, are observed in the flow through (Figure 1B, lane 3). A column wash with 5% buffer B removed any unbound contaminants (Figure 1B, lane 4). Additional isocratic steps at increasing % B removed various contaminants until only Hpx eluted at 50% B at peak 3 (Figure 1A), as shown in the SDS‐PAGE (Figure 1B, lanes 7‐12). Gel densitometry was performed on the purified Hpx fractions, which yielded a purity of ~99%. Based on SDS‐PAGE analysis and given that Hpx and HSA have similar apparent MW, the purified Hpx MW was measured by MALDI‐TOF spectrometry.

MALDI‐TOF Analysis

Mass spectral analysis was performed to determine the protein species present in the fractions. Both the protein cocktail and the purified Hpx were subject to MALDI‐TOF analysis (Figure 2). It is important to note that the intensity of a certain protein relative to another does not indicate its relative concentration in a sample, as each protein has a unique degree of ionization (Hortin and Remaley 2006). MALDI‐TOF analysis shows that the protein cocktail is primarily made up of HSA at 66.5 kDa and transferrin (Tf) at ~79 kDa with a negligible reading corresponding to the MW of Hpx (~60 kDa) (Figure 2A). This is in agreement with what we have previously observed from prior analysis of the protein cocktail and is consistent in all of the stock material used for Hpx purification (Pires et al. 2021; Muller‐Eberhard 1988).

MALDI‐TOF analysis of the protein cocktail and purified Hpx. A: A representative MALDI‐TOF spectra of the protein cocktail which was used as the starting material for the purification of Hpx. The protein cocktail consists primarily of HSA and Tf. B: A representative MALDI‐TOF spectra of the purified Hpx product with a MW of ~60 kDa.

In the purified Hpx samples, a major intensity peak at 59,800 m/z was observed, with a minor intensity peak at ~89,000 m/z, which is potentially a low MW or monomeric form of haptoglobin, Hp 1‐1 (α1β)2 (Figure 2B) (Pimenova et al. 2009). This confirms that peak 3 shown in Figure 1B corresponds to Hpx with a MW of ~60 kDa, and does not have any impurities consisting of HSA at 66.5 kDa or Tf at ~79 kDa. The relatively broad peak of Hpx is due to the fact that Hpx is highly glycosylated, and a range of MW values from 57 kDa to 63 kDa have been reported in the literature (Muller‐Eberhard 1988; Takahashi et al. 1985b). Our values for the mass of Hpx is very similar to previously reported values of Hpx at ~60 kDa (Muller‐Eberhard 1988).

Circular Dichroism Spectroscopy

To determine if the purification process had negative effects on the secondary structure of the purified Hpx, the far‐UV CD spectra of apo‐Hpx and holo‐Hpx was measured (Greenfield 2007). Our findings match previously reported data for apo‐Hpx which exhibited a negative minima at 206 nm and a positive maxima at 231 nm (Figure 3A) and suggests that the purified Hpx has an α‐helical content of ~7% and β‐sheet content of ~57.3% (Karnaukhova et al. 2021).

CD spectroscopy. A: Far UV CD spectra of purified Hpx. The CD spectra was collected for both apo‐Hpx and holo‐Hpx at a protein basis of 9 µM combined with equimolar heme. B: Temperature induced protein unfolding.

Holo‐Hpx has a similar CD spectra compared to apo‐Hpx with a slight change in intensity at 206 nm and 231 nm. This is likely due to conformational changes in the protein upon binding to heme. It has previously been reported in the literature that this conformational change has physiological importance in the binding of holo‐Hpx to the membrane receptor protein LRP1 (Smith et al. 1988). It is difficult to determine if this conformational shift in the secondary structure is reflected in the far UV CD spectra, as even a slight change in the very negative trough at 206 nm would cause the 231 nm peak to shift (BERNARD et al. 1980). However, it is very likely that the change in ellipticity at 231 nm is caused by the tryptophan residues on domain II of Hpx during heme binding (Wu and Morgan 1993).

Temperature induced unfolding of the purified Hpx was measured at 231 nm from 20°C to 90°C with holo‐Hpx at a ligand/protein molar ratio (L/P) of 1.0. For purified Hpx, Tm = 62 ± 0.3, and for holo‐Hpx, Tm = 73 ± 0.5 (Figure 3B). The increase in melting temperature is expected, as heme is known to stabilize apo‐Hpx, and our values are corroborated by the literature (Rosell et al. 2007).

Heme Binding Kinetics

The kinetics of heme (in 0.1 M NaOH) binding to Hpx was measured using the stopped flow spectroscopy. Heme was combined with apo‐Hpx in increasing concentrations from 10 to 40 µM and the change in absorbance at 414 nm was measured (Figure 4A). The plots were fit to a double exponential to obtain the kapparent values for both the fast and the slow rates. The heme to Hpx binding rate constant was determined by Pasternack et. al., with heme solubilized in 40% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in DI water to be a single rate constant of 1.8 × 106 M−1s−1 (Pasternack et al. 1983). From these kinetics studies, we found that a double exponential regression fit the data significant better than a single exponential fit. Therefore, the kapparent values were assigned to the fast step and the slow step in the reaction and were plotted against the concentration in Figure 4B to obtain kFast = 4.4 × 105 M−1s−1 and kSlow = 5.6 × 104 M−1s−1. Our rate constants are slightly lower than what Pasternack et. al., obtained, and this can be attributed to the difference in solutions used to solubilize the heme. DMSO may be a better alternative to NaOH to keep heme in a monomeric form, whereas NaOH may have more dimer heme molecules, thus slowing heme binding to Hpx.

B: Plot of kapparent versus heme concentration. C: Hpx binding to heme measured at 414 nm with increasing heme:Hpx molar ratio (L/P). D: Ferric heme oxidation in the presence of H2O2.

Hpx Activity

The activity of purified Hpx was determined via heme binding to apo‐Hpx in increasing ligand/protein (L/P) molar ratios in Figure 4C. Titration of heme to Hpx showed a significant break in the slope at a L/P ~ 1.0, which corresponds to equimolar binding of heme by Hpx, as previously reported in the literature (Heide et al. 1964). This suggests that the majority of the protein in the purified sample correspond to apo‐Hpx.

Hydrogen Peroxide Mediated Oxidation

The effect of H2O2 mediated oxidation on holo‐Hpx and free heme shown is shown in Figure 4D. In the case of holo‐Hpx, the change in the absorbance of the Soret peak at 414 nm was significantly reduced compared to free heme in PBS, and is similar to previous observations in the literature (Karnaukhova et al. 2021). This indicates that free heme is easily oxidized, while the heme in the heme‐Hpx complex remains stable and is not as readily oxidized by excess chemical oxidant (H2O2). Additionally, this observation is corroborated with heme‐Hpx coordination chemistry, where heme binds to the Hpx heme binding pocket in a bis‐histidyl coordination, where such hexa coordinated species with low spin chemistry is inert and not accessible for H2O2 binding.

Electron Paramagnetic Resonance Spectroscopy

The EPR spectra of Hpx with heme at a ~L/p = 0.9 molar ratio was measured to determine the coordination of heme within the Hpx heme binding pocket (Figure 5). Hpx was combined in excess with heme to prevent any unintended binding of heme to the protein. The observed signal was significantly lower compared to the adventitious or otherwise “junk” iron observed at ~1100 G (g = 5.87) (Cox et al. 1995). This is expected since high spin iron at S = 3/2 is known to have a more intense signal. The 10 K measurement temperature along with an attenuation of 30 dB was chosen to maximize the signal to noise ratio of the protein coordination to the low spin iron of heme as well as to obtain readings below the saturation point.

EPR spectrum of holo‐Hpx. Purified apo‐Hpx at a protein basis of 200 µM was incubated with a L/p = 0.9 ratio of heme.

The g tensor is used to determine the coordination of Hpx to heme. The following g values were observed, g = 2.89, g = 2.27, and g = 1.55, and is characteristic of low spin iron of S = ½ in heme bound to Hpx (Cox et al. 1995; Aisen et al. 1974). Additionally, these g values suggest that the low spin iron in heme is coordinated by 2 histidine residues in the heme pocket of Hpx (Aisen et al. 1974; Peisach and Blumberg 1974).

Plasma Human Hemopexin Levels

Human plasma Hpx reached a steady state concentration at 3 weeks after the initiation of subcutaneous dosing with 300 mg/kg/day. One week post dosing, the plasma concentrations increased from zero to a mean 0.88 ± 0.092 mg/mL and then to a mean of 1.5 mg/mL at three and 5 weeks of dosing (Figure 6). The maximum concentration of 1.5 mg/mL was consistent with upper end normal values in healthy human plasma (Lucero et al. 2025) and exceeded Hpx levels in humans with SCD (MULLER‐EBERHARD et al. 1968). Therefore, short term dosing (~3 weeks) was sufficient to maintain levels of Hpx and provide a constant bioavailability of Hpx during steady state disease.

Tissue Heme/Iron Distribution

SCD is not an iron overload anemia, intrinsically. However, patients are in a state of chronic hemolysis and heme exposure, and this can be modeled in SCD mice (Gbotosho et al. 2021; Bains et al. 2010; Belcher et al. 2014). Here the in vivo effectiveness of heme binding by purified Hpx was tested by quantifying tissue iron distribution to the primary clearance organs (kidneys, spleen, and liver). Renal iron accumulation in non‐transfused SCD mice occurs as a function of persistent intravascular hemolysis and heme exposure. In the present study, subcutaneous treatment with 300 mg/kg/day of Hpx significantly decreased renal tubule hemosiderin by 16% and 27% after 3 and 5 weeks of treatment, respectively (Figure 7). Spleen iron accumulation in the red pulp macrophages of SCD mice occurs as a function of persistent extravascular hemolysis. In the present study subcutaneous treatment with 300 mg/kg/day of Hpx significantly decreased splenic iron by 17% and 27% after 3 and 5 weeks of treatment, respectively (Figure 8). This data was identical to reductions in renal iron, suggesting that both intra and extravascular hemolytic states were attenuated by Hpx treatment.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a single step process for purifying Hpx to high purity from a protein cocktail solution using Ni‐NTA immobilized metal affinity chromatography. The purified protein was characterized to have a MW of 60 kDa and a purity of ~99%. Proper protein folding was determined by CD and matched the literature. Protein activity was assessed using titration with heme, which was observed to saturate at a ligand/protein molar ratio (L/P) ~1.0 and with proper heme coordination. In a SCD mouse model, 5 weeks of daily subcutaneous human Hpx treatment generated steady state levels of plasma Hpx like healthy humans. Further, Hpx treatment significantly decreased both kidney, splenic red pulp parenchymal and macrophage iron deposition, suggesting a reduction in both intra and extravascular hemolysis. We suggest that this novel method of Hpx purification will increase the efficiency of Hpx purification and retain heme binding effectiveness.
