Ferroptosis as a therapeutic vulnerability in ARID1A-deficient bladder cancer
Authors: Mengxue Zhao, Xuefeng Wang, Xianbin Duan, Chunyue Wu, Rui Guo, Yan Wen, Jiaxin Liang, Jie Zhou, Xuefen Lei, Shi Fu, Haifeng Wang, Cheng Peng, Chunming Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractARID1A is among the most frequently mutated genes in bladder cancer, yet no targeted therapy exists for this molecular subset. Here, we identify ferroptosis induction as a synthetic lethal strategy in ARID1A-deficient bladder cancer. We found that ARID1A loss impaired the NRF2-SLC7A11-glutathione (GSH) axis, leading to redox imbalance and profound sensitivity to the ferroptosis inducer RSL-3. In vitro, ARID1A knockdown depleted intracellular GSH and enhanced RSL-3-induced lipid peroxidation and cell death. Mechanistically, ARID1A directly regulated NRF2 transcription and its target SLC7A11, and restoring SLC7A11 or NRF2 activity or GSH levels rescued ferroptosis sensitivity. In vivo, RSL-3 treatment delayed tumor progression, reduced muscle invasion, and improved survival in two types of Arid1a -deficient mouse models. Furthermore, patient-derived organoids from ARID1A-low human tumors exhibited heightened RSL-3 sensitivity rather than ARID1A-high tumor. Our findings establish ferroptosis as a targetable vulnerability in ARID1A-deficient bladder cancer and provide a compelling rationale for clinical translation of ferroptosis-based therapies in this molecularly defined population. Subject terms: Targeted therapies, Bladder, Cancer epigenetics

Introduction

Bladder cancer is the fourth most commonly diagnosed malignancy in men worldwide1–3. Most cases are classified as urothelial carcinoma, with approximately 70% presenting as non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC; stages Ta–T1) and 30% as muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC; stage ≥T2)3–6. Histologically, bladder neoplasia is categorized into carcinoma in situ (CIS), lamina propria-invasive bladder cancer (LIBC), and muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC)7–9. Genomic studies have identified ARID1A as one of the most frequently mutated genes in bladder cancer, with a significantly higher mutation rate in MIBC than in NMIBC10,11. ARID1A encodes a core subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex and is widely regarded as a tumor suppressor12–14. Mutations in ARID1A are present in approximately one-quarter of all bladder cancer patients, many of whom lack effective therapeutic options10,15,16.

Tumor cells often exhibit loss or epigenetic silencing of tumor suppressor genes, a phenomenon that can extend to neighboring genes and create potential therapeutic vulnerabilities17,18. This principle has been successfully exploited in metastatic uveal melanoma through synthetic lethal targeting of the CDS1-CDS2 gene pair19. In other cancer types, ARID1A deficiency has been associated with sensitivity to USP8-FGFR-STAT3 axis inhibition and metabolic reprogramming20. In bladder cancer, previous studies have identified vulnerabilities to PI3K and EZH2 inhibition in ARID1A-mutant contexts21. More recently, Krt5 lineage-specific deletion of Arid1a was shown to promote translation elongation and accelerate tumorigenesis in mice; while translation inhibition selectively inhibited the growth of Arid1a-deficient cells and ARID1A-low human tumors22. However, the identification of additional or more potent vulnerabilities remains an open question in urological oncology.

Although ARID1A-deficient cancer cells have been reported to exhibit sensitization to pyroptosis23, this relationship has not been established in bladder cancer. Beyond pyroptosis, ferroptosis is a recently characterized form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxide accumulation and it has emerged as a promising therapeutic avenue24,25. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) utilizes glutathione (GSH) to detoxify lipid peroxides under physiological conditions, thereby preventing ferroptosis26. GSH, a tripeptide antioxidant, is synthesized from cysteine, glutamate, and glycine, with cysteine availability serving as the rate-limiting step27,28. Cystine is imported through the cystine/glutamate transporter system Xc⁻, assembled by the catalytic subunit SLC7A11 and its chaperone partner SLC3A229,30. Small-molecule compounds such as RSL-3 and erastin induce ferroptosis by inhibiting GPX4 and system Xc⁻, respectively, and these two molecules have demonstrated significant tumor restriction efficacies across multiple cancer types, even in drug-resistant models24,31. However, whether ARID1A deficiency confers sensitivity to ferroptosis in bladder cancer remains unknown.

Beyond the GPX4-SLC7A11 axis, glutathione S-transferase (GST) family enzymes also contribute to glutathione homeostasis32–34. Gamma-glutamyl transferase 1 (GGT1), for instance, is a membrane-bound enzyme that hydrolyzes extracellular GSH, facilitating amino acid recycling and improving intracellular GSH synthesis35. GSTs catalyze the conjugation of electrophilic substrates to GSH, enhancing their solubility and excretion, thereby contributing to cellular detoxification32,36. However, the relationship between the regulation of glutathione homeostasis and ARID1A-mediated tumor sensitization remains largely unexplored.

In this study, we demonstrated that ARID1A-deficient bladder cancer exhibited a synthetic lethal vulnerability to ferroptosis resulting from impaired glutathione homeostasis. We further show that the ferroptosis inducer RSL-3 effectively suppresses ARID1A/Arid1a-deficient bladder tumor growth, supporting that RSL-3 has potential as a synthetic lethal therapy for this molecularly defined subset of bladder cancer.

Results

ARID1A-deficient bladder cancer cells enhanced sensitivity to ferroptosis

To investigate precision therapy approaches for ARID1A-deficient bladder cancer, we generated urothelium-specific Arid1a-knockout mice (Arid1a cKOUpk3a) and induced bladder carcinogenesis using N-butyl-N-(4-hydroxybutyl)-nitrosamine (BBN) for 5 months (Fig. S1A). Transcriptomic analysis of bladder tissues revealed significant enrichment of glutathione metabolism pathways in MUT vs heterozygous (Het), MUT vs WT, and Het vs WT comparisons (Fig. S1B), suggesting altered glutathione (GSH) homeostasis in Arid1a-deficient bladder cancer.

Within the glutathione metabolism signature37, we identified significant downregulation of Ggt1, Gstm1, Gstm2, Gstm6, Gstm7, Gstt1, Gstt2, and Gpx3 transcripts (Fig. S1C). qPCR validation confirmed reduced expression of Ggt1, Gstm1, and Gstm2 in Arid1a cKOUpk3a bladder tissues compared to wild-type controls under physiological conditions (Fig. S1D). Given the crucial role of GSH in cell survival, these findings suggested that impaired glutathione metabolism might represent a therapeutic vulnerability in ARID1A-deficient bladder cancer.

To test this hypothesis, we established stable ARID1A-knockdown (ARID1A-KD) monoclonal cell lines in T24, 5637, and SW780 bladder cancer cells, with knockdown efficiency confirmed at the protein level (Fig. 1A, B). ARID1A-KD T24 and 5637 cells exhibited significantly reduced intracellular GSH levels (Fig. 1C). Treatment with ferroptosis inducers erastin and RSL-338 revealed markedly enhanced sensitivity in ARID1A-KD cells. Notably, RSL-3 sensitivity increased approximately 100-fold in T24 ARID1A-KD cells (IC₅₀: 0.08 μM in KD vs 10.10 μM in WT) (Fig. S1E), while erastin showed more modest effects (Fig. 1D). Similar patterns, though less pronounced, were observed in 5637 and SW780 cells.

Consistent with ferroptosis induction, RSL-3 treatment significantly upregulated protein levels of ferroptosis markers SQLE and PTGS2 specifically in ARID1A-deficient cells (Fig. 1E, F). Fluorescent detection confirmed substantial increases in lipid ROS and Fe²⁺ levels following RSL-3 treatment in ARID1A-KD cells, whereas wild-type cells showed only Fe²⁺ elevation (Fig. 1G, H). GPX4 protein levels were significantly reduced by RSL-3 treatment exclusively in ARID1A-deficient cells (Fig. 1E, F). Together, these results demonstrate that ARID1A deficiency sensitizes bladder cancer cells to RSL-3-induced ferroptosis.

Ferroptosis impaired growth of ARID1A-deficient bladder cancer in vivo

Preliminary data indicated that ARID1A knockdown causes decreased GSH and dysregulated expression of its metabolic genes, suggesting ARID1A-deficient bladder cancer cells were more susceptible to ferroptosis (Fig. 2A). We next evaluated the therapeutic potential of ferroptosis induction in ARID1A-deficient models. In 3D Matrigel cultures, both 5637 and SW780 ARID1A-KD cells exhibited accelerated growth compared to wild-type controls but showed significantly enhanced sensitivity to RSL-3-mediated growth inhibition (Fig. 2B, C). Colony formation assays using T24 cells further confirmed the selective vulnerability of ARID1A-KD cells to RSL-3 (Fig. 2D, E). Both RSL-3 and Erastin demonstrated minimal toxicity in normal bladder epithelial cells at effective concentrations (Fig. S2A).

In immunodeficient RAG1⁻/⁻ mice bearing SW780 xenografts, ARID1A-deficient cells formed larger tumors than wild-type controls. RSL-3 treatment selectively attenuated tumor growth in ARID1A-deficient xenografts (Fig. 2F–I), with no significant body weight loss (Fig. S2B), indicating favorable in vivo tolerability. These results collectively demonstrate that RSL-3 effectively impaired growth of ARID1A-deficient bladder cancer cells and tumors.

The NRF2-SLC7A11-GSH axis mediated ferroptosis vulnerability in ARID1A-deficient cells

Given previous reports linking NRF2 to SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression24,39,40, and SLC7A11 attenuation in ARID1A-deficient colorectal cancer cells41, we hypothesized that ARID1A regulates NRF2 transcriptional activity. Indeed, both NRF2 and SLC7A11 protein levels were reduced in T24 and 5637 ARID1A-KD cells compared to wild-type controls (Fig. 3A).

To delineate the mechanistic basis for RSL-3 sensitivity, we performed rescue experiments in T24 ARID1A-KD cells using the NRF2 activator TBHQ42, SLC7A11 overexpression, and GSH supplementation, additionally, ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 served as the positive control to confirm that RSL-3 treatment specifically induced ferroptosis, which could be rescued by Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, 5 μM, RSL-3, 1 μM). TBHQ treatment dose-dependently increased NRF2 and SLC7A11 protein levels at 48 hours (Fig. S3A) with minimal effects on cell viability (Fig. S3B). SLC7A11 overexpression (Fig. S3C, D) and GSH supplementation (10 mM, Fig. S3E) significantly attenuated RSL-3 sensitivity in ARID1A-KD cells, while TBHQ provided partial protection (Fig. 3B), indicating that the NRF2-SLC7A11-GSH axis mediated ferroptosis vulnerability in ARID1A-deficient cells.

CUT&Tag analysis using an ARID1A antibody revealed ARID1A enrichment throughout the ARID1A and NRF2 gene loci in wild-type but not ARID1A-KD cells (Fig. 3C). NRF2 CUT&Tag demonstrated binding to promoter and 3’UTR regions of SLC7A11, with reduced binding in ARID1A-KD cells that was partially restored by TBHQ treatment in T24 (Fig. 3D). Dual-luciferase reporter assays showed that ARID1A deficiency reduced transcriptional activity of both NRF2 and SLC7A11 promoters, while NRF2 activation or ARID1A overexpression enhanced their activity (Fig. 3E). Co-immunoprecipitation experiments confirmed physical interaction between ARID1A and NRF2 proteins in 293 T cells (Fig. 3F).

In summary, our findings establish ARID1A as an upstream regulator of the NRF2-SLC7A11-GSH axis, directly mediating susceptibility to RSL-3-induced ferroptosis (Fig. 3G).

RSL-3 selectively inhibited tumor progression in Arid1a cKOUpk3a mice in a spontaneous bladder cancer model

Our previous data indicated downregulation of glutathione (GSH)-related genes in Arid1a‑deficient urothelium (Fig. S1A–D). To determine whether RSL-3 confers a therapeutic benefit in a spontaneous bladder cancer model. We performed Arid1a knockout in Upk3a-positive cells and generated conditional knockout mice through breeding with Upk3a-Cre strains (Fig. 4A) and evaluated long-term drug response in BBN-induced carcinogenesis. Conditional deletion of Arid1a (Arid1a cKOUpk3a) in adult mice was confirmed by IHC (Fig. 4B), though no significant difference in whole-bladder GSH levels was detected at this stage (Fig. 4C).

For survival analysis, wild-type and Arid1a cKOUpk3a mice received BBN for 5 months, with RSL-3 or vehicle administered twice weekly from month 2 to month 5. As anticipated, Arid1a cKOUpk3a mice exhibited significantly shorter survival than wild-type controls; this trend was markedly reversed by RSL-3 treatment (Fig. 4D). In contrast, RSL-3 had minimal effect on survival in wild-type mice.

To assess the pathological progression at a critical timepoint, bladder tissues were collected at day 135 (after 2.5 months of RSL-3 treatment), when initial mortality was observed. H&E staining revealed that RSL-3 effectively delayed cancer progression specifically in Arid1a cKOUpk3a mice (Fig. 4E). Both T-stage distribution and pathological area analysis further confirmed the efficacy of RSL-3, showing significant reduction in T2 stage and muscle-invasive area exclusively in Arid1a-deficient mice (Fig. 4F, G). Chronic RSL-3 administration did not alter body weight or blood biochemical parameters (Fig. S2C, D). Together, these results demonstrate that RSL-3 effectively attenuates spontaneous bladder cancer progression in Arid1a-deficient mice.

RSL-3 impaired tumorigenesis in Arid1a cKOShhCE mice and derived organoids

Unlike the Upk3a+ lineage, which primarily gives rise to non-muscle-invasive tumors, the Shh+ lineage is a major source of muscle-invasive bladder cancer in BBN models43–45. To examine whether the Arid1a deletion in this lineage also confers RSL-3 sensitivity, we generated tamoxifen-inducible Arid1a conditional knockout mice (Arid1a cKOShhCE; Fig. 5A). Mice received tamoxifen prior to BBN exposure, followed by 4.5 months of BBN treatment and RSL-3 administration from month 2 (Fig. 5B). RSL-3 significantly improved survival in Arid1a cKOShhCE mice: six of eight RSL-3-treated mutants survived, compared to only two of twelve vehicle-treated mutants at 4.5 months of BBN treatment (Fig. 5B). Histological analysis at endpoint revealed that two of the six RSL-3-treated mutant bladders exhibited markedly reduced invasive lesions (Fig. 5C), indicating that RSL-3 not only prolonged survival but also improved pathological outcomes.

We successfully established bladder cancer organoids from wild-type and Arid1a cKOShhCE mice. RSL-3 treatment induced pronounced vulnerability only in Arid1a-deficient organoids (Fig. 5D, E). Moreover, GSH supplementation abolished this sensitivity (Fig. 5F, G), confirming the GSH-dependent nature of the ferroptosis vulnerability in Arid1a-deficient tumors.

Low-ARID1A human patient-derived organoids exhibit heightened RSL-3 sensitivity

To assess the translational potential of RSL-3, we employed a clinically relevant drug-testing platform utilizing patient-derived organoids (PDOs)46. Three independent PDO lines showed clear responses to RSL-3 (Fig. 6A–F). Notably, organoids derived from muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) displayed greater sensitivity, with visible disintegration after only 24 hours of treatment (Fig. 6A–D). In contrast, organoids from non-cancerous adjacent tissue were less affected (Fig. 6C, D). This differential sensitivity correlated with lower expression of ARID1A, ARID1B, and GSH-related genes (Fig. 6G). The ferroptosis inducer erastin was considerably less effective than RSL-3 across all PDOs (Fig. 6A–F). These findings indicate that RSL-3 potently suppresses growth of aggressive, high-grade human bladder cancer organoids, particularly those with low ARID1A expression. In addition, we performed exome sequencing analysis on two patient-derived bladder cancer organoids with low ARID1A expression to investigate if any mutation exists. The results indicated that these two organoids did not harbor truncating mutations, but only exhibited a single SNP variant on chromosome 1 (Fig. S4A).

To validate whether the correlation between ARID1A loss and downregulation of the NRF2-GSH axis observed in patient-derived organoids (PDOs) holds true in larger clinical cohorts, this study further conducted bioinformatic analysis using transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas Bladder Cancer cohort (TCGA-BLCA). By systematically assessing the correlation between ARID1A mRNA expression and both glutathione metabolism pathway activity and NRF2 target gene signatures, we revealed a widespread positive correlation between ARID1A expression and NRF2/GSH-related genes (excluding GPX4/GPX3) (Fig. S4B), which significantly enhances the clinical relevance of this study.

Discussion

Our study identifies ferroptosis induction as a novel therapeutic strategy for ARID1A-deficient bladder cancer. We demonstrate that ARID1A loss confers vulnerability to ferroptosis inducers through suppression of the NRF2-SLC7A11-GSH antioxidant axis. Conversely, restoration of NRF2 activity, SLC7A11 expression, or GSH levels rescues this sensitivity. NRF2, a master regulator of antioxidant responses, is transcriptionally dampened in ARID1A-deficient cells, leading to downregulation of key GSH-metabolizing enzymes including glutathione S-transferases and gamma-glutamyl transferase47,48. Similarly, SLC7A11, which mediates cystine uptake for de novo GSH synthesis29, is positively regulated by the ARID1A-NRF2 axis. Previous studies have shown that combined inhibition of glutathione metabolism and SLC7A11 synergistically enhances cell death49,50. Our findings align with this concept: ARID1A deficiency creates a susceptible state characterized by impaired NRF2-SLC7A11-GSH signaling, rendering cells hypersensitive to both the system Xc⁻ inhibitor erastin and the GPX4 inhibitor RSL-3. This supports the model that genetic lesions compromising GSH synthesis increase cellular dependence on residual antioxidant capacity, establishing a synthetic lethal interaction with pathway-targeting agents.

Mechanistically, RSL-3 triggers ferroptosis in ARID1A-deficient cells through multiple interconnected pathways. The process involves transferrin receptor-mediated iron uptake, ferritinophagy-driven Fe²⁺ release, and Fenton reaction-generated hydroxyl radicals that propagate lipid peroxidation51,52. Elevated TFRC expression and Fe²⁺ accumulation in ARID1A-deficient cells likely contribute to this sensitivity. Additionally, SQLE upregulation depletes squalene, reducing membrane resistance to peroxidation53,54. Previous studies have shown that ARID1A deficiency impairs DNA repair and elevates basal ROS levels22,50,55. While these cells adapt to endure increased oxidative stress, direct GPX4 inhibition by RSL-3 overwhelms their compromised antioxidant defenses, triggering rapid ferroptotic death.

Interestingly, our data indeed suggests that the synthetic lethality is more pronounced with GPX4 inhibition (RSL-3) than with System Xc⁻ inhibition (Erastin). We hypothesize that ARID1A deficiency not only reduces cystine import via SLC7A11 downregulation but also broadly compromises the cellular antioxidant capacity, including the regeneration of reduced glutathione and the adaptive response to lipid peroxidation.GPX4 is the final defense enzyme that directly repairs lipid hydroperoxides using GSH56. Therefore, when GPX4 is inhibited in cells with a pre-existing depleted GSH pool and compromised NRF2-mediated stress response (due to ARID1A loss), the ferroptotic cascade becomes irrecoverable. In contrast, Erastin only blocks cystine uptake, and cells might still employ weak, alternative pathways (e.g., transsulfuration) to minimally sustain GSH synthesis, allowing partial survival57. This mechanistically explains that inhibiting the downstream terminal enzyme (GPX4) leaves cells with fewer escape routes; consequently, a potentially lower drug concentration may be sufficient to reach the lethal threshold, directly leading to a lower IC50.

ARID1A mutations are frequent in bladder cancer and associated with aggressive disease and poor survival58,59. Our data from BBN-induced models with Arid1a deletion in either Upk3a+- intermediate or Shh+- basal cells confirm this association. RSL-3 treatment significantly extended survival and reduced muscle-invasive progression in Arid1a-deficient mice, consistent with previous reports that Arid1a deletion alone may be insufficient for tumor initiation but accelerates progression22,60. The complete blockage of RSL-3 sensitivity by GSH supplementation in Arid1a cKOShhCE -derived organoids suggests that combining GSH depletion with RSL-3 could represent a potent synthetic lethal strategy. Unlike previously reported combinations targeting EZH2 or PI3K in ARID1A-deficient cancers21, our approach exploits a metabolic vulnerability rooted in glutathione homeostasis.

Intriguingly, studies in patient-derived bladder cancer organoids demonstrated that RSL-3 potently suppressed the growth of organoids with low expression of ARID1A, ARID1B, and GSH-related genes. Furthermore, analysis of transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas Bladder Cancer cohort (TCGA-BLCA) revealed a broad positive correlation between ARID1A expression and NRF2/GSH-related genes- except for GPX4, which showed a significant negative correlation with ARID1A. This finding suggests that ARID1A-low bladder cancers exhibit impaired GSH metabolism and reduced antioxidant capacity, potentially leading to compensatory upregulation of GPX461. However, due to the underlying GSH deficiency, GPX4 cannot function optimally24. Consequently, bladder cancer cells may become increasingly reliant on the residual GPX4 activity for survival, making them exquisitely vulnerable to GPX4 inhibition, which proves devastating to their viability.

In conclusion, this work establishes ferroptosis induction as a promising precision therapy approach for ARID1A-mutated bladder cancer. By identifying and validating the GSH metabolic vulnerability in ARID1A-deficient tumors, we provide a rational basis for developing effective treatments for this molecularly defined patient population.

Materials and methods

All the reagents used in this manuscript are listed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. This study did not generate new unique reagents.

Cell lines and culture

The human bladder carcinoma cell lines T24 (MeiSen CTCC), 5637 (MeiSen CTCC), and SW780 (Procell Life Science) were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A medium (Viva Cell) or RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco), respectively. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% HEPES, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Experiments were conducted when cells reached 80% confluence. RSL-3 powder was dissolved in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich) to prepare a 10 mM stock solution, and then diluted with complete culture medium to different concentrations for cell treatment. All cell lines were regularly tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. The cell lines used in this study were purchased from certified repositories and were authenticated by the suppliers upon delivery, no additional authentication was performed during the experiments.

Animals

All animal studies were performed according to protocols reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Yunnan University. All experiments in this study were conducted using 8-week-old adult male mice in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Arid1aflox/flox mice, in which LoxP sites were inserted flanking exon 8 of the Arid1a gene, were generated to establish a conditional knockout model. The Upk3a-e(2A-EGFP-Cre)1(Upk3a-Cre) mice using the CRISPR/Cas9 technology, the 2A-EGFP-Cre expression cassette was precisely knocked into the stop codon site of the Upk3a gene through homologous recombination. All genetically modified mice, including the immunodeficient Rag1-KO (Rag1-EGFP) strain, were generated by Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. C57BL/6 J mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center, Yunnan University. The Shh-CreERT2 mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology to precisely insert the CreERT2-WPRE-PA expression cassette at the start codon site of the Shh gene through homologous recombination. RSL-3 was dissolved in a vehicle consisting of 10% DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich), 40% PEG300 (MCE), 5% Tween-80 (MCE), and 45% saline (Servicebio). Each mouse received an intraperitoneal injection of 100 μL of the prepared RSL-3 mixture per administration. All experimental animals were euthanized using carbon dioxide inhalation.

Euthanasia procedures

The specific procedure was as follows: mice were placed in a dedicated euthanasia chamber and exposed to a flow of 100% carbon dioxide at a fill rate of 30- 70% for at least 5 minutes until breathing ceased completely. Cervical dislocation was subsequently performed for secondary confirmation. This procedure strictly adhered to the euthanasia guidelines established by the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA)

Organoids culture

Bladder cancer organoids were established from bladder tumors derived from a 5-month BBN-induced mouse model and three independent human patients. In brief, minced tumor tissues were incubated at 37 °C with 2 mg/mL type II collagenase (Worthington Biochemical) for 60-90 minutes. The dissociated cells were passed through a cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. Cells were embedded in Matrigel (Corning) and cultured in organoid medium at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The human organoids culture medium consisted of Advanced DMEM/F12 (Gibco) supplemented with 2% B27 (Gibco), 10 mM nicotinamide (Sigma), 1.25 mM N-acetyl-L-cysteine (Sigma), 100 ng/mL FGF10 (Bio-Techne), 25 ng/mL FGF7 (Bio-Techne), 12.5 ng/mL FGF2 (Bio-Techne), and 5 μM A83-01 (Bio-Techne). The mouse organoids culture medium contained B27, FGF10, FGF7, and A83-01. Organoids were passaged every two weeks. For passaging, organoids were released from Matrigel using Advanced DMEM/F12, collected by centrifugation at 300 g for 3 minutes, and subsequently digested with TrypLE (Gibco) at 37 °C for 3 minutes to generate small cell clusters for reseeding.

ARID1A-knock down and SLC7A11-over expression Cell line

Stable ARID1A-knockdown and SLC7A11-overexpressing cell lines were generated via lentiviral transduction. Bladder cancer cells were transduced with lentiviruses carrying a doxycycline-inducible pLenti-puro-ARID1A shRNA construct or a Phage-Ef1-BSD-SLC7A11 overexpression construct. After 96 hours of culture, transduced cells were selected and maintained in medium containing puromycin (for ARID1A knockdown) or blasticidin (for SLC7A11 overexpression). Knockdown and overexpression efficiency were confirmed by immunoblotting.

GSH detection

Bladder cancer cells were cultured in complete medium until the cell count reached 5 × 106, and the cells were collected. GSH concentration was determined by the Micro Reduced GSH Assay Kit (Solarbio). Additionally, bladder tissue was dissected and homogenized, and then subjected to GSH content measurement.

Cell viability assay

Four thousands cells were seeded into 96-well plates. For treatments, 5 repeats were performed for each concentration. Empty wells with same volume of complete medium were used as blank background. Following drug treatment (e.g., with RSL-3 for the indicated durations), MTT (1 mg/ml) (Beyotime) was added into each well and then incubated for 4 hours at 37 °C. After incubation, the solution was carefully removed and 100 μL DMSO was added to solubilize the violet formazan crystals. MTT absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 490 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific).

Western blot

Cultured cells or tumor tissues were lysed with RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and 1 mM PMSF) on ice for 30 min. Proteins were denatured using SDS loading buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 2% SDS, 10% Glycerol, 0.1% Bromophenol blue, and 100 mM DTT) at 100 °C for 10 min. Proteins were then separated by SDS-PAGE gel (Beyotime) and transferred to PVDF membrane (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Signals were amplified with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (CST) and detected by chemiluminescent substrate (Sage Creation).

Cellular ROS and Fe2+ assay

Briefly, T24 cells were grown in 8 well chambered cover glass overnight at 37 °C. The next day, wild-type and ARID1A knockdown cells were treated with RSL3 (0.1 μM) for 24 hours, followed by the addition of 0.1 μM FerroFarRed (MKbio) or 1 μM CellROX Deep Red Reagent (Warbio) for 1 h. After washing with PBS, cells were imaged using a ZEISS laser confocal microscope with excitation at 646 nm (Fe²⁺) and 644 nm (ROS).

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) was used to isolate total RNA from bladder tissue and bladder cancer cells. RNA-seq data were analyzed, and pathway analysis was done using the Cluster Profiler software package. Meanwhile, we also used the online tool SRplot to draw the cluster heatmap. For RT- qPCR experiments, mRNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme). RT-qPCR analyses were performed using NovoStart® SYBR High-Sensitivity qPCR SuperMix (Novoprotein) on a CFX96TM Real-Time System C1000 Touch detector (BioRad CFX Manager). Relative gene expression levels were normalized to the internal control Gapdh. Gene-specific primers are listed in Supplementary Information Table 2.

Colony formation assay

Wild type and ARID1A knockdown T24 cells (500 cells/well) were grown in 6-well plates overnight to allow cells to attach. Cells were treated with RSL3 (0.1 μM) for single-treatment experiments. Cells were cultured for 10 days, with media containing 10% FBS and compounds being changed every 3 days. Colonies were fixed with methanol and stained with 0.01% crystal violet solution for 1 hour at room temperature and washed with water. An equal volume of DMSO was used as a control.

Cell Xenograft models

Eight-week-old male Rag1-/- mice were subcutaneously implanted with wild type or ARID1A knockdown SW780 cells suspended in PBS on their backs. One week after tumor formation, the mice were randomly divided into two groups (4 mice in each group), ensuring no significant difference in initial tumor volumes. The two groups were treated with DMSO and RSL-3, respectively. The mice were treated with DMSO (10% DMSO, 30% PEG300, 10% Tween-80 and 50% sterile normal saline) or RSL-3 (100 mg/kg, twice a week) via intra-peritoneal injection for 14 days. Tumor size was periodically measured with a vernier caliper for 21 days, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: Volume = (long axis) × (short axis)² × 0.5. At the end of the experiment, the mice were sacrificed, the tumors were harvested for weighing and further analyses. The weights of mice were measured regularly during the drug injection period to assess potential drug toxicity.

Tumor monitoring and volume limits

All murine tumor models used in this study were monitored regularly (Every two days). Tumor volumes were measured using vernier calipers and calculated using the formula Volume = (long axis) × (short axis)² × 0.5. According to the research protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) (Approval Nos: YNU20220176 and YNU20220818), the maximum allowable tumor volume was 2000 mm³. All experimental animals in this study were euthanized as planned before reaching this volume limit, and none of the experiments exceeded this limit. Additionally, we strictly adhered to the Committee’s requirements for humane endpoints, which mandated euthanasia if body weight loss exceeded 20% or if severe signs of distress were observed.

Cut & Tag

Approximately 1 × 105 cells were subjected to a Cut & Tag assay. Cells were washed with 500 μL of washing buffer and centrifuged to remove supernatants. ConA Beads Pro (Vazyme) were prepared in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines, and 10 mL of activated magnetic beads were added to each sample for incubation at room temperature for 10 min. Subsequently, the cells were co-incubated with primary antibodies, secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L; Vazyme), and hyperactive PG-TN5/PA-TN5 transposase (Vazyme), in accordance with the kit’s instructions. The cellular fragments were then generated, and DNA fragments library were generated and sequenced. by Anoroad Company

Dual-Luciferase assay

HEK293T cells were transfected using PEI (Thermo) Transfection Reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were seeded in 24-well plates 24 h. before transfection. Plasmids were mixed in DMEM medium and transfection complexes were then gently added into individual wells of the 24-well plate. Twenty-four hours after transfection, cells were treated with or without NRF2 activator, TBHQ (MCE) and inhibitor, Trigonelline, (MCE) for 24 h (Trigonelline) or 48 h (TBHQ) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Finally, a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay (Promega) was used to detect firefly luciferase and renilla luciferase activities, the ratio of Luciferase to Renilla was used for the analysis.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Protein extracts (0.5 mg each sample) from transfected HEK293T cells were diluted with 2 volumes cold TBS buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH7.4) and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Protein A/G beads (10 μl each) (Engibody) were washed 3 times using PBS buffer before being added to the protein sample. All samples were gently rolled at 4 °C for 4 h. The beads were then washed twice with cold TBS buffer. Next, 30 μl PBS buffer and SDS PAGE loading buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [pH6.8], 1 mM DTT, 20 g/l SDS, 0.1 g/l bromophenol blue, 50% glycerol) was added to the beads and boiled for 10 min at 100 °C. Samples were loaded and ran on a 10% SDS PAGE gel for 100 min at 100 V.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

After deparaffinization of organoid-derived tissue sections from Wild type and Arid1a cKOUpk3a mice bladders, the paraffin sections were placed in 10 mM Sodium citrate dihydrate (Sigma) solution at pH 6 for 10 min for antigen repair. Then, tissue sections were blocked in PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 and Animal-Free Blocking Solution (CST) for 1 h, incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The tissue sections were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies (Vazyme) for 1 hour at room temperature. Cell nuclei were stained using Mayer’s Hematoxylin (Servicebio).

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9, with statistical significance set as p < 0.05. For pairwise data comparisons of datasets that conform to a normal distribution, Student’s t-test was used for analysis. Two-way ANOVA was used for analyzing tumor growth. The experiments were performed with at least two biological replicates. Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± SEM unless otherwise stated. Data is reported in the form of box and whisker plots with individual data points. All mice used for animal experiments were randomized.
