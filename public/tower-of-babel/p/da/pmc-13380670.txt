Cerebrospinal fluid α‐synuclein and Aβ42 link with default mode and salience networks connectivity in dementia with Lewy bodies
Authors: Vincent Gabriel, Elena Chabran, Marion Sourty, Benjamin Cretin, Nathalie Philippi, Candice Muller, Pierre Anthony, Catherine Demuynck, Paulo Loureiro de Sousa, Anne Botzung, Léa Sanna, Olivier Bousiges, Frédéric Blanc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONDementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) and Alzheimer's disease (AD) are neurocognitive disorders characterized by distinct but often overlapping pathological processes. These include α‐synuclein, amyloid‐beta 42 (Aβ42), and tau protein aggregation. While cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers provide in vivo insight into these pathologies, their relationship with large‐scale brain network dysfunction remains poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate the associations between CSF biomarker concentrations and resting‐state functional connectivity in patients with DLB, AD, and mixed AD/DLB.METHODSSixty‐nine DLB patients, 17 AD patients, and 24 patients with mixed AD/DLB underwent clinical and neuropsychological evaluations, lumbar puncture for CSF biomarker analysis (total α‐synuclein, Aβ42, pTau181, and tTau), and resting‐state functional MRI. Patients were stratified by disease stage for subgroup analyses. Besides CSF total α‐synuclein levels, α‐synuclein seeding activity was assessed using real‐time quaking‐induced conversion (RT‐QuIC) assays. ROI‐to‐ROI analyses were conducted using the CONN toolbox to explore associations between CSF biomarker levels and functional connectivity within and between major brain networks.RESULTSIn DLB patients, lower CSF α‐synuclein levels correlated with increased connectivity within the default mode network (DMN) (p FDR < 0.05). In dementia‐stage DLB (d‐DLB), lower Aβ42 levels correlated with reduced connectivity within the salience network (SN) (p FDR < 0.05). In AD, higher tTau levels correlated with decreased connectivity between the DMN and the SN (p FDR < 0.05). No significant associations were observed for CSF pTau181 or any RT‐QuIC metric in any group, and the mixed AD/DLB group showed no biomarker–connectivity correlations at all.DISCUSSIONWe identified distinct patterns of DMN and SN connectivity changes associated with CSF α‐synuclein and Aβ42 levels, respectively. These findings reflect key functional disruptions that may contribute to core clinical symptoms. They underscore the value of combining CSF biomarkers with functional MRI to elucidate DLB pathophysiology.Keywords: alpha‐synuclein, Alzheimer's disease, amyloid beta 42, cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, default mode network, dementia with lewy bodies, resting‐state fMRI, RT‐QuIC, salience network, tau

BACKGROUND

Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) is the second most common cause of neurocognitive disease after Alzheimer's disease (AD), accounting for approximately 15–20% of all neuropathologically confirmed cases 1 . In addition to cognitive impairment, DLB is clinically defined by fluctuating attention, recurrent visual hallucinations, parkinsonian features, and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder (RBD) 1 . However, DLB remains underdiagnosed, in part due to the clinical overlap with both AD and Parkinson's disease 2 . Improving our understanding of its neuropathological mechanisms is crucial to distinguish DLB from related disorders.

DLB belongs to the broader group of neurodegenerative proteinopathies, defined by abnormal protein aggregation. Its pathological hallmark is the accumulation of misfolded α‐synuclein within neurons, forming Lewy bodies 3 . However, co‐occurring AD‐type pathologies, notably amyloid‐beta (Aβ) plaques and hyperphosphorylated tau tangles, are found in over half of DLB cases and are associated with faster cognitive decline and greater clinical severity 4 , 5 , 6 . Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers offer a better characterization of these changes in vivo. In AD, low CSF Aβ42, along with elevated phosphorylated tau (pTau181) and total tau (tTau, a marker of neurodegeneration), are established biomarkers 7 , 8 . In DLB, reduced CSF Aβ42 concentrations have been reported, especially in the later stages of the disease 9 , 10 . These changes are sometimes accompanied by alterations in CSF α‐synuclein levels, although findings remain inconsistent across studies 11 , 12 , 13 . In some patients, coexisting amyloid, tau and α‐synuclein pathologies give rise to a mixed AD/DLB phenotype, defined by overlapping clinical and biomarker features of both conditions. More recently, real‐time quaking‐induced conversion (RT‐QuIC) assays have emerged, enabling the ultrasensitive detection of α‐synuclein seeding activity, and significantly improving the diagnostic accuracy of synucleinopathies 14 .

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a key modality for investigating neurodegenerative disorders. However, beyond structural changes, resting‐state functional MRI (fMRI) allows the assessment of large‐scale brain network alterations, which may serve as key markers of disease‐related dysfunction. Among them, the default mode network (DMN) and the salience network (SN) are of particular interest in DLB. The DMN, which includes the posterior cingulate and medial prefrontal cortices, is involved in self‐referential thought and episodic memory 15 and is consistently disrupted in AD, with reduced connectivity linked to cognitive decline 16 , 17 . While findings in DLB are less consistent, growing evidence suggests that the DMN is also involved in its pathophysiology 18 . The SN, centered on the anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex, detects salient stimuli and processes switching between the DMN and executive control networks 19 . In DLB, SN dysfunction has been reported 17 , 18 , 20 , and underlies cognitive decline and core clinical symptoms such as cognitive fluctuations 17 , 18 .

Some studies have examined the relationship between proteinopathies and functional connectivity in AD 21 , notably linking CSF Aβ42 levels to DMN disruptions 22 . However, to our knowledge, no prior work has directly explored the relationship between CSF biomarker levels and functional connectivity in DLB.

In this study, we aimed to address this issue by investigating the relationships between CSF biomarkers (α‐synuclein, Aβ42, tTau, pTau181, and α‐synuclein RT‐QuIC metrics) and resting‐state functional connectivity of key brain networks in patients with DLB, AD, and mixed AD/DLB. We hypothesized that CSF total α‐synuclein, Aβ42 and RT‐QuIC metrics would be associated with connectivity alterations in DLB, particularly in the DMN and SN. Conversely, we expected that tau and amyloid pathology would relate to network disruptions in AD patients mainly involving the DMN. By integrating CSF biomarker data with resting‐state fMRI, this multimodal approach aims to link molecular pathology with large‐scale brain dysfunction, providing novel insights into disease mechanisms and their clinical expression.

METHODS

Participants

Participants were recruited as part of the AlphaLewyMA longitudinal research protocol, initiated in 2013 at the tertiary Memory Clinic (CM2R) of the University Hospitals of Strasbourg, France (ClinicalTrials.gov, registration number: NCT01876459; URL: https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT01876459). All participants underwent comprehensive clinical and neuropsychological evaluations, high‐resolution brain MRI acquisitions and CSF sampling via lumbar puncture. DLB diagnosis was based on the revised consensus criteria 1 , incorporating cognitive fluctuations 23 , recurrent visual hallucinations 24 , parkinsonian features 25 and RBD 26 . AD diagnosis was based on Albert's and Dubois’ criteria 27 , 28 . Mixed AD/DLB cases were defined when both of the following were present: (1) at least two core clinical features of DLB; and (2) a typical amnestic AD phenotype supported by CSF biomarkers indicative AD pathology. These classifications were confirmed by neurologists, neuropsychologists and biomarker experts. Patients were further stratified into mild cognitive impairment (MCI‐DLB, MCI‐AD, MCI‐AD/DLB) and dementia stages (d‐DLB, d‐AD, and d‐AD/DLB) subgroups using Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) scores 29 and McKeith criteria 30 . Patients lacking either CSF data or high‐quality fMRI acquisitions were excluded from the study. In total, 69 DLB patients (49 MCI‐DLB and 20 d‐DLB), 17 AD patients (9 MCI‐AD and 8 d‐AD), and 24 AD/DLB patients (6 MCI‐AD/DLB and 18 d‐AD/DLB) were included. Detailed clinical, sociodemographic, and CSF biomarker data are provided in Table 1. Subgroup‐level details are presented in Table S1. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the local ethics committee of Eastern France (IV).

Analysis of clinical characteristics

Clinical and sociodemographic data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Group comparisons of demographics and clinical measures were performed with one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (with Tukey post‐hoc) or Kruskal–Wallis (with Dunn post‐hoc) as appropriate, and chi square (χ2) tests for categorical variables (with Bonferroni correction). Significance was set at p < 0.05. Detailed results are presented in Table 1.

CSF samples and analyses

CSF samples were obtained by lumbar puncture using standardized procedures for collection, centrifugation, and storage. Samples were collected in polypropylene tubes (Sarstedt, ref: 61.610.201), transported to the local laboratory within 4 hours, homogenized, and centrifuged at 1700 g for 10 minutes at 4°C. Supernatants were aliquoted into 500 µL polypropylene tubes (Dutscher, ref: 033283) and stored at −80°C until analysis. CSF concentrations of Aβ42, pTau181, and tTau were measured using standardized immunoassays (Innotest ELISA test, Fujirebio, Europe, Ghent, Belgium). α‐Synuclein levels were assessed using hSYN total ELISA kits (AJ Roboscreen, GmbH, Leipzig, Germany).

RT‐QuIC analysis

CSF samples were further analyzed for α‐synuclein seeding activity using the RT‐QuIC assay (for protocol details, see 31 ). For each sample, four replicate wells were tested. The following parameters were recorded: (1) the percentage of positive patients (at least 1 reactive well), (2) the number of reactive wells out of four; (3) the maximal fluorescence intensity of the most reactive well (IMaxMax); and (4) the minimal lag phase of the most reactive well (LagPhaseMin). These metrics provided quantitative insights into the aggregation kinetics of pathological α‐synuclein in the CSF.

fMRI acquisition and preprocessing

Functional and anatomical MRI images were acquired on a 3‐Tesla MR scanner (Verio 32‐channel Tim Siemens scanner; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). Resting‐state functional MRI (blood oxygen level dependent [BOLD]) and arterial spin‐labeling (ASL) sequences were used to capture 121 whole brain T2*‐weighted echo planar images (repetition time = 3s; flip angle = 90°; echo time = 21 ms; resolution = 38 × 64 × 28; field of view = 152 × 256 × 112 mm2; 4 mm isotropic voxels). The first volume, acquired for ASL assessment, was excluded from connectivity analysis. In addition to fMRI, a single whole‐brain T1‐weighted anatomical image was acquired using a volumetric magnetization‐prepared rapid acquisition with gradient‐echo (MPRAGE) sequence (repetition time = 1900 ms; flip angle = 9°; echo time = 2.52 ms; field of view = 256 × 256mm2; resolution 256 × 256 × 256 voxels; slice thickness = 1 mm).

The preprocessing was performed using the Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 software (SPM12, The Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK) within MATLAB R2023a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Preprocessing steps included: low‐pass filtering of ASL frequencies at 0.112 Hz; slice‐timing correction; motion and B0 field inhomogeneity corrections; correction of excessive motion or intensity fluctuations using ArtRepair; co‐registration of fMRI images to T1‐weighted anatomical images; spatial normalization to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using DARTEL; smoothing with an 8 mm Gaussian kernel (full width at half maximum); and denoising using the CONN toolbox 32 .

Functional connectivity analysis

Functional connectivity was assessed using ROI‐to‐ROI analyses in the CONN toolbox. Thirty‐two ROIs corresponding to the main nodes of the DMN, SN, frontoparietal network (FPN), dorsal attention network (DAN), language network (LN), sensorimotor network (SMN), visual network (VN), and cerebellar network (CN) were selected based on the atlas implemented in the CONN toolbox. For each participant, ROI‐to‐ROI connectivity matrices were computed by calculating bivariate Pearson correlations between the mean BOLD time series of each ROI pair. Correlation coefficients were transformed using Fisher's z‐transformation. The six motion parameters obtained during preprocessing were included as covariates of noninterest. Group‐level analyses were then conducted using second‐level general linear model. For each group, separate multiple regression models were used to study the relationship between ROI‐to‐ROI connectivity (dependent variable) and each CSF biomarker (predictor), controlling for age, sex, and total intracranial volume. This allowed us to identify network connections where connectivity strength varied as a function of biomarker level within that group. Correction for multiple comparisons was applied using false discovery rate (FDR) (p FDR < 0.05 at the cluster level, and p < 0.05 uncorrected at the connection level).

RESULTS

Clinical and sociodemographic characteristics

Demographic and clinical characteristics as well as CSF biomarker values are detailed in Table 1. The diagnostic groups were comparable in terms of age, sex distribution, years of education and IADL scores. Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores were significantly lower in the AD/DLB group compared to the DLB group (p < 0.001). The DLB group showed higher frequencies of each core clinical feature of the disease compared to the AD group (p < 0.001; except for tremors at rest where p < 0.05). The AD/DLB group also exhibited significantly higher rates of akinesia and rigidity than the AD group (p < 0.001), while there were no significant differences between AD/DLB and AD regarding fluctuations, hallucinations, tremors at rest, or RBD. Only visual hallucinations were significantly more prevalent in the DLB group compared to the AD/DLB group (p < 0.001). The proportion of patients at the MCI stage differed significantly across diagnostic groups: 71% in DLB (49/69), 53% in AD and 25% in AD/DLB (6/24) (χ 2 = 10.84; p < 0.01).

CSF biomarker concentrations

Detailed boxplots of CSF biomarker concentrations across groups are provided in Figure 1. CSF α‐synuclein concentrations were significantly lower in the DLB group compared to the AD group (p < 0.01) and the AD/DLB group (p < 0.001) (Figure 1A). Aβ42 concentrations were significantly higher in the DLB group compared to the AD and AD/DLB groups (p < 0.001) (Figure 1B). Both pTau181 and tTau concentrations were significantly higher in the AD and AD/DLB groups compared to the DLB group (p < 0.001) (Figure 1C,D). RT‐QuIC analyses confirmed increased α‐synuclein seeding activity in the DLB group compared to the AD group, with significantly higher percentage of positive patients (p < 0.05), numbers of reactive wells (p < 0.001), and IMaxMax values (p < 0.01), along with shorter LagPhaseMin times (p < 0.001). Significant differences in the LagPhaseMin times were also observed between the AD/DLB and AD groups (p < 0.01) (Table 1).

Correlations between biomarkers and functional connectivity

While the focus of this study was to investigate biomarker‐connectivity correlations within each diagnostic group, our previous work highlighted disease‐stage specific patterns of connectivity disruption in DLB, particularly affecting the SN and DMN which are strongly relevant to the findings we present here 17 .

Figure 2 illustrates significant correlations between CSF biomarker levels and both intra‐ and inter‐network resting‐state functional connectivity across diagnostic groups. In DLB patients, reduced CSF α‐synuclein levels were correlated with higher connectivity within the DMN (Figure 2A) (p FDR < 0.05), specifically between the medial prefrontal cortex and both the posterior cingulate cortex and the left lateral parietal cortex. This association between CSF α‐synuclein levels and DMN connectivity was also observed in both the MCI‐DLB and d‐DLB subgroups.

No significant association between Aβ42 levels and connectivity was found in the overall DLB group or in the MCI‐DLB subgroup. In the d‐DLB subgroup, lower CSF Aβ42 levels were significantly correlated with reduced connectivity within the SN (Figure 2B), p FDR < 0.05), specifically between the anterior cingulate cortex and the anterior insular cortices, between the left and right rostral prefrontal cortices, and between the left rostral prefrontal cortex and the right anterior insula.

In the AD group, higher tTau concentrations were significantly correlated with reduced inter‐network connectivity between the DMN and the SN (Figure 2C) (p FDR < 0.05). This involved the posterior cingulate cortex and lateral parietal cortices within the DMN, and the anterior cingulate cortex, left rostral prefrontal cortex, and bilateral insula within the SN. No significant correlations were observed between functional connectivity and either pTau181 levels or RT‐QuIC parameters in any diagnostic group. Moreover, no significant correlations were observed for any CSF biomarkers in the AD/DLB group and its subgroups, nor in the MCI‐AD and d‐AD subgroups.

DISCUSSION

This study highlights distinct associations between CSF biomarkers and resting‐state large‐scale brain network alterations in DLB and AD. In DLB, reduced CSF α‐synuclein levels were specifically associated with increased intra‐DMN connectivity, while lower Aβ42 levels correlated with decreased connectivity within the SN in the d‐DLB subgroup. In contrast, AD patients showed reduced connectivity between the DMN and SN in association with higher CSF tTau concentrations. These findings support the idea that different proteinopathies target specific brain networks, leading to distinct patterns of functional disruption that may contribute to the clinical heterogeneity observed in neurodegenerative diseases.

CSF α‐synuclein and DMN connectivity in DLB

Lower CSF α‐synuclein levels were associated with increased functional connectivity within the DMN in DLB patients. This finding may initially appear counterintuitive, as protein aggregation is typically associated with network disruption. However, such increases in connectivity may reflect either an early compensatory mechanism to maintain network integrity in the face of pathological stress, or a maladaptive hyper‐synchronization driven by early α‐synuclein accumulation. This interpretation is supported by several studies suggesting that the DMN is particularly vulnerable to synuclein‐related dysfunction. For instance, both Parkinson's disease and DLB patients show abnormal DMN connectivity even before substantial structural atrophy occurs, indicating that functional reorganization may precede overt neurodegeneration 33 , 34 . Moreover, prior work has demonstrated increased inter‐network connectivity of the DMN‐SN and DMN‐FPN pathways in DLB compared with control subjects 18 . Altogether, these findings suggest that α‐synuclein pathology may lead to a dynamic reshaping of DMN connectivity, potentially reflecting early compensatory or maladaptive processes rather than a simple functional decline. Importantly, this α‐synuclein‐DMN connectivity association was evident in both MCI‐DLB and d‐DLB subgroups. This suggests that DMN alterations occur early and persists as the disease progresses, so alternative mechanisms deserve consideration. Impaired network switching, potentially due to SN dysfunction 35 , or cholinergic disruption from nucleus basalis of Meynert degeneration 36 , may promote maladaptive DMN engagement. Morevover, higher CSF α‐synuclein levels can also be interpreted as greater neurodegeneration and synaptic loss 37 , which could account for reduced DMN integrity.

CSF Aβ42 and SN connectivity in DLB

In contrast, we found that reduced CSF Aβ42 levels were associated with decreased connectivity within the SN in the d‐DLB subgroup. This result aligns with previous studies on AD and DLB linking amyloid pathology to large‐scale network disruptions, particularly within the SN and DMN 22 , 38 , 39 . Our findings suggest that Aβ42‐related connectivity changes emerge only at the dementia stage of DLB, as no significant associations were found in the MCI‐DLB subgroup or the whole DLB group. This is consistent with prior studies reporting that CSF Aβ42 levels remain relatively preserved in early stages and decline with the disease progression 9 , 10 , a pattern we replicated here (see Table S1). Moreover, we also previously showed that SN alterations become more pronounced at the dementia stage 17 , reinforcing the hypothesis of a cumulative effect of amyloid burden on this network's integrity.

Because the SN coordinates the shift between internally and externally oriented cognitive processes 40 , its disruption in DLB could directly contribute to the pronounced attentional instability characteristic of this disease 17 , 18 . This observation suggests that amyloid‐related network disruption in the SN may be a key driver of attentional instability in DLB, reinforcing its central role in the disease. Moreover, these functional disruptions align with prior structural observations, in which lower CSF Aβ42 levels were associated with poorer gray matter volumes in the insula, a core hub of the SN 41 , which is known to be structurally vulnerable in DLB from the earlier stages of the disease 42 . Thus, amyloid pathology appears to compromise the SN both structurally and functionally as DLB progresses, reinforcing the network's central role in DLB pathophysiology. Notably, the association between Aβ42 levels and SN connectivity was specific to DLB and absent in both AD and AD/DLB patients, in line with previous findings suggesting that the mechanisms of amyloid pathology may differ between DLB and AD 43 , 44 .

Our results also point to dissociation in the functional consequences of α‐synuclein and Aβ42 in DLB. This functional divergence may reflect distinct temporal and spatial dynamics of the disruption caused by the underlying proteinopathies and could help explain the clinical heterogeneity that characterizes DLB.

CSF tTau and DMN‐SN pathway disruptions in AD

AD patients displayed a distinct profile, with higher CSF tTau levels associated with reduced connectivity between the DMN and SN. While CSF tTau is a well‐established marker of neurodegeneration rather than tau aggregation itself 45 , we interpreted this finding within the AT(N) framework, where tTau reflects global neuronal injury (“N”) rather than tau pathology 45 . Despite its limited specificity, tTau is typically elevated in AD compared to DLB 9 , 10 , and may therefore serve as a differential marker of neurodegenerative severity across disorders. Given that functional connectivity can be modulated by both specific pathology and broader neuronal damage, the observed DMN‐SN decoupling in relation to CSF tTau likely reflects the impact of advancing neurodegeneration on large‐scale brain networks integration in AD. Tau pathology in AD has been shown to propagate along functionally connected networks, including the DMN, leading to increased vulnerability of its regions at later Braak stages 46 . Recent positron emission tomography (PET) studies further suggest that tau accumulation spreads from DMN hubs to other large‐scale networks, including attentional systems such as the SN 47 . This network‐based propagation may help explain our observation of a weakened DMN–SN coupling in relation to elevated CSF tTau.

Furthermore, our own previous work revealed a selective disruption of the DMN‐SN pathway in AD compared to DLB, reinforcing the notion that tau‐driven degeneration may particularly impair network dynamics underlying executive function 17 . The dissociation between the DMN and SN may be particularly relevant for understanding cognitive impairment in AD.

Lack of associations

AD and AD/DLB groups were included primarily to contextualize DLB findings and explore whether known Aβ42 and pTau181 connectivity associations reported in AD 8 would replicate under our acquisition protocol and transmitted through the copathological condition. However, no significant associations were found in these groups, which should be interpreted carefully given their smaller sample sizes and the conservative statistical threshold applied. The absence of correlation between Aβ42 or pTau181 and connectivity may reflect reduced statistical power, but also a floor/ceiling effect in biomarker values, rather than the absence of an underlying relationship. In the AD/DLB group, the lack of significant associations may also reflect the intrinsic heterogeneity of this population, where the co‐expression of amyloid and α‐synuclein pathologies could exert divergent or counteracting effects across individuals. These analyses were exploratory in nature and primarily served to strengthen the interpretability of our central results in DLB.

Regarding RT‐QuIC metrics, while we observed significant group‐level differences in seeding activity metrics, no associations were observed with functional connectivity alterations in DLB patients. This may reflect the inherently semi‐quantitative nature of RT‐QuIC outputs, which likely limits their sensitivity to subtle, continuous variations in network‐level brain function, compared to concentration‐based biomarkers, which can capture more subtle and graded changes in pathology.

Finally, especially in mixed AD/DLB cases, the well‐known interplay between amyloid and α‐synuclein pathologies 48 may have additive or synergistic effects on brain networks. Future studies with larger, biomarker‐stratified cohorts will be essential to disentangle these interactions and clarify their contribution to functional connectivity disruptions. In parallel, future work may benefit from adopting purely biomarker‐based classifications (e.g. AT(N) or Neuronal alpha‐Synuclein Disease Integrated Staging System [NSD‐ISS] frameworks) which could offer an alternative framework to investigate connectivity alterations independently of clinical syndromes 45 , 49 .

Limitations

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as mentioned earlier, the relatively small sample sizes in the AD and AD/DLB groups may have limited our statistical power to detect more subtle associations. Although we did not observe significant correlations involving Aβ42, tTau, or pTau181 in these groups, previous studies have reported biologically coherent links with network dysfunction, particularly between Aβ42 and DMN connectivity 22 . Therefore, all findings in AD and AD/DLB groups, and especially their subgroups, should be considered exploratory, and our core conclusions remain centered on the DLB population. Second, the cross‐sectional design of these analyses prevents us from drawing conclusions about the directionality or causality of the observed associations. Longitudinal studies combining repeated biomarker assessments and fMRI acquisitions are needed to clarify how molecular pathology and functional network changes evolve across disease progression. Third, while autopsy confirmation is lacking given the clinical nature of the cohort, the use of established diagnostic criteria, biomarker profiles and the addition of an AD/DLB mixed‐case group enhanced diagnostic accuracy. Fourth, due to missing CSF Aβ40 data in a substantial proportion of the cohort, we could not compute the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio. While this ratio offers improved diagnostic accuracy for amyloid pathology, especially in AD 50 , its absence limits the precision of our amyloid assessment. Notably, in DLB, Aβ42 and Aβ40 appear to be proportionally reduced, preserving the ratio despite amyloid deposition 10 , 43 , making Aβ42 alone still informative. Future studies with complete Aβ42/Aβ40 data will be important to confirm these comparisons, especially with AD.

Finally, while CSF biomarker levels offer valuable information on underlying proteinopathies, they do not offer spatial resolution regarding where pathology accumulates in the brain. Thus, the observed associations between CSF levels and network connectivity must be interpreted cautiously. Future studies integrating molecular PET imaging help better localize and contextualize these functional alterations in DLB.

CONCLUSION

Our study is the first to link CSF biomarker levels to functional connectivity changes in DLB. We found that lower CSF α‐synuclein levels were associated with increased DMN connectivity, while lower Aβ42 levels correlated with disrupted SN connectivity, particularly in more advanced stages. These distinct patterns may reflect different network vulnerabilities to α‐synuclein and amyloid pathologies and could help explain the clinical variability seen in DLB. Our results underscore the relevance of multimodal approaches combining CSF biomarkers and resting‐state fMRI to better understand the complex mechanisms underlying neurodegenerative diseases. Future studies involving longitudinal cohorts, as well as different approaches such as dynamic connectivity or regional PET biomarkers, will be essential to confirm these findings and to better understand how they evolve over time.
