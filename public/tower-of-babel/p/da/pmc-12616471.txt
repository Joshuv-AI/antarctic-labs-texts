Platelets facilitate fat grafting by mitochondrial transfer and reducing oxidative stress in adipose-derived stem cells
Authors: Chen Ke, Kaibo Liu, Wanying Chen, Zhongming Cai, Fangfang Yang, Qing Wei, Yucang He, Jingping Wang, Liqun Li, Binting Ni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutologous fat grafting (AFG), characterized by a broad tissue source and absence of immune rejection, is extensively utilized in plastic surgery. Despite its advantages, AFG is frequently challenged by a high rate of fat resorption and limited volume retention. Recent studies have increasingly focused on integrating platelet-related preparations with adipose tissue to enhance graft survival rates. These investigations have consistently demonstrated the beneficial effects of platelets and their derivatives on adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), facilitating improved outcomes in fat transplantation. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms governing the interaction between platelets and ADSCs remain insufficiently understood. We investigate the potential of platelets to augment the antioxidant stress capacity of ADSCs through mitochondrial transfer, thereby contributing to enhanced fat graft viability. Experimental results revealed that platelets significantly promoted ADSC proliferation, migration, metabolic activity, and mitochondrial function. Co-culture of oxidative stress-induced ADSCs with platelets resulted in improved cell viability and a marked reduction in reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. The mitochondrial transfer from platelets to ADSCs, confirmed via fluorescent labeling, played a pivotal role in restoring mitochondrial function and decreasing glucose consumption under stress conditions. Furthermore, in a murine subcutaneous fat graft model, platelets exhibited a protective effect during the early oxidative stress phase, as evidenced by reduced ROS and malondialdehyde levels, increased glutathione expression, attenuated fibrosis, enhanced graft vascularization, and improved long-term survival. These findings suggest that platelet-mediated mechanisms, including mitochondrial transfer, may contribute to protecting ADSCs and improving fat graft outcomes.Keywords: adipose-derived stem cells, fat transplantation, plastic surgery, platelets

Autologous fat grafting is widely used but limited by high resorption and inadequate volume retention. This study examines platelet–ADSC interactions and indicates that platelet-mediated mitochondrial transfer may enhance ADSC resistance to oxidative stress and support more efficient energy metabolism. In vivo, platelet intervention was associated with reduced graft oxidative stress, improved fat retention, and increased angiogenesis. These findings underscore the importance of mitochondrial quality control and suggest avenues to optimize platelet-based adjuncts and develop platelet-derived therapeutics to improve graft durability.

Introduction

Autologous fat grafting (AFG) is a widely utilized technique in plastic surgery, favored for its extensive tissue availability and absence of immune rejection. However, AFG is often challenged by a high rate of fat resorption and insufficient volume retention.1 Previous studies have demonstrated that, due to early ischemia and hypoxia, fat masses exceeding 1200 μm in diameter may undergo central necrosis. Four weeks post-transplantation, the implanted fat tissue typically differentiates into 3 distinct zones: survival, regeneration, and necrosis.2,3 One week after grafting, capillary formation begins in the survival zone, reaching its peak at 4 weeks, followed by adipocyte regeneration. Some hypotheses propose that fat graft survival primarily depends on cell regeneration, with adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) playing a pivotal role in this process.4,5 Given that hypoxia and oxidative stress prior to neovascularization significantly impair ADSC viability and function, it is essential to develop strategies to enhance ADSC resilience under these conditions.6

Platelets, originating from bone marrow megakaryocytes, are integral to thrombosis, hemostasis, inflammation, and wound healing.7,8 Among platelet concentrates, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is commonly employed in plastic surgery to improve fat graft retention.9,10 PRP, derived from autologous blood, contains a high concentration of platelets and multiple growth factors, offering favorable clinical potential due to its immunocompatibility, biocompatibility, and cost-effectiveness.11 Despite its extensive application, the precise mechanism underlying PRP's efficacy remains partially understood. Emerging evidence indicates that platelet concentrates mitigate oxidative stress across various cell types, including skeletal muscle cells, gingival fibroblasts, and human sperm, and may reduce nephrotoxicity.12,13 Recent findings reveal that platelets can release mitochondria, which subsequently transfer to mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), enhancing their angiogenic potential via metabolic reprogramming.14 In wound healing, platelets have been shown to protect fibroblasts and endothelial cells from oxidative damage through mitochondrial transfer.15,16 These insights suggest that mitochondrial release constitutes a fundamental aspect of platelet-mediated protection against oxidative stress, offering a potential explanation for how platelet-based products safeguard ADSCs in hypoxic environments.

Mitochondrial transfer represents a critical mode of intercellular communication. Initially identified in 2006, it was observed that mitochondria derived from bone marrow stromal cells could migrate to mitochondria-deficient A549 lung cancer cells, thereby restoring aerobic respiration.17 Subsequent research has established that mitochondrial transfer occurs across diverse physiological systems, including the central nervous, cardiovascular, and respiratory systems, where it promotes stem cell function and modulates therapy resistance and inflammation.18,19 This phenomenon provides valuable insights into the role of platelets in enhancing MSC functionality and underscores the need to evaluate platelet mitochondrial integrity before clinical application.20 The importance of mitochondria has been increasingly recognized in recent years, with research on mitochondrial therapy using biological carriers being continuously refined for various disease models. However, there remains a significant gap in our understanding of mitochondrial transfer between platelets and adipose-derived stem cells in fat grafting models, despite its potential therapeutic significance.

This study aims to elucidate the interaction between platelets and ADSCs, with a focus on the role of mitochondrial transfer in augmenting the antioxidant capacity of ADSCs during fat transplantation. By revealing the underlying mechanisms, this research not only highlights the significance of mitochondrial quality but also establishes a theoretical framework for enhancing fat graft survival through platelet-based interventions, thus, informing more effective clinical applications.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) were isolated from subcutaneous adipose tissue obtained from 15 healthy female donors (25-40 years) undergoing elective plastic surgery. The cells were cultured in a complete ADSC growth medium, with all procedures approved by the Ethics Committee in Clinical Research (ECCR) of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Issuing Number: 2025R116) following informed consent from donors. Cells were maintained under standard conditions, with passages performed every 2-3 days, and cell morphology was monitored using an inverted phase-contrast microscope.

Preparation and activation of platelet concentrate

Whole blood from healthy volunteers was collected into EDTA-coated tube s to prevent coagulation. For any experiment in which ADSCs and platelets were used together, the ADSCs and platelet concentrates were obtained from the same donor (autologous pairing); no cross-donor mixing was performed. And all procedures approved by the Ethics Committee in Clinical Research (ECCR) of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Issuing Number: 2025R116). Platelet concentrate was obtained through a double centrifugation technique and subsequently activated via enzymatic methods to optimize platelet functionality.

Platelet mitochondrial inhibition

Freshly prepared human platelets were exposed to rotenone and antimycin A for 3 h at 37 °C with 2 μM rotenone and 2 μM antimycin A to suppress mitochondrial respiration. Briefly, platelets were incubated. Following inhibitor exposure, both untreated and ROT/AA-treated platelets were washed 3 times in serum-free DMEM supplemented with heparin (20 U/mL). After the final wash, platelets were resuspended and activated using our routine protocol.

Establishment of ADSC oxidative stress model

Oxidative stress in ADSCs was induced using H2O2. A concentration gradient ranging from 50 mM to 200 mM H2O2 was evaluated to determine optimal conditions. Cell viability assays identified 150 mM H2O2 exposure for 24 h as significantly impairing viability, thereby establishing the oxidative stress model. Microscopic analysis revealed marked morphological alterations consistent with oxidative stress, confirming the model’s validity. This experiment was performed with 4 biological replicates (n = 4).

Mitochondrial transfer fluorescent labeling

A 1 mM Mito-Tracker Red stock solution was diluted to a final concentration of 20 nM by adding it to the platelet concentrate at a 1:50 000 ratio. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 45 min. Following incubation, the platelets were washed with heparin-supplemented serum-free DMEM and co-cultured with ADSCs for 24 h. ADSCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 10 μM DiO. This experiment was performed with 3 biological replicates (n = 3). After staining, the cells were washed with PBS and visualized using a fluorescence microscope.

Cell migration assay

ADSC migration was assessed using Transwell chambers. Cells were cultured in stem cell medium under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2), with medium changes every 2 days. Upon reaching 90% confluence, cells were harvested, seeded into the upper chamber, and subjected to H2O2 induction, followed by serum starvation for 24 h. Treatment groups included PBS, 5% platelet concentrate, and 10% platelet concentrate. After 12 h of treatment, non-migrated cells were removed, and migrated cells were fixed, stained, and quantified using ImageJ software. This experiment was performed with 3 biological replicates (n = 3).

Cell proliferation assay

To investigate the influence of platelet mitochondrial transfer on ADSCs under oxidative stress, cells were seeded at a density of 2000 cells per well in 96-well plates. Following a 24-h adhesion period, oxidative stress was induced. Subsequently, cells were treated with PBS, platelet concentrate, or platelet concentrate pretreated with a mitochondrial transfer inhibitor. The proliferation rate was assessed using CCK-8 assays at 1, 2, 4, and 7 days, with optical density measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer. This experiment was performed with 3 biological replicates (n = 3).

Measurement of mitochondrial function and glucose metabolism

Intracellular ATP levels were quantified using a chemiluminescence analyzer. ADSCs (5 × 103 cells per group) were cultured in white 96-well plates for 24 h, and ATP content was assessed using a luminescent ATP detection kit. Glucose consumption was calculated by comparing the glucose concentration before and after culture, with absorbance measured at 630 nm. The glucose concentration was determined using a standard calibration curve. This experiment was performed with 3 biological replicates (n = 3).

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from ADSCs using lysis buffer, and protein concentration was determined via the BCA assay. Proteins were denatured, resolved through SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Following transfer, membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were exposed to secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. This experiment was performed with 3 biological replicates (n = 3). Protein signals were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system, and band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

In vivo fat grafting experiment

A total of twenty 4-week-old male athymic nude mice were utilized for the in vivo model. Following isoflurane anesthesia, minced adipose tissue was prepared. Three subcutaneous dorsal points per mouse were injected with 500 μL of lipoaspirate admixed with 10 μL of PBS, platelet concentrate, or mitochondrial inhibitor-pretreated platelet concentrate, with site allocation randomized. Grafts were examined at 1, 2, 4, and 6 weeks post-injection, with observations documented through photography, volume measurement, and biochemical analysis of antioxidant markers. Immunohistochemical staining was performed to assess graft quality and integration.

Flow cytometry for apoptosis detection

Apoptosis was analyzed using the Annexin V-APC apoptosis detection kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After experimental treatments, ADSCs, either subjected to oxidative stress or untreated, were co-cultured with PBS, platelet concentrate, or mitochondrial inhibitor-pretreated platelet concentrate for 24 h. Cells were harvested, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and resuspended in 500 μL of binding buffer at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Annexin V-APC (5 μL) and 7-AAD (10 μL) were added, and the mixture was gently vortexed and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 10 min. This experiment was performed with 3 biological replicates (n = 3). Apoptosis rates were immediately assessed using flow cytometry.

Live/dead staining

Cells were seeded on cell slides and co-cultured with PBS, platelet concentrate, or mitochondrial inhibitor-pretreated platelet concentrate for 24 h after being treated with or without oxidative stress. The culture medium was removed, and cells were washed once with PBS. Calcein AM/PI working solution was added, with volumes adjusted for different plates, and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Initial incubation time was 30 min, adjustable based on staining results. Finally, observe staining under a fluorescence microscope: Calcein AM shows green fluorescence, and PI shows red fluorescence. This experiment was performed with 3 biological replicates (n = 3). Keep the process light-protected, place samples on ice after staining, and perform flow cytometry analysis within 1 h.

Statistical analysis

Within each biological replicate, measurements were made in triplicate wells and averaged to produce a single data point for statistical analysis. Data were analyzed using SPSS 23.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.0.1. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Results

Isolation, identification, and differentiation of ADSCs, and preparation of platelet concentrate

Primary ADSCs were isolated from human abdominal adipose tissue. Initially, these cells exhibited a short, small morphology. Upon 3 to 5 passages, the cells progressively acquired a spindle-shaped or fibroblast-like morphology, displaying increased elongation and a gradual decline in proliferative capacity with successive passages (Figure 1A). Flow cytometry of third-passage cells demonstrated positive expression of surface markers CD44 (100%), CD90 (99.9%), and CD29 (100%), while markers CD31 (0.23%) and CD45 (0.03%) remained negative, confirming that the cell population predominantly comprised stem cells (Figure 1B).

(C) Adipogenic, osteogenesis, and chondrogenic induction of ADSCs. (D) The flowchart of ADSCs and Platelets concentrates preparation. Image created with BioRender.com, with permission. To evaluate the differentiation potential of ADSCs, cells were cultured in adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation media. After an incubation period of 3-4 weeks, the media were aspirated, and the cells were washed with PBS. Fixation was performed using a 4% neutral formaldehyde solution at room temperature for 30 to 60 min. Subsequent to fixation, cells were washed twice with PBS and stained with Oil Red O, Alizarin Red, and Alcian Blue. Microscopic analysis revealed successful staining of lipid droplets, calcium deposits, and acidic mucopolysaccharides, demonstrating the capacity of ADSCs to differentiate into adipocytes, osteoblasts, and chondrocytes, respectively (Figure 1C).

For platelet concentrate preparation, whole blood was obtained from volunteers and processed via a 2-step centrifugation protocol (300 g for 7 min followed by 1000 g for 20 min) to isolate the concentrate (Figure 1D). Platelet counts, determined using a hemocytometer, indicated an initial platelet concentration of 4 × 108/mL. The concentrate was subsequently diluted to achieve 2 distinct platelet concentrations: [Plts 1] at 2 × 107/mL and [Plts 2] at 4 × 107/mL, which were utilized in subsequent cellular and animal assays.

These results substantiate the successful isolation of ADSCs, validation of their stemness and differentiation capacities, and the efficient preparation of platelet concentrates for experimental applications.

Platelets enhance cell viability of ADSCs

To investigate the impact of platelets on ADSCs, platelets were co-cultured with ADSCs for 24 and 72 h, and cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Glucose consumption was evaluated by comparing glucose levels in the culture medium before and after 24 h of co-culture. The data demonstrated that platelet co-culture significantly improved ADSC viability after both 24 and 72 h (Figure 2A). Furthermore, ADSCs exposed to platelets exhibited a more pronounced reduction in glucose concentration compared to the control group, indicating enhanced glucose metabolism (Figure 2B).

To examine the influence of platelets on ADSC migration, Transwell and scratch assays were performed. The Transwell assay indicated that higher platelet concentrations significantly promoted cell viability and increased cell migration to the lower chamber in a dose-dependent manner after 8 h of exposure (Figure 2C). In the scratch assay, the platelet co-culture group achieved 70%-80% confluence at 12 h and complete confluence at 24 h, with no statistically significant differences between groups. Notably, the scratch assay findings differed from those of the Transwell assay, as platelets enhanced cell migration at both low and high concentrations (Figure 2D). Based on these outcomes, a higher concentration of platelet concentrate (Plts 2) was selected for subsequent experiments.

Following the co-culture of ADSCs with varying platelet concentrations, Western blot analysis was conducted to quantify the expression of Cox IV, a mitochondrial inner membrane protein associated with aerobic respiration and ATP production. The results revealed a dose-dependent increase in mitochondrial protein expression in ADSCs following platelet treatment (Figure 2E). These results indicate that platelets exert positive effects on ADSCs, including enhanced proliferation, migration, metabolic activity, and mitochondrial function. In light of these beneficial effects, the study further investigated the protective role of platelets in an oxidative stress model and the underlying mitochondrial mechanisms.

Platelets enhance ADSCs resistance to oxidative stress

To evaluate the protective effect of platelets against oxidative stress in ADSCs, oxidative stress was induced in vitro using H2O2. ADSCs were exposed to varying H2O2 concentrations, followed by medium replacement and continued culture for 24 or 48 h. Microscopic examination revealed significant morphological alterations and cellular damage in ADSCs subjected to oxidative stress, characterized by a marked reduction in cell density compared to untreated controls. CCK-8 assay results indicated that high H2O2 concentrations (100, 150, and 200 mM) significantly compromised ADSC viability. An inverse relationship was observed between H2O2 concentration and cell proliferation, with morphological changes becoming increasingly evident at higher concentrations. Conversely, low H2O2 concentration (50 mM) did not significantly affect cell viability after 24 h and 48 h relative to the control group (Figure 3A). Based on these observations, 150 mM H2O2 was selected for subsequent experiments to induce oxidative stress while minimizing excessive cell death.

(B) CCK-8 assay determination of cell viability in ADSCs co-cultured with platelets under varying degrees of oxidative stress. ***P < .001, ****P < .0001.

To investigate the protective role of platelets in ADSCs under oxidative stress, ADSCs were treated with varying H2O2 concentrations to simulate oxidative conditions. Following medium replacement, ADSCs were co-cultured with platelets for 24 h, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS. CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that platelets mitigated oxidative stress-induced damage, preserving higher ADSC viability across multiple oxidative stress levels (Figure 3B).

Platelets modulate ADSC mitochondrial function and metabolism through mitochondrial transferring

To visualize mitochondrial transfer, MitoTracker® Deep Red was used to label platelet mitochondria, while DiO green fluorescent dye stained ADSC membranes. After 24 h of co-culture, fluorescence microscopy confirmed the translocation of red-fluorescent mitochondria from platelets to ADSCs, an effect that was more pronounced under oxidative stress induced by H2O2. In contrast, mitochondrial transfer was significantly inhibited when platelets were pre-treated with mitochondrial inhibitors (Figure 4A).

Western blot analysis quantified mitochondrial membrane transport proteins Cox IV and TOMM20. Oxidative stress markedly impaired ADSC mitochondrial function, as evidenced by a substantial reduction in TOMM20 expression without significant alteration in Cox IV levels. Co-culture with platelets effectively restored mitochondrial function, as indicated by the upregulation of both TOMM20 and Cox IV. However, this protective effect was absent when platelets were pre-treated with mitochondrial inhibitors, indicating that platelet-mediated mitochondrial transfer is essential for maintaining ADSC mitochondrial function (Figure 4B).

To further investigate changes in mitochondrial activity and cellular energy metabolism, glucose consumption and ATP production were assessed. Following 4 h of H2O2 exposure, glucose consumption was quantified using specific assay kits. Data indicated an increase in glucose uptake under oxidative stress (Figure 4C). Notably, co-culture with platelets reduced glucose consumption in oxidative stress-induced ADSCs, an effect that was not observed when platelets were treated with mitochondrial inhibitors. Additionally, ATP levels were elevated, reflecting improved cellular energy metabolism (Figure 4C).

These results confirm the intercellular mitochondrial transfer from platelets to ADSCs and elucidate its positive impact on mitochondrial functionality and metabolic regulation. The subsequent analysis focused on the phenotypic adaptation of ADSCs under oxidative stress following the acquisition of platelet-derived mitochondria, further substantiating the critical role of mitochondrial transfer in protecting cells from oxidative stress damage.

Platelets enhance ADSC resistance to oxidative stress through mitochondrial transferring

To validate the mitochondrial role in platelet-mediated protection of ADSCs, platelets were pre-treated with mitochondrial inhibitors (ROT/AA) to impair mitochondrial function. Subsequently, ADSCs were co-cultured with untreated platelets, inhibitor-treated platelets, or PBS (control) following oxidative stress induction. Untreated platelets conferred protection against oxidative stress-induced damage, preserving higher cell viability, whereas inhibitor-treated platelets failed to provide comparable protection (Figure 5A and B). These observations indicate that mitochondrial transfer is integral to platelet-mediated protection against oxidative stress.

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were quantified via flow cytometry. H2O2-induced oxidative stress markedly elevated intracellular ROS levels. While untreated platelets significantly reduced ROS accumulation, inhibitor-treated platelets did not, underscoring the necessity of platelet mitochondrial function for mitigating oxidative stress in ADSCs (Figure 5C).

To assess oxidative stress-induced apoptosis, apoptosis rates in ADSCs were examined. Platelets markedly decreased apoptosis, whereas inhibitor-treated platelets exhibited no such effect (Figure 5B). Calcein AM and propidium iodide (PI) staining visually differentiated live and dead cells, revealing that platelets inhibited oxidative stress-induced apoptosis, a protective effect absents in the presence of inhibitor-treated platelets (Figure 5D and E). Molecular analyses of apoptosis-related proteins demonstrated that oxidative stress reduced Bcl-2 expression while elevating Cleaved caspase-3 and Bax levels. Co-culture with platelets restored Bcl-2 levels and decreased Cleaved caspase-3 and Bax expression, whereas inhibitor-treated platelets did not elicit these molecular changes (Figure 5G).

These results indicate that H2O2-induced oxidative stress elevates ROS levels, promotes apoptosis, and impairs proliferative capacity in ADSCs. Platelets enhance the antioxidative resilience of ADSCs, a capacity that is compromised when mitochondrial function within platelets is inhibited. These results substantiate the hypothesis that platelet-mediated mitochondrial transfer is crucial for protecting ADSCs from oxidative stress. Further investigations in a nude mouse fat grafting model aim to corroborate the role of platelet mitochondrial transfer in enhancing fat graft viability.

Antioxidative effect of platelets in early stages of fat grafting

During the early post-fat grafting phase (1-2 weeks), oxidative stress was assessed via ROS fluorescence staining, tissue MDA, and GSH level measurements. Due to the hypoxic environment during this period, early ROS detection is particularly relevant. The results revealed no significant differences in ROS fluorescence intensity among groups at both 1 and 2 weeks. However, the platelet group demonstrated reduced ROS ratios compared to the control and inhibitor groups, with the most pronounced difference observed at 2 weeks (Figure 6C and D). Oxidative stress and antioxidative capacity were further evaluated by measuring MDA and GSH levels, respectively. The platelet group exhibited significantly lower MDA levels and higher GSH levels compared to the control and inhibitor groups, indicating a robust antioxidative effect facilitated by platelets (Figure 6E and F).

Platelets enhance long-term fat graft survival through mitochondrial transfer

To substantiate the role of platelet mitochondrial transfer in fat grafting, a nude mouse subcutaneous fat grafting model was established, comprising control, platelet, and inhibitor-treated platelet groups. Processed fat tissue was injected subcutaneously into the dorsal region, and successful fat grafting was confirmed by the absence of notable volume reduction and fat leakage at the injection site.

Graft volume and weight were monitored at 1, 2, 4, and 6 weeks post-grafting (Figure 7A). At 1 week, graft retention rates did not differ significantly between groups. By 2 weeks, the platelet group demonstrated increased graft weight, while the control and inhibitor groups exhibited decreasing trends. The platelet group consistently maintained higher graft retention rates at subsequent time points (Figure 7B-D).

Inhibition of mitochondrial function nullifies these beneficial effects, indicating that mitochondrial transfer mediated by platelets is fundamental to improving fat graft outcomes. This study supports the potential application of platelet-mediated mitochondrial transfer in optimizing adipose tissue grafting strategies.

Discussion

Fat grafting is a prevalent autologous tissue reconstruction technique favored for its numerous advantages, including abundant donor availability, minimally invasive harvesting, rapid recovery, favorable tissue compatibility, and the absence of foreign body reactions.21 Clinically, it is utilized for wrinkle filling, facial contour enhancement, and breast augmentation. Despite its wide application, fat grafting encounters significant challenges, primarily high post-grafting absorption rates that often necessitate multiple procedures to achieve satisfactory outcomes. This not only extends the patient’s treatment and recovery duration but also escalates the financial burden. Additionally, complications such as fibrosis and cyst formation may occur. Consequently, advancing efficient fat grafting methods and supportive strategies remains a critical focus of research to overcome these limitations.

The “cell replacement theory” posits that ADSCs are pivotal for fat graft survival.1 Following grafting, the majority of adipocytes undergo necrosis within the first 24 h, except those situated in peripheral regions, with subsequent adipocyte regeneration driven by ADSC differentiation.22 Neoangiogenesis typically initiates 5-7 days post-grafting, subjecting transplanted fat particles to hypoxia during the early phase. In this hypoxic microenvironment with limited perfusion, stress-induced apoptosis of cells significantly impairs ADSC differentiation and adipocyte regeneration, leading to the long-term volume reduction of fat grafts.23,24 Furthermore, evidence suggests that adipocyte resistance to hypoxia and macrophage activation critically influence graft survival. This study investigates the role of platelet-mediated mitochondrial transfer in enhancing the capacity of fat grafts during the early phase, with the aim of identifying efficient and accessible antioxidants to mitigate early ADSC apoptosis under adverse conditions and improve the long-term persistence of fat grafts.25

Platelets, derived from the fragmentation of megakaryocytes, are anucleate cytoplasmic fragments containing mitochondria, microtubules, and granules. Upon activation, platelets release various growth factors, initiating biological processes such as chemotaxis, proliferation, differentiation, and angiogenesis. PRP has been employed to enhance the viability of particulate fat grafts, as previous studies have demonstrated its beneficial effects on cell viability, proliferation, and migration. Preliminary experiments in this study revealed that platelet co-culture with ADSCs promoted glucose uptake and enhanced mitochondrial function, indicating that mitochondrial function and metabolic adaptation are integral to platelet–ADSC interactions. Emerging evidence suggests that platelets can release mitochondria and transfer them to MSCs, thereby enhancing their vascular differentiation through metabolic reprogramming.3,26 Additionally, mitochondrial transfer from platelets to fibroblasts and endothelial cells under oxidative stress conditions has been shown to confer cytoprotection during wound healing.6,9 Based on these findings, this study hypothesizes that platelets convey bioactive substances, including mitochondria, to ADSCs, thereby modulating their metabolic profile and providing protection against oxidative stress.

To mimic an ischemic-hypoxic environment, an oxidative stress model was established in ADSCs by exposing them to H2O2.27 This oxidative stress compromised mitochondrial integrity, resulting in electron leakage from the respiratory chain and the generation of substantial ROS.28 Consequently, ADSCs exhibited morphological alterations, diminished cell viability and proliferation, impaired migratory capacity, and ultimately underwent apoptosis. To counteract the oxidative damage, platelet concentrates were administered, effectively reducing intracellular ROS levels and promoting ADSC viability. These findings align with previous reports demonstrating that platelet concentrates alleviate oxidative stress in fibroblasts, endothelial cells, gingival fibroblasts, and human sperm by decreasing intracellular ROS.3,6,9,12,13 The current study confirms the beneficial role of platelets in preserving ADSC function, which has implications for enhancing the long-term survival of fat grafts.

Previous studies have shown that platelets are capable of transferring mitochondria to MSCs through Dynamin-dependent clathrin-mediated endocytosis, thereby initiating mitochondrial fusion within recipient MSCs.29 Mitochondrial transfer has also been documented in co-cultures of platelets with endothelial cells and fibroblasts, with the process being notably more active under H2O2-induced oxidative stress. In the present study, platelets were pre-labeled with MitoTracker and co-cultured with DiO-labeled ADSCs for 24 h. Fluorescence microscopy revealed the transfer of mitochondria from platelets to ADSCs, with increased mitochondrial transfer observed under oxidative stress conditions. In contrast, when platelet mitochondrial function was inhibited, the extent of mitochondrial transfer to both unstressed and oxidative stress-induced ADSCs was significantly reduced. Furthermore, platelets enhanced the expression of mitochondrial-related proteins within oxidative stress-induced ADSCs, thereby improving mitochondrial function. The observed mitochondrial transfer between platelets and ADSCs suggests that platelets serve as a potential source of mitochondrial preparations, capable of restoring mitochondrial functionality in damaged cells and thereby modulating the biological activity of stem cells.

Cellular energy metabolism is intrinsically linked to mitochondrial function.30 To further investigate mitochondrial function and energy metabolism alterations in both normal and oxidative stress-induced ADSCs following the uptake of platelet-derived mitochondria, a mitochondrial inhibitor group was established using platelets pre-treated with mitochondrial inhibitors (ROT/AA).30,31 ROT inhibits complex I (NADH dehydrogenase), blocking electron transfer from NADH to ubiquinone, while AA inhibits complex III (cytochrome bc1), blocking transfer from cytochrome b to cytochrome c1. ROT binds complex I with high affinity and dissociates very slowly; when complexes I and III are blocked, glycolytic compensation is limited. In line with prior reports, a 3-h ROT/AA treatment produces a durable, functionally irreversible suppression of mitochondrial respiration, facilitating our downstream analyses. This approach aimed to confirm the pivotal role of mitochondria in the observed metabolic changes. Measurements of glucose consumption and ATP levels revealed that oxidative stress significantly increased glucose consumption. Intriguingly, co-culture of oxidative stress-induced ADSCs with platelets led to reduced glucose consumption, an effect not observed when co-cultured with inhibitor-treated platelets. Furthermore, ATP levels increased in ADSCs after co-culture with platelets but remained unchanged with inhibitor-treated platelets. This phenomenon can be attributed to the enhanced glycolytic flux observed in MSCs under hypoxic conditions, which decreases the respiration-to-glycolysis ratio. Platelet-mediated mitochondrial transfer likely reprograms the energy metabolism of MSCs, promoting the TCA cycle and citrate-dependent fatty acid synthesis.3 Collectively, these findings indicate that platelets restore mitochondrial function in oxidative stress-compromised ADSCs, facilitating a metabolic shift toward more efficient cellular respiration, thereby reducing glucose consumption in hypoxic environments and increasing ATP production, which supports cell proliferation and differentiation.32

To further substantiate the role of mitochondrial transfer in platelet-mediated protection against oxidative stress-induced ADSC damage, ADSCs subjected to oxidative stress were co-cultured with PBS, untreated platelets, or mitochondrial inhibitor-treated platelets. Remarkably, inhibitor-treated platelets failed to confer the same protective effects as untreated platelets. Microscopic examination revealed pronounced oxidative stress-induced morphological alterations in ADSCs co-cultured with inhibitor-treated platelets. Moreover, the CCK-8 assay demonstrated significant reductions in cell viability under oxidative stress, indicating that mitochondrial integrity is essential for platelet-mediated protection of ADSCs. To directly assess the impact of platelets on oxidative stress levels, intracellular ROS levels were compared across groups. Following H2O2 induction, ROS levels markedly increased. While untreated platelets significantly mitigated ROS accumulation, inhibitor-treated platelets had no such effect, suggesting that platelet-mediated mitochondrial transfer plays a pivotal role in enhancing mitochondrial function and reducing oxidative stress. Additionally, platelets markedly downregulated apoptosis-related gene expression, thereby reducing cell apoptosis, an effect absents with inhibitor-treated platelets. These findings underscore the critical role of mitochondrial function in platelet-mediated inhibition of oxidative stress-induced apoptosis in ADSCs. The results demonstrate that platelets restore mitochondrial functionality through mitochondrial transfer, thereby enhancing ADSC resilience to oxidative stress and preventing early apoptosis in oxidative stress conditions.

To validate the antioxidative effects of platelets in the early stages of fat grafting, histological analysis of fat tissue was conducted within the first 2 weeks post-grafting. During this period, the vascular system remained undeveloped, and the tissue was subjected to peak oxidative stress.33 The study observed no significant difference in ROS ratios between the platelet and control groups within the first week. However, at 2 weeks, the ROS ratio in the platelet group was markedly lower than in the control and inhibitor groups. Additionally, the platelet group exhibited reduced levels of oxidative stress markers (MDA) and elevated levels of antioxidative markers (GSH) compared to the control and inhibitor groups, indicating a robust antioxidative capacity conferred by platelets. These findings are consistent with previous wound healing studies that demonstrated the ability of platelets to enhance the antioxidative capacity of fibroblasts and endothelial cells through mitochondrial transfer. The current study directly visualized oxidative stress markers in an animal model, underscoring the antioxidative role of platelets via mitochondrial transfer.

Previous research has explored the co-injection of fat with platelet-related products such as PRP and platelet lysate, showing significant improvements in fat graft survival.18,19,34,35 This effect was corroborated in the nude mouse subcutaneous fat grafting model used in this study. Fat tissue was injected subcutaneously into the dorsal region of the mice, accompanied by equal volumes of PBS, platelet concentrate (Plts), or mitochondrial inhibitor-treated platelet concentrate (Plts/Int). At 1-week post-grafting, no significant difference in fat graft retention rates was observed among the groups. At 2 weeks, however, the graft weight in the platelet group increased, likely due to vascularization and enhanced tissue density. After 2 weeks, graft survival weight gradually declined, yet the platelet group consistently maintained higher graft retention rates compared to the control and inhibitor-treated groups. Histological analysis revealed that at 2 weeks, all 3 groups exhibited large lipid droplets. By 4 weeks, uniform fat cells were observed earlier in the platelet group than in the other groups. Progressive reduction in fibrosis was noted, with the platelet group demonstrating significantly lower fibrosis levels, indicative of improved graft quality.36 CD31 immunohistochemistry, a marker for capillary formation, showed that the platelet group significantly promoted capillary development compared to the control and inhibitor groups.37

These findings suggest that platelets facilitate graft circulation through mitochondrial transfer, fostering a supportive microenvironment for graft regeneration, enhancing cell viability, and reducing fibrosis, thereby leading to improved long-term fat graft survival and a lower incidence of complications such as nodules and calcifications. This study advances our understanding of platelet-assisted fat grafting mechanisms, complementing current approaches using MSCs and MSC-derived microvesicles in various disease models including ischemia-reperfusion injury and neurological disorders.38–40 Our research indicates platelets could serve as delivery vehicles for mitochondrial therapeutics, warranting further investigation into optimal conditions for platelet mitochondrial transfer, including isolation and activation methods to enhance fat graft outcomes. While this study only male mice were studied to reduce hormonal variability. Future work will include both sexes to assess sex-related effects. Also, ADSCs were cultured in vitro and differ from native counterparts, lacking perivascular niche cues including extracellular matrix, oxygen gradients, and mechanical stimuli, which can alter stress susceptibility and accessibility and uptake of platelet-derived mitochondria. Future studies should employ 3-dimensional culture systems, organoid models, and live imaging to more directly recapitulate and visualize ADSC–platelet interactions in fat grafting. Moreover, while this study investigated mitochondrial influences on fat graft outcomes, it did not include an in-depth interrogation of metabolic mechanisms; addressing this will be a focus of future work.

Conclusion

In conclusion, platelets significantly enhance the antioxidative stress capacity of ADSCs through mitochondrial transfer. This process restores impaired mitochondrial function, reduces ROS production, and promotes cellular activity, proliferation, and migration while inhibiting apoptosis. The direct visualization of mitochondrial transfer demonstrated its pivotal role in modulating cellular metabolism and strengthening antioxidative defenses, thereby providing essential energy for cellular functions. In the murine subcutaneous fat grafting model, platelets conferred protection during early oxidative stress, reducing ROS and malondialdehyde levels, increasing glutathione expression, minimizing fibrosis, and improving vascularization and long-term graft survival. These results provide a foundation for developing platelet-derived mitochondrial products to optimize adipose tissue grafting techniques. The study underscores the significant role of mitochondria in platelet-assisted adipose tissue grafting and support the prospect that rigorously characterized, high-quality platelet-derived mitochondria could serve as a low-immunogenic and efficacious strategy to enhance graft survival.
