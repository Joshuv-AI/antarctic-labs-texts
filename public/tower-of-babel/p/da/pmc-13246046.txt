Desquamating Esophagitis in the Era of Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors: A Rare Upper Gastrointestinal Manifestation
Authors: Sanjeevani Tomar, Kegan Jessamy, Dennis Yang, Maham Hayat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDesquamating esophagitis is a rare sloughing disorder of the esophageal mucosa most commonly associated with medications, autoimmune disease, or mucosal injury. Immune checkpoint inhibitors rarely cause esophageal involvement. We report a case of a 65-year-old woman with metastatic cervical cancer who developed progressive dysphagia 3 months after initiation of pembrolizumab. Endoscopy revealed severe proximal stenosis associated with desquamating esophagitis. She required frequent dilations but demonstrated marked and durable resolution after systemic corticosteroid therapy. This case highlights desquamating esophagitis as an uncommon immune-related adverse event and emphasizes that timely immunosuppression can reverse refractory inflammatory stricture.

INTRODUCTION

Desquamating esophagitis, historically termed esophagitis dissecans superficialis, is an uncommon condition characterized by sloughing of superficial squamous epithelium into the esophageal lumen.1 It is typically associated with medications such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and bisphosphonates, caustic or thermal injury, autoimmune disease, and bullous dermatoses.1,2 Endoscopically, it manifests as longitudinal strips or sheets of sloughed mucosa, often with relatively preserved underlying tissue.1 Histologic findings may include intraepithelial splitting, parakeratosis, and superficial necrosis; however, biopsy results are often nondiagnostic because sloughed mucosa can be lost before sampling.1

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including programmed cell death protein-1 inhibitors such as pembrolizumab, enhance antitumor immunity but may result in immune-related adverse events (irAEs).3 Gastrointestinal irAEs most commonly affect the colon, while upper gastrointestinal involvement is less frequently reported.4–6 Esophageal toxicity associated with ICIs ranges from mild esophagitis to ulcerative esophagitis or stricturing disease.6–13 Desquamating esophagitis in this setting remains rare. We report a case of ICI-induced desquamating esophagitis presenting with severe proximal stricture requiring repeated dilation, with durable resolution following corticosteroid therapy.

CASE REPORT

A 65-year-old woman with stage IIIC2 squamous cell carcinoma of the cervix and metastatic disease was treated with chemoradiation followed by systemic therapy including carboplatin, paclitaxel, bevacizumab, and pembrolizumab.

Three months after initiation of pembrolizumab, she developed progressive dysphagia to solids and liquids. She denied caustic ingestion or use of medications commonly associated with desquamating esophageal injury.

Esophagogastroduodenoscopy revealed a severe proximal esophageal stricture with a pinpoint lumen (Figure 1). Owing to significant dysphagia and nutritional compromise, percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube was placed by interventional radiology. The patient was then referred for further endoscopic management. Serial dilations were initiated, which subsequently allowed advancement of the endoscope to fully examine the esophageal lumen. The mucosa appeared normal on initial examination but demonstrated marked friability upon contact. Longitudinal white strips of esophageal mucosa were noted to slough with minimal contact and even with insufflation, findings highly suggestive of desquamating esophagitis (Figure 2). The patient underwent repeated esophagogastroduodenoscopies approximately every 2–4 weeks over the subsequent year, requiring a total of 11 dilation sessions. Although luminal caliber initially improved transiently with mechanical dilation, repeat endoscopy consistently demonstrated contact-induced mucosal sloughing with persistent luminal stenosis (Figure 3). Two separate biopsy evaluations excluded eosinophilic esophagitis, infectious etiologies, and malignancy. Histopathology demonstrated nonspecific squamous mucosal injury without definitive features of esophagitis dissecans superficialis (Figure 4), likely reflecting the superficial and desquamative nature of the process in which sloughed mucosa may not be captured during sampling, which is well described in the literature.1

Given the temporal association occurring 3 months after ICI initiation, characteristic endoscopic findings, and exclusion of alternative causes, ICI-induced desquamating esophagitis was diagnosed. She was treated with systemic corticosteroids in coordination with oncology. She was treated with intravenous methylprednisolone (1–2 mg/kg/d) for 3–5 days, followed by oral prednisone (1 mg/kg/day) with a gradual taper over 4–6 weeks per established guidelines for moderate to severe irAEs. After completion of corticosteroid course, with continued serial dilations, response in integrity of esophageal mucosa and improvement of esophageal stenosis was noted. Her dysphagia progressively resolved. She resumed full oral intake and tolerates a solid diet without difficulty. The PEG tube was removed, and follow-up endoscopy demonstrated a widely patent esophagus without recurrent stenosis. She now requires dilation only as needed and continues clinical surveillance. She remains on pembrolizumab, with the dosing schedule modified by extending the infusion interval, thereby reducing cumulative treatment intensity.

DISCUSSION

This case illustrates severe ICI-induced desquamating esophagitis presenting with refractory proximal stricture and prolonged need for endoscopic dilation. Although colitis represents the most common gastrointestinal irAE, upper gastrointestinal manifestations are increasingly recognized as ICI use expands across malignancies.4–6

ICI-associated esophageal injury has been described in multiple phenotypes, including erosive esophagitis, ulcerative esophagitis, and stricturing disease.6–14 In systematic analyses, esophageal involvement remains uncommon but clinically significant.5 Panneerselvam et al reported that ICI-related esophagitis may present with dysphagia, odynophagia, or ulceration and can progress to stricture formation.14 Case reports describe severe stenosis after nivolumab therapy requiring repeated dilation, sometimes refractory to corticosteroids.10,12,13 Rarely, alternative immunomodulators such as tocilizumab have been required in steroid-refractory cases.15

Desquamating patterns of injury are particularly rare in this context.6–8 Hart et al proposed endoscopic diagnostic criteria for desquamating esophagitis, including (i) strips of sloughed mucosa greater than 2 cm, (ii) normal underlying mucosa, and (iii) absence of ulceration or adjacent friability.1 Biopsy is not mandatory but is recommended in symptomatic patients or when alternative diagnoses must be excluded. In our patient, characteristic longitudinal sloughing was present, although marked friability and inflammatory stenosis suggested a more aggressive immune-mediated phenotype.

The latency of gastrointestinal irAEs is variable but often occurs within the first several months of therapy, consistent with the 3-month interval observed here.3,5 Mechanistically, programmed cell death protein-1 blockade enhances T-cell activation and may promote epithelial apoptosis and mucosal inflammation.3,16 Superficial epithelial necrosis and intraepithelial splitting may produce the sloughing characteristic of desquamating esophagitis.1 Persistent inflammation likely contributed to subepithelial fibrosis and proximal stricture formation in this case.

Importantly, the prolonged need for 11 dilation sessions over approximately 1 year underscores the inflammatory severity. While some reported cases respond to topical therapies such as oral viscous budesonide, systemic corticosteroids remain first-line treatment for moderate to severe irAEs.3,9,11 In this patient, corticosteroid therapy resulted in durable clinical and endoscopic remission, with complete cessation of dilation requirements and PEG removal. This clinical course supports immune-mediated rather than fixed fibrotic etiology.

This case expands the spectrum of ICI-associated esophageal injury and highlights a severe desquamating phenotype leading to refractory stricture. Early recognition is essential, as timely immunosuppression may reverse inflammatory stenosis, reduce procedural burden, and prevent chronic fibrosis. As immunotherapy use continues to grow, gastroenterologists should maintain awareness of desquamating esophagitis as a rare but clinically significant immune-related adverse event.
