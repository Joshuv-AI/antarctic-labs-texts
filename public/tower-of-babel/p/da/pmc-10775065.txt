Discussion to: Impact of reintervention after index aortic valve replacement on the risk of subsequent mortality
Published: 2023

See Article page 93.

Presenter: Dr William Shi

Dr Alberto Pochettino(Rochester, Minn). Thank you. I appreciate your talk. And although here we mostly have talked thus far in young people, the mean age of your group is 73 years, which is more of the average aortic valve replacement population that we deal with. And I think in light of what has been discussed through the session, this brings out that repeat intervention is not benign. We just heard from the previous talker that we can do 1 procedure, then a value-in-valve procedure, then another procedure, and another valve-in valve procedure. And every time you intervene, according to your data, you lose some life. There is a price to pay in ultimate mortality. So, my first question is, why, for example, do you think that valve-in-valve reintervention has the same risk of long-term mortality as surgical reintervention? So that's the first question. Why do you think that is?

Dr William Shi(New York, NY). Well, I think it's difficult to tell directly from our data, but I would suspect that it's related to, first of all, procedural risks. Even though valve-in-valve is a pretty noninvasive procedure, things can go wrong, of course. But I suspect that prosthesis-patient mismatch, that whole “Russian Doll” effect, is probably driving that longer-term reduction in survival.

Dr Pochettino. My second question is, obviously, have you changed or are you going to change your approach to aortic valve replacement vis-à-vis choice of tissue versus mechanical valve as well as, as was discussed by many others, size of the valve? It may be that the valve-in-valve increased mortality is that you start with a small surgical prosthesis that you only can treat in a limited fashion when you do a valve-in-valve procedure. Then you're left with a patient-prosthesis mismatch. So, you have traded structural valve deterioration for patient-prosthesis mismatch, and you still have a mortality increase. What are your thoughts about future approach, how you're going to do?

Dr Shi. That's an excellent question. Certainly, at Brigham, now, knowing what we know, we're much more aggressive with setting up that initial surgical aortic valve replacement for a successful valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve replacement. So that means, essentially, being more liberal with root enlargement. We're certainly really avoiding putting in 21 mm, at least, certainly avoiding putting in 23 mm for younger, larger patients, and being liberal with root enlargement, and really setting up for that valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve replacement so that they can have a longer survival. And of course, especially in patients who are probably in their early 60s, if they're really well, we certainly highlight the potential benefits of mechanical valves. Of course, there is pressure for us to offer tissue valves, but we nonetheless reinforce that. And of course, more recently, we, like everyone else, are becoming more interested in using the Ross procedure in these patients.

Dr Pochettino. I mean, I agree with your approach. I mean, I would make 1 last additional plea: that aortic root replacement, looking at the Gott data from Hopkins, almost approximates the Ross long-term outcomes. So, if you have a young individual who is okay with a mechanical valve, I would be encouraging those of us that can do that operation safely to think root rather than just the valve. Again, the theoretical issue of having all of this stuff that causes thromboembolic complication on the outside and the data that is very compelling that a root replacement in a young person does very well.

Dr Shi. Thank you.
