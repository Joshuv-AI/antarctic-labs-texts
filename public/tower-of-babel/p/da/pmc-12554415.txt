Assessment of Ventriculo‐Arterial Interactions in Early and Undifferentiated Pulmonary Hypertension Using Wave Intensity Analysis
Authors: Sara Louise Hungerford, Gaurav Gulati, Kay Everett, Katherine Kearney, Edmund M Lau, Daniel Burkhoff, Audrey Adji, Navin K Kapur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary wave intensity analysis uses pressure and velocity data to assess the intensity, direction, type, and timing of waves. Its application in early and undifferentiated pulmonary hypertension (PH) to characterize right ventricular (RV)–pulmonary arterial (PA) interactions has not been previously reported.MethodsWe hypothesized that wave intensity and speed would increase with mean PA pressure, while reflected wave timing would remain unchanged. Sixty patients with suspected PH underwent same‐day right heart catheterization and cardiac magnetic resonance. PA pressure and pulmonary vascular resistance were measured via right heart catheterization; PA flow, RV volumes, and function were assessed by cardiac magnetic resonance. Wave intensity analysis was derived from pressure and velocity changes. Linear and logistic regression were used for analysis.ResultsOf the 60 patients (6 with mean PA pressure <20 mm Hg, 11 undifferentiated, 43 confirmed PH), all exhibited forward compression and decompression waves. A backward compression wave was present in 95%, while a backward decompression wave was found in 32%. Intensities of all 4 dominant waves increased with mean PA pressure (P<0.01). Significant differences in reflection coefficient (P=0.04) and wave speed (P=0.01) were observed across PH subgroups. When present, the backward decompression wave showed the strongest associations with RV end‐diastolic (β=−490.8) and end‐systolic volumes (β=−495.9; both P<0.01). Wave speed demonstrated the best performance for identifying abnormal RV end‐diastolic volume and RV end‐systolic volume (area under the curve, 0.73 and 0.80, respectively).ConclusionsIn early and undifferentiated PH, peak wave intensity and speed increases with mean PA pressure, while reflected wave timing is unchanged. Wave intensity analysis metrics may aid in detecting PA stiffening before overt hemodynamic or structural RV changes.Keywords: pulmonary arterial, pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary vascular hemodynamics, pulmonary vascular resistance, right ventricular, wave intensity analysisSubject Categories: Basic Science Research, Hemodynamics, Mechanisms, Physiology, Vascular Biology

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Pulmonary wave intensity analysis offers complimentary insights into right ventricular–pulmonary arterial interactions and may help identify early pulmonary vascular changes, before the onset of overt pressure elevation or right ventricular dilatation occurs; characterizing pathophysiological differences in early and undifferentiated pulmonary hypertension may contribute to our understanding of disease progression, early diagnosis, risk stratification, monitoring, and therapeutic intervention.

In pulmonary hypertension (PH), current thresholds for assessing the risk of right ventricular (RV) failure rely on elevated mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR). However, as the pulmonary circulation is highly compliant with a substantial ability to recruit blood vessels, 50% to 60% of the pulmonary microcirculation may be diseased before a pulmonary artery (PA) pressure rise is manifest. 1 Thus, elevation of mPAP or PVR is a relatively late manifestation of disease, and newer techniques may better define PH in its earliest stages before deleterious changes to RV–PA coupling occur.

Wave intensity analysis (WIA) is a time‐domain method that uses pressure and velocity measurements to assess the intensity, direction, type, and timing of waves. 2 Traveling wavefronts represent energy transmission between the RV and PA. 3 Forward‐traveling waves originate from the RV, while backward‐traveling waves are reflected from the distal pulmonary vasculature. 3 Wavefronts are classified into 4 types 1 : forward compression waves that increase pressure and velocity, 2 forward decompression waves that decrease pressure and velocity, 3 backward compression waves (BCWs) that increase pressure but decrease velocity, 4 and backward decompression waves that decrease pressure but increase velocity. 3 WIA also quantifies wave speed, reflected wave timing (ie, the point in systole when the reflected wave returns from the distal pulmonary vasculature), and reflection site.

Although WIA has been well studied in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), chronic thromboembolic PH, 4 , 5 , 6 and PH from left‐sided heart failure, its role in early and undifferentiated disease has not been reported. In PAH, for example, forward‐traveling wave intensities are typically diminished, with increased BCW intensity. Earlier return of the BCW is seen more commonly in those with severe PAH and proximal (as opposed to distal) thromboembolic disease. In PH due to left‐sided heart failure, BCWs are more commonly observed in the context of elevated PA pressure and reduced arterial compliance, with wave intensity correlating with pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP). 7

Taken together, established differences in WIA measures provide the physiologic basis for its application in early and undifferentiated disease. We hypothesized that increasing mean PA pressure would be associated with greater intensities of all 4 dominant wave patterns, reflecting heightened ventricular ejection force, while the timing of the BCW (but not necessarily wave speed) would remain unchanged in the absence of established pulmonary vascular remodeling.

Methods

Institutional review board approval was obtained for this study: St Vincent's Hospital Human Research and Ethics Committee (LNR/11/SVH/178).

Study Populations

We screened all patients over a 12‐month period who underwent first diagnostic right heart catheterization (RHC) and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) for unexplained dyspnea, before formal hemodynamic classification or provocative testing (eg, exercise, fluid challenge; Figure 1). All patients underwent RHC and CMR within 4 weeks of referral to a specialist PH clinic. Inclusion criteria were (1) digitally stored RV and PA pressure tracings obtained during RHC and (2) availability of same‐day CMR flow data. Exclusion criteria included (1) RHC or CMR study performed on different days; (2) concurrent antihypertensive, vasodilator, or inotrope therapy administered within 24 hours of study; (3) a significant intracardiac shunt (Qp:Qs >1.5); and/or (4) >1+ valvular pathology. 8 Valvular pathology was assessed via baseline transthoracic echocardiography in accordance with American Society of Echocardiography reporting guidelines. Specifically, patients with >1+ tricuspid regurgitation (ie, moderate or severe tricuspid regurgitation) were excluded, based on integrative parameters including vena contracta width, regurgitant jet characteristics, hepatic vein flow, and valve morphology. A total of 60 patients had complete RHC and CMR data sets for subsequent WIA (Figure 1). Institutional Review Board approval was obtained for the study, and all subjects provided informed consent. Study data are available upon request from the corresponding author.

Right Heart Catheterization

Right heart catheterization was performed with patients fasted (but not sedated) in the supine position using a standard 7.5 Fr triple‐lumen Swan‐Ganz catheter (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA) via the right internal jugular vein. The PA pressure waveform was obtained in the main PA ≈0.5 to 1 cm distal to the pulmonary valve. Pressure waveforms were acquired using S5 Collect software (S/5 Collect software, Datex‐Ohmeda, GE Healthcare, Helsinki, Finland) and stored with a sampling frequency of 300 Hz. Consecutive signals of PA pressure during 15 to 20 seconds without large variation were selected (ensuring that ectopic beats and marked respiratory variation were excluded). 8 , 9 , 10 To remove random noise, pressure signals were smoothed by performing an ensemble average of 10 to 15 pressure beats as described previously. 8 , 9 , 10 RHC measurements included right atrial pressure, mPAP, PCWP (end‐expiration, all mm Hg), cardiac output (thermodilution method, L/min), cardiac index (L/min per m2) and RV stroke work index (mm Hg/mL per m2). Precapillary PH was defined by a mPAP ≥20 mm Hg, PCWP ≤15 mm Hg, and PVR >160 dynes·s·cm−5; postcapillary PH was characterized by mPAP ≥20 mm Hg and PCWP >15 mm Hg; combined pre‐ and postcapillary PH was identified by mPAP ≥20 mm Hg, PCWP >15 mm Hg, and PVR >160 dynes·s·cm−5. Those with an mPAP of 20 to 25 mm Hg with a PVR ≤160 dynes·s·cm−5 were classified as undifferentiated, 11 while the remaining subjects were assigned to the mPAP <20 mm Hg group. While the latter demonstrated normal resting PA hemodynamics, all were referred for evaluation of unexplained dyspnea, and thus the presence of early or subclinical pulmonary vascular disease cannot be excluded.

CMR Imaging Acquisition

CMR was performed in a 3T magnet with dedicated phased‐array cardiac coil during successive end‐expiratory breath holds (Siemens Magnetom, Erlangen, Germany). RV volumes were obtained by contouring endocardial and epicardial borders on the short axis at end‐diastolic and end‐systolic phases. Short‐axis images from base to the apex of the RV were obtained with a slice thickness/interslice gap of 5 mm. 8 , 10 , 12 RV ejection fraction was calculated as (RV end‐diastolic volume–RV end‐systolic volume)/RV end‐diastolic volume×100 (expressed as a percentage). 13 , 14 RV cardiac output was calculated as RV stroke volume×heart rate (expressed as L/min). Volumetric flow in the main PA was measured using phase‐contrast velocity quantification. Contours were drawn around the main PA cross‐section semiautomatically, and the average velocity was multiplied by the area of the contour. A volumetric flow curve was plotted with a sampling period of 12 to 45 ms. Both flow and the cine images were retrospectively gated by the ECG ensuring that the entire cardiac cycle was captured. 8 , 10 , 12 All CMR analyses were performed using CVI 42 (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Calgary, Canada).

Hemodynamic Analysis

Pulmonary vascular resistance was estimated as (mean PA pressure–PCWP)/cardiac output (dynes/s per cm−5). Pulmonary arterial compliance (mL/mm Hg) was measured as the ratio of stroke volume to pulse pressure. 15 To calculate pulmonary vascular impedance, pressure and volumetric flow waveforms were decomposed into their component harmonics using a fast Fourier transformation for frequencies up to 10 Hz. Harmonics were excluded if the modulus was <0.1 mm Hg for pressure, or <5 cm3/s for flow. 16 To account for any differences in heart rate between pressure and flow measurements, harmonics were normalized and interpolated linearly diagnosed into the nearest integer frequencies. 16 Volumetric flow was computed as the product of velocity and main PA cross‐sectional area, yielding units of cm3/s. Impedance at each frequency was defined as the ratio of pressure to flow in the frequency domain. Characteristic impedance was calculated as the mean modulus of impedance between the 2nd and 10th harmonics, expressed in units of dynes/s per cm5 to allow for valid comparison with PVR. 8 , 10 , 12 , 17

Wave separation analysis was performed using the methods described by Parker and Jones. 2 Briefly, pressure and velocity waveforms were sampled at 200 Hz using a semiautomated image analysis tool (WebPlotDigitizer version 4.5). Digitized waveforms were smoothed using Savitsky–Golay filters and compared visually to the original waveforms to ensure accuracy. Multiple cardiac cycles were ensemble averaged to obtain a representative cycle. 2 The incremental change in pressure with respect to time (dP) and the incremental change in flow velocity with respect to time (dU) were obtained from the second‐order polynomials of the Savitsky–Golay filter and were separated into forward and backward components using the water hammer equations: dP±=(dP±ρcdU)/2 and dU±=±(dP±ρcdU)/2ρc. 2 Wave intensity, dI±, was calculated as the product of dP± and dU±. Forward compression wave, decompression wave, BCW (detected within 300 ms of the start of the cardiac cycle) and decompression energies were calculated as the time integral of the corresponding waves on the dI versus time plot. 2 Time to peak BCW was normalized by expressing it as a percentage of the total cardiac cycle duration. 2 Reflection coefficient was defined as the absolute value of the ratio of peak forward and BCW energies. Wave speed was estimated using the sum of squares method: c=1/ρ×√∑ΔP2/∑ΔU2, where c is wave speed (m/s), ρ is blood density (typically ≈1050 kg/m3), ΔP is incremental pressure change, and ΔU is incremental flow velocity change. 7 , 18 To estimate reflection site location, we used the relationship L=c·Δt/2, where c is wave speed and Δt the time interval between forward compression wave onset and BCW peak. 2 Independent interobserver agreement (n=5) of WIA measures from our laboratory was high (Pearson correlation coefficient r=0.96, P<0.001; mean difference of −1.24 units; 95% limits of agreement range, −7.37 to 4.89).

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS‐27 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY). Normally distributed variables are presented as mean±SD, while nonnormally distributed variables are reported as median (interquartile range). Baseline characteristics were compared using Student's t tests for normally distributed continuous variables, Mann–Whitney U tests for nonnormally distributed continuous variables, and χ2 or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. One‐way ANOVA and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to compare subgroup means for normally and nonnormally distributed variables, respectively. Pearson's or Spearman's rank correlations were used to assess univariate associations between continuous variables, depending on distribution. Linear regression analysis (standard models) was used to evaluate associations between WIA parameters and continuous clinical or hemodynamic outcomes, including RV volumes and function. β coefficients, SEs, 95% CIs, and P values are reported for all models. Logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis were performed to evaluate the discriminatory performance of selected WIA parameters in classifying binary outcomes (eg, RV dilatation, reduced RV function). Area under the curve (AUC), 95% CI, sensitivity, specificity, and optimal thresholds were reported. Wald χ2, Akaike information criterion, and AUC were used to compare model performance. A 2‐sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Sixty patients who underwent same‐day RHC and CMR were included in the study. Six patients (10%) had an mPAP <20 mm Hg and were included as the reference group. Thirty‐two percent (n=19) were subsequently identified as having precapillary PH, 25% (n=15) isolated postcapillary PH, 15% (n=9) combined pre‐ and postcapillary PH, and 18% (n=11) were classified as undifferentiated.

Baseline patient clinical, RV parameters, and PA hemodynamic characteristics are reported in Tables 1 and 2. As expected, measures of PA pressure (all P<0.001), PVR (P<0.001), and characteristic impedance (P=0.003) were higher in those with pre‐ or combined PH, while arterial compliance was lower (P=0.001). Despite this, there was no significant difference in measures of RV volume (both end‐diastolic and end‐systolic) and function (including cardiac output, ejection fraction, and stroke work index) between PH subgroups (all P>0.05).

Characterization of Wave Propagation

Representative WIAs from 5 individual subjects are shown in Figure 2. Across the study cohort, all subjects (100%) displayed a dominant forward compression wave related to RV contraction. A BCW was present in 95% (n=57) of patients. All patients displayed a forward decompression wave during diastole, while only 32% (n=19) had a backward decompression wave in diastole. Although the backward decompression wave appeared more frequently in those with an mPAP <25 mm Hg (44% versus 27%), this did not reach statistical significance (P=0.43).

Representative wave intensity profiles for subjects across five hemodynamic classifications: mPAP <20 mm Hg, undifferentiated PH, precapillary PH, postcapillary PH, and combined PH. Each panel displays decomposed wave intensity components, including the FCW (blue), FDW (green), BCW (red), and BDW (purple). The red dotted line indicates the timing of peak wave intensity, while the gray dashed line marks the end of the cardiac cycle. While cardiac cycle duration was relatively consistent across groups, distinct patterns in wave magnitude and timing are evident, particularly with regards to reflected (backward) waves. Forward wave intensities (particularly the FCW) were markedly increased in undifferentiated, combined, and postcapillary PH subjects. The BDW was notably absent in the subject with postcapillary PH, while an earlier return of the BCW was observed in the undifferentiated group. These differences highlight physiologically distinct wave reflection patterns across PH subjects. Absolute values and box and whisker plots of WIA parameters, stratified by PH hemodynamic classification, are reported in Table 2 and Figure 3, respectively. Significant between‐group differences in the reflection coefficient were observed between precapillary and postcapillary PH (P=0.04, Mann–Whitney U test), as well as between subjects with an mPAP <20 mm Hg and postcapillary PH (P=0.04, Mann–Whitney U test). Wave speed differed significantly across PH classifications (P=0.01), with higher values in subjects with precapillary (764±442 cm/s) and combined PH (558±344 cm/s) compared with those with PH due to left‐sided disease (269±152 cm/s). Wave speed was also higher in undifferentiated subjects than the mPAP <20 mm Hg group (328±123 versus 514±246 cm/s; P=0.048). No significant between‐group differences in reflection site, BCW intensity or timing (including both absolute and normalized time‐to‐peak) were observed, likely due to high coefficient of variation (all pairwise comparisons P>0.05).

Hemodynamic Determinants

Pearson's or Spearman's rank correlations of WIA parameters against measures of RV volume and function are presented in Table 3. Linear regression analyses are presented in Table 4. Peak intensities of all 4 dominant waves increased with mPAP and were of statistical significance following linear regression analysis (forward compression wave: β= 0.04, SE=0.01 [95% CI, 0.02–0.07]; P=0.001; forward decompression wave: β=0.03, SE=0.00 [95% CI, 0.02–0.04]; P<0.001; BCW: ßβ=–0.01, SE=0.00 [95% CI, –0.01 to −0.00]; P=0.001; backward decompression wave: β=–0.01, SE=0.00 [95% CI, –0.01 to −0.00]; P=0.004). The reflection coefficient also increased with mPAP, but not significantly so. There was a moderate correlation between PVR, backward decompression wave magnitude (r=−0.531, P=0.023, Spearman's correlation) and wave speed (r=0.49, P<0.01, Pearson's correlation) but neither persisted following linear regression analyses (both P<0.05). No other significant association between WIA measures and traditional vascular afterload measures (ie, PVR, arterial compliance, and/or characteristic impedance), was observed (all P>0.05; Table 2).

Discussion

We investigated pulmonary WIA parameters in early and undifferentiated PH disease. To our knowledge, this the largest published cohort to undergo pulmonary WIA. The findings of this study can be summarized as follows: (1) While forward compression and decompression waves were universally present, backward waveforms—particularly the backward decompression wave—were observed less frequently, the latter occurring in only one third of patients and more commonly in those with lower PA pressures; (2) the backward decompression wave demonstrated the strongest associations with RV volumes, outperforming traditional measures such as mean PA pressure and PVR; (3) both the reflection coefficient and wave speed varied significantly by PH subtype and were distinguishable even among patients with normal mean PA pressure; and (4) wave speed demonstrated the highest discriminatory ability for prediction of RV dilatation. Taken together, our findings suggest that WIA may offer complementary insights into pulmonary vascular physiology and RV–PA coupling in the earliest stages of PH disease, before an overt elevation of PA pressure and/or RV dilatation occurs.

Historical Perspectives

WIA was first described in the systemic circulation by Parker and Jones in 1990 2 and later applied to the pulmonary circulation by Lau et al in 2014. 19 In the study by Lau et al, both healthy controls and subjects with precapillary PH consistently displayed a forward compression, forward decompression, and BCW, with increased magnitude and earlier timing of the BCW in subjects with PH, suggesting a link to increased PA stiffening. 19 Subsequent studies, however, have reported the absence of a BCW in both healthy controls and those with PH from left‐sided disease. 6 , 20 In our cohort, forward compression and decompression waves were universally observed. A BCW was present in 95% of subjects and a backward decompression wave in only 25%. These differences may be explained by our recruitment of patients much earlier in their disease course (ie, before formal PH hemodynamic classification). Nevertheless, based on the above studies and our present cohort, it is reasonable to conclude that there exists the universal presence of a forward compression and decompression wave but not a backward compression and decompression wave. Whether the backward decompression wave may be more “characteristic” of healthy pulmonary physiology, reducing PA pressure and increasing flow velocity, requires confirmation in larger control populations.

Prior studies in PH populations have shown that greater disease severity is associated with increased wave intensity across all 4 waveforms. Su et al reported higher wave intensity in both PAH and chronic thromboembolic PH compared with controls, 21 though high variability limited statistical significance in this study. Our study also supports the supposition that wave intensity is elevated in PH, including PH due to left‐sided disease. Importantly, reflected wave timing, specifically absolute or normalized time to peak BCW, did not consistently differentiate PH in early disease. This differs from prior literature. While Lau et al 19 observed earlier peak BCW timing in PH, later studies have yielded mixed results: Su et al 21 found no between‐group differences among PAH and chronic thromboembolic PH subjects; Fukumitsu et al 9 reported earlier wave return only in proximal chronic thromboembolic PH, and Yim et al 6 , 7 found no change in left‐heart disease. On this basis, our findings do not support the assumption drawn from prior studies of the systemic circulation that pulmonary BCW timing is due exclusively to wave reflection from impedance mismatch and/or elevated PCWP. On the other hand, we did observe a significant increase in wave speed in those with undifferentiated, precapillary, and combined PH, indicating that wave speed may be a more reliable marker of early pulmonary vascular disease, before the onset of elevated PA pressure occurs.

Pathophysiological Insights

As outlined earlier, the efficacy of WIA lies in its ability to distinguish incidents originating upstream from those occurring downstream. Whereas forward compression and decompression waves are generated by active RV contraction and relaxation, backward compression and decompression waves originate from distal vascular reflection sites. 22 The type and polarity of reflected waves are determined by the sign of the impedance mismatch (ie, whether the impedance mismatch is positive [transition to a stiffer segment] or negative [transition to a more compliant segment]). A forward compression wave, for example, is reflected as a BCW at a positive reflection site (ie, when encountering a stiffer or narrowed vascular segment), and as a backward decompression wave at a negative reflection site. Conversely, a forward decompression wave is reflected as a backward decompression wave at a positive reflection site and as a BCW at a negative site. Additional insights are gained by examining the relationship between forward and backward wave pairs as discussed further below. Regardless, these wave reflections alter pressure and velocity depending on the direction and type of impedance mismatch and may be influenced by any multitude of factors including pulmonary vascular remodeling, distal arterial stiffness, and left atrial pressure (ie, PCWP). 22

The finding that forward compression and decompression wave magnitudes were positively associated with RV function and volume aligns with physiological expectations. However, exceptions to this trend were observed. In subjects with postcapillary PH, forward wave magnitudes were elevated despite nonsignificantly reduced RVCO. A similar pattern emerged in the undifferentiated PH group, where forward wave intensity and speed were increased despite only modest elevations in mPAP. In both groups, these findings may partly reflect the passive backward transmission of pressure, a phenomenon whereby elevated downstream pressures (eg, in the left atrium) are transmitted retrogradely through the pulmonary vasculature. However, because PA blood flow is driven by the pressure gradient between the PA and PCWP, sustained elevation in PCWP (as occurs in postcapillary PH from left‐sided heart failure) diminishes this gradient, impairs forward flow, and does not itself produce a reflected wave. Instead, BCWs may also arise from transient increases in left atrial or ventricular pressure (eg, a, c, or v waves), which can transmit retrograde hydraulic energy during systole or early diastole. Overall, while these phasic events may distort the PA pressure waveform, their influence on wave reflection timing or the reflection coefficient is likely minimal, and our findings may be further confounded by substantial coefficient variability.

Importantly, among all waveforms, the backward decompression wave demonstrated the strongest and most consistent associations with measures of RV volume and function in our cohort, outperforming conventional hemodynamic markers such as mPAP and PVR. As the backward decompression wave is theorized to result from negative impedance mismatches at distal, more compliant vascular segments, effectively reflecting forward decompression energy in a manner that lowers pressure and augments forward flow, it may represent a physiologically beneficial reflection, akin to a “venting” or decompressive mechanism within the pulmonary vasculature. This interpretation is supported by its selective presence in patients with lower PA pressures, suggesting that the BDW may be more prominent in early‐stage or healthier pulmonary vascular states, where distal vasculature is still preserved. Whether the BDW could serve as a marker of preserved or adaptive RV–PA coupling, and its absence or attenuation might reflect vascular stiffening or unfavorable remodeling that impairs the transmission of beneficial wave energy, requires further evaluation.

The lack of correlation between WIA parameters and conventional measures of vascular afterload—namely, PVR, compliance, and characteristic impedance—is a notable finding. While both forward and backward waves were associated with mPAP, only the backward decompression wave correlated with PVR, consistent with its purported role in reducing pressure and augmenting flow. No associations were found between wave intensity measures and PCWP, contrasting with prior observations in patients with left‐sided heart failure. 7 However, wave speed showed moderate correlations with mPAP, PVR, and characteristic impedance. Additionally, reflection site distance correlated strongly with characteristic impedance and moderately with both mPAP and PCWP. According to classical wave theory, reflected waves follow forward waves at a predictable interval, corresponding to the round‐trip time to a distal impedance mismatch. This expected symmetry was evident in subjects with precapillary PH (eg, Figure 2), likely reflecting discrete, proximal reflection sites arising from vascular stiffening. In contrast, subjects with undifferentiated or postcapillary PH exhibited more variable or delayed backward wave timing, wave speed, and reflection site location. These findings may reflect more complex vascular geometry, diffuse or distal impedance mismatches, or attenuated reflections that are not readily captured by conventional hemodynamic measures. Together, these observations suggest that the presence, timing, and location of reflected waves are influenced not only by PA pressure and standard afterload metrics but also by the spatial pattern of vascular remodeling, particularly in undifferentiated or postcapillary PH, where reflection sites may be more diffuse or less anatomically defined.

Clinical Implications

Our study highlights several potential clinical applications for pulmonary WIA in the early stages of PH. While conventional hemodynamic classifications remain essential for differentiating PH subtypes, WIA may offer additional insights into RV–PA coupling, particularly in early or indeterminate cases, before overt PA pressure elevation or RV dilatation is evident. Notably, neither our study nor previous investigations have clarified how wave intensity evolves in advanced PH, especially in the presence of RV failure. It is plausible that forward wave magnitudes, particularly the forward compression wave, decline with reduced RV contractility, whereas reflected wave dynamics may persist or even intensify with rising pulmonary vascular afterload. Nevertheless, given the observed associations between WIA parameters and RV structure and function, WIA may serve as a noninvasive means to assess RV performance and detect early remodeling before clinical deterioration. This could support risk stratification and guide early therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving RV function. Finally, it is important to note that this study did not include provocative testing, such as exercise RHC, which can reveal pulmonary vascular abnormalities not apparent at rest. Although WIA performance under exercise conditions has not yet been studied, its ability to capture dynamic RV–PA interactions in real time makes it a promising tool for future investigation.

Limitations

To our knowledge, this represents the largest cohort of patients to undergo pulmonary WIA. We did not evaluate the impact of right bundle branch block pattern on WIA parameters. Similarly, the absence of subjects with more advanced PH disease, RV failure, and/or cardiogenic shock limits our ability to fully comprehend the impact of RV function on wave magnitude, as well as the passive influence of PCWP on reflected wave intensity and timing. A subgroup of patients (n=6) with a resting mPAP <20 mm Hg was included as the reference group in this study. These individuals were referred for unexplained dyspnea but demonstrated normal PA pressures and no structural or other hemodynamic abnormalities. While they may not represent true healthy controls, they nevertheless offer a useful comparator for distinguishing WIA features associated with normal versus elevated PA pressure. Pulmonary artery pressure and flow waveforms were acquired sequentially in this study rather than simultaneously. Although this may have introduced some variability due to changes in loading conditions, all patients remained fasted and studied in the supine position, with no significant changes in heart rate or blood pressure were observed between the RHC and CMR studies. Further, PA pressure waveforms were obtained using fluid‐filled catheters rather than micromanometer‐tipped systems. While fluid‐filled systems can introduce a time delay due to wave transmission through the fluid column, this was minimized by aligning the onset of flow and pressure waveforms. High‐fidelity catheters offer improved temporal resolution, but their cost and limited availability remain barriers to routine use and wider adoption. Given the study's exploratory nature, we used univariable linear regression to evaluate each wave intensity parameter individually against RV volume and function measures. Post hoc power analysis indicates that with a sample size of 60, the study was well‐powered (power >99%) to detect moderate effect sizes (r≈0.36) in these simple models. However, we did not fit multivariable models to avoid overfitting and instead compared model performance using Akaike information criterion and P values.

Conclusions

Over the past decade, there has been growing interest in refining pulmonary vascular afterload assessment to better detect early RV dysfunction, before the onset of elevated PA pressures. Our findings suggest that pulmonary WIA may offer meaningful, complementary insights into RV–PA interactions, beyond those provided by traditional hemodynamic markers. Specifically, the backward decompression wave, when present, demonstrated the strongest and most consistent association with RV volumes—outperforming conventional indices such as mean PA pressure and PVR. Additionally, reflection index and wave speed differed significantly across PH subtypes and were able to distinguish early vascular changes and the presence of RV dilatation, even in patients with normal mPAP. Future studies are needed to validate these findings across broader populations and investigate how pulmonary WIA metrics might be integrated into clinical workflows to improve early diagnosis, guide therapy, and refine risk stratification in pulmonary vascular disease.
