Cystatin C regulates cell division in primary central nervous system lymphoma
Authors: Hiroshi Koyama, Kohei Nakajima, Izumi Yamaguchi, Noriya Enomoto, Taku Matsuda, Hiroshi Kagusa, Keiko T. Kitazato, Yasushi Takagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) is associated with cerebral inflammation characterized by elevated neutrophils, monocytes, platelets, and lymphocytes. However, the comprehensive profile of inflammation-related molecules in PCNSL remains unclear. In this study, we conducted proteomic analysis of PCNSL tissue compared with normal brain to identify factors influencing neuroinflammation and potential therapeutic targets. In contrast to the general elevation of inflammatory molecules, cystatin C (Cyst C) showed markedly reduced expression. This result was confirmed by western blotting and mRNA analysis. Immunohistochemistry revealed widespread Cyst C positivity in glial cells of the normal brain, whereas PCNSL tissue was dominated by Cyst C-negative B cells. To further investigate its role, cultured PCNSL cells were treated with recombinant human Cyst C. Treatment reduced viable cell counts in a dose- and time-dependent manner over 4 days without inducing cell death, indicating inhibition of cell division. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated that Cyst C increased p21 expression and decreased cyclin-dependent kinase 1 and Cyclin B. These findings identify Cyst C as a regulator of PCNSL cell division, highlighting it as a potential therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44039-w.

Introduction

Primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) is a non-Hodgkin lymphoma confined to the central nervous system. Most patients eventually develop relapsed or refractory disease, and the overall prognosis remains poor1. PCNSL is composed mainly of tumorigenic CD20-positive B cells2, with smaller populations of immune cells, oligodendrocytes, and fibroblasts3,4. Tumorigenic B cells exhibit abnormal activation of signaling pathways, including B cell receptor (BCR), Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9), and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), compared with normal peripheral B cells3. These findings suggest that targeted immunotherapy may be valuable, and new therapeutic targets could provide significant advances in PCNSL treatment.

Despite methotrexate (MTX)-based combination therapy, 10–15% of patients develop drug resistance and become refractory4–7, resulting in poor outcomes. Novel therapeutic approaches, including small-molecule inhibitors, immunomodulatory drugs, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy, offer new hope7–9. However, nearly half of patients relapse within 2 years, with survival ranging from only 8 to 18 months even after aggressive salvage therapy. The 5-year overall survival and relative survival rates remain low, at 21% and 22%, respectively. Currently, no standard salvage regimen exists for relapsed or refractory PCNSL.

Recent studies have highlighted mechanisms of pathogenesis and drug resistance, revealing tumor microenvironment heterogeneity. Early mortality is not uncommon, underscoring the urgent need to identify high-risk patients for timely surveillance, diagnosis, and management. Some therapeutic studies in PCNSL have included patients who develop relapsed or refractory (R/R) disease as well as those unable to tolerate immunotherapy, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy7. In these studies, compared with peripheral blood, the PCNSL tumor microenvironment was notably immunosuppressed.

Advances in genomic, transcriptomic, and single-cell sequencing have provided detailed insights into the molecular pathology of PCNSL, facilitating exploration of novel therapeutic approaches. However, progress in defining the molecular profile of PCNSL remains incomplete. To establish effective new strategies, further studies are required to identify essential molecular targets and develop novel therapies10–12.

In this study, we focused on PCNSL pathogenesis associated with neuroinflammation. Although recent research has suggested several pathways, the specific molecules and mechanisms driving disease remain poorly defined and must be considered in light of emerging therapeutic advances13–15. To explore potential therapeutic targets and inform future strategies, we performed an unbiased proteomic analysis of PCNSL tissue.

Here, we demonstrate a widespread elevation of several inflammatory molecules in PCNSL using a commercial inflammation-related proteome analysis platform. Notably, we report for the first time a markedly reduced level of cystatin C (Cyst C).

Furthermore, we examine the role of Cyst C in PCNSL and investigate the mechanisms by which it influences tumor growth, demonstrating that cellular Cyst C plays a crucial role in suppressing PCNSL proliferation. Our findings suggest that maintaining cerebral Cyst C may be important for inhibiting PCNSL cell growth in the brain.

Materials & methods

Clinical specimens

Primary CNS lymphomas pathologically diagnosed as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma were obtained from Tokushima University Hospital. All protocols were approved by the Clinical Research Review Board of Tokushima University (CRB6200001; approval number: 3649-2). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Tumor tissues were snap-frozen, fixed in 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h, and subsequently transferred to phosphate-buffered saline.

Human cytokine array analysis

PCNSL and control brain tissues were analyzed using the Proteome Profiler Human XL Cytokine Array Kit (ARY022B, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For comparison, we used commercially sourced normal brain tissues and tissue samples obtained from the glioblastoma (GBM) periphery that had previously been confirmed, via proteomic analysis, to exhibit expression patterns comparable to normal brain tissue and were stored in a deep freezer. Densitometry was performed using ImageJ software. Negative control values from each sample were subtracted from the corresponding cytokine–sample pairs, and the relative intensities were quantile-normalized16. Data were log2-transformed to enable direct comparisons among cytokines. A complete list of cytokines included in the array is available on the manufacturer’s website.

Cell culture and reagents

JCRB0041 (normal cell line) and JCRB1206 (PCNSL cell line)17, were obtained from the JCRB Cell Bank (NIBIOHN, National Institutes of Biomedical Innovation, Health and Nutrition). Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium with L-glutamine and phenol red (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco-BRL, NY), at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

Cell viability assay

Recombinant human cystatin C (Cyst C) protein (Abcam, ab283919) was used to assess effects on cell viability. Cell counts were performed with 0.4% Trypan Blue (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #15250-061), mixed with cell suspension at 1:1, and incubated for 1–2 min at room temperature. A 10 µL aliquot was loaded onto a counting slide for the TC20 Automated Cell Counter (Bio-Rad), and viable cells were quantified.

Western blotting

Cells or tissue samples were homogenized in RIPA buffer containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. #5872). Protein concentrations were determined with a BCA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (50 µg) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio-Rad), blocked for 1 h with either 5% skim milk or 2% bovine serum albumin in Tris-buffered saline (TBS), and incubated with primary antibodies against Cyst C (Abcam, ab109508) and β-actin (Abcam, ab8226). After washing, membranes were incubated for 1 h with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies, and signals were detected using Amersham enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) Prime (GE Healthcare, UK) on an ImageQuant LAS4000 mini analyzer and analyzed with ImageJ 1.52 software.

Immunohistochemistry

Antibodies were diluted in Can Get Signal Immunostain solution (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan): Cyst C (Abcam, ab109508, 1:1000), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-6170, 1:200), Cyclin B (Proteintech, 20960-1-AP, 1:1000), CDK1 (Proteintech, 20960-1-AP, 1:1000), p21 (Proteintech, 20960-1-AP, 1:1000), and CD20 (Abcam, ab78237, 1:1000). Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed three times in TBS (10 min each), and then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with fluorescein-conjugated secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 594 or 488; Molecular Probes, CA, USA) diluted 1:1000 in Immunostain solution. Sections were mounted with fluorescence mounting medium (Dako) and visualized using a KEYENCE BZ-X710 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan) with image analysis capability.

Quantitative real-time PCR

To determine Cyst C mRNA expression levels, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT–PCR) was performed on a LightCycler (Roche) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA was extracted using the AllPrep DNA/RNA/miRNA Kit (QIAGEN GmbH, Hilden, Germany) and reverse-transcribed with SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis SuperMix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA). Amplification was performed using Platinum SYBR Green qPCR SuperMix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on an ABI StepOne Plus instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Gene expression was normalized to β-actin CT values, and fold changes were calculated using the ΔΔCT method.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using Prism software (GraphPad, version 10.0). Two-group comparisons were evaluated using Student’s t-test, while one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s and Dunnett’s post hoc tests determined significance for multiple comparisons. Error bars represent standard deviations (SDs). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Proteome profile by cytokine array

As shown in Figs. 3 and 102 inflammation-related molecules were assessed using cytokine array kits and compared to non-tumorous peripheral brain tissues excised from patients with GBM and commercially sourced normal brain tissues, which showed comparable cytokine profiles. In PCNSL tissues, many inflammation-related molecules were elevated more than fivefold, particularly IL-16, Endoglin, ICAM-1, RANTES, Angiogenin, RBP-4, and uPAR. In contrast, only a few molecules were decreased, with the most pronounced reduction observed in Cyst C, which was reduced to approximately one-eighth of the normal level, with expression falling to less than one-tenth of normal levels. The relative pixel density for Cyst C was 56.71 (47.58–67.65) in normal controls versus 5.06 (3.83–13.21) in PCNSL, representing a highly significant reduction (p < 0.0001). Therefore, Cyst C may play a key role in PCNSL, warranting further investigation to clarify its functional role.

Scarce cyst C protein expression in PCNSL

To compare Cyst C expression between PCNSL and normal brain tissues, immunohistochemistry was performed. As shown in Fig. 4, nearly all GFAP+ glial cells and NeuN+ neurons were Cyst C-positive (red) in the normal brain, whereas CD20 + B cells (green) in PCNSL tissue were predominantly Cyst C-negative. Thus, Cyst C was abundant in normal brain cells but scarce in PCNSL.

Cyst C expression in PCNSL cell lines

To further evaluate Cyst C in PCNSL, we compared the PCNSL cell line JCRB1206 with the normal lymphocyte line JCRB0041. Immunohistochemistry and western blotting confirmed high Cyst C protein expression in normal lymphocytes but minimal levels in PCNSL cells and patients with PCNSL (Fig. 5a and b). Full-length western blot images corresponding to Fig. 5b are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. Similarly, both mRNA and protein expression were high in normal lymphocytes but markedly reduced in PCNSL cells (Fig. 5b and c). These findings demonstrate that the Cyst C expression profile observed between normal lymphocytes and PCNSL cell lines was consistent with the different findings between PCNSL and normal brain tissues.

Externally added Cyst C delays PCNSL cell division

To assess the effects of Cyst C on cell proliferation, PCNSL-derived JCRB1206 cells were plated at 1 × 10⁵ cells per dish. Cells were treated with 1 or 5 µM recombinant Cyst C in five independent culture dishes and compared with untreated controls. As shown in Fig. 6a, the representative untreated culture cells displayed larger colonies and greater cell numbers. In contrast, the cells treated with 1 or 5 µM Cyst C exhibited slower proliferation and formed smaller colonies on days 3 and 4. Consistent with this, viable cell counts significantly decreased in a dose-dependent manner (p < 0.001, Fig. 6b). However, viability ratios were comparable across groups (Fig. 6c), indicating that Cyst C delays cell division without inducing cell death.

Effects of Cyst C on cell division

To confirm these observations, we examined the expression of key cell-cycle regulators. p21, an inhibitor of cyclin-dependent kinases, and the kinases CdK1 and cyclin B, which promote G2/M progression and M phase entry into mitosis, were evaluated. Following Cyst C treatment, p21 expression increased, while CdK1 and cyclin B levels decreased. These results suggest that Cyst C induces cell cycle arrest by modulating these regulators. Importantly, no evidence of PCNSL cell death was detected, reinforcing that the effect of Cyst C is inhibition of proliferation rather than cytotoxicity.

Discussion

Some studies have reported that PCNSL pathogenesis may be associated with inflammatory changes. Therefore, we performed an inflammation-focused proteomic analysis and found, for the first time, that Cyst C expression was drastically reduced in PCNSL, in contrast to the marked elevation of many inflammation-related molecules. To clarify the role of Cyst C, we examined its effect on cell growth by adding recombinant Cyst C to a PCNSL cell line. Notably, the addition of 1 and 5 µM Cyst C delayed PCNSL cell division in a dose-dependent manner without inducing cell death. Based on these observations, we speculated that these effects might reflect changes in the expression of cell division–related molecules. We therefore added Cyst C directly to PCNSL cell cultures and evaluated the expression of p21, CdK1, and cyclin B.

p21 inhibits cell cycle progression, whereas CdK1 and cyclin B promote cell division. Accordingly, we measured the expression levels of these molecules in PCNSL cells exposed to Cyst C.

Cyst C delayed cell growth in a dose-dependent manner without causing cell death. Consistent with these findings, immunohistochemical analysis showed increased p21 expression and decreased CdK1 and cyclin B in Cyst C-treated PCNSL cells. These results suggest that Cyst C, at least partly, suppresses PCNSL cell growth by modulating key regulators of cell division.

PCNSL is a rare and aggressive form of extra-nodal non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, and an increasing number of case reports describe histologically confirmed neuroinflammatory (sentinel lesions) preceding the development of PCNSL. Based on these observations, we investigated inflammatory changes associated with PCNSL, as suggested by previous studies3,11. We observed broad elevation of many inflammation-related molecules. However, the specific molecules contributing to pathogenesis remain unclear. Notably, our proteomic analysis revealed markedly reduced Cyst C expression despite widespread increases in inflammatory markers (Fig. 3). Immunohistochemistry confirmed scarce Cyst C in PCNSL tissue compared with abundant expression in normal neurons and GFAP+ cells (Fig. 4). Both protein and mRNA levels of Cystatin C were similarly reduced in the PCNSL cell line (Fig. 5b and c). These results suggest that loss of Cyst C may contribute, at least in part, to PCNSL pathogenesis. To further clarify its function, recombinant Cyst C was added to PCNSL cultures. Cell growth decreased in a time- and dose-dependent manner without evidence of cytotoxicity, even at high concentrations (Fig. 6b and c). This finding indicates that Cyst C primarily influences proliferation rather than survival. To confirm this, we analyzed cell-cycle regulators. p21 is a cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) inhibitor that suppresses the cyclin D–CDK4/6 complex during G1 phase and facilitates DNA damage repair. Cyst C increased p21 expression while decreasing CdK1 and cyclin B (Fig. 7a-c), suggesting that it induces cell cycle arrest via modulation of these pathways. Taken together, our study demonstrates for the first time that Cyst C delays PCNSL cell division without inducing cell death. These findings highlight Cyst C as a regulator of tumor growth and a potential therapeutic target, warranting further investigation into its mechanisms and translational relevance.

Consistent with our findings in PCNSL, melanoma studies have reported that exogenous Cyst C suppresses tumor cell growth18. Cyst C regulation involves multiple transcriptional and post-translational mechanisms, but how it becomes downregulated in PCNSL remains unknown. Clarifying this mechanism will be essential for understanding disease pathogenesis.

Cyst C is also implicated in inflammatory diseases3, where immune cells play central roles. Bone marrow–derived cells represent an important source of Cyst C, and identifying these cellular origins may be clinically relevant because Cyst C improves blood–brain barrier integrity after ischemic injury19. As a non-glycosylated, low-molecular-weight protein produced by all nucleated cells, Cyst C contributes to systemic pools, particularly from hematopoietic cells14. Importantly, MTX, used in PCNSL treatment, can elevate plasma Cyst C due to renal dysfunction20. Therefore, careful monitoring during MTX therapy is warranted.

Reduced Cyst C levels have been associated with neurological disorders, underscoring the need for precise regulation to harness its therapeutic potential.

In our experiments, exogenously added Cyst C delayed colony formation and slowed PCNSL cell proliferation over 4 days in a dose-dependent manner, without inducing cell death (Fig. 6a–c). Immunohistochemistry confirmed increased p21 and decreased Cdk1 and cyclin B expression in all PCNSL cells exposed to 5 µM Cyst C (Fig. 7a–c). These findings strengthen the conclusion that Cyst C inhibits PCNSL cell division without triggering apoptosis. These findings were consistent with those observed in the PCNSL cell line compared with the normal cell line. Although we partially demonstrated the mechanisms of Cyst C-mediated regulation of cell division, the degree of growth inhibition was limited, indicating the need for further studies to identify additional factors or combination strategies that could enhance its efficacy.

MTX remains the cornerstone of PCNSL therapy13. Although elevated plasma Cyst C is associated with kidney dysfunction, its effects on PCNSL cell growth, systemic inflammation, and immune regulation remain unclear. Moreover, Cyst C expression in both normal brain and PCNSL has not been fully characterized21. Our findings, together with prior melanoma studies, suggest that exogenous Cyst C prolongs the cell cycle18, exerting cytostatic rather than cytotoxic effects. In PCNSL, these properties may influence disease control and neurological recovery. Understanding Cyst C biology may therefore support the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

PCNSL is confined to the CNS, and most patients ultimately relapse, resulting in a poor prognosis. Consolidative strategies after MTX treatment, such as radiotherapy, maintenance therapy, and autologous stem cell transplantation, aim to prolong therapeutic efficacy. These approaches target mechanisms such as B cell receptor (BCR) signaling and immune evasion. Agents, including the Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitor ibrutinib and immunomodulatory drugs (lenalidomide, pomalidomide), show promising activity in relapsed or refractory PCNSL. However, relapse remains common, with 5-year survival rates of only 30–40%. Understanding Cyst C regulation and function may offer new therapeutic opportunities to overcome treatment resistance. In addition, systemic inflammatory markers have been proposed as prognostic indicators, and patient-tailored combination strategies may enhance treatment efficacy, as single agents are unlikely to be sufficient3.

Compared with normal brain, PCNSL tissues consist predominantly of tumorigenic CD20 + B cells, with smaller populations of immune, oligodendrocytic, and fibroblastic cells. Aberrant activation of BCR, Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9), and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling has been reported in tumorigenic B cells relative to peripheral blood B cells19,22. These abnormalities highlight the therapeutic potential of targeted immunotherapy, which could provide significant advances in PCNSL management. Several strategies, including small-molecule inhibitors, immunomodulatory drugs, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy, have offered new hope2. Nonetheless, 35–60% of patients relapse within 2 years, with a median survival of only 8–18 months despite aggressive treatment. Long-term outcomes remain poor, with 5- and 10-year survival rates of 29.9% and 22.2%, respectively. No standard salvage regimen currently exists for relapsed or refractory PCNSL.

In this study, we demonstrated that Cyst C inhibited PCNSL cell growth, consistent with prior findings in melanoma18. Our results suggest that sustained infusion of Cyst C may transiently suppress PCNSL progression. However, 10–15% of patients receiving MTX-based therapy develop resistance and become refractory, further worsening prognosis23. Therefore, novel therapeutic approaches targeting both molecular drivers and immune mechanisms are urgently required to improve outcomes in this challenging disease.

Several new therapeutic strategies for PCNSL, including small-molecule inhibitors, immunomodulatory drugs, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy23,24, have offered new hope. However, 35–60% of patients relapse within 2 years of diagnosis, with a median survival of only 8–18 months despite aggressive treatment. Long-term outcomes remain poor, with 5- and 10-year survival rates of 29.9% and 22.2%, respectively. No standard salvage regimen currently exists for relapsed or refractory PCNSL. Recent studies highlight heterogeneity in the PCNSL tumor microenvironment25, which is immunosuppressed compared with peripheral blood. Several immune-targeted therapies are now included in NCCN guidelines, particularly for relapsed cases or those unable to tolerate standard regimens. BTK inhibitors such as ibrutinib are increasingly used in frontline therapy, and reductions in circulating tumor DNA correlate with improved outcomes, supporting further development of targeted treatments26.

Our study focused on PCNSL pathogenesis associated with neuroinflammation. We demonstrated markedly reduced Cyst C expression despite elevated inflammatory molecules. Functionally, Cyst C suppressed PCNSL cell division through the p21/CdK1/cyclin B pathway. These findings underscore the potential importance of maintaining Cyst C to inhibit tumor growth. Future studies should evaluate approaches for early detection, mechanisms regulating Cyst C expression, and strategies to induce cell death, with the goal of translating these findings into clinical applications. However, one limitation of this study is the use of non-tumorous brain tissue obtained from GBM resection as a reference control. To address this, we previously obtained commercially sourced normal brain tissues (temporal lobe and hippocampus) from a supplier. By comparing these tissues, we confirmed that the histologically tumor-free regions selected from the GBM periphery exhibited cytokine expression levels comparable to those of the normal brain tissues.
