Sigma-1 receptor exerts protective effects on ameliorating nephrolithiasis by modulating endoplasmic reticulum-mitochondrion association and inhibiting endoplasmic reticulum stress-induced apoptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells
Authors: Hu Ke, Xiaozhe Su, Caitao Dong, Ziqi He, Qianlin Song, Chao song, Jiawei Zhou, Wenbiao Liao, Chuan Wang, Sixing Yang, Yunhe Xiong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOxalate-induced damage to renal tubular epithelial cells (RTECs) is an essential factor in the incident kidney stone, but the specific mechanism is unclear. Recent research has pinpointed interacting areas within the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria, called mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs). These studies have linked endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) and oxidative imbalance to kidney disease development. The sigma-1 receptor (S1R), a specific protein found in MAMs, is involved in various physiological processes, but its role in oxalate-induced kidney stone formation remains unclear. In this study, we established cellular and rat models of oxalate-induced kidney stone formation to elucidate the S1R's effects against ERS and apoptosis and its mechanism in oxalate-induced RTEC injury. We found that oxalate downregulated S1R expression in RTECs and escalated oxidative stress and ERS, culminating in increased apoptosis. The S1R agonist dimemorfan up-regulated S1R expression and mitigated ERS and oxidative stress, thereby reducing apoptosis. This protective effect was mediated through S1R inhibition of the CHOP pathway. Animal experiments demonstrated that S1R's activation attenuated oxalate-induced kidney injury and alleviated kidney stone formation. This is the first study to establish the connection between S1R and kidney stones, suggesting S1R's protective role in inhibiting ERS-mediated apoptosis to ameliorate kidney stone formation.

Introduction

Kidney stones represent a chronic systemic disease with an increasing global prevalence, presenting a substantial clinical and economic challenge [1–3]. Despite considerable progress in minimally invasive surgical techniques for treating kidney stones, the consistently high prevalence and recurrence rates post-surgery highlight the limited efficacy of current preventative strategies for kidney stones [1], which underscores the pressing need for comprehensive studies on the molecular mechanisms that underlie kidney stone formation. Calcium oxalate (CaOx) is the primary constituent of kidney stones, making up approximately 80% of the total [4]. Hyperoxaluria is considered a key risk factor for the formation of kidney stones [1,5]. Chronic hyperoxaluria activates numerous signaling pathways within renal tubular epithelial cells (RTECs), including endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS), oxidative stress, and inflammatory response, which interferes with protein synthesis and homeostasis [6,7]. This persistent intracellular disturbance leads to cell necrosis and apoptosis, ultimately exposing sites for crystal attachment and accelerating the formation and growth of kidney stone crystals [8]. Therefore, maintaining endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondrial homeostasis to mitigate damage to RTECs is of paramount importance for the early prevention and treatment of renal stone formation.

The ER is integral to numerous biological functions, rendering it vulnerable to damage from external stimuli [9,10]. When such stimuli induce ERS, cells initiate the unfolded protein response (UPR) to alleviate ERS and restore ER functionality [11]. However, when ERS is excessive in intensity and duration, the UPR is compelled to mediate cellular damage and apoptosis [12]. Chronic ERS is increasingly recognized as a crucial factor in the pathophysiology of a wide range of common diseases. This concept is well-substantiated in affected tissues across a diverse array of diseases [12]. Currently, the targeting of ERS or the UPR is in its early stages as a potential strategy for drug development in treating related conditions. This approach will necessitate extensive research for validation and future clinical translation [13,14].

Mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum membranes (MAMs), the specialized physical structures that link the ER and mitochondria, play a pivotal role in regulating inter-organelle communication. They serve as a fundamental platform for various cellular functions, thereby preserving cellular health [15,16]. Under stressful conditions, MAMs become critical determinants of cell fate [17–19]. The sigma-1 receptor (S1R), a transmembrane protein localized on MAMs, acts as a crucial intermediary between the ER and mitochondria [20]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that S1R can inhibit ER-mediated apoptosis and modulate mitochondrial function and autophagy by functioning as a molecular chaperone [20–22].

Consequently, the activation of S1R emerges as a potential target for disease treatment. Dimemorfan (Dim), a dextromethorphan analogue, is a selective Sigmar1 agonist that has been safely utilized in clinical practice for many years [23]. Existing research on S1R has primarily focused on diseases such as neurodegenerative disorders, tumors, and ischemia-reperfusion injury. However, the role of S1R in the urinary system remains underexplored. Recent studies have confirmed the presence of S1R expression in proximal RTECs [24]. Nevertheless, the relationship between S1R expression and renal stone formation, as well as the correlation between S1R and oxalate-mediated damage to RTECs, remains to be elucidated. This gap in knowledge underscores the need for further research in this area.

In the present study, we embarked on a series of cellular and animal experiments to investigate the role of the S1R in the formation of kidney stones. Our results unveiled that the activation of S1R could effectively mitigate ERS-mediated apoptosis. This protective effect was mediated by inhibiting the Protein Kinase R-like Endoplasmic Reticulum Kinase (PERK) – Activating Transcription Factor 4 (ATF4) – C/EBP Homologous Protein (CHOP) pathway, thereby offering protection against oxalate-induced injury in RTECs. Moreover, our findings highlighted the beneficial effects of S1R activation in alleviating renal injury induced by oxalate. These novel insights pave the way for potential preventative measures and therapeutic strategies for kidney stone management. Our study underscores the importance of further research in this promising field.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

Human proximal tubule epithelial cells (HK-2), procured from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM/F12, Gibco, China), which contains 10% fetal bovine serum (10091, Gibco, China) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin(G4003, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The culture medium of the cell was renewed daily, and cells were passaged when the density reached 70–80%. Based on our prior research and pre-experimental findings, we selected 2 mM oxalate (75688, Sigma, Germany) for a 24-hour incubation period to induce oxalate-induced cell injury [25]. Combined with the instructions and references, dimemorfan phosphate ((Dim), HY-B2215, MCE, USA) was dissolved in dH2O to a final concentration of 1 mM. Then, to choose the Dim's best concentration, we first detected the changes in cell viability in the HK-2 cells after treatment with different concentrations of Dim (5, 10, 20, and 40μM) intervention. Subsequently, the above tests were repeated with the addition of 2 mM oxalate. Finally, we chose the Dim concentration of 10μM for follow-up experiments. NC group was cultured without treatment; the Dim group was cultured with 10μM Dim; the Ox group was treated with 2 mM oxalate; the Ox + Dim group was co-intervened with 2 mM oxalate and 10μM Dim. All cells were cultured in a complete medium for 24 hours in an incubator (Binder, Tuttlingen, Germany) with 5% CO2 and 37°C.

siRNA transfection

The S1R's small interfering RNA (siRNA) was obtained from Sangong Biotech Inc. Shanghai, China. HK-2 cells were transfected by S1R-siRNA and negative control siRNA for 24 hours using Transfection Reagent (HY-K1014, MCE, USA), during which the cell's confluency was grown to 70%. The efficiency of knockdown was verified by protein and mRNA levels. The siRNA sequences were as follows: siRNA-NC (Sense: 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’; Antisense: 5’-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3’), siRNA-SIGMAR1 (Sense: 5' – GCUGGCCUUCUCUCGUCUGAUTT-3’; Antisense: 5' – AUCAGACGAGAGAAGGCCAGCTT-3’).

Cell viability assay

We used the Cell Counting Kit-8 ((CCK8), BS350b, Biosharp, China) to determine cell viability. HK-2 cells were subjected to their respective interventions in a 96-well plate for this experiment. Next, 100μl of CCK8 solution (CCK8 10μl + basal medium 90μl) was added to each well. After thorough mixing, the cells were incubated in an incubator (37°C, 5% CO2) for 2 hours. Subsequently, the absorbance was tested by the microplate reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) at 450 nm.

GSH detection

To assess the cell's ability to resist oxidative stress, we quantified the antioxidant biomarker GSH using a GSH detection kit (A006-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng, China). Following the manufacturers’ Instructions, we administered various treatments to each cell group. Subsequently, we measured the absorbance by a microplate reader at 405 nm.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

The levels of ROS in each group were assessed using the ROS Assay kit (S0033S, Beyotime, China). After the intervention, cell samples were washed with PBS. According to the kit instructions, the ROS reagent was prepared and added to the groups, followed by incubation for 30 minutes. Then, the wells were rewashed using basal medium to eliminate residual DCFH-DA that had not entered the cells. Eventually, cells in each group were imaged with a fluorescence microscope (BX53, Olympus, Japan) at 200x magnification. The mean signal intensity of ROS green fluorescence was analyzed by ImageJ software (NIH, USA)

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) detection

The changes in MMP indicate the damage to mitochondria. Following the reagent instructions, we added the well-prepared JC-1 (C2006, Beyotime, China) solution into the cell climbing tablets of each group, incubating them for 20 minutes in a 37°C incubator. After discarding the staining solution and washing the climbing tablets twice with JC-1 staining buffer (1×), the cells were mounted with Antifade Mounting Medium (containing DAPI) (Beyotime, China) and imaged under a fluorescence microscope at 400× magnification. Finally, the mean signal intensities of red and green fluorescence were analyzed by ImageJ software.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) assay

After cell intervention, total RNA was extracted by Trizol reagent (15596026, Thermo Fisher) following the reagent instructions. After using the NanoDrop™ One (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to detect the concentration and purity of the sample, the cDNA was synthesized using the Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (gDNA digester plus) (11139ES60, Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, the mRNA expression of the S1R gene was detected by LightCycler480 (Roche Diagnostics, USA) after adding Hieff UNICON® Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (11184ES08, Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Finally, the results were recorded and quantified using 2-ΔΔCt methods with GAPDH as the housekeeping gene. The primer sequences used for RT–PCR were as follows: SIGMAR1 (forward: 5′ – GTCCGAGTATGTGCTGCTCTTC-3′; reverse: 5′-GAAGACCTCACTTTTGGTGGTGC-3′), GAPDH (forward: 5′ – GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCG – 3′; reverse: 5′ – ACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA-3′).

Western blot

We used RIPA buffer (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) to lyse the HK-2 cells and rat renal tissues to extract the total protein in each group. We used electrophoresis to separate these samples after assaying protein concentrations with the BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). After being transferred to the PVDF Membrane (Millipore, USA) and blocked with rapid blocking buffer (Epizyme, Shanghai, China) for 10 min, the membranes containing the protein were washed once with Tris-buffered Saline with Tween 20 (TBST), and then incubated with the these primary antibodies at 4°C overnight: SIGMAR1(Proteintech, 15168-1-Ap), GRP78 (Proteintech, 11587-1-AP), PERK (Proteintech, 20582-1-AP), ATF4 (Proteintech, 10835-1-AP), CHOP (Proteintech, 15204-1-AP), BAX (ABclonal, A12009), cleaved caspase-3 ((cleaved C3) Proteintech, 19677-1-AP), and GAPDH (Abcam, UK, ab181602). After rewashing, the blots were placed in secondary antibodies (5151, Cell Signaling Technology, Inc, USA) at room temperature for 1 hour. After being washed again and subsequently added the enhanced chemiluminescence reagent, the blots were scanned by the chemiluminescence imager (ChemiDoc™ Touch, Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA) and quantized by Image J software.

Animal experiments

Twenty-four male Sprague–Dawley rats (6–8 weeks old, 200 ± 20 g) were randomly and equally separated into four groups: The control group (Con) was fed by using normal drink water and injected with Saline via intraperitoneal every other day; Dimemorfan group (Dim) consumed water and received Intraperitoneal injections with 5 mg/kg dimemorfan at one-day intervals. The kidney stone model group (Ksm) was fed water containing 0.75% ethylene glycol (EG) and received Saline injected via intraperitoneal at one-day intervals; the Kidney stone model + Dimemorfan group (Ksm + Dim) was fed water with 0.75% EG and injected intraperitoneally with 5 mg/kg dimemorfan. After five weeks, the rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (60 mg/kg) and sacrificed, and their kidney tissues, blood, and urine were collected. All animal experiment procedures were approved by the Animal Care Committee of Wuhan University (Wuhan, China), and the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University also approved the study (number: 20230310F).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Intervented HK-2 cells were gently scraped and immobilized with electron microscope fixation liquid (G1102, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 2 hours at 4° C. Following a sequence of procedures, including infiltration, embedding, sectioning, and so on, we observed the morphological changes of MAMs using the TEM (HT7800, Hitachi, Japan).

Animal biochemistry indexes detection

Before being sacrificed, the urine samples of rats were collected in sterile containers for Kidney Injury Molecule-1 (Kim-1) detection. After euthanized, blood samples were gathered to measure serum creatinine (Scr) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels. Additionally, we utilized a GSH assay to measure the content of GSH in renal tissues according to the reagent Instructions.

Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining and silver nitrate staining

Rat kidney samples were immersed in 10% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 mm thickness. After deparaffinizing, these slices were counterstained using HE staining solution (G1120, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and immersed in Von Kossa dye solution (G1043, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), respectively. Then, the slices were sealed with neutral glue and imaged with a fluorescence microscope.

Immunofluorescence and immunohistofluorescence

HK-2 cells were planted on climbing tablets and cultured in the incubator. After the intervention mentioned earlier, we removed the culture medium and washed HK-2 cells twice with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were immobilized into 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, broken with 0.3% Triton-X-100 (Servicebio, China) for 10 min, and blocked using 5% BSA for 30 min at room temperature. After further washing, the cells on the climbing tablets were incubated in respective primary antibodies in darkness at 4 °C overnight. Next, the secondary antibodies (5220–0336, SeraCare, USA) were applied and treated again at dark room temperature for 30 minutes. Finally, the cells are stained with DAPI staining solution, observed, and imaged by fluorescence microscope.

The obtained paraffin sections of the kidney sample were deparaffinized, rehydrated, recovered, permeabilized, and blocked, and then treated by the primary antibody for 12 hours at 4 °C. Subsequently, the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody about anti-rabbit or anti-mouse (8890, Cell Signaling Technology, Inc, USA) was added to these sections and incubated for 1 hour. Finally, these sections were mounted and observed by fluorescence microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

Following dewaxation, the slices of kidney tissue were rinsed with dH2O. After several procedures, including antigen repair sealing, antibodies (ATF4, CHOP, and BAX) were added to the slices and treated at 4 ◦C overnight. After rewashing, the secondary antibodies (5220–0336, SeraCare, USA) were added. Subsequent steps included color development, counterstaining, differentiation, dehydration, and sealing. Finally, the samples were imaged under a fluorescence microscope. ImageJ software was utilized for quantifying and analyzing the respective protein expression.

DHE staining

After being sacrificed, the four groups’ rat kidneys were immediately frozen and subsequently prepared into fresh frozen sections. Then, these sections were cultivated by dihydroethidium (GDP1018, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) in darkness for 30 minutes. The sections were then imaged with a fluorescence microscope and quantitatively analyzed with Image J software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of data was performed by GraphPad Prism software 9.0. The independent sample t-test was used for the comparison of two independent samples. In contrast, the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Turkey's multiple-comparison test was used to compare multiple groups. Our data were all from three or more separate experiments and expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean. The p-value < 0.05 indicated statistically significant.

Results

Activation of S1R attenuated oxidative stress and excessive ER-mitochondrial contact in oxalate-induced HK-2 cell

Based on our previous study, 2 mM oxalate was chosen to handle HK-2 cells for 24 hours to construct cell models. To clarify the effect of Dim on cell viability, we incubated HK-2 cells with different concentrations of Dim (0, 5, 10, 20, and 40μM) for 24 hours and assayed the viability using CCK-8 Assay Kit. We found that within the provided concentration range, Dim did not exert toxic effects on HK-2 cells (Figure 1A). Subsequently, the above test was repeated by adding 2 mM oxalate. We found that the viability of cells in the oxalate group that were also treated with Dim (5, 10, 20, and 40μM) was improved compared to cells in the oxalate group alone, and the Dim concentration of 10μM is the most pronounced (Figure 1B). Therefore, we selected 10μM as the Dim intervention concentration for subsequent experiments. Furthermore, after detecting the effect of Dim on S1R. We observed an ascending trend in S1R expression with increasing Dim concentration (Figure 1C), which suggests that Dim activates S1R function, at least in part, by up-regulating S1R expression. To assess the cell's oxidative status, we also detected ROS generation and mitochondrial function in the four groups of cells using fluorescence microscopy by DCFH-DA assay and JC-1 assay, respectively. The results showed the Ox group exhibited an increase in ROS production but a significant decrease in MMP compared with the NC group. In contrast, ROS generation was decreased, but the MMP was increased after Dim treatment (Figure 1D-G). Next, we also evaluated the antioxidant index (GSH). Compared to the NC group, the GSH content in the Ox group was significantly lower. Interestingly, while the GSH content in the Dim group was not significantly changed to the NC group, after the Dim treatment, the Ox + Dim group exhibited elevated GSH levels compared to the Ox group (Figure 1H). Additionally, TEM was used to observe the dynamic structure of MAMs in HK-2 cells and found that the decreased distance between the mitochondrial membrane and the ER membrane in the cells of the Ox group, along with a corresponding increase in contact ratio compared with the NC group, while the treatment of Dim significantly alleviated this trend (Figure 1I).

The above findings suggest that Dim alleviated oxalate-induced activation of oxidative stress and excessive ER-mitochondrial contact in HK-2 cells.

Activation of S1R reduced oxalate-induced ERS and apoptosis by inhibiting the PERK-ATF4-CHOP signaling pathway in HK-2 cells

We used oxalate-intervened HK-2 cells to determine whether Dim suppressed ERS within RTECs. We found that the S1R expression of the Ox group decreased compared to the NC group. Instead, S1R expression was increased in the Ox + Dim group compared to the Ox group (Figure 2A, F), which may be relevant to Dim's mild induction of upregulation of S1R expression, especially under stress. WB showed that, compared to the NC group, the expression of ERS-associated proteins (including GRP78, PERK, ATF4, and CHOP) was significantly up-regulated in the Ox group. In contrast, it was reduced in the Ox + Dim group treated with Dim (Figure 2A-E). In addition, the results of ATF4 and CHOP expression assessed by immunofluorescence were consistent with the observations of WB (Figure 2G-J), which suggests that Dim alleviated oxalate-induced ERS in HK-2 cells by attenuating PERK-ATF4-CHOP pathway signaling. Excessive ERS activates the apoptotic pathway, which induces cells to undergo apoptosis. Therefore, to assess whether Dim plays an inhibitory role in ERS-induced apoptosis, we initially used TUNEL staining to evaluate apoptosis in each group of cells. The results showed a higher rate of positive cells in the Ox group than in the NC group, indicating increased apoptosis. In comparison, the apoptosis of the Ox + Dim group was significantly decreased compared to the Ox group (Figure 3A-B). Subsequently, we also detected apoptosis-related pathway proteins (BAX and cleaved C3). The detection of BAX by immunofluorescence confirmed that Dim could reduce this protein's expression in the Ox + Dim group (Figure 3C-D). The results of WB also showed that the expression of BAX and cleaved C3 was up-regulated in the Ox group compared to the NC group, while it was reduced in the Ox + Dim group treated with Dim (Figure 3E-G). This is consistent with the previous trend of expression of ERS-related proteins. These data suggest that Dim can attenuate ERS-induced apoptosis in a model of oxalate injury to HK-2 cells and that this signaling is mediated, at least in part, through the PERK-ATF4-CHOP pathway. Knockdown of S1R reversed the protective effect of S1R activation against oxalate-induced HK-2 cell injury

To further research the correlation between S1R and the PERK-ATF4-CHOP pathway mediating apoptosis. We transfected HK-2 cells using S1R-siRNA and verified the knockdown effect (Figure 4A). As shown in Figure 4B, the content of GSH was markedly lower in the si-S1R + Ox + Dim group compared with the si-NC + Ox + Dim group. Figures 4C and E show that the green fluorescent region of ROS was significantly increased in the si-S1R + Ox + Dim group compared with the si-NC + Ox + Dim group. In addition, JC-1 staining showed that the red fluorescent area representing normal mitochondrial membrane potential was reduced in the si-S1R + Ox + Dim group, whereas the green fluorescent region representing reduced membrane potential was significantly increased (Figure 4D, F). All results suggest that the knockdown of S1R inhibited Dim's antioxidant capacity and protective function on mitochondrial homeostasis in oxalate-induced HK-2 cells. S1R activation alleviates the renal impairment and the deposition of crystals in the rat kidney stone model

Based on many research bases, 0.75% EG is used to establish a kidney stone model. To verify the role of S1R in kidney stone formation, we utilized EG to establish the kidney stone model in SD rats (Figure 6A). Dim was used as an agonist of S1R. First, assessment of kidney damage by blood levels of Cr and BUN and urinary levels of Kim-1 showed that treatment with Dim alone did not result in kidney damage. In the Ksm group, the CRE, BUN, and Kim-1 levels were significantly increased, whereas Dim alleviated the elevation of CRE, BUN and Kim-1 (Figure 6B-D). Secondly, HE staining of paraffin sections of rat kidney tissues showed that the proximal tubules in the Con group were thick-walled, with small and irregular lumens, unclear cell borders, and brush border on the lumen surface, while the Ksm group showed noticeable pathological changes: the epithelial cells of the proximal tubules were swollen, with reduced or even absent brush borders, and necrotic cell fragments could be seen in part of the tubules. Compared with the Ksm group, the changes in renal tissue were significantly alleviated in the Ksm + Dim group: mild oedema of RTECs, with a few detached epithelial cells in the tubule lumen (Figure 6E). These results reveal that Dim enables ameliorate oxalate-induced renal impairment. In addition, we found that much crystal deposition was visible in the tubule lumen in the Ksm group by VonKossa staining of paraffin sections of rat kidney tissues, indicating the stone model was successfully developed. The crystal deposition was reduced in the Ksm + Dim group compared with the Ksm group (Figure 6F), suggesting that Dim may have some function in ameliorating renal stone formation. Discussion

The aetiology of kidney stones is multifaceted and complex. As such, pinpointing and eliminating the causative factors are crucial steps in the prevention and treatment of kidney stones, as well as in reducing the recurrence of these stones [26,27]. Oxalate, a key component in the genesis of renal stones, plays a significant role [28]. Numerous studies have established that the interaction of oxalate with RTECs is a prerequisite for RTEC damage, which is likely the initial stage of CaOx stone formation [29]. Furthermore, it has been reported that inhibiting or attenuating the damage to RTECs induced by high levels of oxalate can aid in preventing the formation of renal stones and reducing their recurrence [30], which underscores the importance of further research in this area to develop effective strategies for kidney stone management. Our current study indicates that oxalate-mediated damage to RTECs disrupts the cellular homeostatic system. This disruption was caused by the initiation of endoplasmic reticulum stress and oxidative stress, which in turn impairs mitochondrial function [6,7,31]. These disturbances interact and create a vicious cycle that culminates in necrosis or apoptosis. As such, these mechanisms, along with intracellular specialized structures or protein molecules that associate two or more mechanisms, present potential therapeutic targets for inhibiting kidney stone formation [31].

MAMs have been reported to play a crucial role in facilitating communication between the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria [32–34]. Proteomic studies have confirmed the presence of multiple resident proteins, including the S1R, within MAMs. These proteins are involved in stabilizing the sub-structure of MAMs, coordinating signal exchange between the two organelles, and regulating a variety of cellular functions to maintain intracellular homeostasis [19,35–37]. In this study, we discovered that oxalate down-regulates the expression of S1R in RTECs. However, Dim, an S1R agonist, was found to inhibit oxalate-induced endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative stress, and apoptosis in RTECs. Furthermore, Dim demonstrated effectiveness in attenuating renal injury, reducing crystal deposition, and inhibiting renal stone formation in a rat kidney stone model. These findings underscore the potential of S1R as a therapeutic target in the management of kidney stones.

The S1R is predominantly located in the MAMs. However, when activated, S1R can migrate from MAMs into the ER lumen (mostly), plasma membrane, or mitochondria and bind to other signaling proteins to function [38]. As a ligand-manipulated chaperone protein, S1R regulates the activity of other proteins [39]. Under normal conditions, S1R binds to immunoglobulin (GRP78) on MAMs, inhibiting its activity. However, the addition of agonists causes S1R to dissociate from GRP78 and activate its role as a molecular chaperone [38,39], which is crucial in the pathophysiology or prevention of various diseases [40]. It has been discovered that S1R agonism attenuates endoplasmic reticulum swelling, stabilizes the structure of MAMs, and reduces apoptosis by inhibiting ERS and oxidative stress [41]. Additionally, S1R regulates the flow of Ca2+ from the endoplasmic reticulum to mitochondria by binding and stabilizing IP3R, thereby preventing mitochondrial dysfunction caused by ‘calcium overload’ [19,41]. Our study demonstrated that S1R activation reduced ERS-related protein expression and ROS production, alleviating oxalate-induced damage in RTECs. However, the knockdown of S1R eliminated this effect.

Oxidative stress is primarily characterized by elevated intracellular ROS production, impairment of antioxidant mechanisms, and compromised mitochondrial function [42,43]. Concurrently, ERS is also implicated through various pathways [44]. Oxalate forces RTECs to consume substantial amounts of energy, leading to mitochondrial overload and impaired mitochondrial function. When the content of endogenous antioxidants (GSH) decreases, the level of ROS relatively increases, resulting in oxidative stress characterized by increased oxidative damage and decreased MMP to cells. Our study showed that S1R activation increased GSH levels, down-regulated ROS, and improved MMP. However, the trend could be reversed by the knockdown of S1R. These results suggest that S1R activation has an antioxidant effect and can attenuate oxidative damage in cells.

When ERS occurs, cells generate the UPR to mitigate ERS and restore protein homeostasis. The UPR is mediated by GRP78 and three stress sensors: inositol-requiring protein 1 (IRE1), protein kinase R-like ER kinase (PERK), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) [45]. Studies have shown that while ERS activates these three sensor proteins, they exhibit different sensitivities to ERS induced by various stimuli. Furthermore, recent evidence suggests that ERS does not simultaneously activate these sensors. ATF6 and IRE1 are activated early in ERS and decay over time, whereas PERK activation, following ATF6 and IRE1 closely, persists during chronic endoplasmic reticulum stress [46,47]. Under normal conditions, GRP78 binds to these sensors and inhibits their function. However, ERS up-regulates GRP78 level and promotes its separation from these sensors. Free GRP78 has a higher affinity for unfolded/misfolded proteins. It mitigates ERS via binding to these proteins and promoting their correct folding or degradation, but this effect is limited under excessive ERS. In contrast, dissociated IRE1, PERK, and ATF6 activate the corresponding downstream signaling pathways and mediate subsequent cellular effects. C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) is a specific transcription factor for ERS-induced apoptosis [48]. Alterations in S1R have been reported to negatively correlate with CHOP expression, which may be the mechanism by which S1R has a cytoprotective effect [49]. To clarify how S1R regulates CHOP expression, we investigated the upstream of CHOP. CHOP is mainly controlled by the PERK-ATF4 pathway in ERS. As one of the starting points of the UPR branch, PERK dissociates from GRP78 and auto-phosphorylates during endoplasmic reticulum stress. Activated PERK induces transcription and activation of ATF4, and subsequently, the latter enters the nucleus, decreasing overall protein synthesis and up-regulating protein expression of translated UPR-related genes. In contrast, under chronic ERS conditions, ATF4 tends to up-regulate CHOP transcription and expression [7,50]. Our study showed that PREK, ATF4, and CHOP protein expressions were all increased by oxalate treatment of RTEC cells for 24 hours, whereas Dim treatment down-regulated these protein expressions about ERS. Conversely, the knockdown of S1R counteracted the agonist's effect and even partially aggravated ERS.

ERS triggers the apoptotic pathway's activation of pro-apoptotic proteins and inhibits anti-apoptotic proteins [51,52]. Chronic ERS induces apoptosis mainly through activation of the PERK-ATF4-CHOP pathway. Studies have shown that CHOP up-regulates the translation of pro-apoptotic genes, including BAX and BIM, while inhibiting the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 [53,54]. In addition, CHOP is also involved in apoptosis by ERS-activated caspase-3 pathway [55]. Therefore, we used S1R agonists to elucidate the relationship between S1R and the CHOP pro-apoptotic pathway. Interestingly, S1R agonists could inhibit BAX and caspase-3 proteins up-regulated by oxalate, significantly reducing apoptosis in RTECs. These results suggest that S1R can inhibit ERS-mediated apoptosis, at least in part through the CHOP pathway.

Despite these findings, this study has some limitations. Firstly, while S1R is mainly localized to MAMs and undergoes translocation under ERS, due to insufficient experimental conditions, we did not isolate the organelles individually and examine the S1R expression of each component. Secondly, although we found that S1R regulates CHOP expression through the PERK-ATF4 pathway, it is unclear whether there is a direct protein interaction between S1R and CHOP, which warrants further investigation. Nevertheless, despite its limitations, our study provides evidence that oxalate-induced injury to RTECs can be mitigated by inhibiting ERS-mediated apoptosis. It demonstrates that activation of S1R may be a prospective strategy for preventing kidney stones.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our research revealed that the S1R enhanced renal function in a rat model of CaOx stones. Furthermore, this study demonstrated that S1R activation mitigated ERS-induced apoptosis, primarily through the inhibition of the PERK-ATF4-CHOP pathway. This protective mechanism was effective against high oxalate-induced injury in RTECs. These findings suggest that S1R could potentially serve as a therapeutic target for the prevention of CaOx kidney stones. However, further research is necessary to validate the clinical translation of S1R agonists, which underscores the importance of continued exploration in this promising area of study.
