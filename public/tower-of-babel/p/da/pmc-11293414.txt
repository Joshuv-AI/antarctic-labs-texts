Trends in the burden of female infertility among adults aged 20–49 years during 1990–2019: an analysis of data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019
Authors: Yijie Wang, Wei Wang, Hongfeng Li, Qiao Du
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe aim of this analysis was to investigate the worldwide distribution and trends in the burden of female infertility (FI).Design and settingAnalysis of cross-sectional data from 1990 to 2019 from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2019.ParticipantsAdults aged 20–49 years in various geographical regions, Sociodemographic Index (SDI) levels and populations across 204 countries worldwide.Outcome measuresDisability-adjusted life years (DALYs), age-standardised DALY rates and estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs) for FI over the research period.ResultsThe global burden of FI, as measured by DALYs and age-standardised DALY rates among adults aged 20–49 years, exhibited an upward trend from 1990 to 2019. Regions such as Tropical Latin America (EAPC: 3.76, 95% CI 2.76 to 4.77), South Asia (EAPC: 1.74, 95% CI 1.01 to 2.48) and Andean Latin America (EAPC: 8.54, 95% CI 6.79 to 10.32) had higher DALYs. The age-standardised DALY rates notably increased in low-middle-SDI and low-SDI countries, with EAPCs of 1.42 (95% CI 0.76 to 2.10) and 1.25 (95% CI 0.30 to 2.21), respectively. Despite lower overall DALYs in high-SDI countries, they experienced the highest EAPC in DALYs (1.57, 95% CI 1.30 to 1.83) during the period 1990–2019. China and India consistently had the highest DALYs for FI globally in both 1990 and 2019, while Africa and Latin America bore a significantly greater burden of the disease compared with other regions.ConclusionThe GBD Study data on FI among adults aged 20–49 years revealed a rising global trend in FI from 1990 to 2019, with notable variations across different regions.

Introduction

Female infertility (FI) is characterised by the inability to conceive after 1 year of regular sexual intercourse for women under 35 years of age without contraception, and after 6 months for women over 35 years old.1 The factors contributing to FI are diverse and complex,2 posing challenges to treatment. Treatment for FI can be costly, placing a financial strain on patients and families, as well as requiring significant healthcare and professional resources. Globally, an estimated 12% of married women experience FI, impacting not only individual physical and mental health but also society and countries at large.3 The disease burden of FI can lead to population decline, labour shortages, increased pressure on social security systems and limited economic development.4 Despite these significant consequences, FI remains an under-recognised public health issue, with limited studies investigating its global distribution and trends over time. Previous research using Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data has shed light on the prevalence and trends of infertility on a global scale. Sun et al1 examined infertility prevalence and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) across 195 countries and territories from 1990 to 2017, highlighting significant regional disparities and temporal patterns. Borumandnia et al3 investigated infertility rate trends in 198 countries and territories, emphasising the impact of socioeconomic and geographical factors on these rates. Furthermore, Bhattacharjee et al4 conducted a thorough demographic analysis of global fertility trends spanning from 1950 to 2021, with projections extending to 2100. Their work underscored the intricate relationship between demographic variables and fertility and infertility rates.

Previous studies have provided a foundation for comprehending the worldwide scope of infertility. Our research further contributes to this field by concentrating on FI in the 20–49 years age group and analysing trends from 1990 to 2019 with the latest GBD data. The GBD 2019 database has systematically collected, organised and analysed epidemiological data on a wide range of diseases globally to assess their impact on global health. This study used the GBD 2019 database to analyse the GBD for FI among adults aged 20–49 years from 1990 to 2019. The goal was to provide insights into the trends and distribution of FI burden worldwide, offering valuable information for FI management globally.

Methods

Data sources

The data for this study were sourced from the GBD 2019 database, which consolidates disease-related data from various sources such as disease registries, cause-of-death statistics, and medical records across multiple countries and regions worldwide. It provides health status information categorised by age group and gender for all countries and regions. The database serves as a tool for comprehensive assessment of the GBDs and facilitates comparisons of health status across different regions and countries.5 6 This study used publicly available data and did not involve ethical approval.

Definitions

Infertility is clinically defined as the inability to achieve pregnancy after 1 year of regular, unprotected intercourse, or after 6 months for women over 35 years of age. Infertility is classified as primary or secondary depending on whether a pregnancy has been achieved before. Primary infertility refers to the inability to achieve a clinical pregnancy, while secondary infertility occurs after at least one previous clinical pregnancy. Diseases are classified using the International Classification of Diseases, 11th edition codes for FI (GA 31.0, GA 31.1 and GA 31.Z).

Analysis

The study used DALYs and DALY rates for FI from the GBD 2019 database across different regions and countries from 1990 to 2019 as key indicators for analysis. DALYs serve as a comprehensive measure of the overall health impact of a disease, quantifying the number of years of healthy life lost due to a specific disease or injury over a specified time period. DALYs are calculated as the sum of years of life lost (YLL) from premature death and years lived with disability (YLD): DALYs = YLL + YLD. DALYs are not only used to measure the number of healthy years lost due to a specific disease or injury over time, but also to evaluate the overall health status of diverse populations, regions or countries. The DALY rate is determined by comparing DALYs to the total population or a specific group, typically presented as the disease burden per 1000 individuals in a particular area, year or demographic. A higher DALY rate indicates a more significant disease burden for that region or population group.

Comparisons between regions with varying population structures, or between different years within the same region for disease indicators, are typically conducted using the age-standardised rate (ASR) per 100 000 population. The ASR is calculated as follows:

ASR=∑i=1Aaiwi∑i=1Awi×100000

where ai denotes a particular disease burden indicator for the ith age group, for example, the DALY rate, and wi denotes the corresponding standardised population weight.7 In this study, we standardised the data by age and used the age-standardised DALY rates for various countries and regions between 1990 and 2019. Furthermore, we employed the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) along with its 95% CIs to describe notable shifts in the overall trends of FI from 1990 to 2019.8

The EAPC is commonly used for evaluating trends in disease burden indicators over time and is computed using the following formula:

y = α+βx+ε, y = ln(age−standardised rate)

EAPC = 100 ×(exp(β)−1)

The Sociodemographic Index (SDI) is a holistic indicator that integrates economic, educational and reproductive data to assess the level of social development.9 Typically ranging from 0 to 1, higher values signify regions with more advanced socioeconomic and demographic progress. We classified global regions into five categories based on SDI levels—high, medium-high, medium, medium-low and low—and examined the corresponding disease burden status in each group. The growth proportion is calculated as the percentage change in DALYs between 1990 and 2019. It serves as a metric to assess the relative increase or decrease in the burden of a disease over a specific time period.

Statistical analysis and data visualisation for this study were conducted using R V.4.3.1 software.

Results

DALYs and age-standardised DALY rates and changes in FI in different global regions during 1990–2019

The age-standardised DALY rate for FI among adults aged 20–49 years globally in 2019 was 41.44 (95% uncertainty interval (UI) 41.34 to 41.54), showing an increase compared with 1990 (32.89, 95% UI 32.78 to 33.00) (table 1, figure 1). The global DALYs for FI from 1990 to 2019 exhibited a rising trend (EAPC: 0.53, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.80), with a growth proportion of 1.87. Geographically, regions such as East Asia, Eastern Europe, Oceania, South-East Asia and Southern sub-Saharan Africa had high age-standardised DALY rates in both 1990 and 2019 (figure 1). East Asia had the highest DALYs of all regions in 1990, with 162 922.06 DALYs, nearly half of the global total (table 1, figure 1); by 2019 East Asia reported 201 904.58 DALYs with a growth proportion of only 1.24. South Asia recorded 182 358.36 DALYs in 2019, a growth proportion 3.06 times higher than in 1990 (59 to 595.56). The most significant increase in DALYs was observed in Andean Latin America (EAPC: 8.54, 95% CI 6.79 to 10.32), with a growth proportion of 7.39 (table 1).

Global DALYs and DALY rates for FI and related trends, 1990–2019

The distribution of DALYs for FI among adults aged 20–49 years globally in 1990 and 2019 can be observed in online supplemental figure 1. In 1990, the countries recording the highest DALYs were China (158 801.0), India (45 657.4), the Russian Federation (18 069.1), Indonesia (14 631.7) and Pakistan (10 688.3) (online supplemental table 1). By 2019 the countries with the highest DALYs had shifted to China (197 046.5), India (166 332.8), Indonesia (35 370.1), Nigeria (20 792.3) and the Philippines (16 659.8) (online supplemental table 1). Notably, China and India maintained their positions as the top two countries with the highest DALYs for FI disorders globally, both in 1990 and 2019.

Online supplemental figure 2 illustrates the global distribution of DALY rates for FI per 100 000 individuals aged 20–49 years in 1990 and 2019. Significant variations in DALY rates are evident across different countries. In 1990, the Philippines (95.73), Djibouti (91.22), Gabon (81.89), Comoros (72.55) and the Maldives (71.06) were among the countries with the highest DALY rates, while Peru (0.51), Australia (1.26) and Ecuador (1.26) had the lowest DALY rates. Ecuador (1.35), New Zealand (1.43) and Denmark (1.54) also exhibited low DALY rates and disease burdens (online supplemental table 1). By 2019 the Central African Republic (91.77), Djibouti (85.40), the Philippines (84.72), Comoros (76.68) and Eritrea (73.66) had some of the highest DALY rates globally, while Australia (1.11), Peru (1.36), New Zealand (2.99) and Denmark (3.89) maintained low DALY rates (online supplemental table 2).

Figure 2 illustrates the changes in DALYs and DALY rates related to FI across countries globally from 1990 to 2019. The data highlight distinct regional variations in the burden of disease attributed to FI, with countries in Africa and Latin America experiencing significant increases in disease burden (figure 2A). Ecuador and Togo exhibited the most substantial growth in DALYs between 1990 and 2019, with growth rates of 1122% and 1012%, respectively (online supplemental table 1). Additionally, Jordan (991%), Colombia (889%), Ghana (761%) and 52 other countries observed growth rates ranging from 300% to 1000%. Similarly, Somalia (296%), Bahrain (296%), Uganda (293%) and 114 other countries saw growth rates between 100% and 300% (online supplemental table 1). Conversely, 36 countries, including the Cook Islands (98%), Sweden (98%) and Mauritius (97%), experienced growth rates below 100%, leading to a decline in DALYs from 1990 to 2019. Based on the EAPC values, countries experiencing a rise in DALYs were primarily located in Latin America, Africa, the Middle East and the Mediterranean (figure 2B). Ecuador exhibited the highest EAPC at 13.39 (95% CI 10.64 to 16.2), followed by the Philippines at 10.23 (95% CI 6.3 to 14.31), Bolivia at 9.84 (95% CI 6.38 to 13.42) and Mali at 8.75 (95% CI 8.05 to 9.45). These countries showed a noticeable upward trend in DALYs from 1990 to 2019. In terms of EAPCs for age-standardised DALY rates, countries in Latin America, Africa and the Mediterranean continued to see significant increases in the burden of disease for FI (figure 2B). However, within these regions, Niger had an EAPC of −1.48 (95% CI −2.5 to –0.64), Congo had −1.20 (95% CI −2.20 to –0.19) and Mozambique had −1.05 (95% CI −1.92 to –0.17) between 1990 and 2019, indicating a decreasing trend in the age-standardised DALY rate （figure 2C）.

Discussion

The findings of this study suggest that the GBD related to FI among adults aged 20–49 years has increased from 1990 to 2019. Geographically, the burden of FI varied across regions, with higher rates observed in East Asia, South Asia and Andean Latin America compared with other regions. This was further supported by the global distribution of disease burden among adults aged 20–49 years. Notable disparities in disease burden profiles were noted among countries, with China and India showing the highest DALYs for FI globally in both 1990 and 2019. Several other countries in Asia and Africa also exhibited high DALY rates. Moreover, countries in Latin America and Africa experienced significant increases in disease burden over time. In conclusion, there were substantial regional disparities in the GBD related to FI, with Asia, Latin America and Africa bearing a heavier burden compared with other regions.

Several studies conducted in regions such as Asia, Latin America and Africa1012 have identified various risk factors associated with FI. These factors include exposure to toxic substances, HIV infection, reproductive tract infections, early age at first birth, inadequate care following unsafe abortions and postpartum infections. The agrarian nature of these countries often results in poor health and hygiene practices, further increasing women’s exposure to harmful chemicals10 and subsequently raising the risk of FI. Moreover, the prevalence of HIV infection in these regions, particularly in Africa, not only impacts fertility directly but also heightens the likelihood of other reproductive tract infections.11

Sociocultural factors in various regions significantly impact FI. Factors such as early marriage, young age at first birth and inadequate care after unsafe abortions can increase the risk of FI to some extent.12 These sociocultural factors play a role in influencing women’s reproductive decisions and health behaviours. The lack of knowledge and awareness about FI, including its causes, treatment and prevention, along with a lack of understanding about the importance of seeking medical help, further exacerbates the burden of FI.13 The higher prevalence of FI in Asia, Africa and Latin America compared with other regions poses a significant epidemiological risk in terms of disease burden.14 Limited healthcare resources in these regions, such as equipment, technology and specialised personnel for diagnosis and treatment, result in inadequate access to healthcare services for FI patients,1517 further increasing the disease burden. These findings highlight the geographical variability and severity of FI, particularly in disadvantaged regions of Asia, Africa and Latin America, underscoring the importance of developing targeted interventions for these areas.

The GBD for FI among adults aged 20–49 years varied between countries based on their SDI levels from 1990 to 2019. Low-middle-SDI and low-SDI countries experienced significant increases in the burden of disease, while high-SDI countries had a lower overall burden but the highest rate of increase. Multiple factors contribute to these differences: in high-SDI countries, the population generally has higher education levels and women are more engaged in occupational and educational activities, which delays childbearing plans and increases the risk of FI.17 Despite the relatively low burden of FI in high-SDI countries, the trend of delayed childbearing has led to an increase in FI cases. Disparities in infertility care services exist between developed and less-developed countries, with high-SDI countries having better access to advanced healthcare resources and technologies. While some complementary treatment programmes have been available for a long time,18 individuals in less-developed countries may face challenges in accessing these programmes due to limited public funding among other factors.19 The varying levels of SDI among countries have led to differences in DALYs related to FI. High-SDI countries generally have superior diagnostic and treatment options for FI, resulting in better outcomes and more effective treatment approaches.20 Conversely, lower SDI countries may experience challenges due to inadequate healthcare resources, such as limited specialised facilities, outdated medical technologies and shortages of medical personnel for infertility care. These limitations can hinder patients from accessing necessary medical support and treatment, thereby exacerbating the burden of disease. Various factors including cultural norms, economic status and healthcare systems influence the burden of FI differently across countries and regions. Further research and analysis are essential to comprehensively understand these disparities and implement appropriate strategies to alleviate the burden of FI.

Analysis of the global age distribution of the DALY rate for FI in 2019 revealed a trend of increasing rates with age, peaking among adults aged 40–44 years, while the rate among those aged 45–49 years was comparatively low. Maheshwari et al21 conducted a diagnostic survey of 7172 women to investigate the relationship between age and causes of FI. The study found a correlation between women’s age and the causes of infertility, indicating differences between older and younger women. Notably, the likelihood of unexplained infertility was significantly higher in women over 35 years old. This underscores the need for more detailed and comprehensive research on infertility in women across different age groups. Future studies could delve into the fertility intentions and reproductive health of younger women, as well as the infertility risks and treatment requirements of middle-aged and older women. Such targeted investigations would facilitate the development of interventions aimed at alleviating the burden of infertility in women of varying ages.

This study investigated the distribution and changes in burden of disease indicators, specifically focusing on DALYs and DALY rates of FI among adults aged 20–49 years globally, analysing variations by region, country and age. By comparing DALYs and DALY rates from 1990 to 2019, the study provided quantitative insights into the global burden of FI, shedding light on its scale, impact and distribution. It also synthesised global trends in FI burden from diverse perspectives. However, the study has limitations, including potential gaps or inaccuracies in data from certain regions or populations, which could have affected the precision of disease burden estimates. Moreover, disparities in data collection and reporting practices among countries and regions may have led to inaccuracies in the study findings. While our analysis primarily focuses on comparing data from 1990 and 2019, it is essential to acknowledge that GBD data for intermediate time points are also available. Assuming linear trends between these two points may not fully capture potential non-linear trends and variations across regions or SDI groups. Using more detailed time series data could offer insights into these non-linear trends and reveal variations that may not be evident from a straightforward comparison of just two time points.

As the global burden of FI continues to rise, there is a growing need to enhance research efforts focused on FI, delve deeper into the associated burden of disease, and establish a scientific foundation for the development of more effective intervention strategies. Our study aims to investigate and evaluate the impact of various factors on the burden of disease related to FI, such as sociocultural and economic aspects, geographical variations, education levels, and healthcare resources. By gaining a comprehensive understanding of the interplay between these factors, we can more accurately assess and explain the formation and changes in the burden of disease associated with FI.

Conclusions

Analysis of data from the GBD Study revealed a rising trend in FI among adults aged 20–49 years globally from 1990 to 2019, with notable variations across different regions. The escalation of disease burden was particularly prominent in high-SDI countries, although low-middle-SDI and low-SDI countries also experienced significant increases.
