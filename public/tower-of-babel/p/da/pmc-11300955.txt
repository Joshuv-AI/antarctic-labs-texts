A Predictive Model for Initial Platinum‐Based Chemotherapy Efficacy in Patients with Postoperative Epithelial Ovarian Cancer Using Tissue‐Derived Small Extracellular Vesicles
Authors: Shizhen Shen, Conghui Wang, Jiaxin Gu, Feifei Song, Xiaodong Wu, Fangfang Qian, Xiaojing Chen, Lingfang Wang, Qiaohua Peng, Ziyu Xing, Lingkai Gu, Fenfen Wang, Xiaodong Cheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) is an often‐fatal malignancy marked by the development of resistance to platinum‐based chemotherapy. Thus, accurate prediction of platinum drug efficacy is crucial for strategically selecting postoperative interventions to mitigate the risks associated with suboptimal therapeutic outcomes and adverse effects. Tissue‐derived extracellular vesicles (tsEVs), in contrast to their plasma counterparts, have emerged as a powerful tool for examining distinctive attributes of EOC tissues. In this study, 4D data‐independent acquisition (DIA) proteomic sequencing was performed on tsEVs obtained from 58 platinum‐sensitive and 30 platinum‐resistant patients with EOC. The analysis revealed a notable enrichment of differentially expressed proteins that were predominantly associated with immune‐related pathways. Moreover, pivotal immune‐related proteins (IRPs) were identified by LASSO regression. These factors, combined with clinical parameters selected through univariate logistic regression, were used for the construction of a model employing multivariate logistic regression. This model integrated three tsEV IRPs, CCR1, IGHV_35 and CD72, with one clinical parameter, the presence of postoperative residual lesions. Thus, this model could predict the efficacy of initial platinum‐based chemotherapy in patients with EOC post‐surgery, providing prognostic insights even before the initiation of chemotherapy.Keywords: epithelial ovarian cancer, platinum‐based chemotherapy, predictive model, tissue‐derived small extracellular vesicles

INTRODUCTION

Ovarian cancer remains the most lethal malignancy of the female reproductive system, with epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) comprising 80% of these cases (Reid et al., 2017; Torre et al., 2018). The therapeutic approach for EOC involves maximal cytoreductive surgery followed by platinum‐based chemotherapy. Although approximately 80% of patients with EOC experience symptom alleviation after the initial surgery and chemotherapy, approximately 15% of the patients experience platinum‐resistant recurrence within a short timeframe (within 6 months) after the completion of chemotherapy (Yang et al., 2022). Additionally, a subset of patients with EOC exhibit poor responsiveness to platinum‐based chemotherapy from the outset, characterized by failure of the residual lesions to shrink or an inadequate decrease in serum CA125 levels during chemotherapy, a phenomenon termed primary platinum resistance (Matulonis et al., 2016). Existing biomarkers have shown value in predicting patient responses to platinum‐based chemotherapy; however, their precision is not ideal. Therefore, the identification of novel molecular markers to enhance the predictive efficacy of platinum resistance remains a crucial scientific challenge in the current landscape of EOC research (Bonifácio, 2020).

Extracellular vesicles (EVs), particularly small EVs (sEVs), transport various cell‐derived biological components, including proteins, nucleic acids, lipids and various metabolites (Yuana et al., 2013). As carriers of biological information, the study of the molecules contained within sEVs opens up a new field for the discovery of novel biomarkers. Protected by a phospholipid bilayer membrane structure, substances within sEVs exhibit higher stability than traditional liquid biopsy markers, providing an important window for capturing the state of source cells (Shuhong Wang et al., 2022).

Plasma‐derived sEV (psEV) proteins, in particular, have been extensively researched in the field of tumour diagnosis (Wang et al., 2022). For instance, in non‐small cell lung cancer, researchers constructed a diagnostic model consisting of proteins by capturing sEVs from the plasma of patients using an exosomal protein chip (Fan et al., 2020). In colorectal cancer (CRC), researchers identified psEV protein and fibrinogen α chain that could effectively differentiate between healthy individuals and those with early‐stage CRC. They also established a rapid detection method based on data‐independent acquisition mass spectrometry (DIA‐MS) technology (Zheng et al., 2020). In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the level of polymeric immunoglobulin receptor in psEVs of patients with HCC was significantly higher than that in the healthy cohort, and its predictive effect on HCC surpassed that of the commonly used α‐fetoprotein (Arbelaiz et al., 2017).

Nevertheless, when it comes to predicting the therapeutic efficacy or prognosis of patients with cancer, most research involves the omics sequencing of tumour tissues to identify molecular biomarkers. In 2017, a systematic and rigorous method for extracting tissue‐derived sEVs (tsEVs) was established (Vella et al., 2017), enabling the isolation of intercellular sEVs from various tissues. While ongoing research primarily focuses on optimizing extraction methods or identifying subgroups of tsEVs (Cheng et al., 2020; Crescitelli et al., 2020, 2021; Huang et al., 2020; Jang et al., 2019; Muraoka et al., 2020), there is currently a lack of detailed studies analyzing the correlation between tsEV content and therapeutic outcomes in patients with cancer.

In the initial phase of our research, we optimized the extraction method for EOC tsEVs and confirmed that there was no significant difference in protein composition between sEVs extracted from tissues stored at −80°C and those extracted from fresh tissues (Shen et al., 2023). Given this background, we used EOC tissues obtained within the past 2 years and stored at −80°C for our investigation. By extracting tsEVs from these tissues and using their protein expression profiles and clinical parameters as independent variables, we aimed to construct a machine‐learning model for predicting the efficacy of platinum‐based chemotherapy in EOC prior to its initiation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient cohort screening and clinical data collection

Inclusion criteria

Patients with EOC who underwent initial tumour debulking surgery at the Women's Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Zhejiang, China, between January 2021 and June 2022, were included in the study. The participants of the study were over 18 years old, had EOC tumours in situ with a diameter exceeding 5 cm, had no history of malignancy at other sites, and had not received neoadjuvant chemotherapy before surgery. The patients underwent six cycles of routine platinum‐based chemotherapy postoperatively. Comprehensive clinical and pathological data were collected, and all patients were followed up for efficacy assessment until 6 months after the completion of chemotherapy.

Over 150 patients who met the aforementioned criteria and provided reliable follow‐up information were included, of whom 33 were platinum‐resistant. Due to the low proportion of platinum‐resistant patients, all eligible platinum‐resistant tissue samples were included, and platinum‐sensitive samples were included in a 1:2 ratio based on baseline matching. Upon subsequent tsEV sample quality control, the study ultimately enrolled 58 and 30 platinum‐sensitive and resistant patients with EOC, respectively. Table S1 summarizes the clinical and pathological information of these 88 patients.

EOC tissue sample acquisition and storage

EOC tissue blocks (>0.5 g) were obtained from the in situ EOC lesions at the site of surgical resection (without affecting the patient's pathological diagnosis or subsequent clinical treatment). These tissue blocks were placed in 50 mL centrifuge tubes containing 15 mL of RPMI 1640 serum‐free culture medium mixed with 1% penicillin‐streptomycin solution. Samples were collected under aseptic conditions and immediately transported to the laboratory after collection. The tissues were washed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), transferred to cryotubes, labelled with patient information, and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen before being stored at −80°C in a freezer.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Women's Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Ethical Approval No: IRB‐20210220‐R) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Extraction of tsEVs from EOC tissues

Tissue dissociation

EOC tissues were aseptically placed in a sterile culture dish, thawed on ice, washed twice with PBS, and weighed after removing the surface adipose and connective tissues. The tissue was mechanically fragmented using a blade with a diameter of less than 2 mm. Subsequently, each gram of tissue was added to 10 mL of RPMI 1640 medium containing type I collagenase (1 mg/mL) and type I DNase (0.2 mg/mL). This mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 min for enzymatic digestion. The culture dish was then retrieved, and the resultant tissue dissociation mixture was aspirated into a 50‐mL centrifuge tube using a pipette. Centrifugation was performed at 4°C at a rotational speed of 300 × g for 10 min. After two centrifugation cycles, the supernatant was meticulously extracted to obtain the tissue lysate.

Isolation of tsEVs

To isolate tsEVs, the tissue lysate was filtered through a 70‐µm pore size filter at 4°C, followed by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 15 min and subsequent centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 30 min. The supernatant was then carefully aspirated. Subsequently, an ultracentrifuge was performed at 4°C and a rotational speed of 100,000 × g for 90 min, and the resulting supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of PBS and loaded into a size‐exclusion column (Echo Biotech, Beijing, China). As the liquid level descended to the upper sieve plate without flow from the bottom collection port, PBS washes were added in 500‐µL increments. Fractions 3−7 were collected and transferred to ultracentrifuge tubes. Centrifugation was again performed at 100,000 × g and 4°C for 90 min. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 200 µL PBS and transferred to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube, representing one sample of tsEVs.

Plasma‐sEV (psEV) extraction from patients with EOC

Plasma samples

Since June 2022, upon informed consent, 5 mL of preoperative blood was collected from each patient with EOC treated in our hospital. The blood plasma was separated and stored at −80°C. Moreover, the chemotherapeutic effect in each patient was followed up on after surgery. In this study, samples were selected from 10 and 10 platinum‐sensitive and resistant patients with EOC, respectively, to extract sEVs for the external validation of the model indicators. Table S2 presents the detailed clinical parameters of these 20 patients. This assessment was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Women's Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Ethical Approval No: IRB‐20220346‐R).

psEV isolation

After thawing the aforementioned 5 mL of plasma sample, it was centrifuged at 2000 g and 4°C for 15 min, then the supernatant was extracted and centrifuged at 10,000 g and 4°C for 30 min. Approximately 2.5 mL of the resulting supernatant was poured into the prepared size‐exclusion column (Echo Biotech, Beijing, China). Once the column material absorbed the entire sample (with no liquid remaining above the upper sieve and none dripping from the outlet below), a 2‐mL collection tube was positioned beneath it, it was washed with PBS, and the eluate was collected in 1‐mL increments until reaching a total of 2 mL. Next, the eluate was poured into an ultracentrifuge tube and centrifuged at 120,000 g and 4°C for 90 min. Finally, the supernatant was discarded and the sediment was carefully resuspended in 200 µL of PBS to obtain the purified psEVs.

Western blotting and enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Proteins extracted from EOC tissues or EVs were separated using 10% sodium dodecyl‐sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) (GenScript Biotech, Piscataway, NJ, USA) and transferred to 0.2‐µm polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (1620177, Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) using an eBlot L1 Fast Wet Transfer System (L00686C, GenScript Biotech). The membranes were then blocked with 5% milk. After blocking, primary and secondary antibodies were added to the membranes according to the manufacturer's protocol. Membranes were treated with the FDbio‐Pico ECL Kit (Fude Biological Technology, Hangzhou, China) and visualized using ImageQuant LAS 4000mini (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) (Kowal et al., 2017).

The primary antibodies used were as follows: anti‐CD81, anti‐HSP70 (1:1,000, ExoAb Antibody Kit, System Biosciences, Palo Alto, CA, USA), anti‐CCR1, anti‐CD72 (1:1,000, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), anti‐TSG101 (C‐2) and anti‐calnexin (AF18) (1:200; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA). The secondary antibodies used were horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated anti‐rabbit IgG and anti‐mouse IgG (1:5,000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China). CCR1 and CD72 ELISA kits (human, Cusabio, Wuhan, China) were used for further validation of the expression of CCR1 and CD72.

RNA extraction and real‐time quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the cells using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA was reverse transcribed using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with a gDNA eraser (Perfect Real Time) (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). PCR was conducted using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Takara Bio), which includes the TB Green fluorescent dye for real‐time PCR detection. The reactions were performed on a 7900HT Fast Real‐Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The primer sequences are listed in Table 1. The relative mRNA expression was normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Tissue protein extraction and protein concentration assay

Two hundred microliters of radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (containing 1% phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride) were added to 0.2 g of tissue, and the mixture was vigorously shaken with steel beads for 20 min. Afterward, the sample was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C, and the resulting supernatant was carefully removed. The concentrations of proteins in both EOC tissue and tsEVs were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), with subsequent adjustments to a concentration of 1 µg/µL. The protein solution underwent denaturation at 100°C for 20 min in 5× loading buffer (Fude Biological Technology) and was subsequently stored at −80°C for further analysis (Hosseini et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2019).

4D DIA‐MS PROTEOMICS

Enzymatic digestion and desalting

The tsEV solution was supplemented with a final concentration of 5 mM dithiothreitol and incubated at 37°C for 1 h, followed by thawing to room temperature. Subsequently, a final concentration of 10 mM iodoacetamide was added, and the solution was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 45 min. The sample was then diluted four‐fold with 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate, and trypsin was added at a protein‐to‐enzyme ratio of 50:1. The mixture was incubated overnight at 37°C. The following day, formic acid was added to adjust the pH to less than 3, thereby terminating the enzymatic digestion. The sample was desalted using a C18 column activated with 100% acetonitrile and balanced with 0.1% formic acid. Each sample was loaded onto the column, washed with 0.1% formic acid to remove impurities, and eluted with 70% acetonitrile to collect the flow‐through. Peptide quantification was performed at this stage, and equal quantities of peptides (10 µL or the minimum peptide quantity per sample) were freeze‐dried.

Liquid chromatography‐MS (LC‐MS) analysis

Separation was performed using the nanoElute ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA). Mobile phase A consisted of a water solution containing 0.1% formic acid and 2% acetonitrile, and mobile phase B consisted of an acetonitrile‐water solution containing 0.1% formic acid. The liquid‐phase gradient settings were as follows:

0−10 min: ∼9%−24% B

10−14 min: ∼24%−35% B

14−17 min: ∼35%−80% B

17−20 min: 80% B

The flow rate was maintained at 1000 mL/min. Peptides separated by the UHPLC system were ionized in a capillary source and subjected to data collection using the timsTOF Pro MS system (Bruker). The ion source voltage was set to 2.0 kV, and both the precursor ions and their secondary fragments were detected and analyzed using time‐of‐flight. Data collection utilized the DIA parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (DIA‐PASEF) mode. The first MS scan range was set to 100−1700 m/z, followed by 10 PASEF scans after each primary scan. The secondary MS scans ranged from 100 to 1700 m/z with 25 m/z windows.

Peptide database search

MaxQuant v.1.6.15.0 software was employed to process raw MS data. Peptide identification, matching, and integration were performed using the UniProtKB database. Key parameters included the enzyme set as trypsin, precursor ion mass tolerance set at 20 ppm, fragment ion mass tolerance set at 0.02 Da, and a maximum of two missed cleavages allowed. The minimum score for peptide detection was set at 40. Stringency criteria for accurate identification were set at 1% false discovery rate (FDR) at the spectral, peptide and protein levels. Protein identification requires at least one unique peptide. The intensity values were log2‐transformed, and a new expression matrix was constructed by averaging identical protein names for subsequent analysis.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

Twenty microliters of the tsEV suspension were appropriately diluted in PBS to achieve concentrations ranging from 1×107 to 1×109 EVs/mL. The diluted suspension was examined using the ZetaView PMX 110 system (Particle Metrix, Meerbusch, Germany) equipped with a 405‐nm laser for the precise determination of both the size and quantity of the isolated nanoparticles (Desgeorges et al., 2020). Specific nanoparticle concentrations were calculated based on the number of scattered particles. A comprehensive nanoparticle size distribution map was constructed by monitoring the Brownian motion trajectories of the nanoparticles, and the mean‐squared displacement of the nanoparticles per unit time interval was calculated. The pertinent parameters for this analysis included a maximum particle size of 3000 nm and a minimum particle size of 1 nm.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The tsEV samples were negatively stained using a previously established method (Svennerholm et al., 2017) and subsequently observed using TEM. In brief, 5 µL of the tsEV solution was diluted 5‐fold and carefully applied onto a copper mesh at room temperature for 1 min. Excess liquid was absorbed using filter paper. Subsequently, a droplet of 1% uranyl acetate was deposited onto the copper mesh, and after 1 min, the excess stain was blotted away using filter paper. The copper mesh was air‐dried for 2 min under incandescent light before being photographed using a JEM‐1400 flash microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) operating at 80 kV.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) slides were baked in an oven at 65°C for 4 h, dewaxed with xylene, rehydrated using ethanol solutions and incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was performed by microwave treatment (MWT) with citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking with normal Goat Serum (10%) for 10 min, the samples were incubated with an anti‐CCR1 antibody (1:400, ab139399, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) overnight at 4°C. Staining was performed using 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB). The IHC scores ranged between 0 and 12 and were calculated by multiplying the staining intensity score (ranging between 0 and 3, indicating negative, weak, moderate and strong staining, respectively) with that of the positive cell proportions (ranging between 0 and 4, representing negative as well as <10%, 10%−50%, 50−75% and >75% positive cells in the staining area, respectively).

Multiplex IHC staining

Multicolour IHC staining was performed using a PANO IHC kit (Panovue, Beijing, China). The deparaffinization and rehydration processes were similar to those implemented for IHC. Antigen retrieval was performed by MWT using an antigen retrieval buffer (pH 6.0 or 9.0). Primary antibody incubation was followed by horse radish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibody treatment and fluorophore working solution to generate a fluorescent signal. As the fluorescent signal relies on covalent binding and is not affected by MWT, MWT was then applied to remove the detected antibody, while conserving the fluorescent signal. The procedures including MWT, primary and HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody incubation as well as signal generation were repeated for the remaining antibodies. Nuclei were stained with 4′−6′‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI, Sigma‐Aldrich), then the slides were mounted with mounting medium and covered with coverslips. The primary antibodies used for multiplexed IHC staining targeted CD4 (1:400, ab133616, Abcam, USA), CD8α (1:100, D8A8Y, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), CD68 (1:5000, E3O7V, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), CD163 (1:400, ab182422, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) and CK (1:5, Gene Tech, Shanghai, China). Images were captured at 20 × magnification (Vectra Polaris, Perkin Elmer, USA) and analyzed with inForm 2.6 software, including spectral unmixing, tissue segment, cell segment, phenotype and IHC scoring. The tissues were segmented into tumour and stromal cells, and the cells into the nucleus, cytoplasm and membrane compartments. The phenotyping algorithm was trained by assigning 10 cells to each phenotype. The cells were reclassified as marker‐positive if their IHC score was beyond the threshold. Stromal cells were classified as CD4+, CD8+, CD68+, CD163+ and so forth.

Homologous recombination repair (HRR) functionality assessment

The MagPure FFPE DNA LQ Kit from Magen was used to extract genomic DNA from FFPE tumour tissues. For library construction, a total of 100 ng of DNA was used, and 500 ng of these libraries were subsequently applied for hybrid capture with the AmoyDx® HRD Complete Panel (AmoyDx, Shanghai, China), encompassing 24K and 20 HRR6 linked SNPs and genes, respectively. The DNA libraries were sequenced via the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform, using Paired‐End Reads with 2×150 cycles. Following the mapping of the processed raw data to the human reference genome (hg19) and subsequent correction by UMI, the data was subjected to statistical analysis. Key quality control metrics, including coverage, DepthNoise index and BAFNoise index, were computed. Ideally, the coverage, DepthNoise index and BAFNoise index should be minimum of 95%, maximum 0.35 and maximum 0.05, respectively.

By examining SNP frequency shifts within the tumour tissues, chromosomes were segmented based on their copy number variations. These chromosomal copy number (CN) segments were then categorized based on physical attributes such as length (large: >15 Mb, middle: ∼10–15 Mb and small: <5 Mb), location (telomere, centromere and other) and type (LOH, allele‐specific CNV, and allele‐balanced CNV). In total, 28 distinct CNV features were constructed, encompassing combinations of the three CNV lengths, sites and types. The number of breakpoints spread across the genome. Leveraging the CNV features of each sample and their respective weights, the GS (genomic scar) score was determined using the GS model.

The gene variations detected within the CDS regions of the 20 genes encompassed base substitutions, small insertions and deletions. The sequencing process ensured an average effective depth of at least 180×, with a variation detection limit set at 3%. The classification of these gene variations adhered to the guidelines established by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG), resulting in five distinct categories: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variants of unknown significance (VUS), likely benign and benign. The list of 20 genes encompassed 16 and 4 HRR and genetics‐ and therapy‐related genes, respectively. Samples with GS scores of minimum 45 or the presence of a pathogenic or likely pathogenic BRCA1/2 mutation were ascribed GSS‐positive (Fountzilas et al., 2023).

Bioinformatics analysis

The GSE15372, GSE33482, GSE26712 and GSE63885 microarray datasets were acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE15372 and GSE33482 represent EOC cell line‐derived RNA microarray datasets, comprising 5 and 5 and 6 and 6 platinum‐sensitive and resistant cell line samples, respectively. In addition, GSE26712 and GSE63885 derive from tumour tissues of patients with EOC, most of whom have accompanying follow‐up data. GSE26712 and GSE63885 involve 195 and 101 EOC patients, respectively. The Cancer Genome Atlas Ovarian Cancer (TCGA‐OV) is a compilation that includes transcriptomic sequencing data and clinical information from the tumour tissues of 379 patients with EOC, sourced from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Samples with missing clinical information were potentially excluded from the analysis when constructing Kaplan–Meier curves based on data from the GSE26712, GSE63885 and TCGA‐OV datasets.

Principal component analysis (PCA) and heat maps were generated using Metware Cloud, a freely accessible online platform for data analysis (https://cloud.metware.cn). Protein‐protein interaction networks were constructed using data from the STRING website and visualized using Cytoscape software. Pathway Interaction Database (PID) analysis was performed using the Sangerbox platform (http://vip.sangerbox.com/home.html) (Shen et al., 2022). GO analysis was performed with the ‘clusterProfiler’ package in R software.

The cut‐off for differentially expressed proteins was uniformly set at log2 fold‐change (FC) > 1 and FDR < 0.05, which were identified with t‐tests using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Random grouping of training and testing sets was performed with the ‘Car’ and ‘Survival’ packages in R software. The statistical table for clinical data was constructed with the ‘Tableone’ package in R software. Lasso regression was conducted with the ‘Glmnet’ package in R software. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed with the ‘Rms’ package in R software. Nomogram and calibration curves were generated and plotted with the ‘Rms’ package in R software. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was plotted with the ‘pROC’ package in R software. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed and depicted with the ‘Survival’ and ‘Survminer’ packages in R software, while the analysis treating progression‐free survival as the primary outcome was supported by the KMPLOT website (https://kmplot.com/analysis/). Grouped scatterplots were created using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), with final adjustments and layouts in Adobe Illustrator.

Assigning codes to categorical variables

For the outcome variable, platinum sensitivity was designated as 0 and platinum resistance as 1; for Fédération Internationale de Gynécologie et d'Obstétrique (FIGO) staging, stages I and II were coded as 0, whereas stages III and IV were coded as 1; for pathological subtypes, high‐grade serous carcinoma was assigned 0, and clear cell carcinoma and endometrioid carcinoma were assigned 1; for homologous recombination repair functional status (HR_Status), HR_deficient (HRD) was coded as 0, whereas HR_proficient (HRP) was coded as 1; for postoperative residual lesions, R0 (microscopically negative for residual tumor) was coded as 0, and >R0 was coded as 1.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software) and R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and the Student's t‐test was employed for intergroup comparisons. For non‐normally distributed data, the results are expressed as median ± interquartile range. The Mann–Whitney test, a non‐parametric rank‐sum test, was utilized for between‐group comparisons. Categorical variables were assessed using the chi‐squared test for multiple groups.

Categorical variables included FIGO stage, pathological type, HR repair status and postoperative residual lesions. Age and body mass index (BMI) were considered normally distributed continuous variables, whereas CA125 and CA153 levels were treated as non‐normally distributed continuous variables. Statistical significance was denoted as follows: ns for p > 0.05; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001. A significance level of p < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

RESULTS

Schematic diagram of the study and the clinical parameters of the patients

Between January 2021 and June 2022, more than 200 patients diagnosed with EOC underwent debulking surgery at the Women's Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Tumour tissue specimens were collected from all patients. More than 150 patients received complete chemotherapy postoperatively and provided follow‐up information 6 months after completing chemotherapy. The patients were classified based on the definition of platinum sensitivity and resistance in EOC, that is, the patients were labelled as platinum‐sensitive if no recurrence occurred within 6 months after the last chemotherapy; otherwise, they were deemed platinum‐resistant. Given the need for patients with complete clinical and pathological information and the relatively low proportion of platinum‐resistant patients at initial treatment in clinical practice (approximately 10%−20%), all available platinum‐resistant tissue samples were included. A 1:2 ratio was used to randomly match platinum‐sensitive samples based on clinical data, resulting in 95 EOC in situ tissue samples (including 62 platinum‐sensitive patients and 33 patients with platinum‐resistant recurrence or primary resistance).

Following the technical methods outlined in Figure 1a, tsEVs were extracted from 95 samples (see Section 2 for details). After BCA quantification of the tsEV samples, an equal amount of protein was subjected to SDS‐PAGE, and silver staining was performed for quality control. As shown in Figure S1, 95 silver‐stained bands were obtained. Based on the BCA quantification and silver staining results, seven samples with excessively low protein concentrations were excluded (highlighted in red in Figure S1).

The remaining 88 tsEV samples were used for 4D DIA‐MS proteomic analysis, resulting in a matrix of protein expression for 7590 proteins, including 745 immune‐related proteins (IRPs). Simultaneously, clinical parameters were collected for these 88 samples, including age, FIGO stage, pathology type, HR_Status, serum CA125 and CA153 levels, postoperative residual tumour (classified as R0 and >R0), and BMI. Statistical tests such as the chi‐squared test, Mann–Whitney test and t‐test were applied to compare the baseline levels of these clinical parameters between the two groups, revealing significant differences in ‘postoperative residual tumor’, ‘HR_Status’ and ‘CA153’ (Table 1). Subsequently, for stratified sampling, 88 samples were randomly assigned in an 8:2 ratio to form a training set of 71 samples (including 47 platinum‐sensitive and 24 platinum‐resistant samples) and a testing set of 17 samples (including 11 platinum‐sensitive and six platinum‐resistant samples) using R (Figure 1b). Moreover, we performed statistical analysis on the parameters of the patients assigned to the training and test sets to determine whether they had consistent baselines. The results indicated no significant differences across these parameters (Table 2).

Initially, we characterized and identified representative samples from both platinum‐sensitive and platinum‐resistant groups. We observed a characteristic cup‐shaped structure with a central depression in both sample sets using TEM (Figure 2a). NTA revealed particle sizes ranging from 30 to 200 nm (Figure 2b). Western blotting analysis confirmed the expression of the classical exosomal markers TSG101 and CD81 in representative samples from both groups, whereas calnexin was not expressed in either group (Figure 2c).

Subsequently, 4D DIA‐MS proteomic sequencing was conducted on 58 platinum‐sensitive and 30 platinum‐resistant EOC tsEV samples after quality control. Although there was no significant difference in the yield (mass of tsEVs obtained per unit tissue) between the two groups, the platinum‐resistant group demonstrated a significantly lower abundance of proteins in tsEVs than the platinum‐sensitive group (Figure 2d). Further analysis revealed that despite the lack of notable difference in the overall protein intensity between the two groups, the top 50 protein total intensities markedly increased in the platinum‐resistant compared to the platinum‐sensitive group (Figure S2a). These proteins encompass EV markers (e.g., ACTB, MVP, ANXA2 and CD9), referencing the list of the top 100 EV proteins in the EVpedia database. Moreover, PCA revealed a distinct clustering of protein spectra between the two groups (Figure 2e).

Further analysis of differentially expressed proteins was performed using a training set consisting of 47 platinum‐sensitive and 24 platinum‐resistant samples. Among the 7590 proteins examined, 595 exhibited significant differential expression in the two groups (cut‐off set at |log FC| > 1, FDR < 0.05), among which 184 were upregulated in the platinum‐resistant group (Table S3). A heat map illustrating the top 100 differentially expressed proteins is shown in Figure 2f. Enrichment analysis using the protein Pathway Interaction Database (PID) revealed a noteworthy enrichment in immune‐related pathways, predominantly involving the CD8_ T cell receptor (TCR), B cell receptor (BCR) and TCR pathways (Figure 2g). Furthermore, our GO analysis corroborated this finding, indicating that these differentially expressed proteins were significantly enriched in biological processes (BP) pertaining to ‘immune response’ and ‘immune effector processes’ (Figure S2b).

We randomly selected 10 and 10 platinum‐sensitive and resistant individuals from a total of 88 cases, respectively. The FFPE slides from these patients were subjected to a multicolour immunohistochemical (mIHC) analysis targeting CD4, CD8, CD68 (macrophage), CD163 (M2 macrophage) and CK (a tumour cell membrane) markers. The results indicated that CD163+ positive cells were notably abundant in the resistant compared to the sensitive group, whereas CD8+ positive cells were conspicuously scarce (Figure S2c), thereby underscoring a potential disparity in the immune infiltration level within the tissue samples between the two groups.

Identification of EOC tsEV IRPs and clinical parameters highly correlated with platinum resistance outcome

Owing to the extensive information contained in the entire protein spectrum of tsEVs and enrichment analysis indicating differentially expressed protein enrichment in immune‐related pathways, we focused specifically on immune protein components within the tsEV proteome. We downloaded a gene set comprising 2499 IRPs from the ImmPort website (Bhattacharya et al., 2014). Subsequently, we extracted the expression profiles of these genes from the complete matrix and obtained a dataset encompassing the expression profiles of 745 IRPs in the tsEVs. Given the substantial magnitude of the original signal intensity values, a logarithmic transformation to log2 was applied for all protein expressions to facilitate subsequent calculations and visualization.

Based on the results of the differential protein analysis (cut‐off set at |log FC| > 1, FDR < 0.05), we constructed a volcano plot for these IRPs (Figure 3a). Thirty‐three IRPs exhibited differential expression between the platinum‐sensitive and platinum‐resistant groups, with three IRPs upregulated and 30 IRPs downregulated in the platinum‐resistant group. Using STRING, we generated an interaction network for these proteins, emphasizing CCR1, SYK and CD19 (with six edges) as well as CXCR3 and IL10RB (each with five edges) as key hub proteins (Figure 3b).

Considering the relatively large number of independent variables, we employed LASSO regression to further filter out IRPs that were highly correlated with platinum chemotherapy outcomes among the 33 differentially expressed IRPs. As illustrated in Figure 3c,d, 10 IRPs were selected based on the results obtained with lambda.1se. Notably, the expression level of PDGFA positively correlated with resistance outcomes, whereas the expression levels of the remaining nine IRPs (CCR1, KDR, IGHV3‐35, IGKV3‐15, APOBEC3A, ZC3HAV1L, NR2F6, CD72, WFDC2) negatively correlated with resistance outcomes.

Subsequently, we conducted univariate logistic regression analysis on the nine clinical factors (Age, Disease type, FIGO Stage, Pathology type, serum CA125 level, serum CA153 level, HR_Status, Postoperative residual tumour, BMI) (Table 2) and 10 IRPs identified by LASSO regression. The results revealed that, in addition to the 10 IRPs, four clinical parameters (FIGO stage, serum CA153 level, HR_status and Postoperative residual tumour) correlated with platinum chemotherapy outcomes (Figure 3e, p < 0.1).

Establishment and evaluation of the prediction model for initial platinum chemotherapy outcome in patients with EOC

We incorporated the four clinical factors identified through univariate logistic regression analysis and the 10 tsEV IRPs selected by LASSO into multivariate logistic regression analysis. The results revealed four independent factors that were significantly associated with responsiveness to platinum. These included one clinical factor (the presence of a postoperative residual tumour) and three tsEV IRPs (CCR1, CD72 and IGHV_35). Simultaneously, a nomogram based on the results of multivariate logistic regression was generated (Figure 4b). Referencing the corresponding scores for each indicator in the forest plot, we calculated the total points (an alternative representation of the risk score) by adding the scores for the four indicators. This total score was used to determine the likelihood of a positive event, that is, the probability of platinum chemotherapy resistance, for each patient. The risk score was calculated according to the aforementioned formula, and as a result, in both the training and testing sets, the risk score for patients in the platinum‐resistant group was significantly higher than for patients in the platinum‐sensitive group (Figure 4c).

To assess the predictive accuracy of the model, we constructed a calibration plot. The agreement between the predicted and observed positive rates within a specific range was evaluated as the risk score increased. Through repeated sampling of 71 samples from the training set with a threshold set at 1000 (i.e., resampling to a sample size of 1000), we found that the predicted positive rate of the model closely matched the observed values (red line closely adhering to the dashed line), with an average deviation error of 0.048 (less than 0.1). This suggests that the model has a high predictive accuracy (Figure 4d). ROC curves were constructed for the training and testing sets, revealing area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.960 and 0.864 for the training and testing sets, respectively (Figure 4e).

Validation of key parameters in the model

Our model posits that the decreased expression levels of CCR1 and CD72 proteins in tsEVs from EOC suggest a higher probability of resistance to initial platinum‐based chemotherapy. Validation experiments were conducted using western blotting (Figure 5a; n = 3) and ELISA (Figure 5b; n = 11) to evaluate the expression levels of these two IRPs (CCR1 and CD72) in the two groups of tsEV samples. The results revealed a substantial downregulation of both proteins in the platinum‐resistant group.

We extracted plasma‐derived sEVs (psEVs) from 10 and 10 platinum‐sensitive and resistant EOC cases, respectively, following the outlined protocol (Figure 5c), and submitted them to a 4D‐DIA proteomics. Among CCR1, IGHV3‐35 and CD72, only IGHV3‐35 could be detected by mass spectrometry, revealing IGHV3‐35 underexpression in the platinum‐resistant psEVs (Figure 5d; n = 10). Furthermore, CCR1 and CD72 ELISA tests revealed that CCR1 was also down‐regulated in the platinum‐resistant psEVs group, while no significant CD72 expression‐related difference could be observed between the two groups (Figure 5e; n = 10).

Immunohistochemical assays were performed to investigate the expression of CCR1 and CD72 proteins between platinum‐sensitive and platinum‐resistant EOC tissues (Figure 5f; n = 20) and qPCR experiments were performed to examine the expression of CCR1, CD72 and IGHV3‐35 mRNA between platinum‐sensitive and platinum‐resistant EOC cell lines (Figure 5g) and tissues (Figure 5h) (A2780‐cisR is a platinum‐resistant cell line established in our laboratory by subjecting the EOC cell line A2780 to prolonged low‐dose cisplatin stimulation). Remarkably, the expression of both CCR1, CD72 and IGHV3‐35 was significantly lower in the platinum‐resistant group than in the platinum‐sensitive group. In addition, we obtained and analyzed two RNA chip datasets, GSE15372 and GSE33482, which substantiated the downregulation of CCR1 and CD72 mRNA in platinum‐resistant ovarian cancer cell lines (Figure S3a).

Moreover, our analysis of the TCGA‐OV, GSE26712 and GSE63885 datasets delved into the correlation between CCR1 and CD72 mRNA expressions in EOC tumour tissues and patient survival outcomes. The resulting Kaplan–Meier curves clearly indicated that the reduced expression of both genes significantly correlated with shorter progression‐free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS), thereby indicating a poorer prognosis for patients with EOC (Figure S3b,c).

DISCUSSION

Platinum‐based drugs remain the preferred choice for initial chemotherapy for post‐surgical treatment of EOC. However, 10%−20% of patients with EOC exhibit resistance to treatment during the first cycle of platinum‐based chemotherapy, characterized by either progression of chemotherapy resistance during treatment or recurrence of chemotherapy resistance within 6 months after chemotherapy cessation. Despite the inherent limitations of using a platinum‐free interval exceeding 6 months as the sole criterion to distinguish platinum‐sensitive from platinum‐resistant EOC, this approach remains to be the most widely adopted in the clinical treatment protocols referenced by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) (Armstrong et al., 2022). Consequently, our study adhered to this established standard for the classification of patients with EOC. Notably, platinum‐resistant EOC patients often face a grim prognosis. Therefore, predicting the efficacy of initial chemotherapy in patients with EOC through reliable biomarkers or assessment models could serve as a crucial guide for personalized chemotherapy selection (Ghoneum et al., 2021). An effective clinical method for predicting the efficacy of platinum‐based chemotherapy in patients with EOC is currently lacking. Common predictive indicators include lineage/germline BRCA status (Le Page et al., 2021), HRD status (Wen et al., 2022), expression levels of associated proteins (Alblihy et al., 2020), immune cell infiltration type (Bogani et al., 2020) and circulating tumour DNA (Macintyre et al., 2018). However, challenges such as the absence of a universally optimal cutoff for HRD quantification and potential false positives due to BRCA1/2 reversion mutations hinder the accuracy of these indicators (Tobalina et al., 2021). In our study, HR status passed the screening of univariate analysis but was eventually eliminated from the model by multivariate analysis, indicating that it is not a more valuable independent factor compared to other variables. This result also reflects that predicting platinum reactivity through HR status might not be as specific as predicting PARP inhibitor efficacy (Konstantinopoulos et al., 2015).

Our study drew inspiration from the widespread application of plasma sEV proteins in cancer diagnostics and pioneered the exploration of the role of tsEV proteins in predicting the efficacy of platinum‐based chemotherapy in EOC. TsEVs, which exist in the intercellular spaces of the tumour microenvironment and have close connections with stromal cell structures, providing a promising avenue to reveal a novel perspective on the platinum reactivity and immune reactivity of EOC. Preliminary analysis revealed that the platinum‐resistant group exhibited a slightly higher tsEV yield compared to the platinum‐sensitive group. This result aligns with that of the previous study, indicating that when normalized for equivalent mass, softer and more porous EOC masses tend to possess a higher intercellular fluid volume, subsequently resulting in higher tsEV quantities (Shen et al., 2023). Furthermore, based on our clinical expertise, we observed that cystic EOC with a looser texture displayed a higher propensity for metastasis. Supporting this observation, Choi et al. (2016) described similarly that softer EOC masses exhibit a higher degree of malignancy compared to harder masses. Therefore, the result that the platinum‐resistant group produces a higher tsEVs yield is in line with current understanding and inferences. The striking detected protein quantity reduction in the tsEVs of the platinum‐resistant compared to those in the platinum‐sensitive group was an unexpected result. We hypothesize that the high‐abundance EV proteins prevalent in the resistant group, which align with the Top 100 EV proteins listed in the EVpedia database (Kim et al., 2013), consumed a significant portion of the sequencing depth, thereby resulting in the failure to detect certain low‐abundance proteins.

Notably, our study is the first to identify significantly enriched immune‐related pathways in the differentially expressed proteins of tsEVs between platinum‐sensitive and platinum‐resistant EOC groups, confirming the close association between specific tsEV immune proteins and the efficacy of platinum chemotherapy in patients with ovarian cancer.

We visualized the model using the nomogram and evaluated its performance. The AUC values were 0.960 and 0.864 for the training and test sets, respectively, both exceeding 0.85. This performance compares favourably with the accuracy of a predictive model based on plasma sEV microRNA expression profiles established by Li et al. (2023) to distinguish between benign and malignant ovarian tumours. Our model, built on the tsEV IRP expression profile, identified three key molecules: CCR1, CD72 and IGHV‐35. CCR1, a G protein‐coupled receptor, is a member of the chemokine receptor family, which promotes immune cell migration to inflammatory and infection sites by interacting with chemokines (Gilchrist & Echeverria, 2022). Some studies have suggested that CCR1 overexpression is associated with the development and progression of inflammatory diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease and asthma. CCR1 antagonists and selective agonists are currently being tested in clinical trials (Shao et al., 2022). CD72 is a B‐cell surface molecule that regulates B‐cell activation, proliferation and antibody production and plays a role in antigen recognition to maintain immune system balance. IGHV‐35 is a variant of the immunoglobulin heavy chain that is involved in antibody formation. It undergoes gene recombination after B cells are stimulated, producing specific antibodies that help the body respond to infections. After conducting a comprehensive survival analysis across various EOC datasets, we identified CCR1 and CD72 as protective genes that significantly impact EOC prognosis. While the absence of IGHV3‐35 detection in the chip analysis represents a limitation, this observation also underscores the reliability of the variables selected for our model. Unfortunately, current functional studies on CCR1, CD72 and IGHV3‐35 in EOC remain scarce, highlighting the need for further in‐depth investigations to understand their biological roles and therapeutic potential. In addition, notably, another key variable in this model, ‘postoperative residual tumour’, is also a widely acknowledged critical determinant of tumour prognosis (de Jong et al., 2007). However, in this study, due to the EOC sample collection limitations, the number of cases included was relatively small.

In fact, platinum‐resistant EOC tissue is currently regarded as a ‘cold tumour’ marked by low expression levels of immune factors and reduced immune cell infiltration, especially CD8+ T cells, typically not exceeding 8% (Chen et al., 2022). Instead, there is an abundance of functionally exhausted T cells, contributing to a weakened immune response within the tumour microenvironment and hindering the effective recognition and attack of tumour cells, ultimately contributing to chemotherapy resistance (Khan et al., 2021). This low immune reactivity may lead to platinum resistance in patients with ovarian cancer (Le Saux et al., 2021). Although this study was based on a model established using two groups of patients with different platinum responsiveness, through mIHC experiments, we also discovered significant differences in the immune cell infiltration types between the two sample groups. Consequently, patients to a certain extent, our model could be potentially used to make preliminary predictions on immunotherapeutic efficacy. However, including a new patient cohort with clearer immunotherapeutic outcomes for model construction would be necessary for more accurate predictions.

Furthermore, due to the sequencing depth‐ and low CCR1 and CD72 protein expression level‐related limitations, these proteins were not detected by mass spectrometry. ELISA analysis revealed no significant difference in the CD72 expression between the two psEV groups, indicating potential substantial variations in the tissue‐ and plasma‐derived sEV protein components. Indeed, this is because plasma sEVs could originate from any tissue and cell in the human body, leading to a significant EOC‐specific sEV dilution (Shen et al., 2023). To investigate the predictive power of plasma psEV proteins on chemotherapeutic outcomes, a separate psEV proteomics analysis could be necessary.

For the first time, our study identified the three proteins CCR1, CD72 and IGHV‐35 in EOC tsEVs and found significant differences between platinum‐sensitive and platinum‐resistant samples. Using CCR1, CD72 and IGHV‐35 as independent variables along with the clinical parameters of postoperative residual tumour, we successfully employed machine learning to construct a nomogram for model fitting. It is anticipated that the nomogram developed in this study can assist in the prospective prediction of the efficacy of initial platinum‐based chemotherapy in patients with EOC prior to treatment initiation for future clinical diagnosis and treatment, thus providing a valuable reference for clinical decision‐making. In future studies, we plan to expand the sample size for external validation.
