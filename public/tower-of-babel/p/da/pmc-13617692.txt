Physics-guided framework for climate dependent disease prediction: coupling compartment model with deep learning
Authors: Tengbiao Li, Weide Li, Shujuan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfluenza-like illness (ILI) serves as a representative climate dependent disease due to its climate sensitivity, widespread transmission, and significant impact on public health and societal functioning, highlighting the urgent need for reliable ILI prediction techniques that combine climate factors to support effective prevention and intervention. Existing compartmental model-based methods have limited ability to incorporate climate data and often struggle to capture the complex ILI transmission patterns. Many deep learning models generally lack interpretability and tend to overlook the interactions between climatic factors and ILI transmission. This study aims to develop a physics-guided framework that leverages the dynamical knowledge embedded in compartmental models and the multi-source data learning and representation capabilities of deep learning.MethodsOur materials are the ILI case and climate data from Lanzhou, Gansu Province, China from 1st January 2023 to 26th October 2024 and Xi’an, Shaanxi Province, China from 1st January 2010 to 10th November 2016. Regarding the proposed model, first, we employ a physics-informed neural network (PINN) to learn the underlying dynamical knowledge under differential equation constraints and embed it into a long short-term memory network (LSTM) through a physics-guided representation alignment mechanism. Then, the transferred dynamical knowledge guides the LSTM to learn the mapping from climate and case data to ILI dynamics, enabling more accurate predictions jointly driven by domain knowledge and multi-source data.ResultsThe results demonstrate that the proposed framework generally achieves better prediction accuracy and trend consistency than five advanced baselines. Specifically, compared with suboptimal baselines in Lanzhou, our model achieves reductions of at least 21.81%, 20.76%, 21.86%, and 19.44% in NRMSE, SMAPE, ND, and MAPE, respectively, while improving PCC by at least 14.96%. In addition, we derive explicit functional relationships between transmission rates and climate factors via symbolic regression, and verify the effectiveness and reliability of the model by statistical test and ablation study.ConclusionsThe study finds that physics-guided representation alignment mechanism not only offers the potential to enhance ILI prediction under varying climatic conditions but also provides new insights and methodological support for epidemic prediction.

Background

Influenza is an acute respiratory infectious disease, and laboratory evidence has shown that climatic factors, particularly temperature and humidity, directly affect influenza virus activity [1, 2]. Moreover, influenza virus viability is enhanced under low-temperature and low-humidity conditions [3]. Meanwhile, influenza-like illness (ILI) exerts substantial impacts on multiple aspects of society, and the serious complications and deaths it may cause put a substantial disease burden on the public. Therefore, scholars have shown increasing interest in the relationship between climate change and ILI consequences. Predicting ILI-related health outcomes, such as infection cases and mortality, based on meteorological factors has emerged as a prominent research topic at the intersection of atmospheric science and biomathematics [4–10].

Compartmental models are built upon rational assumptions about transmission mechanisms and employ mathematical mechanisms to describe the dynamics of infectious diseases. Many studies have assumed that the transmission rate of ILI depends on climatic factors and have used compartmental models to investigate how climate change affects outcomes such as the severity, intervention intensity and duration of ILI. For example, in 2023, Servadio, Thai, Choisy et al. [11] linked climatic factors in tropical regions with influenza patterns and investigated whether influenza exhibits seasonality in tropical regions. In 2024, Shi, Wang, and Jin [12] examined the correlation between the periodic transmission rate of influenza and patterns of climatic indicators, while emphasizing the influence of climate trends on influenza prediction. In 2025, Ruan, Liang, Sun et al. [13] employed an SIRS model to investigate the regulatory effect of seasonal temperature increases on influenza patterns. Li, Liu and Qi [14] developed a metapopulation infectious disease model with seasonal fluctuations in climatic factors and applied it to study the seasonal outbreaks of influenza A viruses in temperate regions. These models describe and predict ILI dynamics based on mechanistic assumptions and capture certain characteristics of disease transmission to some extent. Meanwhile, climate variables have been incorporated into the above compartmental models to investigate their effects on ILI transmission dynamics. However, these models are primarily intended to improve the understanding of epidemiological mechanisms rather than to perform ILI prediction, which would limit their performance in prediction tasks. In addition, their construction and predictive performance rely heavily on model assumptions, making it difficult to capture the complex patterns of ILI and to generalize effectively to novel prediction tasks with heterogeneous external data.

Deep learning is a data-driven nonlinear mathematical tool with powerful learning capabilities and has demonstrated remarkable success in addressing a wide range of complex tasks. Considering that learning the characteristics of infectious diseases from data can help reveal the laws of transmission dynamics and the influence of real-world climatic factors, many scholars have developed climate data-driven deep learning frameworks and applied them to mathematical epidemiology. For example, in 2023, Amendolara, Sant, Rotstein [15] trained an LSTM model using ILI data and climatic factors, and it demonstrated excellent performance in short-term prediction tasks. In 2024, Zhu, Chen, Qin et al. [16] divided the ILI data into three periods(overall, non-epidemic, and epidemic) to analyze the effects of climatic factors on ILI across different periods, and employed an ensemble model integrating bi-directional LSTM and random forest for short-term prediction. In 2025, Li, Huang, Xie et al. [17] validated the relationship between climatic data and ILI incidence using Pearson correlation analysis, and introduced the Kernel Attention Network to achieve more accurate predictions of ILI incidence. Wang, Xiao and Song [7] employed deep neural networks to learn the incidence rate and, by integrating symbolic regression and sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics, explored optimal explicit form between incidence and climatic factors. Notably, they emphasized the significant nonlinear effects of climatic factors on ILI transmission dynamics, highlighting the need for caution when incorporating these factors into models. These models employ multi-source data to train general deep learning frameworks, thereby improving infectious disease prediction performance. However, they overlook the integration of climatic factors with mechanistic disease transmission modeling, resulting in inadequate characterization of infectious disease dynamics, limited interpretability, and challenges in providing reliable support for public health planning and response.

To balance accuracy, scalability, and interpretability in infectious disease prediction, integrating epidemiological knowledge with deep learning has emerged as a novel framework with transformative potential in epidemiology. The combination of deep learning and traditional compartmental models has advanced rapidly in recent years. For example, neural networks have been employed to estimate unknown components or parameters of compartmental models [18, 19], and Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) [20], which combines differential equations with neural networks, has been proposed and widely adopted [21, 22]. However, to our knowledge, despite their success in related tasks, these works remain limited in incorporating external multi-source data and capturing complex temporal dependencies. Thus, effectively integrating deep learning techniques with mechanistic models to achieve accurate and interpretable epidemic prediction remains a significant challenge.

To address this challenge, on the one hand, compartmental models contain dynamic knowledge that guides epidemic prediction, while neural networks possess data-driven representational power. Their combination enables neural networks to follow infectious disease dynamics during the learning process. Accordingly, we employ PINN to capture the potential epidemic dynamics, since PINN can embed physical knowledge through unsupervised loss functions derived from differential equations. On the other hand, the multilayer perceptron architecture of PINN tends to describe independent and uniformly distributed data, making it less effective at capturing complex temporal dependencies. It is also limited in modeling climatic variables that are not involved in differential equations. Accordingly, we utilize LSTM to learn the temporal patterns of external data, as its memory cells and gating mechanisms make it particularly suitable for capturing long-term and cross-modal temporal dependencies.

This study develops a physics-guided framework for ILI prediction, which integrates the capability of learning underlying epidemic dynamics with the ability of multi-source data-driven modeling. First, PINN is employed to learn interpretable dynamic knowledge under the physical constraints of differential equations. Then, the learned knowledge is transferred to LSTM, enabling LSTM to perform data-driven epidemic modeling and prediction based on multi-source input data.

Methods

Compartmental model for mechanism discovery

In this study, to simulate the transmission dynamics of ILI, we used the susceptible-infectious-recovered-susceptible (SIRS) [23] epidemic model. The SIRS model divides total population (N) into three compartments: Susceptible (S), Infectious (I), and Recovered (R).

By solving system (1), the underlying epidemic dynamics characterized by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Psi _t$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega _t$$\end{document} can be inferred within the observation period \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{t_1, t_2, \ldots , t_L\}$$\end{document}.

PINN module for dynamical knowledge

PINN, as a data-driven neural network algorithm, can not only accurately fit data using compartmental models but also effectively estimate time-varying parameters. The PINN module is parameterized by a multilayer perceptron that takes time t as input, i.e., the neural network NN(t) is a function of the time variable t. It learns the underlying dynamical knowledge from the observed data and predicts the SIRS model state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Psi _t$$\end{document} at time t. The SIRS model describes the rate of change of certain functions with respect to t, that is, the gradient, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{\text {SIRS}}(t)$$\end{document}. Considering that the gradient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dfrac{dNN(t)}{dt}$$\end{document} can be computed via automatic differentiation, this enables PINN to solve the SIRS model by jointly minimizing the residual between the two gradients and the observation error.

To ensure that the PINN produces predictions consistent with the dynamics described by system (1), we denote the output of NN(t) as the state variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Psi _t$$\end{document}, and require it to satisfy the constraint \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dfrac{d\Psi _t}{dt} = f_{\text {SIRS}}(\Psi _t, \Omega _t)$$\end{document}. Meanwhile, when fitting the observational data, both the observation error loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Loss_{\text {Data}}$$\end{document} and the SIRS loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Loss_{\text {SIRS}}$$\end{document} are minimized. Furthermore, since the employed SIRS model is time-varying, we introduce an additional time-varying parameter update loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Loss_{\text {Para}}$$\end{document}.

Since the state variables are governed by the continuous-time SIRS system, the physics-informed loss is constructed using differential residuals. In contrast, the time-varying parameter vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega _t$$\end{document} is estimated at discrete observation times rather than being governed by an explicit differential equation. Therefore, a first-order difference regularization term is introduced to encourage smooth temporal evolution of the parameters and avoid unrealistic fluctuations. The three loss functions are formulated as follows.2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Loss_{\text {Data}} = \frac{1}{L} \sum \limits _{t=t_1}^{t_L} \left( \hat{I}_t - I_t\right) ^2, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Loss_{\text {SIRS}} = \frac{1}{L} \sum \limits _{t=t_1}^{t_L} \left( \dfrac{d\Psi _t}{dt} - f_{\text {SIRS}}(\Psi _t, \Omega _t)\right) ^2, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Loss_{\text {Para}} = \frac{1}{L} \sum \limits _{t=t_1}^{t_L} \left( \Omega _{t+1} - \Omega _t\right) ^2, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{I}_t$$\end{document} represents the number of ILI infections predicted by PINN at time t and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_t$$\end{document} represents the observed data of the number of ILI infections at time t.

It is worth noting that the time-varying parameter update loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Loss_{\text {Para}}$$\end{document} essentially serves as a regularizer. By constraining the magnitude of parameter updates between adjacent time steps, it prevents sharp fluctuations in the parameter sequence, encourages the model to capture the gradual evolution of the ILI transmission process, and mitigates overfitting under substantial data volatility, thereby enhancing the robustness and interpretability of the model.

LSTM module for multi-source knowledge

LSTM is an improved recurrent neural network designed to model long-term dependencies in time-series data [24–27]. The LSTM module takes multi-source time-series feature vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{X}_t$$\end{document} related to ILI (Including temperature, humidity, and ILI infections cases) as input; that is, the neural network \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$NN^{SM}(t)$$\end{document} is a function of the input feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{X}_t$$\end{document} that varies over time t. It learns the evolutionary trends of the given external data and predicts the SIRS model state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Psi _t^{SM}$$\end{document} at time t.

We denote the output of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$NN^{SM}(t)$$\end{document} as the state variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Psi _t^{SM}$$\end{document} and require that it satisfies the SIRS dynamical constraint \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dfrac{d\Psi _t^{SM}}{dt} = f_{\text {SIRS}}(\Psi _t^{SM}, \Omega _t)$$\end{document}. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{e}_t$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{e}_t^{SM}$$\end{document} denote the latent embeddings generated by the PINN and LSTM, respectively. Since the LSTM receives multi-dimensional feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{X}_t$$\end{document} rather than time alone, the temporal derivative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dfrac{d\Psi _t^{SM}}{dt}$$\end{document} cannot be directly computed via automatic differentiation.

Assuming that latent representations update smoothly and continuously over time, neighboring embeddings are expected to exhibit similar temporal evolution patterns. Through latent representation alignment, the LSTM is encouraged to preserve the dynamical information learned by the PINN, thereby maintaining physical interpretability while enhancing predictive flexibility.

Physics-guided framework for final ILI prediction

Transfer learning aims to utilize the teacher model to extract knowledge from the source domain and transfer this knowledge to the target domain, thereby facilitating learning of the target task [28]. Inspired by transfer learning, PINN, LSTM, and ILI prediction are respectively regarded as the teacher model, student model, and target task within the proposed physics-guided framework in this study. Specifically, PINN is used to learn domain knowledge (i.e., potential ILI dynamics and time-varying parameters) from the source domain and transfer its learned representations to the target domain. LSTM is employed to accomplish the ILI prediction task in the target domain. By leveraging the knowledge transferred from PINN, it achieves both physical interpretability and high prediction accuracy while retaining a data-driven learning paradigm.

We choose LSTM as the student model because it is more suitable for handling multivariate time series data and can incorporate multi-source data that are not included in the SIRS model, thereby learning the mapping from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{X}_t$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Psi _t^{SM}$$\end{document} and realizing ILI prediction. Meanwhile, we perform knowledge transfer based on embedding approximate gradients and introduce the knowledge distillation loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Loss_{\text {Dist}}$$\end{document} [29] to enable LSTM to further learn the prediction curves and physical laws from PINN, as shown in the following formula:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Loss_{\text {Dist}} = \frac{1}{L} \sum \limits _{t=t_1}^{t_L} \left( \Psi _t^{SM} - \Psi _t\right) ^2. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

For the ILI prediction model proposed under the physics-guided framework, losses (2), (5), and (6) ensure that the model outputs remain close to the observed data, while losses (3) and (7) enforce consistency with differential equation constraints, loss (4) guarantees smoothness and rationality of the parameter sequence, and loss (8) further enhances model performance. Moreover, instead of simply concatenating the teacher and student models, we employ the pre-trained teacher model as the entry point of physics-guided representation alignment mechanism, that is, importing the PINN module and SIRS parameters as teacher knowledge into the new training stage. In this stage, knowledge transfer is accomplished by generating \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dfrac{d\Psi _t^{SM}}{d\textbf{e}_t^{SM}}$$\end{document} through forward propagation and computing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dfrac{d\Psi _t^{SM}}{dt}$$\end{document} using the chain rule. This allows the physical evolution laws learned by the PINN module to be transferred to the LSTM module, while the LSTM module only needs to learn how to adjust the dynamical parameters according to exogenous features based on these physical laws, and then make predictions on this basis.

Results

The datasets and indicators of case analysis

We use ILI infection data of Lanzhou dataset (from 1st January 2023 to 26th October 2024) and Xi’an dataset (from 1st January 2010 to 10th November 2016) [7], as well as temperature and humidity data for the same period collected from the China Meteorological Administration (https://weather.cma.cn/). Then, we group the data by week, took the mean within each group as the weekly data, and apply the Henderson moving average filter [30] to smooth the time series data. For visualization purposes, a Henderson moving average was applied only to the representative prediction window shown in this subsection. Since the Henderson filter is a symmetric smoothing method, it is not strictly causal. However, the smoothed series was used solely for trend visualization and was not involved in model training, parameter estimation, or forecasting. All quantitative forecasting evaluations reported were conducted using the original unsmoothed epidemic data. Meanwhile, for the infectious disease prediction task in this study, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention usually require a 4-week-ahead forecast [31]. Therefore, we set the number of prediction weeks K = 4 for short-term prediction, and twice the common planning horizon K = 8 for long-term prediction. We further investigate whether the forecasts generated by different models in the long-term prediction task can accurately capture the actual ILI trends.

Then, following [32, 33], we adopt the Normalized root mean squared error (NRMSE), Symmetric mean absolute percentage error (SMAPE), Normal deviation (ND), and Mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) to evaluate the prediction performance, and the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) to assess the correlation of prediction trends. Among them, lower values of NRMSE, SMAPE, ND, and MAPE indicate better performance, whereas a higher PCC reflects stronger agreement with the actual trend. Experimental results

Overall comparison results in Lanzhou and Xi’an

The Lanzhou dataset obtained a total of 95 weekly samples, corresponding to 202301-202443. The Xi’an dataset obtained a total of 358 weekly samples, corresponding to 201001-201645. To preserve the temporal structure of epidemic transmission and avoid information leakage, the dataset is divided sequentially rather than randomly shuffled. Specifically, for Lanzhou dataset, the first 59 weeks (202301-202407) were used as the training set, the subsequent 18 weeks (202408-202425) were used as the validation set, and the remaining 18 weeks (202426-202443) were used as the test set. For Xi’an dataset, the first 238 weeks (201001-201430) were used as the training set, the subsequent 60 weeks (201431-201538) were used as the validation set, and the remaining 60 weeks (201539-201645) were used as the test set.

To further assess the reliability and practical applicability of Ours, we additionally conduct rolling prediction over the entire test set. Specifically, after training the proposed model on the training and validation sets, rolling predictions are generated sequentially for each week in the test period. At each step, the model estimates the epidemic state at the corresponding future time point, and the prediction horizon is gradually extended until the entire test interval is covered.

Discussion and conclusion

In this study, to develop an infectious disease prediction model that integrates dynamics knowledge-driven modeling with multi-source data, we design a novel physics-guided representation alignment mechanism that transfers the dynamics learned by PINN into an LSTM model capable of incorporating climatic factors. Our results demonstrate that the proposed framework effectively combines the strengths of SIRS, PINN, and LSTM while overcoming their respective limitations. Specifically, the lack of flexibility and generalizability of SIRS in modeling complex ILI patterns, the exclusion of exogenous climate data in PINN, and the limited predictive interpretability in LSTM can all be reasonably explained and mitigated from the perspective of knowledge transfer.

The results demonstrate that the proposed model achieves competitive prediction performance across different tasks, particularly in capturing epidemic trends and maintaining relatively reliable prediction accuracy. This success arises from two key factors: first, the effective integration of transmission mechanisms derived from the compartmental model into the neural network; and second, the seamless incorporation of multimodal data through a physics-guided representation alignment mechanism, which enhances both learning and representation capabilities. As a result, the proposed model not only enables gradient alignment and knowledge transfer through embedding approximation and the chain rule, but also leverages physical knowledge from the source domain to guide the LSTM in mapping input data to the underlying ILI dynamics. Furthermore, it is worth noting that this study employs a specific compartmental model (i.e., the SIRS model) to generate dynamic knowledge within the physics-guided framework. This design allows decision-makers to extend the approach by directly modifying the compartmental model, thereby adapting it to the transmission and prediction of various emerging infectious diseases.

Note that in classical compartmental models, the impact of climatic factors on ILI is usually modeled by assuming specific functional forms in advance-for example, a mixture of sinusoidal and linear functions [38], a purely sinusoidal function with time variables [39–41], an exponential function derived from laboratory experiments [42], or a quadratic function in tropical and subtropical regions [43]. In other words, for ILI under different scenarios, it is often necessary to search for relevant prior knowledge to predefine the functional form of climatic factors, which significantly constrains the performance of compartmental models. The proposed model effectively overcomes this limitation by eliminating the need for compartmental models to provide domain knowledge about climatic factors. Instead, it leverages climate data in the target domain to drive the student model LSTM, which can automatically capture the influence of climatic factors on ILI. This general framework can be flexibly applied across different ILI scenarios, thereby improving the adaptability and reliability of ILI prediction and supporting public health surveillance and intervention planning.

On the one hand, based on the analysis of the prediction results for Lanzhou during 202326-202333, we observe that LSTM performs poorly in both ILI prediction and trend matching tasks, whereas the proposed model achieves strong performance in terms of both accuracy and correlation. This suggests that relying on a small amount of data for prediction with LSTM may result in unsatisfactory outcomes, while physics-guided representation alignment can provide an effective mechanism for epidemic prediction under limited-data scenarios. Its results should be regarded as one of the multiple sources of evidence for public health decision-making, which aligns with the conclusions of existing studies [44, 45]. On the other hand, in the case studies across three periods, we find that the performances of SIRS and PINN (domain knowledge-driven) as well as LSTM and TCN (multi-source data-driven) are unstable. The former indicates that fixed sources of domain knowledge may not perfectly adapt to epidemic prediction tasks under varying scenarios, while the latter suggests that generating reliable infectious disease prediction results may require not only observational data, but also additional epidemiological constraints and dynamical prior knowledge. These two findings are consistent with existing studies [33, 46]. Therefore, a coupled framework that jointly integrates multi-source data and dynamical knowledge can help reduce prediction errors and trend fluctuations in ILI prediction.

Although we believe this study opens new avenues for incorporating epidemic domain knowledge and exogenous climatic factors into deep learning models for more reliable ILI trend prediction, several limitations remain. On the one hand, the compartmental model we employed does not consider the effects of non-pharmaceutical interventions or vaccination, which somewhat constrains its ability to capture the actual epidemic transmission process. Incorporating these factors into the proposed model is necessary to more comprehensively capture the dynamics of epidemic spread. On the other hand, we have not conducted a systematic assessment of transferability before applying source-domain knowledge to the target domain, which may introduce the risk of “Negative Transfer”. Selecting epidemiological models and prior assumptions that are better suited to specific real-world transmission scenarios is crucial for mitigate such risks and enhance predictive performance. Furthermore, although the model achieves satisfactory prediction accuracy, it does not generate predictive distributions or confidence intervals that could characterize forecast uncertainty. In practical epidemic surveillance and public health decision-making, uncertainty information is often valuable for risk assessment and resource allocation. Addressing these issues will be the focus of our future research.
