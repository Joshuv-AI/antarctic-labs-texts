ABSTRACT
Transportation plays a crucial role in daily life, enabling people to move from one location to another using either a single mode or multiple modes of transport. Many countries are facing the challenge of promoting for Multimodal transportation as the best transportation choice. This study develops and empirically tests an integrated model for assessing multimodal transport success in smart urban contexts.Multimodal transport success is conceptualizes multimodal transport success as a latent construct driven by integration and connectivity, accessibility, data-driven decision-making, sustainability orientation, and service coverage. Using survey data from mobility professionals in Dubai and applying Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM), the study reveals significant direct and mediating relationships among these constructs. the study examines direct and mediating relationships among these constructs. Results indicate that accessibility, integration, sustainability orientation, and digital capabilities are significantly associated with perceived multimodal performance. Introduction

Multimodal transport has been widely recognized as a central pillar of sustainable urban mobility due to its potential to improve system efficiency, reduce environmental impacts, and enhance user experience through the coordinated use of multiple transport modes1–4. Prior research has demonstrated that effective multimodal systems depend on accessibility, network integration, service coordination, and technological enablement5–9. However, despite this growing body of work, multimodal transport research remains theoretically fragmented, with most studies adopting indicator-based, infrastructure-specific, or mode-oriented perspectives rather than unified explanatory frameworks10–14. Elaboratively, accessibility has been extensively examined as a determinant of travel behaviour and transport equity15–18, while integration and connectivity have been studied primarily in the context of hub optimization, timetable coordination, and transfer efficiency19–22. Additionally, sustainability, meanwhile, is predominantly conceptualized as an environmental outcome of transport systems, measured through emissions, energy consumption, or modal shift23–26. While data-driven decision-making has emerged as a parallel stream focusing on intelligent transport systems, real-time optimization, and smart mobility platforms27–30. Although these strands provide valuable insights, they remain weakly connected at the theoretical level, offering limited explanation of how operational and strategic dimensions jointly determine multimodal transport success. Moreover, conceptual boundaries between accessibility, coverage, and integration remain ambiguous across studies31–33, and few models explicitly distinguish between strategic antecedents, operational mechanisms, and system-level outcomes34–36. As a result, existing frameworks often lack causal clarity and theoretical transferability, limiting their applicability for performance governance and strategic planning in smart cities.

Recent studies have called for theory-driven, system-level approaches capable of synthesizing multimodal performance dimensions into coherent explanatory structures37–40. In response to calls for system-level approaches to multimodal performance, this study develops and tests an integrated framework that conceptualizes multimodal transport success as a latent construct shaped by integration and connectivity, accessibility, data-driven decision-making, sustainability orientation, and service coverage37–46. The proposed framework by this study models both direct and mediating relationships among these constructs, enabling a structured assessment of how operational and strategic factors jointly influence multimodal performance.Dubai is used as the empirical context for evaluating the proposed model. The city represents a complex multimodal environment characterized by advanced transport infrastructure, rapid urban development, and persistent car dependence, making it a relevant setting for examining multimodal transport performance5,6. While the analysis is grounded in Dubai’s mobility system, the study aims to provide a structured approach that may inform assessment and planning in other urban contexts with similar characteristics.Accordingly, this study seeks to answer the following research questions:

What latent factors explain multimodal transport success within an integrated theoretical framework?How do these factors interact through direct and mediating relationships to shape multimodal transport success?How can this theoretical structure support multimodal performance governance and strategic decision-making?

Systematic literature review process.

The filtered literature covers a wide range of multimodal transport themes, including integration and connectivity, accessibility, sustainability, and data-driven decision-making. Most studies were published between 2020 and 2025, reflecting the growing interest in urban mobility, digitalization, and sustainability during this period. In total, 53 studies were reviewed. 30 studies dedicated to urban passenger transport, 15 to logistics and freight, and 8 combined both domains. While, in terms of mode, the greatest attention was devoted to system-level multimodal frameworks (16 studies). Other areas of focus included rail and high-speed rail (8 studies), bus/ bus rapid transit (BRT) and feeder services (7), active and micro-mobility (5), shared/on-demand mobility (4), and marine transport (2). The inclusion of freight or logistics-focused literature is justified due to their methodological contributions to measuring multimodal performance (e.g., indicators such as transit time, transfer optimization, and network integration), which are equally relevant to passenger contexts. In terms of region, most studies originated from the Asia-Pacific (18) and Europe (16), where multimodal systems are more mature and empirical data is widely available. By contrast, contributions from the North America (7), Middle East and North Africa (4), and Latin America (2) were limited, highlighting potential regional bias toward contexts with well-established public transport infrastructures. Therefore, to mitigate the imbalance in region distribution and inclusive of freight-focused literature, the extracted operational indicators from literature were extracted, then subsequently validated through structured consultation with Dubai-based subject matter experts. In addition, only peer-reviewed, English-language studies indexed in Scopus were included, ensuring methodological rigor and strengthening the contextual applicability of the extracted constructs.

The systematic literature review has identified that MMT research has matured across several dominant streams, notably (1) network integration and connectivity, (2) accessibility and equity assessment, (3) sustainability and environmental evaluation, and (4) digital mobility management and data-driven operations. While each stream has advanced understanding of specific MMT dimensions, the cumulative evidence remains weakly integrated at the theoretical level, with many studies focusing on isolated indicators, specific modes, or localized interventions rather than providing an integrated explanation of how operational drivers collectively shape multimodal system success8–12,16,30,36. Consequently, the literature offers limited causal clarity on interdependencies across constructs such as accessibility, coverage, and integration, and it seldom models performance as a latent construct with transmission mechanisms (e.g., mediation) that translate upstream enablers into user-perceived success10,16,21,30,36.

Further conceptual limitations are evident. First, accessibility and coverage are frequently conflated in empirical applications, despite their distinct planning meanings (i.e., ease of access versus spatial or service provision extent), which complicates interpretation and governance implications8,16,45. Second, sustainability is often positioned exclusively as an outcome of successful multimodal deployment (e.g., emissions reductions), whereas policy-oriented and behavior-sensitive perspectives also imply that sustainability orientation can operate as an antecedent that shapes investment priorities, service design, and user willingness to adopt greener mobility bundles23,24,42,45. Third, data-driven decision-making is commonly treated as a technological add-on, rather than theorized as an operational capability that improves integration and system responsiveness through real-time information, optimization, and digital service innovation19,38.

In response, this study synthesizes the fragmented evidence into a coherent latent-variable framework that (a) distinguishes operational enablers from outcome constructs, (b) explicitly models transmission mechanisms among constructs, and (c) integrates passenger and logistics performance perspectives where operational indicators are transferable to passenger mobility contexts. The objective is not to import freight user-preference assumptions into passenger transport, but to leverage the measurement and performance-management logic developed in multimodal logistics such as transfer optimization, network coordination, and time-based reliability metrics; which are also fundamental to passenger-oriented multimodal operations14,20,26–28,31–33.

Construct extraction proceeded in two stages. First, recurring performance themes were identified through thematic coding (integration/connectivity, accessibility, sustainability/environment, data-driven decision-making/innovation, and success outcomes). Second, measurable indicators were mapped to each theme based on the literature, definitional coherence, and suitability for professional survey assessment. This approach aligns with indicator synthesis practices used to operationalize complex transport performance concepts into measurable items8,10,21. The resulting framework conceptualizes MMT success as a latent outcome construct and positions integration/connectivity, accessibility, sustainability orientation, data-driven decision-making/innovation, and service coverage as explanatory constructs, including mediation pathways grounded in the literature on data-enabled integration and coverage-enabled accessibility19,45.

Indicator tables (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5) report definitions and the supporting studies that motivated their inclusion. All constructs and indicators were subsequently validated with Dubai-based subject matter experts to ensure contextual relevance and appropriate interpretation for passenger mobility governance. The success of MMT factor

Consistent with user-centered mobility evaluation, this study conceptualizes MMT success as the perceived and experienced value delivered as an outcome to users through multimodal travel. The literature commonly operationalizes this value through generalized travel-cost components (monetary cost and travel time) and perceived service quality12,13,26,27. Accordingly, the outcome construct is measured through cost savings (MMT1), travel time (MMT2), and quality of service / user perception (MMT3). This operationalization is appropriate for capturing the success of multimodal services at the user and system interface, while remaining measurable through professional assessment and comparable across contexts12–16,26,27. Accessibility is modeled as a direct antecedent of multimodal transport success because it represents the most immediate interface between system design and user experience. Unlike coverage or integration, which describe network-level provisions, accessibility reflects users’ actual ability to reach, enter, and utilize multimodal services in a convenient, safe, and affordable manner. Prior studies consistently demonstrate that improvements in station distribution, physical accessibility, affordability, and safety translate directly into higher perceived service quality, reduced travel burden, and greater user acceptance of multimodal systems16,17,45,52.

Sustainability and environmental indicators.

While emissions reduction is frequently treated in the literature as a downstream outcome of multimodal transport deployment, this study conceptualizes sustainability as a strategic enabling condition that shapes system design, policy priorities, and user adoption behavior. Prior studies demonstrate that sustainability objectives increasingly guide investment decisions, regulatory frameworks, and service configuration in multimodal systems, particularly through ESG-oriented governance, renewable energy deployment, and soft-mobility integration23,24. From a behavioral perspective, sustainability also influences user willingness to shift toward public and active transport modes, thereby acting as a precursor to multimodal system utilization rather than solely a consequence of it24,51. Moreover, sustainability-oriented pricing schemes, park-and-ride incentives, and environmental policy instruments have been shown to directly shape travel behavior and modal choice before measurable emissions reductions occur45. Similarly, emergency and resilience-oriented multimodal sustainability planning further positions sustainability as an operational driver embedded in system planning logic rather than a purely retrospective impact metric42. Accordingly, in this study sustainability is retained as a strategic antecedent construct that reflects governance orientation, infrastructure investment priorities, and behavioral motivation. This conceptualization aligns with the theoretical objective of identifying operational and strategic conditions that enhance multimodal system credibility, legitimacy, and long-term adaptability from both user and institutional perspectives.

Research methodology

This research adopted the mixed-method approach with the following methodological steps (Fig. 3):

Fig. 3Research methodology process.

Research methodology process.

The first part of the research conducted a literature review to explore and identify the factors influencing the success of MMT and their corresponding indicators. As a result, a proposed theoretical framework was developed as shown in Fig. 2. This framework consisted of four independent latent factors: integration and connectivity (INT), accessibility (ACC), data-driven decision-making and innovation (DAT), and sustainability and environmental impact (SUS), along with one dependent latent factor: multimodal transportation success (MMT). The proposed model includes 21 indicators.

Subsequently, a structured interview was designed to validate and refine the research conceptual model in the context of Dubai, incorporating feedback from subject matter experts. Following this, a quantitative research method was employed using a survey as the data collection tool. The survey was distributed to the target population, consisting of employees and experts working in the field of mobility in Dubai. The targeted sample size was 180 participants, selected randomly. Finally, the collected data was analyzed using Smart PLS software, with the aim of discussing the results and providing constructive conclusions and recommendations to enhance the performance of MMT measurements in Dubai.

This study strictly adheres and all procedures were conducted in compliance with the relevant scientific research ethics guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants prior to data collection. Participation was voluntary, respondents were informed about the purpose of the study, and they were free to withdraw at any time without penalty. No personally identifiable information was collected, and all responses were analyzed in aggregated form to ensure confidentiality.

Results and analysis

Interview

A structured interview was conducted with four subject matter experts (SMEs). The research questions were divided into three sections. The first section was an introductory segment aimed at understanding the expertise of the SMEs in the field of mobility and MMT systems. SME (A) has more than 15 years of experience managing mobility projects in Dubai, while SME (B) specializes in the planning and design of multimodal transport, particularly soft mobility modes. SME (C) has around 5 years of experience in maintaining mobility systems and ensuring their resilience, and SME(D) has more than 15 years of experience in measuring mobility performance. The diverse areas of expertise of the SMEs proved valuable in shaping the research’s goal of proposing a comprehensive framework to measure MMT performance.

All participants confirmed the importance of the proposed factors namely, integration and connectivity, accessibility, data-driven decision-making and innovation, and sustainability and environmental indicators. However, SMEs (A) and (B) rejected the parameter ACC3, which relates to the safety of the transport mode and infrastructure, as a measure of accessibility. Although safety is frequently associated with accessibility in transport research, expert validation and construct refinement led to its exclusion from the accessibility domain in this study. The literature indicates that safety in mature transport systems is increasingly treated as a baseline regulatory and service-quality requirement rather than a discriminating accessibility attribute16,17,45. Retaining safety risked conceptual overlap with quality-of-service and governance constructs27,52. Consequently, accessibility was operationalized through spatial, functional, and financial access dimensions to preserve theoretical coherence and discriminant validity. Furthermore, SMEs (A), (B), and (D) argued that innovation to enhance customer experience (DAT4) and adding new value (DAT5) were difficult to measure and should not be part of the framework for MMT success. Therefore, DAT4 and DAT5 were excluded based on expert consensus due to limited measurability and conceptual overlap with the service quality dimension of MMT success too.

Additionally, SME (D) proposed adding a new factor called “coverage”. Coverage is introduced as a distinct construct to address a persistent conceptual gap in multimodal transport literature, where it is frequently conflated with accessibility or integration despite representing a fundamentally different planning dimension. While accessibility reflects users’ ability to reach and utilize services, and integration reflects coordination among modes, coverage captures the spatial and service availability of multimodal transport across the urban fabric, including route density, service reach, and modal presence across zones8,16,45,46. Treating coverage as an independent construct enables clearer theoretical separation between supply-side network provision and user-side access conditions. Moreover, coverage is proposed to be measured by the optimized distribution of public transport stations (COV1), frequency of public transport trips (COV2), and variety of multimodal transport modes (COV3) as shown in Table 6. Questionnaire surveys

The questionnaire served as the primary research tool for collecting empirical data on factors influencing the success of multimodal transportation (MMT) systems. The instrument was carefully developed through a systematic process. First, a comprehensive review of the literature on transport integration, accessibility, technology adoption, sustainability, and coverage informed the identification of the main constructs. These constructs were then operationalized into measurable indicators tailored to the Dubai context. For example, the construct of integration was translated into items measuring transfer time between modes, optimized route availability, and collaboration between operators. Similarly, the construct of sustainability was expressed through indicators such as reduction in greenhouse gas emissions, share of renewable-energy transport modes, and percentage of soft mobility ridership. The final questionnaire was divided into two parts. The first part assessed the factors affecting MMT system success across five domains: integration, accessibility, technology and innovation, environmental sustainability, and coverage. The second part measured performance indicators related to passenger acceptance, including cost savings, travel time, and perceived quality of service. In total, the instrument comprised 26 items, each designed to be clear, concise, and context-specific. To capture both objective and subjective dimensions, questions combined operational measures (e.g., “Integration between modes is measured by the transit time required for passengers to transfer from one mode to another”) with perception-based statements (e.g., “Accessibility is measured by the affordability of different modes for various socio-economic groups”). All items were rated on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree.

The formulation process ensured both validity and relevance: items were adapted from established indicators in prior research, refined to reflect Dubai’s unique transport context, and reviewed for clarity and face validity by domain experts. This approach provided a balance between scientific rigor and practical applicability, making the survey a reliable instrument for evaluating MMT system performance and passenger acceptance. The survey questions are provided in Table S1.

The sampling frame was defined through purposive targeting of stakeholders with expertise in mobility planning, multimodal and intermodal transportation, mobility infrastructure, and public transport services. Respondents included employees, service providers, and experts from government agencies (e.g., RTA Dubai), transport operators, and academic institutions, ensuring a mix of policy, operational, and technical perspectives. The survey did not explicitly collect socio-demographic data. The authors recognize this as a limitation, as gender-specific mobility needs and social inclusion issues were not explicitly addressed, though these represent valuable areas for future research.

The survey was distributed online via email and professional social networks to more than 180 potential respondents between February and May 2024, yielding 50 valid responses. While the sample size may appear modest, it is justified by the purposive sampling strategy adopted in this study. The research specifically targeted professionals with direct expertise in transportation planning, infrastructure, and multimodal mobility, thereby ensuring that only knowledgeable respondents contributed to the dataset. This purposive approach naturally limited the size of the eligible respondent pool but significantly enhanced the quality and relevance of the collected data. In such exploratory studies, methodological guidelines indicate that 30–50 valid responses are acceptable for structural modelling when constructs are well-defined and context-specific67,68. Moreover, prioritizing expert insights over quantity provided robust evidence for examining the success factors of multimodal transportation in Dubai. PLS-SEM was selected as the primary analytical technique because the objective of this study is theory-driven model validation and exploration of complex latent relationships rather than causal discovery. The proposed multimodal transport success framework consists of multiple unobservable constructs measured through perceptual and operational indicators, requiring simultaneous assessment of measurement reliability, validity, and structural relationships. PLS-SEM is particularly appropriate for such contexts due to its robustness with relatively small sample sizes, non-normal data, and prediction-oriented research designs68,69. Moreover, PLS-SEM enables explicit modeling of mediation effects, which is central to this study’s theoretical contribution in explaining how upstream operational drivers (e.g., data-driven decision-making and coverage) influence multimodal transport success through integration and accessibility. To strengthen validity, the dataset was subjected to reliability and convergent validity assessments, including Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability, and average variance extracted (AVE). These tests confirmed that the measurement model met the required thresholds, reinforcing the credibility of the analysis despite the limited sample size68,69.

The rest of this section will therefore focus on testing assessing the reliability and validity of the external constructs using Cronbach’s Alpha statistical test and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). This is followed by testing relationships between the exogenous and endogenous factors of the model using Partial Least Squared Structural Equation Modelling (PLS- SEM). Smart-PLS, a widely recognized software application for PLS-SEM, is employed for the statistical analysis. It is particularly suitable for small sample sizes and when data normality cannot be assumed69. This software is used to test the relationships between variables within the structural model.

Discussions and conclusions

This study develops and empirically tests an integrated performance model to examine multimodal transport (MMT) success in Dubai. Using PLS-SEM and survey data from mobility professionals, the model examined the causal relationships among integration and connectivity, accessibility, data-driven decision-making and innovation, sustainability orientation, coverage, and MMT success. The findings offer empirical support and contribute to the conceptual understanding of multimodal transport performance. Moreover, the results confirm that integration and connectivity, accessibility, data-driven decision-making, and sustainability have significant positive effects on MMT success. These findings are consistent with prior research emphasizing the importance of network coordination, transfer efficiency, and digital enablement in multimodal systems7,8,10,36,62. However, this study advances the literature by embedding these dimensions within a unified latent causal structure, rather than treating them as isolated performance indicators. MMT success is conceptualized as a user-centered latent construct reflecting perceived value in terms of cost, time, and service quality, consistent with accessibility and service-oriented mobility evaluations12,16,17,27,57. This shifts the theoretical focus from infrastructure provision toward service value creation at the user–system interface. Additionally, the mediation analysis provides important theoretical insight. Coverage does not directly influence MMT success; instead, its effect is transmitted through accessibility. This confirms that spatial or service availability only contributes to success when it translates into meaningful user access, reinforcing accessibility-based planning theory16,45,52. Similarly, data-driven decision-making influences MMT success indirectly through integration and connectivity, demonstrating that digital capabilities act as systemic enablers of coordination, responsiveness, and service coherence rather than as independent performance outcomes18,19,38,39.

Moreover, the model exhibits strong explanatory power, with substantial R2 values and robust reliability and validity statistics, confirming that the selected constructs adequately capture the operational logic of multimodal performance. Additionally, The assessment of collinearity among the exogenous constructs indicates that coverage (COV) and data-driven decision-making (DAT) exhibit relatively higher variance inflation factor (VIF) values compared to other predictors, while remaining below the critical threshold of 5.0 adopted in this study. This pattern reflects substantive interdependence rather than problematic multicollinearity. In multimodal transport systems, service coverage and digital capabilities are structurally linked to accessibility and integration, as expanded spatial reach and real-time data infrastructures jointly enable seamless transfers and system coordination. Accordingly, consistent with this interpretation, the structural model reveals very strong relationships between coverage and accessibility (COV→ACC = 0.875) and between data-driven decision-making and integration (DAT→INT = 0.882), suggesting that these constructs may operate through hierarchical relationships rather than as fully independent drivers. In this dataset, coverage appears to influence user-perceived success primarily through accessibility, while data-driven capabilities function mainly as enablers of integration. These findings indicate that multimodal performance emerges from interconnected operational mechanisms rather than isolated improvements in individual system components. Future research could explore higher-order modelling approaches in which accessibility subsumes coverage and integration subsumes data-driven capabilities, thereby refining the theoretical structure of multimodal transport performance.

In conclusion, this study provides new insights into multimodal transport performance in several important ways:

Collectively, these contributions move multimodal transport research beyond descriptive assessment toward explanatory and predictive theory development.

From a policy and planning perspective, the findings provide clear and actionable guidance for transport authorities and urban planners. Strengthening integration and connectivity requires prioritizing real-time journey planning platforms, seamless ticketing systems, and effective institutional coordination through public–private partnerships, in line with digital mobility integration research18,36,62. While accessibility can be improved by expanding transport networks in underserved areas and adopting affordability policies that lower financial barriers to multimodal use16,17,45,52. Concurrently, sustained investment in data-driven decision-making is essential, as advanced analytics, AI-based optimization, and smart mobility platforms enable predictive demand management, congestion mitigation, and service reliability enhancement19,38,39,59. Moreover, sustainability should be strategically embedded through soft-mobility integration, fleet electrification, and renewable energy deployment, strengthening both environmental performance and user acceptance24,63,65. Collectively, these measures support the development of a resilient, efficient, and user-centric multimodal transport ecosystem in Dubai.

Although Dubai serves as the empirical context, the proposed framework may be applicable to the evaluation of multimodal transport performance in other smart city and urban settings. The causal logic and construct definitions are applicable to other urban contexts seeking to benchmark multimodal maturity and guide strategic investment. Therefore, future research may extend this work through longitudinal analysis, cross-city benchmarking, and integration with land-use and behavioral models56,57,65. Such extensions would further strengthen the predictive capacity and policy relevance of multimodal transport theory.

Finally, this study demonstrates that successful multimodal transport systems are not driven by infrastructure provision alone, but by the strategic orchestration of accessibility, integration, digital capability, sustainability orientation, and service coverage. By developing and empirically testing an integrated performance model with direct and mediating relationships, the study provides conceptual clarification and practical guidance for assessing multimodal transport performance in smart urban contexts.

Limitations and recommendations for future research

This study is subject to several limitations that also open promising avenues for future research. First, the scope of the proposed framework intentionally focused on operational and governance-related key performance indicators (KPIs) that are directly actionable by transport authorities and service providers, such as integration, accessibility, coverage, sustainability orientation, and data-driven decision-making. Consequently, non-controllable contextual factors including legal and regulatory environments, institutional arrangements, organizational maturity, and socio-demographic attributes of respondents, were not explicitly modelled. Future research could extend the framework by incorporating such contextual variables to examine their moderating or conditioning effects on multimodal transport success.

Second, while the use of (PLS-SEM) is appropriate for theory development and exploratory causal modelling, the empirical analysis relied on a purposive expert sample of 50 respondents, drawn from a targeted population of mobility professionals. Although this sample size satisfies established PLS-SEM requirements, future studies should aim to expand both sample size and diversity, including representation from multiple emirates, international cities, and a broader range of public and private stakeholders. In addition, incorporating end-user (commuter) perspectives would allow future research to triangulate professional assessments with lived travel experiences, thereby strengthening the behavioural validity of the framework.

Third, land use and urban form are widely recognized as critical determinants of multimodal performance, while they were not explicitly operationalized as constructs in this study. This exclusion reflects a deliberate theoretical choice to prioritize operational KPIs over structural urban conditions. Nevertheless, future research should integrate spatial variables such as population density, land-use mix, transit-oriented development, and first/last-mile walkability to examine how urban form interacts with operational enablers to shape multimodal outcomes.

Fourth, while this study conceptualizes sustainability as a strategic antecedent that enables multimodal transport success to be consistent with policy-driven and behavioral perspectives, alternative causal logics remain plausible. In particular, future longitudinal or comparative studies could test reciprocal or reverse causal pathways (i.e., multimodal success as a driver of sustainability outcomes), thereby enriching theoretical understanding across different policies and urban contexts.

Finally, emerging technologies including autonomous vehicles, artificial intelligence, advanced analytics, and urban air mobility, were beyond the scope of the present study but represent critical frontiers for future research. Examining how these technologies interact with integration, accessibility, sustainability, and governance mechanisms will be essential for advancing multimodal transport theory and practice in rapidly evolving smart city ecosystems.
