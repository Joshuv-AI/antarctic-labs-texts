First reported recovery and genomic characterization of a previously uncharacterized Mycobacterium species from human blood cultures in an immunocompromised patient: A case report
Authors: Nobuhiro Asai, Yuriko Igarashi, Narimi Miyazaki, Arufumi Shiota, Yuka Yamagishi, Akiko Nakamura, Asami Osugi, Yuki Matsumoto, Shota Nakamura, Satsuki Murakami, Akiyoshi Takami, Satoshi Mitarai, Hiroshige Mikamo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mycobacteremia is an uncommon opportunistic infection in immunocompromised hosts. We report the recovery of a previously uncharacterized Mycobacterium species from two independent blood culture sets obtained from an immunocompromised patient with persistent fever. She presented with persistent fever, and two independent blood culture sets yielded a Mycobacterium species. No focal source of infection was identified. Initial identification by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry suggested Mycobacterium diernhoferi; however, species-level identification could not be confirmed by molecular methods. Whole-genome sequencing demonstrated that the isolate could not be assigned to any currently recognized Mycobacterium species. Average nucleotide identity analysis showed 90.5% similarity to M. diernhoferi and 84.4% to Mycobacterium frederiksbergense, suggesting a previously uncharacterized species. Combination antimicrobial therapy was followed by resolution of fever and no recurrent positive blood cultures during treatment. Although the patient ultimately died because of progression of the underlying hematological malignancy, the patient showed sustained clinical improvement without recurrent positive blood cultures during therapy. To our knowledge, this is the first reported recovery of this genomically distinct Mycobacterium species from human blood cultures. This case highlights the value of whole-genome sequencing in recognizing previously uncharacterized Mycobacterium species recovered from human blood cultures. The pathogenic role of this organism requires further investigation.

Introduction

Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are environmental organisms that can cause a wide range of clinical infections [1], [2]. Although pulmonary disease is the most common manifestation, disseminated NTM (dNTM) infection may occur in immunocompromised individuals and is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality [2], [3]. Historically, dNTM has been reported mainly in patients with advanced human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection; however, increasing numbers of cases have recently been recognized among non-HIV patients with hematological malignancies, solid organ transplantation, or other conditions associated with impaired cell-mediated immunity.

Advances in molecular diagnostic techniques and whole-genome sequencing have facilitated the identification of previously unrecognized mycobacterial species [4]. However, the clinical significance and optimal management of infections caused by these organisms remain poorly understood. We report recovery of a previously uncharacterized Mycobacterium species from blood cultures obtained from an immunocompromised patient with persistent fever [5].

Case presentation

A 75-year-old woman with a history of diabetes mellitus was diagnosed with myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) with refractory anemia with excess blasts−2 (RAEB−2) during a routine outpatient visit. She had received 24 cycles of azacitidine (AZA) every four weeks over four years, during which her disease remained stable. However, chemotherapy was discontinued because of AZA-induced liver dysfunction, and she subsequently received best supportive care.

Two weeks before admission, she developed a persistent high-grade fever that did not resolve despite symptomatic treatment. She had no indwelling vascular catheter or other implanted medical devices. Because the fever persisted, she was admitted to our hospital for further evaluation. On physical examination, no localized signs of infection were identified. No skin lesions, soft tissue abnormalities, or palpable lymphadenopathy were present. Two sets of blood cultures obtained on admission became positive for Mycobacterium species, with a time to positivity of 90 h. Chest and abdominal computed tomography demonstrated no apparent infectious focus, including pulmonary lesions, abscesses, or lymphadenopathy. No alternative source of fever was identified. Transthoracic echocardiography performed during the clinical course revealed no evidence of infective endocarditis. MALDI-TOF MS initially identified the isolate as Mycobacterium diernhoferi. We subsequently performed 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which demonstrated high sequence similarity to Mycobacterium frederiksbergense (99.3%), Mycobacterium bacteremicum (98.7%), and Mycobacterium fluoranthenivorans (98.7%); however, species-level identification was not possible. Whole-genome sequencing was subsequently conducted at the Department of Mycobacterium Reference and Research, Research Institute of Tuberculosis, Japan Anti-Tuberculosis Association. Discussion

Mycobacterial bloodstream infection is an uncommon but serious opportunistic infection that predominantly affects patients with impaired cell-mediated immunity. Although disseminated nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) disease has historically been associated with advanced human immunodeficiency virus infection, an increasing number of cases have recently been recognized among non-HIV patients with hematological malignancies, organ transplantation, and other conditions associated with immunosuppression [2], [3], [6]. In the present case, prolonged azacitidine treatment for myelodysplastic syndrome, advanced age, and diabetes mellitus likely contributed to impaired host immunity and increased susceptibility to mycobacterial infection [7], [8].

The diagnosis of mycobacteremia was supported by positive results from two independent sets of blood cultures, the absence of alternative pathogens, resolution of fever following antimicrobial therapy, and persistently negative follow-up blood cultures after treatment initiation. Although computed tomography and transthoracic echocardiography failed to identify a focal infectious source, NTM bloodstream infection should be considered a clinically significant finding in immuno-compromised patients. Clinicians should therefore include NTM infection in the differential diagnosis of persistent fever in patients with hematological malignancies, even when conventional imaging studies are unrevealing [2], [6]. Although isolation of the organism from two independent blood culture sets together with the clinical response to antimicrobial therapy supports a possible pathogenic role, definitive causality cannot be established from a single case. Alternative explanations, including transient bacteremia or contamination, cannot be completely excluded. Nevertheless, contamination appears less likely because the organism was isolated from two independent blood culture sets and no alternative pathogen was identified.

One of the most remarkable aspects of this case was the identification of a previously uncharacterized Mycobacterium species. Initial identification by MALDI-TOF MS suggested Mycobacterium diernhoferi; however, 16S rRNA gene sequencing and whole-genome analysis did not support assignment to any currently recognized species. The isolate demonstrated only 90.5% average nucleotide identity (ANI) with M. diernhoferi and 84.4% with M. frederiksbergense, both substantially below the generally accepted species delineation threshold of 95–96% [1], [4]. These findings strongly suggest that the organism represents a distinct genomic species. The genome sequence has been deposited in GenBank under accession number GCF_043974665.1 (BioProject PRJNA1074501) [5]. To our knowledge, this is the first reported human infection caused by this organism and highlights the value of whole-genome sequencing for the recognition of novel mycobacterial pathogens.

The optimal treatment of infections caused by rare or previously uncharacterized NTM species remains unclear [3], [4]. Current treatment strategies are largely extrapolated from experience with established NTM pathogens and generally rely on multidrug therapy guided by antimicrobial susceptibility testing [9], [10], [11]. In the present case, empirical combination therapy with clarithromycin, moxifloxacin, minocycline, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole was initiated and subsequently modified according to susceptibility results. Blood cultures became negative after treatment initiation, and no recurrence of bacteremia was observed during six months of therapy. These findings suggest that susceptibility-guided multidrug therapy may be effective for bloodstream infections caused by uncommon NTM species [3], [9], [10]. Although microbiological control was successfully achieved, the patient ultimately died because of progression of the underlying hematological malignancy. This clinical course underscores the importance of distinguishing infection-related outcomes from mortality attributable to the underlying disease in immunocompromised patients [3], [6].

In conclusion, we report a case of mycobacteremia caused by a previously uncharacterized Mycobacterium species in a patient with myelodysplastic syndrome. This case highlights the utility of whole-genome sequencing for accurate pathogen identification and suggests that susceptibility-guided multidrug therapy can achieve microbiological control even in infections caused by previously unrecognized Mycobacterium species.
