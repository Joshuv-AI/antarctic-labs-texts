Dual-modular-nanobody CAR-T cell technical platform against the solid tumor microenvironment
Authors: Yangzi Li, Xuan Wang, Shangkun Zhang, Tao Peng, Zihan Gong, Haodong Jiang, Xianing Huang, Shenxia Xie, Heng Liu, Fengzhen Mo, Tongcun Zhang, Xiaomei Yang, Xiaobing Jiang, Xiaoling Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCAR-T therapy is effective in hematologic cancers but faces challenges in solid tumors due to antigen heterogeneity and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Systemic CTLA-4 blockade enhances immunity but often causes severe adverse events. To overcome these limitations, we developed a dual-modular nanobody-based CAR-T platform targeting fibroblast activation protein (FAP) on cancer-associated fibroblasts and locally releasing an anti-CTLA-4 nanobody within the tumor stroma.MethodsFAP/CTLA-4 dual-module CAR-T cells were generated and assessed in vitro for antigen-specific cytotoxicity, cytokine release, and exhaustion. Antitumor efficacy was evaluated in xenograft models, measuring tumor growth, survival, and T-cell infiltration (Ethics Approval Number: 202001011). One patient with refractory glioblastoma received intrathecal infusion; clinical response, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytokines (Ethics Approval Number 2022-0553-01), and safety were monitored. Tumor and immune microenvironment changes were analyzed via transcriptomic sequencing and multiplex immunofluorescence staining.ResultsIn vitro, engineered CAR-T cells showed potent cytotoxicity, cytokine production, and reduced exhaustion. In vivo, they induced tumor regression, prolonged survival, and increased T-cell infiltration. In the glioblastoma patient, intrathecal administration resulted in disease stabilization, elevated CSF cytokines, and a favorable safety profile. Transcriptomic sequencing and multiplex immunofluorescence staining indicated TME remodeling toward an immunologically active state.ConclusionsFAP/CTLA-4 DMN CAR-T overcomes the immunosuppressive solid tumor microenvironment through localized immunomodulation, demonstrating promising efficacy in preclinical models and a patient with refractory glioblastoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13045-026-01821-7.

Introduction

Currently, the standard clinical management of solid tumors primarily involves surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. Although these modalities have extended patient survival to some degree, a considerable proportion of patients still experience local recurrence or distant metastasis following treatment, indicating that current therapeutic strategies remain inadequate for achieving long-term disease control [1–4]. Conventional treatments mainly function by directly eliminating tumor cells. However, due to the high intrinsic heterogeneity of solid tumors and the combined physico-biological barriers constituted by the tumor and its immunosuppressive microenvironment, existing approaches struggle to fundamentally break the vicious cycle of “treatment–recurrence–metastasis“ [5, 6].

The advent of immunotherapy has provided a new direction to alter this predicament. Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies, represented by PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in various solid tumors [7]. However, they have failed to meet primary endpoints in pivotal clinical trials for certain cancer types, particularly those with low immunogenicity. Such tumors are often classified as “cold” tumors, characterized by features including low tumor mutational burden (TMB), weak antigen-presenting capacity, and insufficient infiltration of effector immune cells, which collectively hinder the effective initiation of anti-tumor immune responses by immune checkpoint inhibitors [8].

Notably, preclinical and some clinical studies suggest that antibodies targeting another crucial immune checkpoint, CTLA-4 (e.g., ipilimumab), may exhibit stronger T-cell activating capability and superior anti-tumor activity compared to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in treating certain solid tumors [9, 10]. Nevertheless, systemic administration of CTLA-4 antibodies is frequently associated with significant immune-related adverse events (irAEs), including colitis and hypophysitis, whose incidence and severity are notably high, thereby limiting the clinical applicability of this therapeutic approach [11, 12].

Consequently, although CTLA-4 antibodies hold mechanistic advantages, their systemic toxicity constrains the safe application of conventional intravenous administration in solid tumors. These challenges collectively indicate that immune checkpoint inhibition strategies face difficulties in effectively activating local anti-tumor immunity while maintaining systemic safety, and also underscore the fundamental limitations of current therapies in reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment of solid tumors [13].

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are core components constituting the stromal barrier of solid tumors and specifically overexpress fibroblast activation protein (FAP) on their surface [14, 15]. CAFs represent not only an ideal therapeutic target but also a critical breakthrough point for dismantling the physical and immune barriers of solid tumors: they secrete collagen, fibronectin, and other components to form a dense physical barrier of extracellular matrix (ECM), while simultaneously producing immunosuppressive factors such as TGF-β and CXCL12, which impede T-cell infiltration and impair their function [16, 17]. Therefore, targeted elimination of FAP-positive CAFs can directly disrupt the tumor’s physical barrier and alleviate local immunosuppression [18].

While the concept of arming CAR-T cells with secreted immunomodulatory payloads has been previously explored, existing designs differ fundamentally from our approach. For instance, Rafiq et al. demonstrated that CAR-T cells secreting anti-PD-1 scFv could enhance anti-tumor efficacy while avoiding systemic toxicity associated with checkpoint blockade [19]. Similarly, Xie et al. reported nanobody CAR-T cells secreting anti-CTLA-4 or anti-PD-L1 single-domain fragments, but these were paired with tumor-targeting CARs (e.g., anti-gp75/TRP1 CAR for melanoma), not stromal-targeting constructs [20]. On the other hand, FAP-targeting CAR-T cells have been established as a strategy to deplete cancer-associated fibroblasts and remodel tumor stroma, as demonstrated by Wang et al. and Kakarla et al., but these studies did not arm the CAR-T cells with any secreted immunomodulatory payloads [21, 22]. Importantly, seminal work by Feig et al. demonstrated that depletion of FAP-expressing carcinoma-associated fibroblasts in pancreatic cancer uncovers the antitumor effects of both anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-L1 antibodies, which are otherwise ineffective [23]. This mechanistic insight provides the biological rationale for our dual-modular design: by physically removing the FAP-positive stromal barrier while simultaneously delivering CTLA-4 blockade, our platform addresses both the physical and immunological barriers that prevent effective CAR-T therapy in solid tumors. To our knowledge, no prior study has reported a FAP-targeting CAR-T cell engineered to secrete an anti-CTLA-4 nanobody.

We reasoned that concurrently breaching the stromal barrier imposed by cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and antagonizing CTLA-4-mediated local immunosuppression would potentiate CAR-T cell activity in solid tumors. Accordingly, we constructed a dual-nanobody CAR-T platform: the CAR targets fibroblast activation protein (FAP), a surface marker of CAFs, while the T cells are engineered to secrete a CTLA-4-blocking nanobody. Unlike prior designs in which only a single module employs a nanobody, this platform configures both the CAR and the secreted immune checkpoint blocker as nanobodies, thereby maximizing their synergistic advantages. Nanobodies, at ~ 15 kDa, are roughly one-tenth the size of full-length antibodies and markedly smaller than scFvs, enabling deeper penetration into dense desmoplastic stroma; moreover, their single-domain architecture circumvents the variable-domain mispairing that frequently complicates multispecific scFv-based designs [24,25].

Preclinical studies demonstrated that this therapy can effectively recognize and kill FAP-positive cells in vitro. In solid tumor animal models, it significantly enhanced T-cell infiltration, prolonged median survival, and exhibited a favorable safety profile. This strategy has advanced to a phase I clinical trial. In the first treated patient with recurrent high-grade glioma, a trend toward tumor volume reduction was observed with good tolerability. Additionally, increased intratumoral immune cell infiltration was noted. These preliminary findings suggest that this strategy holds promise for reshaping the immune microenvironment and promoting stromal remodeling and enhancing CAR-T infiltration into solid tumors. Evidence for conversion of immunologically “cold” tumors to “hot” tumors was observed only in the clinical case (based ontranscriptomic sequencing and multiplex immunofluorescence staining analyses of human tumor tissue), as the immunodeficient NOD/SCID mouse models lack the endogenous immune system required to comprehensively evaluate immune remodeling.

Thus, FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T we constructed offers two key advantages: it recognizes the stromal marker FAP, and it secretes CTLA-4 nanobody to remodel the immunosuppressive microenvironment. By combining FAP-CAR mediated stroma clearance with secretory CTLA-4 nanobody to relieve Treg-mediated suppression, FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T enhances antitumor activity through two synergistic pathways. This study provides a new direction for combinatorial therapy to address the dual barriers of the solid TME: physical obstruction and immune suppression.

Results

Construction of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T

The Nanobody-based CAR T cells generated in this study were constructed as illustrated in Fig. 1A. Viral vectors were engineered to encode a fluorescently labeled FAP CAR linked via an IRES sequence to a secreted CTLA-4 nanobody. The CAR sequence was amplified by PCR and subsequently cloned into the lentiviral vector GV400. Lentiviral particles were produced using a three-plasmid packaging system and used to transduce T cells. Transduction efficiency was assessed based on the expression of a fluorescent reporter molecule (Fig. 1B). To confirm surface expression of the CAR, transduced and control CAR T cells were stained with a fluorescently labeled anti-His-tag antibody. His-tag and GFP expression were detectable in 30–50% of transduced cells (Fig. 1C). Secretion of the CTLA-4 nanobody was confirmed by detecting the His-tag label in the supernatant of CAR T cultures using specific immunoassays (Fig. 1E).

To further investigate the functional characteristics of the secreted CTLA‑4 nanobody (from FAP/CTLA‑4‑DMN CAR‑T), we performed a series of in vitro binding and functional assays. ELISA‑based assessment demonstrated that supernatants derived from FAP/CTLA‑4‑DMN CAR‑T cells exhibited dose‑dependent binding to immobilized CD80, with an apparent IC50 dilution of approximately 1:497.6 (Fig. 2A). We next sought to further validate the function of the secreted CTLA‑4 nanobody. In a mixed lymphocyte reaction (MLR) set up as a one-way assay (irradiated or mitomycin-treated DCs as stimulators), addition of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cell supernatants to DC : T cell co-cultures increased IFN-γ and IL-2 secretion. This result suggests that the supernatant disrupts CD80-CTLA-4 inhibitory signaling, thereby enhancing T cell activation. This enhanced cytokine production was substantially diminished upon competitive blockade with soluble CTLA‑4 and was not observed with control supernatant, suggesting that the observed immunostimulatory effect was attributable to FAP/CTLA‑4‑DMN CAR‑T ‑mediated disruption of CD80‑CTLA‑4 engagement (Fig. 2C-D). Collectively, these data indicate that FAP/CTLA‑4‑DMN CAR‑T cells secrete a functionally active CTLA‑4 nanobody capable of binding CD80 and interfering with the CD80‑CTLA‑4 inhibitory pathway, which may contribute to enhanced T cell activation within the tumor microenvironment.

Antigen recognition capability was verified via flow cytometry by incubating CAR T cells with recombinant FAP protein, followed by staining with a fluorescently labeled anti-FAP-PE antibody (Fig. 3A). In CAR-T cells generated via lentiviral transduction, the expression levels of CD4 and CD8 showed no significant differences compared to untransduced T cells (Fig. 3B).

To evaluate the functional response of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR T cells, co-culture with HepG2-FAP cells was conducted. Significant proliferation of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR T cells was observed upon antigen stimulation. This proliferation was effectively inhibited by soluble FAP protein, confirming the specificity of antigen binding (Fig. 3C). In summary, these results demonstrate that the FAP-specific nanobody retains high antigen-binding specificity when incorporated into the CAR architecture, supporting its suitability as a recognition module in CAR T cell engineering.

FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR T effectively reduces T exhaustion

FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR T and control CAR-T cells were challenged with irradiated HepG2-FAP for 48 h, these CAR-T cells were collected and stained with fluorescent antibodies. Flow cytometry revealed that the percentages of CD25+, CD69+, FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR T were significantly higher than the control groups (Fig. 4A-B). Notably, while FAPNb CAR‑T cells and FAPNb CAR‑T cells supplemented with exogenous CTLA‑4 nanobody also showed increased activation relative to irrelevant CAR‑T cells or CTLA‑4Nb CAR‑T cells alone, the integrated FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR T construct displayed the most pronounced activation phenotype, as evidenced by the highest proportions of CD25⁺ and CD69⁺ cells among all groups.

We further examined T cell differentiation status by co‑staining for CCR7 and CD45RA to delineate four functional subsets: naïve (CCR7⁺CD45RA⁺), central memory T cells (TCM, CCR7⁺CD45RA⁻), effector memory T cells (TEM, CCR7⁻CD45RA⁻), and terminally differentiated effector memory T cells re‑expressing CD45RA (TEMRA, CCR7⁻CD45RA⁺). FAP/CTLA‑4‑DMN CAR‑T cells exhibited a differentiation profile characterized by relatively higher proportions of naïve and TCM subsets compared with FAPNb CAR‑T cells and other control groups (Fig. 4 C). Conversely, the proportion of TEM cells was correspondingly lower in the FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR T group, suggesting a less terminally differentiated state. The proportion of TEMRA cells appeared comparable across groups. Taken together, these observations suggest that the FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR T design not only promotes T cell activation but may also favor the maintenance of a less differentiated, potentially more persistent T cell phenotype, which could be advantageous for sustained anti‑tumor efficacy in vivo.

To evaluate whether FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells retain anti-tumor activity and resist exhaustion under chronic antigen stimulation, we set up a long-term co-culture system using FAP-expressing target cells. Analysis of FAP expression in tumor cell lines by flow cytometry revealed that HepG2-FAP cells exhibited high surface FAP expression, U87 cells showed low FAP expression, and no FAP expression was detected on HepG2 cells (Fig. 5 A). CAR-T cells were co-cultured with tumor cells for two weeks (Fig. 5B), after which we assessed exhaustion marker expression and functional responses to re-challenge.

After prolonged antigen exposure, FAPNb CAR-T cells showed clear upregulation of PD-1, LAG-3, and TIM-3, consistent with activation-induced exhaustion. In contrast, FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells expressed substantially lower levels of all three markers (Fig. 5C-E), indicating that local CTLA-4 nanobody secretion may alleviate T cell exhaustion under persistent antigen stimulation.

We next asked whether the reduced exhaustion markers translated into preserved effector function. CAR-T cells recovered from the two-week co-culture were re-challenged with fresh tumor cells. Upon re-stimulation, FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells secreted markedly higher levels of IL-2, TNF-α, and IFN-γ than FAPNb CAR-T, FAPNb CAR-T plus exogenous CTLA-4 nanobody, and irrelevant CAR-T controls (Fig. 5F-H). Consistent with the cytokine data, tumor cell killing assays at effector-to-target ratios of 1:1, 5:1, and 10:1 showed that FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells retained the strongest cytotoxicity against FAP-positive tumor cells after prior chronic antigen exposure (Fig. 5I). Together, these results suggest that the integrated FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T not only limits exhaustion during prolonged antigen stimulation but also preserves polyfunctional effector responses upon re-challenge—features that may support sustained anti-tumor activity in vivo.

In Vitro Activity of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T : cytokine production and cytotoxicity

To verify whether FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells mediate tumor cell killing through specific recognition of FAP antigen, we co-cultured FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells and control cells with target cells (HepG2-FAP, U87, HepG2) at various effector-to-target (E: T) ratios (1:1, 5:1, 10:1) for 20 h. The proportion of PKH26⁺7-AAD⁺ double-positive cells (i.e., dead target cells) was then assessed by flow cytometry. The in vitro cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells specifically lysed FAP-positive cells but showed no significant killing activity against FAP-negative cells. This cytotoxic effect was dependent on the E: T ratio (Fig. 6A-D). Furthermore, the addition of soluble FAP protein to block the interaction between CAR-T cells and FAP antigen reduced the killing efficacy of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells against FAP-positive targets. In contrast, irrelevant CAR-T cells exhibited no significant cytotoxicity against FAP-positive cells. Together, these results confirm that FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells effectively and specifically kill FAP-positive tumor cells in vitro.

To evaluate whether FAP-positive target cells (HepG2-FAP) could activate FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells and stimulate the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines in vitro, we conducted the following experiments. FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells and control cells were co-cultured with equal numbers of irradiated target cells (HepG2-FAP, U87, HepG2) for 12 h. The concentrations of interferon-γ (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-2 (IL-2) in the culture supernatants were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The results showed that upon stimulation with FAP-positive HepG2-FAP and U87 cells, the levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 in the supernatants of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells were significantly higher than those in the control groups. Moreover, the ability of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells to secrete these cytokines increased with higher FAP expression levels on the target cells. In contrast, when stimulated with FAP-negative HepG2 cells, the levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 in the supernatants of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells showed no significant difference compared to the controls (Fig. 6E-G). Enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISPOT) assay further demonstrated that the number of IFN-γ-secreting spot-forming cells was significantly greater in FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells following stimulation with FAP-positive cells compared to the control groups (Figure 6H). Collectively, these data indicate that FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells can be effectively activated by FAP-positive target cells and release elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines.

In vivo application of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-t slows growth of solid tumors

Given the potent antitumor activity demonstrated by FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells in vitro, we further established three xenograft tumor models to evaluate their efficacy in vivo. Tumor-bearing models were generated by inoculating target cells (HepG2-FAP, U87, HepG2) into NOD/SCID mice. When tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm³, the mice were randomly assigned to different treatment groups. Mice received FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cell therapy (5 × 10⁶ cells per injection) via weekly injections for two weeks, alongside other control treatments. Tumor volume and weight were measured every three days thereafter. The results showed that in both HepG2-FAP and U87 tumor-bearing models, FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells significantly suppressed tumor growth compared to other treatment groups. Specifically, in the HepG2-FAP and U87 models, mice treated with FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells exhibited markedly delayed tumor progression and prolonged survival (Fig. 7A-C).A consistent effect was observed in a patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model(Derived from post-resection samples of clinical glioma patients), where FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells also suppressed the growth of primary human tumor cells in mice. Moreover, in the PDX model, flow cytometry analysis of tumor tissue following two treatment cycles revealed that FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells were effectively retained within the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, analysis of the PDX model after two treatment cycles demonstrated effective intratumoral retention of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells as measured by flow cytometry (Fig. 7D).

To evaluate the safety of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cell therapy, we assessed potential toxicity in treated animals 30 days after treatment. Histopathological examination of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain) revealed no overt pathological changes in the FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T group compared with PBS controls (Figure S1A), indicating an absence of significant organ toxicity.

We next measured serum ALT, AST, and BUN levels to assess liver and kidney function. Among all groups, FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T-treated mice showed the lowest ALT and AST levels, including relative to PBS and other CAR-T controls (Figure S1B). BUN levels remained comparable across most groups, with no notable elevation in the FAP/CTLA-4-DMN group, suggesting preserved renal function (Figure S1C).

Body weight was monitored throughout the study. Mice receiving FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells maintained stable body weight over the 30-day observation period, whereas several control groups—particularly PBS- and UTD-treated animals—displayed progressive weight loss (Figure S1D). This finding suggests that FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T therapy was well tolerated and may have contributed to better overall condition, likely due to its anti-tumor efficacy.

Given that cytokine release syndrome (CRS) is a major safety concern in CAR-T therapy, we measured serum IL-6 levels as a key CRS indicator. FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T-treated mice exhibited the lowest IL-6 levels among all groups, comparable to PBS controls (Figure S1E). In contrast, the FAPNb CAR-T and FAPNb CAR-T plus exogenous CTLA-4 nanobody groups showed markedly elevated IL-6, consistent with a more severe inflammatory response. Together, these data indicate that the integrated FAP/CTLA-4-DMN design achieves potent anti-tumor activity without inducing appreciable systemic toxicity, organ damage, or CRS-related cytokine elevation, supporting its favorable safety profile.

FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T for glioma patients

Based on prior preclinical data, we initiated a Phase I clinical trial to evaluate the safety and efficacy of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cell therapy in patients with glioma. The trial enrolled one patient who had not previously received any CAR-T cell therapy. Pre-treatment imaging revealed Giloblastoma (WHO grade). The patient had undergone multiple prior therapeutic regimens, all of which failed to effectively control intracranial tumor progression, resulting in a critical clinical condition.

To address this therapeutic challenge, we employed an innovative intrathecal administration approach, directly delivering FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). This strategy was designed to enhance local immunotherapeutic drug concentrations while minimizing systemic adverse reactions. Following enrollment, the patient received intracavitary injections of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells once weekly for three consecutive weeks, followed by a one-month rest period, constituting one treatment cycle (Fig. 9A). Immunohistochemical analysis of the pre-treatment tumor revealed elevated expression of FAP and CTLA-4, thereby meeting the indication criteria for FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T therapy (Fig. 9B).

Discussion

T cell-based immunotherapy has profoundly reshaped the landscape of modern cancer treatment. However, despite the groundbreaking success of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells in hematological malignancies, their efficacy against solid tumors remains substantially limited [26]. This constraint stems primarily from two inherent challenges posed by solid tumors: first, target-related obstacles such as antigen heterogeneity and therapy-induced antigen loss; and second, a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Together, these factors significantly hinder the full potential of CAR-T cell function [27, 28]. To concurrently address these challenges, there is an urgent need to develop CAR-T cells capable of synergistically recognizing tumor antigens while modulating the TME [29, 30].

The conventional engineering of CAR-T cells itself presents technical bottlenecks. A key issue is the risk of mispairing between the variable domains of the heavy (VH) and light (VL) chains in single-chain antibody fragments, which can compromise the functional integrity of the antigen-binding domain. Furthermore, the relatively large molecular size of these conventional antibody fragments may also restrict their effective penetration into the deeper regions of solid tumors [31].

In recent years, nanobody have emerged as a promising solution for next-generation CAR-T design by virtue of their unique molecular properties. They not only effectively circumvent chain mispairing issues but also facilitate enhanced tumor tissue penetration due to their compact size. More importantly, nanobodies are exceptionally well-suited for constructing modular CAR-T systems—enabling, for instance, coordinated multi-target recognition or the assembly of CAR-T cells capable of localized secretion of immunomodulatory molecules [32, 33].Despite the considerable promise of this modular strategy, the development and reporting of CAR-T cells that can simultaneously target the TME and locally secrete nanobody to remodel the immunosuppressive microenvironment remains an unmet need and has not yet been reported.

In this study, we successfully developed and evaluated a novel modular CAR-T cell therapy, FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T, designed to overcome two major hurdles in solid tumor treatment—inadequate tumor targeting and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME)—through a synergistic mechanism. Our findings demonstrate that this design not only exhibits robust antitumor efficacy but also offers a novel strategy for addressing CAR-T cell dysfunction in solid tumors by improving T-cell differentiation status and persistence.

A key advantage of this design lies in its clever utilization of nanobody properties to construct a novel CAR structure capable of simultaneously performing dual functions of “recognition” and “modulation.” Compared to conventional CAR designs, this strategy effectively mitigates the risk of heavy and light chain mispairing, ensuring the correct assembly and expression of functional proteins [24, 25]. Functional assays confirmed that FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells specifically recognize FAP-positive tumor cells and reverse local immunosuppression through the localized secretion of anti-CTLA-4 nanobodies. This dual-pronged mechanism of action enabled these cells to exhibit superior cytotoxic activity and tumor growth suppression compared to single-target CAR-T cells in both in vitro and in vivo models.

Furthermore, in vitro proliferation assays demonstrated that FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells exhibited a significantly enhanced proliferative capacity compared to various control groups, suggesting that these cells possess not only superior immediate cytotoxic function but also a sustained expansion potential. Importantly, this robust proliferative capacity was associated with a more favorable differentiation profile. Specifically, these cells showed a tendency to differentiate towards a central memory T cell (Tcm) phenotype, which is linked to enhanced persistence and long-term replicative potential. This observation aligns with recent studies suggesting that rational CAR signaling design can steer T cells towards a more advantageous differentiation fate. Moreover, under conditions of persistent antigen stimulation, FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells displayed significantly lower surface expression of exhaustion markers (e.g., PD-1, TIM-3, LAG-3), indicating that our modular design may intrinsically delay the process of T-cell exhaustion, thereby sustaining long-term antitumor immune surveillance.

From a clinical translation standpoint, the preliminary data obtained in this study are encouraging. In one patient with recurrent glioblastoma refractory to multiple lines of therapy, intrathecal administration of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T cells led to disease stabilization lasting 2-year, with manageable treatment-related toxicity. Elevated cytokine levels (including IL-6 and IFN-γ) in the cerebrospinal fluid confirmed CAR-T cell activation and expansion at the tumor site, whereas the absence of parallel changes in peripheral blood underscored the targeting precision of locoregional delivery. Analysis of paired pre- and post-treatment tumor specimens yielded early mechanistic insights: transcriptomic and multiplex immunofluorescence data showed suggestive evidence of tumor microenvironment remodeling, reflected by increased immune infiltration and diminished stromal barriers, though these findings await corroboration in larger cohorts and functional studies. Given the intrinsic heterogeneity of glioblastoma and the recognized possibility of spontaneous stabilization [34], this single-patient observation cannot establish therapeutic efficacy and requires validation in prospective cohorts.

In addition, it is important to acknowledge that the NOD/SCID immunodeficient mouse model used in our preclinical studies lacks a functional endogenous immune system, including T cells, B cells, and NK cells. Consequently, these models cannot recapitulate the effects of anti-CTLA-4 nanobody secretion on endogenous immune cell populations, Treg modulation, dendritic cell activation, or the conversion of immunologically ‘cold’ tumors to ‘hot’ tumors. The antitumor effects observed in these models reflect primarily the direct cytotoxic activity of FAP-targeting CAR-T cells against FAP+ stromal cells, rather than the combined direct and immunomodulatory effects expected in an immunocompetent host.

From a mechanistic perspective, the majority of cytokine-secreting CAR-T cells release IL-12, IL-15, or IL-18 upon antigen engagement, acting predominantly in an autocrine or paracrine fashion to potentiate T-cell and NK-cell effector functions. Our design, by contrast, creates two cooperating antitumor pathways: direct elimination of tumor stromal cells by FAP-targeted CAR-T cells, and re-activation of endogenous T cells within the tumor microenvironment through local checkpoint blockade. The latter represents the defining distinction from conventional secretory CAR-T strategies. This mechanistic divergence also clearly differentiates our platform from an ongoing Nectin4/FAP dual-targeting CAR-T trial (NCT03932565) that secretes IL-7, CCL19, and IL-12, exhibiting differences in both targeting logic and the class of effector molecules deployed. Furthermore, the 4-1BB costimulatory signal incorporated into our CAR construct offers a mechanistic foundation consistent with the manageable safety profile and the reduced propensity for CAR-T cell exhaustion observed thus far.

In summary, this study systematically validates FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T therapy as a feasible and safe strategy for solid tumors, with initial evidence of efficacy from proof-of-concept to preclinical and early clinical evaluation. Our work offers a modular approach to address key challenges of CAR-T therapy in solid tumors and provides a practical basis for designing next-generation cell therapies. Future efforts will focus on larger clinical trials, combination regimens, and mechanistic studies, aiming to benefit patients with refractory solid tumors such as glioblastoma.

Materials and methods

Culture of cell lines

All the cells lines(HepG2,HepG2-FAP, U87)were cultured, as per American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia) recommendations, in standard complete medium, consisting of DMEM (Gibco, Invitrogen, Merelbeke, Belgium), supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS, Gibco, Invitrogen) and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin.

Production of lentiviral vector

The lentiviral vector encoding FAP nanobody with CTLA-4 nanobody, FAP nanobody, CTLA-4 nanobody, Irr nanobody CAR, (CAR) was constructed based on the GV400 lentiviral vector provided by Genechem. Four additional CAR constructs (FAP Nb, CTLA-4 Nb and FAP/CTLA-4 Nb, CD19/CTLA-4) were synthesized and cloned into a second-generation lentiviral plasmid backbone. All CAR constructs contained a CD8 transmembrane domain in tandem with an intracellular 4-1BB costimulatory domain and a CD3ζ signaling domain. And all CAR with both sequences flanked by a His-tag element. All constructs also contained a transgene coding for the fluorescent reporter, GFP, to aid in the evaluation of transduction efficiency. Lentiviral supernatant was collected at 48 H after transfection and puromycin selection and frozen until use.

Generation of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T expressing human T cells

PBMCs were isolated by Ficoll density centrifugation of healthy donor blood, obtained under an ethically approved study protocol after obtaining informed consent from participants. Human T cells were activated on day using CD3,CD28 antibody (Life Technologies) and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium 10% FBS and 4ng/ml recombinant human IL-2. Lentiviral transduction of cells was performed on day 1,after 96 h observation of transfection with fluorescence microscope. After 5 day, Transduced cells were detected by GFP expression and an anti-HIS tag antibody to aid in the evaluation of transduction efficiency.

Mixed lymphocyte reaction (MLR) assay

In vitro functional validation of CTLA-4 checkpoint blockade was assessed using a one-way human allogeneic mixed lymphocyte reaction (MLR) assay. Human monocyte-derived dendritic cells (MoDCs) were generated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of healthy donors by culture in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 ug/mL streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 ng/mL recombinant human GM-CSF (PeproTech), and 50 ng/mL recombinant human IL-4 (PeproTech) for 6 days. Immature MoDCs were subsequently matured with 1 ug/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h. Mature MoDCs were co-cultured with CD3 + T cells (isolated by negative selection using the EasySep Human T Cell Isolation Kit; StemCell Technologies) at a stimulator-to-responder ratio of 1:10 in 96-well round-bottom plates (Corning; 1 × 10^5 T cells/well). FAP/CTLA-4-DMN or control CAR-T cell supernatants were added to the co-cultures at a final concentration of 10% (v/v). Where indicated, a neutralizing anti-CTLA-4 monoclonal antibody (clone BNI3, 10 ug/mL; BD Biosciences) was added 30 min prior to supernatant supplementation to abrogate CTLA-4-mediated signaling. Cells were cultured at 37 C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator for 3 days. For cytokine detection, cell culture supernatants were collected at 72 h and stored at −80℃until analysis. Concentrations of IL-2, IFN-gamma were determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; DuoSet kits, R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All assays were performed in three independent experiments using different allogeneic donor pairs.

T-cell activation and functional assays

Different CAR-T cell which stained with PKH26 constructs coculture with mitomycin treated tumor targets at an E/T ratio of 1:1 for 5d. The cells were collected every day, and the fluorescence was attenuated by flow cytometry after 5 days. At the same time, collecting the supernatant in order to detect secreting nanobody by ELISA.In experiments where activation markers CD4, CD8, CD25 and CD69, were assessed, CAR-T cells were incubated with Mitomycin treated HepG2-FAP at an E/T of 1:1. Cells were cocultured for 72 h and then subjected to flow cytometric analysis.

Re-challenge assay after chronic antigen stimulation

To assess the functional resilience of CAR-T cells following prolonged antigen exposure, a chronic co-culture and re-challenge assay was established. Briefly, CAR-T cells (FAP/CTLA-4-DMN, FAPNb, FAPNb plus exogenous CTLA-4 nanobody, or irrelevant CAR-T control) were co-cultured with FAP-expressing HepG2-FAP target cells at an effector-to-target (E: T) ratio of 1:1 in complete medium. The co-culture was maintained for two weeks, with fresh target cells added every 3–4 days to sustain chronic antigen stimulation. At the end of the two-week period, CAR-T cells were harvested, counted, and assessed for exhaustion marker expression via flow cytometry (PD-1, LAG-3, TIM-3). For functional re-challenge, the recovered CAR-T cells were re-stimulated with freshly thawed HepG2-FAP cells at an E: T ratio of 1:1 for 24 h. Supernatants were then collected to measure cytokine secretion (IL-2, TNF-α, and IFN-γ) using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). In parallel, cytotoxicity was evaluated by co-incubating the recovered CAR-T cells with CFSE-labeled HepG2-FAP cells at E: T ratios of 1:1, 5:1, and 10:1 for 4–6 h. Specific lysis was determined by flow cytometric quantification of propidium iodide (PI) or 7-AAD positive cells among the target cell population. All experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated at least three times independently.

Study design and patient population

We analyzed 1 patient who received FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T therapy at Xiehe Medical College of HUST between May 2023 and Jun 2024, all of whom had failed multiple lines of therapy and with high FAP expression. According to the 2021 WHO classification, the patient was diagnosed with grade IV glioma (glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype) based on histopathology and molecular markers. Prior standard-of-care treatments included surgical resection followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy with temozolomide, but the patient had progressed on these therapies.

The study regimen was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiehe Medical College of HUST (Ethics Approval Number：202001011), and all patients were informed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration in writing. This Phase I trial of FAP/CTLA-4-DMN CAR-T in patient with the primary objectives of assessing feasibility of manufacturing, safety and tolerability, Assessment of clinical activity is the secondary objective. Patients were eligible for enrollment if they had a pathologically confirmed diagnosis of patients with FAP-expressing gliomahad completed standard radiotherapy and were not receiving corticosteroid therapy.

CAR T-cell administration

All previous treatments were discontinued at least 4 weeks before intrathecal CAR-T cell infusion. No corticosteroids were administered before or during the entire CAR-T cell treatment period. Lymphodepleting chemotherapy (cyclophosphamide 500 mg/m2/day andfludarabine25mg/m2/day ondays-4,−3,−2) was administered followed by IV CAR T-cell infusion on Day 0. Patients were monitored closely, including routine ICP measurements, in the patient setting through toxicity resolution. For patients receiving infusions, CAR-T-cells were infused ICV via the Ommaya. The patient was treated with CAR-T cells once a week over a period of three weeks, with a second round of CAR-T infusion after a one-month break.

Clinical response assessment

Tumor response was evaluated using RANO criteria for gliomas. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed at baseline and at 8 weeks post infusion; the best overall response was stable disease (SD), maintained for a documented duration, and no pseudoprogression was observed.

Adverse events were graded according to CTCAE v5.0. Importantly, no treatment-related adverse events were observed throughout the follow-up period. Specifically, there was no cytokine release syndrome (CRS), no immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), no dose-limiting toxicity, and no grade ≥ 3 laboratory abnormalities. The study regimen was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiehe Medical College of HUST, and the patient was informed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration in writing.

Cytotoxicity assays

For cytotoxicity assays, CAR-T were incubated with stained by PKH26 and 7-AAD target cells at indicated E/T ratios(1:1, 5:1, 10:1)18 h, the target cells with double positive PKH26 + 7-AAD + were tested with Flow cytometry to evaluate the killing rate of effect cells. The killing rate was calculated by the following equation: [(Experimental apoptotic rate-Spontaneous apoptotic rate)/(100-Spontaneous apoptotic rate)]x100.

ELISA Assay and ELISPOT assay

We collected the supernatant from CTLA-4 nanobody-secreting CAR-T cells and used it in an in vitro ligand–receptor binding blockade assay (ELISA-based). Recombinant human CTLA-4-Fc was coated onto ELISA plates, and biotinylated CD80 was added in the presence of serially diluted test supernatants. As a positive control, a commercial anti-CTLA-4 blocking antibody (ipilimumab) was tested at eight serial dilutions (1:10, 1:100, 1:200, 1:300, 1:400, 1:500, 1:600, and 1:700). Supernatant from control CAR-T cells (non-secreting nanobody) was used as a negative control. Binding was detected with HRP-streptavidin.

For detecting IFN-γ, IL-2, TNF-a, supernatant was collected from CAR-T cell and target cell 16 h co-culture at 1:1 ratio. The detection was performed with IFN-γ, IL-2, TNF-a, ELISA kit (Dakewe, Shenzhen, China) as the introduction indicated.

To quantify the splenocytes capable of producing interferon-γ, suspensions of splenocyte harvested from each group of mice were seeded in 96-well plates (1 × 105 cells per well) (Dakewe, Shenzhen, China) and stimulated with TRP2 peptide (2 µg/well) at 37 °C for 16 h. Then, the IFN-γ positive cells were detected using an ELISPOT assay kit (Dakewe Co., Shenzhen, China), imaged under a CTL Immunospot analyzer (CTL, Shaker Height, OH, USA), and analyzed with Immunospot 5.0 Pro software (CTL).

Flow cytometry and antibodies

Staining of surface markers was performed in DPBS with 1% FCS using the antibody concentration recommended by the supplier. All populations analyzed were devoid of dead cells based on propidium iodide negativity and of doublets based on FSC-A FSC-W ratios. The following anti-human monoclonal antibodies were used: PE-conjugated-CD69(eBioscience, Merelbeke, Belgium), PE-conjugated-CD25(BDBiosciences), FITC-conjugated-CD45RA (BD Biosciences), PE-conjugated-CCR7 (BD Biosciences), PE-conjugated-LAG-3 (BD Biosciences), PE-conjugated-TIM-3(BD Biosciences),PE-conjugate-PD-1 (BD Biosciences).

HepG2 expressing FAP, HepG2 or U87 both were coated in 24-well plates (NUNC, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and mouse anti human FAP antibody was added in different dilutions. After incubation for 30 min at 4◦C, cells were Labelled with goat anti-mouse-PE (BD Biosciences, Erebodegem, Belgium) for 30 min at 4℃. Cells were analyzed by flowcytometry after a final wash step.

Xenogeneic mouse models

Female 6 to 8 week-old NOD/SCID mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories at Beijing. All mice were housed in the Animal Core Facility at the GUANGXI Medical University. All mouse experiments were performed in accordance with Guangxi Medical Universityy and Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines and were approved by the Guangxi Medical University.(Ethics Approval Number:2022-0553-01). For Xenogeneic mouse models, Tumor cells(HepG2-FAP)were injected intravenously into 8 to 10 week-old female NOD/SCID mice. Two weeks later, mice were infused intravenously with CAR T cells. Multiplex Immunofluorescence Staining

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed on FFPE tumor Sect. (4 μm) obtained from patients before and after CAR-T therapy using a TSA-based cyclic staining platform. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval, and blocked with serum. Sequential staining rounds were conducted using antibodies against immune and stromal markers, including CD3, CD56, CD14, CD19, CD11c, FAP. Each round consisted of incubation with primary antibody, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, and TSA-conjugated fluorophores (Opal system), followed by microwave stripping to remove antibody complexes. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Multispectral imaging was performed using the Vectra Polaris system, and images were analyzed with inForm software for cell segmentation, phenotype identification, and spatial distribution analysis to evaluate CAR-T-induced remodeling of the tumor microenvironment.

Bulk RNA sequencing of cerebrospinal fluid cells

Total RNA was extracted from cerebrospinal fluid cells collected from patients before and after CAR-T therapy using the RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen). RNA-seq library preparation, sequencing, and data analysis were performed by LC-Bio Technology (Hangzhou, China). Briefly, libraries were constructed using the SMART-Seq Stranded Kit (Takara) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired-end 150 bp reads. Raw data were processed using standard pipelines for adapter trimming, alignment to the human reference genome (GRCh38), and gene quantification. Differential expression analysis between pre- and post-treatment samples was performed, with significantly changed genes defined as |log2 fold change| > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis was conducted to identify treatment-altered biological pathways, with a particular focus on immune-related gene signatures.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

On day 18 after Tumor cells inoculation, mice tumors were excised, mounted in optimum cutting temperature (OCT) compound, frozen at −20℃, cut into 4-µm sections using a cryostat, and mounted onto gelatin-coated glass slides. After routine fixation and rehydration, sections were incubated with CD3 antibody and FAP antibody (BD Biosciences) at 4 °C overnight. After washing in PBS, the slides were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody for 30 min. Subsequently, slides were mounted with coverslips and sealed using Neutral balsam. Apoptosis was evaluated by TUNEL assay using an in situ cell-death fluorescein detection kit (Roche, Madison, WI, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were captured using an optical microscope (Tokyo, Japan) and signals were analyzed from five random fields within each section.

Statistical analysis

Flow cytometry data were analyzed and processed using FlowJo software. Experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way ANOVA was used for comparisons among multiple groups. Data are presented as Mean ± SD. Mouse survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and the log-rank test was used for statistical comparison. In the results description, asterisks indicate the significance of differences between groups (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001; ns, not significant).
