Therapy With Doxycycline and Hydroxychloroquine Enables Surgical Excision of Ulcerated Cutaneous Granulomas in a Patient With Ataxia Telangiectasia
Authors: Luis Murguía‐Favela, Sofia Maruschak‐Love, Robertson A. Harrop, Marie‐Anne Brundler, Kyle Kurek, Kevin Fonseca, Michele L. Ramien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe rubella virus vaccine strain can cause granulomas in patients with inborn errors of immunity that may evolve into chronic, recalcitrant ulcers. This report details treatment of ulcerated rubella granulomas in a patient with ataxia telangiectasia with doxycycline and hydroxychloroquine resulting in partial ulcer healing within 4 weeks, which in turn enabled surgical excision. Though this treatment did not cure or clear the rubella virus, that is known to persist in patients with inborn errors of immunity like ataxia telangiectasia, it enabled durable local lesion control of typically chronic, highly morbid ulcers. Histopathology of skin and bone lesions, the latter not previously described in the literature, is included.

Introduction

Rubella virus vaccine strain (Wistar RA27/3) has a recognized potential to establish chronic latent infection in skin macrophages resulting in the development of granulomas (rubella granulomas) in some vaccinated patients with inborn errors of immunity (IEI) [1]. Ataxia telangiectasia (AT) is the IEI most commonly associated with rubella granulomas in pediatric patients [2]. The latency between vaccination with live‐attenuated MMR vaccine and onset of rubella granulomas is highly variable, from 3 weeks to decades, with onset generally being earlier in patients with DNA repair disorders such as AT [2, 3]. Rubella granulomas may be chronic and associated with recalcitrant cutaneous ulceration with concomitant bacteremia and sepsis with these ulcers as a portal of entry. Currently, there are no evidence‐based therapies for treatment of rubella virus‐induced cutaneous granulomas in IEIs. Topical and systemic corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulins, nitazoxanide, ribavirin, sirolimus, anti‐tumor necrosis factor agents, and interferon‐alpha have been used as treatments with inconsistent results [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. This report focuses on the clinical application of commonly used anti‐granulomatous dermatological medical therapies for a patient with intractable ulcerated rubella granulomas to achieve durable local lesion control and enable resection.

Case Report

A 10‐year‐old boy with AT presented with chronic cutaneous granulomas that first appeared at 2 years of age [9]. The primary lesions were located above the right elbow and on the lateral aspect of the left knee extending to the distal thigh. The granulomas demonstrated seasonal fluctuations, with recurrent painful ulcerations developing in the winter months, and then partially resolving over 8–12 weeks with intensive wound care. Despite intermittent partial healing of his ulcers, the granulomatous plaques persisted year‐round, and with each passing year, the areas involved progressively enlarged and penetrated deeper into the subcutaneous tissue, making surgical resection technically challenging. Histopathology showed granulomatous inflammation (Figure 1) and biopsies from both lesions were positive for rubella virus, genotype 1a, which by sequencing was determined to be closely related to the Wistar RA27/3 vaccine strain (reported as the first Canadian case of rubella granulomas in a patient with AT [10]). Rubella PCR testing and WHO standardized genotyping was performed on a skin biopsy from a granuloma at the Public Health Agency of Canada's National Microbiology Laboratory, a WHO‐accredited Regional Reference laboratory for rubella. As previously reported, tissue cultures for bacteria, deep fungi, and mycobacteria were negative [9]. The patient experienced recurrent secondary bacterial infections with Staphylococcus aureus and Group A Streptococcus requiring multiple courses of oral and intravenous antibiotics.

Lesion excised from the lateral aspect of the left foot shows nodular granulomatous inflammation in dermis and subcutis (H&E, ×2).

For his immunodeficiency, the patient was on subcutaneous immunoglobulin replacement therapy since diagnosis at 20 months of age. His immune phenotype prior to replacement showed undetectable IgA levels, low IgG, and normal IgM, with adequate specific antibody responses to protein antigens but reduced specific antibody responses to polysaccharide antigens. Lymphocyte immunophenotyping by flow cytometry has shown T‐cell and B‐cell lymphopenia with normal NK‐cell numbers. His CD4+ T cell count has always been below 300 × 109cells/L and with multiple restrictions in T cell‐receptor V‐beta specificities (therefore has been on Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis with trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole since diagnosis). Prior to his diagnosis, he received a single dose of measles‐mumps‐rubella‐varicella vaccine at the age of 12 months, per the Canadian immunization schedule for children. All further live vaccines have been deferred.

Treatments attempted for his skin lesions had included topical corticosteroids, topical tacrolimus, and a wide array of specialized dressings, all of which were unsuccessful at achieving complete wound healing.

During a recurrence of ulcerations on the arm and leg (Figure 2), and with the supposition that this granulomatous process could be treated like other idiopathic granulomas, the patient was initiated on a combination of oral doxycycline 50 mg BID (4 mg/kg/day divided twice daily) and hydroxychloroquine 100 mg once daily (4 mg/kg/day). At the time of treatment initiation, both ulcerations measured approximately 3 cm in diameter and at least 0.5 cm in depth. Within 4 weeks, both lesions demonstrated complete re‐epithelialization with resolution of visible inflammation (Figure 3). After 3 months, surgical excision of the residual lesions was performed, with the resulting defects closed directly without the need for skin grafting. The wounds healed completely. Doxycycline‐hydroxychloroquine therapy was continued for one additional month postoperatively (total treatment course was 4 months) to suppress granulomatous inflammation during the healing phase. No adverse effects were observed during or after treatment. Previous arranged ophthalmology examination shortly after completing his course of combination therapy did not find signs of ocular toxicity. At the 7‐month follow‐up, the affected sites remained free of ulceration and granuloma recurrence (Figure 4). He is now 5 years out from surgical excision and has not had recurrences at those surgical sites.

This combination therapy has not prevented the appearance of granulomas at other sites nor did it treat the replicating vaccine‐strain rubella virus found in the lesions. However, when new lesions have appeared elsewhere (cheek, medial aspect of left ankle and left foot), initiating therapy has again prevented their growth and ulceration and allowed for early surgical excision, including of a granuloma that extended into a metatarsal bone (Figures 5 and 6). Molecular studies of all additional excised lesions confirmed the persistent presence of vaccine‐strain genotype 1a rubella virus. In addition, deeper genotype analyses of the virus retrieved from all historical tissue samples from our patient showed divergence in sequence evolution across tissues and time.

Discussion

The immunomodulatory, non‐antimicrobial properties of tetracyclines have been shown to include inhibition of matrix metalloproteinases that break down connective tissue proteins, downregulation of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐α and IL‐1β, inhibition of leukocyte chemotaxis, and inhibition of granuloma formation in other dermatologic conditions [11, 12]. Likewise, hydroxychloroquine has been shown to reduce toll‐like receptor signaling resulting in decreased activation of dendritic cells and reduction of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as interferons [13]. Clinically, the combination of doxycycline and hydroxychloroquine is recommended for idiopathic granulomatous diseases like granuloma annulare and sarcoidosis [14]. This case illustrates how dermatologic principles, in this case anti‐granulomatous therapy, can be repurposed to help etiologically distinct but morphologically and pathologically similar clinical challenges. Nitazoxanide, an antimicrobial therapy previously reported for this use with limited success [5, 15], is not available in Canada. Complete surgical excision of rubella granulomas is generally curative; however, this may be challenging in the case of large granulomas with chronic ulceration. The observed response in this case suggests that the anti‐granulomatous properties of doxycycline and hydroxychloroquine can be leveraged in a neo‐adjuvant (pre‐surgical) manner in IEI patients to reduce ulceration and overall size of rubella granulomas. When initiated early, this treatment may also limit enlargement and ulceration in new granulomatous lesions, increasing the likelihood of complete surgical excision.
