Ultra-high resolution CT angiography for the assessment of intracranial stents and flow diverters using photon counting detector CT
Authors: Riccardo Ludovichetti, Dunja Gorup, Mikos Krepuska, Sebastian Winklhofer, Patrick Thurner, Jawid Madjidyar, Thomas Flohr, Marco Piccirelli, Lars Michels, Hatem Alkadhi, Victor Mergen, Zsolt Kulcsar, Tilman Schubert
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe patency of intracranial stents may not be reliably assessed with either CT angiography or MR angiography due to imaging artifacts. We investigated the potential of ultra-high resolution CT angiography using a photon counting detector (PCD) CT to address this limitation by optimizing scanning and reconstruction parameters.MethodsA phantom with different flow diverters was used to optimize PCD-CT reconstruction parameters, followed by imaging of 14 patients with intracranial stents using PCD-CT. Images were reconstructed using three kernels based on the phantom results (Hv56, Hv64, and Hv72; Hv=head vascular) and one kernel to virtually match the resolution of standard CT angiography (Hv40). Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) measurements were calculated. Subjective image quality and diagnostic confidence (DC) were assessed using a five point visual grading scale (5=best, 1=worst) and a three point grading scale (1=best, 3=worst), respectively, by two independent neuroradiologists.ResultsPhantom images demonstrated the highest image quality across dose levels for 0.2 mm reconstructions with Hv56 (4.5), Hv64 (5), and Hv72 (5). In patient images, SNR and CNR decreased significantly with increasing kernel sharpness compared with control parameters. All reconstructions showed significantly higher image quality and DC compared with the control reconstruction with Hv40 kernel (P<0.001), with both image quality and DC being highest with Hv64 (0.2 mm) and Hv72 (0.2 mm) reconstructions.ConclusionUltra-high resolution PDC-CT angiography provides excellent visualization of intracranial stents, with optimal reconstructions using the Hv64 and the Hv72 kernels at 0.2 mm.RegistrationBASEC 2021-00343.Keywords: CT Angiography, Flow Diverter, Stent

Introduction

Patients with intracranial flow diverters (FDs) and non-flow diverting stents (NFDs) require imaging follow-up to assess stent patency and to rule out in-stent stenosis, with DSA currently the reference standard.1 2 Despite its efficacy, DSA, being an invasive technique, is associated with a low but certain risk of periprocedural complications.3 Consequently, MR angiography (MRA) constitutes the preferred non-invasive imaging modality4 for follow-up of endovascularly treated intracranial aneurysms. However, assessment of stent patency is often unreliable due to radiofrequency shielding effects and susceptibility artifacts.1 5

CT angiography (CTA) has been gaining popularity as an alternative imaging method for follow-up evaluations because it has the advantage of being non-invasive6 and more efficient in terms of time and costs,7 and involves lower radiation exposure than DSA.8 However, when imaging intracranial stents with conventional energy integrating detector (EID) CT, stent related beam hardening artifacts may hinder accurate assessment of the in-stent lumen and adjacent aneurysms.9 In addition, blooming artifacts can cause visual enlargement of highly attenuating objects, leading to an underestimation of the size of the vascular lumen.10,12 These shortcomings hamper the identification of stent related complications, such as in-stent stenosis.13 Enhancing the spatial resolution of CT scans has emerged as a promising strategy to counteract these constraints by diminishing the impact of blooming artifacts from strongly attenuating structures. However, spatial resolution is inherently limited by the capabilities of conventional detector technology.14

The emergence of the first clinical dual source photon counting detector (PCD) CT signifies a revolutionary departure in this context. PCD-CT distinguishes itself from EID-CT by the direct conversion of incident X-ray photons into electrical signals within a semiconductor substrate. In a PCD, the individual detector elements are defined and separated from each other by a strong electric field, making additional separation layers between them, as required for EIDs, unnecessary. As a result, PCD-CT enables high spatial resolution imaging with a maximum in-plane resolution of 0.11×0.11 mm2 and a maximum through plane resolution of 0.16 mm1015,18 at very good radiation dose efficiency. Ultra-high resolution (UHR) images of PCD-CT already demonstrated excellent in-stent lumen visualization of coronary stents, effectively mitigating beam hardening artifacts.19 In light of this, UHR CTA of an intracranial stent using PCD-CT holds great potential for superior visualization of the stent and detection of in-stent stenosis or suboptimal stent opening, all while optimizing dose efficiency.1 2

The purpose of this study was to assess the potential of UHR PCD-CTA in the evaluation of intracranial stents and FDs. The study had two parts: (1) a phantom study to evaluate the quality of PCD-CT images acquired using the UHR mode, with a specific focus on determining the optimal reconstruction parameters; and (2) a patient study to examine visualization of NFDs and FDs on intracranial UHR PCD-CTA reconstructed with the optimal reconstruction parameters determined in the phantom study.

Material and methods

Phantom study

Phantom design, scan acquisition, and image reconstruction

A vascular phantom (Biomodex, Paris, France) with two different FDs implanted (Pipeline Embolization Device, Medtronic, Dublin, Ireland, and Evolve, Stryker Neurovascular, Fremont, California, USA) was placed into a skull phantom, which subsequently was submerged in a water tank. The two FDs are commonly used at our department. The skull phantom is designed to assess the attenuation correction for positron emission tomography scans and mimics the attenuation of the bony skull.20 The vascular phantom was filled with a mixture of contrast agent and water to achieve an attenuation of 550 HU on polychromatic images from EID-CT, matching the average intravascular attenuation achieved in CTA. This phantom set-up served to assess the sharpness and subjective image quality of the intracranial stent depending on the reconstruction parameters.

The vascular phantom was scanned using both a third generation dual source EID-CT (SOMATOM X.cite; Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany) and a first generation, full body, dual source PCD-CT (NAEOTOM Alpha; Siemens Healthineers). EID-CT scans were acquired with a collimation of 64×0.6 mm at a rotation time of 0.3 s and a tube voltage of 120 kV. UHR scans with the PCD-CT were acquired with a collimation of 120×0.2 mm at a rotation time of 0.5 s and a tube voltage of 120 kV. To achieve three distinct dose levels, specified by the volume CT dose index (CTDIvol) of 10 mGy, 15 mGy, and 20 mGy, adjustments to the tube current–time product (mAs) were made manually.

Images were reconstructed using head vascular kernels with two different levels for the EID-CT scans (Hv40 and Hv56; Hv=head vascular), and six different sharpness levels for the UHR PCD-CT scans (Hv40, Hv56, Hv64, Hv72, Hv80, and Hv89). For EID-CT, images were reconstructed with the minimal slice thickness of 0.6 mm, a matrix of 512×512, a field of view of 150×150 mm, and applying advanced model iterative reconstruction (ADMIRE) at a strength level of 3. EID-CT images served as a reference. For PCD-CT, images were reconstructed with a slice thickness of 0.2 mm for all sharpness levels. Additionally, PCD-CT scans were reconstructed with a slice thickness of 0.6 mm using the Hv40 kernel to match the EID-CT reconstructions. PCD-CT images were reconstructed applying quantum iterative reconstruction (QIR) at a strength level of 4. Matrix sizes were adjusted to the resolution properties of the kernels (ie, 512×512 pixels, or 1024×1024 pixels with a constant field of view of 150×150 mm). The selection of these settings was based on the approximate measure of the maximum spatial frequency achieved by the kernel, defined as the frequency at which the modulation transfer function is 10% of its starting value at ρ=0 Lp/cm (ρ10). The resolution parameters were adjusted so that the maximum image resolution (ρmax) exceeded the respective kernel’s ρ10 reported by the PCD-CT manufacturer, except for the Hv89 kernel whose ρ10 was 38.58 Lp/cm, compared with the ρmax of 34.13 Lp/cm (online supplemental table 1).

Also, modulation transfer functions were analyzed using the IndoQCT v22a.105 software (Diponegoro University, Indonesia)21 and compared with the ρ50% and ρ10% values provided by the PCD-CT manufacturer. This reconstruction protocol is easily reproducible by any center with the same PCD-CT.

Sharpness analysis

Attenuation profiles were automatically measured along lines defined perpendicularly to the ED’s surface. Briefly, segmented EDs were skeletonized and each point of the skeleton was connected to the point on the circle in the plane perpendicular to the skeleton, to detect multiple attenuation profiles across the ED (as shown in figure 1). Attenuation courses were fitted with single or double sigmoid functions using the computing platform MATLAB (V.R2022b) (Mathworks, Natick, Massachusetts, USA), as previously described by Yang et al.22 The slope of the function determined the image sharpness, with a greater slope meaning a higher sharpness.

The images show the progression of kernel sharpness from (A) to (F): (A) Hv40, (B) Hv56, (C) Hv64, (D) Hv72, (E) Hv80, and (F) Hv89. Hv, head vascular.

Assessment of the image quality

The image quality of the reconstructions from EID-CT scans and UHR PCD-CT scans was evaluated by two independent readers. Both readers were board certified neuroradiologists with 17 years (TS) and 6 years (MK) of experience, respectively. Overall, image quality was assessed using a five point visual grading scale: 5=excellent visualization of the in-stent lumen and stent with no perceivable blooming and precise visualization of the adjacent vessel lumen, 4=good and above average stent and vessel lumen visualization, 3=acceptable stent and vessel lumen visualization, 2=suboptimal stent and vessel lumen visualization, and 1=unacceptable stent and vessel lumen visualization.

Patient study

This single center retrospective study was conducted at a tertiary academic medical center and received institutional review board and local ethics committee approval (BASEC 2021–00343). All patients provided written informed consent to allow the inclusion of their anonymized data in retrospective analyses. We used the STARD checklist when writing our report.23

Between September 2022 and July 2023, 14 patients (six women, eight men; mean age 65±15 years) with intracranial stents who underwent PCD-CT at our institution were included. Inclusion criteria were patients with intracranial stents placed at our institution, without coils, aged ≥18 years, who provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included contraindications to PCD-CT, such as severe claustrophobia or inability to remain still, severe comorbid conditions that precluded safe imaging, failure to provide written informed consent, and known allergies to contrast agents used in PCD-CT. Patients formed a consecutive series. Among them, nine patients (64%) underwent treatment with 10 intracranial NFD stents following percutaneous transluminal angioplasty for symptomatic arteriosclerotic stenotic lesions, while the remaining five patients (36%) received treatment for intracranial aneurysms, with six FD implanted.

Regarding NFD stent placement, four stents were deployed during endovascular thrombectomy, two in emergent procedures without previous thrombectomy, and four in planned procedures. The stents used were the Neuroform Atlas (Stryker Neurovascular) in four cases, Enterprise 2 (Cerenovus, Irvine, California, USA) in three cases, Onyx Resolute (Medtronic) in two cases, and LVIS EVO (Microvention, Aliso Viejo, California, USA) in one case.

Regarding FDs, four patients received FDs in planned procedures and one patient in an emergent setting. One patient had two FDs in different vessels implanted, which were analyzed as separate samples. Two types of FDs were used: three pipeline embolization devices (Medtronic) in three cases, and three Surpass Evolve devices (Stryker Neurovascular) in two cases.

Scan acquisition and image reconstruction

The patient scan protocol with the PCD-CT comprised a non-enhanced head scan followed by UHR intracranial angiography. Intracranial angiography was initiated after injection of 60 mL of contrast material (Iopromide, Ultravist 370 mg iodine/mL; Bayer Healthcare, Berlin, Germany) with a power injector at a weight based flow rate of 3.3–4.4 mL/s. A saline chaser of 30 mL was administered after the contrast material injection. Based on the visual results of the previous phantom scans, images of the UHR intracranial angiography were reconstructed using three vascular kernels (Hv56, Hv64, and Hv72, respectively) with a slice thickness of 0.2 mm applying QIR at a strength level of 4.

In addition, all angiographic scans were reconstructed using the conventional Hv40 kernel with a slice thickness of 0.6 mm applying QIR at a strength level of 4. These images served as a reference to match the reconstruction of the EID-CT scans. The volume CT dose index (CTDIvol) of the patient scans ranged from 12 mGy to 17.4 mGy.

Quantitative measurements

Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) of PCD-CTA in patients were evaluated by manual measurements conducted by a board certified radiologist (RL) with 5 years' experience in neuroradiology. To obtain these metrics, single regions of interest (ROIs) were positioned in the stent lumen and the right frontal horn, which contains clear cerebrospinal fluid. These ROIs were selected on the same image slice and made as large as possible while carefully avoiding neighboring structures. To ensure consistency, ROIs were then copied to all image reconstructions.

SNR and CNR were calculated as follows:

SNR = HUED / SDCSF;

CNR = (HUED− HUCSF) / SDCSF;

HUED and SDED refer to the attenuation and SD of attenuation in the ROI placed in the stent, respectively. HUCSF refers to the attenuation of the ROI placed in the brain ventricle.

Qualitative analysis

Image quality analysis of PCD-CTA was carried out for each patient by the same two independent readers as in the phantom study, applying the same five point visual grading scale used for the vascular phantom. Diagnostic confidence was rated on a three point scale: complete (1), moderate (2), or no diagnostic confidence (3). In addition, each rater independently assessed the grade of the underlying in-stent stenosis as follows: no stenosis (1); 1–50% (2); 50–99% (3); or as a complete stent lumen occlusion (4). In the case of different results, both readers reached a consensus. Stenoses were assessed on Hv72 0.2 mm reconstructions. The assessment was conducted using two-dimensional images as well as multiplanar reconstructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software (V.27) (IBM, released 2020. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, V. 27.0, Armonk, New York, USA: IBM). Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normal distribution of continuous variables. A mixed ANOVA for repeated measurements was applied to data that followed a normal distribution, while the Kruskal–Wallis H test and Friedman test were used for non-normally distributed data to assess quantitative differences between the reconstructions.

A Tukey post hoc test was performed when equal variances were assumed, and the Games–Howell post hoc test was used if equal variances were not assumed, with Bonferroni adjustment of the CI. Quantitative variables are expressed as mean±SD when normally distributed and as median (quartiles) when non-normally distributed. We used multiple regression analysis to evaluate the influence of various factors on sharpness, identifying the factor with the strongest contribution based on the highest standardized regression coefficient.

Inter-reader agreement was assessed using Krippendorf’s alpha (α) using the following interpretation of the alpha value: (0.0, 0.2) slight agreement, (0.2, 0.4) fair agreement, (0.4, 0.6) moderate agreement, (0.6, 0.8) substantial agreement, and (0.8, 1.0) near perfect agreement.24

Results

Phantom measurements

Sharpness analysis

Multiple regression analysis of sharpness on phantom images revealed that the reconstruction kernels had the strongest influence on the measured slope of the fitted sigmoid curve (r2=0.305, P<0.001, with standardized coefficients beta for kernel, slice thickness, and level 0.496,–0.094, and −0.011, respectively), as shown in online supplemental figure 1. The higher the kernel value, the narrower the curve, yielding higher sharpness values (figure 1).

Slice thickness and radiation dose output both had a weaker impact on sharpness, with P values of 0.036 and 0.764, respectively (online supplemental figure 2). Notably, when the stent axis aligns with the scanning direction, slice thickness has only a minor effect; nevertheless, if the stent is rotated by 90°, slice thickness affects sharpness.

Image quality

Median image quality of the EID-CT across the three dose levels in both FDs was 3 (range 2.75–3) for both Hv40 and Hv56. For PCD-CT, median image quality across dose levels for 0.2 mm reconstructions was 3 (2.75–3, Hv40), 4.5 (4.5–4.75, Hv56), 5 (5–5, Hv64), 5.0 (4.75–5, Hv72), 4.5 (4.25–4.5, Hv80), and 4 (3.75–4, Hv89). Results are illustrated in online supplemental table 2. The two independent readers achieved acceptable inter-rater reliability of α=0.64. No stenosis analysis was performed in the phantom.

Patient study

No adverse events occurred during the performance of UHR PCD-CT intracranial angiography.

Discussion

In this study, we optimized reconstruction parameters for PCD-CTA for intracranial stents in a phantom, and applied the results to a cohort of patients. These were treated with intracranial stents for intracranial atherosclerotic disease and aneurysms.

Our results showed that UHR PCD-CTA provided excellent visualization of the lumen of intracranial EDs, both in the phantom and patients. In the phantom, it outperformed standard EID-CT in subjective image quality, with the highest values achieved at Hv56, Hv64, and Hv72. In patients, the best image quality and the highest diagnostic confidence were achieved with high sharpness kernels (Hv64 and Hv72) with a 0.2 mm slice thickness. Reference images reconstructed with the Hv40 kernel and a 0.6 mm slice thickness, despite achieving the highest SNR and CNR, had the lowest subjective image quality and diagnostic confidence. Similar findings were reported in a recent study by Tóth et al,25 who investigated different levels of reconstruction kernel sharpness in the evaluation of intracranial aneurysms in 10 patients. They concluded that despite the increase in image noise and subsequent reduction in SNR and CNR, readers preferred a high sharpness kernel. This is in line with a previous study on EID-CT by O’Meara et al,25 26 who demonstrated that sharper kernels improved the precision of anatomical visualization of cerebral aneurysms compared with smoother kernels, approaching the quality of three-dimensional rotational angiography. This emphasizes that achieving clear visualization of the stent lumen through a sharp reconstruction kernel is more important than achieving high SNR and CNR, as it enables better delineation of the vicinity of the metallic structure without blurring. PCD-CT seems ideal to implement the high sharpness kernels with its inherent low image noise.

Intracranial stents that are used to treat intracranial steno-occlusive arteriosclerotic disease have different imaging characteristics compared with FDs, used for the treatment of intracranial aneurysms. Most stents have radiopaque proximal and distal markers with a much less opaque intermediate part, consisting of thin nitinol struts. In contrast, FDs used in this study consisted of a high density braided mesh of 46–64 cobalt–chromium wires without proximal and distal markers. Due to these characteristics, different imaging challenges were present for stents and FDs. The main problem in imaging intracranial stents arises from the assessment of the vessel adjacent to the radiopaque proximal and distal markers whereas in FDs, the main difficulty is the visualization of the area close to the high density mesh to exclude substantial intimal hyperplasia or clot formation. With PCD-CT, visualization of the lumen of both stents and FDs was excellent, and even minimal intimal hyperplasia and incomplete wall apposition could be detected. Despite variations in materials and composition between stents and FDs, our investigation revealed only minimal differences in the optimal reconstruction kernels for achieving the best image quality, with Hv64 and Hv72 offering the best results. These kernels also resulted in the highest diagnostic confidence for both stents and FDs. We chose the minimal slice thickness of 0.2 mm for all kernels, because we found an effect of slice thickness on sharpness in the phantom experiment, although this was not statistically significant.

Clear visualization of the intra-device lumen is crucial in clinical practice, particularly in the follow-up of both FDs and stents. A definite knowledge of the in-stent conditions is mandatory to switch from dual antiplatelet to single antiplatelet therapy as well as to discontinue antiplatelet therapy after FD treatment.27 This is especially important, as unnecessary long treatment with antithrombotic medication increases the bleeding risk, especially prolonged intervals of dual antiplatelet therapy.28 So far, gathering definite information about the intra-device lumen of intracranial ED was only possible with DSA through three-dimensional flat detector CT acquisitions with intra-arterial contrast administration being especially useful to gather information about intimal hyperplasia, stent deformation, and in-stent stenosis.29 Our results indicated that with the advent of PCD-CT, there might be a non-invasive tool available that allows excellent visualization of the in-stent lumen and may provide sufficient information to guide antithrombotic therapy, overcoming the limitation of EID-CT to depict changes in intraluminal devices in very small vessels. An example of multimodal imaging of a basilar tip aneurysm treated with FD is given in online supplemental figure 3.

Two recent studies investigated the intracranial vasculature with PCD-CTA compared with traditional CTA with EID-CT. Michael et al30 investigated the quality of polyenergetic and virtual monoenergetic reconstructions of PCD-CTA of the head and neck. When compared with older data from dual layer CT literature, it appears that similar image quality can be obtained in head and neck CTA performed with PCD-CT in terms of quantitative measures, potentially with reduced contrast agent volume and lower radiation exposure.30 A study by Symons et al14 assessed the image quality of a CT system using a spectral PCD-CT for examining the primary arteries in the head and neck, in comparison with traditional single energy CT scans performed with EID-CT. The study found that PCD-CT images had significantly higher quality scores compared with EID images, with lower noise and fewer artifacts. Numerous in vitro and in vivo studies have studied the efficacy of PCD-CT systems in delivering precise and intricate depictions of metallic stent meshes. These investigations, predominantly concentrated on coronary stents, consistently report heightened measurement accuracy when compared with conventional EID-CTs.1031,33 Furthermore, the ultra-high resolution capabilities inherent in PCD-CT systems confer significant advantages in the visualization of both vessel lumens and plaque formations. For comparison, in coronary artery imaging, the reduction of blooming artifacts contributes to heightened clarity in vessel lumen depiction and facilitates the improved delineation of fibrotic and lipid-rich plaque constituents.16 34 In alignment with these findings, our study emphasizes the potential of PCD-CTA for the evaluation of intracranial vasculature.

This study had limitations, including a small sample size and the absence of a direct comparison with the reference standard, DSA, for intracranial vascular device assessment. Patients with diverse devices and conditions were included, beneficial for this proof-of-concept, but warranting further evaluation in larger studies. A limitation of the study was also that it tested only the highest strength of iterative reconstruction. While the use of iterative reconstruction is appropriate, determining the optimal combination of QIR would be beneficial. We emphasized the importance of initially focusing on characterizing image quality and identifying optimal reconstruction parameters in the appraisal of a novel methodology. Subsequent analyses addressing diagnostic accuracy and broader implications are more appropriate once these foundational aspects are adequately addressed.

In conclusion, our study showed that PCD-CT was an effective high resolution imaging modality for the assessment of intracranial stents and FDs. The best imaging quality in UHR mode was achieved with high sharpness kernels Hv64 and Hv72 and a 0.2 mm slice width. Minimal differences between both kernels were detected with Hv64 showing a slightly higher image quality for stents and Hv72 for FDs. However, this did not manifest in differences in diagnostic confidence.
