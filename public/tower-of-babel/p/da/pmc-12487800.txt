Macular Sublayer Thickness in Healthy Iranian Children: An Optical Coherence Tomography Study from the Population-Based Shiraz Pediatric Eye Study
Authors: Athar Zareei, Reza Razeghinejad, Mohammad Reza Talebnejad, Mohammad Reza Khalili, Masoumeh Sadat Masoumpour, Zahra Shayan, Hamideh Mahdaviazad, Maryam Keshtkar, Elham Mohammadi, Zahra Tajbakhsh, Maryam Shahmohammadi, M. Hossein Nowroozzadeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To establish normative values for macular sublayer thickness in healthy Iranian children using optical coherence tomography (OCT) and to assess the effects of age and gender.Methods:This study was part of the population-based Shiraz pediatric eye study. Of 2400 children aged 6–12 years invited, 480 were randomly selected for optical biometry and macular spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT) imaging. Finally, 431 OCT scans from children with medium axial length (AL; 21.5–26.5 mm) were analyzed. The OCT device automatically segmented seven retinal sublayers, and their thickness was measured across Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) subfields. Thickness in the central 1-mm subfield was assessed by gender and age groups (6–9 vs. 10–12 years), adjusted for AL. Regression analysis examined the impact of age, sex, and AL on retinal sublayer thickness. Only data from the right eye were used.Results:The mean age of participants was 9.12 ± 1.59 years (range, 6–12), with 254 (58.9%) being girls. The mean AL was 22.91 ± 0.71 mm, and the mean foveal thickness was 258 ± 8 µm. A normative database was created for the total retinal thickness and the seven retinal sublayers across the nine ETDRS subfields. Boys had longer globes (by approximately 0.4 mm; P < 0.001) and thicker foveae (by about 5 µm; P = 0.001) compared to girls. Among the seven sublayers studied, boys had a thicker ganglion cell complex layer (P = 0.014) and outer nuclear layer (ONL; P = 0.012), while girls had a thicker retinal pigment epithelium (RPE; P = 0.029). The inner nuclear layer and outer plexiform layer showed no significant differences (P = 0.075 and P = 0.810, respectively). The mean AL was 22.78 ± 0.68 mm in the 6–9 age group and 23.10 ± 0.72 mm in the 10–12 age group (P < 0.001). The older age group (10–12 years) exhibited thicker ONL (P = 0.009) and RPE (P = 0.002) layers compared to the younger group.Conclusions:This study provides normative data for macular sublayer thickness in Iranian children aged 6–12 years using Heidelberg SD-OCT. Boys had longer ALs and thicker maculae, while girls had a thicker RPE layer. Older children had longer globes and thicker retinas, mainly due to increased ONL and RPE thickness.

INTRODUCTION

The macula is a specialized area of the retina responsible for central vision and visual acuity, consisting of several distinct sublayers. Examining the characteristics of the macula and its sublayers in children provides valuable insights into visual development. This assessment can be performed using imaging techniques such as optical coherence tomography (OCT), which allows for noninvasive, high-resolution imaging of the retinal layers.12

Changes in the thickness or morphology of the macula in children may indicate various eye disorders. Some conditions predominantly affect the outer retinal layers (ORLs), such as macular dystrophy, while others primarily involve the inner layers, such as glaucoma.345 By monitoring spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT) changes in the macula and its sublayers over time, it is possible to detect and manage these conditions at the earliest stage, thereby preserving visual function in children.

Although most developmental changes in the retina occur within the first few years of life, certain aspects of foveal development may not reach adult levels until early adolescence.1 As a result, children may display different macular topographic characteristics compared to adults, making adult-based OCT normative references unsuitable for pediatric use.6

Given the importance of this information and the limited number of relevant studies in our region, our aim was to: (1) Establish a normative database for macular sublayer thickness maps using the Spectralis SD-OCT software (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) in healthy children, and (2) analyze the effects of age and gender on these parameters.

METHODS

The population-based Shiraz pediatric eye study,7 conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.REC.1394.S689). Informed consent was obtained from legal guardians. The study involved children aged 6–12 years from the four educational districts of Shiraz, with participant enrollment taking place between 2015 and 2016. A stratified random sampling method was used to select 400 participants from all districts.2 Of these, 2001 students (83.3%) participated in the study, slightly exceeding the expected proportion of 80% used in the sample size calculation.

During the initial clinical phase, parents were required to review and sign an informed consent form outlining the study’s primary objectives and examination procedures. Comprehensive data were collected on various factors, including age, gender, and socioeconomic status. Subsequently, optometric and ophthalmologic assessments were conducted at five different sites. Of the 2001 children who participated, 480 (one-fifth of the original sample size, as determined by sample size calculation) were randomly selected to undergo optical biometry and SD-OCT imaging.

Previous studies,89 have demonstrated a relationship between axial length (AL) and macular thickness, underscoring the importance of considering AL when interpreting OCT reports. Furthermore, in eyes that are either exceptionally short or long, measurement errors may occur in the lateral dimensions if the condition is not accurately defined for the device software.810 To ensure a reliable reference range, we included only children with medium AL (as defined by the Spectralis software), which comprised 90% (431/480) of the cases. The remaining 10% of cases, which involved very short or long AL, were excluded from the report due to limited sample size and the potential for measurement errors, which could affect the reliability and comparability of the reference ranges.

The study included Iranian children aged 6–12 years who met the following inclusion criteria: reliable IOLMaster and OCT images and an AL between 21.5 and 26.5 mm. Children with ALs outside this range, as well as those with corneal, retinal, or optic disc diseases, strabismus, or amblyopia, were excluded from the study.711 In addition, children with systemic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, autoimmune diseases, or genetic and metabolic disorders were also excluded.

AL was measured using an optical biometry device (IOLMaster 500; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany), while macular thickness parameters were assessed with the Spectralis OCT device (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). Trained examiners evaluated the accuracy of all measurements in accordance with the guidelines of the manufacturers.

To obtain a 30° volume scan of the macula for each eye, we recorded the first successful scan that achieved a quality score of at least 20.12 Images with significant artifacts or uncorrectable segmentation errors were excluded from the analysis. The thickness of each retinal layer within the nine Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) segments was calculated using the integrated Heyex software. All scans were performed with the software’s default “medium” AL adjustment (24.00 ± 2.50 mm).

The integrated Heyex software offers automated segmentation, allowing for efficient and reproducible measurements of macular sublayer thicknesses. It provides data for total retinal thickness as well as for the following sublayers in each ETDRS subfield: Retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), ganglion cell layer (GCL), inner plexiform layer (IPL), inner nuclear layer (INL), outer plexiform layer (OPL), outer nuclear layer (ONL), and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). The ganglion cell complex (GCC) was calculated as the sum of RNFL, GCL, and IPL. We categorized the data into three regions: The central circle (1 mm), the inner ring (comprising four subfields located 1–3 mm from the center), and the outer ring (comprising four subfields located 3–6 mm from the center).

According to the Heyex software, the inner retinal layer (IRL) is defined as the area between the internal limiting membrane and the external limiting membrane, while the ORL is defined as extending from the external limiting membrane to the RPE.

Statistical analysis

Means and standard deviations (SDs) of thickness values were recorded for each sublayer in every ETDRS subfield. Subsequently, the means and SDs of thickness values for the inner and outer rings were calculated. To evaluate the effect of age, sex, and AL on OCT values across the center, inner ring, and outer ring subfields, we performed a multiple regression analysis. The analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) test was used to calculate the estimated marginal means (EMMs) for macular sublayers in different groups (boys vs. girls and ages 6–9 vs. 10–12 years), adjusting for AL.

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26; SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with a Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons. Due to the very high correlation between measurements of the fellow eyes (intraclass correlation coefficient > 0.95 for all measurements), only data from the right eyes were included in the report.

RESULTS

The average age of the 431 participants was 9.12 ± 1.59 years (range, 6–12 years), with 254 (58.9%) being girls. The participants had an average spherical equivalent of refraction of 0.014 ± 0.89 diopters (D), ranging from −5.50 D to +4.50 D, and an average AL of 22.91 ± 0.71 mm, ranging from 21.37 to 24.84 mm. We also analyzed the total retinal thickness and GCC in the inner and outer rings, categorized by age group. For the inner ring, the mean ± SD (EMM [adjusted for AL], SEM) of total retinal thickness was 334.01 ± 12.49 µm (EMM: 333.93; SEM: 0.80) for the 6–9-year group and 337.01 ± 12.84 µm (EMM: 337.12; SEM: 0.99) for the 10–12-year group, with a statistically significant difference (P = 0.014). DISCUSSION

In this study, we established a normative database for macular thickness across the nine ETDRS subfields [Figure 1 using Heidelberg SD-OCT in healthy Iranian children aged 6–12 years. Our findings demonstrated that age, sex, and AL significantly influenced the thickness of specific retinal sublayers in selected ETDRS zones [Tables 1-3].

Previous studies have shown that ethnicity affects OCT macular parameters in both children and adults.1314 Specifically, research has found that Caucasian children tend to have the thickest macular parameters, while South Asian children have the thinnest, with the most notable differences occurring in the central 1-mm zone.13 In our study of Iranian children (race, white; ethnicity, Middle Eastern), we observed lower macular thickness compared to findings from other studies.151617

Differences in retinal thickness among ethnic groups may result from a combination of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors. Genetic variations related to retinal development, pigmentation, and susceptibility to specific eye diseases can contribute to the observed differences in macular parameters among ethnicities. In addition, variations in AL, corneal curvature, and lens characteristics may affect how light is focused on the retina, influencing the measurement of macular parameters. Environmental factors such as sunlight exposure, air pollution, levels of physical activity, and dietary habits could also impact retinal health and macular thickness.

Our study found that foveal thickness in boys is approximately 5 µm greater than in girls. All retinal sublayers in the central 1-mm subfield were thicker in males than in females, except for the IPL and OPL, which showed no significant difference, and the RPE, which was slightly thicker in females.

Our results regarding the correlation between gender and macular retinal layer thickness are consistent with other studies.18 One possible explanation for this finding is that the male brain has more neurons than the female brain in several regions.19 Since the retina is part of the neural system, the same may apply to it. Whether hormonal differences between genders contribute to the observed macular thickness disparity remains a topic for further research.

We found a significant difference of < 4 µm in total retinal thickness between the two age groups [Table 3]. Many studies have investigated the effect of aging on retinal thickness, yielding controversial findings: some have reported no correlation,20 while others have observed both thickening21 and thinning.22 These discrepancies may result from a failure to control for other factors that can affect retinal thickness, such as refractive errors, magnification issues, and AL.

In a cohort study of over 4000 subjects, von Hanno et al. found that foveal thickness increases with age.23 Conversely, Turk et al. assessed the correlation between macular OCT measurements, age, and AL in 107 healthy children aged 6–16 years and found no significant correlation.15 In adults, foveal retinal thickness demonstrates a nonlinear association with age: it gradually increases, peaking at around 60 years, and then sharply decreases.23

Regarding retinal sublayers, our study showed that only the ONL and the RPE were thicker in the older age group. It is well established that age significantly affects the metabolism of the RPE layer. Over time, the content of lipofuscin increases due to the shedding of photoreceptor outer segments,24 and there are cumulative extracellular deposits between the Bruch membrane and the RPE.25 These changes may lead to thickening of the Bruch-RPE complex with aging, even in the pediatric population.

In addition, consistent with our findings, a previous study showed that the ORL in the macula of children is 36% thinner than that of adults,7 while another study reported that the outer segments of 4-year-old children are 30%–50% shorter than those of adults.13

In the present study, AL was found to negatively affect the full retinal thickness in the outer ETDRS ring, consistent with the findings of the study by Invernizzi et al.26 They reported no correlation in the inner ring or the central fovea. Another study examining the association between AL and macular thickness using SD-OCT in young myopic Chinese patients found a negative correlation between AL and macular thickness in both the outer and inner ETDRS rings.27

When considering the retinal sublayers, we observed a correlation between AL and the IRL but not with the ORL in the outer ring. This suggests that changes in AL primarily affect retinal thickness through alterations in the IRLs. Higashide et al. suggested that the thickness of IRLs and AL is correlated due to magnification effects.28 However, we specifically limited our study population to patients with medium AL to minimize this effect. The Heidelberg SD-OCT system has an embedded correction for magnification that takes AL into account. Furthermore, magnification predominantly impacts lateral measurements on the retinal plane rather than axial measurements.829

Given the inevitable effects of AL on OCT measurements, we employed an ANCOVA model to create EMMs for evaluating OCT parameters within sex and age subgroups while controlling for AL. This approach allowed for more reliable comparisons between groups.

In the current investigation, we used the Spectralis OCT to obtain measurements. It is important to note that the numerical reports from OCT devices can vary due to differences in wavelength, optics, and segmentation algorithms.30 This variability should be considered when comparing our results with those of other studies that utilize different OCT devices.

The strength of our study in providing reference ranges for macular OCT sublayers lies in its robust design, the use of a reliable OCT device, and the adjustment for AL. These factors help to bridge a gap in the current literature and enhance our understanding of ocular health in this specific population. However, our study included a limited age group of children (6–12 years old) with homogeneous ethnicity and a medium AL (21.5–26.5 mm). Therefore, the results should not be generalized to other populations with different races, ethnicities, age groups, or eyes with AL outside our inclusion criteria. Additionally, the influence of different OCT technologies or brands on retinal measurements should also be considered.

In conclusion, this study provides a reference database for macular sublayer thickness in Iranian children aged 6–12 years using Heidelberg SD-OCT. Our findings showed that boys had longer ALs and thicker maculas compared to girls, with mean differences of approximately 0.4 mm and 5 µm, respectively. In contrast, girls exhibited a thicker RPE, with a mean difference of 0.5 µm. In addition, older children (10–12 years) had longer ALs (by about 0.3 mm) and thicker retinas (mean difference: 4 µm) compared to younger children (6–9 years), primarily due to increased thickness in the RPE and ONL sublayers in the older group. The significance of these findings in enhancing our understanding of retinal development during childhood extends beyond the current scope and warrants further investigation in future studies.
