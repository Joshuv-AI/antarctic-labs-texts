A systematic review on clinical and imaging criteria for liposuction candidacy in lower extremity lymphedema
Authors: Star-Kayla Lewis, Brooke Rothberg, Courtney Pina, Noah Sirkin, Samantha Cotes, Jacqueline Morales, Adam Iddriss, Michael Cooper, Thomas O’Donnell, Alisha Oropallo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective/BackgroundLower-extremity lymphedema is difficult to treat, especially after failing conservative management. Liposuction (also termed lipoaspiration or suction-assisted lipectomy) has been identified as a treatment option for patients who failed conservative management in lower-extremity lymphedema. This systematic review evaluates the clinical and imaging criteria used to determine candidacy for liposuction in adults with lower-extremity lymphedema.MethodsA systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. The review searched Embase, PubMed, and Cochrane Library from inception through April 2026. Six reviewers screened 3057 studies using Covidence software. Of the 74 full-text articles assessed, seven studies met inclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria were cohort studies with greater than 50 participants and systematic reviews containing extractable lower-extremity liposuction data in patients with lymphedema. Studies were excluded for wrong study design, wrong surgical procedure, or absence of extractable lower-extremity data. Outcomes of interest were limb volume reduction, cellulitis frequency, quality of life, functional status, and compression garment dependency. Evidence was synthesized narratively due to heterogeneity across studies.ResultsSeven studies were included: 3 prospective cohort studies (n = 190), 2 retrospective cohort studies (n = 145), and 2 systematic reviews with quantitative synthesis. Five categories of candidacy criteria were identified: inadequate response to conservative management, International Society of Lymphology (ISL) clinical stage II to III, tissue composition assessment (including nonpitting edema test and magnetic resonance imaging), lymphedema etiology (primary vs secondary), and confirmed capacity for lifelong compression garment compliance. ISL stage II patients demonstrated significantly better volume reduction outcomes than stage III patients (P < .001). Secondary lymphedema responded more favorably than primary lymphedema (101% vs 79% volume reduction at 24 months). Volume reduction was sustained up to 96 months but required continuous compression therapy. Magnetic resonance imaging was the only imaging modality explicitly required as a formal candidacy criterion in any included study. Quality of life and functional status were inadequately assessed across all studies.DiscussionThe evidence is constrained by the absence of randomized controlled trials evaluating liposuction candidacy criteria in the lower extremity. Nonpitting edema, ISL stage II to III disease with fibroadipose predominance and confirmed compression compliance are the most consistently supported criteria for liposuction candidacy in lower-extremity lymphedema. Prospective validation of candidacy algorithms and adoption of standardized patient-reported outcome measures are needed.Keywords: Lymphedema, Lower extremity, Liposuction, Lipo aspiration, Patient selection

Lymphedema is a chronic and progressive condition, which leads to impaired lymphatic transport.1 This causes persistent tissue swelling, fibroadipose deposition, recurrent cellulitis, and functional impairment.2,3 Lower-extremity lymphedema can arise from primary developmental abnormalities of the lymphatic system or from secondary causes, such as inguinal or pelvic lymph node dissection completed in cancer treatment.4,5 Compared with the upper extremity, the lower extremity poses distinct management challenges including persistent fluid accumulation due to the effects of gravity, the larger volume of the lower limb, the demands of weight bearing, and a higher susceptibility to recurrent soft tissue infections.6,7

Complex decongestive therapy (CDT) and compression therapy remain the foundation of lymphedema management and are endorsed as first-line treatment across all major clinical practice guidelines.8, 9, 10 Despite this, a meaningful proportion of patients do not achieve adequate improvement with conservative measures alone. When the disease has progressed to a stage characterized by fibroadipose tissue, it is no longer amenable to reduction through conservative fluid-mobilizing therapies and instead requires surgical intervention for volume reduction.11,12

Among the surgical techniques available for lymphedema, liposuction (also termed lipoaspiration or suction-assisted lipectomy) occupies a unique mechanistic position.13, 14 Unlike physiologic procedures such as lymphaticovenous anastomosis or vascularized lymph node transfer, liposuction directly removes the hypertrophied adipose tissue that accumulates in chronic lymphedema.14, 15, 16 It is effective only when the excess limb volume is predominantly composed of adipose tissue rather than accumulated lymphatic fluid.15, 16, 17, 18

Despite growing clinical experience with liposuction for lymphedema, the specific clinical and imaging criteria used to determine which patients are appropriate candidates have not been systematically evaluated.19 Selection criteria vary across institutions and are often based on individual surgeon experience rather than on a structured, evidence-based framework. Furthermore, the criteria that differentiate favorable from unfavorable responders within liposuction cohorts (ie, ISL stage and tissue composition), have not been synthesized across the available evidence.

The purpose of this systematic review is to identify and synthesize the clinical and imaging criteria used to determine candidacy for liposuction in adults with lower-extremity lymphedema, and to evaluate which criteria are associated with favorable surgical outcomes including sustained volume reduction and improved quality of life.

Methods

Study eligibility

Studies were eligible for this review if they evaluated liposuction for the treatment of lower-extremity lymphedema in adults and reported at least one candidacy or selection criterion and at least one outcome of interest. We included randomized controlled trials, cohort studies with greater than 50 participants, and systematic reviews that included primary studies meeting these sample size thresholds with extractable lower-extremity lymphedema subgroup data.

Studies were excluded if they were case reports, case series or cohort studies with fewer than 50 participants, narrative reviews without systematic methodology, conference abstracts without full-text publication, limited exclusively to upper-extremity lymphedema without extractable lower-extremity data, evaluated only physiologic microsurgical procedures (such as lymphaticovenous anastomosis and vascularized lymph node transfer) without concurrent liposuction data, or evaluated only excisional or debulking procedures (ie, Charles procedure) without liposuction technique. Outcomes of interest were limb volume or circumference reduction, cellulitis frequency, quality of life, functional status, and compression garment dependency.

Search strategy

A comprehensive systematic search was conducted in September 2025 and again on April 16, 2026, across three electronic databases: Embase (Elsevier), PubMed (National Library of Medicine), and the Cochrane Library (John Wiley and Sons). No filters or limits were applied. The search strategy was developed around three core concepts: (1) lower limb/lower extremity, (2) lymphedema, and (3) surgical intervention (including lipectomy and liposuction). Controlled vocabulary terms (MeSH terms in PubMed and Cochrane; Emtree terms in Embase) were combined with free-text synonyms searched in titles, abstracts, and keywords. The three concept blocks were combined using the Boolean operator AND. Proximity operators (NEXT/2 in Embase; NEAR/2 in Cochrane) were used where appropriate to capture relevant phrase variations. The search strategy was adapted to the syntax and controlled vocabulary of each database. The complete search strategies for all databases are presented in Supplementary Appendix 1 (online only).

Study selection

The initial search of Embase, PubMed, and Cochrane Library yielded 4103 references. All references were imported into Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation) for screening and data management. A total of 1046 duplicate references were identified and removed: 954 through automated detection within Covidence and 92 through manual review, leaving 3057 unique studies for screening.

A team of six reviewers, working in duplicate pairs, independently screened titles and abstracts for potential eligibility. Of the 3057 studies screened, 2973 were excluded at the title and abstract stage, and 84 full-text articles were retrieved and assessed for eligibility. Following a full-text review, 76 studies were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria.

In total, seven studies were included. Disagreements at both screening stages were resolved by discussion and consensus among reviewers. The study selection process is summarized in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram (Fig 1).

Data extraction and analysis

A team of reviewers extracted data using Covidence software with an electronic extraction template. Extracted variables included study design, publication year, sample size, patient demographics, lymphedema etiology (primary vs secondary), International Society of Lymphology (ISL) clinical stage, liposuction technique performed, clinical and imaging criteria used for patient selection, preoperative and postoperative compression or CDT protocols, outcome measures and measurement methods, follow-up duration, and results. For systematic reviews and meta-analyses, we extracted pooled data, individual study characteristics, and subgroup analyses relevant to the lower extremity. Disagreements in data extraction were resolved by consensus among reviewers.

Due to heterogeneity in study designs, outcome measures, and populations across the included studies, quantitative meta-analysis of primary data was not performed. Evidence was synthesized narratively following a structured approach organized by candidacy criterion category. Within each category, the direction, consistency, and strength of findings across studies were evaluated.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Prospective cohort studies

Stewart et al20 evaluated lipoaspiration with controlled compression therapy in 72 patients with lower extremity lymphedema, including both primary and secondary etiologies, with follow-up of up to 96 months.

Karlsson et al23 evaluated liposuction in 72 patients (59 analyzed; 30 with lower-extremity lymphedema) using the Brorson protocol with follow-up to 5 years. The Brorson protocol, developed by Håkan Brorson,26,27 is a standardized surgical technique for liposuction in chronic lymphedema. It involves circumferential suction-assisted lipectomy performed under tourniquet control through multiple small incisions, using power-assisted liposuction cannulas to remove hypertrophied subcutaneous adipose tissue in a circumferential fashion from distal to proximal. A critical and distinguishing component of the protocol is the immediate application of postoperative compression garments in the operating room, followed by lifelong continuous compression garment use to maintain volume reduction. The protocol was originally developed and validated in upper-extremity lymphedema and has subsequently been applied to the lower extremity with modifications to accommodate the larger limb volume and gravitational challenges. Karlsson23 study is notable for providing the most explicit and detailed candidacy criteria of any included study, including the only study requiring magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as a mandatory selection criterion for confirming adipose tissue predominance with minimal fluid component.

Lamprou et al21 evaluated circumferential suction-assisted lipectomy with controlled compression therapy in 88 patients with unilateral lower-extremity lymphedema with a 24-month follow-up. Patient populations included secondary lymphedema from cancer treatment (the majority across all studies) and primary lymphedema of congenital or hereditary etiology. ISL stages II through III comprised of the predominant disease stage.

Retrospective cohort studies

Xin et al22 evaluated tumescent liposuction in 62 patients with cancer-related (secondary) unilateral lower-extremity lymphedema (ISL stage II, n = 29; stage III, n = 33) with 3-month follow-up. No imaging criteria were used for candidacy assessment. Subgroup analyses were performed by sex, ISL stage, and recurrent erysipelas history.

Campisi et al24 evaluated a new technique named Fibro-Lipo-Lymph-Aspiration with a Lymph Vessel Sparing Procedure in 146 patients, including 83 patients with lower-extremity lymphedema who received lymphatic microsurgery and had residual fibrotic adipose tissue (advanced disease, Campisi stages IIB-IIIB). Presurgical mapping of the superficial veins in the surgical sites was performed using Echo Doppler ultrasound guidance and marked on the skin with an indelible pen in order to identify and avoid injury to superficial venous structures during cannula insertion and lipoaspiration. This minimizes the risk of hemorrhage and preserving venous drainage in the treated limb. Outcome included limb volume reduction.

Systematic reviews with quantitative synthesis

Carl et al25 systematically reviewed surgical treatment of lymphedema across 69 studies, including five liposuction studies with one study including lower-extremity lymphedema data. Lastly, Chang et al11 performed a systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating eight liposuction studies; only three of the studies included lower-extremity subgroup data.

Clinical and imaging criteria for liposuction candidacy

Five categories of clinical and imaging criteria were identified across the seven included studies as determinants of liposuction candidacy in lower-extremity lymphedema.

Inadequate response to conservative management

Across all included studies, liposuction candidacy was predicated on an inadequate response to previous conservative management. However, all studies failed to acknowledge a clear time point that indicated failure. Both the Stewart20 and Lamprou21 cohorts described surgical candidates as patients who had undergone CDT, compression therapy, or both without satisfactory improvement in limb volume. The Karlsson23 study required that “previous conservative lymphedema management had resulted in a stable limb volume,” explicitly defining the endpoint of conservative therapy as a prerequisite rather than merely documenting its failure. Campisi cohort deemed a surgical candidate for Fibro-Lipo-Lymph-Aspiration with a Lymph Vessel Sparing Procedure after receiving lymphatic surgery with excess fibrotic adipose tissue resistant to conservative management (ie, lymphatic massages, intermittent peristaltic pressure devices, elastic stockings, and/or multilayer bandaging). Inadequate response to conservative management therefore represents a universal prerequisite for liposuction candidacy.

ISL clinical stage

The ISL clinical stage was the most consistently reported criterion across all included studies and functioned as both a threshold for candidacy and a predictor of differential outcomes.

ISL candidacy threshold

All studies that reported ISL staging enrolled patients with stage II or stage III disease. The Stewart20 and Lamprou21 cohorts enrolled ISL stage II-III patients with a fibroadipose component. Campisi study had an “initial advanced stage disease” (stages IIB-IIIB of the Campisi staging28). The Karlsson23 study required “ISL late-stage 2 or stage 3, resulting in significant functional, physical, and psychological morbidity.” The Xin22 study enrolled ISL stage II and stage III (n = 33) patients. The Carl et al25 liposuction studies reported ISL stage II-III. The Chang et al11 consensus encompassed “moderate to advanced” lymphedema, which the authors equated to ISL stage II-III.

No included study enrolled patients with ISL stage I for liposuction, and no study reported favorable outcomes from liposuction in stage I disease. Stage I lymphedema, characterized by reversible pitting edema, is considered amenable to conservative management and was implicitly or explicitly excluded from all liposuction candidacy frameworks.

Outcomes by ISL stage

The Xin22 study provided the most granular stage stratified outcome data for lower-extremity liposuction. ISL stage II patients demonstrated significantly better outcomes than stage III across all measures. The percentage volume difference was calculated as [(affected limb volume − contralateral unaffected limb volume)/contralateral limb volume × 100]. A positive value indicates residual excess volume in the affected limb relative to the contralateral limb, and a negative value indicates that the affected limb has been reduced to a volume smaller than the contralateral unaffected limb. Stage II patients achieved a negative postoperative percentage volume difference (mean, −3.2%), indicating overcorrection within the affected limb. On the other hand, stage III patients retained a significant residual excess of 20.4% at 3 months (P < .001). These findings support ISL stage as both a threshold criterion for candidacy and a predictor of outcome magnitude, with earlier intervention at stage II achieving superior results compared with stage III disease.

Tissue composition assessment

The tissue composition determines whether residual limb volume is predominantly adipose tissue vs accumulated lymphatic fluid. Nonpitting edema serves as the clinical surrogate for this assessment, and one study used objective methods (MRI imaging) for tissue composition characterization.

Nonpitting edema on clinical examination

Nonpitting edema confirmed by the clinical pitting test was the most universally endorsed candidacy criterion across all included studies. The Karlsson23 study required no or minimal pitting; the Carl et al25 algorithm identified lack of pitting as the key clinical indicator revealing fibrosis and fat deposition; and the Chang et al11 consensus endorsed physical examination for confirming fat hypertrophy. Standardized pitting test methodology involves pressing with the thumb for 1 minute and estimating depression, with acceptable thresholds of 6 to 8 mm or less for leg lymphedema, whereas pitting edema is explicitly designated as a contraindication indicating fluid dominant swelling amenable to conservative treatment rather than adipose hypertrophy. Nonpitting edema therefore functions as a binary gate for candidacy, representing the most accessible method for distinguishing liposuction candidates from noncandidates.

Magnetic resonance imaging

The Karlsson23 study was the only included study to require MRI as a mandatory candidacy criterion, specifying that candidates must demonstrate “increased amount of adipose tissue in the affected limb with minimal fluid component, measured with MRI.” This criterion was assessed using a previously validated MRI segmentation protocol (Sen et al17,29) that differentiates fat from fluid in lymphedematous limbs. The study's 90% excess volume reduction in legs at 1 year suggests that imaging-confirmed candidacy may contribute to favorable outcomes, though the absence of a comparison group selected without MRI creates bias. Tissue composition assessment therefore represents the most mechanistically important candidacy criterion but is assessed with varying rigor across studies, ranging from clinical pitting test alone to mandatory MRI.

Lymphedema etiology

Lymphedema etiology influenced candidacy assessment and was associated with differential outcomes. Secondary lymphedema was predominant across all studies, demonstrating consistently favorable responses. The Lamprou21 cohort provided the most detailed etiology stratified data, with secondary lymphedema achieving 101% volume reduction compared with 79% for primary lymphedema at 24 months, attributed to shorter disease duration and greater water content. The Karlsson23 study reported no significant difference between etiologies at 1 and 2 years, whereas the Stewart20 cohort reported greater reduction in secondary lymphedema. Etiology therefore functions as an outcome modifier rather than binary candidacy criterion, as both etiologies achieved clinically meaningful reduction.

Compression compliance capacity

Confirmed capacity for lifelong compression garment compliance was the most consistent postoperative requirement and candidacy prerequisite across all included studies. The Stewart20 cohort demonstrated volume reduction maintained only with continuous lifelong compression over 96 months, with complete reduction requiring 4 to 5 years and gravity identified as a specific lower extremity barrier. The Karlsson23 and Xin22 studies both included willingness to wear compression garments as explicit candidacy criteria, with lower extremity protocols requiring up to two to three-layered garments. Compression compliance therefore functions as a mandatory prerequisite, and noncompliance is effectively a contraindication as volume reaccumulation is expected without continuous compression.

Cellulitis frequency

Cellulitis reduction was reported across multiple studies but was not formally measured with standardized methodology in any (see Summary of data on Table III). The Lamprou21 cohort reported postoperative cellulitis decreasing to 0.2 attacks/year (primary lymphedema) and 0.3 attacks/year (secondary lymphedema). The Karlsson23 study reported that patients described reduction in cellulitis frequency30 after surgery, with previous publications showing 65% reduction in legs. However, in this study, Karlsson23 mentioned loss to follow-up prevented statistical analysis.

Quality of life

Quality of life data were limited and inadequately measured across all included studies. The Xin22 study assessed eight subjective symptoms at 3 months, finding heaviness and fatigue improved while stiffness, tightness, and tenderness worsened, with pain and numbness unchanged. The Carl et al25 review reported improved well-being, decreased depression and anxiety, and improved personal activity index across three liposuction studies, though all were upper extremity or mixed populations. No included study used a validated lower-extremity-specific lymphedema quality of life instruments such as Lymphedema Quality of Life Questionnaire or LYMPH-Q Lower Extremity, representing a significant gap in the current evidence base. Functional status

No included study systematically assessed functional status as an independent outcome in lower-extremity liposuction patients. Functional outcomes including mobility, range of motion, activities of daily living, and occupational or recreational participation were not formally measured in any original study.

Long-term maintenance

Long-term data were available from three studies (Table V). The Stewart20 cohort demonstrated volume reduction maintained up to 96 months exclusively with continuous compression, with complete reduction requiring 4 to 5 years and gravity identified as a specific barrier. The Karlsson23 study demonstrated maintenance of excess volume reduction in legs at 90% at 1 year, declining to 72%. The Lamprou21 cohort demonstrated maintenance at 24 months with continuous compression.

Safety of liposuction for lower-extremity lymphedema

The safety profile of liposuction for lower-extremity lymphedema was favorable across all included studies (Table VI). Both systematic reviews reported 0% complication rates across all included liposuction studies. However, these rates reflect the absence of major acute surgical events rather than a comprehensive assessment of subclinical lymphatic injury. No included study systematically evaluated pre- and postoperative lymphatic transport function, and the potential for iatrogenic damage to residual functioning lymphatic collectors cannot be excluded. Among individual cohorts, the Xin22 study reported no skin necrosis, effusion, or infection, though three patients required intraoperative blood transfusion. The Karlsson23 study reported no major complications, with five minor superficial pressure wounds from compression garments healing by secondary intention within weeks. The Lamprou21 and Stewart20 cohorts reported no major surgical complications over follow-up periods extending to 96 months. No study reported mortality or limb threatening complications.

Discussion

This systematic review of seven studies, identified through a comprehensive search of 3057 references screened by six independent reviewers, and found five categories of clinical and imaging criteria that inform liposuction candidacy assessment for adults with lower-extremity lymphedema. The categories include inadequate response to conservative management, ISL clinical stage (II-III), tissue composition (such as nonpitting edema on clinical examination and MRI), lymphedema etiology, and compression compliance capacity. These criteria were not prospectively validated in any included study but were associated with differential outcomes, providing an evidence-based framework for clinical decision-making.

Integration of candidacy criteria into a decision framework

The criteria identified in this review can be organized into a sequential assessment pathway (Fig 2). Candidates must first complete an adequate trial of conservative management achieving stable nonpitting edema, as liposuction is contraindicated in the presence of significant pitting. ISL stage II to III disease serves as the threshold criterion, with evidence supporting superior outcomes in stage II. Tissue composition is confirmed through clinical pitting assessment at minimum or MRI when available, particularly when pitting test results are equivocal. Finally, commitment to lifelong continuous compression garment use is a mandatory prerequisite, as all evidence demonstrates volume reduction is compression dependent.

Evidence-based algorithm for liposuction/lipoaspiration candidacy assessment in lower-extremity lymphedema. Evidence-based algorithm for liposuction/lipoaspiration candidacy assessment in lower-extremity lymphedema. The algorithm integrates six categories of candidacy criteria identified in this systematic review: (1) inadequate response to conservative management, (2) the International Society of Lymphology (ISL) clinical stages II to III, (3) nonpitting edema on clinical examination, (4) tissue composition assessment demonstrating adipose predominance, (5) imaging confirmation when available [magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) preferred], and (6) confirmed capacity for lifelong compression compliance. Safety screening (no active cancer, medical fitness) is included as a prerequisite. Outcome modifiers (such as etiology, sex, stage, and disease duration) inform patient counseling but are not exclusionary. The algorithm emphasizes that liposuction is contraindicated in pitting edema, ISL stage I disease, fluid-predominant swelling, and patients unable to commit to lifelong compression. Postoperative continuous compression and long-term follow-up are mandatory. CDT, Complex decongestive therapy; LVA, lymphaticovenous anastomosis; VLNT, vascularized lymph node transfer.

The relationship between CDT and compression garment therapy in the context of liposuction candidacy warrants clarification.31 CDT constitutes the intensive treatment phase designed to achieve maximum volume reduction through mobilization of interstitial fluid. Compression garment therapy represents the maintenance phase in which volume reduction is sustained through continuous use of fitted garments. In the liposuction candidacy pathway, CDT must be completed first to determine whether residual limb volume excess is fluid responsive or represents nonreducible fibroadipose tissue. Since liposuction does not restore lymphatic transport function, volume reaccumulation is expected. Therefore, compression compliance capacity should be viewed as a clinical criterion of equal importance to tissue composition and ISL stage in determining liposuction candidacy.

Comparison with existing literature

The candidacy criteria identified in this review are broadly concordant with published expert consensus statements, but this systematic review advances the field by explicitly categorizing the specific criteria underlying candidacy decisions. The Xin et al22,28 stage stratified data provide the most direct evidence that ISL stage predicts differential outcomes, with stage II patients achieving greater volume reduction than stage III patients who retained 20.4% residual excess, supporting earlier intervention within the candidacy window. The Karlsson28 study's mandatory MRI based tissue composition assessment represents the most rigorous selection approach, with sustained volume reduction through 4 years suggesting imaging confirmed candidacy may contribute to favorable outcomes.

Furthermore, the distinction between primary and secondary lymphedema carries important implications for liposuction candidacy.32 Primary lymphedema arises from developmental abnormalities representing an intrinsic structural deficiency, whereas secondary lymphedema results from extrinsic damage to a previously normal system.4,5 These differences influence tissue composition and residual lymphatic transport capacity. Primary lymphedema patients may present with more fibrotic tissue changes due to lifelong lymphatic insufficiency, limiting achievable reduction. Despite these differences, both etiologies achieved clinically meaningful reduction.

The significance of the pitting test threshold

The Schaverien review provided a numerical threshold for the pitting test in the context of liposuction candidacy: ≤6 to 8 mm for lower-extremity lymphedema.33 This threshold has not been prospectively validated but represents the most specific clinical measurement available for distinguishing liposuction candidates from noncandidates. The clinical significance of these thresholds lies in their potential to standardize a subjective clinical assessment and reduce variability in candidacy decisions across centers.

Iatrogenic lymphatic injury during liposuction

An important consideration not addressed by any included study is the potential for iatrogenic damage to residual functioning lymphatic vessels during liposuction. In patients who have progressed to ISL stage II or III, lymphatic transport capacity is significantly compromised34 Liposuction involves blind subcutaneous cannula insertion and mechanical tissue disruption, raising concern that residual lymphatic collectors could be injured. Frick et al35 and Van de Pas et al36 demonstrated that lymphatic collectors in the lower extremity are located within the subcutaneous tissue, with implications for potential lymphatic injury during liposuction. This suggests that limiting liposuction to superficial planes could reduce injury risk. In addition, Brorson et al37,38 showed through lymphoscintigraphy that liposuction does not significantly alter impaired lymph transport, though this was derived from upper-extremity data. No included study assessed postoperative lymphatic transport function systematically. Future studies should incorporate pre- and postoperative lymphatic function assessment and evaluate whether technique modifications such as cannula depth limitations and intraoperative lymphatic imaging can minimize this risk.

Limitations

This systematic review has several important limitations. The included studies varied substantially in design (3 prospective cohorts, 2 retrospective cohorts, and 2 systematic reviews), follow-up duration (3-96 months), surgical technique, compression protocols, and outcome definitions. This heterogeneity precluded quantitative meta-analysis and limits the certainty of conclusions drawn from narrative synthesis. Findings supported by multiple concordant studies across different designs, such as the requirement for conservative management failure and compression compliance, carry greater confidence than those derived from single studies or short-term follow-up. Readers should interpret all conclusions within this context.

In addition, the evidence base is further limited by incomplete outcome reporting across included studies. Quality of life was inadequately measured, with no study using validated lower-extremity-specific instruments. Functional status was not systematically assessed in any study. Cellulitis frequency, while reported in multiple studies, was not measured using standardized methodology, and attrition in at least one study prevented statistical analysis. These reporting gaps limit the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the full spectrum of patient-centered outcomes following liposuction. These gaps highlight the need for future studies to adopt comprehensive, standardized outcome assessment protocols.

Implications for clinical practice

Despite these limitations, the findings support clinically actionable principles for surgical decision-making. Liposuction should be offered only after adequate conservative management achieving nonpitting edema, with ISL patients with stages II to III representing the appropriate surgical population and evidence suggesting superior outcomes in stage II. The pitting test using standardized methodology serves as the minimum candidacy assessment, whereas MRI-based tissue composition assessment provides the most objective confirmation of adipose predominance when available or when clinical findings are equivocal. Compression compliance must be confirmed preoperatively as a mandatory prerequisite, as all evidence demonstrates volume reduction is entirely dependent on continuous lifelong compression garment use.

Conclusions

This systematic review identifies five categories of criteria informing liposuction candidacy in lower-extremity lymphedema: inadequate response to conservative management, ISL stages II to III disease, tissue composition assessment, lymphedema etiology, and confirmed lifelong compression compliance capacity. Future research should prioritize prospective studies evaluating the predictive value of specific candidacy criteria, including MRI-based vs clinical assessment alone. Standardized patient reported outcome measures such as Lymphedema Quality of Life Questionnaire or LYMPH Q Lower Extremity should be adopted, and consensus on quantitative thresholds including minimum volume difference and pitting test cutoffs should be established to reduce institutional variability and improve patient selection.
