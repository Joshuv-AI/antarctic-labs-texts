Hypercalcemia Secondary to Hyperparathyroidism Mimicking Eclampsia During Postpartum Period in a Young Hypertensive Woman: A Case Report
Authors: Wanni Arachchige Dhanushka Prasad Wanniarachchi, Rathigashini Raamachandran, Rasika Herath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report a rare case of hyperparathyroidism due to parathyroid adenoma causing hypercalcemia, presenting as a generalized prolonged seizure on the seventh postpartum day mimicking eclampsia. Eclampsia developing on the seventh postpartum day in a woman without features of impending eclampsia is rare. Therefore, other differential diagnoses should be considered including primary hyperparathyroidism. Diagnosis is essential as complete cure is possible with surgery.Keywords: hypercalcemia, hyperparathyroidism, parathyroid adenoma, postpartum, pregnancy, seizure

Introduction

Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is the third most common endocrine disorder (next to diabetes and thyroid disease) affecting 0.3% of the general population, with a higher predilection for females [1]. PHPT diagnosed during pregnancy and the peripartum period is a rare event [1, 2]. The diagnosis is usually missed due to its nonspecific presentations [2, 3]. Symptomatic cases were associated with severe fetal and maternal morbidity and mortality [3]. Parathyroid adenoma is also associated with preeclampsia [4, 5, 6].

The diagnosis of PHPT is usually overlooked as both pregnancy and PHPT can have overlapping symptoms. Pregnancy is associated with apparent low blood calcium concentrations due to hypoalbuminemia, increased calcium transport through the placenta, and the increase in glomerular filtration with the consequent rise in urinary calcium excretion masking the true hypercalcemia of PHPT [7].

We present a case of a 28‐year‐old hypothyroid, young hypertensive woman who developed a postpartum hypertensive crisis and persisting generalized seizures mimicking eclampsia, secondary to primary hyperparathyroidism due to a parathyroid adenoma.

Case Presentation/Examination

A 28‐year‐old primigravida with a history of young hypertension for four years and hypothyroidism was admitted for induction of labor at 38 weeks of gestation. She has been investigated for young hypertension prior to the pregnancy. However, she did not comply with the follow‐up plan and did not complete the investigations as per request. She has been on antihypertensives from the beginning of the pregnancy and aspirin from 12 weeks of gestation. Her fetal growth was satisfactory, and hypothyroidism was well controlled throughout the pregnancy. On admission, she was on methyldopa 500 mg three times per day, diltiazem 90 mg twice daily, and levothyroxine 100 microgram daily. There were no features suggestive of preeclampsia.

She denied episodic headaches associated with sweating, palpitations, and no personal or family history of recurrent nephrolithiasis or thyroid malignancies.

Following induction of labor with prostaglandin E2, she developed generalized tonic–clonic seizures. After stabilizing her with MgSO4 infusion, she underwent emergency lower segment cesarean section under spinal anesthesia. Her blood pressure remained high despite being on captopril, labetalol, and diltiazem in maximum doses during the postpartum period, although she recovered well from cesarean section.

Differential diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

She developed a generalized tonic–clonic convulsion with a blood pressure of 200/100 mmHg nine days after delivery. She managed as having another eclamptic seizure with intravenous MgSO4. Non‐contrast computed tomography of the brain performed was normal. The following day, she developed continued seizures despite being on MgSO4, diazepam, levetiracetam, and phenytoin infusions, hence requiring intubation and ventilation.

Her hemogram was normal aside from mild normochromic normocytic anemia. Liver function tests, clotting profile, thyroid function tests, and inflammatory markers remained normal.

Her serum sodium and potassium were normal. But she had moderate hypercalcemia with corrected calcium of 3.1 mmol/L (NR; 2.1–2.54 mmol/L). Her phosphate level was in the lower normal margin at 0.8 mmol/L (NR; 0.8–1.5 mmol/L) and had mild coexisting hypomagnesemia (Mg2+ 0.6 mmol/L NR; 0.7–0.9). Her intact parathyroid hormone level was 180 pg/mL (NR: 7.5–53.5 pg/mL). Coexisting pheochromocytoma was excluded by assessing urinary metanephrines. A tentative diagnosis of primary hyperparathyroidism was made. She was hydrated thoroughly with intravenous 0.9% saline, 3–6 L/24 h, with the detection of symptomatic hypercalcemia. Subsequently, intravenous zoledronic acid 4 mg was started. She recovered fully without long‐term neurological sequelae following correction of hypercalcemia and hypomagnesemia.

Ultrasound scan of the thyroid was inconclusive. She underwent a 4D computed tomography scan of the parathyroid gland which found a left parathyroid adenoma measuring 11 mm × 10 mm × 7 mm is shown in Figure 1.

Outcome and follow‐up

She underwent parathyroid adenoma excision with further optimization for definitive cure. Hypercalcemia resolved abruptly following the surgery. The macroscopic picture of the parathyroid adenoma is shown in Figure 2.

However, she developed hypoparathyroidism causing hypocalcemia. She was on calcium supplements for 22 months, until her calcium returned to normal. Discussion

Primary hyperparathyroidism is a disorder of one or more of the parathyroid glands producing a high level of parathyroid hormone resulting in hypercalcemia. Although PHPT is the third most common endocrine disorder in the general population, less than 1% of all cases of PHPT are diagnosed during or around pregnancy [7]. Further, PHPT is the most common cause for hypercalcemia during pregnancy [3].

Mechanism of hyperparathyroidism causing hypertension is complex. This is thought to be due to the interaction between the parathyroid hormone, renin–angiotensin system, sympathetic nervous system, and the vascular endothelium [8].

This patient has been investigated for secondary causes of hypertension prior to pregnancy, though a specific reason was not established as she did not comply with the management plan and had not completed the investigations. Her blood pressure was difficult to control during pregnancy and needed three antihypertensives, and this could have been due to PHPT.

It is customary to manage generalized tonic–clonic seizures as eclampsia in the immediate postpartum period. But the delayed onset of a second generalized tonic–clonic seizure and persistence of the seizure despite the conventional treatment led us to investigate for hypercalcemia as a causative factor. Once the causative parathyroid adenoma was removed, her clinical condition improved rapidly.

PHPT in pregnancy is a rare event, and hypercalcemic crisis with subsequent seizures is even more rare [9]. Undetected cases of hypercalcemia carry significant risk to both mother and fetus. Hyperparathyroidism with subsequent hypercalcemia may result in hyperemesis, nephrolithiasis, acute pancreatitis, hypertension, and preeclampsia, or even hypercalcemic crisis with cardiac arrythmia and seizures [2, 3, 10, 11]. There is an increased risk of acute pancreatitis and seizures secondary to hypercalcemia, especially during the postpartum period, as there is an abrupt cessation of maternal transfer of calcium to the fetus [11]. Our patient presented during the immediate postpartum period, was probably as a result of the above phenomena.

Poorly controlled chronic hypertension and preeclampsia in patients with PHPT can lead to complications, including intracerebral hemorrhage, retinal hemorrhage, parathyroid hemorrhage, and hypertensive crisis. Furthermore, PHPT is known to be associated with fetal growth restriction, preterm delivery, intrauterine fetal demise, and neonatal tetany if the mother remains untreated [2, 8, 9].

Symptoms like fatigue, nausea, vomiting, constipation, muscle ache, cramps, increased urinary frequency, and mood changes are common to both hypercalcemia and normal pregnancy, leading to a diagnostic dilemma [6]. Furthermore, relative hypocalcemia observed during normal pregnancy masks the biochemical recognition of hypercalcemia [5].

However, diagnosis of PHPT can be made during pregnancy by recognizing elevated serum total calcium or ionized calcium level, hypophosphatemia, and elevated parathyroid hormone level in the absence of any other cause for hypercalcemia [10]. Ultrasonography is the preferred imaging modality during pregnancy, as computed tomography and radioisotope scans are contraindicated. In diagnosing parathyroid adenoma, Ultrasonography has sensitivity and specificity of 76%–87% and 94%–96%, respectively [10]. The technetium‐99m sestamibi scan is considered the gold standard for imaging parathyroid tumors [4]. In ultrasound‐negative patients, modern multiphase 4D computed tomography techniques can be used to localize lesions of the parathyroid gland and are considered relatively safe due to mitigating the risk of radiation exposure with lead shielding [5].

Management of pregnant women with PHPT should be individualized, with multidisciplinary inputs. It should be based on the symptoms, gestational age, and risk to the health of both the woman and fetus [5]. PHPT during pregnancy can be managed conservatively with hydration, a low calcium diet, and vitamin D supplementation. Pharmacological agents like calcitonin, cinacalcet, or bisphosphonates are not used during pregnancy due to a lack of safety data [4]. Parathyroidectomy is the definitive treatment for PHPT, which can be safely performed during pregnancy. All the cases reported on primary hyperparathyroidism in pregnancy secondary to parathyroid adenoma were treated with surgery with successful outcomes, as in our patients. This includes two patients who were presented with seizures during the antenatal period and intrapartum [2, 9]. The optimum timing of surgery during pregnancy is not rigid; however, intervention during the second trimester is preferable due to incomplete organogenesis in the first trimester and the risk of preterm delivery in the third trimester [3, 4, 5].

Conclusions and Results

Primary hyperparathyroidism is a rare event in pregnancy. Diagnosis is challenging as hypercalcemia is masked by the increased demand for calcium during pregnancy and overlapping nonspecific symptoms. So, PHPT should be suspected in young hypertensive women with resistant gestational hypertension and persistent generalized seizures mimicking eclampsia who are poorly responding to conventional treatment.
