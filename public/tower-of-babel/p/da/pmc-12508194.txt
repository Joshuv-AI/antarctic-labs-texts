The role of fiber alkalization in enhancing the physical and mechanical performance of false banana fiber and its composites in geopolymer matrices
Authors: Lulseged Belay Addis, Eshetu Getahun, Addisu Workiye, Girma Zenebe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural fibers are increasingly being employed as sustainable reinforcements in composite products. However, industrial applications are limited due to challenges related to moisture absorption, weak fiber-matrix bonding, and inconsistent processing outcomes. Alkaline pretreatment is commonly used to improve fiber surface properties, but little emphasis has been given to how the treatment apparatus affects the resulting fiber surface changes and composite performance. In this study, false banana fibers were alkaline-treated using two different methods: a traditional magnetic stirring setup and an Atlas Syrris automated synthesis reactor. To isolate the influence of the treatment apparatus, all treatment parameters for both instruments were kept constant, including temperature, NaOH concentration, treatment time, fiber volume content, fiber length, and fiber-to-liquor weight ratio. The study focused on how the treatment affected the fibers’ physical properties, such as weight, diameter, surface roughness, and morphological changes. Surface roughness was evaluated using ImageJ analysis with the SurfCharJ Plugin. Additionally, the tensile strength and reinforcing efficacy of treated fibers in geopolymer composites were assessed. Results indicate that the reactor-assisted treatment greatly improves fiber modification, reducing diameter and weight by 24.4% and 26.54%, respectively, indicating more effective removal of amorphous and soluble components. Scanning electron microscopy and surface roughness study revealed the improved fiber surface texture, which is beneficial for interfacial bonding. Furthermore, the tensile strength of fibers treated in the Atlas Syrris reactor increased by 22% and when used in geopolymer matrix composites, they demonstrated superior mechanical performance, boosting compressive strength by 56% and splitting tensile strength by 68% when compared to unreinforced geopolymer paste specimens. These improvements outperformed those obtained with fibers treated using magnetic stirring, revealing the superior efficiency and industrial relevance of reactor-assisted alkaline treatment in the production of high-performance natural fiber-reinforced composites. Introduction

Natural fibers are becoming increasingly popular across a wide range of applications due to their environmental benefits, economic viability, widespread availability, biodegradability, and favorable mechanical properties such as high strength-to-weight ratios1–8. These benefits are driving a global shift from synthetic to natural fibers in composite material development across various industries. Although natural fiber-reinforced polymer composites have lower mechanical properties than synthetic counterparts, they have proven to be successful in improving the strength and ductility of concrete structures while drastically cutting reinforcement costs9. Consequently, current research efforts are largely focused on improving their performance and broadening their applications in the automotive, biomedical, civil engineering, and food packaging sectors.

For instance, Li et al.10 highlighted the benefits of natural fiber-reinforced composites in the automotive sector, where their lightweight and biodegradable nature contributes to reduced fuel consumption and environmental impact. Developments like fiber surface modification and hybridization have markedly enhanced the mechanical performance of these composites, rendering them suitable for structural vehicle components. Complementing this, Neto et al.11 had emphasized the importance of hybridization in augmenting the mechanical performance of natural fiber-reinforced composites.

In a biomedical context, Faheed12 emphasized the critical role of biocompatibility in applications such as wound care, tissue engineering, and drug delivery systems. The inherent biodegradability and non-toxic characteristics of natural fibers make them particularly suited for medical applications involving direct contact with biological tissues. Recent advancements have also focused on the development of advanced biomaterials, including hydrogels and scaffolds derived from natural fibers12. Similarly, Kuram13 proposed the potential of natural fiber composites for tissue and bone reconstruction and repair. Lau et al.14 further demonstrated the importance of a fully bioresorbable polymer composite made from silk fibers within a polylactic acid matrix for bone fixation, which facilitates bone healing by degrading gradually as bone cells regenerate, thereby avoiding the need for secondary surgeries associated with conventional steel fixators.

Likewise, in the construction sector, natural fibers provide a sustainable alternative to conventional building materials, applicable in both new construction and retrofitting processes, contributing to reduced energy consumption and carbon emissions15,16. Mehra and Singh16 documented the efficiency of natural fibers in strengthening concrete structures, thereby enhancing mechanical properties and durability. Furthermore, natural fibers are increasingly employed as biodegradable replacements for plastic packaging, aligning with environmental sustainability goals17. Zhang et al.18 noted the critical role of packaging in modern society for product protection, sales promotion, and logistics facilitation.

Despite these merits, the broader implementation of natural fiber-reinforced composites is limited by several technical challenges. The hydrophilic nature of natural fibers results in weak interfacial bonding with hydrophobic polymer matrices, high moisture uptake, and lower dimensional stability. Other limitations include fiber heterogeneity, poor thermal resistance, comparatively limited mechanical properties, flammability, and fiber aggregation during processing, all of which impede their competitiveness against synthetic fibers in high-performance engineering applications19–23. Addressing these limitations is essential to unlocking the full potential of natural fibers in composite technology.

Improving fiber-matrix interfacial bonding is critical for increasing the mechanical performance of natural fiber-reinforced composites. In this regard, alkaline treatment (alkalization) of natural fibers has emerged as one of the most effective and widely used methods. This process removes non-cellulosic components such as lignin, hemicellulose, waxes, and surface impurities, thereby increasing fiber surface roughness and promoting better mechanical interlocking with matrices, ultimately improving overall composite performance.

Nevertheless, the quantitative evaluation of changes in fiber surface roughness and diameter post-alkaline treatment has been largely overlooked, despite their critical influence. Moreover, while numerous studies have examined the mechanical improvements provided by alkaline treatment, the impact of the treatment apparatus on fiber modification has received very little attention. Most investigations rely on simple, traditional equipment, such as magnetic stirrers or hot plates, and often lack precise control over key process parameters like temperature, agitation speed, and reaction time, leading to inconsistencies in fiber modification and complicating the scalability of treatment protocols for industrial applications. False banana (Ensete ventricosum), an indigenous and endemic plant in Ethiopia, is widely farmed, particularly in the southern regions of the country, where its starchy pseudostem and corm provide a staple food source for almost 20 million people24.

Its production, however, produces a considerable amount of fibrous by-products, which remain largely underutilized25, despite their abundance and potential value. This abundant agricultural residue is a readily available and inexpensive natural fiber source, making it appealing for value-added applications. The fibers are characterized by their high cellulose content, relatively low lignin content, high tensile strength, and low density, all of which contribute to good specific strength and stiffness26. According to Addis et al.26, their thermal stability is comparable to other commonly employed lignocellulosic fibers, such as luffa cylindrica fibers27, oil palm fruit fibers28, and sisal fibers29. Furthermore, as previously noted, false banana fiber is a potential reinforcement material for geopolymer composites30.

Thus, the selection of false banana fiber for this study is based on its advantageous material properties and the potential to generate value-added applications from an abundant local resource, while also supporting waste valorisation and the broader goal of advancing environmentally friendly composite technology. While prior research has identified the optimal fiber content and length for alkali-treated false banana fiber-reinforced geopolymer composites using a magnetic stirrer at room temperature30, the effect of treatment apparatus on fiber modification efficiency and the resulting composite performance has yet to be thoroughly investigated. To address this gap, the present work compares fiber treatment using a conventional magnetic stirrer and an Atlas Syrris automated synthesis reactor under identical process conditions.

Thus, by keeping all other treatment factors constant and isolating the effect of the treatment apparatus, this study aims to provide novel insights into how processing equipment influences fiber treatment quality and reinforcing performance in composite material fabrication, using the optimal fiber content and length determined in the previous study30. The reactor provides a programmable, closed-system environment that allows for precise control of temperature, agitation speed, and reaction time, resulting in a higher degree of repeatability. Unlike traditional open system approaches, it delivers better treatment consistency, scalability for batch and continuous processes, and can be integrated into automated fiber treatment systems, which are crucial for industrial adoption. Such control is required for scaling fiber treatment procedures in an industrial setting where consistency and reproducibility are vital, as it reduces human error and improves fiber quality. Thus, the purpose of this work is to see how the choice of treatment apparatus, specifically the Atlas Syrris reactor against conventional magnetic stirring, affects the physical and mechanical properties of alkali-treated false banana fibers, as well as their reinforcing performance in geopolymer composites.

Materials and methods

Materials

False banana fiber was obtained at a local market in Yirga Chefe, Southern Region of Ethiopia. Carl Roth, Germany, supplied analytical-grade Liquid sodium silicate and 99% purity sodium hydroxide (NaOH) pellets. STEAG Power Minerals GmbH in Germany also supplied Fly ash. Recent studies26,31 have reported information about the chemical composition of fly ash, as well as the physico-mechanical and compositional properties of false banana fiber.

Methods

Fiber surface treatments

This study employed alkali-treated false banana fibers to fabricate geopolymer green composites. Prior to their incorporation into the geopolymer matrix, the fibers underwent several preparation steps. Initially, the raw fibers, which contained various impurities and contaminants, were thoroughly cleaned by washing them with fresh water in a hot water bath set at 50 °C for 2 h using a 2.5% mild detergent solution. The fibers were then rinsed with fresh water, followed by deionized water, to remove any detergent residue. After rinsing, the fibers were air-dried for an extended period of time to ensure that all absorbed moisture had evaporated. To facilitate interfacial compatibility between the fibers and the geopolymer matrix, the dried fibers were treated in a 5% NaOH solution (w/v) for 2 h at room temperature, with a fiber-to-liquor weight ratio of 1:20, using an Atlas Syrris automated reactor and a conventional magnetic stirring setup. Previous studies30,32–34 had shown that a 5% NaOH solution concentration, a 2-hour treatment time, and a fiber-to-liquor weight ratio of 1:20 are effective in removing non-cellulosic fiber components while minimizing severe damage to the cellulose structure. This equilibrium improves fiber matrix adherence without considerably reducing fiber strength.

The treatment temperature in the Atlas Syrris reactor was controlled by a circulating oil from the Huber Pilot ONE heat source, and it was continuously monitored and regulated by a thermocouple attached to the reactor. The fiber samples were gently rolled and immersed in the alkaline solution using a 100 mL Pyrex glass, as shown in Fig. 1, and the treatment was carried out at a constant overhead stirrer speed of 100 rpm. Following the alkali treatment, the NaOH solution was drained, and the fibers were repeatedly rinsed with freshwater, followed by distilled water, until the washing Liquor attained a neutral pH, which was confirmed with a pH meter. The washed fibers were air-dried for one day before being oven-dried at 80 °C for another 24 h. In a recent work31, the optimal set of parameters for preparing a geopolymer paste was identified as: (i) activator to binder ratio of 0.46; (ii) NaOH concentration of 14 M; (iii) Na2SiO3 to NaOH mass ratio of 1.5; and (iv) 72-hour standing time. Based on this optimal parameter, a geopolymer composite was created using alkali-treated oven-dried false banana fiber, which was treated using a conventional magnetic stirring device, and it was found that a 1.4% fiber volume fraction at 10 mm length yields the best composite mechanical performance30.

Experimental setup of alkali-treatment of fibers: (a) Fiber samples; (b) Atlas Syrris automated reactor; (c) Reactor reaction temperature.

In this study, geopolymer composites were prepared using an alkali activator to binder ratio of 0.46, a NaOH concentration of 14 M, a Na2SiO3 to NaOH mass ratio of 1.5, a standing time of 72 h, and a fiber volume fraction of 1.4% at 10 mm length as the optimal parameters. The fibers were alkaline-treated using two methods: a conventional magnetic stirring setup and an Atlas Syrris automated synthesis reactor. To isolate the effect of the treatment equipment, all treatment parameters were held constant for both techniques, including NaOH concentration (5% w/v), treatment time (2 h), fiber-to-liquor weight ratio (1:20), and processing temperature (ambient conditions).

The composites were then cast in a flexible silicone rubber mold that was 15 mm in diameter and 30 mm in height. All freshly cast geopolymer composite specimens were immediately placed in a properly sealed polypropylene plastic bag to prevent air interaction and allowed to stand at ambient conditions for 72 h, as determined by a prior study31. The samples were then cured in an oven (Model: PH-030 A) at 70 °C for 24 h without removing the polypropylene plastic bag. Following heat curing, the specimens were allowed to cool to ambient temperature before being removed from the mold. They were then allowed to cure in the open air for the remaining days until the day of mechanical testing. On the 28th day, the specimens were tested for compressive and splitting tensile strength.

Fiber diameter determination

The diameter of the fiber (alkali-treated and untreated) was determined using ImageJ analysis software by capturing an image of the fiber using an optical microscope. Ten fibers were randomly selected from the bundle of fibers, and three measurements (top, middle, and tail) were taken from each fiber filament to account for thickness variation along its length. The image of the untreated and alkali-treated fibers was captured by an LEICA M50 optical microscope at 400x magnification using LAS V3.8 software, as shown in Fig. 2. The fiber’s diameter was then calculated by taking the average of thirty samples.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and fiber surface roughness

The morphology of fiber samples before and after alkali treatment was investigated using a Carl Zeiss scanning electron microscope (SEM) with an acceleration voltage of 1 kV. The SEM images were then postprocessed using the SurfCharJ Plugin, which is included in the ImageJ software, to define roughness and morphological changes caused by the alkaline treatment.

Single fiber tensile strength test

The tensile strength of both raw and alkali-treated false banana fibers was measured using an Instron Universal testing machine in accordance with ISO 2062 – EN ISO 2062 Traction On Yarns protocols. Fiber samples were chosen at random and glued to a paperboard frame with a 25 mm gauge length using double-sided sticking tape before being carefully gripped into the Instron machine. The edges of the paperboard frame were then cut away following the cutting lines, as shown in Figs. 3 and 4. A 100 N load cell with a constant crosshead speed of 1 mm/min was applied until the fiber was broken. Tensile strength was evaluated on twenty randomly selected fiber samples, and the results are given as a mean with a 95% confidence interval.

Mechanical testing

Cylindrical geopolymer composite specimens with a nominal diameter of 15 mm and a height of 30 mm were made using a silicone rubber mold and tested for compressive and splitting tensile strength after 28 days of curing in accordance with ASTM C 39/C39M36 and ASTM C 496/C496M37 procedures, respectively. The specimens were loaded with a 100 kN loading capacity universal testing machine and applied continuously without shock at a constant loading rate of 50 N/s to comply with the given ASTM standards. For each experiment, five replications were done, and the average values were used for analysis. For the splitting tensile test, the strength of the composite was calculated using Eq. (2).

Results and discussion

False banana fiber diameter

Figure 5 shows the frequency distribution of false banana fiber diameters in untreated and alkali-treated samples. The graph describes the variations in fiber diameter caused by alkaline treatment using two different treatment apparatus: (i) a conventional magnetic stirring setup; and (ii) the Atlas Syrris automated synthesis reactor. Overall, the alkali-treated fibers show a noticeable leftward shift in fiber diameter values when compared to the untreated (as-received) samples, indicating a decrease in fiber diameter across the treated groups due to chemical modification. This shift is more prominent in the reactor-treated group, suggesting a greater degree of fiber modification under the controlled reaction environment provided by the automated system.

Frequency distribution of false banana fiber diameter for: (a) as-received fiber; (b) alkaline treated fibers in a conventional magnetic stirring plate; (c) alkaline treated fibers in Atlas Syrris reactor.

As can be seen from Fig. 5, the untreated fibers have an average diameter of around 201 ± 56 μm. When treated with a 5% NaOH solution for two hours with magnetic stirring equipment, the mean diameter was reduced by about 12%. On the other hand, the same treatment carried out in the Atlas Syrris reactor resulted in a more substantial decrease in average diameter to 152 ± 0.053, representing a 24.4% reduction relative to the as-received fiber samples. This demonstrates the enhanced effectiveness of the reactor-assisted systems in modifying fiber morphology. A one-way ANOVA was performed to determine the statistical significance of the differences in fiber diameter between the two fiber treatment apparatus. As shown in Table 1, the type of treatment apparatus had a significant effect on fiber diameter (p = 0.049) at the 95% confidence level.

Furthermore, the frequency distribution plot shows that fibers treated with the Atlas Syrris reactor have a narrower and more uniform diameter range of 0.05 mm to 0.27 mm compared to those treated with the traditional magnetic stirring method, which ranged from 0.05 mm to 0.33 mm. This narrower dispersion indicates that the reactor-treated samples had more consistent and homogeneous fiber modification. Additionally, the frequency of fibers falling within each diameter interval is higher for the reactor-assisted treatment, suggesting improved uniformity and reproducibility in fiber dimensions. This homogeneity is critical for enhancing the mechanical performance and processing consistency of fiber-reinforced composites in industrial applications, as well as their scalability.

In general, the reduction in false banana fiber diameter following alkaline treatment in both treatment groups demonstrates the effectiveness of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) in altering the fiber’s physical structure. The larger decrease (24.4%) seen with the Atlas Syrris reactor highlights the importance of the treatment apparatus in determining the degree of fiber modification.

The alkaline solution largely removes non-cellulosic components such as waxes, pectin, hemicellulose, and other soluble compounds, as well as surface impurities, resulting in this dimensional reduction. The resulting finer fiber morphology increases surface area and facilitates better interfacial bonding within composite matrices, which contributes to improved mechanical properties. Furthermore, finer fibers distribute stress more evenly and have greater flexibility, which contributes to enhanced composite performance in a range of applications.

Kathirselvam et al.38 suggest that the decrease in fiber diameter after alkaline treatment could be attributed to the breakdown of hemicellulose and lignin, which disrupts intermolecular bonds and causes the fibers to split into smaller fibrils. Sun19 reported that alkaline treatment decreased fiber diameter while increasing fiber length-to-diameter ratio. Several studies obtained similar results39–42. According to Luqman et al.39, chemical treatment can remove amorphous components while reducing the fiber’s hemicellulose component. Alkaline (NaOH) treatment usually causes the diameter of natural fibers to decrease38. A reduction in fiber diameter of natural fibers, which results in a higher aspect ratio, often leads to improved material properties43,44.

As noted by Saha et al.42, such treatments do not completely remove hemicellulose or lignin due to the presence of chemically resistant carbon-carbon bonds in lignin molecules, as well as strong hydrogen bonding in residual hemicellulose and cellulose microfibrils. Previous studies have similarly reported that weight loss in natural fibers increases with higher alkali concentrations, longer treatment durations, or more intensive reaction conditions. For instance, Roy et al.45 observed a gradual increase in jute fiber weight loss as the alkali concentration and treatment time increased. These findings, which are reported by other literature38,42, support the current results and confirm that the Atlas Syrris reactor allows for more effective chemical modification by promoting deeper penetration and reactivity of the alkali solution within the fiber matrix. The greater weight reduction observed in the reactor-treated fiber samples demonstrates the higher efficiency and intensity of the treatment process in this automated system, likely due to controlled reaction parameters and consistent mechanical agitation.

Yimer and Gebre46 studied the effects of NaOH treatment on the surface roughness of sisal fibers designed for use in concrete composites. They found that treating the fibers with a 5% NaOH solution for 24 h resulted in a significant increase in surface roughness. When the fibers were subjected to alkaline treatment, a noticeable improvement in tensile strength of the fibers was recorded. This improvement is attributed to the partial removal of non-cellulosic components and surface impurities during the alkali treatment. Leaching of these components promotes fiber fibrillation, in which a single natural fiber composed of several much smaller fibrils tightly packed together begins to split partially or completely into finer subunits (microfibrils or fibrils) and improves the alignment of these fibrils along the direction of the applied load. As a result, the fibers are better able to bear loads, resulting in increased tensile strength. The elimination of these amorphous, loosely bonded constituents exposes the underlying crystalline cellulose microfibrils and increases the effective load-bearing capacity of the fiber. Moreover, the alkaline solution disrupts the hydrogen bonding and weak intermolecular forces within the fiber structure, allowing for better alignment of cellulose chains, which contributes to increased tensile strength.

As can be seen from Table 5, fibers treated with the magnetic stirrer had a strength range of 230 to 914 MPa and an average tensile strength of 442 MPa. This corresponds to a 16% improvement over the raw fibers’ average tensile strength. On the other hand, fibers treated with the same alkaline solution in an Atlas Syrris reactor had a tensile strength value ranging from 85 to 1207 MPa, with an average of 467 MPa, representing an approximate 22% increase in tensile strength relative to untreated fibers.

The larger variability in tensile strength observed in fibers treated with the Atlas Syrris reactor could be the result of the interplay between the natural heterogeneity of the fibers and the reactor’s enhanced processing conditions. While the reactor’s precise control over temperature, mixing, and agitation improves treatment efficiency and average fiber strength, it also amplifies fiber-to-fiber differences arising from structural, compositional, and morphological variations. In some cases, the intensified treatment may expose or exacerbate pre-existing defects and fiber-to-fiber inconsistencies, resulting in lower strength values; however, optimally treated fibers have significantly improved tensile performance due to better cellulose alignment and increased crystallinity as amorphous content is reduced. Thus, while these conditions enhance average fiber strength, they may also result in a broader range of tensile strength values.

The superior performance of reactor-treated fibers indicates that the treatment environment plays a key role in the extent of fiber modification. The Atlas Syrris reactor provides enhanced process control, including better temperature regulation, consistent mixing, and uniform chemical exposure, which likely facilitates more effective and homogeneous removal of non-cellulosic materials. This results in improved fiber morphology, greater surface area, and possibly better molecular orientation within the cellulose matrix, all of which contribute to higher tensile performance. In general, the alkaline treatment enhances the mechanical properties of false banana fiber by altering its internal and surface structure. The level of improvement is largely influenced by the treatment system employed, with the Atlas Syrris reactor outperforming the conventional magnetic stirrer. These findings emphasize the importance of the processing apparatus in optimizing natural fiber properties for producing composite materials at an industrial scale.

Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 5, alkalizing the fiber reduces fiber diameter, thereby increasing fiber aspect ratio (i.e., length to diameter ratio), the most important factor influencing fiber mechanical properties40, because fibers have less porosity and weak links as diameter decreases41, resulting in improved material properties. In addition to this, alkaline-treated fibers have a higher crystallinity index value than untreated fibers39,40, which improves crystalline packing and fiber tensile strength41. However, alkaline treatment did not always result in increased strength of natural fibers. The concentration of the alkaline solution and treatment time are key elements that must be carefully controlled, as they can negatively impact fiber strength due to the corrosive nature of the chemical NaOH39. In this context, Kumar and Das47 emphasized the significance of optimizing the alkali treatment process in order to improve fiber tensile properties, as severe alkali treatment made the fiber very weak by destroying its structure.

According to a report compiled by Reddy et al.40, hemicellulose remains dispersed in the interfibrillar region, isolating cellulose chains from one another in untreated fibers and acting as a barrier for stress transfer between interfibrillar regions, reducing the fiber’s tensile strength. The interfibrillar region refers to the space or matrix between cellulose fibrils (long, thread-like structural units) in a natural fiber.

In untreated plant fibers, this region is largely made up of hemicellulose, lignin, pectin, and other non-cellulosic components. These materials act like a glue or filler, holding the fibrils together while also separating and isolating the cellulose fibrils from direct contact. Alkaline treatment largely eliminates these interfibrillar components, reducing these barriers and improving the fiber’s ability to carry load. However, excessive removal (from high alkali concentrations or prolonged exposure) might destroy the fibrils, lowering strength.

Compressive and splitting tensile strengths of the composites

Fig. 9 illustrates the compressive and splitting tensile strengths of the geopolymer paste reinforced with false banana fibers. The baseline compressive strength of unreinforced (neat) geopolymer paste specimens (designated as R17) was previously reported as 24.96 MPa31. Incorporating 1.4 vol% of alkali-treated false banana fiber, with a fiber length of 10 mm, increased compressive strength to 32.67 MPa30. When the fibers were treated with the same alkaline solution concentration using an Atlas Syrris reactor rather than a conventional magnetic stirring plate, the compressive strength increased to 38.86 MPa, representing a 56% increase over the neat geopolymer paste samples. This treatment approach increases compressive strength by approximately 19% when compared to composites made with fibers treated with the same treatment solution in a magnetic stirring setup.

Effect of alkali treatment equipment on the mechanical strength of geopolymer composite samples.

Similarly, the optimal splitting tensile strength of unreinforced geopolymer paste samples (i.e., R17) has previously been determined to be 4.4 MPa31. The splitting tensile strength improved to 5.84 MPa by adding 1.4 vol% alkali-treated false banana fiber (10 mm length), which was treated using a conventional magnetic stirrer plate30. When the fibers were treated with the same concentration of alkaline solution in the Atlas Syrris reactor, their splitting tensile strength increased to 7.40 MPa, which was 68% higher than the plain paste samples. This results in a 27% improvement over geopolymer composites reinforced with fibers treated with a magnetic stirring plate in the same alkaline solutions.

The superior composite strength obtained when fibers were treated in the Atlas Syrris reactor versus those treated with a conventional magnetic stirring plate can be attributed to the reactor’s ability to provide more uniform, controlled, and efficient chemical treatment of the false banana fibers. Alkaline treatment in the magnetic stirrer setup is based on mild localized mechanical agitation. This often leads to inconsistent alkaline solution penetration into the fiber bundles, incomplete removal of hemicellulose, lignin, and surface impurities, and ineffective fibrillation of the fiber. As a result, fiber-matrix interfacial bonding in the geopolymer composite remains inadequate, reducing stress transfer efficiency when loaded. In contrast, the Atlas Syrris reactor offers precise temperature control, consistent mixing, and agitation, which enhance the kinetics of alkali-fiber reactions. This results in more homogeneous chemical modification of the fiber surface and cross-section. Better removal of amorphous components (hemicellulose and lignin) may reveal crystalline cellulose microfibrils, resulting in a rougher fiber surface. This promotes mechanical interlocking between the fiber and the geopolymer matrix.

The data in Fig. 9 demonstrates these improvements. The strength gain achieved in geopolymer composites made from fibers treated in the reactor setup (i.e., 19% improvement in compressive strength and 27% increase in splitting tensile strength compared to the magnetic stirring device) confirms that the improved fiber surface characteristics achieved in the reactor directly improve the composites’ mechanical performance. Overall, the Atlas Syrris reactor outperforms and shows more promise than the magnetic stirrer because it produces fibers that are more thoroughly cleaned, rougher, have a higher aspect ratio, and have higher tensile strength, resulting in stronger fiber-matrix interface adhesion and, as a result, higher compressive and splitting tensile strengths in geopolymer composites. Therefore, alkaline treatment with the Atlas Syrris reactor more effectively removes surface waxes and non-cellulosic components, reduces fiber diameter, and increases fiber aspect ratio, thereby enhancing composite mechanical performance, as supported by Gudayu et al.43 and Borchani et al.48, who explained the importance of fiber aspect ratio in improving the mechanical properties of fibers and their products. The Atlas Syrris reactor also promotes fiber fibrillation, which reduces structural flaws and improves fiber tensile strength when compared to conventional magnetic stirring. Another study, Luqman et al.39, noted that alkali treatment can realign fibrils along the loading direction, allowing for better load sharing and greatly boosting fiber tensile strength.

Conclusions

In this study, alkali-treated false banana fiber-reinforced geopolymer composites were fabricated using fibers treated with two different treatment apparatus: (a) a conventional magnetic stirring setup; and (b) the Atlas Syrris automated reactor. In both treatment setups, the fibers were treated with a 5% NaOH solution for two hours, and the geopolymer composites were made with a 1.4% fiber volume fraction of 10 mm alkali-treated false banana fiber. Recommendations for future work

Although the current study provides novel insights, optimizing treatment parameters such as variable alkali concentration, treatment time, agitation speed, and temperature variations is crucial for maximizing these benefits in large-scale production. Furthermore, the microstructural study was limited to fiber SEM analysis and roughness measurements; techniques such as X-ray diffraction (XRD) and SEM analysis of the composite material were not performed. Scaling up the reactor-assisted process and conducting a cost-benefit analysis for integrating such reactor systems into commercial production are also important.
