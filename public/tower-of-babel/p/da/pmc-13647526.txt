Prevalence and factors associated with controlled blood pressure among hypertensive patients in a Malaysian primary health clinic-a cross-sectional study
Authors: A’qilah Baharudin, Faiz Baharudin, Aneesa Abdul Rashid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundControlled Blood pressure (BP) in hypertensive patients is essential in reducing the risk of cardiovascular diseases. Even a slight reduction in BP control may significantly improve cardiovascular outcomes. Several factors have been associated with controlled BP. We aimed to determine the prevalence of controlled BP among hypertensive patients and its associated factors in a Malaysian primary health clinic.MethodA cross-sectional study was conducted among Malaysian adults aged 18 years and above with underlying hypertension who attended a public healthcare clinic located in a suburban area of Melaka, Malaysia, from December 2024 to February 2025. Participants were selected via systematic random sampling. Data were collected through self-administered questionnaires, medical records, and clinical blood pressure measurement. Blood pressure control status was determined based on clinic blood pressure readings measured during visits. Documented home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) records, when available in the medical records, were reviewed as supplementary clinical information. A multiple logistic regression was conducted to determine the factors associated with controlled blood pressure.ResultsA total of 460 hypertensive patients were included in this study, with a response rate of 96.8%. Only 41.5% of the participants (n = 191) had controlled BP. The median age was 64 (IQR:17) and the majority were female (57.4%, n = 264). The significant factors associated with controlled blood pressure among the study population were increasing age (aOR = 1.037, 95% CI [1.018,1.057], p < 0.001), female gender (aOR = 2.092, 95% CI [1.388,3.151], p < 0.001), participants who took one or two anti-hypertensive (aOR = 1.921, 95% CI [1.145,3.224],p = 0.013) and (aOR = 2.830, 95% CI [1.637,4.895], p < 0.001) respectively, participants without diabetes (aOR = 1.689, 95% CI [1.112,2.566], p = 0.014), and participant who had documented HBPM (aOR = 4.489, 95% CI [2.337,8.623], p < 0.001).ConclusionsThe findings suggest that the prevalence of controlled blood pressure, 41.5% is still low among the study population. Controlled blood pressure was associated with increasing age, female gender, patients on fewer than three antihypertensive medications, hypertensive patients without diabetes mellitus, and those who have documented home blood pressure monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03447-7.Keywords: Hypertension, Blood pressure, Anti-hypertensive, Home blood pressure monitoring, Medication adherence

Background

Hypertension is one of the leading risk factors for cardiovascular disease and premature death worldwide [1, 2]. The relationship between blood pressure (BP) and the risk of cardiovascular events is continuous, consistent, and independent of other risk factors; the higher the BP, the greater the chance of myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, and kidney disease [3]. Globally, the prevalence of hypertension is increasing due to population ageing and greater exposure to lifestyle risk factors such as unhealthy diets and physical inactivity [4]. In Malaysia, the prevalence of hypertension among adults aged 18 years and above is 29.2%, with one in three adults affected [5]. However, only 17.3% are aware that they have the disease, and more than half of these individuals are under Ministry of Health clinic follow-up [5]. According to the Malaysian Management of Hypertension Clinical Practice Guideline (CPG) 2018, controlled BP is defined as BP < 140/90 mmHg for all patients < 80 years old, < 150/90 mmHg for patients aged 80 and above, < 140/80 mmHg for patients with diabetes, and < 130/80 mmHg for patients with ischemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, or renal impairment [3]. Despite various measures to improve BP control, only 48% of Malaysians with hypertension achieve target BP levels [5].

Controlling BP remains challenging due to multiple contributing factors. Local studies have shown that medication adherence [6], the number of antihypertensives [7], and comorbid diabetes mellitus [6, 7] are associated with BP control. However, limited published data have examined the effect of HBPM on BP control in Malaysian primary care. Although HBPM is recommended in Malaysian guidelines, its use in the general population is low; the most recent National Health and Morbidity Survey (NHMS) 2023 reported that only 44.3% of hypertensive patients had a HBPM device at home [5]. Furthermore, a regional survey across Asia found that while 95.9% of physicians recommended HBPM, only 33.5% believed patients recognised or understood its importance [8], indicating a persistent knowledge and practice gap.

Previous studies have identified several barriers to HBPM implementation, including limited patient health literacy, financial constraints related to the cost of home BP devices, lack of structured patient education, and insufficient physician endorsement or reinforcement during clinic visits. In addition, inconsistent documentation and limited integration of HBPM readings into routine clinical decision-making further reduce its perceived value among patients. These barriers highlight the importance of examining the role of documented HBPM in blood pressure control within the Malaysian primary care setting.

Understanding the factors associated with controlled BP, particularly HBPM is essential for reducing hypertension-related morbidity and mortality in Malaysia. By identifying these factors, this study can contribute valuable insights into the barriers that hinder optimal hypertension management. Currently, there is limited evidence regarding documented HBPM practices prevelance and their association with blood pressure control among hypertensive patients in Malaysian primary care settings. This study aims to address this gap by determining the prevalence and factors associated with controlled BP among hypertensive patients attending Klinik Kesihatan Seri Tanjung, Melaka, Malaysia.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted from December 2024 to February 2025 to evaluate the prevalence and factors associated with controlled BP among hypertensive patients in primary care. Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Review and Ethics Committee (MREC), Ministry of Health Malaysia (NMRR ID: 2400402-OMQ [IIR], approved 24 May 2024). The University Putra Malaysia Ethical Committee (JKEUPM) was informed, and written permission to conduct the study at Klinik Kesihatan Seri Tanjung was granted by the Director of Jabatan Kesihatan Negeri Melaka (JKNM) on 5 December 2024. A systematic random sampling method was used to recruit the participants. During the data collection period, the first patient was selected using a generated computer random number from the name list. The patient was screened for the inclusion and exclusion criteria and invited to participate in this study. Subsequently, every third patient of the day was screened for the inclusion and exclusion criteria and invited to join until the target number of respondents was achieved. Participants completed a self-administered, structured questionnaire in Malay or English. The questionnaire consisted of four sections: (1) sociodemographic characteristics, (2) clinical characteristics, (3) Malaysia Medication Adherence Assessment Tool (MyMAAT), and (4) HBPM practice. Clinical measurements (weight, height, waist circumference, BP) were taken by trained healthcare personnel following Malaysian CPG 2018 protocols [3]. BP was measured twice, 5 minutes apart, and averaged. for HBPM readings, ≤135/85 mmHg was considered controlled. No therapeutic intervention was applied.

Setting and sample

The study was conducted at Klinik Kesihatan Seri Tanjung, a Type 3 government primary health clinic in Tanjung Kling, Melaka, Malaysia, serving approximately 35,169 residents with 300–350 daily patient attendances. Eligible participants were Malaysian adults aged ≥ 18 years with a diagnosis of hypertension who had been receiving antihypertensive treatment for at least six months. Patients who were pregnant, had severe cognitive impairment, or were acutely ill at the time of recruitment were excluded. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

The required sample size was calculated based on the estimated prevalence of controlled blood pressure among hypertensive patients in Malaysia (45%) reported in the National Health and Morbidity Survey (NHMS) 2019 [9] using a 95% confidence level and a precision of 5%. The minimum required sample size was 380 participants. After accounting for a 20% non-response and incomplete data rate, the target sample size was increased to 475 participants. A total of 475 eligible patients were approached, of whom 460 consented and completed the study, yielding a response rate of 96.8%.

Clinical measurement

Clinical measurements (weight, height, waist circumference, and average blood pressure) were performed by trained healthcare personnel on the same visit day before questionnaire completion. Weight (kg) was measured using a calibrated weighing scale; height (cm) and waist circumference (cm) were measured using a measuring tape positioned midway between the lowest rib margin and iliac crest. BP measurements were taken upon patient arrival at the vital sign counter. BP was measured with a calibrated electronic BP device (Omron, upper arm blood pressure monitor HEM-7180-AP) using an appropriately sized cuff, following standard pre-measurement protocols (no smoking, eating, caffeine, or exercise within 30 minutes; 5-minute rest; supported back and arm; legs uncrossed). BP was measured in both arms, with the higher reading taken as the first measurement. A second measurement was performed 5 minutes later on the same arm, and the two readings were averaged for the final BP value. The average duration for completing the questionnaire and clinical measurements was approximately 10 minutes. Blood pressure control status was determined using the average of two clinic blood pressure measurements taken 5 minutes apart. Controlled BP is defined as BP <140/90 mmHg for all patients <80 years old, <150/90 mmHg for patients aged 80 and above, <140/80 mmHg for patients with diabetes, and <130/80 mmHg for patients with ischemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, or renal impairment [3]. Controlled BP was categorized based on patients' co-morbidities as recommended by the Malaysian Clinical Practice Guideline. Clinic blood pressure measurements were used as the primary outcome measure for Blood pressure status. Documented home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) records, when available in the medical records, were reviewed as supplementary clinical information but were not used independently to classify blood pressure control status.

Medical record review

Medical records using a manual (paper-based) documentation system were reviewed for information on patients' co-morbidities, anti-hypertensive medication, and documented home blood pressure monitoring. HBPM refers to home blood pressure readings recorded in the patient’s medical record during routine follow-up for the past 1 year by healthcare providers based on patient-provided HBPM records.

Outcome measures

Clinic blood pressure measurements were used as the primary outcome measure for Blood pressure status categorised as controlled or uncontrolled. Using the average reading from 5-min intervals as their BP value, after at least 5 min of rest. A controlled BP was defined according to the Malaysian CPG 2018 [3]. Documented HBPM readings when available in the medical records, were reviewed as supplementary clinical information but were not used independently to classify blood pressure control status. Secondary outcomes included the influence of socio-demographic profile, clinical characteristics, medication adherence (assessed using the validated MyMAAT questionnaire), and the HBPM practice on the primary outcome.

Missing data handling

To ensure questionnaire completeness, completeness for questionnaire were done immediately after the participant finishes answering the questionnaire. Participants were asked to complete the questionnaire immediately if any missing responses. As a result, the final sample size for analysis was 460 respondents. No significant missing data were identified for the variables included in the final analysis; therefore, complete case analysis was performed.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Independent variables, including socio-demographic characteristics, clinical characteristics, medication adherence, and HBPM use, were summarised descriptively. Categorical variables were presented as frequency (n) and percentage (%). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean (standard deviation), while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range). The dependent variable, BP control, was categorised as controlled or uncontrolled based on the study’s operational definition. Associations between controlled BP and independent variables were first examined using simple logistic regression for univariate analysis. Variables with p < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multiple logistic regression (MLR) model to identify factors independently associated with controlled BP. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). All variables included in the final model demonstrated VIF values below 5 indicating no significant multicollinearity. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The final model demonstrated adequate fit. Final MLR results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Discussion

This study evaluated controlled BP and its associated factors among hypertensive patients in a primary care setting. The overall controlled BP rate was 41.5%, which is consistent with findings from other local and regional studies that reported control rates ranging between 45% and 54.9% [6, 7, 11]. Despite high levels of treatment and follow-up, more than half of the participants remained uncontrolled, reflecting the persistent challenge of achieving optimal BP management in routine practice.

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics were important determinants of controlled BP. Older age was positively associated with controlled BP, a finding that has been reported in previous studies [7, 12]. This may be due to greater health awareness, more frequent healthcare visits, or better treatment compliance among older individuals. Female patients were also significantly more likely to achieve BP control compared with males, consistent with other studies showing that women generally demonstrate better health-seeking behaviours and treatment adherence [13, 14].

Medication use was another strong factor. Patients prescribed one or two antihypertensive agents were more likely to achieve control compared to those on three or more medications, suggesting that individuals requiring multiple therapies may represent a more resistant hypertensive group [15, 16]. The presence of diabetes mellitus was associated with poore BP control, in line with existing evidence that comorbid diabetes complicates hypertension management and increases treatment burden [17, 18].

Medication adherence, assessed using the validated MyMAAT tool [10], was generally high, with 78.5% of participants classified as adherent. However, adherence was not significantly associated with controlled BP in this study, which contrasts with findings from other studies [19, 20]. The lack of association between medication adherence and controlled blood pressure may partly reflect overestimation of adherence arising from social desirability and recall biases inherent in self-reported measures. Furthermore, clinical inertia may contribute to this finding, whereby treatment regimens are not intensified despite uncontrolled blood pressure, resulting in persistent hypertension even among adherent patients. the influence of other factors such as comorbidities and treatment complexity also contributes to lack of association between medication adherence and controlled blood pressure. These highlights that adherence alone, while necessary, may not be sufficient to achieve optimal BP outcomes.

Consistent with previous Malaysian studies, blood pressure control remains suboptimal and is influenced by multiple patient- and system-level factors, including medication adherence, comorbidities, and healthcare delivery constraints. This study extends existing evidence by demonstrating that objectively documented HBPM practice is more strongly associated with controlled blood pressure compared to self-reported practice, suggesting that documented HBPM may be an indicator of clinically meaningful patient engagement and ongoing hypertension management in routine primary care.

HBPM practice showed a stronger association with controlled BP. Although only 30.0% of participants reported HBPM, and just 12.0% had verifiable documentation, those with documented HBPM were more than four times as likely to achieve controlled BP. This finding is consistent with international reporting an association between HBPM use and improved hypertension management, treatment adherence, and physician decision-making [8, 21, 22]. Documented HBPM practice showed a stronger association with blood pressure control than self-reported HBPM, suggesting that objective measures of monitoring behaviour may better reflect clinically meaningful engagement. This difference may be attributed to biases inherent in self-reported data, including recall and social desirability bias. In addition, documented HBPM may reflect not only patient adherence but also active clinician involvement, such as reviewing home blood pressure readings, reinforcing adherence, or adjusting treatment accordingly. As such, documented HBPM may capture both behavioural and system-level factors, that are associated with controlled blood pressure.

However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Given the cross-sectional nature of the study, the possibility of reverse causation and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Patients with documented HBPM may represent individuals who are more actively engaged in their healthcare, attend follow-up appointments more regularly, or receive greater clinician attention and support. These factors may independently contribute to better blood pressure control and could partially explain the observed association. Therefore, while documented HBPM was associated with controlled blood pressure in this study, causality cannot be inferred. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether documented HBPM directly contributes to improved blood pressure control.

Although the documented HBPM was highly significant, the confidence interval is notably wide (2.337–8.623), which might suggest lower precision due to the small size of the subgroup (n = 55).

Although working status, smoking and BMI showed associations in the univariate analysis, they were not significant after adjustment of confounders, consistent with previous local studies [6, 7]. The lack of association with adherence, despite its established role in BP control [23, 24], may reflect the high prevalence of reported adherence in this sample and possible overestimation due to self-report bias. Overall, the factors identified in this study—older age, female gender, fewer antihypertensive agents, absence of diabetes, and documented HBPM are consistent with established literature while also highlighting specific gaps in local practice. Importantly, the strong association observed between documented HBPM and controlled blood pressure highlight the need for further research to determine whether structured home monitoring programmes contribute to improved blood pressure control in Malaysian primary care settings.

These findings highlight potential areas for intervention, including structured HBPM programmes, optimisation of treatment regimens where clinically appropriate, and targeted strategies for groups with lower rates of controlled blood pressure, such as men and patients with diabetes. However, the effectiveness of these approaches cannot be determined from the present cross-sectional study. Future research should explore longitudinal and interventional approaches to confirm causality and evaluate strategies to increase controlled BP. Taken together; the results reinforce the need for comprehensive, patient-centred hypertension management in primary care settings.

Strength and limitations

This study was conducted in a primary health clinic setting, reflecting real-world clinical practice and patients’ behaviour. The findings may represent hypertensive patients attending follow-up care in a public primary care clinic and may not represent all hypertensive adults in the community. The Malaysian Medication Adherence Assessment Tool (MyMAAT) used is a validated tool developed locally in 2020 [10], culturally and contextually appropriate, enhancing accuracy and reliability. BP control was defined from the average of two measurements 5 min apart, with documented HBPM if available were reviewed for those with uncontrolled clinic BP—an approach rarely used. Self-reported and documented HBPM were analysed separately to give greater insight into self-care behaviour. Multiple logistic regression was applied to adjust for confounding variables, improving credibility [25].

However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not allow temporality to be established. Therefore, the identified factors may not have preceded blood pressure control. Second, selection bias may be present because participants were recruited from a single public primary care clinic and represent patients actively attending follow-up care rather than all hypertensive adults in the community. Consequently, the findings may be applicable to similar public primary care settings but may not be generalisable to all healthcare settings or populations in Malaysia. Third, measurement bias may have occurred because medication adherence and self-reported HBPM were assessed using self-reported data, which are subject to recall and social desirability bias. In addition, documented HBPM may have been subject to misclassification due to variations in the completeness and consistency of clinical documentation, and information regarding the timing and frequency of HBPM was not consistently available. Fourth, residual confounding cannot be excluded, as non-pharmacological factors such as dietary salt intake, physical activity, stress, sleep quality, health literacy, and motivation were not measured. Furthermore, some subgroup analyses involved small numbers of participants, particularly for Indian ethnicity, stroke history, and alcohol consumption, consequently, resulting in wider confidence intervals and potentially unstable estimates. Finally, because controlled blood pressure was a relatively common outcome (41.5%), the odds ratios generated from logistic regression may overestimate the magnitude of association compared with risk ratios. Therefore, the strength of the reported associations should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, logistic regression was retained as the primary analytical approach because it remains a widely accepted method for cross-sectional studies and facilitates comparison with previous hypertension research.

Conclusions

This study found that the prevalence of controlled blood pressure was 41.5%, which remains suboptimal and suggests that many hypertensive patients do not achieve recommended therapeutic targets. Factors associated with controlled blood pressure were older age, female sex, use of fewer antihypertensive medications, absence of diabetes mellitus, and documented HBPM practice. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions, particularly for male patients, those with multiple medications, and patients with diabetes. Given the observed association between documented HBPM and controlled blood pressure, further studies are warranted to explore the role of HBPM in hypertension management and to evaluate strategies that support accurate measurement and documentation in routine clinical practice. Where feasible, antihypertensive regimens should be simplified to improve adherence. Primary care should focus on personalised management plans, especially for patients with multiple comorbidities. At the policy level, initiatives that improve access to validated home blood pressure monitors and promote public awareness of hypertension self-management may warrant consideration, although further evidence from longitudinal and interventional studies is needed. Future multicentre longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to assess temporal relationships and determine whether factors identified in this study, particularly documented HBPM, have a causal role in achieving blood pressure control.
