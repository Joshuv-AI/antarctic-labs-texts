Ultrasonic assisted three-phase partitioning extraction and bioactivities validation of polysaccharides from Flammulina velutipes residues
Authors: Yao Zhang, Changjiao Zhong, Shiyu Liu, Linna Du, Hongxia Ma, Haipeng Zhang, Yuting Li, Chengguang He, Lili Guan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mushroom residues, especially Flammulina velutipes residues (FVR) rich in bioactive polysaccharides, are under exploited due to inefficient extraction methods conflicting with green chemistry. This study developed an ultrasonic-assisted three-phase partitioning (UAE-TPP) approach to extract crude polysaccharides (cTP-FVRP) from FVR, combined with structural and functional characterization. An integrated optimization strategy combining response surface methodology (RSM), artificial neural network (ANN), and genetic algorithm (GA) identified global optimum conditions (18 min, 1:1 crude extract to n-Butanol, 44 % (NH₄)₂SO₄), yielding cTP-FVRP at 3.49 ± 0.05 %-significantly higher than that of UAE method (2.38 ± 0.04 %). Purified TP-FVRP was a 108.13 kDa non-starch polysaccharide with a triple helix, α-linkages, and 9 monosaccharides, stable under physiological conditions. It exhibits potent antioxidant activity, biocompatibility, and immunomodulatory potential. UAE-TPP reduces solvents while preserving polysaccharide integrity. This green technique enables high-value utilization of FVR, aligning with sustainability goals and providing a scalable model for industrial by-product valorization.

Introduction

Flammulina velutipes residues (FVR) is a byproduct remaining after the large-scale cultivation of F. velutipes (Fig. S1). With the expansion of commercial production of this algae, such residual biomass has accumulated in considerable quantities, leading to serious environmental pollution and resource wastage (Li et al., 2021). The annual output of FVR exceeds 1 million tons (Liang et al., 2025), and currently, the utilization rate of FVR is still low. Processing farmland through incineration, disposal, or crushing can easily cause environmental pollution. Incineration, disposal, or crushing of FVR easily causes environmental pollution, yet it remains a valuable source of high-value raw materials (Ikeda et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2024). Reducing the generation of this type of waste with high potential value will significantly support the sustainable development of the food and agro-food industries (Liang et al., 2025). FVR is rich in protein, crude fat, polysaccharides, detergent fiber, calcium, phosphorus, etc. Flammulina velutipes residues polysaccharide (FVRP) is one of the main active ingredients of Flammulina velutipes residues, possesses multiple pharmacological activities, comprising immunomodulatory, antioxidant abilities, gut microbiota regulation, protective effects on heavy metal toxicity and diabetes nephropathy (Liu et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). The emphasis on waste FVRs has accelerated the continuous application of extraction methods for polysaccharides.

Current extraction methods for FVRP include hot water (HWE), ultrasonic (UAE), microwave (MAE), acid/alkaline, and enzyme-assisted extraction (Liu et al., 2022; Yang, Ren, et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024), face significant limitations: pre-treatments (drying, pulverization) incur high energy/equipment costs (Dong et al., 2016), while multi-step purification (alcohol precipitation, deproteinization) prolongs processing and reduces efficiency (Li et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022). HWE, for example, requires 2–4 h plus deproteinization, acid/alkaline methods risk polysaccharide degradation, and even UAE method demands 3–5 post-processing stages, extending total time beyond 8 h (Liu et al., 2022). These drawbacks drive the need for greener, streamlined alternatives. .

Three-phase partitioning (TPP) method has emerged as a sustainable solution for bioactive isolation (Wang & Li, 2022), integrating extraction, purification, and concentration via salt-organic solvent interactions that precipitate macromolecules at the aqueous-organic interface (Yan et al., 2018). This one-step process eliminates pulverization, decolorization, and deproteinization, qualifying as simple, time-efficient, and eco-friendly (Gagaoua, 2018). This simplicity reduces energy consumption by ∼40 % compared to multi-step methods and shortens processing time to <2 h (Tan & Lovrien, 1972), aligning with green chemistry principles. TPP has been applied to polysaccharides from rice bran (Wang et al., 2020), Inonotus obliquus (Liu et al., 2019), F. velutipes fruiting bodies (Wei et al., 2022). Phellinus baumii fermentation broth (Wei et al., 2022), and garlic bulbs (Wang et al., 2019). In summary, TPP technique is a simple, time efficient, economical, non-toxicity, and green approach for the separation of polysaccharides from FVR.

The current study pioneers ultrasonic-assisted three-phase partitioning (UAE-TPP) method for FVRP extraction, combining the cell-disruptive efficiency of UAE method with the streamlined purification of TPP method, and employs a hybrid response surface methodology (RSM)-artificial neural network (ANN) strategy to refine conditions, unlike prior TPP studies relying on single-parameter optimization (Wang et al., 2019). The purified TP-FVRP was also characterized via ultraviolet-visible (UV), fourier transform-infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), high-performance gel permeation chromatography (HPGPC), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and thermogravimetry (TG), and assess its biocompatibility, antioxidant and immunomodulatory activity. Novel contributions include: (1) UAE-TPP method reduces processing time and eliminates pre-treatments, addressing conventional method limitations; (2) RSM-ANN strategy enhances optimization precision versus single-method approaches; (3) comprehensive FVRP characterization fills critical gaps in the research on polysaccharides extracted by UAE-TPP method from mushroom residues. This work establishes a green framework for FVR valorization, supporting sustainable utilization of agricultural by-products.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

FVR were presented by Gaorong Biotechnological Co., Ltd. (Changchun, China). DEAE Sepharose Fast Flow column (HA-0550) was obtained from Rui Da Heng Hui Science &Technology Development Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH, B25609) was obtained from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS, A276045) was supplied by Aladdin Biotchnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Ascorbic acid (Vc, A8101), quercetin (SQ8030) and gallic acid (SG8040) were supplied by Solarbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The sterile defibrous sheep blood (JS16086) was purchased from Jvshi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Henan, China). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS, L2630) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Griess reagent (G4410) was supported by Beyotime Biotchnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) offer to detect the Oxide (NO) level. Interleukin-6 (IL-6, MU30044) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, MU30030) Elisa kit were supplied by Bioswamp Biotechnology Co. (Wuhan, China). All chemicals and reagents in the current study were analytical standard grade.

Preparation of extraction solution

FVR were precisely ground by a laboratory mill with a 0.25 mm mesh screen, which was degreased by 95 % ethanol at a ratio of 1: 5 (w/v) overnight. Degreased FVR was dried and mixed ultrapure water in a ratio of 1: 50 and extracted with 540 W of ultrasound at 75 °C for 20 min to obtain solution I.

Degreased FVR was mixed ultrapure water in a ratio of 1: 50 at 75 °C for 20 min to obtain solution II.

Screening of organic solvents

Polysaccharides were extracted from solution I by UAE-TPP method according to extraction process. Ethanol, Ethyl Acetate, Isobutanol, t-Butanol, and n-Butanol with the same condition that crude extract: organic solvents was 1:1, extraction time was 15 min, extraction temperature was 25 °C were used to detect the impact of various organic solvents in the three-phase system on the yield of polysaccharides from Flammulina velutipes residues. Extraction solution was mixed with (NH4)2SO4 (mass fraction 10 %–60 %), and shaken in a vortex shaker for 30 s at room temperature, then a certain amount of organic solvent was added. The mixture was shaken at a speed of 400 rpm for 15–90 min and then centrifuged (2700 g, 15 min) to obtain three clear phases. The upper organic phase were recovered through vacuum evaporation. The lower aqueous phase solution was concentrated to remove organic reagents using rotary evaporation. The yield (%) of polysaccharides from Flammulina velutipes residues was determined by the below formula:(a)Y%=CL×VLM×100%

Among them, M is the total mass of FVR powder, CL and VL are the concentration and volume of polysaccharides in the aqueous phase.

Optimization of the three-phase partitioning extraction condition

Single-factor experiment

For purpose of optimize the TPP extraction condition of polysaccharides from Flammulina velutipes residues, single-factor tests and RSM were designed. Crude extract: n-Butanol ratio was set 1:2, 1:1, 2:3, 2:1, 2:5, and 3:1, and the remaining factors were stationary as following: (NH4)2SO4 concentration of 40 %, time of 15 min, and temperature of 25 °C to screen the suitable crude extract: n-Butanol ratio. Time was set 2, 5, 10, 15, 30, 45, 60, 75 and 90 min, and the remaining conditions were fixed as follows: Crude extract: n-Butanol ratio of 1:1, (NH4)2SO4 concentration of 40 %, and temperature of 25 °C to screen the suitable extraction time. (NH4)2SO4 concentration was set 10 %, 20 %, 30 %, 40 %, and 50 %, and the remaining conditions were fixed as follows: Crude extract: n-Butanol ratio of 1:1, time of 15 min, and temperature of 25 °C to screen the (NH4)2SO4 concentration. Temperature was set 20 °C, 25 °C, 30 °C, and 35 °C, and the remaining conditions were fixed as follows: Crude extract: n-Butanol ratio of 1:1, time of 15 min, and (NH4)2SO4 concentration of 40 % to screen the suitable temperature. The yield was calculated as described in formula (a).

RSM analysis

A response surface experiment was designed using a 3 × 3 factorial design, with the mass fraction of (NH4)2SO4 (A, %), Crude extract: n-Butanol ratio (B), and temperature (C, °C) as unattached input variables, and the yield of crude polysaccharides from FVR as the response value. The optimized parameters was tested according to the Box-Behnken design with each variable coded at three levels (−1, 0, +1) (Table 1). ANN analyisis

Additionally, feedforward artificial neural network with backpropagation was chosen for the changing modeling of the TPP method based on MATLAB software (MATLAB R2009a, MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA), containing various networked artificial neurons and divided to three basic units (input layer, hidden layer, and output layer) (Xiao et al., 2020). Among them, 11 samples were selected for training, two samples were adopted for validation, and two samples were chosen for testing. In the current study, ANN was trained by the Levenberg-Marquardt backpropagation algorithm. Due to that the number of hidden nodes has a severe influence on the efficiency of the ANN model, the degree of approximation (Da) was selected to filter the best value of this number in accordance with the previous approach (Chen & Xue, 2018). The mathematical formula of Da was as follows:(b)Da=cnc/n×RMSEc+nt/n×RMSEt+∣RMSEc−RMSEt∣where n signifies the total number of the calibration set and test set, nc and nt are the number of the calibration set and test set, and RMSEc and RMSEt are the root-mean-square error (RMSE) of the validation set and test set, respectively, the constant c was set to 3.

Once an optimal ANN model was founded, GA was applied referring to the literature method to predict the maximum responding value and the best value for each factor (Meng et al., 2016). Experimental verification was carried out according to the optimal conditions of RSM and ANN model.

Extraction yield validation

Solution I obtained by Section 2.2 was divided into three parts to conducted the extraction process with the optimal conditions optimized by RSM and ANN, and ethanol precipitation and deproteinization. The corresponding solutions were named UTR, UTA, and UAE. Similarly, solution II obtained by Section 2.2 was also performed the same processes. The corresponding solutions were named HTR, HTA, and HWE. The yield was calculated as described in formula (a).

Purification of crude polysaccharides

The UTR solution was precipitated with the final concentration of ethanol of 75 %. The freeze-dried crude polysaccharide cTP-FVRP was obtained by dialysis, concentration and freeze-drying.

A 20 mg/mL of cTP-FVRP solution was prepared with ultrapure water, and further purified on DEAE Sepharose Fast Flow column (16 mm × 400 mm) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min with different concentration of NaCl. The main Peak 3 was be collected, purification was conducted again using Sephacryl S-400 column, named TP-FVRP.

Physicochemical properties identification

The contents of polysaccharides, uronic acids, proteins and phenols of cTP-FVRP and TP-FVRP were detected with phenol sulfuric acid method, Coomassie Brilliant Blue method, m-hydroxybiphenyl method and gallic acid method, severally (Nie et al., 2019).

Molecular weight (mw) identification

The Mw of TP-FVRP was detected by HPGPC equipped with Agilent 1260 Infinity ELSD evaporative light scattering detector, TSK-gel G-3000PWXL chromatographic column (7.8 × 300 nm), detailed in our former study (Li et al., 2022).

Determination of monosaccharide composition

TP-FVRP were degraded with trifluoroacetic acid (2 M) at 121 °C for 2 h, and dried with nitrogen, and then re-dissolved in ultrapure water and filtered through 0.22 μm microporous filtering film for further measurements. Then the dried sample was measured on Thermo ICS 5000+ ion chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with Dionex™ CarboPac™ PA20 column (150 × 3.0 mm, 10 μm). Quantified data were organized and analyzed.

Spectrometric analysis

UV spectroscopy analysis was conducted in the range of 200–400 nm by a full wavelength UV spectrophotometer (TECAN-Spark, Switzerland) to verify the existence of proteins and nucleic acids in TP-FVRP. TP-FVRP and KBr powder were pushed into thin sheets. Then, FT-IR was tested from the range of 4000 cm−1–400 cm−1 on a Nexus 470 FT-IR spectrometer (Equinx55, Brucher, Germany).

Helix-coil transition analysis of TP-FVRP

Congo red method is commonly selected for detect the helical conformations of polysaccharides (Nie et al., 2018). 1 mL of TP-FVRP solution (2 mg/mL) was blended with 1 mL of Congo Red solution (0.2 mM) and NaOH (1 M) with different concentration of NaOH from 0 to 0.5 M. TP-FVRP-Congo red melange was reacted at room temperature for 5 min, and the maximum absorption wavelength (λmax) was tested within the wavelength range of 400–700 nm on a UV spectrophotometer (TECAN-Spark, Switzerland).

I2-KI assay

2.0 mL of TP-FVRP (1.0 mg/mL) was add to 1.2 mL of iodine solution (0.2 % KI + 0.02 % I2), and tested by a Microplate Reader (TECAN-Spark, Switzerland) from 300 nm to 700 nm (Lv et al., 2024).

Thermal characteristics of TF-FVRP

The thermal characteristics of TP-FVRP were studied by differential scanning thermogravimetric analyses (TG), which were carried out in aluminium crucibles of 5 mm diameter heated from 20 °C to 800 °C on Simultaneous Thermal Analyzer TG/DSC1/1600LF (Mettler-Toledo, Switzerland) with a heating ramp of 10 °C/min, in a nitrogen atmosphere at a flow rate of 20 mL/min in TG assay. Savitzky-Golay smoothing algorithm was chosen for TG curves (Alpizar-Reyes et al., 2017).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The microstructures of TP-FVRP were recorded on a high resolution field emission scanning electron microscopy system (FE-SEM, SU8010) (Hitachi, Japan). The accelerating voltage was 10 kV with magnifications of 1.00k and 5.00k.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The crystal structures of TP-FVRP was identified by X-ray diffraction (Rigaku SmartLab SE, Japan). In a nutshell, the dried sample powder was uniformly placed on a glass plate and scanned at a rate of 2°/min over a 2θ range of 5–90°.

Determination of in vitro antioxidant abilities

The antioxidant ability of TP-FVRP against ABTS, DPPH and reducing power were measured. Vc was chosen as a positive control (Wang et al., 2018).

Haematocompatibility assay

Haematocompatibility assay was conducted with 1 % fresh sheep blood according to the reported protocol (Dong et al., 2019). PBS was a negative control, and 0.2 % Triton X-100 was a positive control. TP-FVRP with different concentration (1, 50, 100, 200, 400, 600 μg/mL) was mixed with blood cells at 1:1, and incubated in a 37 °C incubator for 1 h, and the absorbance was read at OD570 after centrifuged at 1000 g for 6 min.

In vitro immunomodulatory assay

In vitro immunomodulatory of TP-FVRP was determined pursuant to our previous method (Yang et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024). RAW264.7 cell was cultured at 37 °C in an incubator with 5 % CO2 in DMEM medium containing 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin, respectively. The cell viability was assessed by CCK-8 assays. 100 μL of cell dilution (1.5 × 104 cells/mL) was put into 96-well plates for 24 h and then treated with different concentration of FVRPs (6.25–400 μg/mL) for 24 h. LPS (2 μg/mL) was selected as positive control. The CCK-8 reagent was then added to 96-well plates for 1.5 h to measure the absorbance at 450 nm. Furthermore, the level of NO, IL-6, and TNF-α of RAW264.7 cells supernatant were detected according to the instructions.

Statistical analysis

All measured values were performed for three times, and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way ANOVA by SPSS 26.0 software were employed for comparing the significance of multiple samples and use t-test for statistical analysis between two sets of data.

Results and discussion

Optimal organic reagents of cTP-FVRP

Organic solvents not only affect the dielectric constant and polarity of organic solvents in the three-phase system, but also have an impact on the three-phase process. The stability of the system has a crucial impact. Therefore, appropriate organic solvents in the three-phase method exhibits a critical role. The yield of polysaccharides can be achieved 2.82 ± 0.01 % using n-Butanol as the organic medium for the three-phase system, while that of Ethanol, Ethyl Acetate, Isobutanol, and t-Butanol was 1.19 ± 0.04 %, 2.69 ± 0.05 %, 2.21 ± 0.02 %, 2.54 ± 0.03 %, respectively. n-Butanol produced significant higher yield than other organic solvents (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1A). The highest yield (1.83 %) of Inonotus obliquus polysaccharides was produced by t-Butanol as an organic solvent in a three-phase system (Liu et al., 2019), which was different from the current work. n-Butanol is applied as an organic solvent replacing t-Butanol in the three-phase system (Wu et al., 2024), possibly owing to that the molecular size and branched chain structure of n-Butanol prevent its penetration into the folding tertiary structure in proteins, and make the protein phase less susceptible to damage and thereby stabling the three-phase system. Therefore, n-Butanol was chosen as the solvent for the isolation and purification of polysaccharides from FVR by TPP method.Fig. 1Effect of (A) organic reagents, (B) crude extract: n-Butanol ratio, (C) extraction time, (D) (NH4)2SO4 concentration, (E) extraction temperature on the yield of cTP-FVRP. Analysis of single factor experiments

Crude extract: n-Butanol ratio, extraction time, (NH4)2SO4 concentration, and temperature were employed to evaluate the critical role on the yield of crude polysaccharides from FVR with TPP method. When the Crude extract: n-Butanol ratio was 1:1, the highest yield was 2.79 ± 0.09 %, and the yield decreased with time (Fig. 1B). The yield of crude polysaccharides from FVR was highest at 15 min (2.32 ± 0.02 %), and then the yield decreased (Fig. 1C), possibly due to the polysaccharide broken with the prolonged time (Chen et al., 2016). (NH4)2SO4 results in the proteins precipitation by salting out (Qiu et al., 2024). The concentration of polysaccharides in the middle phase emerged due to flocculation resulting from (NH4)2SO4, causing depressed yield of polysaccharides. However, lower concentration (NH4)2SO4 (10 %–30 %) induced lower the yield of polysaccharides owing to deficient modification of protein hydrophobic patch surfaces (Tabtimmai et al., 2024). The maximum yield of crude polysaccharides from FVR was 2.95 ± 0.05 % with 40 % (NH4)2SO4 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1D). A comparable outcome was observed in the extraction of polysaccharides from Chlorella spp. by the TPP method with the similar (NH4)2SO4 concentration (40 %) (Zhao et al., 2018). The phenomenon may be devoted to the fact that the increment in (NH4)2SO4 concentration causes the impaired formation of hydrogen bond network between low-phase polysaccharides and water molecules (Wang et al., 2020). Therefore, 40 % (w/v) (NH4)2SO4 was adopted as the optimal element for more examinations. Temperature is a key physical parameter influencing the yield of polysaccharides extracted by TPP method (Tabtimmai et al., 2024), which were affected the mass-transfer rate (Yan et al., 2017). The yield of polysaccharides exhibited no significant difference with increasing temperature from 20 °C to 35 °C (2.71 ± 0.13 %, 2.83 ± 0.09 %, 2.77 ± 0.03 %, 2.67 ± 0.10 %, respectively) (p > 0.05) (Fig. 1E), which was higher than that of polysaccharides from Schizophyllum commune by TPP method (Tabtimmai et al., 2024). However, higher temperature will cause more energy consumption. The temperature at 30 °C for the TPP system was also selected by Tan et al. (Tan et al., 2015), leading to the optimal yield of levan and aloe polysaccharide. Hence, 30 °C was chosen for further experiments.

To evaluate the extraction efficacy of polysaccharides from FVR under various processing conditions, a series of systematic experiments were performed, with outcomes detailed in Fig. S2. In UAE approaches, the polysaccharide yields obtained via the UAE-TPP method (designated as UTR and UTA) were 3.15 ± 0.43 % and 3.49 ± 0.05 %, respectively. These values not only surpassed those acquired by conventional UAE (2.34 ± 0.04 %) but also indicated that the integration of a RSM-ANN model further enhanced polysaccharide recovery. Under non-ultrasonication conditions, the extraction yields for HTR, HTA, and HWE were 1.91 ± 0.10 %, 2.10 ± 0.05 %, and 1.87 ± 0.32 %, respectively, revealing that the TPP technique yield higher polysaccharide quantities relative to the classical HWE method. Overall, these findings indicate that UAE-based strategies generally promote superior extraction efficiency for FVR polysaccharides. Among these, the UTA protocol combining UAE with TPP achieved the maximal yield, whereas the conventional HWE resulted in the lowest polysaccharide recovery. This outcome highlights the significant contribution of ultrasonic treatment in enhancing polysaccharide extraction, attributable to its acoustic cavitation effect, which disrupts the structural integrity of FVR cell walls and facilitates improved solvent permeation and mass transfer within the cellular matrix (Liu et al., 2025). Furthermore, TPP method functions as a pivotal yield-enhancing adjuvant, leveraging a multiphase synergistic mechanism that not only impairs the structural integrity of FVR cell walls but also promotes the dissolution and isolation of target polysaccharides (Zhou et al., 2025). Hence, the exceptional extraction performance of UAE-TPP method was attributed not only to ultrasound-induced cavitation but also to the TPP-mediated three-phase system, which effectively removes impurities (e.g., proteins) during extraction. This dual action not only elevates the dissolution efficiency of polysaccharides, but also improves their final purity, thereby further highlighting the distinct advantages of the TPP-integrated strategy for polysaccharides extracted from FVR.

The above results implied that artificial intelligence-based methods were viable and stable for the extraction optimization of FVR by TPP method. Furthermore, ANN showed a better fit and prediction compared to RSM, which was in line with the prior literature (Ameer et al., 2017). Hence, extraction time of 18 min, (NH4)2SO4 concentration of 44 %, crude extract: n-Butanol ratio of 1:1 were confirmed to extract cTP-FVRP for the following investigation.

The absorption of TP-FVRP at wavelengths 260 nm and 280 nm was recorded by UV spectroscopic scanning method (Fig. 4D). Mw of TP-FVRP

The Mw of the TP-FVRPs was determined by HPGPC. cTP-FVRP displayed single and symmetrical chromatographic peaks (Fig. 4A). Moreover, the Mw values of cTP-FVRP was calculated to be approximately 330.58 kDa, which had no difference with that of UAE method (335.30 kDa) (Liu et al., 2022). TPP method had no dramatic impact on polysaccharides, which was identical to ScP1 (Tabtimmai et al., 2024). In addition, the molecular weight distribution result indicates that pure TP-FVRP exhibited a single symmetrical peak (Fig. 4C), and the Mw of TP-FVRP was 108.13 kDa. Therefore, TP-FVRP was proposed to be a homogeneous polysaccharide, which was used for the following study.

Different extraction methods have little influence on the monosaccharides types,

but they do change the proportion of monosaccharides, giving them different physical and chemical properties and bioactivity (Chen et al., 2020). TP-FVRP was composed of galactose (Gal, 32.9 %), mannose (Man, 13.5 %), glucose (Glc, 12.6 %), galactonic acid (GalA, 11.1 %), xylose (Xyl, 9.4 %), arabinose (Ara, 8.6 %), rhamnose (Rha, 4.8 %), fucose (Fuc, 3.7 %), and glucuronic acid (GlcA, 3.4 %) (Fig. 4E and Table S1), implying TP-FVRP was a complex polysaccharide, with Gal being the major monosaccharide, followed by Man. This result was different from that of FVRP with Xyl being the main monosaccharide, followed by Man (Liu et al., 2022).

FT-IR result

The FT-IR of TP-FVRP showed a broad and sharp characteristic peak of hydroxyl (—OH) at about 3421 cm−1 (Fig. 4F) (Wang et al., 2025), the peak at 2922 cm−1 may be due to the existence of C—H and aliphatic C—H bonds, and the wide absorption peak of about 1418 cm−1 was likely appeared by the deformation vibration of carbon‑hydrogen bonds. There were two absorption peaks in the range of 1080–1040 cm−1 and an absorption peak at 809 cm−1 imply that the glycan residues of TP-FVRP contained furanose rings and α-type glycosidic bonds (Mondal et al., 2025). The above result was similar with that of FVRP (Liu et al., 2022), clarifying that FVRP and TP-FVRP possessed α-type glycosidic bonds and furanose rings. The above data further confirmed TPP method did not destroy the polysaccharides structure, and is a green and mild approach for the extraction of polysaccharides.

SME of TP-FVRP

The surface morphology of the TP-FVRP was recorded by SEM. Freeze-drying-induced intermolecular interactions and van der Waals forces among polysaccharides significantly affect their final particle and aggregation morphologies, with polysaccharide chain length and molecular structure being key determinants of such intermolecular forces (Wang et al., 2021). TP-FVRP exhibited a thin and irregularly shaped morphology with varying sizes at low magnification (1.00 k), and there were also pores distributed on the thin and unsmooth films (Fig. 5). Furthermore, numerous fine pore like cracks were observed at a higher magnification (5.00 k), which was not obvious at low magnification due to the pore structure not penetrating the thin film. The morphology is likely attributed to the saturation level of (NH4)2SO4 within the range of 30 %–50 %, which induced the formation of a more permeable network structure (Wang et al., 2020). I2-KI assay

TP-FVRP and iodine potassium iodide reagent were mixed and reacted before undergoing UV visible spectral scanning. Polysaccharides exhibited a maximum absorption peak at 340 nm, whereas no absorption peak was detected at 565 nm (Fig. 6B), implying that TP-FVRP possessed a structure with complex chains comprising longer and more branched chains (Zhu et al., 2020). The reaction solution still had the color of I2-KI, implying TP-FVRP was a non-starch polysaccharide.

Thermal stability determination of TP-FVRP

TG has characteristics of simple and accurate and are usually employed for testing the thermal stability of polysaccharides (Xu et al., 2023). The thermal stability of polysaccharides are linked to the composition, water content, and aggregated structure (Chen & Xue, 2018). TG result was shown in Fig. 6C. The maximum loss of TP-FVRP was achieved −16.35 %/min at 94 °C, mainly because of the loss of physically adsorbed moisture. Furthermore, the maximum mass loss rate of TP-FVRP was 13.83 %/min, when the temperature rises to 317 °C, displaying that TP-FVRP was extensively degraded and further cracked to generate a large amount of CO2 and water vapor. Finally, the residual mass of TP-FVRP was 46.19 %, implying TP-FVRP may have strong thermal stability.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

XRD is a mighty technology used for the evaluation of the crystalline structure of materials. The XRD patterns of TP-FVRP were between 5° and 90° with broad and strong crystalline diffraction peaks (Fig. 6D), showing high overall crystalline, as “bun-shaped” X-ray diffraction curves, which was identical to the former research (Qian et al., 2024). The higher crystalline promotes the mechanical characterastics of polysaccharides, it can serve as a valuable resource for the fabrication of hydrogels, which are applicable in sequential drug delivery systems and tissue engineering applications (Chen et al., 2022). Hence, the current result enlightens a new application direction that TP-FVRP possessed the potential for the hydrogels preparation for drug delivery.

In vitro immunomodulatory ability of TP-FVRP

In most cases, the activation of macrophages leads to the induction of nitric oxide (NO) production (Huo et al., 2020). Thus, the effect of TP-FVRP on macrophages can be evaluated by NO level. The NO production of RAW264.7 cells treated by TP-FVRP and FVRP (6.25–400 μg/mL) were dose-dependently increased as depicted in Fig. 8C–D. Furthermore, FVRPs significantly improved relative phagocytosis in RAW264.7 cells compared with the CON group in a dose dependent manner (Fig. 8E–F). In addition, the promotional effect observed in the 200 μg/mL TP-FVRP group (170.41 ± 1.73 %) was significantly higher to that in the 400 μg/mL group (155.62 ± 0.01 %), which was different from that the promotion effect observed in the 400 μg/mL FVRP group (140.11 ± 1.47 %) was significantly higher to that in the 200 μg/mL group (150.34 ± 0.41 %). High concentration of polysaccharide was reported to restrict the relative phagocytosis in RAW264.7 cells by reducing its relative enzyme activities (Chen et al., 2024).

As reported by Pires et al. (Pires et al., 2021), cytokines are regulatory proteins that play a crucial role in modulating the immune system and inflammation. In the context of the immune response, IL-6 and tumor necrosis TNF-α facilitate macrophage activation and antigen presentation, and they modulate immunity through distinct mechanisms (Wang & He, 2020), which are also important indicators of immunomodulatory effect (Zeng et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 8G-H, LPS significantly stimulated the secretion of TNF-α (23.00 ± 0.52 pg/mL) and IL-6 (193.79 pg/mL) in RAW264.7 cells in comparison with the CON group (p < 0.05). The highest secretion of TNF-α (16.97 ± 0.93 pg/mL) was observed by TP-FVRP (200 μg/mL), which was 1.84-fold higher than that of the CON group. With the gradual increase in TP-FVRP concentration, the secretion of IL-6 shows a trend of first increasing and then decreasing. Moreover, the highest secretion of IL-6 was 167.08 ± 2.59 pg/mL induced by 50 μg/mL TP-FVRP, which was 1.17 times higher than that of the CON group. The prior outcomes were in line with higher secretion of cytokines induced by soybean extracts and low molecular weight peptides of Mytilus coruscus, similar to NO, indicates the immunomodulatory effects of peptides (He et al., 2021). Hence, TP-FVRP had higher activity on regulating immunity than FVRP, indicating it can be a potential immunoenhancer.

The results provides the direction and theoretical basis for the development of FVRP and TP-FVRP as feed additives with immune enhancing effects. Of course, the current work still have many shortcomings in this work. In the future, more in-depth work on novel biological activities of TP-FVRP, especially the immunomodulatory mechanism will be explored using various technical means, such as in vitro and in vivo animal experiments, and multi-omics analysis (microbiomics, transcriptomics, proteomics and metabolomics).

Conclusions

This study demonstrates TPP as a uniquely advantageous method for extracting polysaccharides from Flammulina velutipes residues (FVR), addressing critical limitations of conventional techniques. Unlike methods requiring energy-intensive pre-treatments (e.g., drying) and multi-step purification, TPP integrates extraction and purification into one step, perfectly suited to the high-moisture, lignocellulosic matrix of FVR. This efficiency directly mitigates FVR's environmental burden-massive annual volumes cause landfill accumulation and emissions-while enabling resource recovery. The method was optimized via a hybrid RSM-ANN strategy, and achieved a crude yield of 3.49 ± 0.05 % under extraction conditions of 44 % (NH₄)₂SO₄, 1:1 extract: n-Butanol ratio, and 18 min. Purified TP-FVRP (108.13 kDa) was a novel non-starch polysaccharide with a triple helix structure, α-glycosidic bonds, and a 9-monosaccharide profile, exhibiting thermal stability and a porous surface. Functional assays confirmed potent antioxidant activity (DPPH/ABTS scavenging), good biocompatibility, and dose-dependent immunomodulatory effects (enhanced RAW264.7 proliferation, phagocytosis, and NO/TNF-α/IL-6 secretion). The novelty of TPP method resides in its tailored compatibility with FVR, as its salt-solvent system efficiently disrupts fibrous matrices while preserving polysaccharide structural integrity; this represents a key advantage over techniques like UAE method. This work bridges waste management and high-value utilization, supporting FVR's valorization in functional foods, pharmaceuticals, and feed additives, with future studies focusing on immunomodulatory mechanisms.
