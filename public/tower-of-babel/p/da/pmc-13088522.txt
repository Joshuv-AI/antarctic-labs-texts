Influence of BDNF rs10835210 on right parahippocampal volume and its related cognitive function in first- and multiple-episode bipolar disorder
Authors: Ruilan Yang, Jianshan Chen, Tianlang Ke, Xiaofei Zhang, Xiaofang Cheng, Qiuxia Wu, Jiaqi Sun, Wenhao Deng, Xiongchao Cheng, Wenjin Zou, Yizhi Zhang, Chanjuan Yang, Liqian Cui, Biyu Ye, Xuan Li, Liping Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) polymorphism rs10835210 has been implicated in bipolar disorder, but its effects on brain morphology and cognition remain unclear.MethodsA total of 43 first-episode mania patients (FEM), 110 multiple-episode mania patients (MEM) and 80 healthy controls were enrolled in our study. We investigated the impact of BDNF rs10835210 on hippocampal and parahippocampal volumes, and also the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery.ResultsWe found a significant interaction between BDNF genotype and diagnosis affecting the volume of the right parahippocampus as well as cognitive measures of memory and attention functions. Specifically, risk A allele carriers exhibited reduced right parahippocampal volumes in both FEM (p < 0.001) and MEM (p = 0.044) groups; however, the risk A allele was associated with declines in memory and attention functions exclusively in FEM patients (p < 0.05). Furthermore, we demonstrated that lower right parahippocampal volume was correlated with worse attention function in A allele carriers among FEM (r = 0.341, p = 0.025).ConclusionsThis is the first study to demonstrate that BDNF rs10835210 is linked to right parahippocampal volume reductions across illness stages and to parahippocampus-dependent cognitive deficits specifically in FEM. These findings highlight rs10835210 as a candidate marker of neuroimaging alterations and early cognitive dysfunction in bipolar disorder.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-07949-7.Keywords: Bipolar disorder, First episode, BDNF, Rs10835210, Parahippocampal volume, Cognitive function

Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a severe psychiatric disease and known to be substantially influenced by genetic factors [1]. Among candidate genes, the brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) gene is particularly important due to its crucial roles in neurogenesis, development, survival and maturation of cells, synaptic plasticity and memory consolidation [2–4]. These neurotrophic functions position BDNF as a key mediator connecting genetic vulnerability with clinical manifestations, while brain morphology and cognitive function serve as critical intermediate phenotypes that may elucidate the genetic mechanisms underlying BD [5, 6].

The hippocampus and parahippocampus, as core components of the fronto-limbic circuitry, play a pivotal role in emotion regulation [7–9]. These regions form a functional complex, because they are tightly connected, and the parahippocampus serves as a funnel for cortical input into the hippocampus [10]. A number of studies have linked the BDNF rs6265 (val/met) polymorphism to structural/functional abnormalities of the hippocampus and parahippocampus. Individuals carrying the Met al.lele typically exhibit reduced hippocampal volumes and decreased gray matter density in the parahippocampus [11–13]. These structural alterations are accompanied by marked cognitive impairments. Our previous findings demonstrated that BD patients with the Met al.lele showed poorer performance on memory tests compared to healthy controls (HC) and patients with major depression [14]. Similarly, the Met variant of BDNF Val66Met has a significant effect on memory performance, and on both the structure and physiology of the hippocampus [15], as well as worse delayed memory performance in diabetic patients [16]. These findings collectively suggest that BDNF genetic variations may influence hippocampal and parahippocampal morphology and cognitive function across various psychiatric disorders and healthy populations.

Recently, another important BDNF polymorphism, rs10835210, has garnered increasing research attention. This intronic variant has shown significant associations with multiple psychiatric disorders, including BD [17], attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) [18], alcoholism [19], schizophrenia [20] and internalizing disorders [21]. Furthermore, BDNF rs10835210 has been linked to cognitive performance in healthy Polish subjects [22]. While its precise molecular mechanism requires further clarification, the role of non-coding variation in regulating BDNF expression is well-established. Genetic variants in non-coding regions of the BDNF locus can modulate its brain expression [23], with specific 3‘UTR variants altering expression via mechanisms like disrupted microRNA-mediated regulation [24]. Notably, emerging evidence links the risk A allele of rs10835210 to enhanced transcription of BDNF antisense RNA (BDNF-AS), which suppresses BDNF mRNA and protein synthesis [25]. Given the essential role of BDNF in hippocampal synaptic plasticity, such allele-specific downregulation represents a plausible mechanism that could contribute to alterations in brain morphology and cognition. However, it remains unknown whether BDNF rs10835210 specifically affects hippocampal and parahippocampal structure and related cognitive performance in patients with bipolar disorder, warranting further investigation.

Moreover, repetitive manic episodes in BD patients is associated with reduced brain morphology and poor neurocognitive performance [26, 27], which suggests a neuropsychological impact of manic episodes. However, none of the previous genetic neuroimaging studies have been carried out in first-episode mania patients (FEM), whether disease stage moderates the effects of rs10835210 on brain and cognition is unclear.

Therefore, in the present study, we recruited three well-characterized groups of subjects: FEM, multiple-episode mania patients (MEM) and HC. We investigated the impact of BDNF polymorphisms rs10835210 on hippocampal and parahippocampal volumes, as well as cognitive function performance including memory, attention and executive function among the three groups. We hypothesized that the BDNF polymorphisms rs10835210 would be associated with abnormalities in hippocampal and parahippocampal volumes, as well as related cognitive performance in FEM and MEM.

Methods

Study participants

The current study included three cohorts of participants: FEM, MEM, and HC. Patients were recruited from the outpatient clinic and inpatient department of the Affiliated Brain Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University, China. The HC were recruited through a media advertisement in the local paper. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards at the Affiliated Brain Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University, and we obtained written informed consent from all participants. The Structured Clinical Interview for the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-IV (DSM-IV) Axis I Disorders was used to confirm manic episodes in BD patients [28], and no lifetime axis I psychiatric diagnosis in HC groups. The FEM group included patients with only one lifetime manic episode. The MEM group was defined as individuals with a history of two or more lifetime mood episodes of either manic or hypomanic severity. Participants were excluded based on the following conditions: contraindication of MRI examination, neurological disease, a history of head injury with loss of consciousness, or somatic disorders affecting brain function. For the HC, exclusion criteria also included a history of mental health and neurological diseases, current use of psychiatric medication, or a familial history of mental disease. BD patients were also excluded if they had any other axis I disorder. We assessed patients on the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAM-D) [29], Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) [30], and also illness, duration, diagnostic subtypes, and age of illness onset. We also obtained information on patients’ gender, age, years of education, and body mass index (BMI).

Genotyping

The phenol-chloroform method was used to obtain DNA samples from blood cells [31]. Typing for BDNF rs10835210 was performed using the MassARRAY Assay (Sequenom, California, USA). As A-allele in rs10835210 is thought to be a risk factor for BD, participants were further divided into two groups: a risk A-carrier group (participants with AA or AC genotypes) and a CC homozygotes group (participants carrying CC genotype).

MRI acquisition and Image processing

High resolution T1-weighted whole-brain images were acquired on a 3.0 Tesla MRI system (Achieva X-series, Philips Medical Systems, Best, Netherlands) in the department of radiology at the Affiliated Brain Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University within 3 days after clinical assessment. Images were collected using a sagittal T1-weighted 3-dimensional (3-D) turbo field echo (T1W 3D TFE) sequence (field of view 256 × 256 mm2; repetition time 8.2 ms; echo time 3.8 ms; view matrix 256 × 256; slice thickness 1 mm, number of slices = 188).

Structural MRI data were processed using FreeSurfer version 6.0 (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) [32–38] with the standard fully automated recon-all processing pipeline. This pipeline performs a series of predefined processing steps, including conversion of raw MRI data into FreeSurfer-compatible format, motion correction, removal of non-brain tissue using a hybrid watershed and surface deformation algorithm, automated Talairach transformation, intensity normalization, segmentation of subcortical white matter and deep gray matter structures, tessellation of the gray/white matter boundary, automated topology correction, and surface deformation to optimally place the gray/white and pial surfaces, followed by cortical parcellation and volumetric labeling. All processing steps were performed using default parameters without manual intervention. Hippocampal volumes were extracted from the automated subcortical segmentation output file (aseg stats), and parahippocampal volumes were obtained from the cortical parcellation statistics file (aparc.stats) based on the Desikan–Killiany atlas. Volumes were extracted separately for the left and right hemispheres and used for subsequent statistical analyses.

To ensure reproducibility and anatomical accuracy, all segmentation outputs were visually inspected using Freeview. Segmentations were considered unsatisfactory if they showed issues like gross errors in skull stripping or mislabeling of major anatomical structures. In such cases, the dataset was reprocessed using the standard pipeline; if segmentation errors persisted, the subject was excluded from subsequent analyses.

Cognitive assessment

Our study investigated the impact of BDNF rs10835210 on the hippocampus and parahippocampus. Since the hippocampus and parahippocampus are thought to be associated with memory, attention and executive function. In the present study, we measured memory, attention and executive function using four tests from the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB) (Table S1) [39]. The details are listed as follows:

A.Executive function: Spatial working memory (SWM): this task measured subject’s ability in executive function. The measurements extracted were SWM total errors and SWM strategy.

Executive function: Spatial working memory (SWM): this task measured subject’s ability in executive function. The measurements extracted were SWM total errors and SWM strategy.

B.Memory tests: (1) Pattern recognition memory (PRM): this test was used for visual recognition memory. The measurements of interest were PRM percent correct immediate and PRM percent correct delayed. (2) Delayed matching to sample (DMS): this task tested visual memory. We measured DMS total correct and DMS total correct all delays for the next step of analysis.

Memory tests: (1) Pattern recognition memory (PRM): this test was used for visual recognition memory. The measurements of interest were PRM percent correct immediate and PRM percent correct delayed. (2) Delayed matching to sample (DMS): this task tested visual memory. We measured DMS total correct and DMS total correct all delays for the next step of analysis.

C.Attention test: Rapid visual information processing (RVP): used as a visual continuous performance task for assessing attention. Indices that were used included total false alarms, mean latency, and probability of hit.

Attention test: Rapid visual information processing (RVP): used as a visual continuous performance task for assessing attention. Indices that were used included total false alarms, mean latency, and probability of hit.

All CANTAB tests were conducted in quiet rooms following the standardized procedures provided by the manufacturer. This procedure included providing standardized screen prompts and mandatory practice sessions for each module before the formal recording of the test, to ensure that participants fully understood the task requirements.

Statistical analyses

All analyses of the data were conducted with Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS v.22.0, SPSS Inc, Chicago, Illinois). Demographic clinical variables were calculated between the BD and HC groups by using analysis of variance (ANOVA) and chi-square. Differences in bilateral hippocampal and parahippocampal volumes (4 regions) and cognitive function (9 measures) among FEM, MEM, and HC groups were assessed using 2 × 3 ANOVAs with diagnosis (FEM/MEM/HC) and genotype (A-allele carriers/CC) as between-subject factors. Significant differences in years of education were observed among the groups. To minimize the potential confounding influence of this variable on cognitive performance, years of education was included as a covariate in all relevant statistical models. Volumetric and cognitive analyses controlled for age, sex, illness duration, and number of prior mood episodes. Intracranial volume (ICV) was added as a covariate for volumetric analyses, and education for cognitive analyses. In the present study, main effects of diagnosis on volumetric and cognitive measures were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method. Given the exploratory nature of this investigation and the scarcity of prior evidence regarding the moderating role of BDNF rs10835210 in illness stage-specific brain-cognitive relationships in bipolar disorder, we prioritized minimizing false negatives (Type II errors) to allow for the detection of potential interaction effects, which may inform and guide future hypothesis-driven studies [40]. Therefore we reported uncorrected p-values for the genotype × diagnosis interaction analyses. Then we used the partial correlation analysis to assess the relationship between right parahippocampal volume and cognitive function across different genotype group, controlling for age, gender, ICV, and education.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The participants of our study included 80 HC, 43 FEM and 110 MEM. All patients in the FEM group were diagnosed with BD-I. In the MEM group, the vast majority were also BD-I, with four patients diagnosed with BD-II. Demographic characteristics are shown in Table 1. We found no significant difference in gender, age, BDNF genotype distributions among the FEM, MEM, and HC (p > 0.05). HAM-D scores and YMRS scores showed no significant difference between FEM and patients with MEM (p > 0.05). As expected, the MEM group had a longer illness duration (p < 0.05) and more previous mood episodes (p < 0.05) compared to the FEM group. Years of education differed across groups, with fewer years in both patient groups compared to HC (p < 0.05). Regarding medication use, a comparison between FEM and MEM groups revealed a significant difference only in the proportion of patients using antidepressants (p < 0.05). No significant differences were found for lithium, anticonvulsants, antipsychotics, or anxiolytics (all p > 0.05) (Table 1).

There were no significant differences in volumetric measurements between the FEM and MEM groups. We found no main effect of genotypes.

Cognitive performance

Cognitive performance differed significantly among the three groups after controlling for covariates. Following multiple comparison correction, significant group differences were observed for PRM percent correct delayed, DMS total correct, DMS total correct all delays, and RVP probability of hit, with both FEM and MEM groups exhibiting poorer performance compared to HC (all adjusted p < 0.05) (Table 3). For SWM strategy, only the MEM group demonstrated significant impairment relative to HC after correction (adjusted p < 0.05). The remaining measures, including PRM percent correct immediate, SWM total errors, RVP total false alarms, and RVP mean latency, showed no significant between-group differences following adjustment (all adjusted p > 0.05).

Differential effects of BDNF genotype on right parahippocampus-cognition relationships

Building upon our findings that FEM patients carrying the A allele showed both reduced right parahippocampal volume and the most severe cognitive impairment, we specifically examined how genotype modulates structure-cognition relationships in early-stage illness by analyzing FEM and HC subgroups. In A allele carriers, right parahippocampal volume positively correlated with attention performance (RVP probability of hit: r = 0.341, p = 0.025, df = 41), while no significant associations emerged for memory measures (all p > 0.05) or in CC homozygotes (all p > 0.05), highlighting the A allele-specific nature of these structure-function relationships (Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analyses

To examine the potential influence of diagnostic heterogeneity, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding the four patients with BD-II from the MEM group and re-ran all primary statistical models for neuroimaging and cognitive data. The results of this sensitivity analysis were consistent with the primary analyses. Specifically, the significant BDNF genotype × diagnosis interactions on right parahippocampal volume (F = 3.972, p = 0.020) and on key cognitive measures (DMS total correct all delays: F = 4.321, p = 0.015; DMS total correct: F = 3.408, p = 0.036; RVP probability of hit: F = 3.835, p = 0.024) remained robust. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Figs. S1 & S2.

Discussion

This study is the first to investigate the effects of BDNF rs10835210 on neuroanatomical and cognitive profiles in FEM, MEM, and HC. Key findings revealed: (1) After controlling for illness duration and episode number, the main effect of diagnosis on hippocampal and parahippocampal volumes did not survive multiple comparison correction; (2) Both patient groups exhibited cognitive deficits in memory and attention domains; (3) BDNF rs10835210 A allele in FEM and MEM patient groups is associated with smaller right parahippocampal volume; (4) FEM A allele carriers showed disproportionately severe memory and attention deficits compared to other groups; and (5) Right parahippocampal volume correlated with attention performance specifically in FEM A allele carriers.

Hippocampal and parahippocampal morphology, and cognitive dysfunction among FEM and MEM

After controlling for illness duration and episode number, the main effect of diagnosis on hippocampal and parahippocampal volumes did not survive multiple comparison correction. This suggests that a substantial portion of the volumetric differences commonly reported between BD patients and healthy individuals may be attributable to the cumulative burden of the illness itself, aligning with the neuroprogression hypothesis. The observed data trends, where reductions in right hippocampal volume were evident in both patient groups, and bilateral parahippocampal reductions appeared more pronounced in FEM. This is consistent with prior literature documenting early morphological alterations in BD [41, 42] and right hippocampal deficits across stages [14, 43, 44]. One possible explanation is that long-term lithium treatment more common in MEM than FEM, has been associated with increased brain volume [45]. These observations suggest that parahippocampal morphology may vary across the course of BD, with bilateral parahippocampal volume reductions specifically evident in FEM. Thus, the parahippocampus may represent a potential neuroanatomical marker of early-stage BD, although its role in later illness stages requires further clarification.

FEM and MEM both had worse performance on memory, attention and executive function compared to HC, and these results are in line with previous studies [46]. It was reported that FEM were significantly impaired on tests of attention and executive function compared to normal control [47, 48], and a systematic review for cognitive impairment in first-episode mania revealed that the most consistent finding was a deficit in working memory during the remission phase [49]. Additionally, MEM were reported to be more impaired on verbal memory, attention, and verbal fluency tasks than HC [50]. Furthermore, consistent with these findings, we also observed that MEM patients displayed more extensive cognitive deficits on executive function compared to FEM. Manic episodes were found to be a predictor for poor cognitive outcomes in BD [51]. Indeed, two previous studies reported that multiple-episode BD patients had worse cognitive outcomes than first-episode BD patients and HC [52, 53]. Together, cognitive deficits might appear at the early stage of BD, and decline along with the recurrence of mania.

The impact of BDNF polymorphism rs10835210 on right parahippocampal volume in BD patients and HC

We found that reduced right parahippocampal volume is significantly correlated with BDNF rs10835210 A allele in FEM and MEM patients. Specifically, among A allele carriers, both FEM and MEM patients exhibited smaller volumes compared to HC, while no group differences were found in CC homozygotes. This differential susceptibility pattern suggests that the A allele may confer specific vulnerability to disease-related structural alterations in the right parahippocampus.

The precise molecular pathways underlying these structural effects are not yet established. The discussion that follows outlines a plausible, albeit unverified, mechanistic hypothesis based on extrapolation from related research. Nevertheless, based on extant literature, a parsimonious explanation can be posited: at the molecular level, emerging evidence indicates that the rs10835210 A allele, as an intronic variant, may regulate BDNF expression through non-coding mechanisms, particularly by enhancing transcription of BDNF antisense RNA (BDNF-AS) which suppresses BDNF mRNA expression and reduces protein synthesis, as demonstrated in prefrontal cortical tissue [25]. Given the parahippocampal region’s characteristically high BDNF expression and its critical dependence on neurotrophic support for maintaining structural integrity [54], we propose that A allele carriers likely experience compromised BDNF signaling that may be further exacerbated by disease-related neuroinflammatory processes [55] and environmental stressors [56], collectively contributing to the observed volumetric reductions specifically in patient populations. However, we did not measure inflammatory markers or stress exposures, so this pathway remains conjectural. The absence of structural differences among CC homozygotes regardless of diagnostic status is consistent with a possible gene-environment interplay underlying these neuroanatomical alterations in bipolar disorder.

Declined cognitive function in FEM carrying risk BDNF rs10835210 A allele

The BDNF rs10835210 polymorphism also impacts memory and attentional performance in FEM. FEM carrying the risk A allele have presented with reduced scores on DMS total correct, DMS total correct all delays and RVP probability of hit, which represent the cognitive performance on memory and attention, compared to MEM and HC with risk A carriers. However, we found no diagnosis × genotype interaction on all cognitive measurements between the MEM and HC.

While prior research has primarily focused on the functional Val66Met (rs6265) polymorphism’s association with memory deficits in bipolar disorder [57] and attentional abnormalities in schizophrenia [58], our findings extend this understanding to BDNF rs10835210’s specific effects. The cognitive impairments we observed in FEM patients carrying the BDNF rs10835210 A allele align with emerging evidence across psychiatric and healthy populations. Notably, in school-aged Chinese children who carried risk A allele show 3.29-fold increased risk to ADHD [18], with attention deficits being a core symptomatic manifestation. Besides, Polish healthy carriers demonstrate altered working memory [22]. Furthermore, recent evidence indicates that in individuals with methamphetamine use disorder, the rs10835210 A allele interacts with childhood adversity to specifically impair attentional performance [59]. Taken together, this convergent evidence across diagnostic boundaries suggests that the rs10835210 A allele may mark a shared, genetically influenced vulnerability in attention, rather than conferring risk for a single disorder. Within this framework, the first manic episode in bipolar disorder may act as a potent biological stressor or ‘unmasking event’ for this pre-existing genetic diathesis.

We observed a dissociation whereby the A allele’s association with right parahippocampal volume reduction is present across illness stages, but its link to measurable cognitive deficit is restricted to the early (FEM) stage, which can be coherently interpreted through a neuroprogression perspective. We propose that in the early stage, the cognitive profile is closer to the genetic baseline [60], allowing this specific vulnerability to be detectably expressed. In contrast, by the chronic (MEM) stage, the cumulative burden of the illness, including repeated mood episodes, prolonged and heterogeneous medication exposure [61, 62], and associated neurobiological changes, becomes the dominant driver of cognitive variance. These factors introduce substantial noise and are likely to overshadow the more circumscribed effect of a single genetic variant, explaining the attenuated genotype-cognition relationship in MEM. Thus our study also provides evidence that FEM might be the better cohort to observe the impact of BDNF rs10835210 on cognitive function, relative to MEM.

Mechanistically, as an intronic variant, rs10835210 may regulate BDNF expression through non-coding mechanisms [63], potentially via antisense RNA transcription [25]. This could particularly impact early-stage bipolar disorder when neural circuits are reorganizing [64].

Decreased right parahippocampal volume and cognitive impairment: relationship to BDNF rs10835210 genotype

Given the predominant effects of the A allele in FEM identified in our primary analyses, we focused specifically on FEM and HC subgroups to elucidate genotype-modulated structure-function relationships. Notably, Consistent with our findings of A allele-specific cognitive deficits in FEM, the right parahippocampal volume correlated with attention performance only in A allele carriers, but not in CC homozygotes. These results indicate an A-allele-specific association between right parahippocampal volume and attention in FEM. The parahippocampus is one of the primary hubs that control attention [65]. One study showed that the activation of the parahippocampus predicted alteration in object-selective attention when subjects were instructed to attend to images [66]. Another study showed that selective attention to visual scenes was associated with higher parahippocampus activation [67]. The reason why the correlation between right parahippocampal volume and cognitive function was susceptibility to BDNF rs10835210 genotype may involve BDNF-dependent neuroplasticity. Further studies should test whether this genotype effect extends to functional connectivity or is moderated by BDNF levels.

Limitations

Our study offers novel insights into the genetic and neuroanatomical aspects of bipolar disorder, but has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design prevents us from establishing causality between the A allele and the observed deficits. Second, participants were not enrolled under a strict, uniform clinical state. Although low symptom scores suggest most were in partial remission, this heterogeneity may have influenced results. Third, while we controlled for key covariates such as illness duration, episode number, and education, other potential confounders including detailed medication usage and age at first depressive episode were not adjusted for. Future studies should collect comprehensive data on medication dosage and duration to clarify their effects. Fourth, the sample size, particularly within genetic subgroups of the first-episode cohort, was modest, limiting generalizability and power to detect small effects. Although our primary interactions remained robust after controlling for key covariates and in sensitivity analyses, replication in larger, independent samples is needed. Fifth, in the genotype × diagnosis interaction analyses, uncorrected p-values were reported based on a priori hypotheses. We acknowledge this increases the risk of Type I error, and these results should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, future studies with larger, independent samples and more stringent correction methods are warranted to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

In summary, our study is the first to report that BDNF rs10835210 variant is associated with the changes of right parahippocampal morphology in both first- and multiple-episode mania patients, while specifically linking this genetic variant to parahippocampus-dependent cognitive deficits in FEM. These findings suggest the A allele may serve as a biomarker of early parahippocampal and cognitive vulnerability, underscoring its potential utility for early intervention in BD.
