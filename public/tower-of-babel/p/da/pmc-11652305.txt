Plug and play: vascular plug therapy for refractory colocutaneous fistula
Authors: Shiv D Gandhi, Harris Feldman, Ikechukwu Achebe, Eric Then, Richard Perugini, Navine Nasser-Ghodsi, Prashanth Rau, Neil B Marya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Current endoscopic options for the management of enterocutaneous fistulae include fibrin sealants, enteric stenting, over-the-scope clipping, and endoscopic suturing.1, 2, 3, 4 Clinical success is variable using these techniques, with studies showing rates of clinical success approximating to 37% for fibrin sealants,1 62% for enteric stenting,5 46% for over-the-scope clipping,6 and 80% for endoscopic suturing.7 Vascular plugs are devices used to close peripheral vascular defects but could be used off-label to manage GI fistulae as well. In the following case, we demonstrate how a vascular plug could be used to manage a refractory colocutaneous fistula that persisted despite other endoscopic therapies.

Case presentation

A 62-year-old man presented with a sigmoid diverticular perforation requiring a Hartmann’s procedure. The patient underwent reversal of his colostomy 10 months after the index procedure, and his postcolostomy reversal course was complicated by an anastomotic stricture and colocutaneous fistula. The fistula persisted despite central parenteral nutrition and bowel rest. He was referred for endoscopic evaluation and management. A sigmoidoscopy demonstrated an anastomotic stricture and colocutaneous fistula. A cold 20 mm × 10 mm lumen-apposing metal stent (AXIOS; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Mass, USA) was deployed traversing the stenosis and covering the fistula. One month later, the lumen-apposing metal stent was removed, and the anastomotic stricture had improved. Contrast injection demonstrated persistence of the colocutaneous fistula. The fistula was then closed using an over-the-scope suturing system (OverStitch; Apollo Endosurgery, Austin, Tex, USA). After the procedure, the patient noted improvement but also had persistent staining of his clothes at the cutaneous site of the fistula, which raised question of a sinus tract or a persistent fistula. This finding prompted further investigation with another sigmoidoscopy, which demonstrated endoscopic and fluoroscopic evidence of a recalcitrant fistula (Fig. 1A and B). The decision was then made to deploy a vascular plug endoscopically. To allow for endoscopic deployment, a 10-7-5 biliary dilation catheter (Cook Endoscopy, Winston-Salem, NC, USA) had to be modified (Fig. 2) so that a 6 mm × 6 mm vascular plug (Amplatzer II; Abbott, Chicago, Ill, USA) could be deployed into the fistula. The vascular plug chosen is available in sizes ranging from 3 mm to 22 mm, and the package contents are reviewed separately.8 The vascular plug was deployed successfully, and post-therapy fluoroscopy demonstrated no opacification of the fistula tract (Fig. 1C and D). A follow-up endoscopy demonstrated resolution of the fistula. At a 7-month follow-up, the patient reported clinical resolution of the fistula with no further drainage (Video 1, available online at www.videogie.org).

Case resolution

In over 12 months since vascular plug deployment, the patient has not experienced symptoms of his prior colocutaneous fistula. Although further studies are needed to assess the long-term outcomes, vascular plug therapy is a novel and promising option for nonoperative enterocutaneous fistula closure in select patients.
