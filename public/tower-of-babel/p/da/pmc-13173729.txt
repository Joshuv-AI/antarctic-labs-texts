Psychological skills and their educational outcomes in adolescence: self-regulation, time management, and academic self-efficacy
Authors: Necati Çobanoğlu, Serkan Gökalp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe primary objective of this study is to investigate the relationships between the fundamental psychological concepts that influence university students’ academic self-efficacy. Specifically, the direct and indirect interactions between self-regulation, time management, and academic self-efficacy were analyzed using structural equation modeling. The study was conducted with a sample of 450 undergraduate students enrolled at a public university in Turkey utilizing a quantitative research design. Findings revealed that self-regulation was strongly associated with time management, which was in turn significantly related to academic self-efficacy. Furthermore, results indicated that self-regulation was directly and indirectly associated with academic self-efficacy through time management. Correlation analyses supported significant and positive relationships among all variables. Collectively, these results highlight the important role of behavioral regulation skills in relation to academic self-efficacy. The study highlights the imperative for educational contexts to foster both time management skills and self-regulation competencies, suggesting that a comprehensive, integrative approach to these interrelated constructs is essential for optimizing students’ academic performance and strengthening their self-belief.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04368-x.Keywords: Academic self-efficacy, Self-regulation, Self-regulation, Time management, University students

Introduction

Academic achievement is increasingly conceptualized as a multidimensional construct shaped not only by cognitive ability but also by motivational, emotional, and behavioral processes. A substantial body of educational psychology research highlights that students’ academic functioning is influenced by how effectively they regulate their learning, sustain effort, and manage academic demands [22, 28, 29, 37]. In higher education contexts, where students are expected to function with a high degree of autonomy, psychological competencies such as self-regulation, time management, and academic self-efficacy play a particularly critical role in supporting academic engagement and performance [6, 21, 30].

Among these competencies, academic self-efficacy has been consistently identified as a robust predictor of students’ persistence, motivation, and resilience in the face of academic challenges [1, 17, 31, 33]. Similarly, self-regulation enables students to initiate and sustain goal-directed academic behavior, resist distractions, and maintain long-term engagement with learning tasks [8, 10, 18, 37]. Time management, which involves the planning and organization of academic activities within limited time resources, has also been linked to academic performance, stress reduction, and adaptive learning behaviors [3, 14, 15, 20, 24, 35]. Although these constructs are theoretically related, existing research has often examined them in isolation, providing limited insight into how they jointly contribute to students’ academic functioning. Against this backdrop, the university context offers a particularly relevant setting for examining the interplay among self-regulation, time management, and academic self-efficacy, as students navigate complex academic demands with increasing responsibility for their own learning processes.

Theoretical framework

The present study is theoretically grounded in Bandura’s self-efficacy theory (1997) and Zimmerman’s model of self-regulated learning (2002, 2008), which together provide a comprehensive framework for understanding how psychological regulation processes shape academic functioning. Bandura’s theory emphasizes that academic self-efficacy develops primarily through mastery experiences and plays a central role in motivation, persistence, and performance [1, 27, 33]. Complementing this perspective, Zimmerman conceptualizes learning as a cyclical process involving forethought, performance, and self-reflection phases, in which self-regulatory capacities guide behavior and inform subsequent evaluations of competence [36, 37, 39]. Integrating these perspectives, the current study positions self-regulation as a foundational psychological capacity, time management as the behavioral mechanism through which this capacity is enacted, and academic self-efficacy as the resulting cognitive outcome.

Self-regulation refers to individuals’ ability to regulate their attention, emotions, and behavior in pursuit of personal goals [28, 37]. In academic contexts, self-regulation encompasses processes such as goal setting, effort regulation, attentional control, and the monitoring of learning strategies [8, 38]. Empirical research consistently identifies self-regulation as a key predictor of academic engagement, persistence, and achievement across childhood, adolescence, and higher education [5, 6, 9, 10, 32]. Rather than reflecting specific task-level behaviors, self-regulation represents a higher-order regulatory capacity that enables students to initiate academic tasks, resist distractions, and sustain effort toward long-term goals [18].

Time management is generally defined as the planning, prioritization, and organization of tasks within limited time resources [3, 4]. In academic settings, effective time management involves scheduling study activities, adhering to plans, allocating effort efficiently, and minimizing procrastination [24, 35]. Although closely related to self-regulatory processes, time management is conceptually distinct in that it reflects the external behavioral organization of academic activities rather than internal regulation of attention, motivation, or impulse control. Empirical evidence suggests that self-regulation and time management are related but non-redundant constructs, each accounting for unique variance in academic behavior and outcomes [15, 35].

Academic self-efficacy refers to students’ beliefs in their ability to successfully perform academic tasks and meet academic demands [1, 31]. Extensive research demonstrates that academic self-efficacy is associated with greater persistence, effort, resilience, and academic achievement [17, 29]. According to self-efficacy theory, mastery experiences constitute the most influential source of efficacy beliefs, highlighting the importance of successful task engagement and perceived control over learning processes [1, 33]. Thus, academic self-efficacy is both an outcome of prior academic experiences and a motivational force shaping future engagement.

Theoretical and empirical studies suggest that self-regulation influences academic self-efficacy both directly and indirectly through behavioral mechanisms such as time management. Students with stronger self-regulatory capacities are more likely to engage in effective time management practices, including planning, prioritization, and consistent task engagement [12, 14, 15]. These behaviors increase the likelihood of successful task completion, thereby fostering mastery experiences that strengthen academic self-efficacy beliefs. From this perspective, time management functions as a behavioral pathway through which self-regulatory capacities are translated into cognitive evaluations of academic competence.

Despite extensive research on self-regulation, time management, and academic self-efficacy, these constructs have often been examined in isolation. Studies on self-regulation have primarily focused on impulse control, persistence, and attentional regulation, whereas research on time management has emphasized planning behaviors, academic stress, and procrastination [12, 24, 35]. Empirical studies explicitly testing integrated mediation models remain limited, and existing findings regarding the indirect role of time management are inconsistent [14, 15]. Moreover, research conducted in non-Western contexts—particularly among Turkish university students—is notably underrepresented in the literature.

Addressing these gaps, the present study adopts an integrated theoretical framework to empirically examine the relationships among self-regulation, time management, and academic self-efficacy within a single structural model. By testing the mediating role of time management, the study seeks to clarify the mechanisms through which self-regulatory capacities translate into academic self-efficacy beliefs. In doing so, the study aims to contribute to both international and Turkish educational psychology literature and to provide a coherent theoretical foundation for the research questions and hypotheses presented in the following section.

Purpose, research questions, and hypotheses

The primary purpose of the present study is to examine the relationships among self-regulation, time management, and academic self-efficacy in university students within an integrated theoretical framework. Specifically, the study aims to test whether time management functions as a mediating mechanism in the relationship between self-regulation and academic self-efficacy. By examining these constructs within a single structural model, the study seeks to clarify the mechanisms through which self-regulatory capacities are translated into academic self-efficacy beliefs.

In line with this purpose, the study addresses the following research questions:

Method

Research design

This study adopted a quantitative research paradigm and utilized a correlational survey design to examine the relationships among the key variables. This design allowed for the systematic investigation of the strength and direction of associations between university students’ academic self-efficacy, self-regulation, and time management skills. To assess both direct and indirect effects, Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was employed as the primary analytic technique, offering a comprehensive framework for testing hypothesized pathways and evaluating the mediating role of time management. Within this model, self-regulation was specified as a predictor of time management, which in turn was hypothesized to influence academic self-efficacy. Path analysis within the SEM framework enabled the simultaneous estimation of these relationships, providing an integrated understanding of the mechanisms underlying academic self-efficacy.

Population and sample

The target population of this study comprises undergraduate students enrolled at a public university in Turkey. This group was deliberately selected as the university experience represents a critical developmental juncture in which young adults refine and consolidate their self-regulatory competencies. The multifaceted nature of academic demands, the necessity of balancing scholastic responsibilities with social engagements, and the heightened requirement for autonomous decision-making collectively render the university context a pivotal environment for the advancement of time management and self-regulation skills. These psychological competencies are instrumental in shaping students’ beliefs in their capacity to achieve academic success, thereby exerting a direct and substantive influence on their overall academic self-efficacy. Given the practical challenges of reaching all university students in the target population, a more manageable subset of participants was selected to represent the population. Accordingly, the sample was determined using a convenience sampling strategy. Convenience sampling involves selecting participants who are easily accessible to the researcher and offers practical advantages in terms of time and cost efficiency while enabling data collection within real-world constraints.

The study sample consisted of 450 undergraduate students recruited from various faculties of a public university. Participation was entirely voluntary, ensuring ethical compliance and minimizing potential response bias. Furthermore, the demographic characteristics of the sample (e.g., gender, age, year of study) were systematically examined and reported using descriptive statistical analyses to provide a comprehensive profile of the participant group and enhance the interpretability of the findings. Although the distribution of participants across faculties appears numerically balanced, this pattern reflects the natural enrollment structure of the university rather than any post-hoc adjustment. No modifications were made to the collected data.

Data collection instruments

Data for this study were obtained through the administration of three standardized measurement instruments, each of which is described in detail below.

Academic self-efficacy scale

The Academic Self-Efficacy Scale was developed by Kalkan et al., [19] following the scale development procedures outlined by DeVellis [7]. The development process commenced with an extensive review of the literature to establish the theoretical foundations of academic self-efficacy. Content validity was ensured through expert evaluations, after which an initial 42-item draft version of the scale was constructed. Subsequent pilot testing and item-level analyses resulted in the refinement of the instrument to a final version comprising 25 items.

The scale employs a 5-point Likert-type response format, ranging from “Strongly Disagree (1)” to “Strongly Agree (5).” Total scores can vary between 25 and 125, with higher scores indicating greater levels of academic self-efficacy. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) revealed a five-factor structure, with factor loadings ranging from 0.44 to 0.81, collectively accounting for approximately 60% of the total variance. According to the scale’s reliability findings, the Cronbach’s Alpha values for the five factors are as follows: Belief in Self-Efficacy 0.87, Social Competence 0.84, Academic Reputation 0.80, Academic Motivation 0.77, and Social Satisfaction 0.56. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) further demonstrated a good model fit (χ²= 493.36, df = 265, χ²/df = 1.86, RMSEA = 0.058, SRMR = 0.05, GFI = 0.87, CFI = 0.97, NNFI = 0.97, IFI = 0.97, RFI = 0.93). The scale demonstrated high internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.92), with test–retest reliability of 0.95 and split-half reliability indicated by a Spearman–Brown coefficient of 0.86, confirming its psychometric robustness for assessing academic self-efficacy.

Self-regulation scale

To assess participants’ levels of self-regulation, this study employed the Adolescent Self-Regulatory Inventory (ASRI), originally developed by Moilanen [25]. The ASRI is designed to measure adolescents’ capacities to effectively initiate, monitor, sustain, inhibit, and adapt their emotions, cognitions, attention, and behaviors. The instrument consists of 32 items rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale, ranging from “Never (1)” to “Always (4).” Moilanen’s conceptual framework delineates self-regulation into two distinct dimensions: short-term and long-term regulatory capacities. The Turkish adaptation of the ASRI was conducted by Mehmet Harma [16], ensuring its cultural and linguistic suitability for use within the Turkish context.

In the original validation study, the ASRI demonstrated strong internal consistency for both adolescent and parent-report forms (short-term self-regulation: α = 0.84; long-term self-regulation: α = 0.89). In the present research, EFA revealed a two-factor structure: Self-Regulation Success (e.g., “I can find a way to stick to my plans and goals”) and Self-Regulation Failure (e.g., “I have trouble focusing during boring classes”). The self-regulation success dimension accounted for 47.07% of the total variance, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85, whereas the self-regulation failure dimension explained 21.36% of the variance and demonstrated a reliability coefficient of α = 0.80. Subsequent CFA supported this two-factor structure, providing evidence for the construct validity and psychometric soundness of the scale in assessing self-regulatory capacities.

Time management scale

To assess students’ time management skills, this study utilized the Time Management Skills Scale (TMSS) developed by Esra Oran [26]. The instrument was specifically designed for secondary school students and underwent rigorous validation and reliability testing. The TMSS comprises four subdimensions—planning, adherence to plans, effective use of time, and procrastination—encompassing a total of 17 items. The scale employs a 5-point Likert-type response format, ranging from “Strongly Disagree (1)” to “Strongly Agree (5).” Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) supported the construct validity of the instrument, yielding good model fit indices (χ²=379.10, df = 115, χ²/df = 3.29, CFI = 0.93, IFI = 0.93, NFI = 0.90, TLI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.04, SRMR = 0.03). Reliability analyses further demonstrated high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.80 and McDonald’s omega of 0.79, indicating strong internal reliability.

Data analysis

The quantitative data obtained in this study were first examined to assess the normality assumption. Skewness and kurtosis coefficients were calculated for all variables, and the results showed that these values remained within the acceptable threshold of ± 1.96 for all scales. This indicates that the data set meets the criteria for normal distribution [11]. For all analyses, mean scores were used for each scale and subscale, in accordance with standard psychometric practices. All factor loadings and structural coefficients reported in the CFA and SEM analyses represent standardized estimates.

Although the scales used in this study are based on Likert-type response formats, prior methodological research indicates that composite scores derived from 5-point Likert scales can be treated as approximately continuous, particularly when distributional assumptions are met. Given both the near-continuous nature of the composite scores and the large sample size (N = 450), Pearson correlation coefficients were deemed appropriate and were therefore used instead of Spearman’s rho.

Prior to the analyses, a preliminary data screening was conducted. Missing data patterns were examined, univariate and multivariate outliers were assessed using z-scores, and univariate and multivariate normality assumptions were evaluated. After confirming that the data set met the necessary assumptions, descriptive statistics, correlations, CFA, and SEM analyses were performed. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using AMOS software to evaluate the measurement models of the employed scales. During the CFA process, covariances between latent variables were initially added to build the model. Covariances with poor model fit were then removed to simplify the model. Fit indices supporting the construct validity of the scales were reported, confirming that the measurement models adequately represented the underlying theoretical constructs. Standard CFA fit indices (χ²/df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR) were examined, and all scales demonstrated acceptable model fit. Item factor loadings ranged from 0.44 to 0.81 for Academic Self-Efficacy, 0.52 to 0.78 for the Self-regulation (ASRI) scale, and 0.49 to 0.76 for the Time Management scale. To examine the potential influence of Common Method Bias, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted. The first unrotated factor accounted for less than 50% of the total variance, indicating that Common Method Bias was not a significant concern. Discriminant validity was assessed using the Fornell–Larcker criterion. For each construct, the square root of the AVE value exceeded its correlations with the other constructs, supporting adequate discriminant validity.

After confirming adequate fit for the measurement model, the structural model was tested to assess direct, indirect, and mediating effects among the latent constructs. The hypothesized structural model was tested using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). Within this framework, self-regulation was treated as a direct predictor of time management skills, while time management was modeled as both a direct predictor of academic self-efficacy and a mediator between self-regulation and academic self-efficacy. The direct and indirect pathways among these constructs were examined through path analysis, enabling a comprehensive assessment of the proposed structural relationships within the model.

Age and gender were included in the structural model as observed control variables predicting academic self-efficacy. The inclusion of these covariates did not produce significant effects, and the hypothesized relationships among self-regulation, time management, and academic self-efficacy remained stable.

To evaluate the validity of the structural model, goodness-of-fit indices were examined, including χ², χ²/df, GFI, IFI, TLI, CFI, and RMSEA. The values of these indices were compared to recommended thresholds, and those within acceptable limits indicated that the structural model fit the data well [23]. Furthermore, reliability analyses were conducted for each measurement instrument to assess internal consistency. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated using SPSS software, and all scales demonstrated α values exceeding the 0.70 criterion, indicating a high level of internal consistency and psychometric adequacy [13]. Proposed structural model

Based on the data, a measurement model that provided the best fit indices was developed and validated with the study sample. The model demonstrated a good fit with the research data (χ²=1296.54, d = 343, χ²/df = 3.78, p=0.001, RMSEA = 0.05) and produced acceptable fit indices (GFI = 0.86, IFI = 0.90, TLI = 0.90, CFI = 0.90). Within the model, which incorporated measurement errors, a strong and statistically significant relationship was found between self-regulation and time management, while moderate and significant relationships were identified between self-regulation and academic self-efficacy and between time management and academic self-efficacy. These findings, depicted in Fig. 1, provide empirical support for the hypothesized structural relationships among the constructs. A latent variable SEM approach was employed, in which self-regulation, time management, and academic self-efficacy were modeled as latent constructs represented by their observed items.

Discussion and conclusion

This study investigated the dynamic relationships among academic self-efficacy, self-regulation, and time management, revealing that these three constructs interact in a cyclical and interconnected manner. The findings provided robust support for all hypothesized relationships within the proposed model. Notably, self-regulation was found to be significantly associated with academic self-efficacy both directly and indirectly through the mediating role of time management. These results suggest that an individual’s ability to regulate their behavior may be linked to higher levels of academic self-confidence via their capacity to manage time effectively.

One of the most salient findings of this study is the strong positive correlation observed between self-regulation and time management (r = 0.81), indicating that individuals’ ability to regulate their own behavior is a powerful predictor of how effectively they organize and utilize their time. This result aligns closely with the observations of Claessens et al., [4], who emphasized that time management skills are fundamentally rooted in self-regulatory capacities. Similarly, Duckworth and Seligman [9], identified self-discipline as a key predictor of academic success.

Moreover, the positive correlation between time management and academic self-efficacy (r = 0.69) suggests that effective time management skills extend beyond facilitating task completion to also strengthening students’ self-efficacy beliefs. Those who manage their time effectively experience a greater sense of control over academic tasks, which in turn enhances their self-confidence. This finding is consistent with Britton and Tesser’s [3] argument regarding the indirect impact of time management on academic performance.

Analysis of the structural model reveals a strong direct effect of self-regulation on time management (β = 0.72, p<0.001), alongside a significant effect of time management on academic self-efficacy (β = 0.61, p<0.001). This pattern of associations is consistent with Bandura’s [1] self-efficacy theory, which emphasizes the relationship between perceived control over behavior and success. Furthermore, the direct effect of self-regulation on academic self-efficacy (β = 0.44, p<0.001) highlights the contribution of active engagement in one’s own learning processes to the development of academic self-confidence, underscoring the integral role of self-regulatory capacities in fostering self-efficacy beliefs.

Although the overall model demonstrated acceptable fit, the GFI value (0.86) was slightly below the commonly cited 0.90 threshold. This is not unusual in complex structural models with multiple indicators and latent variables, as GFI is known to be sensitive to sample size and model complexity. Given that the remaining fit indices (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, IFI) were within acceptable or good ranges, the slightly lower GFI likely reflects the multidimensional structure of the constructs rather than substantive model inadequacy. Nevertheless, this can be considered a minor limitation, and future research might explore alternative model specifications to further improve global fit.

Another key finding of the model is the partial mediating role of time management in the relationship between self-regulation and academic self-efficacy. This indicates that behavioral self-regulation not only contributes directly to students’ self-confidence but also exerts its influence indirectly through time management processes. In other words, the way students allocate and manage their time both strengthens and structures the effect of self-regulation on self-efficacy. Consistent with Schunk and DiBenedetto’s [30] cyclical interaction model, this finding confirms that these constructs operate in a mutually reinforcing manner, dynamically shaping one another over time. International studies conducted in the United States, Europe, and East Asia similarly indicate that self-regulation and time management function as core predictors of academic outcomes. Integrating these global findings suggests that the associations observed in the present study may be relevant across diverse educational systems [12, 15, 34].

The findings of this study further advocate for a systemic perspective as an alternative to the traditionally predominant single-variable approaches in educational psychology. The results demonstrate that self-regulation, time management, and academic self-efficacy do not function as isolated constructs but rather form an interconnected, dynamically interacting system. This aligns with Boekaerts’ [2] assertion that fostering students’ learning processes necessitates a holistic and contextually grounded approach, wherein cognitive, behavioral, and motivational factors are addressed in an integrative manner.

Limitations and directions for future research

This study is limited by its cross-sectional design, which precludes making definitive causal inferences regarding the observed relationships. Although the structural model is theoretically grounded, the cross-sectional nature of the data limits conclusions regarding temporal ordering and directionality among variables. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of statistically significant associations rather than causal effects. Longitudinal and experimental studies are necessary to determine the direction and stability of these relationships over time. Because the sample was recruited through convenience sampling from a single university, the generalizability of the findings is limited. Future studies should include multi-institutional and cross-cultural samples to examine whether the observed mechanisms operate consistently across different educational contexts. Additionally, all data were collected through self-report measures, raising the potential for social desirability bias. Future research could address these limitations by employing longitudinal designs to examine causal pathways over time and by incorporating qualitative methodologies to gain deeper insights into students’ time management practices and self-regulatory strategies. Such mixed-method approaches would enrich the understanding of the dynamic interplay among self-regulation, time management, and academic self-efficacy. Future studies could incorporate multiple data sources (e.g., behavioral observations, supervisor ratings) to improve methodological robustness. Another limitation of this study is the absence of control variables such as age, gender, or prior academic achievement. These variables were not included because the primary aim of the study was to focus on the structural relationships among the psychological constructs rather than demographic differences. However, future studies should consider incorporating relevant control variables to more clearly isolate the unique contribution of self-regulation and time management to academic self-efficacy.

The high correlation between self-regulation and time management (r = 0.81) suggests that these two constructs may be conceptually overlapping to some extent. Although the Fornell–Larcker criterion supported discriminant validity, this strong association represents a potential limitation regarding the distinctiveness of the constructs. Future studies should examine these variables using alternative measurement instruments or different model specifications to more clearly establish their conceptual separation.

Practical implications

Implications for teaching practice

The findings of this study offer several important implications for instructional practice in higher education. Given the strong influence of self-regulation and time management on academic self-efficacy, educators are encouraged to incorporate structured interventions that explicitly target students’ behavioral self-regulation capacities. Instructional practices may include activities focusing on goal setting, attentional regulation, planning, and task prioritization, which can enhance students’ ability to manage academic demands more effectively. In addition, educators can reinforce academic self-efficacy by modeling effective planning behaviors and providing structured guidance during routine academic tasks, thereby supporting the development of more adaptive learning behaviors.

Implications for student support and academic advising

From a student support perspective, academic advisors can play a proactive role in identifying students who experience difficulties with time management and self-regulation. Individualized guidance, such as the development of personalized weekly study plans or brief self-regulation coaching sessions, may help students translate self-regulatory capacities into effective academic behaviors. Integrating self-regulatory skill-building strategies into routine advising processes can provide sustained support for students’ academic adjustment and engagement, particularly during critical transition periods such as the first year of university study.

Implications for institutional policy and curriculum design

At the institutional and policy level, the findings underscore the importance of embedding self-regulation and time management competencies within higher education curricula. Universities may consider integrating these skills into first-year orientation programs, academic skills courses, or broader curricular frameworks to support students’ long-term academic functioning. Policymakers are also encouraged to recognize self-regulatory behaviors as foundational competencies that underpin academic resilience and performance. Institutionalizing these skills within educational policy and curriculum design may contribute to more holistic approaches to student development that extend beyond cognitive achievement alone.
