mTORC1 activity suppresses ferroptosis through a SCARB1-dependent HDL-tocopherol uptake pathway
Authors: Thomas A O’Loughlin, John S Stiles, Pritika Acharya, Abolfazl Arab, Laine Goudy, Raymond Dai, Ashir A Borah, William A Weiss, Uche N Medoh, Luke A Gilbert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYAberrant activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway is a common feature of cancer, but while mTOR kinase represents an attractive drug target, mTOR inhibitors have not seen broad success as single agents. To identify strategies to enhance the utility of third-generation bi-steric mTORC1 inhibitors, we performed genome-scale CRISPR interference chemogenomics screens, which revealed that mTORC1 inhibitor-mediated cytostasis leaves cells exquisitely dependent on the lipid peroxide scavenging enzyme GPX4. Mechanistically, using unbiased CRISPR activation chemogenomics screens, we demonstrate that mTORC1-dependent control of ferroptosis occurs, in part, through regulation of SCARB1 expression. Specifically, we find that the high-density lipoprotein (HDL) can suppress ferroptosis through interaction with its receptor SCARB1 and delivery of vitamin E to target cells. Our work highlights combining mTORC1 with GPX4 inhibition as one of the most promising combinatorial approaches for mTOR-targeted cancer therapies and defines an HDL-SCARB1 ferroptosis-suppression system that is regulated by mTORC1 activity.

INTRODUCTION

The mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway is a key regulator of cell growth and proliferation, which is frequently activated in cancer. The mTOR kinase itself, a component of the larger mTORC1 and mTORC2 complexes, is the central node in this pathway and integrates environmental cues from a diverse range of molecules, such as growth factors or nutrients, to affect responses in major cellular processes through phosphorylation of an array of downstream targets.1 Perhaps the best characterized outcome of mTORC1 activation is the relief of inhibition on capdependent protein translation and promotion of protein synthesis through phosphorylation of ribosomal protein S6 kinase (RPS6K) and eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E (eIF4E)-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1). More broadly, mTOR also regulates lipid and nucleotide synthesis and autophagy/lysosome homeostasis; thus, it impacts a wide array of essential metabolic functions.1,2 As a result, cancer cells often acquire perturbations that dysregulate mTOR signaling and drive aberrant growth in otherwise undesirable environments. Indeed, mutations in the PI3K kinase and the PTEN tumor suppressor, which are directly upstream of mTOR, are among the most frequently observed in cancer, and many other common perturbations in oncogenic signaling networks or metabolic pathways work to maintain mTOR activity by other less direct means.3–5

The importance of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling in cancer has motivated considerable interest in developing mTOR inhibitors as anticancer agents, and several have been approved for use in humans to treat cancer and other mTOR-associated pathologies.6 First-generation inhibitors (rapalogs), based on the natural inhibitor rapamycin, allosterically inhibit mTORC1 complex assembly via the recruitment of FKBP12 to mTOR without impacting mTORC2.7–9 However, whereas this class of molecules effectively inhibits RPS6K phosphorylation, they incompletely inhibit 4EBP1 phosphorylation, which limits their efficacy.10 In contrast, second-generation inhibitors target the ATP-binding domain of mTOR and thus inhibit mTOR kinase activity in both mTORC1 and mTORC2 complexes.11–13 These molecules potently inhibit the phosphorylation of both RPS6K and 4EBP1, but they also inhibit phosphorylation of mTORC2 targets such as AKT. Because mTORC2 has important roles in the regulation of the cytoskeleton and glucose homeostasis, these drugs can have undesirable side effects, such as hyperglycemia,14 or induce compensatory signaling mechanisms.15–17 Newly developed bi-steric mTORC1 inhibitors combine the selectivity and specificity of rapamycin-based inhibition with the potency of ATP-competitive mTOR kinase inhibitors by covalently linking both allosteric and orthosteric molecules.18–20 With appropriate dosing, these drugs affect durable inhibition of mTORC1 substrates RPS6K and 4EBP1 without impacting AKT phosphorylation18–21 and represent a promising avenue to potently inhibit mTORC1 in cancer without triggering side effects associated with mTORC2 inhibition.

Despite the development of several highly specific and potent small-molecule inhibitors of mTOR, clinical results with these drugs have not fulfilled initial promise.22–25 In addition, mTOR inhibitors are largely cytostatic in vitro and in vivo preclinical tumors treated with these drugs typically recur after a period of remission.21

To identify genes that can protect cells from loss of mTOR signaling, we conducted genome-scale CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) chemogenomic screens in cancer cells treated with next-generation bi-steric mTOR inhibitors. From these unbiased experiments, we find that GPX4, a critical regulator of ferroptotic cell death, strongly promotes resistance to the inhibition of mTORC1. Loss of GPX4 is almost uniquely able to push cells from a cytostatic state driven by mTORC1 inhibition (mTORi) into active ferroptotic cell death. Next, through genome-scale CRISPR gene activation (CRISPRa) chemogenomic screens, we define a mechanism involving mTORC1-dependent regulation of SCARB1 expression as a factor contributing to the ferroptosis sensitivity of mTORC1-inhibited cells. Mechanistically, we discover that high-density lipoprotein (HDL), the ligand of SCARB1, can profoundly protect against ferroptosis by facilitating delivery of lipid-soluble radical-trapping tocopherols. This work defines an HDL-SCARB1-tocopherol ferroptosis suppression system, which is promoted by mTORC1 activity.

RESULTS

Loss of GPX4 drives cell death in cytostatic mTORC1-inhibited cells

Recently developed bi-steric mTOR inhibitors, such as RMC-6272 (RM-006), are highly specific for mTORC1 and its substrates RPS6 and 4EBP1, while minimally affecting the activity of mTORC2 and its substrates, such as AKT20 (Figure 1A). The inhibition of phosphorylation of RPS6 and 4EBP1 by RM-006 occurs in a dose-dependent manner, which correlates with the induction of cytostasis (Figures 1A and 1B). However, despite the selectivity and potency of these inhibitors, much like earlier generations, they remain largely cytostatic (Figure 1B). We reasoned that mTORi might render cells sensitive to perturbation of a second gene or pathway and promote a transition from cytostasis to active cell death. To identify such combinations, we performed genome-scale CRISPRi screens in LN229 glioblastoma cells treated with high- and low-dose RM-006 (Figure 1C; see STAR Methods). Screens were performed at doses that had minimal impact on cell proliferation (0.098 nM) or near-complete growth arrest and loss of mTORC1 substrate phosphorylation (1.56 nM), but without impacting mTORC2 activity (Figures 1A–1C). Data from these screens indicated that mTORi induced sensitivity to loss of the mTORC1 subunit RPTOR at low-dose RM-006, which is suggestive of on-target drug activity (Figures 1D, S1A, and S1B; Table S1). Furthermore, RPTOR and MTOR knockdowns both exhibited sensitizing phenotypes that fell below our significance threshold (Figure S2A). Strikingly, our screens indicated that the loss of GPX4 sensitized cells to both low- and high-dose RM-006 (Figures 1D and S2A; Table S1). Indeed, at high-dose RM-006, GPX4 was ranked 1st among only 6 genes passing our sensitizing hit threshold, compared with > 1,500 resistance hits identified in the same screen. In addition, genes involved in selenoprotein and, therefore, GPX4 synthesis (SEPHS2, PSTK, EEFSEC, and SEPSECS) also showed sensitizing phenotypes at high-dose RM-006 but fell just below our hit threshold (Figure S2A). These screens also identified the poorly characterized gene BEND7 as another possible dependency of mTORC1-inhibited cells but was not explored further. Overall, our screen data indicate that mTOR-inhibited LN229 cells are almost exclusively dependent on the activity of the selenoprotein GPX4 to remain viable. By contrast, our interpretation of resistance hits is that disrupting diverse essential processes, and stalling the cell cycle, promotes resistance to mTOR inhibition in an indirect or pleiotropic manner.

We confirmed these findings in LN229 and additional CRISPRi cell lines and observed that mTORi synergized with GPX4 knockdown to induce cell death in LN229 cells, which could be rescued by Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), as well as across several cancer cell lines from diverse lineages (Figures 1E, S2B, and S2C). Strong synergy could also be observed between RM-006 and several ferroptosis-inducing small molecules, including the GPX4 inhibitors RSL3, ML210, and FIN56; SLC7A11 inhibitor erastin; and GCLC inhibitor BSO (Figures 1F, 1G, S2D, and S2E). Additionally, the alternative mTOR inhibitors rapamycin and INK128, or the knockdown of mTOR directly, similarly sensitized cells to genetic or chemical inhibition of GPX4 (Figures S2F–S2H). To assess the generality of the phenotype and identify cell populations that might benefit from a combination approach, we tested the effect of RM-006 on the IC50 of RSL3 across a panel of cell lines. These experiments revealed a range of synergies across different cell types and lineages, with potent sensitivity observed in several lines (Figure 1H). We also asked whether RM-006 treatment led to lipid peroxidation using C11-BODIPY,26,27 and observed elevated probe oxidation upon treatment in LN229 cells, which could be reversed by the synthetic vitamin E analog Trolox (Figure 1I). Together, our unbiased screen data indicate that mTORi induces an elevation in lipid peroxidation and a dependency on GPX4 activity in several cancer contexts.

Gain-of-function screen identifies mTORC1-regulated ferroptosis modifiers

Inhibition of mTOR leads to widespread transcriptomic changes through regulation of the transcription factors, including Sterol regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBP) and NRF2,28,29 both of which have been implicated in ferroptosis.30,31 Using RNA-seq, we measured gene expression changes induced by RM-006 in LN229 cells and confirmed that many genes were differentially expressed, including an upregulation of genes associated with ribosome and translation pathways and a downregulation of genes associated with mitochondrial metabolism, SREBP signaling, cholesterol and lipid homeostasis, and ferroptosis (Figures 2A and 2B; Table S2). We reasoned that these differentially expressed genes might underlie the ferroptosis sensitivity induced by mTORi.

To identify the relevant transcripts, we conducted a genome-scale CRISPRa gene activation screen in an LN229 CRISPRa dCas9-SunTag cell line,32–34 treated with vehicle, RM-006, and RM-006 in combination with RSL3 to see whether re-expression of downregulated genes, or overexpression of other genes, could rescue ferroptosis resistance (Figure 2C). Cells were treated with RM-006 to induce cytostasis, or with RM-006 and low-dose RSL3, which cooperated to induce robust cell death (Figures 2D, S3A, and S3B; Table S3; see STAR Methods). Comparison of hit genes identified in the cytostatic RM-006-treated screen with those in the RM-006+RSL3 combination-treated screen revealed overexpression of known ferroptosis regulators, including GPX4, FSP1, SLC7A11, LPCAT1, SCD, NQO1, and MBOAT2 as resistance factors, and LPCAT3 overexpression as a sensitizing factor (Figure 2E). Notably, two genes related to the uptake of extracellular lipoproteins were among the strongest hits. Specifically, overexpression of SCARB1 promoted resistance; conversely, overexpression of LDLR promoted sensitivity to the combination of RM-006 and RSL3. We validated these findings in targeted assays and confirmed that the overexpression of GPX4, SCARB1, NQO1, SCD, and FSP1 could promote ferroptosis resistance, and that LPCAT3 and LDLR could promote sensitivity (Figures S4A–S4C). Comparing the CRISPRa data with our LN229 RNA-seq data, we identified several genes, including SCARB1, SCARA5, RNF125, and SCD, as possible ferroptosis suppressors whose expression is downregulated in response to RM-006 (Figure 2F). Notably, SCD has previously been implicated in mTOR-dependent ferroptosis regulation, validating this approach.31 We focused on SCARB1 because it was one of the strongest ferroptosis resistance factors identified in our CRISPRa screen. Titrations with the GPX4 inhibitors RSL3 or ML210 showed that SCARB1 overexpression by CRISPRa or cDNA confers resistance to ferroptosis (Figures 2G, 2H, S4A, S4D, and S4E).

HDL suppresses ferroptosis downstream of GPX4

As SCARB1 is a receptor for HDL,35 we explored a role for HDL in ferroptosis suppression. We tested whether HDL or other lipoproteins themselves could confer resistance to the knockdown of GPX4, which typically induces profound cell death in LN229 cells. Titration of HDL in LN229 CRISPRi cells expressing GPX4-targeting sgRNAs showed HDL was strongly protective with an EC50 of 7.6 μg/ml (Figure 3A). Expanding on this finding, we tested whether HDL could rescue loss of GPX4 in different cell lineages. LN229, LK2, and MiaPaca2 cells showed extensive synergy between RM-006 and GPX4 inhibitors and profound cell death upon knockdown of GPX4 (Figure 1E). Additionally, treatment of LN229, LK2, or MiaPaca2 CRISPRi cells expressing a GPX4-targeting sgRNA with either Ferrostatin-1 or HDL dramatically rescued cell viability, whereas treatment of the same cells with LDL at the same dose offered negligible protection (Figures 3B–3D). Next, we assessed lipid peroxidation using C11-BODIPY and observed that HDL, but not LDL, could rescue RSL3-induced membrane oxidation in LK2 cells, which mirrors our viability assays (Figures 3E and 3F). Together, these data indicate that GPX4 activity is essential for cell survival at HDL concentrations present in cell culture medium but becomes dispensable upon supplementation with exogenous HDL.

Ferroptosis suppression by HDL requires SCARB1

Cell culture medium contains significantly less HDL than is likely present in the tissue microenvironment,36,37 and we find that supplementation of cell culture medium with a modest 20 μg/ml HDL was sufficient to rescue GPX4 knockdown lines from ferroptosis (Figures 3B–3D). We, therefore, tested the extent to which extent SCARB1 overexpression using CRISPRa could promote ferroptosis resistance when combined with exogenous HDL supplementation. Strikingly, combining SCARB1 overexpression with exogenous HDL supplementation at a physiological level led to a greater than 40-fold increase in resistance to RSL3 when compared with cells expressing a non-targeting sgRNA and cultured without exogenous HDL (Figure 4A). This data demonstrates that SCARB1 can confer additional ferroptosis protection in the presence of supplemental HDL.

Structural data indicate that SCARB1 binding to HDL can be abolished by mutation of M159 and T165 residues.38 To confirm that SCARB1 protects cells from ferroptosis through HDL binding, we cloned SCARB1 wild-type (WT) and M159E and T165E mutant cDNAs and generated LN229 stable cell lines (Figure 4B). Whereas the LN229 SCARB1 WT cells showed elevated binding to fluorescently labeled HDL (Dil-HDL), mutation of M159 and T165 to glutamic acid reduced levels of HDL binding to that of GFP-expressing control cells (Figure 4C). Consistent with the notion that HDL suppresses ferroptosis, LN229 SCARB1 WT cells showed resistance to RSL3 when compared with GFP control cells, but this resistance was lost in the M159E and T165E mutant lines (Figure 4D). This suggests that HDL binding is required for ferroptosis resistance promoted by SCARB1.

We questioned whether the protective effect of SCARB1 was restricted to specialized cell types. CRISPRa screens can identify genes that are not typically expressed in the cell type in question, and SCARB1 is expressed at high levels in the adrenal gland and liver, where it plays important roles in corticosteroid synthesis and cholesterol uptake, respectively.39 In support of a broad role for SCARB1 in ferroptosis suppression, expression data from public sources indicate that SCARB1 is almost ubiquitously expressed at more modest levels in other tissues and in many established cancer cells (Figures S5A and S5B). This prompted us to explore whether the loss of SCARB1 is a general ferroptosis suppressor. We generated LN229, LK2, and MiaPaca2 SCARB1 KO cells using Cas9 nuclease and confirmed the loss of protein expression by immunoblot (Figure 4E). Titrations of RSL3 in SCARB1 KO LN229 cells indicated a modest increase in sensitivity to GPX4 inhibition compared with control cells (Figure 4F). However, this difference was amplified in the presence of HDL, which indicates the protective effect of HDL is, at least in part, mediated by SCARB1 in LN229 cells (Figure 4F). More prominently, similar RSL3 titrations in LK2 and MiaPaca2 cells revealed that SCARB1 KO promoted increased sensitivity to GPX4 inhibition in standard culture media and a striking loss of ferroptosis suppression in culture media supplemented with HDL (Figure 4F). Using C11-BODIPY, we observed that SCARB1 KO in LK2 cells triggered an elevation in membrane oxidation compared with RSL3-treated control cells and completely reversed the protective effect of HDL, which stands in contrast to Fer-1, which still exhibited potent ferroptosis suppression in both control and SCARB1 KO cells (Figures 4G and 4H). Together, these data confirm that HDL protects cells from ferroptosis in a SCARB1-dependent manner.

We investigated the relationship between SCARB1 expression and the protective effect of HDL across a panel of GPX4 knockdown cell lines (Figure S2C). Flow cytometry revealed a spectrum of SCARB1 surface levels, with H460 and U87MG cells displaying the highest and lowest expression, respectively (Figure S5C). HDL titration assays demonstrated that SCARB1-high H460 cells were largely protected from ferroptosis upon GPX4 knockdown, whereas SCARB1-low U87MG cells could not be rescued even at maximal HDL concentrations (Figures S5D and S5E). Other cell lines displayed intermediate SCARB1 levels and corresponding sensitivity to HDL rescue, whereas H2030 cells showed high sensitivity to HDL despite low expression of SCARB1 (Figures S5C–S5E).

HDL has been implicated in vitamin E metabolism,40 and vitamin E (especially α-tocopherol) is an important lipid-soluble radical-trapping antioxidant that protects against ferroptosis.41,42 We questioned whether HDL present in fetal bovine serum (FBS) might be an important source of vitamin E for cells in culture. Consistently, increasing the FBS concentration of culture media was protective against GPX4 inhibition (Figure S6A). Next, we measured α-tocopherol by mass spectrometry and HDL cholesterol by plate-based assay in different serum preparations. From these experiments, we observed that α-tocopherol and HDL are both depleted from lipoprotein-depleted FBS and elevated by HDL supplementation when compared with standard FBS (Figures S6B–S6D). In our hands, dialyzed FBS appeared to contain higher levels of HDL cholesterol compared with standard FBS and was more protective against GPX4 inhibition when used to supplement either DMEM or Human Plasma-Like Media (HPLM) (Figures S6C and S6E), although we cannot rule out source or batch effects or other composition changes induced by dialysis.

The observation that media α-tocopherol levels correlated with HDL levels prompted us to use mass spectrometry to measure α-tocopherol content of WT or SCARB1 KO cells before or after treatment with supplemental HDL. Consistent with the notion that the SCARB1-HDL interaction protects cells from ferroptosis by facilitating α-tocopherol delivery to cells, treating WT cells with supplemental HDL elevated α-tocopherol content, but this was abolished by SCARB1 KO (Figure 4I). Together, these data suggest serum-derived HDL can deliver α-tocopherol to cells via SCARB1 to protect cells from ferroptosis.

mTORC1 regulates SCARB1 expression via SREBP1 activity

Our RNA-seq data demonstrated that SCARB1 mRNA expression was reduced upon mTORi by RM-006 across a dose-response curve (Figure 5A). We further confirmed this finding and determined that the loss of mRNA expression corresponded to a decrease in SCARB1 protein levels by quantitative immunoblotting, as well as by flow cytometry, in LN229 and LK2 cells (Figures 5B, 5C, and S7A). Genetic perturbation of mTOR expression by CRISPRi had similar effects in both LN229 and LK2 cells (Figures S7B and S7C).

To determine the mechanism by which mTORC1 regulates SCARB1 expression, we first explored data from publicly available datasets. Analysis of genome-wide Perturb-seq experiments,43 identified the key SREBP regulators INSIG1, SCAP, and MTBPS2 among the strongest regulators of SCARB1 expression, which we could confirm by knockdown of MBTPS2 in LK2 CRISPRi cells (Figures S7D and S7E). Furthermore, DepMap data indicated that SCARB1 expression was highly correlated with several SREBP target genes (Figure S7F). This is consistent with previous research implicating mTORC1 in regulating SREBP activity,29,44 as well as our RNA-seq data from RM-006-treated LN229 cells, which also exhibited an SREBP signaling signature (Figure 2B). To directly assess the role of SREBP1, we overexpressed SREBP1 cDNA and then measured gene expression changes relative to a GFP control by RNA-seq. Consistent with elevated SREBP1 activity, these cells exhibited an upregulation of known SREBP1 target genes such as FADS2, INSIG1, and SCD, with SCARB1 presenting as one of the most highly upregulated transcripts (Figures 5D and 5E; Table S4). Flow cytometry confirmed that ectopic SREBP1 expression increased cell surface SCARB1 levels relative to GFP controls (Figure 5F). Importantly, this induction was significantly inhibited by pretreatment with RM-006 (Figure 5F). Together, these data implicate SREBP1 in the mTORC1-dependent regulation of SCARB1 expression.

mTORC1 inhibition perturbs HDL and α-tocopherol uptake

We next sought to determine whether the reduction in SCARB1 levels upon inhibition of mTORC1 by RM-006 resulted in reduced HDL uptake. In accordance with our transcriptomic and protein data, RM-006 treatment attenuated HDL binding to a degree proportional to the reduction in SCARB1 levels across both LN229 and LK2 cells (Figure 5G). To spatially resolve this deficit, we analyzed HDL internalization via confocal microscopy. While control cells exhibited an accumulation of HDL within the lysosomal compartment, treatment with RM-006 markedly reduced the overall levels of HDL associated with cells, as well as causing a particularly dramatic reduction in lysosomal localization (Figures 5H–5J).

Finally, we tested whether mTORC1 inhibition by RM-006 treatment impacted the ability of HDL to confer ferroptosis resistance. In the absence of RM-006, HDL could rescue the toxicity associated with GPX4 knockdown almost completely and to a similar degree as that observed with Fer-1 (Figures 5K and S7G). However, in cells treated with RM-006, whereas Fer-1 was able to rescue almost all cells, HDL was less effective (Figures 5K and S7G). This phenotype was particularly prominent in LK2 cells, where HDL lost almost all of its protective effect in RM-006-treated cells (Figure 5K). Importantly, and consistent with these observations, treating LK2 cells with RM-006 also perturbed the uptake of α-tocopherol from exogenous HDL as determined by mass spectrometry (Figure 5L). Together, these data indicate that mTORC1 can regulate SCARB1 expression and that this can modify the ferroptosis resistance conferred by HDL by reducing the uptake of the lipid-soluble radical-trapping antioxidant vitamin E.

DISCUSSION

Preclinical studies of bi-steric mTOR inhibitors revealed that they promote an initial tumor regression followed by regrowth.21 This recurrence highlights the need to identify pathways mediating resistance or possible drug combinations to drive durable remissions. Here, using unbiased genome-scale genetic screens, we found that cytostasis driven by the bi-steric mTORC1 inhibitor RM-006 led to a specific dependency on the lipid peroxide scavenging enzyme GPX4. GPX4 was possibly the only target whose repression was able to switch mTOR-inhibited cells from a cytostatic state to active cell death, or more specifically, ferroptosis. Recent work has highlighted that cell cycle arrest is coincident with sensitization to GPX4 inhibition,45 and studies of drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells revealed a dependency on GPX4 that correlated with the depth of cell cycle arrest.46–48 Our work lends further credence to the notion that cytostasis resulting from inhibition of mTOR signaling represents a major mechanism underlying the dependency of DTP cells on GPX4 activity. Interestingly, recent research has revealed that the DTP state shows marked similarities to developmental diapause,49 a state of suspended development triggered by unfavorable environmental conditions. It is notable then that mTOR inhibitors also lengthen diapause in embryonic stem cells.50 Consistent with our data, these observations suggest cytostasis driven by mTOR inhibition as a mechanism to protect cells from unfavorable environmental conditions or toxic insults (i.e., chemotherapy) while simultaneously inducing a dependency on GPX4 and lipid detoxification pathways.

Given the core role of mTOR in regulating many aspects of cellular metabolism, including lipid and redox homeostasis,1,2 it is unsurprising that mTOR has been implicated in ferroptosis previously.31,51–53 Several studies of mTOR in ferroptosis have conflicting results, with some mTOR inhibitors driving resistance,51,52 and others sensitivity,31,53 possibly resulting from use of different mTOR inhibitors (e.g., allosteric versus orthosteric) or ferroptosis inducers (e.g., GPX4 or SLC7A11 inhibitors). Furthermore, the mechanistic basis of these relationships appears varied and context-specific, with some groups describing mTOR inhibitor-induced sensitivity to ferroptosis resulting from inhibition of GPX4 translation or reduced SCD expression altering lipid homeostasis, and mTOR inhibitor-induced ferroptosis resistance resulting from elevated lysosomal cysteine uptake promoting increased glutathione synthesis.31,51–53 Through an unbiased gain-of-function screen, we identified a novel mechanism through which loss of mTORC1 activity can promote ferroptosis sensitivity. This screen identified SCARB1 overexpression as one of the most potent suppressors of RSL3-induced ferroptosis in mTORC1-inhibited cells and showed that SCARB1 expression was significantly downregulated by mTORi. In this work, we found that mTORi with RM-006 led to reduced expression of several SREBP target genes, consistent with the known role for mTOR in SREBP activity,29,44 and that overexpression of SREBP1 could induce SCARB1 expression, among other SREBP1 target genes, in an mTOR-dependent fashion. Building on these findings, we discovered that the SCARB1 ligand, HDL, could completely inhibit ferroptosis induced by GPX4 knockdown and that the anti-ferroptotic activity of HDL required SCARB1. Furthermore, we find that loss of SCARB1 perturbs delivery of the lipid-soluble radical-trapping antioxidant α-tocopherol from HDL to cells, which suggests this is likely a key functional molecule, although roles for other lipophilic small-molecule antioxidants, bioactive lipids, or protein antioxidants, which might also be present in HDL particles, cannot be completely ruled out. Notably, data from the Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap) database indicate that SCARB1 expression correlates modestly with a genetic dependency on GPX4 and that cells which are categorized as GPX4 independent, based on DepMap essentiality thresholds, express higher levels of SCARB1 (Figures S8A and S8B). Similarly, SCARB1 expression also correlates modestly with drug sensitivity to the GPX4 inhibitors RSL3, ML210, and ML162 (Figures S8C–S8E).

In humans, an HDL cholesterol concentration of 400–600 μg/ml in plasma is considered normal. Standard cell culture medium contains HDL derived from FBS, but in the range of 100–300 μg/ml36 (Figure S6C), which is likely to be less than that encountered by cells in a physiological context.37 Many studies, including screens performed as part of the DepMap consortium, may have underestimated the role of SCARB1 in ferroptosis due to the limited availability of HDL and lipoproteins in in vitro culture systems. Our data show that cells exhibit a stronger genetic interaction between SCARB1 and GPX4 with increasing concentrations of HDL. We found that supplementation of cell culture media with 20 μg/ml HDL increased α-tocopherol ~5-fold and was sufficient to prevent induction of ferroptosis in several GPX4 knockdown cell lines, whereas manipulating FBS concentration up or down was similarly able to alter susceptibility to GPX4 inhibition. Recent work has arrived at a similar conclusion, demonstrating that lipoproteins can suppress ferroptosis via delivery of vitamin E,54 but in contrast to our work, this study implicates glycosaminoglycans in lipoprotein uptake.54 Here we show a highly specific role for SCARB1 in mediating α-tocopherol delivery to cells. Notably, some of the cell lines tested in the work are only partially dependent on SCARB1 expression for HDL-mediated ferroptosis suppression, whereas others, such as U87MG, appear to be completely resistant, suggesting possible cell-type-specific roles for alternative uptake pathways or receptors, including glycosaminoglycan-mediated uptake. It is notable then that U87MG is a glioblastoma cell line, and their lack of response to HDL may be a response to the privileged environment in the central nervous system, where lipoprotein levels are significantly lower than in the periphery.55–57 Additionally, our CRISPRa screens demonstrate that overexpression of LDLR can increase ferroptosis sensitivity, suggesting varied roles for lipoproteins in ferroptosis sensitivity. Along with GPX4, the FSP1-CoQ10 axis represents a major cell-autonomous ferroptosis-suppressing antioxidant pathway,58,59 but CoQ10, much like vitamin E, is primarily delivered to cells bound to lipoproteins, in particular low-high density and very low-density lipoproteins.60 Whereas we find that LDL has a limited impact on ferroptosis susceptibility in our assays, further research is warranted to explore the interplay between distinct lipoprotein species, their pro- and antioxidant properties, and their impact on ferroptosis susceptibility.

In summary, our data highlight HDL uptake by SCARB1 as a potentially important mode of ferroptosis regulation and that this pathway is regulated by mTORC1 and SREBP1 activity. A detailed understanding of how lipoproteins regulate ferroptosis and how they might contribute to therapy resistance or ferroptosis sensitivity in vivo will be an important avenue for future research. Our systematic genome-scale approach also emphasizes ferroptosis induction as the most promising combinatorial approach with mTORC1 inhibitors, although the lack of effective GPX4 inhibitors for in vivo use makes it challenging to test this currently.61 We hope an appreciation of the role of lipoprotein biology in the response to mTOR inhibitors may contribute to improving clinical responses to this promising drug class in cancer and other diseases.

Limitations of the study

Whereas our findings identify the HDL-SCARB1 axis as a potent suppressor of ferroptosis across several cell types in vitro, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the physiological relevance of this pathway in vivo remains to be established. Specifically, the systemic and tissue-specific bioavailability of HDL, alongside the relative contribution of SCARB1 versus alternative uptake mechanisms, remains uncharacterized in human physiological environments. Furthermore, this study focused on a specific HDL-SCARB1 interaction; however, the cellular response likely involves a complex interplay of diverse lipoproteins possessing varying pro- and antioxidant properties. Future studies will be required to establish their synergistic or antagonistic roles in regulating ferroptotic cell death.

CRISPR screening

LN229 CRISPRi or CRISPRa cells were transduced with compact genome-scale dual-sgRNA libraries generously donated by Jonathan Weissman.64 Cells were transduced at an MOI of ~0.3 to achieve representation of each sgRNA in an average of >500 cells (500x coverage). At 48 and 72 hours post-transduction cells were dosed with 3 μg/ml puromycin to select for sgRNA-positive cells. Following selection, sgRNA-positive cells were subsequently recovered in fresh medium for an additional 3 days. 6 days post-transduction cells pellets of 25 million cells were frozen to determine sgRNA composition at selection initiation (T0). Remaining cells were split into treatment conditions in triplicate. For CRISPRi screens conditions were: DMSO, RM-006 1.56 nM, and RM-006 0.098 nM. For CRISPRa screens conditions were: DMSO, RM-006 1.56 nM, RSL3 46.875 nM, and RM-006 1.56 nM+RSL3 46.875 nM. Cells were maintained under selection pressure at 1000x library coverage with passaging every 1 to 4 days as necessary for a total of 14-15 days for the CRISPRi and 12 days for the CRISPRa screens. At the screen endpoint cells were harvested and frozen in 25 million cell pellets prior to library preparation for sequencing.

CRISPR NGS library preparation

Genomic DNA was purified from cell pellets using NucleoSpin Blood L gDNA extraction kits (Nacherey-Nagel # 740954) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions and yields and purity were confirmed by nanodrop. Amplicons were generated by PCR to introduce sequencing adaptors and sample-specific indexes using a common forward primer within the U6 promoter and reverse primers which bind the sgRNA constant region downstream of the second sgRNA and contain the sample-specific indexes. PCR reactions comprised 2x NEB Next Ultra II Q5 polymerase (NEB # M0544), 10 μg of purified gDNA and 1 μl of each 100 μM primer per 100 μl reaction. PCR amplification was performed with the following cycling conditions: denaturation at 98 ° C for 30 seconds, 22 cycles of denaturation at 98 ° C for 10 seconds then annealing and extension at 67° C for 75 seconds, and a final extension at 72° C for 5 minutes. All gDNA was PCR amplified and all reactions for each sample were mixed post-amplification. 300 μl of amplified PCR was subject to a 0.5-0.65X double-sided SPRI selection to enrich for the amplicon and remove excess primer. Sample purity and concentration were determined using an Agilent Tapestation and used to calculate equimolar sample pooling. Pooled libraries were sequenced on a NextSeq550 or NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired end reads using custom read 1, read 2, and index read 1 primers and 10% PhiX achieving a minimum of 6 million reads per sample (i.e. ≥300 reads/library element).

Targeted α-tocopherol quantitation

Cell pellets were resuspended by adding 1 mL 2:1 chloroform:methanol and vortexed for 1 hour at 4 ° C. This was followed by the addition of 200 μL 0.9% NaCl, vortexing for 10 minutes at 4° C, and centrifugation for 5 minutes at 3000 × g at 4° C. The bottom chloroform layer was collected and dried in a speed vacuum concentrator. The dried extract was then derivatised by adding 50 μL acetonitrile containing 5 mg/mL 2-fluoro-1-methylpyridinium-p-toluenesulfonate (FMP-TS) and 1% (v/v) triethylamine. The mixture was vortexed for 2 minutes at room temperature and incubated at 40° C for 15 minutes. The reaction was quenched with 1 mL 2:1 chloroform:methanol containing 0.1% acetic acid (MS-grade), vortexed for 1 hour at 4° C, supplemented with 200 μL of 0.9% NaCl, and vortexed for an additional 10 minutes at 4° C. The mixture was centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 minutes at 4° C, and the bottom chloroform layer was collected into a new tube and dried at 4° C in a speed vacuum concentrator for 1–2 hours. The dried material was resuspended in 65:35:5 isopropanol:acetonitrile:water with 0.1% formic acid, vortexed for 10 minutes at 4° C, and centrifuged at maximum speed for 10 minutes at 4° C. The supernatant was transferred to glass autosampler vials.

Derivatised tocopherols were separated on an RRHD Eclipse Plus C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) (Agilent) with a ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 guard column (2.1 × 5 mm, 1.8 μm) (Agilent) and connected to a 1290 Infinity II LC system. A 6495D triple quadrupole (QQQ) mass analyser equipped with an LC-ESI probe was coupled to the LC system. An external mass calibration was performed using the standard calibration mixture every seven days, and the instrument was tuned every three months. An injection volume of 3 μL was used for each sample in positive mode. Mobile phase A1 was composed of 5:3:2 water:acetonitrile:isopropanol with 10 mM ammonium formate and 5 μM Agilent deactivator additive. Mobile phase B1 was composed of 1:9:90 water:acetonitrile:isopropanol with 10 mM ammonium formate. Needle wash S1 was composed of 1:1 butanol:methanol. The chromatographic gradient was as follows: 100% A1 held from 0–1 min; linear decrease to 85% A1 (15% B1) from 1–1.5 min; 85% A1 held from 1.5–3.5 min; linear decrease to 50% A1 (50% B1) from 3.5–4 min; linear decrease to 25% A1 (75% B1) from 4–4.5 min; linear decrease to 0% A1 (100% B1) from 4.5–5 min; 0% A1 held from 5–11 min; linear increase to 100% A1 from 11–11.5 min. The flow rate was set to 0.400 mL/min with a maximum pressure limit of 1200 bar, and lipids were run in standard mode to maintain compatibility with the derivatised analytes. The column compartment and autosampler were held at 45° C and 4° C, respectively.

The mass spectrometer parameters were as follows: the capillary voltage was set to 4000 V in positive mode with a nozzle voltage of 0 V, the gas temperature was held at 215° C, and the sheath gas temperature was held at 250° C. The gas flow and sheath gas flow were 15 L/min and 12 L/min, respectively. The nebulizer was maintained at 35 psi. The method was optimised for detection of the [M]+ ion at m/z 522.4.

HDL cholesterol assays

HDL cholesterol was quantified using a colorimetric Cholesterol Assay Kit (abcam # 65390) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, to separate HDL and LDL/VLDL fractions, samples were mixed with 2x LDL/VLDL Precipitation Buffer, incubated at room temperature for 10 minutes, and centrifuged for another 10 minutes at 2000xg. The HDL fraction obtained after LDL/VLDL precipitation was separated and diluted as recommended to fit standard curve readings in triplicate. Cholesterol standards supplied with the kit and the Total Cholesterol Reaction Mix were added to wells, incubated for 60 minutes at 37° C, and absorbance at 570 nm was measured on a BioTek Cytation 3 plate reader.

Immunofluorescence

LN229 cells were seeded onto glass coverslips and treated with vehicle or 1.56 nM RM-006 for 72 hours. 18 hrs prior to harvest, cells were incubated with Dil-HDL at 5 μg/ml. Cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed in 4% (wt/vol) formaldehyde for 20 minutes, permeabilised with 0.1% saponin in PBS for 10 minutes, and blocked with 1% BSA in PBS (blocking buffer) for 1 hour. Coverslips were inverted onto drops of LAMP1 antibody (Invitrogen; eBioH4A3; # 14-1079-80; 1:100) in blocking buffer and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. Coverslips were washed 3 times with PBS then incubated with AlexaFluor488 secondary antibody (Invitrogen; # A21206; 1:500) and Hoechst33342 (Invitrogen # H3570; 1:1000) for 45 minutes at room temperature. Coverslips were washed 3 times with PBS then mounted onto glass slides using ProLong Gold Antifade reagent (Invitrogen # P36980) and dried. Samples were imaged in Zeiss Zen Black (v14) software on an inverted Zeiss LSM780 confocal microscopy system using an oil immersion Zeiss Plan-Neofluar 40x/0.9 objective and Diode (405 nm; 3% power), Argon (488 nm; 5% power), and DPSS (561 nm: 5% power) lasers. Detector pinholes were set to give a 2.5 μm optical section and pixel dwell time was 3.15 μs with line averaging of 2. Image sizes were 1024x1024 (212.55x212.55 μm) and 12-bit depth.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistical analysis including statistical tests used, replication, and measure of dispersion are indicated in figure legends or outlined below. Unless specified otherwise, statistical testing was performed in GraphPad Prism (v10).

CRISPR screen analysis

ScreenPro2 package (v0.2.10) was used for processing dual-guide (V3) CRISPRa/i screens – https://github.com/ArcInstitute/ScreenPro2 (or doi:10.5281/zenodo.18808050). Paired-end deep sequencing FASTQ files were first processed using the ‘Guide-Counter’ module to capture guide pairs counts mapped to the reference guide sequences provided in a library table. Then, the output count matrix per sample was used to calculate drug screen phenotypes using the ‘PhenoScore’ module. Final phenotype scores and p-values were then reported as tables and visualised in R using ggplot.

Cell viability and drug synergy scoring

Normalised growth rate inhibition (GR) values were calculated using the GRcalculator online webtool.68–70 GR values are growth scores in which the number of cell divisions occurring in a treatment group during an experiment are presented relative to the number of cell divisions occurring in a control population such that 1 = same growth rate as control condition, <1 but >0 indicates some degree of growth inhibition, 0 = complete cytostasis, and negative values indicate cell death. Drug titration curves using relative cell viability or GR value measurements were plotted using GraphPad Prism (v10). ZIP synergy scores were determined using the SynergyFinderPlus webtool.71,72

Differential expression and gene set enrichment analysis

For RNA-seq in Figure 2, UMIs were extracted from the fastq files with umi_tools74 and cutadapt75 was used to remove short and low-quality reads. The reads were aligned to the human GENCODE v.34 reference genome using STAR aligner76 and the duplicate reads were collapsed using umi_tools.74 Gene level counts were measured using HTSeq.77 Samples from different treatment and dose were compared using pyDESeq2.78 Analysis of RNA-seq in Figure 5 was performed by Plasmidsaurus using their standard in-house pipeline. The fold-change values for the highest treatment dose (1.56 nM RM-006), or for SREBP1 cDNA overexpression, were used for pathway analysis using the blitzgsea python package.79

Flow cytometry analysis

Collected data were analysed using FCS Express 7. Fluorescence intensity of unlabelled, or IgG control labelled, cell populations were subtracted from each sample before calculation of relative Dil-HDL fluorescence, C11-BODIPY oxidised/reduced fluorescence ratios, or SCARB1 or LDLR fluorescence.

LC-MS analysis

High-throughput α-tocopherol annotation and relative quantification were carried out using qualitative analysis software for MassHunter acquisition data, alongside QQQ quantitative analysis (Quant-My-Way) software. α-tocopherol intensities were validated by manually verifying the peak alignment using the Qualitative software, ensuring that retention times and MS/MS spectra matched the characteristic fragmentation patterns of standard compounds. To further confirm accurate identification and quantification, two transitions for the same compound were analysed for consistent relative responses. The MRM method and retention times were employed to quantify all α-tocopherols using the quantitation software, with the raw peak areas exported to Microsoft Excel for further processing. Raw abundance levels were normalised to protein input determined by BCA assay.

Image quantification

Immunoblot densitometry quantification was performed in Fiji (version 2.14.0/1.54f).73 To quantify protein expression from immunoblots, first regions of interest were defined around each protein band and used to generate lane profile plots (histograms) representing the distribution of pixel intensities. Non-specific membrane noise was removed from histograms using a straight line to connect the lowest points on either side of the peak. The area under the curve was calculated by manual integration of the defined peak. For accurate comparison, SCARB1 signal was subsequently normalised to a tubulin loading control quantified in the same way.

For microscopy analysis, cells acquired from randomly selected fields of view were subject to post-acquisition processing and quantification in Fiji (version 2.14.0/1.54f).73 For colocalisation and intensity analysis, images were first background subtracted by uniformly subtracting a mean intensity value taken from a cell-free area of the image and cells were then manually segmented. Mean intensity of Dil-HDL within the segmented cells was calculated using the built-in ‘measure’ function and colocalisation between LAMP1 and Dil-HDL signal determined using the default settings in the Coloc2 package.

Perturb-seq differential expression analysis

We analysed SCARB1 expression changes in a K562 genome-wide (9,851 perturbations) Perturb-seq dataset from Replogle et al.43 Raw count data was normalized to 10,000 counts per cell using scanpy’s normalize_total. For each perturbation with ≥10 cells, we calculated log2 fold change relative to control cells as log2((mean_perturbed + 0.1) / (mean_control + 0.1)) and assessed significance using the Mann-Whitney U test. P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Activators were defined as TFs whose knockdown decreased SCARB1 expression (negative log2FC), while repressors increased expression upon knockdown (positive log2FC).
