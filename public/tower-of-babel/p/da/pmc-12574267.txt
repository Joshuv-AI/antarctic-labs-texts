Elevated serum cobalamin levels in childhood: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Furkan Kalayci, Metin Yigit, Gozde Melisa Kalyoncu Arican, Muhammed Yasin Gokdol, Metehan Yasar Tekin, Orhun Kerem Kalayci, Kaan Celebier, Dilek Kacar, Nese Yarali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCobalamin (Cbl) is a water-soluble vitamin. While its deficiency is well recognized, the clinical significance of elevated serum Cbl levels remains unclear, particularly in children. This study aimed to investigate the clinical and laboratory findings in pediatric patients with excessively high Cbl levels to identify potential underlying causes and outcomes.MethodsIn this retrospective study, pediatric patients with serum Cbl levels > 1500 ng/mL at Ankara Bilkent City Hospital between January and May 2024 were evaluated. Clinical and laboratory data — including demographics, Cbl treatment, chronic disease history, lab results, and imaging — were analyzed.ResultsA total of 388 patients (median age 6, 46.4% male) were included. Cbl treatment was reported in 45.4% of patients, and significantly higher Cbl levels were observed in this group (p < 0.001). Among those with chronic diseases, leukemia and tumors were the most common. Of the children with elevated Cbl levels, 81.3% were asymptomatic. Five patients with no known chronic diseases or history of supplementation at presentation were later diagnosed with rhabdomyosarcoma, AML, B-cell lymphoma, and CML. Ultrasonographic abnormalities, such as steatosis, hepatosplenomegaly, and increased renal echogenicity, were more frequently observed in patients with high Cbl levels (p = 0.013). Patients with chronic diseases exhibited higher Cbl levels and prolonged normalization periods (p < 0.001).ConclusionAlthough high serum Cbl levels in children are often incidental, persistently elevated levels without a clear cause may indicate serious underlying conditions, including malignancies. Close monitoring, further investigation, and avoidance of unnecessary Cbl supplementation are essential for appropriate clinical management.Keywords: Children, Cobalamin, Hypercobalaminemia, Vitamin B12, Pediatric

Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin, [Cbl]) is a water-soluble vitamin primarily sourced from animal-derived foods. In humans, dietary proteins that contain Cbl are enzymatically hydrolyzed in the stomach and intestines, releasing the vitamin. Subsequently, the liberated Cbl binds to intrinsic factor (IF), a glycoprotein secreted by gastric parietal cells. The resulting IF-Cbl complex is actively absorbed in the terminal ileum and then released into the portal circulation. Within plasma, Cbl is bound either to transcobalamin II (hTC), its biologically active transporter, or to storage proteins collectively referred to as haptocorrins (HC), which include transcobalamin I and transcobalamin III. Approximately 6–20% of circulating Cbl is bound to hTC, whereas 80–94% is associated with HC. hTC is predominantly synthesized by hepatocytes and undergoes renal reabsorption [1–3]. Vitamin B12 plays critical roles in nucleotide synthesis and methyl group transfer reactions [4]. In pediatric populations, deficiency may manifest as megaloblastic anemia and neurological deficits [5].

Elevated serum Cbl levels are often regarded as benign by clinicians due to their asymptomatic nature, and there is no clear consensus regarding their clinical significance. The most commonly identified cause of elevated Cbl levels is high-dose Cbl supplementation. However, elevated Cbl levels have also been documented in conditions such as myeloproliferative disorders, leukemia, solid tumors, as well as liver and kidney diseases [1]. Elevated serum Cbl may either be a consequence of an underlying pathology or represent its initial manifestation. Due to the scarcity of extensive studies, its interpretation remains challenging. Nevertheless, some studies in adults suggest associations between elevated serum Cbl levels and increased mortality and malignancies [6–8]. In contrast, research investigating elevated Cbl levels in pediatric populations remains considerably limited.

In children with elevated serum Cbl levels, the underlying etiological factors remain largely unknown. Furthermore, there is insufficient information regarding the short- and long-term clinical consequences and appropriate management strategies for this condition. This study aimed to investigate the clinical and laboratory findings in children presenting with markedly elevated Cbl levels, with the objective of identifying potential underlying causes and outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study population

Children admitted to the pediatric outpatient clinics at Ankara Bilkent City Hospital between January 2024 and May 2024, and had serum Cbl levels measured for any reason were retrospectively analyzed. Those with serum Cbl levels exceeding 1500 ng/mL and who were evaluated and followed up were included in the study. Patients with acute infections, serum Cbl levels under 1500 ng/mL, those with serum Cbl levels above 1500 ng/mL but not followed up, and those with incomplete clinical data were excluded from the study.

The Cbl supplementation received by the patients was evaluated retrospectively. The supplementation group included patients who had been prescribed Cbl by the clinician in cases of detected serum Cbl deficiency, conditions with accelerated biosynthesis and catabolism, or neurometabolic disorders, as well as patients who used over-the-counter Cbl-containing products as dietary supplements. In the entire study cohort, treatment was found to have been discontinued at the initial presentation, and subgroups were formed as those not receiving supplementation, those receiving oral therapy, and those receiving intramuscular therapy. These subgroups were utilized to evaluate the contribution of supplementation to baseline Cbl levels at presentation and to assess the normalization processes following cessation of supplementation, in the context of comparing potential endogenous and exogenous causes.

Data collection

Patient demographic characteristics (age, sex), serum Cbl levels, follow-up visit dates and corresponding serum Cbl levels, history of oral or intramuscular Cbl supplementation, presence of chronic diseases, hematologic disorders identified during follow-up, laboratory parameters, as well as findings from abdominal and urinary ultrasonography and echocardiography were retrieved from the hospital data management system. Serum Cbl measurements were performed using the chemiluminescence method on the Beckman Coulter Dxl 800 analyzer (CA, USA) (normal range: 211–911 ng/mL).

Data from patients who developed hematologic diseases during follow-up were thoroughly evaluated, and their diagnostic workup following the detection of elevated serum Cbl levels was assessed in detail. Additionally, accompanying findings, and presenting symptoms at the time of elevated Cbl detection were systematically analyzed.

Statistics and ethics

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, Chicago, Illinois, USA). Normality of numerical and continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and by visual inspection of histograms. Data that did not show a normal distribution were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were represented as percentages (%) and frequencies (n).

The Mann-Whitney U test was utilized for comparisons between two groups of non-normally distributed continuous variables, whereas the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied for comparisons of more than two groups. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Pearson Chi-square test or Fisher’s Exact Test, as appropriate. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate relationships between non-normally distributed variables. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Ankara Bilkent City Hospital in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

A total of 388 patients were included in this study. Among these, 46.4% (n = 180) were male, with a median age of 6 years (IQR: 3–12). The median serum Cbl level was 1960 ng/mL (IQR: 1630–2960 ng/mL).

Of the patients, 45.4% (n = 176) were receiving Cbl treatment; among them, 51.7% (n = 91) were treated with oral formulations, while 48.3% (n = 85) were receiving intramuscular injections. The median serum Cbl level in patients not receiving treatment was 1779 ng/mL (IQR: 1582–2247 ng/mL), whereas in those receiving treatment, the median level was 2444 ng/mL (IQR: 1838–3660 ng/mL). Serum Cbl levels were significantly higher in patients receiving treatment (oral or intramuscular) compared to those not receiving it (p < 0.001). Furthermore, intramuscular treatment resulted in significantly higher serum Cbl levels than oral treatment (p < 0.001). Cbl treatment was discontinued at the initial visit in all patients who were receiving it.

Among the patients, 28.1% (n = 109) had underlying chronic diseases. Leukemia was the most frequently identified chronic condition, followed by solid tumors and Cbl metabolism disorders, respectively (Table 1). The overall median Cbl level in patients with chronic diseases was 2628 ng/mL (IQR: 1791–4258). Of these patients, 56.8% (n = 62) received Cbl treatment. The median Cbl level was 1871 ng/mL (IQR: 1698–3803) in those not receiving treatment, compared to 3134 ng/mL (IQR: 2141–4479) in those who were treated, showing a statistically significant difference (p = 0.004). A total of 71.9% (n = 279) of patients had no underlying chronic disease. Among these patients, the median Cbl level was 1746 ng/mL (IQR: 1577–2083) in those not receiving treatment, whereas it was 2188 ng/mL (IQR: 1748–2976) in those receiving Cbl treatment. A statistically significant difference was observed between these two groups (p < 0.001).

Distribution of chronic diseases and supplementation rates

Of the patients without underlying diseases, 81.3% (n = 227) had incidentally detected elevated Cbl levels without any associated clinical symptoms, while 18.7% (n = 52) presented with nonspecific symptoms. The most frequently reported symptoms in these patients were loss of appetite (n = 11, 21.2%), fatigue (n = 10, 19.3%), headache (n = 6, 11.5%), and abdominal pain (n = 6, 11.5%). Less commonly reported symptoms included malaise (n = 5, 9.6%), nausea (n = 4, 7.7%), musculoskeletal pain (n = 3, 5.8%), palpitations (n = 3, 5.8%), dizziness (n = 2, 3.8%), and restlessness (n = 2, 3.8%). In contrast, patients with chronic diseases (n = 109) primarily presented due to disease-related symptoms or during routine follow-up visits.

Among patients without chronic diseases, the median serum Cbl level was 2693 pg/mL (IQR = 2288–3753) in those presenting with nonspecific symptoms, compared to 1759 pg/mL (IQR = 1581–2086) in asymptomatic individuals. A statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups (p < 0.001). In the nonspecific symptom group, 21.2% (n = 11) did not attend follow-up visits, and 26.9% (n = 14) exhibited persistently elevated Cbl levels at follow-up. The median time to normalization in this group was 3 months (IQR = 1–8). In the asymptomatic group, 58.6% (n = 133) did not attend follow-up visits, and 5.3% (n = 12) had persistently elevated Cbl levels at follow-up. The median time to normalization in this group was 4 months (IQR = 2–10). No significant difference was found between the two groups in terms of time to normalization (p > 0.05).

In the overall study population, weak positive correlations were observed between serum Cbl levels and creatinine, total bilirubin, and direct bilirubin. In contrast, weak negative correlations were found between Cbl levels and albumin, white blood cell count, absolute lymphocyte count, and hemoglobin levels. Among patients not receiving Cbl treatment, Cbl levels showed weak negative correlations with albumin, ALP, ALC, Hemoglobin, and HCT, while weak positive correlations were identified with total and direct bilirubin levels. In patients undergoing Cbl treatment, a weak negative correlation was noted with albumin, whereas weak positive correlations were again observed with total and direct bilirubin levels (Table 2). Ultrasonographic (USG) pathologies were more frequently observed in patients with elevated Cbl levels (p = 0.013) (Table 3).

Ultrasonographic findings of the study group

It was determined that 47.4% of patients (n = 184) did not attend their follow-up appointments. Among those who attended, despite the discontinuation of Cbl treatments across the entire study group, 24.5% (n = 50) continued to exhibit elevated Cbl levels, while 75.5% (n = 154) returned to normal levels. The median normalization time for Cbl levels was four months (IQR: 2–9 months). During the follow-up visits, no statistically significant differences were observed in Cbl levels between patients who received treatment and those who did not (p > 0.05). Similarly, no significant differences were found between treated and untreated patients among those without chronic illnesses (p > 0.05). Additionally, no significant differences were detected between patients receiving oral and intramuscular treatments during the follow-up period (p > 0.05).

Patients whose Cbl levels remained elevated during follow-up had significantly higher initial Cbl levels at presentation compared to those whose Cbl levels returned to normal (p < 0.001). Moreover, serum Cbl levels during follow-up were higher in patients with chronic diseases compared to those without (p < 0.001). However, a more pronounced decline in serum Cbl levels was observed in patients without chronic diseases (p = 0.015).

Five patients initially presented with nonspecific symptoms, and elevated serum Cobalamin (Cbl) levels were incidentally identified, representing 1.29% of the study cohort. None of the patients had a known medical history, and none were receiving Cbl treatment. All were subsequently diagnosed with hematologic or solid malignancies following the detection of elevated serum Cbl levels at presentation. These diagnoses were determined to have been established concurrently with the initial presentation in which elevated Cbl levels were detected. Patient 1 presented with hearing and vision loss; the initial Cbl level was 1661 ng/mL, and a diagnosis of rhabdomyosarcoma was established. The Cbl level returned to the reference range within six months. Patient 2 presented with fatigue; the initial Cbl level was 3019 ng/mL. A diagnosis of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) was made, and the Cbl level normalized within five months. Patient 3 presented with neck pain; the initial Cbl level was 1697 ng/mL, and B-cell lymphoma was diagnosed. The Cbl level returned to normal within three months. Patient 4 also presented with fatigue; the initial Cbl level was 1664 ng/mL, and AML was diagnosed. The Cbl level normalized within five months. Patient 5 presented with dyspeptic symptoms; the initial Cbl level was 3878 ng/mL, and a diagnosis of chronic myeloid leukemi was made. The Cbl level returned to the reference range within two months. None of these patients had a previously known systemic illness, and none had been receiving Cbl treatment. All diagnoses were made following the incidental detection of elevated serum Cbl levels at the time of presentation (Table 4).

Discussion

The clinical significance of elevated serum Cbl levels remains unclear in both adults and children; moreover, the current literature on this topic is limited. No standardized follow-up strategy has been established for incidentally discovered elevated Cbl levels. The coexistence of any medical condition in children with elevated Cbl often raises concerns among clinicians and parents. This study aims to elucidate potential causes of elevated Cbl levels and explore their relationship with chronic diseases in a large pediatric population.

It has been reported that treatment is the most common cause of elevated Cbl levels. Particularly in children, challenges in accurately adjusting the oral or intramuscular dosages according to available formulations and inadvertent overdosing may lead to elevated serum Cbl levels [9, 10]. A published meta-analysis found no significant difference between oral and intramuscular supplementation in the correction of Cbl deficiency [11]. However, some studies suggest that higher doses of oral preparations may be required to achieve serum Cbl levels comparable to those obtained via intramuscular administration [12, 13]. In our study, consistent with existing literature, children receiving supplementation demonstrated higher serum Cbl levels. Similarly, intramuscular supplementation was associated with higher Cbl levels compared to oral preparations. Despite higher initial serum Cbl concentrations in children receiving intramuscular treatment, both groups reached normalization within a similar timeframe after treatment discontinuation. This finding suggests that, even with a greater Cblload, the body may activate compensatory mechanisms to enhance elimination efficiency, reflecting the adaptive nature of Cbl metabolism.

The clinical significance of elevated serum Cbl levels remains unclear. Previous studies noted that elevated Cbl levels are primarily incidental findings without specific clinical manifestations (1–3,11,15). However, several studies suggest that patients with elevated serum Cbl levels may paradoxically exhibit symptoms similar to those of Cbl deficiency. This condition is believed to result from impaired tissue uptake and altered biological activity of Cbl (15). In our study, symptomatic patients primarily presented with either nonspecific symptoms or complaints directly related to their underlying chronic diseases. Determining the origin of nonspecific symptoms was not possible due to the retrospective design and the limitations of available records. These symptoms may be associated both with elevated serum Cbl levels and with the clinical manifestations of comorbid conditions. Therefore, in our study, symptoms are presented solely in a descriptive manner, without any inference of causality. However, the finding that patients without chronic diseases but presenting with nonspecific symptoms had higher median serum Cbl levels, and that this elevation persisted at a higher rate compared to asymptomatic patients, could suggest a potential association between these symptoms and elevated Cbl levels. Nevertheless, the limited sample size reduces the strength of this interpretation.

In a study conducted on adults, a weak positive correlation was found between serum levels of Cbl and AST, direct bilirubin, and creatinine; conversely, a weak negative correlation was observed between serum Cbl and albumin, RBC count, and hemoglobin levels [14]. Another study revealed significant differences between serum Cbl levels and creatinine, albumin, hemoglobin, and alkaline phosphatase levels. Furthermore, an increase in serum Cbl was specifically correlated with lower albumin levels. The same study, however, found no significant relationship between serum Cbl levels and complete blood count parameters (26). A pediatric study similarly reported a negative correlation between serum albumin and Cbl levels [10]. In our current research, we have also identified various parameters exhibiting weak positive or negative correlations with serum Cbl. Consistent with previous findings, albumin levels demonstrated a persistent negative correlation with serum Cbl. Although the clinical implications of this relationship have not yet been clearly defined in the literature, increasing clinicians’ awareness of this association may prove valuable in the future.

Globally, measured serum Cbl levels reflect both the active and storage forms of the vitamin. The serum Cbl levels measured in our study similarly reflect both the active and storage forms. Therefore, patients with elevated serum Cbl levels may exhibit high, normal, or low levels of the active form, hTC. Although previous studies have generally indicated a positive correlation between serum Cbl and hTC levels, Albayrak et al. reported laboratory Cbl levels greater than 1000 pg/mL in 40 pediatric patients, whose hTC levels remained within normal ranges [10, 15, 16]. However, hTC is not commonly utilized as a routine laboratory indicator, and its measurement is more costly compared to serum Cbl analysis. Some research suggests that evaluating hTC may offer slightly superior diagnostic accuracy relative to Cbl measurement alone [17, 18]. Similarly, other studies emphasize the advantages of hTC assessment [19, 20]. Conversely, Miller et al. [21] have recommended evaluating both parameters concurrently. Although hTC is not routinely measured in all centers due to its higher cost, its assessment may be particularly beneficial in selected cases.

USG is one of the most frequently utilized imaging methods in the clinical evaluation of liver and kidney diseases. The literature frequently highlights the association between elevated serum Cbl levels and liver and kidney disorders [4, 5, 22, 23]. In our study, USG findings from more than half of the group were analyzed, revealing that the most commonly observed pathological findings included fatty liver, hepatosplenomegaly, and increased renal echogenicity. These pathological features predominantly indicate liver or kidney disease, thereby reinforcing the association between elevated serum Cbl levels and these disorders, as reported in the literature. The primary cause of elevated serum Cbl levels in kidney disease is believed to be related to decreased renal clearance, while in liver diseases, an increased release from damaged hepatocytes and reduced hepatic uptake are considered the primary mechanisms [1–3]. Although it is presumed that Cbl supplementation administered to patients with chronic diseases contributes to elevated serum Cbl levels, our study observed that approximately half of the patients with chronic diseases did not receive any supportive therapy, suggesting that the observed elevation may primarily result from inherent physiological mechanisms.

Elevated Cbl levels may occur in acute leukemias, myeloproliferative diseases such as polycythemia vera, lymphoproliferative disorders, and multiple myeloma [1]. The increased serum Cbl in hematological conditions is thought to be associated with an increased release of haptocorrin from proliferating leukocytes [1–3]. Notably, our study identified children with diagnoses such as rhabdomyosarcoma, acute myeloid leukemia (AML), B-cell lymphoma, and chronic myeloid leukemia among those presenting with elevated serum Cbl levels. The identification of five newly diagnosed malignancies among patients presenting with elevated serum Cbl levels is of particular interest. While this number may seem small relative to the overall study population, the fact that elevated Cbl levels were most commonly observed in patients diagnosed with leukemia and solid tumors supports a potential association between elevated Cbl levels and underlying malignancies. In the literature, leukemia has been reported to develop in 2 of 40 pediatric patients with elevated serum Cbl levels [10]. This proportion is substantially higher than the reported annual incidence of new leukemia cases in children, approximately 4 per 100,000 [24]. In our study, the incidence of newly diagnosed malignancies (1.29%) also appears elevated compared to the general population. However, given that our institution is a tertiary referral center and the retrospective design of the study, it cannot be definitively concluded whether this difference reflects a true increase in risk. Additionally, patients with chronic diseases exhibited higher serum Cbl levels, which also declined more slowly during follow-up compared to patients without chronic diseases. While vitamin B12 supplementation may partly explain elevated levels, approximately half of the chronic disease patients in our study did not receive supplementation, suggesting that intrinsic physiological mechanisms may be responsible. Given the limited clinical manifestations of hematological disorders in pediatric populations, careful monitoring of children with elevated serum Cbl levels may be warranted to identify potential hematological diseases in their early stages.

Limitations

While this study provides meaningful insights into serum Cbl levels in the pediatric population, several limitations should be acknowledged. Its retrospective and single-center design may have contributed to variability in follow-up intervals and data completeness. Moreover, detailed information on the duration and dosage of vitamin B12 treatment was unavailable. The chronic disease group encompassed heterogeneous clinical entities, including leukemia, solid tumors, and metabolic disorders. Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the limited number of patients in each subgroup, statistical analyses were performed only as “presence versus absence of chronic disease.”. Although most patients with chronic conditions were regularly monitored at our center, baseline Cbl levels at the time of diagnosis were not consistently documented across the hospital system, potentially limiting subgroup analyses. In our cohort, the malignancy group was also heterogeneous, and the small number of patients within each subtype precluded statistical comparisons across malignancy subtypes. The fact that our institution is a tertiary referral center, along with specific population characteristics, may have contributed to the higher observed malignancy rates compared to the general population. Therefore, the malignancy frequency reported in this study should not be interpreted as a population-based estimate of true risk increase. Additionally, in oncology patients receiving chemotherapy, complete information on treatment timing and protocols was not available. Consequently, the potential effects of chemotherapy on hematologic and biochemical parameters could not be assessed in a separate analysis. This limitation may particularly affect the interpretation of relationships between these parameters and serum Cbl levels. Despite these limitations, our study offers valuable preliminary insights into serum Cbl dynamics in children and may serve as a foundation for future prospective research in this field.

Recommendations & conclusion

Clinicians should adopt a systematic approach when elevated serum Cbl levels are encountered. A comprehensive clinical history and detailed physical examination are essential during the initial evaluation. If the patient is receiving Cbl supplementation, it should be discontinued at the time of presentation, and appropriate follow-up assessments should be scheduled. In the absence of pathological findings, re-evaluation of serum Cbl levels after four months is recommended. If clinical or physical abnormalities are present, condition-specific investigations should be pursued. In cases where hepatic or renal involvement is suspected, abdominal ultrasonography may be warranted. Additionally, periodic monitoring of serum Cbl levels every four months may be considered for surveillance purposes. In individuals with chronic conditions, serum Cbl levels may remain elevated or decline more slowly, even in the absence of supplementation. Therefore, regular monitoring of serum Cbl levels in this population is advised, as persistent elevation may serve as an early indicator of potential hematologic disorders later in life.
