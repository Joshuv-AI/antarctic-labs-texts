A large-scale evaluation of provider-patient matching in an employer-sponsored mental health program
Authors: Emily J. Ward, Matt Hawrilenko, Minyu Wu, Jon Novak, Millard Brown, Adam M. Chekroud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Many patients do not improve during their first round of psychotherapy, partly due to variability in the quality of the patient-provider relationship. Matching patients to providers based on providers’ historical performance with similar patients offers a proactive, data-driven solution. In a real-world retrospective cohort study, 24,303 participants using a mental health benefit (Spring Health) from 2021–2024 chose either matched therapists (i.e., based on providers’ continuity of care, their historical patient-improvement percentile, and clinical alignment) or self-selected a therapist from a general list. Patients with depression using matched rather than self-selected therapists improved 8.5% faster (b = 0.12-point reduction on PHQ-9 per log-day, [95% CI: −0.15 to −0.09], p < 0.001) and were more likely to achieve reliable change (OR = 1.09 [1.05–1.14], p < 0.001), with similar results for anxiety and PTSD. Higher patient-provider fit scores predicted stronger therapeutic alliance (b = 0.05 [0.04–0.06], p < 0.001). Matching also reduced the total cost of care by 12.9% per reliable change and 10.8% per recovery. Thus, a data-driven patient-provider matching algorithm that is informed by providers’ historical records with similar patients can provide modest but consistent acceleration of symptom improvement, increase clinical outcomes, and yield cost savings.

Introduction

Despite an increasing emphasis on evidence-based interventions in mental health care, fewer than half of adults starting psychotherapy achieve reliable improvement in their first course of care1. While some treatments may work better than others, genuine differences in efficacy exist for only a handful of disorders2, suggesting that a combination of individual patient characteristics3–5 and therapists’ performance6,7 moderates how well a given treatment protocol works. Overall, the quality of the relationship between patient and provider remains a consistent predictor of clinical outcomes8–10. This suggests, however, that the strongest predictors of a patient’s clinical outcomes depend on which clinician they happen to meet.

A strong therapeutic alliance, defined by mutual agreement on goals, the tasks to achieve those goals, and a positive bond11,12, frequently determines whether a patient stays in treatment13 and whether meaningful improvement occurs8–10. This relationship extends beyond the provider’s theoretical orientation or training in specific evidence-based protocols. However, the alliance can only be measured after treatment has begun, making it a lagging metric. When it becomes evident that a patient-provider match is poor, patients drop out of treatment early13 after investing time, money, and emotional energy, thus losing the chance to improve. Indeed, the average dropout rate for psychotherapy for depression is around 17–20 percent14, and higher for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)15.

Most mental health systems still assign patients to the first available slot; however, they leave the therapeutic alliance to chance and economics, despite previous meta-analyses16 and empirical studies17,18 suggesting that providers’ historical performance meaningfully predicts future outcomes. Few implementations have assessed this approach at scale5 for various reasons, such as a lack of a centralized electronic health record (EHR) and consistent measurement-based care (MBC) to create these needed datasets.

We report the first implementation of a patient-provider matching algorithm at scale. The algorithm recommends providers based on their proven success with clinically similar patients. Embedded within a centralized, employer-sponsored mental health benefit, the algorithm generated a patient-provider “fit” score after an initial clinical assessment, ranking over 6000 licensed therapists and surfacing the top four providers specific to each patient. For patients initiating care between January 2021 and November 2024, over 24,000 chose a therapist from the recommended matches or self-selected based on their own browsing. We compared symptom improvement, likelihood of reliable change or recovery, and cost savings for patients who used matched providers compared to self-selected providers. To determine the specificity of the effects, outcomes were evaluated for patients who screened positive for depression, anxiety, or PTSD.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Yale Institutional Review Board (IRB protocol ID: 2000029276) and classified as not involving research with human subjects. Informed consent was not required, as the data were anonymized prior to analysis. The research adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational studies.

Program design

Data came from an employer-sponsored digital mental health benefit (Spring Health; Spring Care Inc) that includes free or low-cost access to psychotherapy and medication management through video or in-person sessions, care navigation, and digital self-help mental wellness resources. Care is coordinated through a centralized data system in which all provider notes, patient data, assessment results, and patient scheduling are integrated. This centralized system supports several evidence-based components to increase access to and utilization of mental healthcare: provider availability in about one day nationwide, direct patient booking into their selected provider’s appointment, and implementation of measurement-based care.

Participants completed an initial assessment (see Questionnaires) and received a personalized care plan according to their responses. They could browse available providers and then select and schedule appointments on their own (see Provider Recommendations). Therapists held master’s or doctoral-level licenses, and all medication providers were either physicians or psychiatric nurse practitioners.

To lower the financial barriers to accessing care, participants could book an unlimited number of free appointments with a care navigator and had access to a varying number of employer-sponsored sessions with a therapist and/or medication provider (median = 8, IQR = [7–10]). Additional sessions were covered as an in-network benefit via the health plan and were subject to deductibles, co-pays and co-insurance rules of each health plan.

Setting

Treatment was primarily delivered through video conferencing, and assessments were collected using the program platform. The study period was from January 1, 2021 and November 1, 2024, and only the first epoch of care (no more than 6 months could elapse between therapy sessions) was considered. Appointment and assessment data were collected up to 6 months after the first therapy appointment. The final study size was determined by how many participants met the inclusion criteria (see Participants).

Participants

Participants were employees and dependents eligible for the benefit during the study period. Participants were included in the current study if they took an assessment and started treatment within this study period, if they were located in the U.S., were over 18, had a baseline assessment within 30 days prior to starting treatment, if they screened positive for depression and/or anxiety (PHQ-9 ≥ 10 or GAD-7 ≥ 10) and if they had an additional assessment after treatment began. Participants must have started therapy during the study date and remained eligible for the benefit for at least six months after starting, across which period data were collected. Only the first epoch of care (no more than 6 months could elapse between therapy sessions) was considered, and assessments taken up to 6 months after the first therapy appointment were included. Participants did not receive a monetary reward.

Questionnaires

The program included a digital mental health assessment tool for initial screening and outcome monitoring. For their initial screening, individuals completed a series of self-report questionnaires to identify common mental health difficulties (such as stress, anxiety, eating, or substance use issues), including the Patient Health Questionnaire 9-item scale (PHQ-9)19,20 for depression and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7)21 and targeted questionnaires for any specific issues. (e.g., patients indicating any issues related to trauma were given the PC-PTSD questionnaire). All assessments were optional. For longitudinal outcome monitoring, participants were reminded every 2–4 weeks to complete follow-up questionnaires, consisting of PHQ-9 and/or GAD-7 assessments for most conditions. While some providers encouraged patients to take follow-up assessments, assessments were not required during or at the end of treatment. As a result, many participants stayed in treatment but had sparse assessment data.

Measures

Depression symptoms were measured with the PHQ-9 (range, 0–27), consisting of nine items assessing the frequency of a range of depression symptoms. Anxiety symptoms were measured with the GAD-7 (range, 0–21), consisting of seven items assessing the frequency of a range of anxiety symptoms. PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores were outcome variables for assessing symptom change (continuous outcomes), reliable improvement22 (5-point decrease in the PHQ-920 and 4-point decrease in the GAD-7), recovery (achieving a score in the subclinical range19, corresponding to a PHQ-9 score <10 or a GAD7 score of <10), and recovery with reliable improvement. Treatment response (50% reduction in symptomatology) was also included as an outcome variable to facilitate comparison to recent meta-analyses23 (Supplemental Results). Depression and anxiety symptoms were modeled separately. For patients scoring positive for PTSD, GAD-7 was used as a pragmatic proxy to capture symptoms because the GAD-7 assessment was taken more consistently across treatment and yielded substantially more longitudinal coverage than PTSD-specific measures. In contrast, condition-specific instruments for PTSD (e.g., PCL-5) were completed by a smaller subset of patients, limiting statistical power and comparability across providers.

The primary outcome was change in clinical symptoms over time (log-days in treatment, measured from the start of therapy [t = 0]). Log-day refers to a nonlinear, log-transformed version of treatment time that reflects the fact that clinical change is often faster early in treatment and slows over time. In the clinical outcome models that use log-day, equal changes in log-day correspond to proportionally faster changes early on and slower changes later, allowing the models to better capture early symptom improvement followed by stabilization. Clinical outcomes were estimated at 1-week post-treatment, corresponding to the average duration of care across all patients plus one week [t = 97 days (average duration) + 7 days]. Number of appointments was also used to predict reliable improvement as an alternative measure of time in care. Therapeutic alliance (range, 0–10) was the average score across three items on which patients rated their providers on (mutual goals, good working relationship, and agreement on best approach). Note that not all patients provided feedback on their alliance with their providers, so in analyses including therapeutic alliance as a factor, only those patient-provider dyads with scores were included.

Provider recommendations

Participants selected therapists through an online appointment booking system that allows participants to search for a provider based on filters such as availability, location, and provider characteristics (gender, race/ethnicity, conditions treated, etc.). Providers were listed based on the next available appointment. In addition, 4 providers, representing the best potential clinical match to that specific participant, were recommended to participants via a “Your Top Match” flag as part of the therapist profile. Thus, participants could choose to start care with either a “self-selected” therapist (from the overall list of providers) or a “matched” therapist (from the recommendations).

The recommendation system had some limitations due primarily to patient browsing patterns, resulting in many patient-provider pairs lacking a selection method designation (Fig. 1, final step). Patients searched freely for therapists without booking an appointment and commonly searched at different times before committing to start care. This freedom, combined with the dynamic nature of the recommendation algorithm, made assigning a selection method to patient-provider pairs difficult. For example, (1) providers could show up as recommended at one point, then shift to the self-selection list at another point (or vice versa) as their historical data and fit scores were updated, (2) providers may not have had any availability when the patient first searched for a provider, but became available later, or (3) a new provider could have shown up in the self-selected list, then shifted to the recommendations after the algorithm features were updated to better promote new providers. Additionally, if the recommendation feature was not yet available for a specific employer, it was not possible to classify the provider as matched or self-selected. As a result, the providers who ultimately delivered care may have been biased toward those who consistently appeared as available and those who demonstrated more consistent historical performance (either stronger or weaker) in order to rise to the recommendation level or always remain outside of it.Fig. 1Diagram depicting patient participant flow through the study from identification to study inclusion.

Patient-provider matching

The recommended providers were based on a score calculated using a weighted combination of the (1) provider’s rate of continuity of care, (2) the provider’s historical patient performance, and (3) the clinical alignment between patient and provider.

Continuity of care was defined as how often a provider’s patients continue treatment for at least three appointments within 90 days. This measure was not personalized per se to the patient searching for a provider, but calculated over all of the provider’s prior patients. Historical performance was defined as the percentile rank of provider-level historical improvement in depression symptoms or the percentile rank of provider-level historical improvement in anxiety symptoms. If the patient scored positive for depression, the historical performance for depression improvement was used, and the same for anxiety (although not for any more specific subsets of conditions). Clinical alignment was the similarity between the provider’s self-report of their areas of specialty and the mental health conditions that flagged positive during that specific patient’s intake assessment. For both provider and patient, there were four categories: depression or anxiety, substance use disorders, eating disorders, and/or post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). To calculate the clinical alignment between a patient and provider, the conditions were coded as vectors of 1’s or 0’s, yielding a vector of four values for both provider and patient, and the cosine similarity was calculated between the two vectors. For instance, a therapist specializing in substance use disorder and anxiety would score higher for a patient flagged for these two conditions compared to a therapist specializing in just one or the other.

Fit scores were calculated using continuity of care, historical improvement percentile, and clinical alignment. This provided a fit score between 0 and 1, where 0 represents the worst possible fit, and 1 represents the best possible fit. For providers with little to no historical data, an additional slot was added to recommendations to support newer providers, ensure balanced utilization, and help prevent over-recommending of top therapists. Scores were dynamically calculated by the platform backend for many patient-provider pairs (but not all, see Fig. 1, final step), and those with the top scores were surfaced as recommendations to patients. Patients were not aware of the underlying numeric score.

Missing data

Although this study was missing demographic data and many people were excluded from analyses due to a lack of an appropriately timed baseline assessment, the type of missing data most likely to bias the results was missing data due to assessment drop-out, where patients did not complete any follow-up assessment after starting care. 55% of potential patients were excluded for this reason (Fig. 1). Assessment drop-out could correspond to treatment drop-out, and if the matched and self-selected groups actually differed in terms of the amount of treatment they received, but this was obscured by the follow-up assessment requirement, it could bias our results. The impact of this missingness was assessed by reweighting the data according to the treatment dosage distribution of those without follow-up assessments (Supplemental Results).

Statistical analyses

Predictor variables were mean-centered and standardized prior to analysis. Provider selection method [matched vs. self-selected] was binarized and mean-centered. We used 3-level mixed-effects regression models to estimate clinical outcomes, with random intercepts to account for clustering of repeated observations (level-1) within participants (level-2) and providers (level-3). An identity link was used for continuous outcomes (depression and anxiety symptoms) and a logit link for categorical outcomes (reliable change). Symptom change, modeled using log-days since the start of treatment, was the main effect of interest. To determine whether choosing a matched therapist was associated with symptom change, the provider selection method by log-days was included as an interaction term. Reliable improvement and recovery were predicted separately using logistic models with log-days since the start of treatment as the main effect. Regression coefficients from these models were converted to probabilities to estimate the percentage of participants who showed reliable improvement from depression and anxiety symptoms. Reliable improvement was also predicted using the number of appointments, with marginal effects estimated using the delta method24. The treatment effect size was quantified using Cohen’s d, calculated by dividing the total treatment improvement in the measure of interest by the baseline SD25. Effect sizes were categorized using established thresholds of small (d < 0.50), medium (0.50 < d < 0.8), and large (d > 0.8).

All statistical tests were 2-sided with statistical significance set at α levels of .05. All analyses were conducted in R, version 2024.04.226.

Results

Participant and provider characteristics

Of 13,002,939 total eligible people, 1,305,481 (10%) enrolled in the benefit during the study period. Among those, 24,303 met the eligibility criteria (Fig. 1) and had an appointment with a provider who could be classified as chosen from recommendations or self-selected. Participants were treated by 6119 providers.

Providers were predominantly female (82.3%). 51.3% were white, 22.9% were black, 8.2% were Hispanic or Latino, and 5.2% were Asian. The top credentials were LICSW (36.4%), LMHC (37.8%) and LMFT (9.6%). On average, they had 9.3 months of tenure at Spring Health and had received their licensure 9.7 years prior. Across the provider network, approximately 58% of therapists were shown in recommendations; 20% of providers included in recommendations were new to the network.

Participants’ age ranged from 18–80 years (mean = 37.2, SD = 11.4). 1943 of 2702 (71.9%) participants with known gender information were female; and 3066 of 5159 (59.4%) participants with known race information were white. 19,334 participants screened positive for depression (scores ≥ 10 points) and 18,538 screened positive for anxiety (scores ≥ 10 points). 13,569 screened positive for both depression and anxiety, corresponding to a 55.8% comorbidity rate, similar to the general population27–29. Among the participants screening positive, baseline scores were moderate to severe for both PHQ-9 (mean [SD], 15.4 [4.3]) and GAD-7 (mean [SD], 14.6 [3.3]).

Overall utilization

During the study period, the average number of business days to the next available appointment was 1.2 days (SD = 0.4 days). The average number of therapy sessions was 6.7 (SD = 4.7), and the average number of medication management sessions (among those who had any) was 2.7 (SD = 2.0). Participants spent approximately 3 months in treatment (97 [58] days), with 1-week post-treatment defined as 104 days. Including the baseline assessment, the average number of total assessments was 3.3.

Algorithmically matched vs. self-selected therapists

The median fit score was 0.81 (IQR: 0.72-0.86) for self-selected therapists and 0.85 (IQR: 0.82–0.90) for matched therapists. 13,305 patients used self-selected therapists (16,225 patient-participant dyads), 12,662 used matched therapists (12,885 dyads), and 1664 patients had appointments with both kinds (Supplemental Table 1). The difference in the number of dyads reflects the fact that some participants had appointments with multiple providers: patients using matched therapists had appointments with an average of 1.02 providers, whereas patients using self-selected therapists had appointments with an average of 1.14 providers.

Patients who started care with matched therapists spent 96.6 days in care, and those who started care with self-selected therapists spent 97.2 days; those with matched therapists attended an average of 6.6 sessions, while those with self-selected therapists attended 7.1 sessions. 21.4% of those with self-selected therapists and 18.7% of those with recommended therapists also accessed a psychiatry/medication session (χ2 = 30.56, p < 0.001). Those with matched therapists took on average 3.4 assessments (including baseline), and those with self-selected therapists took 3.3.

For participants scoring positive for depression, depression symptoms decreased by -1.48 points (95% −1.49 to −1.46, p < 0.001; Table 2) with each log-day in care. Symptom reduction was 8.5% faster in matched versus self-selected therapists (b = −0.12, 95% CI: −0.15 to −0.09, p < 0.001) and resulted in a 0.68-point difference (d = 0.2) in depression symptoms by the end of treatment. For reliable change, there was a significant interaction between provider selection method (matched and self-selected) by log-day (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.05–1.14, p < 0.001; Table 2), corresponding to 86.9% of patients using matched therapists achieving reliable change by 1-week post-treatment, compared to 82.6% of patients using a self-selected therapist (absolute difference = 4.3%) (Fig. 2A). Reliable change by time in treatment and provider selection method

For participants with PTSD, anxiety symptoms decreased by −1.31 points (95% −1.33 to −1.28, p < 0.001) (Table 1) with each log-day in care. Symptom reduction was 6.4% faster with matched vs. self-selected therapists (b = −0.08, 95% CI: −0.13 to −0.03, p < 0.001), corresponding to a difference of 0.50 points at post-treatment (d = 0.15). In terms of reliable change, there was a significant interaction between provider selection method (matched and self-selected) by log-day (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.04–1.15, p = 0.001; Table 2), corresponding to 87.3% of patients using matched therapists achieving reliable change by 1-week post-treatment, compared to 82.8% of patients using a self-selected therapist (difference = 4.5%) (Fig. 2C). For recovery, there was also as a significant interaction between provider selection method (matched and self-selected) by log-day (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.04–1.16, p = 0.001), corresponding to 77.3% of patients using matched therapists achieving recovery compared to 71.3% with self-selected therapists (absolute difference = 6.0%). When considering reliable change and recovery together, 72.1% of patients using matched therapists achieved reliable change and recovery by 1-week post-treatment, compared to 65.3% of patients using a self-selected therapist (absolute difference = 6.8%).

Components of patient-provider fit

Overall, choosing a matched therapist led to greater symptom reduction and a higher likelihood of achieving reliable change and recovery. Furthermore, within the group using matched therapists, the better the fit between the patient and provider (i.e., a score generated from continuity of care, historical improvement, and clinical alignment), the better the therapeutic alliance (b = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.04–0.06, p < 0.001). This indicates that higher fit scores served as a leading indicator of a stronger patient-provider relationship.

Which of these components of the patient-provider fit contributed to clinical improvement? Within the matched group, regression analysis revealed continuity of care and provider historical performance significantly predicted reliable change (continuity: OR = 1.037, 95% CI: 1.013–1.062, p = 0.003; historical performance: OR = 1.039, 95% CI: 1.014–1.065, p = 0.002), and alignment did not (p = 0.684). Across both matched and self-selected groups, the pattern of results was similar (Supplemental Table 2, continuity: OR = 1.043, 95% CI: 1.018–1.069, p = 0.001; historical performance: OR = 1.051, 95% CI: 1.027–1.075, p < 0.001; alignment OR = 1.003, 95% CI: 0.985–1.023, p = 0.727). When these components were included in the model, the selection method remained significant (OR = 1.074, 95% CI: 1.017–1.134, p = 0.010), suggesting that the clinical benefit of matched vs. self-selected therapists is not fully explained by the components used to generate the match. However, the selection method did not interact with any of the components (ps > 0.254), indicating that the benefit of higher continuity of care and historical performance does not depend on the selection method.

Cost savings for matching providers

Provider matching also reduced the total cost of the care per patient who reliably improved, with 12.9% lower cost per reliable change and 10.8% lower cost per recovery for those using matched providers compared to those using self-selected providers (Supplementary Table 3; Supplementary Results).

Discussion

We evaluated the first implementation of a patient-provider matching algorithm at scale. Patients who chose an algorithm-recommended therapist improved faster, were more likely to achieve reliable change or recovery, and cost less to treat than those who self-selected a provider, despite receiving the same benefit design. While the size of these effects was small, they were consistent and statistically significant across depression, anxiety, and PTSD, providing evidence of modest, incremental advantages of algorithm-recommended matching rather than large, transformative changes at the individual level.

By leveraging data on historical provider performance, such as the provider’s rate of continuity of care, their historical patient-improvement percentile, and the clinical alignment between patient and provider, the algorithm recommends providers who are better able to keep patients from dropping out of care early (a persistent problem for psychotherapy14) and who consistently achieve successful results with patients of similar profiles. Moreover, because the algorithm relied on dynamic, pragmatic input and promoted new providers, the majority of therapists (58%) appeared in recommendations at one point or another. Rather than overloading a small subset of exceptional providers, matching as implemented in this study allows a wide variety of therapists to better build a strong patient-provider relationship, as evidenced by the positive relationship between fit score and therapeutic alliance—even though “fit” was calculated prior to the initiation of treatment. By improving the accuracy of patient–provider matching at treatment initiation, the algorithm increases system efficiency and reduces early mismatches and allows most therapists to have more impact.

The key components driving the success of the patient-provider match were the provider’s rate of continuity of care and their historical patient-improvement, but not the clinical alignment between patient and provider, which was calculated based on the patient’s initial assessment and the specialties that providers self-endorse. This result suggests that although matching patients with providers with appropriate specialization may create the potential for better outcomes, the actual driver of improvement is the provider’s record of successfully treating similar patients (using historical performance specific to the patients’ condition) and their ability to keep such patients engaged in care (possibly a more general skill of the provider). An alternative, statistical interpretation is that by using historical performance specifically for depressive patients or anxious patients, alignment is already implicitly included in the model, and so when it is added as a standalone variable, it is unable to explain further variance. When considered together in the form of a “fit score,” these components predicted therapeutic alliance. In other words, the matching algorithm helps lay the groundwork for a positive alliance, and consistent engagement allows that alliance to flourish and translate into clinical gains.

The effect size for the advantage of using matched rather than self-selected therapists is comparable to or exceeds those seen among different therapy modalities (e.g., from near-zero30 to d = 0.202) in meta-analyses. This finding shows that how patients and providers are paired can matter to a similar degree as differences observed between specific evidence-based treatments—especially in large, heterogeneous samples—while also underscoring that such effects are typically modest on average. Studies examining whether patient characteristics can be used to match individuals to one evidence-based treatment over another have similarly found small average effects, despite statistical significance, alongside substantial heterogeneity in who benefits most from matching31. Thus, matching strategies—whether focused on treatment modality or provider characteristics—are unlikely to produce large mean differences across all patients, but may nonetheless yield meaningful benefits when applied within specific subgroups or at scale.

In our study, a centralized care model in which all providers used a shared electronic health record (EHR) and measurement-based care (MBC) was routine, improved care delivery within the benefit program and enabled patient-provider matching. Recent meta-analyses23 show that treatment response rate in psychotherapies at 2 (±1) months after baseline was 41% (95% CI: 38 ~ 43). In comparison, we saw high rates of reliable change and recovery: across all clinical conditions, over 65% of patients achieved reliable change with recovery, with approximately 6.5% more patients achieving the same outcomes with matched therapists. After better equating for methodology differences (Supplementary Results), patients with self-selected therapists exhibited a similar treatment response rate (46.3%), while those using matched therapists continued to see an advantage (54.9%). Beyond methodological differences, outcomes may also reflect differences in the study population and care delivery model: participants accessed employer-sponsored care with no out-of-pocket costs, rapid treatment initiation, and sustained, measurement-based support, likely reducing common barriers such as delays, financial strain, and early dropout. Integrating features that enhance access and support engagement alongside psychotherapy yields outcomes that meet or exceed clinical benchmarks32.

This centralized care model also conferred both the means to refine matching algorithms continuously and the operational flexibility to guide patients toward the providers most likely to help them. Because providers are largely contractors, the program can routinely triage care toward the best-fitting providers without needing to worry about filling caseloads of poorer-performing providers or those who are simply a good fit for fewer patients. The program can quantify and capitalize on a diversity of therapeutic approaches and provider qualities by directly measuring key outcomes, rather than controlling a specific care protocol. Moreover, employing an algorithm to match patients to providers on straightforward metrics may yield incremental improvements in outcomes without requiring additional provider training or added effort from patients. Finally, the scale of the benefit program enables even modest effects to be translated into substantial population-level gains, given the high volume of patients who engage in care33. For example, a 4.3% absolute difference in reliable improvement for those patients who used matched therapists compared to self-selected therapists corresponds to tens of thousands of people when considered across the population eligible for the benefit.

Taken together, these findings suggest both constraints and opportunities for implementing algorithm-guided provider matching in standard clinical settings. Limited caseload capacity and therapist availability may constrain the ability to act on matching recommendations, and the use of relative provider rankings may be more sensitive in in-person settings where clinicians are more likely to know one another. In addition, successful adoption depends on consistent use of MBC and supporting data infrastructure, and as we have shown, expected effect sizes are small. At the same time, the matching approach in this study uses relatively simple and widely available signals: basic appointment data to capture continuity of care, standardized symptom assessments to measure patient improvement and generate provider historical performance, and straightforward alignment between patient intake needs and provider-reported specialties. As a result, while direct replication may be difficult, core elements of the matching framework could plausibly be adapted to smaller practices to reduce early dropout and strategically optimize caseloads for more impact.

A key strength of this evaluation is that it employs real-world data from a large, employer-sponsored mental health program, providing the statistical power to examine multiple conditions and produce generalizable insights. The MBC framework used across all providers ensures consistent data collection and the opportunity for ongoing quality improvement. However, the observational design means the study cannot fully exclude selection bias, whereby patients who opt for recommended providers may differ systematically from those who do not. Moreover, although the algorithm benefits from substantial EHR data, the inclusion criteria for the study result in the exclusion of a substantial number of potential participants, and the applicability of the results may be limited to systems with similar infrastructure and patient demographics. Finally, while fit scores serve as a useful leading indicator of the therapeutic alliance, certain unmeasured factors (e.g., unique interpersonal skills of the provider, patient motivation, external life stressors) may influence outcomes and warrant consideration when interpreting the results. Investigating how recommendations perform in specific subpopulations, or how best to incorporate patient preferences and clinical judgment, could offer deeper insights into maximizing the effectiveness of patient-provider matching.

In summary, the present results illustrate that a recommendation engine—leveraging a matching algorithm based on prior provider performance with similar patients as tracked through a centralized electronic health record—can shorten the path to symptom improvement. Although the individual-level effects are small, these effects can translate into substantial population-level gains, given the high volume of patients who engage in care33 and can reduce the total cost of care per patient who reliably improved or recovered by more than 12%. Thus, matching patients to providers may reduce overall healthcare costs and patient distress, underscoring the financial and clinical value of implementing a matching algorithm in behavioral healthcare at scale.
