Survival analysis of primary molars following pulpectomy performed under dental general anesthesia: a five-year retrospective study
Authors: Zhaohong Chen, Meimei Li, Yanan Chen, Yuanwen Xie, Xiyan Chen, Huanhuan Qiu, Huacui Xiong, Ke Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrently, the indications for pulpectomy of primary molars performed under dental general anesthesia vary across countries. Therefore, we retrospectively investigated the five-year survival rate of primary molars following pulpectomy performed under dental general anesthesia and the impact of this treatment on permanent successors, assessed the risk factors related to overall survival and clarified the indications for pulpectomy.MethodsThe medical records of children receiving pulpectomy of primary molars under dental general anesthesia from August 1, 2013, to November 30, 2023, were reviewed. Potential risk factors, including gender, age, general health, tooth type, tooth location, endodontic diagnosis and quality of root filling, were assessed via univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models, and the survival rate was examined via the Kaplan‒Meier technique. Moreover, the rate of resorption of the root canal filling materials, degree of resorption of the overfilled/over-extended root canal filling materials and development of permanent successors were assessed by clinical and radiographic examination.ResultsThe study included 320 teeth from 161 children (86 boys and 75 girls). The overall five-year survival rate was 38.2%, and the mean overall survival time was 54.2 months. Endodontic diagnosis was considered a significant risk factor (P < 0.05). In the first, second and third years, 57.4%, 81.8%, and 94.8%, respectively, of obturation materials in the root canals were resorbed at a faster rate than the roots. There was an altered eruption direction in 7 permanent teeth, and 4 permanent teeth were diagnosed with enamel hypoplasia.ConclusionsIn this study, the 60-month survival rate of primary molars treated by pulpectomy under dental general anesthesia was 38.32%. Operators should have an accurate assessment of the status of the pulp, have a strict grasp of the preoperative indications and select the appropriate treatment method according to the guidelines. Individual cases suggest overfilling, overextension and periapical periodontitis in primary molars have an impact on enamel hypoplasia and altered eruption direction in permanent teeth.Keywords: Pulpectomy, Primary molars, Dental general anesthesia, Survival analysis, Success rate

Introduction

In China, the fourth national oral epidemiological survey revealed that the prevalence of caries among children aged 3–5 years was 50.8%, 63.6%, and 71.9%, yet the proportions of teeth treated with fillings were only 1.5%, 2.9%, and 4.1%, respectively [1]. Caries in primary teeth develop quickly and usually affect the pulp rapidly, causing irreversible pulpitis, periapical periodontitis and necrosis. Primary molars play important roles not only in chewing but also in occlusion and the development of the facial skeleton in children [2].

Pulpectomy of primary teeth is a type of therapy for root pulp exhibiting irreversible inflammation or necrosis, which mainly manifests as different indications in different countries (Table 1). Endodontic therapies for primary teeth in both China [3] and Japan [4] involve root canal treatment. The operation and indications are basically the same, within the scope of the indications for periapical periodontitis that does not involve the permanent tooth buds. While the indications in the endodontic therapy guidelines of Britain [5] and America [6] include root pulpitis or pulp necrosis and no significant apical resorption, without mention of root canal treatment. In Britain and America, for cases of periapical periodontitis, dentists will choose extraction. Dental general anesthesia (DGA) is a safe and efficient method for dealing with uncooperative children, as it won’t be interfered by their resistant behavior [7, 8].

Therefore, this study was conducted as a long-term follow-up to retrospectively investigate the five-year survival rate of pulpectomy of primary molars performed under DGA and assess the risk factors related to overall survival, including gender, age, general health, tooth type, tooth location, endodontic diagnosis and quality of root filling, and the impact of this procedure on permanent successors, with a view to clarify the indications for pulpectomy and improve the clinical efficacy of pediatric dental treatment.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

The Stomatological Hospital Ethics Committee, School of Stomatology, Southern Medical University (KQIIT2021003), approved this retrospective study, which was carried out in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects. Informed consent to use the records was obtained from the patients’ guardians. Healthy children who underwent pulpectomy for primary molars between August 2013 and July 2023 were chosen as study participants from the comfort treatment team in the Department of Pediatric Dentistry at the Stomatological Hospital, School of Stomatology, Southern Medical University and Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center.

Pulpectomy treatment procedure

All the teeth were treated by highly skilled operators who had over 8 years of experience in the field. After the caries were removed and the top of the pulp chamber was uncovered to determine the pulp status, those pulps that met the conditions for inclusion were extracted from the radicular tissue via broaches. Then, using manual (Dentsply M-Access #15 or #20 K files, Sirona, USA) or rotary (M3-Mr #2004, 2504, 3004, United Dental, Shanghai, China) files, the root canals were prepared and smoothed out. Endodontic K files were used to clean and shape the canals. An electronic apex locator (ProPex Pixi, Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) was used to estimate each root’s working length, and the operators’ experience was also considered. A 1% sodium hypochlorite solution and regular saline solution were used to irrigate the canals, which were subsequently dried via sterile paper tips. Following their full preparation, the canals were sealed with Vitapex (Neo Dental Chemical Products Co. Ltd., Tokyo-to, Japan). The pulp chamber was then filled with a glass ionomer. A stainless-steel crown was used to routinely repair the tooth.

Follow-up

All children were advised to attend regular follow-up appointments with our institution every three months until all of their primary teeth were replaced with permanent teeth. At each follow-up, clinical status was evaluated, and panoramic radiography was performed every twelve months. If any abnormalities were found by clinical examination during the follow-ups, an apical image was taken immediately. Pulpectomy success was assessed in accordance with the clinical guidelines for pulp therapy of primary teeth established by the Pediatric Stomatology Professional Committee of the Chinese Stomatological Association and with the expert experience of the operators, who were all pediatric dentists and had a good command of the requisite clinical skills [3]. The criterion for clinical success was considered full freedom from signs and symptoms, including discomfort, abscess, fistula, and abnormal mobility. The requirements for radiographic success were the absence of pathologic external root resorption, radiographic lesions for pulpitis, and the resorption and stability of any prior radiographic lesion for periapical periodontitis. Relapse was described as the presence or exacerbation of periapical or furcation radiolucency, discomfort, fistula or abscess, expansion of the periodontal ligament, or pathological root resorption.

In addition, the radiographic evaluation included the quality of root canal filling (optimal filling, underfilling, overfilling, overextension, for multirooted molars; if one of the root canals exhibited overfilling, overextension or underfilling and the other canals showed optimal filling, the tooth was be classified as overfilled, overextended or underfilled, respectively [filling materials: Vitapex, Neo Dental Chemical Products Co. Ltd., Tokyo-to, Japan]). Moreover, the rates of degradation of the root canal filling materials (faster than the roots, no change, slower than the roots, similar to the roots [the most apical tip of the root filling was less than 1 mm from the tooth root apex on radiographs]), and the degree of resorption of overfilled/overextended root canal filling materials (complete resorption, partial resorption, no change) were determined.

Data collection

The data collection included both electronic and paper dental records and DGA-related documents. Child-level predictors included age at surgery, gender, and general health (good or poor; poor referred to children with special care needs, including those with autism, mental retardation, developmental delay, and osteogenesis imperfecta, and patients who underwent surgery under general anesthesia due to various illnesses). Tooth-level predictors included the tooth location (maxillary or mandibular), tooth type (first or second molar), endodontic diagnosis (irreversible pulpitis or periapical periodontitis, on the basis of AAPD clinical‐radiographic criteria), quality of root canal filling (optimal filling, underfilling, overfilling, overextension, for multirooted molars; if one of the root canals was overfilled, overextended or underfilled and the other canals showed optimal filling, the tooth was classified as overfilled, overextended or underfilled [filling materials: Vitapex]). Moreover, the rates of degradation of the root canal filling materials (faster than the roots, no change, slower than the roots, similar to the roots [the most apical tip of the root filling is less than 1 mm from the tooth root apex on radiographs]), and the degree of resorption of overfilled/overextended root canal filling materials (complete resorption, partial resorption, no change) were determined.

Two qualified pediatric dentists who had undergone clinical and radiographic calibration were in charge of conducting the evaluations. When the examiners’ decisions were in conflict, reevaluations were conducted in an effort to reach a consensus. The good reproducibility of the two investigators’ evaluations was demonstrated by Cohen’s kappa statistic, which indicated an intraexaminer reliability of 0.87 and a measurement agreement of 0.85 over a two-week period.

Data analysis

All the data were tabulated in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Inc., Redmond, Wash., USA) in 2019, and a digital database was created. R4.2.3 software was used to categorize and analyze the data. Counts are expressed herein as frequencies and percentages (%), and measures are statistically described by means and standard deviations (SDs). The overall survival curves were estimated via a Kaplan‒Meier plot. The log-rank test was used to compare the survival curves of the two groups. To assess potential risk factors for survival, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed. The ratio of hazard rates to failure risk was assessed to determine the hazard ratio (HR). P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance in each case.

Results

Patient and tooth characteristics

The study included 320 teeth from 161 children (86 boys and 75 girls). Data were collected and updated until November 30, 2023. The mean age at the time of the operation was 4.35 ± 1.08 years. The average follow-up time was 19.27 ± 19.20 months. The median survival time for primary molars was 54.2 months (95% CI: 40.3-NA). Patient and tooth characteristics are presented in Table 2.

Survival and recurrence

The median survival time of the 320 treated teeth was 54.2 months, according to the Kaplan‒Meier study results. The survival curve is displayed in Fig. 1. The survival rates at 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months were 93.70%, 80.81%, 62.76%, 55.30%, and 38.32%, respectively. For the irreversible pulpitis group, the survival rates at 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months were 96.32%, 86.87%, 66.67%, 59.17%, and 42.06%, respectively. For the periapical periodontitis group, the survival rates at 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months were 83.89%, 54.78%, 46.25%, 38.54%, and 23.12%, respectively. The irreversible pulpitis group had a longer survival time than the periapical periodontitis group did, according to Kaplan‒Meier analysis (P < 0.001, Fig. 2). Discussion

Chinese specialists advise that pulpectomy of primary molars in children performed under dental general anesthesia should, in principle, be completed in a single visit [10]. Pediatric dentists have always been concerned about the success rate of pulpectomy, particularly when it is performed while the patient is generally anesthetized. Research has demonstrated that there is no discernible variation in success rates or postoperative pain between single-visit and multiple-visit randomized controlled trials [11, 12]. However, data from long-term follow-up studies of pulpectomy carried out under dental general anesthesia are still lacking.

Currently, the success rates after pulpectomy of primary molars in children performed under dental general anesthesia vary across studies [13–16]. For short-term forecasting, our findings are consistent with those of other studies. We have gained considerable experience since our operators were the first to perform dental general anesthesia in South China. Research has suggested that procedures performed by more-experienced professionals lead to longer survival times; in contrast, junior operators’ limited ability to recognize root canal anatomical variations and their lack of technical experience could contribute to reduced survival [14, 17]. While the five-year success rate was lower than expected, this study revealed that preoperative diagnosis has an impact on the long-term survival rate, with only 23.12% survival rate of teeth being diagnosed with periapical periodontitis. Chen et al. [16] reported that the quality of root filling also has an impact and that underfilling increases the chance of bacterial colonization and reinfection. In addition, we found that the resorption of the root filling material also has some impact.

This study is consistent with the majority of other publications [15, 16], which reported that teeth with irreversible pulpitis had a better prognosis and higher success rate than teeth with periapical periodontitis did. In this study, only 23.12% of the teeth with periapical periodontitis survived to the fifth year, compared to 42.06% of the teeth with a diagnosis of irreversible pulpitis. Additionally, a multivariate study revealed that teeth with periapical periodontitis had a 2.40-fold lower failure risk than teeth with irreversible pulpitis prior to treatment. Currently, the indications differ across countries. Therefore, as Chinese experts have noted, there is still controversy about the applicability of pulpectomy to teeth with periapical periodontitis [3]. We believe that, on the basis of our clinical experience as well as research findings, the indications need to be strictly determined preoperatively; we also recommend that teeth with periapical periodontitis involving permanent tooth buds and more than 1/3 root resorption should be extracted.

We observed a yearly increase in the number of failures over the first three years in teeth with irreversible pulpitis and a sudden drop in survival in the third year. The early resorption of Vitapex may provide a narrow channel for bacterial growth, lead to reinfection in the root canal, and increase the likelihood of bacterial colonization and reinfection when paired with our statistics on the comparison of resorption rates of materials in the roots and in the root canals [18]. Several studies have reported a correlation between rapid resorption of root canal filling materials and failure and hypothesized that the rapid rate of degradation of Vitapex reduces its success rate [13, 14, 19]. The root canal filling material Vitapex, the composition of which comprises calcium hydroxide, iodoform, silicone oil, and inert matter, has strong antimicrobial effects [20], as well as absorbability, however, which also poses some risk of recurrence. Researchers have studied the resorption rates of different root canal filling materials and their effects on permanent teeth [21]. They reported that the degradation rate and biocompatibility of the improved materials are good. We look forward to the studies and development of more modified root canal filling materials.

No statistically significant difference between the first and second primary molars was found in this study, which was consistent with the results of previous studies [13, 22].

There were also several variables, including gender, age, general health, tooth type, tooth location, and quality of root filling, that were not significantly correlated with the outcome of pulpectomy in primary molars. However, correlations have been reported in several other studies [10–13], suggesting that numerous variables influence the primary molar pulpectomy success rate, thus requiring a high level of dental skill and experience. Researcher compared the survival rates of primary molars treated by pulpectomy in children under dental general anesthesia at five-year intervals and reported a statistically significant difference in the success rate as the dentists’ skill and experience increased [9]. Research has suggested that more experienced professionals conduct procedures yielding longer survival because junior operators’ reduced ability to recognize root canal anatomical variations and their lack of technical experience may contribute to reduced survival [14, 17]. Therefore, we suggest that medical management should impose certain requirements as the entry threshold for medical institutions that carry out dental general anesthesia, and medical institutions should also assess the surgical qualifications of dentists.

We observed altered eruption directions in 7 permanent teeth and 4 permanent teeth, which were diagnosed with enamel hypoplasia (Table 3). It has been observed that periapical periodontitis of primary molars has an impact on the inherited permanent germ, resulting in impaired shape and structure, such as enamel hypoplasia, changes in the direction of eruption or stagnant tooth development [23]. Second, two of the teeth showed overfilling/overextension on postoperative radiographs, which may have been the direct cause of the change in the direction of eruption and enamel hypoplasia, even though the excess material was fully absorbed at the time of review. Third, insufficient space for tooth eruption due to underdevelopment of the jaws can also lead to changes in the direction of eruption. In addition, premature loss of neighboring teeth may also be a cause of changes in the direction of eruption.

Currently, studies concerning pulpectomy of primary molars in children performed under dental general anesthesia are insufficient; this study provides long-term follow-up data. However, there are certain drawbacks to this investigation. First, there might be a recording error in these case records because this was a retrospective study based on existing data. When the records were evaluated, we also scrutinized all of the X-ray images to ensure that there were no false records. Second, other potential factors, including the degree of root resorption, dmft/dmfs, and the type of rinse used, were not evaluated in this study. Future studies should focus more deeply on the causes of failure after pulpectomy. It is also anticipated that improved root canal filling materials, which maintain long-term sealing efficacy without jeopardizing the replacement of permanent and deciduous teeth, will be created to achieve the same success rate for pulpectomy of primary teeth as for root canal treatment of permanent teeth. Although pulpectomy is a valuable tool for saving damaged teeth, pediatric dentists must follow rigorous restrictions.

Conclusions

In this study, the 60-month survival rate of primary molars treated by pulpectomy under dental general anesthesia was 38.32%. Operators should have an accurate assessment of the status of the pulp, have a strict grasp of the preoperative indications and select the appropriate treatment method according to the guidelines.

Individual cases suggest overfilling, overextension and periapical periodontitis in primary molars have an impact on enamel hypoplasia and altered eruption direction in permanent teeth.
