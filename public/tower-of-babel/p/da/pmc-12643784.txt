Personal history of irradiation and risk of osteomyelitis: A Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Xue Yun, Zhaopeng Fan, Yuxin Fu, Hua Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study employed Mendelian randomization (MR) to clarify the causal relationship between personal radiation exposure and osteomyelitis onset. We used a 2-sample MR approach to examine the causal association between personal irradiation history and osteomyelitis, leveraging genome-wide association study datasets: radiation exposure data from the IEU genome-wide association study database (UK Biobank cohort, 463,010 European-ancestry participants with self-reported radiotherapy or occupational exposure) and osteomyelitis data from the FinnGen Consortium (842 cases and 209,575 controls of European ancestry). Inverse variance weighting was the primary analytical method, supplemented by MR-Egger regression, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode. Sensitivity analyses included Cochran Q test, MR-Egger intercept, and leave-one-out validation. After rigorous quality control, 98 instrumental variables strongly associated with radiation exposure were selected. Inverse variance weighting analysis revealed a significant positive causal association (odds ratio = 1.29, 95% confidence interval = 1.01–1.66, P = .04), indicating a 29% increased risk of osteomyelitis per unit of genetically proxied radiation exposure. Cochran Q test (P = .71) showed no significant heterogeneity, and the MR-Egger intercept (P = .33) ruled out horizontal pleiotropy. Leave-one-out analysis confirmed stable results. This study provides genetic evidence for a causal link between personal irradiation history and elevated osteomyelitis risk. Clinically, these findings underscore the need for enhanced monitoring of bone health in high-dose radiotherapy populations and support targeted preventive strategies to reduce infection susceptibility, with implications for optimizing care in at-risk individuals.

1. Introduction

Osteomyelitis is a common orthopedic sickness primarily caused by infection from pathogenic microorganisms, leading to damage to bone tissue. Based on its pathogenesis, osteomyelitis can be classified into several types: traumatic osteomyelitis (mainly caused by the migration of nearby microorganisms), osteomyelitis due to vascular dysfunction (commonly seen in diabetic patients), and acute osteomyelitis.[1] Research shows that approximately 30% of patients with acute osteomyelitis may develop into chronic osteomyelitis. Therefore, this disease has become an important focus of global public health. Traumatic osteomyelitis typically occurs after open fractures, surgical procedures, or joint replacements, with common pathogenic microorganisms including Staphylococcus aureus, hemolytic streptococci, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Among these pathogens, S aureus stands out as the most prevalent, responsible for approximately 75% of the bacterial spectrum observed in osteomyelitis cases.[2,3] Currently, the main treatment methods for osteomyelitis include prolonged antibiotic therapy and surgical debridement. With early diagnosis and treatment with sensitive antibiotics, the cure rate for acute osteomyelitis can be as high as 80%, while surgical debridement has good therapeutic value for ischemic sclerosis of the affected bone caused by some cases of chronic osteomyelitis.[4,5] Despite significant advances in antibiotic and surgical treatments, patients may still face the risk of persistent infections or poor health outcomes after receiving treatment. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the novel pathogenesis of osteomyelitis, which will provide new insights into the treatment and prevention of osteomyelitis.

Personal radiation history refers to the occurrence and development of diseases caused by radiation therapy, exposure to metallic elements, and X-ray imaging.[6–8] Research indicates that high-dose radiation therapy is a widely utilized approach for treating malignant tumors. While this method can be effective in targeting and reducing tumor size, it unfortunately carries the risk of various side effects. This occurs because radiation does not have the capability to differentiate between malignant tumor cells and the healthy cells located in surrounding organs. As a result, healthy tissues may also be adversely affected by the treatment, leading to complications that can impact the patient’s overall health and quality of life. Therefore, while high-dose radiation therapy remains a critical component of cancer treatment, careful consideration, and management of its potential side effects are essential for optimizing patient outcomes.[9] Studies have shown that radiation may exert detrimental effects on healthy bone tissue through mechanisms such as inducing DNA damage, suppressing bone marrow function, and impairing bone microvasculature. This, in turn, increases the risk of osteomyelitis by weakening the ability to clear pathogens and promoting bacterial translocation.[10,11] For example, research has reported a close association between secondary breast cancer or bone necrosis and radiation therapy, as well as other diseases.[12,13] Moreover, there have been reports over the years regarding its role in the occurrence of osteomyelitis. According to the literature, in patients with previous radiation therapy, the probability of developing cranial base osteomyelitis significantly increases in regions receiving the highest radiation doses,[14,15] Therefore, it can be concluded that the occurrence of osteomyelitis is typically confined to the radiation area or its vicinity. When patients receive a radiation dose >66 Gy, the incidence of skull base osteomyelitis is 57.2%. However, the underlying mechanism (whether radiation directly increases infection susceptibility via bone vascular damage, immune suppression, or altered microbial colonization) remains unclear. Additionally, the threshold dose for increased osteomyelitis risk is difficult to define, as it is influenced by multiple factors including radiation field size, radiotherapy fractionation regimens, patient age at treatment, and concurrent hormone therapy.[14] These complexities highlight the need for further research to clarify the causal relationship between radiation exposure and osteomyelitis.

Existing observational studies on radiation and osteomyelitis are limited by confounding factors (e.g., concurrent chemotherapy, underlying malignancy, or comorbidities like diabetes) and potential reverse causation (e.g., osteomyelitis-related bone surgery may require subsequent radiotherapy).[15] These limitations hinder definitive conclusions about causality. Mendelian randomization (MR) is a powerful statistical method that uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causal relationships, leveraging the random allocation of alleles during gamete formation to avoid confounding and reverse causation.[16–18] This approach has been widely used to validate causal associations between environmental exposures and diseases, making it ideal for addressing the limitations of previous studies on radiation and osteomyelitis.[19]

Thus, this study aims to: use 2-sample MR to investigate whether personal irradiation history is causally associated with osteomyelitis risk; provide genetic evidence to support or refute the hypothesis that radiation exposure increases osteomyelitis susceptibility; and inform targeted prevention strategies for high-risk populations.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

MR analysis is a sophisticated statistical method that leverages genetic variation as an instrumental variable. This approach allows researchers to assess causal relationships between risk factors and health outcomes by exploiting naturally occurring genetic differences among individuals.[20] In MR analysis, 3 important assumptions must be met. The 1st hypothesis states that the chosen heritable variation, when utilized as instrumental variables, must demonstrate a significant association with the exposure of interest. The 2nd assumption posits that the relationship between the chosen instrumental variables and the outcome (such as disease occurrence) should not be influenced by confounding factors. The 3rd assumption asserts that the interaction between the instrumental variables and the outcome should operate solely through the exposure.[17] This study relies on publicly available data obtained from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) conducted at the population level. In this research, because the data utilized originates from published sources, there is no need for additional ethical approval or informed consent for this research. Aggregate data on personal radiation history

Personal history of radiation GWAS utilized data from the IEU GWAS database at the University of Bristol (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk). A meta-analysis was carried out in this study involving 463,010 samples (covering 9,851,867 single nucleotide polymorphism [SNPs]) from the UK Biobank cohort (Project ID: ukb-b-14446). Participants were of European ancestry and radiation history was defined as self-reported prior radiotherapy or occupational radiation exposure (ICD-10 codes Z92.3).

Aggregate data on osteomyelitis

The summary data for the GWAS on osteomyelitis comes from the R5 version of the FinnGen Consortium (https://r5.finngen.fi/, last accessed on November 5, 2024). The study project includes 842 osteomyelitis cases and 209,575 control samples (total N = 212,703) with 16,380,449 SNPs.

All participants in this study are of European ancestry and have provided informed consent to participate. For further details about the participants, including specific demographic characteristics, recruitment procedures, and informed consent protocols, readers are referred to the comprehensive information available on the FinnGen website (https://finngen.gitbook.io/) and the UK Biobank website(https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/).[21,22]

2.2. Selection of instrumental variables

In the 2-sample MR analysis, we initially adhered to the fundamental MR principle by setting the significance threshold at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸). However, due to the limited number of SNPs associated with personal history of irradiation that met this strict criterion, we opted for a more lenient threshold (P < 5 × 10⁻⁴) for SNP selection. This approach is validated by prior MR studies,[23,24] which have demonstrated that using relaxed thresholds is a feasible strategy when genome-wide significant SNPs are scarce, as it allows for the inclusion of a sufficient number of instrumental variables to support robust causal inference, particularly for exposures with modest heritability. The use of a relaxed threshold, while necessary to obtain an adequate number of SNPs, does introduce a potential trade-off: an increased risk of weak instrument bias, where SNPs may have a weaker association with the exposure, thereby compromising the validity of causal estimates. To mitigate this risk, we rigorously evaluated the strength of each selected SNP using the F-statistic, ensuring all SNPs had an F-value > 10. This strict threshold for instrument strength, as recommended in MR methodology [25], helps counteract the potential weakness introduced by the relaxed significance threshold, ensuring that the selected SNPs remain strongly associated with personal history of irradiation. Additionally, in order to make certain the robustness and dependability of this research and to obtain a sufficient number of SNPs, we performed a cluster analysis using a threshold of r² < 0.001 and a clustering distance of 10,000 kb to eliminate linkage disequilibrium, thereby ensuring the independence of physical variables. (r² is a value between 0 and 1, where r² = 1 indicates that the 2 SNPs are completely unbalanced, and r² = 0 means that the 2 SNPs are in perfect linkage equilibrium, meaning their distribution is entirely random).[26] Finally, allele harmonization was conducted to align the effect alleles between the exposure and outcome datasets. We checked for consistency in allele definitions and excluded SNPs with ambiguous palindromic alleles to avoid misinterpretation of effect directions. SNPs with conflicting allele information between the 2 datasets were also removed.

2.3. MR analysis

Regarding the causal inference of personal radiation history on the impact of osteomyelitis, we employed 5 complementary methods for MR analysis. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) approach was designated as the primary analytical technique due to its efficiency under standard MR assumptions.[27] To specifically address potential violations of these assumptions, particularly directional pleiotropy, we deployed sensitivity analyses: MR-Egger regression to test for and correct pleiotropic bias via its intercept, weighted median estimator robust to invalid instruments when up to 50% of the weight comes from pleiotropic variants, simple mode, and weighted mode methods.[28–31]

MR-PRESSO primarily identifies and removes outlier SNPs driving pleiotropy,[32] but our MR-Egger intercept test already detected directional pleiotropy, and leave-one-out analysis further evaluated individual SNP influence, reducing the need for additional outlier-specific methods. MR-RAPS is designed to handle weak instruments and moderate pleiotropy by down weighting less reliable IVs,[33] however, our strict F-statistic threshold (>10) ensured strong instruments, this reduces the necessity of applying MR-RAPS.

All MR analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.2; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Primary analyses leveraged the “TwoSampleMR” package (version 0.5.11) for data extraction, harmonization, core MR methods (IVW, MR-Egger, weighted median), sensitivity tests (heterogeneity, pleiotropy, leave-one-out), and scatter/funnel plots. The “Mendelian Randomization” package (version 0.10.0) was utilized for the simple mode and weighted mode estimators. Forest plots were generated using the “forestploter” package.

2.4. Sensitivity

Ultimately, we performed a comprehensive sensitivity analysis aimed at evaluating the robustness of our results, which included assessments for heterogeneity and the potential presence of horizontal pleiotropy. To detect heterogeneity among the estimates, we employed the Cochran Q test, which provides valuable insight into the consistency of SNP effects across different analyses.[34] A P-value of less than .05 in Cochran Q test suggests the presence of heterogeneity among the SNP estimates, necessitating the use of a random effects IVW model to account for this variability.[35] Additionally, we evaluated the potential for horizontal pleiotropy by examining the intercept term in the MR-Egger regression analysis. This approach allows us to assess whether the instrumental variables are influencing the outcome through pathways other than the exposure of interest, thereby enhancing the validity of our MR findings.[28] A P-value of less than .05 signifies the presence of horizontal pleiotropy, whereas a P-value >.05 indicates its absence.

To further validate our findings, we conducted a “leave-one-out” analysis, which involved systematically removing 1 SNP at a time to evaluate whether this exclusion would lead to significant changes in the results of the IVW analysis. This technique helps to ensure that our findings are robust and not unduly influenced by any single genetic variant, thereby enhancing the reliability of our conclusions.

3. Results

3.1. MR analysis

The IVW analysis demonstrates a significant causal relationship between personal history of irradiation and osteomyelitis, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.29 and a 95% confidence interval of 1.01–1.66, along with a P-value of .04 (Table 1), this means that the risk of its onset will increase by 29%. Although methods such as MR-Egger and weighted median did not reach statistical significance (P > .05), their effect directions were consistent with IVW (ORs > 1), supporting the consistency of the results.

3.3. Sensitivity analyses

The Cochran Q value for the IVW method is 88.97 with 97 degrees of freedom and a P-value of .71, indicating no significant heterogeneity among the instrumental variables (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q696). This non-significant result (P > .05) suggests that the 98 selected SNPs, as instrumental variables, exhibit consistent effects in estimating the causal relationship between personal history of irradiation and osteomyelitis risk. Such consistency reinforces the reliability of the pooled effect derived from the IVW method, as it implies that the observed association is not driven by divergent effects of individual SNPs but rather reflects a shared directional impact of genetic variants related to radiation exposure on osteomyelitis susceptibility. The results of the MR-Egger intercept test, where the intercept is 0.08 (SE = 0.08, Pval = .33), further support the robustness of our findings. The P-value >.05 indicates the absence of horizontal pleiotropy, meaning that the selected SNPs do not influence osteomyelitis through alternative pathways independent of personal history of irradiation. This aligns with the core assumptions of MR, ensuring that the observed causal association is less likely to be confounded by pleiotropic effects of genetic variants (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q696). Additionally, detailed information on each SNP, including their effect alleles, beta values, standard errors, P-values, and other relevant metrics, is provided in the Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q696. These data allow for a comprehensive evaluation of the individual contributions of each SNP to the overall association, confirming that no single outlier SNP drives the observed effect.

Sensitivity analyses also demonstrated that the estimated effects remained stable, as removing individual SNPs in a leave-one-out manner did not result in significant changes to the overall results (Fig. 5). This reinforces the conclusion that the estimated effect of individual radiation exposure on osteomyelitis is not driven by any single genetic variant, thereby enhancing the robustness of our causal inference.

4. Discussion

Prior research have shown a significant relevance between individual radiation exposure and osteomyelitis. Nevertheless, due to the interference of confounding factors, the findings of observational studies may be subject to various biases, leading to inaccuracies. Consequently, this study aims to clarify the causal relationship between individual radiation exposure and osteomyelitis using the MR method. The results indicate a significant association between individual radiation exposure and osteomyelitis, with an OR of 1.29 (95% confidence interval: 1.01–1.66), translating to a 29% increase in relative risk per unit of genetically proxied radiation exposure. Although this represents a modest effect size compared to strong risk factors like traumatic osteomyelitis,[2,3] its clinical impact is substantial in high-exposure populations. For instance, in patients receiving cranial radiation > 66 Gy (where baseline osteomyelitis incidence reaches 57.2%[14]) this risk elevation could lead to approximately 17 additional cases per 100 exposed individuals. Such absolute risk increments underscore the need for targeted prevention strategies in radiotherapy cohorts, including vigilant long-term monitoring of bone health in high-dose fields (e.g., mandible, spine), consideration of prophylactic antibiotics during invasive procedures,[4] and adoption of precision radiotherapy techniques to minimize bone dose. These measures may mitigate radiation-induced vascular damage and immune suppression,[10] thereby reducing infection susceptibility. Through 2-sample MR, revealing that personal radiation exposure is a risk factor for osteomyelitis. This finding is consistent with the results of most epidemiological studies regarding the relationship between radiation and osteomyelitis, although not all studies have reached the same conclusion.

The inconsistent conclusions drawn from previous epidemiological studies may be attributed to multiple factors, including differences in exposure dosage, age at the time of exposure, and individual variability. For example, in an animal experiment, 10 adult male rats were selected, with the right femur distal end designated as the experimental group, receiving a radiation dose of 30 Gy weekly, with a radiation field of 15 mm × 15 mm. The left femur distal end served as the control group. Follow-up was conducted 4 weeks later, revealing that the bone quality changes in the exposed group were significantly altered compared to the control group,[36] ultimately leading to the occurrence of osteomyelitis. Research has also reported that after a single exposure to a radiation dose of 20 Gy, bone marrow hemorrhage, early edema, eosinophilia, and fibrosis were observed. These findings suggest that radiation dosage may be a critical factor contributing to the inconsistency, as different doses can induce varying degrees of bone tissue damage, thereby affecting the occurrence and development of osteomyelitis.

Radiation is ubiquitous in our daily lives, and with changes in lifestyle, we often encounter low doses of radiation during our everyday activities. This exposure has raised public health concerns, as it occurs not only in the general environment but also in occupational settings. Studies have reported that ionizing radiation, metallic elements, X-rays, and radiotherapy can lead to direct biochemical changes at the cellular level, as well as indirect damage to bones and joints. At the cellular and molecular levels, radiation may damage bone vascular endothelial cells, leading to reduced blood supply to bone tissue, which in turn impairs the delivery of nutrients and immune cells to the bone, weakening the local immune defense function and increasing the susceptibility to infection. Additionally, radiation can suppress the immune system, reducing the body’s ability to clear pathogenic microorganisms such as S aureus, which is the most common pathogen in osteomyelitis cases.[10]

For external radiation, the bones within the irradiated field may be affected, leading to conditions such as bone loss and malignancies, which can result in pathological fractures. This, in turn, may lead to bacterial displacement or hematogenous infections, ultimately resulting in osteomyelitis.[37,38] Moreover, radiation may alter the microbial colonization in the body, disrupting the balance of the local microecosystem and creating conditions conducive to the growth and reproduction of pathogenic bacteria, thereby increasing the risk of osteomyelitis.[39] In terms of individual variability, differences in genetic background may lead to varying responses to radiation.[40,41] Some individuals may have genetic variants that make their bone tissue or immune system more sensitive to radiation damage, thus increasing the risk of osteomyelitis after radiation exposure, while others may be less affected. Age at exposure is also a contributing factor; for example, immature bone tissue in children may be more vulnerable to radiation damage compared to adults, leading to a higher incidence of osteomyelitis.

In conclusion, this 2-sample MR study provides genetic evidence supporting a causal relationship between personal history of irradiation and an increased risk of osteomyelitis. These findings align with the majority of prior epidemiological observations and strengthen the causal inference that radiation exposure is a risk factor for developing osteomyelitis. The potential mechanisms, as suggested by existing literature, may involve radiation-induced bone vascular damage, immune suppression, and alterations in microbial colonization, ultimately compromising local bone defenses and increasing susceptibility to infection. However, it is important to note that this MR study did not directly test these specific mechanisms; future research is needed to elucidate the precise biological pathways.

However, this study also has some limitations, 1 of the main ones being its focus on a specific European population, which may introduce population bias. Therefore, for further research, it is essential to include populations from different regions (e.g., Asian, African ancestry or integrating clinical data). Additionally, there is considerable overlap between exposure and outcome data, which may lead to the risk of model overfitting and could exaggerate the observed correlation, thereby affecting the accuracy of causal estimates.[42] Third, while horizontal pleiotropy was addressed via MR-Egger (intercept P = .33), undetected vertical pleiotropy remains possible.[28,32] Fourth, the relaxed SNP threshold (P < 5 × 10⁻⁴) risks weak instrument bias, potentially diluting effect estimates.[25] Fifth, in this MR analysis, 5 statistical methods were used with a relatively large number of SNPs as instrumental variables, and the lack of multiple testing correction (such as Bonferroni or FDR) may increase the risk of type I errors, introducing uncertainty into the statistical significance of findings: for example, the significant association in the IVW analysis might need reevaluation with a stricter corrected threshold.[43] Future studies should integrate multiple testing correction strategies to enhance the robustness of causal inferences.

5. Conclusions

This research demonstrates a causal relationship between individual radiation history and an elevated risk of osteomyelitis, consistent with previous epidemiological observations. Critically, radiation exposure may represent a modifiable risk factor for osteomyelitis, particularly in patient populations undergoing high-dose radiotherapy. Consequently, future studies could implement dynamic monitoring in high-risk populations to evaluate the feasibility of prophylactic antibiotic interventions and their potential application in clinical translation.
