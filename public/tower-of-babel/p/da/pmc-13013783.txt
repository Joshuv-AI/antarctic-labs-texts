RNA binding protein Sam68 promotes germinal center reaction and IgG response through regulation of miR29
Authors: Moumita Datta, Valerio Renna, Manish Kumar, Palash Chandra Maity, Hassan Jumaa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The RNA binding protein Sam68 (Src associated during mitosis of 68 kDa) has recently been shown to be involved in DNA double strand break repair. In the course of development and immune response, B cells undergo physiological double strand breaks during V(D)J recombination and class switch recombination. Therefore, envisaging a crucial role of Sam68 in B cell development and function, we analyzed Sam68 complete knockout (KO) mice. Despite normal B cell development, these animals exhibit impaired germinal centre (GC) reactions and antibody responses. Using a competitive bone marrow chimera model, as well as adoptive B cell transfer model, we could demonstrate that Sam68 deficient B cells are less competent to participate in GC than WT B cells. This reduced competence is mainly attributed to impaired B cell-intrinsic CD40 signaling as Sam68 KO B cells are less responsive to CD40 stimulation in vitro. Sam68 deficient B cells up-regulate the expression of microRNA miR29a and b which in turn repress the expression of Tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 4 (Traf4) gene, a downstream effector molecule of CD40 pathway. Thus, Sam68 mediated Traf4 regulation through miR29 plays an important role in CD40 signaling in B cells, hence in GC response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06145-w.

Introduction

Sam68, also known as K homology domain-containing, RNA-binding, signal transduction-associated protein 1 (KHDRBS1) is a member of signal transduction and activation of RNA metabolism (STAR) family protein that plays role in a wide range of cellular processes including signal transduction, DNA binding, transcription and RNA metabolism[1]. Structurally, the protein contains a heteronuclear ribonucleoprotein K homology (KH) domain through which it binds to RNA and regulates various RNA processing reactions including RNA stability, pre-mRNA splicing, RNA export and translation. The presence of other protein–protein interaction domains, e.g. proline rich domains (PR) or tyrosine rich regions (YY) that serve as docking sites for Src homology 3 (SH3) and SH2 domain containing proteins, makes it a suitable scaffold protein for various signal transduction pathways[1]. Sam68 expresses ubiquitously in all tissues and controls diverse tissue specific functions such as germ cell development [2], bone metabolism [3], adipogenesis [4], neuronal synaptic regulation [5] etc. Evolving evidence suggests that the protein is involved in DNA damage response. DNA double strand breaks (DSB) induced by radiation or DNA alkylation recruit Sam68 at the lesion site where it stimulates the catalytic activity of Poly [ADP-ribose] polymerase 1 (PARP1)[6]. Efficient poly ADP-ribosylation of PARP1 substrates including PARP1 itself, NBS1 and Ku70 leads to the assembly of DNA repair machinery which in turn repairs DSBs and maintains genome integrity[7]. In line with this, deletion of Sam68 completely abolishes PARP1 induced poly ADP-ribose (PAR) production upon genotoxic stress. Consequently, Sam68 deleted cells cannot efficiently repair damaged DNA and Sam68 complete knock out (KO) mice are sensitive to γ-irradiation[7].

Apart from radiation/chemical induced DNA damages, DSBs are also created physiologically for generating combinatorial diversity in adaptive immune system [8]. The process of V(D)J recombination occurring in developing B and T lymphocytes to generate the great diversity of unique antigen receptors on their surface also involves programmed DSBs induced by Rag1 and Rag2 proteins which after recombination is further repaired by the ubiquitous non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) machinery[8]. Similarly, during immune response, antigen experienced activated B cells undergo class switch recombination (CSR) whereby the immunoglobulin (Ig) constant (C) region is switched from Cμ to Cγ, Cα or Cε leading to the production of IgG, IgA or IgE with different effector functions. CSR also requires DNA DSBs induced by activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID), uracil-DNA glycosylase (UNG) and apurinic-apyrimidinic endonuclease 1 (APE1) in specific intronic areas called switch (S) regions which after intrachromosomal recombination are joined by NHEJ[9]. Thus, it is intriguing that proteins that are involved in DSB recognition or repair might also play a role in immune regulation. Indeed, several DNA damage responsive molecules such as Ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM), p53 binding protein 1 (53BP1), γH2AX are shown to be essential for CSR and mice lacking these genes manifest impaired CSR[10–12]. The components of DNA dependent protein kinase (DNA-PK) holoenzyme, Ku70-Ku80 and DNA-PK catalytic subunit are part of NHEJ pathway and are indispensable for V(D)J recombination. Consequently, mice lacking any of the three components do not develop B cells[13, 14].

The facts mentioned above clearly indicate that Sam68, a newly identified DNA damage sensor molecule could also impact development and function of B cells which has not been studied so far. Immuno-regulatory role of Sam68 is documented for T cells, for e.g. it acts as an adapter protein recruited to T cell receptor (TCR) and is a target of Fyn and Lck mediated phosphorylation[15, 16]. Sam68 also controls CD25 expression on activated T cells through NFκB pathway[17]. In a recent study, Sam68 is shown to be a positive regulator of T helper 1 (Th1) polarization as Sam68 KO T cells cannot differentiate into interferon gamma (IFNγ) expressing Th1 cells in response to IL12.[18]. Consequently, Sam68 complete KO mice were more susceptible to Mycobacterium bovis Bacillus Calmette-Guerin (BCG) infection leading to death due to increased bacterial load in the lungs compared to WT mice. However, such studies in the context of B cells are lacking. Therefore, in the current manuscript, we investigate the role of this STAR protein in B cell development and immune regulation.

Materials and methods

Animals

The Sam68 complete KO mouse model (B6.129(C)-Khdrbs1tm1Rchd/J) [3]was maintained by heterozygous mating in our facility. The resulting WT and heterozygous (Het) littermates were used in the study along with the KO. Rag−/−γc−/− mice (B6.129S4-Il2rg < tm1Wjl >/J x B6(Cg)-Rag2 < tm1.1Cgn >/J) and CD45.1 WT mice (B6.SJL-Ptprc < a > Pepc < b >/BoyJ) were maintained by homozygous mating. CD45.1/CD45.2 heterozygous WT mice were obtained by interbreeding CD45.1 WT with CD45.2 WT (C57BL/6 J) mice. All mice were group housed in individually ventilated cages (IVC) with food and water ad libitum and 12 h day-night cycle. All animal interventions were ethically approved by the regional council of Baden-Württemberg (Regierungspräsidium Tübingen, TVA 1435 and o.207–10). Mice were deeply anesthetized with CO2 inhalation and killed by cervical dislocation.

Cell sorting

HSPCs were FACS sorted from bone marrow as follows: lineage markers (CD3, CD4, CD8, B220, CD11b, Ter119, Gr1) negative (Lin−) cells were further divided depending on cKit and Sca1 expression as Lin− cKit+ (LK) and Lin− Sca1+cKit+ (LSK) population. Sorted LK and LSK cells were kept in RPMI medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, PAN Biotech) before proceeding with further experiment. B cell subsets from different organs were sorted as follows: BM- Hardy fractions A + B (CD43lo B220+ CD24+), D + E (CD43− B220lo) and F (CD43− B220+). From spleen, follicular (FO, CD19+ CD23+ CD21−), transitional (Tr, CD19+ CD23− CD21−) and marginal zone (MZ, CD19+ CD23− CD21+) B cells. From peritoneal cavity, B1a (CD19+ B220lo CD5+), B1b (CD19+ B220− CD5−) and B2 (CD19+ B220hi CD5−) B cells.

Immunization

Immunization protocol was as previously described [19]. Briefly, Sam68 KO and gender matched littermate WT mice of 8–12 weeks were immunized intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 150μl of 10% sheep red blood cell (SRBC, Cedarlane Labs). For TNP-OVA (2,4,6‐trinitrophenyl hapten conjugated to ovalbumin- comprising 10–20 TNP epitopes per Ova molecule) immunization, 200 μg of TNP-OVA (Biosearch) was mixed with 0.4% Alu‐S‐gel (Alum; Serva) and injected intraperitoneally (i.p.). The mock immunized mice obtained PBS + Alum.

Generation of competitive bone marrow chimera and immunization

Generation of competitive bone marrow chimera is as described before [20]. Briefly, gender matched 8–10 weeks old Rag−/−γc−/− mice were injected with 150 μg of ACK2 (anti-c-Kit) antibody 5 days prior to bone marrow transplantation [21]. Total bone marrow derived cells were isolated from age and gender matched Sam68 KO (expressing homozygous CD45.2) and WT (expressing homozygous CD45.1) mice and mixed in 1:1 ratio. ~ 20 × 106 of this mixed BM cells were injected i.v. into the ACK2 treated Rag−/−γc−/− mice. After 4–6 weeks, chimerism was checked in blood and the chimeric mice were immunized with 10% SRBC i.p. After 4 days, the chimeric mice were sacrificed to analyze GC in the spleen.

Adoptive transfer (AT) of B cells and immunization

B cell adoptive transfer and immunization was done as reported previously [22]. Briefly, 8–12 weeks old WT recipient mice expressing homozygous CD45.1 (WT45.1/45.1, for non-competitive AT) or heterozygous CD45.1/CD45.2 (WT45.1/45.2, for competitive AT) were pre-immunized with 10% SRBC i.p. three weeks prior to AT. For non-competitive AT, splenocytes were isolated from Sam68 KO and WT mice (both expressing homozygous CD45.2) and B cells were purified using CD43 magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec). ~ 10 × 106 purified B cells were injected i.v. into pre-immunized WT45.1/45.1 recipient mice. For competitive AT, B cells were similarly purified from Sam68 KO (expressing homozygous CD45.2) and WT (expressing homozygous CD45.1) mice and mixed in 1:1 ratio. ~ 10 × 106 of this mixed B cells were injected i.v. into pre-immunized WT45.1/45.2 recipient mice. The recipient mice for both non-competitive and competitive AT were then further immunized with 10% SRBC i.p. 16–20 h post AT. After 4 days, the recipient mice were sacrificed to analyze GC in the spleen.

Flow cytometry-based analysis of anti-SRBC IgG in serum

Serum samples from SRBC immunized mice at different dilutions are incubated with 10μl of 10% SRBC for 15 min on ice. After washing with PBS, cells (SRBC) are stained with 1:200 dilution of FITC labelled anti-mouse IgG1 antibody (#406606, Biolegend), for 15 min on ice in dark. After wasing, they were measured using LSR-Fortessa (BD Bioscience).

Cells isolated from different organs of mice were taken in FACS buffer (PBS containing 3% FBS) and treated with 1:100 dilution of the anti-Fc receptor-blocking antibody (CD16/32, Clone 2.4G2, Biolegend) on ice for at least 5 min. List of fluorescently labelled antibodies used in FACS

For intracellular FACS analysis, cells were first surface stained, washed with PBS followed by staining with 1:1000 diluton of fixable viability dye for 15 min on ice. After washing, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature (RT) for 30 min in dark. Next, cells were permeabilized with 90% ice cold methanol for at least 1 h on ice in dark followed by staining with 1:50 dilution of either PE conjugated anti-mouse BCL6 or APC conjugated anti-FLAG antibody (R&D Systems) for 15–20 min at RT. After washing, cells were acquired using LSR-Fortessa.

In vitro B cell proliferation and class switching assay

CD43− splenic B cells were isolated from WT and Sam68 KO mice using magnetic cell sorting (AutoMACS, Miltenyi Biotec). Equal numbers (1 × 106) of WT and KO cells were labelled with cell trace far red cell proliferation dye (#C34564, Invitrogen) according to manufacturer’s instruction. Labelled cells were plated in 200μl of Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Media (Sigma) supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco), 100 units/ml of penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) and 50 μM beta-marcaptoethanol (Gibco) in flat bottom 96-well plate and were stimulated with 2.5 ng/ml mouse IL4 (#214–14, Peprotech), 2.5 ng/ml mouse IL4 + 1 μg/ml LPS (#tlrl-eklps, Invivogen) and 2.5 ng/ml mouse IL4 + 5 μg/ml of anti-mouse CD40 antibody (#102,914, Biolegend). A small part of the labelled cells was measured in FACS to determine the fluorescence signal on day 0. After 96 h, cells were collected, washed with FACS buffer and acquired using LSR-Fortessa. Data is analyzed by the proliferation model fitting tool of Flowjo.

For class switching analysis, cells were washed with FACS buffer and surface stained with FITC labelled anti-mouse IgG1 antibody (#406,606, Biolegend, 1:100 dilution) and detected by LSR-Fortessa.

Calcium flux analysis

Calcium flux analysis was done as previously described [23]. Briefly, splenic B cells from WT and Sam68 KO mice were isolated using CD43 negative selection. Next, 1 × 106 cells/treatment was loaded with Calcium sensitive dye Indo-1 AM (#I1223, Invitrogen) and 0.5 mg/ml of pluronic F-127 (#P3000MP, Invitrogen) in Iscove’s media supplemented with 1% FBS at 37 °C for 45 min. After washing, additional cell surface staining was done with 1:100 dilution of FITC labelled anti-CD23 (Biolegend) and PE labelled anti-CD1d (Biolegend) antibodies in media for 10 min on ice. Cells were then washed, and Calcium flux was measured in LSR-Fortessa (BD Bioscience). Cells were pre-warmed at 37 °C for 5 min. The baseline signal for calcium was measured for 1 min followed by stimulation with 5 μg/ml of anti-Kappa light chain antibody (Southern Biotech).

CD69 expression analysis

CD43− splenic B cells isolated from WT and Sam68 KO mice were incubated either with 2.5 ng/ml mouse IL4 or different concentrations of anti-Kappa and anti-IgM antibodies (Southern Biotech) in 96 well plates. After 24 h, the cells were surface stained with respective antibodies (Table 1) followed by acquisition using LSR-Fortessa.

RNA isolation and gene/miRNA expression analysis

For the analysis of Sam68 gene expression in HSPC and different B cell populations, cells were sorted as described previously. RNA was isolated from the sorted cells using ReliaPrep Cell RNA miniprep kit (#Z6011, Promega). Fifty nanogram of total RNA was reverse transcribed using high-capacity RNA to cDNA kit (#4387406, Thermo Fisher) and gene expression was measured by Taqman assay using the probe Khdrbs1 (Sam68) (Mm00516130_m1) and Taqman Gene expression master mix (#4369016, Thermo Fisher) following manufacturer’s protocol. For the analysis of miRNA expression, CD43− splenic B cells were isolated from WT and Sam68 KO mice and treated with 2.5 ng/ml mouse IL4 + 5 μg/ml of anti-mouse CD40 antibody (#102914, Biolegend) for 24 and 96 h. Total RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent (#15596026, Thermo Fisher) following manufacturer’s protocol followed by DNase I digestion to remove contaminating DNA. Ten nanogram of total RNA was reverse transcribed using TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (# 4,366,596, Thermo Fisher) and miRNA specific RT primers following manufacturer’s protocol. Next, 1.3μl of the RT product was PCR amplified using miRNA specific Taqman probes (20X) and TaqMan Universaler Mastermix II, no UNG (# 4440040, Thermo Fisher). The following miRNA assays were used: miRNA29a (Assay ID: 002112), miRNA29b (Assay ID: 000413), miRNA29c (Assay ID: 000587) and U6 snRNA (Assay ID: 001973, control). For Traf4 gene expression analysis, 50 ng of Trizol RNA was reverse transcribed using high capacity RNA to cDNA kit (#4387406, Thermo Fisher) and gene expression was measured by Taqman assay using the probe Traf4 (Mm00493829_m1) and Taqman Gene expression master mix (#4369016, Thermo Fisher) following manufacturer’s protocol.

Cloning of Traf4 and miR29

For stable overexpression in mouse B cells, an ecotropic γ-retrovirus vector named pMIPw-mTraf4 -DsRed was constructed using a CMV promoter-controlled (pCMV) expression cassette of N-terminal 3xFLAG-tagged mTraf4 cDNA (NCBI GeneID 22032) linked to p2a self-cleavage peptide coupled DsRed2 fluorescent protein reporter followed by internal ribosomal entry site (IRES) and puromycin resistance (PuroR) as selection marker (Supplementary Figure S7B). The retroviral plasmid backbone was derived from pMIG-w (Addgene ID 12282) upon linearization with BglII and SalI sites.

For overexpression of miR29a (miRbase ID: MI0000576), an expression cassette of mature miR stem loop structure with 100 bases flanking sequences on both up and downstream sites was cloned into the truncated version of second intron of the human β globin (HBB) mRNA (NCBI GeneID 3043) with parts of adjacent exons on both sides. The entire cassette was cloned in our retroviral plasmid under the CMV promoter followed by a fusion protein construct for mRFP1 reporter and PuroR selection markers, and SV40 polyadenylation (SV40 PA) sequence mimicking the complete 3’ exon of modified HBB mRNA (Supplementary Figure S7A).

Retroviral transduction

Stable transductions in B cells were performed as previously described [19]. In brief, Phoenix-Eco cells were transfected with retroviral plasmids together with ecotropic packaging helper plasmid pCL-Eco (Addgene ID 12371) using the GeneJuice Transfection Reagent (Millipore) according to manufacturer’s protocol. Culture supernatants containing the matured retroviral particles were collected 48–72 h after transfection. For transduction of murine splenic B cells, CD43- B cells were pretreated with 2.5 ng/ml of murine IL4 + 1 μg/ml LPS for 48 h. Cells were then incubated with viral supernatant and transduction is carried out by the spin-infection method in presence of 5 µg/ml polybrene (Millipore). The transduction efficiency was evaluated two to four days after inoculation by measuring the percentage of the DsRed2 + or mRFP1 + cells.

Electroporation of B cells

Electroporation was carried out using the Neon® transfection system (Invitrogen) as described before [24]. Briefly, 5 × 106 cells were resuspended in 100µl Resuspension Buffer R and mixed with 10 µg plasmid and then electroporated using the optimized condition of 1450 V for 20 ms width of single pulse in a 100µl Neon® Tip. Electroporated cells were immediately inoculated in a 6 well plate containing 5 ml complete IMDM medium with 10% FCS, Pen-Strep and Glutamine. The efficiency was evaluated two to three days after inoculation by measuring the percentage of the mRFP1 positive cells.

Western blot

Western blotting was done as previously described [25]. Briefly, cells were collected by centrifugation and washed with ice cold PBS. Cell extract was prepared by resuspending the cell pellet in 100μl of radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with 1X protease inhibitor cocktail (#78429, Thermo Fisher), 1 mM sodium orthovanadate (Sigma-Aldrich), and 10 mM beta-glycerophosphate (Sigma-Aldrich). Protein cencentration was measured by Bradford reagent (Biorad). Twenty microgram of total protein was heat denatured and run on 10% SDS-PAGE. Gels were transferred to PVDF membranes by semi-dry method (Biorad), blocked with 3% BSA solution and incubated with 1:3000 dilution of anti-Traf4 antibody (D1N3A, # 18527 T, Cell signaling technology) or 1:8000 dilution of anti-GAPDH (14C10, #2118, Cell signaling technology) antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The next day, primary antibodies were washed, and membranes were probed with 1:8000 dilution of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated Anti-rabbit IgG (#7074, Cell Signaling technology) at RT for 2 h. Blots were developed using Immobilon ECL Ultra Western HRP Substrate (#WBULS0500, Millipore), and images were acquired with a Fusion SL gel imaging system (Vilber Lourmat, Eberhardzell, Germany). Band intensities were analyzed by Image J.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in GraphPad Prism 6.0 software. Specific statistical tests are mentioned in the figure legends. Unless otherwise mentioned, all experiments were independently done at least three times.

Results

B cell development and compartmentalization are not altered in the absence of Sam68.

The homozygous deletion of Sam68 in the complete KO mouse is reported to show uncharacterized lethality at birth[3]. Therefore, we closely monitored the breeding performances of this line. When maintained as heterozygous (Het) breeding pairs, only 13% (58 of 439) mice were homozygous KO, (Supplementary Figure S1A) showing a serious deviation from the expected 25% of Mendelian inheritance due to neonatal lethality. Nevertheless, the newborn Sam68 complete KO pups survived and lived normally albeit slight growth retardation as reported before[3, 4]. Using real-time gene expression analysis, we measured the level of Sam68 expression in different B cell populations from bone marrow (BM), spleen, and peritoneal cavity (PC) as well as hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPC) from WT and KO mice. As expected, Sam68 expression was undetectable in any of the populations in KO mice (Supplementary Figure S1B). Intriguingly, in WT mice, Sam68 mRNA level remains consistently high throughout the B cell developmental stages and maximizes at the transitional (Tr) and follicular (FO) stages (Supplementary Figure S1B).

To test whether Sam68 deletion affects B cell development, we analyzed the distribution of different B cell subsets in BM, spleen, lymph node (LN) and PC from WT, Het and Sam68 KO mice. In accordance with the minor growth retardation of the KO mice, we observed a slightly reduced spleen size and a concomitant significant reduction in total splenic cell number (Fig. 1A). However, the relative abundance of different B cell subsets such as Hardy’ fractions A-F in BM, follicular (CD23+ CD21lo), marginal zone (CD21hi CD23lo) and transitional (CD23− CD21−) B cells in the spleen, follicular (CD23+ CD21lo) B cells in the lymph node and B1a (CD5+ B220lo), B1b (CD5− B220lo) and B2 (CD5− B220hi) cells in the peritoneal cavity did not show any alteration between WT, Het and KO mice (Fig. 1B). We also analyzed the development of T cells in the thymus. The distribution of CD4+, CD8+, CD4+ CD8+ double positive (DP) and CD4− CD8− double negative (DN) cells were unaltered between genotypes (Fig. 1B). Sam68 complete KO mouse exhibits impaired T cell dependent (TD) but unaltered T-independent (TI) immune response

Knowing the normal B cell development in Sam68 complete KO mice, we asked the question whether the B cell effector function differs between the genotypes. To this end, we analyzed the B cell activation and spontaneous germinal center (GC) formation in the gut associated lymph nodes such as mesentric lymph node (MLN) and Peyer’s patch (PP) of Sam68 KO mice. Strikingly, the percentage of activated B cells (CD95+ CD38−) were significantly lower in both MLN and PP of the KO mice compared to WT already by 6 weeks of age (Supplementary Figure S2A-B). At later age (28 weeks), this difference between WT and KO remained significant in the MLN despite overall accumulation of activated B cells, suggesting a defect in spontaneous GC formation. In contrast, the frequency of PP associated activated B cells were increased equally in both genotypes. The inguinal LN and spleen showed very low B cell activation for both genotypes as spontaneous GCs are rare in these tissues. Thus, spontaneous B cell activation seems to be lower in the Sam68 KO mice.

Next, we immunized WT and Sam68 KO mice with robust multivalent TD antigen SRBC and measured immune response after 10 days (Fig. 2A). Strikingly, Sam68 complete KO animals could not mount effective germinal centre (GC) response against SRBC as depicted by significantly reduced B cell activation (CD95+ CD38−) in these mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 2B and D). The expression of GC marker GL7 was also reduced in Sam68 KO mice compared to WT (Fig. 2C). Concomitantly, serum titre of SRBC specific IgG antibodies were significantly lower in KO than WT (Fig. 2E). We also tested the relative abundance of T-follicular helper (TfH) cells that provide survival and proliferation signals to the activated B cells in GC. Since the D10 time point used for GC analysis above is relatively early for measuring proper IgG response, we analyzed the memory response of the B cells in WT and Sam68 KO mice. As depicted in Fig. 3H, we immunized age matched WT and KO mice with TNP-Ova and monitored the serum IgG level over time. Four weeks later, we rechallenged them with another dose of the antigen. Unlike WT mice, which produced a sharp increase in serum IgG level (Fig. 3I) as well as antigen specific IgG level (Fig. 3J) within 7 days after the booster, KO mice failed to boost IgG production (Fig. 3I). The increase in anti-TNP IgG in the KO after booster was significantly lower compared to the WT (Fig. 3J). This clearly corroborates with our previous observation of defective GC reaction in KO mice. In contrast, T-independent (TI) immune response remained unaltered between WT and KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 3A-C). Age matched WT and Sam68 KO mice were immunized with TI antigen TNP-Ficoll and serum titre for IgM and anti- TNP IgM was measured 7 and 14 days post-immunization (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Both the genotypes exhibited equally elevated serum IgM (Supplementary Fig. 3B) and anti-TNP IgM (Supplementary Fig. 3C) indicating an unperturbed TI response.

Taken together, Sam68 complete KO mice are defective in mounting TD immune response while TI response remain intact.

Sam68 KO B cells exhibit defect in GC response in competitive bone marrow chimeric Rag−/−γc−/− model

The defect in TD immune response as observed above may also result from defective T cell function in Sam68 complete KO mice. To address the relative competence of Sam68 KO B and T cells in immune response, we performed competitive bone marrow reconstitution in immunodeficient Rag−/−γc−/− mouse followed by immunization. We have established a non-irradiation based model of reconstitution (Supplementary Fig. 4 A). Rag−/−γc−/− mice were injected with anti-cKit antibody ACK2 to deplete the hematopoietic stem cells [20]. Five days later, 1:1 mixture of bone marrow cells derived from CD45.1+ WT mice and CD45.2+ Sam68 KO mice were injected intravenously in these mice. After 4–5 weeks, reconstitution efficiency was tested in blood by FACS. As evident from Supplementary Fig. 4B, Sam68 KO BM cells had significantly reduced ability to reconstitute the T cell compartment in Rag−/−γc−/− mice compared to WT BM cells while it was reverse in case of B cells most likely due to a compensatory effect. This again indicates that Sam68 KO has no effect on B cell development as seen before while in case of T cells, Sam68 seems to be important for normal development. Additionally, the percentage of engraftment seemed to be stable over time for both B and T cells (Supplementary Fig. 4C).

Next, the reconstituted mice were immunized with SRBC and GC response was analyzed 10 days post-immunization. Although the CD45.2+ KO B cells constitute ~ 60% of the total B cell pool in blood and spleen, the contribution of KO B cells in GC (CD38− CD95+ GL7+) was reduced to ~ 44% while that of CD45.1+ WT cells increased from 40% in spleen to 53% in GC (Fig. 4A-B). This suggests that Sam68 KO B cells were less competent than the WT B cells to enter GC even though proper T cell help was available from the WT T cells. Notably, the SRBC immunization was equally effective in the reconstituted mice as in the WT mice as evident from the percentages of activated CD95+ CD38− B cells in WT (1.34%, Fig. 2B) and the reconstituted Rag−/−γc−/− mice (1.05%, Fig. 4A). Thus, the reduced participation of Sam68 KO B cells in GC cannot be attributed to ineffective immunization. As a control, we also generated competitive bone marrow chimera of CD45.1+ WT and CD45.2+ WT BM cells in Rag−/−γc−/− mice and immunized them with SRBC similar to as described above. We observed that the ratio of CD45.1+ WT and CD45.2+ WT remained same for both blood and GC (Fig. 4C-D) indicating that the expression of the congenic markers does not affect the outcome of the experiment. Reduced contribution of Sam68 KO B cells in GC response in B cell adoptive transfer model

To comprehensively assess the GC potential of Sam68 KO B cells in vivo, in addition to the bone marrow chimeric model, we also performed adoptive B cell transfer. The bone marrow chimeric model involves co-transfer of Sam68-deficient T cells and follicular dendritic cells (FDCs)—both of which contribute to GC reactions. Thus, a suboptimal performance of these non-B cell types can make it difficult to analyze the B cell specific contribution. In contrast, the AT model involves transfer of pure B cells thereby rulling out the contribution of T cells and FDCs. To this end, we transferred B cells from CD45.2 expressing WT and Sam68 KO mice to CD45.1 expressing recipient WT mice pre-immunized with 10% SRBC (Fig. 5A). Next day, the recipient mice were further immunized with 10% SRBC and GC was analyzed 4 days post-secondary immunization (Fig. 5A). The transfer efficiency for CD45.2+ WT or Sam68 KO B cells remained ~ 2–4% in the spleen of the host mice (Fig. 4B). However, CD45.2+ Sam68 KO donor B cells were under-represented in the GC B cell population (CD38− CD95+ GL7hi) compared to CD45.2+ WT donor B cells (Fig. 5C-D). The ratio of CD45.2+ B cells in the GC to total spleen was significantly reduced for KO B cells compared to WT (0.58 vs. 1.4, Fig. 5D). Notably, there was no difference in the over-all activation level of B cells in the recipient mice receiving either WT or KO B cells indicating that the lower abundance of the Sam68 KO cells in GC was not due to the failure of immunization of the mice receiving KO cells (Fig. 5E-F). Sam68 deficiency down-regulates Traf4 expression in B cells through elevation of miR29 expression

A recent report has demonstrated that microRNA (miRNA) 29 expression is down-regulated by Sam68 during T-helper1 cell differentiation and anti-microbial response[18]. It is therefore intriguing that Sam68 mediated miRNA regulation also plays an important role in B cell functions. To investigate that, we analyzed miR29a, b and c expression in WT and Sam68 KO splenic B cells. In the resting state, no difference in microRNA expression was observed in WT and KO B cells (Fig. 7 A, 0 h). However, when stimulated with anti-CD40 antibody, both miR29a and b expressions were significantly up-regulated in Sam68 KO B cells compared to WT B cells 24–96 h post stimulation (Fig. 7A). For miR29c, we only observed a trend of up-regulation (Fig. 7A). This suggests that in absence of Sam68, miR29 regulation is altered in B cells. As shown in Fig. 7B, upon anti-CD40 antibody stimulation, Traf4 gene expression was significantly increased in WT B cells from 0 to 96 h (Fig. 7B). To demonstrate that miR29 indeed targets Traf4 in B cells, we cloned miR29a in a custom retroviral plasmid with mRFP reporter (see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 7A) and attempted to overexpress the miR by retroviral transduction. Althogh the contsruct was successfully expressed in the Phoenix-Eco retroviral packaging cell line (Supplementary Figure S7C-D), it failed to express in B cells upon transduction. Therefore, we overexpressed it in WT mouse B cells by electroporation (Fig. 7C). Compared to empty vector (EV) electroporated cells, ~ eightfold upregulation of miR29a was observed in the overexpressed cells (29a OE, Fig. 7D). Concomittantly, a signficant reduction of Traf4, both in RNA (Fig. 7E) and protein level (Fig. 7F-G) was observed in the 29a OE cells compared to EV. This clearly shows that Traf4 is regulated by miR29a in mouse B cells.

Next, to investigate whether Traf4 deficiency in Sam68 KO B cells is indeed responsible for the reduced proliferation of these cells upon CD40 ligation, we cloned mouse Traf4 gene (mTraf4) in our custom retroviral vector with a N-terminal 3X FLAG tag and DsRed reporter (Supplementary Fig. 7B). WT and Sam68 KO B cells were retrovirally transduced with EV and mTraf4 constructs (Fig. 7H). Overexpression of Traf4 in both cell types was detected by intracellular FACS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7E) as well as by western blot (Supplementary Fig. 7 F). Next, we stimulated these cells with anti-CD40 antibody and analyzed proliferation over a period of 48 h. Although Traf4 overexpression did not accelerate proliferation of the WT B cells any further, it significantly increased proliferation in Sam68 KO B cells (Fig. 7I-J). This clearly suggests that miR29 mediated Traf4 regulation is playing an important role in limiting the CD40 responsiveness of Sam68 KO B cells. Interestingly, miR29 mediated Traf4 down-regulation has recently been shown in B cells from patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) [27]. Our study shows that such regulation also prevails in normal splenic B cells and Sam68 is crucial for miR29 regulation and propagation of CD40 signaling in B cells.

Discussion

In the current manuscript, we identify Sam68 as a critical regulator of B cell immune response. Sam68 complete knock out mouse exhibits impaired GC reaction and compromised humoral immunity. Specifically, Sam68 deficient B cells have reduced CD40 responsiveness, a key factor in GC development and maturation [28, 29]. At the molecular level, we demonstrate that Sam68 knockout leads to elevated miR29 expression in B cells, which subsequently downregulates its target gene Traf4, a crucial adaptor molecule in CD40 signaling. Thus, our findings collectively elucidate a novel role for Sam68 in B cell function and uncover a previously unidentified miRNA-mediated regulatory pathway in immune responses.

The potential role of Sam68 in immune cell development and function is particularly compelling given its role as a DNA damage sensor and facilitator of DSB repair [6, 30]. Immune cells, as part of their developmental and functional process continuously undergo DNA breakage and repair [8]. Thus, an effect of Sam68 in immune cell regulation is plausible. Consistent with this hypothesis, Sam68 has been shown to influence T cell differentiation toward the IFNγ-producing Th1 phenotype [18]. Critically, the absence of Sam68 and consequently a compromised Th1 response leads to fatal outcomes in mice infected with BCG, highlighting the essential role of Sam68 in protective immunity. Our findings extend these observations to the B cell compartment, demonstrating impaired GC formation, primary antibody response and memory response in Sam68 knockout mice following immunization with two distinct antigens of varying immunogenicity (Fig. 2 and 3).

GC is a multicellular event that involves the complex interplay between B cells, T cells and follicular dendritic cells [31]. Therefore, the precise contribution of B cell-intrinsic defects to the observed phenotype is hard to determine in a complete knockout model. To analyze such cell-intrinsic defects, one employs either cell specific gene manipulations using floxed target allele and Cre deleter lines or generates bone marrow chimeric and adoptive transfer models. Notably, the success of a tissue-specific or cell type specific conditional gene ablation depends on both the efficacy of Cre and accessibility of the floxed locus in the chromatin context. As a result, incomplete deletion, specifically in GC like complex proliferation centres, makes it difficult to exclude the contribution of non-deleted B cells. We, therefore, employed bone marrow chimeric and adoptive transfer models. A 1:1 competitive chimera generated using congenically distinguishable WT and Sam68 KO bone marrow cells in immunodeficient mice revealed a significantly reduced participation of KO B cells in GC compared to their overall splenic abundance (Fig. 4). The potential functional impairment of Sam68 KO T cells that prevailed in the complete KO model can be masked by the WT T cells in this setting.

Interestingly, bone marrow reconstitution also highlighted a role for Sam68 in T cell development. KO donor-derived T cells were consistently lower in chimeric mice compared to WT-derived T cells (Supplementary Fig. 4). This suggests that Sam68 KO lymphoid progenitors have a reduced capacity to commit to the T cell lineage, leading to a compensatory bias towards increased B cell engraftment from equivalent input of donor cells. On one hand, this supports the notion that Sam68 has no effect on B cell development as observed in the complete knockout mice (Fig. 1). On the other hand, it also reflects a general T cell development/differentiation defect as previously identified in BCG infected Sam68 knockout mice failing to differentiate into IFNγ-producing Th1 phenotype [18].

To further investigate the B cell-intrinsic role in GC formation, we transferred mature splenic B cells of WT or KO origin, either individually or as a 1:1 mixture, into immunocompetent WT hosts. Both experimental setups demonstrated a predominant contribution of WT B cells to GC formation despite similar overall splenic representation (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 5). Although functional impairment of other non-B cell types cannot be ruled out, these results clearly indicate that B cell-intrinsic defect plays an important role in the compromised immune response in Sam68-deficient mice. Notably, the B cell intrinsic effect on GC as observed in the bone marrow chimera and the adoptive transfer model were comparatively weaker than those observed in the complete KO model suggesting a critical role of Sam68 in other immune cell types such as follicular dendritic cells or T cells. This is in line with the reduced Th1 response observed in BCG infected Sam68 complete KO mice [18]. Further studies using cell type-specific deletion models with the newly generated Sam68 floxed mice [32] will elucidate these possibilities.

The preservation of T-cell independent immune responses in KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 3) indicates that KO B cells are not globally activation-deficient. Instead, they exhibit specific hypo-responsiveness to stimuli involved in TD immune responses. In line with this, KO B cells exhibit reduced proliferation in response to anti-CD40 antibody stimulation in vitro whereas LPS or anti-BCR stimulation have no effects (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 6E-G). CD40, a member of TNF receptor (TNFR) super family is expressed on B cells and interacts with its cognate ligand CD40L (CD154) on activated T cells [33]. This interaction is crucial for B cell proliferation, class switching and maintenance of GC. Thus, the reduced CD40 responsiveness in the KO B cells could explain the limited ability of these cells to participate in GC resulting in compromised humoral immunity. Notably, previous studies in chicken lymphoma cell line DT40 demonstrated that Sam68 deletion slows cell growth through prolonged S and G2/M phases [34, 35].

Finally, we investigated the mechanism of CD40 regulation by Sam68 in B cells. Sam68 is a regulator of RNA metabolism including small non-coding RNAs [1]. It is known to interact with Drosha and Dicer, the enzymes involved in miRNA biogenesis[36]. Recent study has demonstrated that Sam68 controls miR29 expression in T cells[18]. In absence of Sam68, miR29 is overexpressed leading to down-modulation of the target genes T-bet and Eomes and subsequently preventing Th1 polarization. Additionally, miR-29 was reported to target Traf4, a CD40 signaling adaptor, in CLL B cells[27]. Combining these observations, we tested whether such regulation prevail in Sam68 deleted B cells. Although the expression of miR29 does not change between WT and KO B cells under resting condition, it is considerably up-regulated in the KO cells upon CD40 stimulation (Fig. 7A). Consequently, unlike the WT B cells, Traf4 expression failed to increase in the KO cells in response to CD40 ligation resulting in reduced cell proliferation (Fig. 7B). Overexpression of Traf4 could rescue this proliferation defect in KO B cells indicating a direct involvement of Sam68 in miR29 mediated Traf4 regulation (Fig. 7I-J).

Although these findings suggest a miR-29-mediated mechanism, alternative explanations cannot be excluded. For instance, CD40 is a TNFR member (TNFR5) and Sam68 is known to associate with TNFR1 [37]. Therefore, a direct adaptor function of Sam68 in CD40 signaling might also be involved.

As stated before, Sam68 KO mice are severely affected by BCG infection due to lack of Th1 polarization of CD4+ T helper cells and subsequent clearance of bacilli within the infected macrophages[18]. Given the context of additional B cell intrinsic defects in Sam68 KO mice through our findings, it is relevant to test the possibility of repressed anti-BCG antibody repertoire despite adequate antigen presentation through recruited macrophages and dendritic cells. The lack of diverse antibody repertoire perturbs the opsonization and adequate phagocytosis. In addition, the activation of pattern recognition receptor (PRR) upon binding to BCG, including TLR2 and TLR4, are important for primary activation of macrophages and subsequent Th1 polarization of CD4+ T cells [38]. Like CD40 pathway, TLR signalling also relies on TRAF-family adapter proteins including Traf4 [39]. As we find downregulation of Traf4 gene expression and subsequent CD40 signalling defects in Sam68 KO B cells, it is worth to test a similar causality and defective TLR signalling in macrophages.

Collectively, our results highlight Sam68's critical role in B cell immune responses. Further research is necessary to fully elucidate its functions, particularly in the context of B cell malignancies and immunodeficiency.
