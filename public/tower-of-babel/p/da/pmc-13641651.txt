Association Between Acute Gastrointestinal Injury and Mortality Risk in Critically Ill Patients: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Tianye Gao, Jie Liu, Yangwen Du, Jing Yang, Guanyun Sheng, Lingyao Zhou, Yue Qiu, Qing Zhang, Manlin Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:To systematically evaluate the association between severe acute gastrointestinal injury (AGI)/gastrointestinal dysfunction score (GIDS) and mortality risk in adult intensive care unit (ICU) patients.METHODS:We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis. We searched the MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials databases for articles published between January 2016 and January 2025. Observational cohort studies reporting mortality outcomes in ICU patients with AGI (grades III–IV vs 0–II) or GIDS (scores 2–4 vs 0–1) were included. Studies focusing on specific subpopulations such as patients after cardiac surgery or with COVID-19 were excluded to maintain population homogeneity. The primary outcome was short-term all-cause mortality. Random-effects meta-analysis using inverse-variance weighting was performed using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).RESULTS:Eight studies involving 2,786 critically ill patients were included. The pooled analysis demonstrated that severe GI dysfunction (AGI III–IV or GIDS 2–4) was significantly associated with increased mortality risk (OR 2.78, 95% CI 2.19–3.52, I2 = 42.5%). Subgroup analyses by outcome type (28-day/ICU mortality: OR 2.70, 95% CI 2.04–3.58; in-hospital mortality: OR 4.27, 95% CI 1.63–11.18) and scoring system (AGI: OR 2.75, 95% CI 2.07–3.67; GIDS: OR 3.18, 95% CI 1.43–7.07) showed consistent results. The addition of a large-scale prospective Chinese study (n = 1,102) and a multicenter European cohort (n = 540) strengthened the findings and broadened generalizability.DISCUSSION:Severe AGI is strongly associated with increased mortality in critically ill patients. Early recognition and assessment of GI dysfunction using standardized grading systems may facilitate risk stratification and guide clinical management.

INTRODUCTION

Gastrointestinal (GI) dysfunction represents a common and clinically significant complication in critically ill patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs), with reported prevalence rates ranging from 30% to 62% (1,2). The GI tract plays a multifaceted role in maintaining homeostasis through digestive, absorptive, immune, endocrine, and barrier functions (3). During critical illness, the disruption of these functions can trigger a cascade of pathophysiological events that contribute to systemic complications, including bacterial translocation, inflammatory mediator release, and progression to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (4,5).

In 2012, the Working Group on Abdominal Problems (WGAP) of the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine (ESICM) introduced a standardized definition and 4-grade classification system for acute GI injury (AGI) to address the lack of uniformity in assessing GI dysfunction in critically ill patients (6). This classification system stratifies AGI into 4 grades based on the severity of the GI symptoms, feeding intolerance and intra-abdominal complications. Grade I represents a self-limiting condition with increased risk of developing GI dysfunction, grade II indicates GI dysfunction requiring intervention, grade III represents GI failure where function cannot be restored with interventions, and grade IV denotes dramatically manifesting GI failure that is immediately life-threatening (6,7). The introduction of this standardized grading system has facilitated the research and clinical assessment of GI dysfunction in the ICU setting.

Subsequently, alternative scoring systems have been developed to quantify GI dysfunction more comprehensively. The GI dysfunction score (GIDS), proposed by Reintam Blaser et al (8,9), provides a simplified numerical assessment of GI function using a 0–4-point scale based on clinical parameters. This scoring system has been validated in a large international multicenter prospective study (the iSOFA study), which demonstrated its prognostic value for predicting mortality in critically ill patients across diverse ICU settings (9). The GIDS offers a practical tool for the bedside assessment of GI dysfunction severity.

Despite growing recognition of the importance of GI dysfunction in critical illness, the precise relationship between AGI severity and clinical outcomes, particularly mortality, remains incompletely characterized (10,11). A recent consensus statement (12) noted broad support for incorporating GI dysfunction into risk stratification but was unable to include it in the final recommendations because of a scarcity of clinical data. Although several observational studies have demonstrated associations between higher AGI grades or GIDS and increased mortality risk, the strength and consistency of these associations vary across different patient populations and clinical settings (13,14). Some studies have reported AGI as an independent predictor of mortality with odds ratios (ORs) ranging from 1.5 to 4.5 (15,16), whereas others have found weaker or nonsignificant associations after adjusting for confounding variables such as illness severity scores and organ dysfunction markers.

The heterogeneity in study designs, patient populations, outcome definitions, and the timing of GI assessment across existing studies underscores the need for a comprehensive synthesis of available evidence. Previous meta-analyses have examined the overall prevalence and impact of AGI on ICU outcomes (17), but focused quantitative assessment specifically examining the dose-response relationship between AGI severity grades and mortality risk has been limited. Furthermore, the comparative prognostic value of different GI assessment tools (AGI grading vs GIDS) has not been systematically evaluated.

Understanding the magnitude of the association between severe GI dysfunction and mortality has important clinical implications for risk stratification, resource allocation, and potentially, guiding targeted interventions in ICU patients (18,19). The early identification of patients with severe AGI may enable clinicians to implement proactive management strategies, optimize nutritional support, and intensify monitoring for complications (20). Moreover, GI dysfunction scores could potentially be incorporated into multidimensional prognostic models to enhance the prediction of adverse outcomes in critically ill patients (21).

Against this background, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to (i) quantitatively synthesize the association between severe AGI (AGI grades III–IV or GIDS 2–4) and short-term mortality in adult ICU patients, (ii) examine whether this association is consistent across different outcome definitions and GI assessment tools, and (iii) evaluate the quality of available evidence and identify areas requiring further investigation.

METHODS

Study design and reporting

We conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 statement. All analyses were performed using predefined methods to minimize bias and ensure transparency in reporting.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were selected based on the population, intervention/exposure, comparator, outcome, study design framework. The population of interest comprised adult patients (aged ≥18 years) admitted to ICUs. For the exposure assessment, we included studies that evaluated AGI using the ESICM-WGAP 2012 four-grade classification system (grades 0–IV), with the maximum grade during the first week of ICU admission serving as the exposure intensity (6). The exposure group was defined as patients with AGI grades III–IV, and the comparator group consisted of patients with AGI grades 0–II. Studies using the GIDS, scored from 0 to 4 points, were also eligible, with exposure defined as a GIDS of 2–4 and the comparator as a GIDS of 0–1 (9). The primary outcome was short-term all-cause mortality, prioritizing 28-day or ICU mortality. Studies reporting only in-hospital mortality were included but were analyzed separately in subgroup analyses to examine their potential influence on pooled estimates.

Regarding study design, we included prospective and retrospective observational cohort studies that clearly defined AGI or GIDS according to the specified criteria and reported mortality outcomes. To ensure population homogeneity, we excluded studies focusing on specific subpopulations such as patients who had undergone cardiac surgery, in whom AGI is more closely related to cardiopulmonary bypass than general critical illness pathophysiology, and studies conducted exclusively in COVID-19 populations, where distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and treatment strategies (e.g. prone positioning) may differentially affect GI function and confound the AGI-mortality relationship. Additional exclusion criteria included (i) non-ICU populations; (ii) pediatric studies; (iii) studies without AGI or GIDS definitions or those unable to be mapped to the predefined dichotomization criteria; (iv) studies not reporting mortality outcomes; (v) randomized trials or intervention studies in which AGI or GIDS was the intervention target rather than an exposure variable; (vi) case series, reviews, and conference abstracts; (vii) duplicate publications or studies with overlapping cohorts (in which case, we retained the report with the largest sample size or most comprehensive information); and (viii) studies focusing exclusively on highly selective populations such as those requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, which were considered only in prespecified sensitivity or subgroup analyses.

Information sources and search strategy

We systematically searched the following electronic databases: MEDLINE through PubMed, Embase, the Web of Science Core Collection, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. To capture studies published in Chinese, we supplemented our search with the China National Knowledge Infrastructure and Wanfang databases. The search period was restricted to January 1, 2016, to January 31, 2025, with no language restrictions imposed. The starting date of 2016 was chosen to capture studies conducted after the widespread adoption of the ESICM-WGAP 2012 AGI classification system. The search strategy was developed iteratively, with an input from a medical librarian and combined subject headings with text words related to GI injury, critical illness, and mortality.

The search strategy used the following PubMed combined title/abstract and Medical Subject Headings terms: (“acute gastrointestinal injury”[Title/Abstract] OR AGI[Title/Abstract] OR “gastrointestinal dysfunction score”[Title/Abstract] OR GIDS[Title/Abstract]) AND (ICU[Title/Abstract] OR “intensive care”[Title/Abstract] OR “critically ill”[Title/Abstract]) AND (mortality[Title/Abstract] OR death[Title/Abstract] OR survival[Title/Abstract] OR prognosis[Title/Abstract]). Equivalent keyword and subject heading combinations were used for searches in other databases, adapted to the specific requirements and indexing vocabularies of each database. We also manually screened the reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles to identify additional potentially eligible studies that may not have been captured by the electronic searches.

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of all the records identified through the database searches to determine potential eligibility. Full-text articles of potentially relevant studies were then retrieved and assessed against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements between the 2 reviewers during either the title/abstract screening or full-text evaluation stages were resolved through discussion, with the involvement of a third reviewer when consensus could not be reached. The study selection process and reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented and are presented in Figure 1 (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram).

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data from the included studies using a standardized, pilot-tested data extraction form. The following information was systematically collected from each study: (i) first author name; (ii) year of publication; (iii) country and number of centers; (iv) study design (prospective or retrospective cohort); (v) ICU type and characteristics; (vi) total sample size and sample sizes for exposure and comparator groups; (vii) patient baseline characteristics including age, sex distribution, illness severity scores (e.g. acute physiology and chronic health evaluation II [APACHE II], sequential organ failure assessment [SOFA]) and comorbidities; (viii) AGI or GIDS assessment windows and classification criteria; (ix) outcome definitions and follow-up timepoints; (x) the number of deaths and total patients in severe and mild/no injury groups (2 × 2 contingency table data when available); and (xi) adjusted effect estimates (ORs or hazard ratios [HRs]) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and lists of covariates included in multivariable models. When studies reported outcomes at multiple timepoints (e.g. both 28- and 60-day mortality), we preferentially extracted 28-day mortality data to maintain consistency across studies. For studies reporting multiple expressions of the exposure variable, we prioritized assessments based on the maximum AGI grade or GIDS during the first week of ICU admission and dichotomized according to our predefined criteria. Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion and by referring back to the original publications.

Risk-of-bias assessment

Methodological quality and risk of bias in the included cohort studies were assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS), a validated tool designed for evaluating nonrandomized studies in meta-analyses. The NOS assesses 3 domains: (i) study group selection (maximum 4 points), (ii) group comparability (maximum 2 points), and (iii) outcome ascertainment (maximum 3 points), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 9 points.

Studies were categorized as high quality (7–9 points), moderate quality (4–6 points), or low quality (0–3 points) based on their total NOS scores. Two reviewers independently conducted the quality assessment for each included study, with disagreements resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer when necessary. The results of the quality assessment are presented in the Results section.

Effect measures

The primary effect measure was the OR comparing mortality risk between patients with severe GI dysfunction (AGI III–IV or GIDS 2–4) and those with mild or no dysfunction (AGI 0–II or GIDS 0–1). For studies providing 2 × 2 contingency table data (number of deaths and total patients in each exposure group), we calculated unadjusted ORs. When a cell in the contingency table contained zero events, a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to all cells to enable the calculation of the OR. For studies that only reported adjusted effect estimates from multivariable regression models (adjusted ORs or HRs with 95% CIs), we extracted the log-transformed effect sizes and their SEs, which were calculated from the CIs using the following formula: SE = (ln[upper limit] − ln[lower limit])/(2 × 1.96). These log-transformed estimates were then pooled using the generic inverse-variance method. In the context of short-term mortality outcomes, HRs were considered to approximate relative risk and were treated as comparable with ORs for the purpose of combining effect estimates across studies.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the meta package (version 8.1-0) and metafor package (version 4.8-0). For the primary analysis, we used a random-effects model with inverse-variance weighting using the DerSimonian-Laird method for between-study variance estimation. Studies with available 2 × 2 count data (calculating unadjusted ORs) and studies reporting only adjusted ORs and HRs were unified on a log relative effect scale using the metagen() function. All effect sizes were expressed as ORs with 95% CIs after back-transformation from the log scale. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the Q statistic (with statistical significance set at P < 0.10), between-study variance (τ2), and the I2 statistic, which describes the percentage of total variation across studies caused by heterogeneity rather than chance; I2 values of ∼25%, 50%, and 75% were interpreted as representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively.

Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and assess the consistency of associations across different study characteristics. These included (i) scoring system type (AGI classification vs GIDS) and (ii) outcome type (28-day or ICU mortality vs in-hospital mortality). Tests for subgroup differences were performed using the χ2 test. Prespecified sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of our findings, including by (i) restricting analysis to studies using only the AGI classification system, (ii) limiting analysis to studies reporting 28-day or ICU mortality outcomes, (iii) analyzing only studies providing extractable 2 × 2 count data, and (iv) adopting leave-one-out analysis where the pooled estimate was recalculated sequentially with each study removed to identify influential studies. As a supplementary analysis focused on count data, we separately pooled studies with 2 × 2 table data using the metabin() function to generate a forest plot displaying events and totals in each group, consistent with RevMan formatting conventions.

Publication bias was evaluated through visual inspection of funnel plot asymmetry and Egger regression test for small-study effects (implemented using the metabias() function). We acknowledged that when fewer than 10 studies are included in a meta-analysis, the statistical power of these tests is limited, and results should be interpreted with caution.

RESULTS

Study selection and characteristics

The systematic search of electronic databases yielded 622 records. After removing 171 duplicates, 451 unique records remained for title and abstract screening. The initial screening excluded 389 records that clearly did not meet the inclusion criteria (non-ICU populations, absence of AGI/GIDS assessment, no mortality outcomes, inappropriate study designs, or other reasons). A total of 67 full-text articles were retrieved for detailed evaluation. Of these, 59 were excluded for the following reasons: lack of a control group or prepost design only (n = 13), intervention not AGI-related (n = 14), failure to report primary outcomes (n = 10), insufficient data to compute effect sizes (n = 11), populations not critically ill or inseparable mixed samples (n = 6), noncomparable groups (n = 2), and specific populations such as COVID-19 (n = 2) or postcardiac surgery (n = 1). Among the excluded studies, 3 prospective cohort studies that otherwise seemed potentially eligible were excluded because they focused exclusively on COVID-19 populations or patients who had undergone cardiac surgery, in whom the pathophysiology of GI injury differs substantially from that of general ICU populations, as detailed in the Eligibility Criteria section. Ultimately, 8 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the meta-analysis, comprising 6 studies that provided extractable 2 × 2 count data and 2 studies that reported only adjusted effect estimates (Figure 1).

The characteristics of the 8 included studies are summarized in Table 1. The studies were published between 2016 and 2024. Six studies were conducted in China, and 1 multicenter study was conducted across multiple European ICUs, thereby extending the geographic diversity of the evidence base. Six studies utilized the ESICM-WGAP AGI grading system (grades III–IV vs 0–II), and 2 studies used the GIDS (scores 2–4 vs 0–1). Study designs included 6 prospective cohorts and 2 retrospective cohorts. Sample sizes ranged from 90 to 1,102 patients, with a total of 2,786 critically ill patients across all studies. Seven studies reported 28-day mortality as the primary outcome, and 1 study reported in-hospital mortality. The mean age of patients across studies ranged from 52.3 to 68.7 years, with male proportions between 53.1% and 71.3%. Illness severity, as measured by APACHE II scores, ranged from 15.2 to 24.8 where reported, and SOFA scores ranged from 5.8 to 10.4 (Table 1). Where these data were not available from individual studies, this was noted.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality assessment using the NOS revealed that most included studies were of moderate to high quality (Table 2). Six studies achieved scores of 8–9, indicating that they were of high quality with clear selection criteria, adequate comparability adjustment, and appropriate outcome assessment. Two studies received scores of 6, reflecting moderate quality with some limitations in sample size or confounding control. The primary potential sources of bias identified across studies included the subjective nature of the AGI/GIDS assessment (which relies partially on clinical judgement), potential residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment, and the retrospective design of 2 studies that may introduce selection bias. However, all studies clearly defined exposure and outcome variables and used standard diagnostic criteria.

Primary meta-analysis

The primary random-effects meta-analysis combining all 8 studies demonstrated a strong and statistically significant association between severe GI dysfunction and mortality risk (Figure 2). The pooled OR was 2.78 (95% CI 2.19–3.52, P < 0.001), indicating that patients with severe AGI (grades III–IV) or high GIDS (scores 2–4) had nearly three-fold higher odds of death than those with mild or no GI dysfunction. Between-study heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 42.5%, P = 0.09), which was expected given the inclusion of studies from diverse geographic settings and using different scoring systems. The Q test for heterogeneity was not statistically significant at the conventional threshold (Q = 12.17, df = 7, P = 0.09).

Forest plot of the primary meta-analysis showing the association between severe gastrointestinal dysfunction (AGI III-IV or GIDS 2–4) and mortality in critically ill patients (8 studies). Random-effects pooled odds ratio: 2.78 (95% CI 2.19–3.52), I2 = 42.5%, p for heterogeneity = 0.09. CI, confidence interval; GIDS, GI dysfunction score.

Individual study effect estimates ranged from an OR of 1.94 (95% CI 1.12–3.37) in the study of Shen et al (26) to an OR of 4.27 (95% CI 1.63–11.18) in the study of Hai et al (25). The study of Reintam Blaser et al (9) contributed an OR of 2.15 (95% CI 1.76–2.63), Li et al (22) reported an OR of 2.74 (95% CI 1.28–5.84), Hu et al (23) reported an OR of 3.18 (95% CI 2.07–4.89), Li et al (24) reported an OR of 2.42 (95% CI 0.74–7.89), Liu et al (16) (using GIDS) reported an OR of 3.18 (95% CI 1.43–7.07), and Wang et al (27) contributed a large sample (n = 1,102) with an OR of 3.86 (95% CI 2.78–5.37). All point estimates consistently favored increased mortality risk in the severe dysfunction group, with 7 of the 8 studies showing statistically significant associations at the individual study level.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analysis stratified by outcome type revealed consistent associations regardless of mortality definition (Figure 3). Studies reporting 28-day or ICU mortality (n = 5) yielded a pooled OR of 2.70 (95% CI 2.04–3.58, I2 = 0%, P = 0.71 for heterogeneity). The single study that reported in-hospital mortality (25) yielded an OR of 4.27 (95% CI 1.63–11.18). The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (χ2 = 0.80, df = 1, P = 0.37), indicating no evidence that the association differs meaningfully between 28-day/ICU mortality and in-hospital mortality outcomes.

Sensitivity analyses

Multiple sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our primary findings. When restricting analysis to only AGI-graded studies (n = 5), the pooled OR was 2.75 (95% CI 2.07–3.67) with I2 = 0.0% (Figure 5), consistent with the primary analysis. Limiting analysis to studies reporting 28-day or ICU mortality (n = 5) yielded an OR of 2.70 (95% CI 2.04–3.58, I2 = 0.0%) (Figure 6), again demonstrating consistency. When analyzing only the 6 studies with extractable 2 × 2 count data using the metabin function (excluding Shen et al [26] and Reintam Blaser et al [9], which provided only adjusted estimates), the pooled unadjusted OR was 3.46 (95% CI 2.76–4.33, I2 = 0%, P = 0.91 for heterogeneity) (Figure 7), with Wang et al (27) contributing the largest weight (46.6%). Leave-one-out analysis showed that no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled estimate, with recalculated pooled ORs ranging from 2.58 to 3.01 across iterations.

Publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plot (Figure 8), which was constructed using the 6 studies with directly calculable effect estimates, showed a reasonably symmetric distribution around the pooled effect estimate, with no obvious asymmetry suggesting publication bias or small-study effects. However, formal statistical assessment of publication bias was limited by the small number of studies (n = 6). The Egger regression test was performed but should be interpreted cautiously given the insufficient statistical power when k < 10 studies. The test did not identify statistically significant asymmetry, but this finding does not definitively exclude publication bias given the inherent limitations of these methods in small meta-analyses.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides robust quantitative evidence that severe AGI, assessed using either the ESICM-WGAP AGI grading system or the GIDS, is strongly associated with increased mortality risk in critically ill patients. Our pooled analysis of 8 observational cohort studies encompassing 2,786 ICU patients demonstrated that patients with severe GI dysfunction (AGI grades III–IV or GIDS 2–4) had nearly three-fold higher odds of death than those with mild or no GI dysfunction (pooled OR 2.78, 95% CI 2.19–3.52). This association remained robust across multiple subgroup and sensitivity analyses. These findings underscore the prognostic importance of GI dysfunction assessment in the ICU setting and support the integration of standardized GI evaluation tools into clinical risk stratification algorithms.

Our updated analysis incorporates 2 additional high-quality studies that substantially strengthen the evidence base. The pivotal study by Reintam Blaser et al (iSOFA study) (9) validated the GIDS in a diverse international cohort across European ICUs, confirming the prognostic value of GI dysfunction (OR 2.15, 95% CI 1.76–2.63). This study is particularly important because it extends the evidence beyond Asian populations and broadens the geographic diversity of the evidence base. Furthermore, the addition of the recent large-scale study by Wang et al (27) (n = 1,102) significantly strengthened our findings, contributing the largest individual study weight and demonstrating that severe AGI was associated with a nearly 4-fold increase in mortality risk (OR 3.86, 95% CI 2.78–5.37) in their development cohort. The inclusion of these studies increased our total sample from 1,144 to 2,786 patients, substantially narrowing CIs and enhancing the precision of our pooled estimates.

The magnitude of association observed in our meta-analysis aligns with and extends previous research examining the impact of GI dysfunction on outcomes in critical illness. Gastrointestinal dysfunction in critically ill patients can manifest through various mechanisms, including intestinal ischemia, mucosal barrier breakdown, altered motility, and feeding intolerance, all of which contribute to systemic inflammation and organ dysfunction (28). The “gut hypothesis” of critical illness posits that the GI tract serves as the motor of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, with bacterial translocation from a dysfunctional gut triggering systemic inflammatory cascades (29). Our findings provide epidemiological support for this mechanistic framework, demonstrating that more severe grades of GI injury correlate with substantially elevated mortality risk. Similar to other organ dysfunctions recognized in critical care, such as acute kidney injury and respiratory failure, GI dysfunction represents a significant and clinically meaningful risk factor that warrants systematic assessment and monitoring (30,31).

An important finding from our subgroup analyses is the consistency of the mortality association across different GI assessment tools. Both the AGI grading system and the GIDS demonstrated a similar predictive value, with overlapping CIs and no statistical evidence of differential association (P = 0.74 for subgroup difference). This suggests that although the 2 scoring systems use different methodological approaches, they capture similar underlying pathophysiological severity and provide comparable prognostic information. The AGI grading system, based on the 2012 ESICM-WGAP recommendations, incorporates clinical symptoms, intra-abdominal pressure measurements, and feeding tolerance into a categorical 4-grade classification (6,27). By contrast, the GIDS uses a numerical scoring approach that may offer enhanced simplicity and potential for serial monitoring (16,32). The demonstrated equivalence in prognostic value suggests that clinicians may select either system based on local expertise, available resources, and clinical context with confidence that both provide valid risk assessment.

The consistent association between severe GI dysfunction and mortality across 28-day, ICU and in-hospital outcomes suggests that the prognostic impact of AGI extends beyond the immediate ICU period, influencing both short-term and intermediate-term survival. This observation has important implications for understanding the natural history and long-term consequences of GI dysfunction in critical illness. Although the single study reporting in-hospital mortality showed a numerically higher OR (4.27) than studies reporting 28-day mortality (pooled OR 2.70), this difference was not statistically significant and likely reflects sampling variation or differences in patient populations rather than a true biological difference (33,34). The sustained association across different outcome timeframes highlights that severe AGI represents a marker of fundamental physiological derangement that affects both immediate survival and recovery trajectories.

The moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 42.5%) observed in our updated meta-analysis warrants discussion and likely reflects the increased diversity of the included studies. Several factors may contribute to this heterogeneity. First, the inclusion of the study of Reintam Blaser et al (9), which reported an adjusted HR rather than an unadjusted OR, introduced methodological variability; the lower effect estimate from this study (HR 1.40) is expected given multivariable adjustment for confounders. Second, the geographic expansion from exclusively Chinese populations to include European centers introduced clinical heterogeneity related to different ICU practices, patient demographics, and underlying disease distributions (11,35). Third, variations in scoring systems (AGI vs GIDS) and study designs (prospective vs retrospective) contributed additional between-study variability. Importantly, despite this moderate heterogeneity, all individual study estimates were in the same direction and all CIs overlapped, supporting the overall consistency of the finding.

The clinical implications of our findings are multifaceted. First, these results support the incorporation of systematic GI function assessment into routine ICU practice as part of comprehensive organ dysfunction monitoring (36). Given the strong association between severe AGI and mortality, the early identification of patients with high-grade GI dysfunction could facilitate the timely implementation of targeted interventions, enhanced nutritional support strategies and appropriate resource allocation (37). Second, AGI grading or the GIDS could potentially be integrated into multidimensional prognostic models alongside established severity scores such as APACHE II or SOFA to improve mortality prediction accuracy (38,39). Third, the consistent association observed across different scoring systems and clinical contexts suggests potential utility for these tools in clinical trial risk stratification and in guiding family discussions regarding prognosis. Fourth, the findings emphasize the importance of evidence-based approaches to managing GI complications in critically ill patients, including the optimization of enteral nutrition delivery, the proactive management of feeding intolerance, the monitoring and management of intra-abdominal hypertension, and the consideration of prokinetic or other adjunctive therapies when appropriate (40,41).

From a research perspective, our findings identify several important knowledge gaps and directions for future investigation. First, although observational studies consistently demonstrate an association between AGI and mortality, the mechanisms underlying this relationship require further elucidation. Prospective studies incorporating biomarkers of gut barrier function, intestinal perfusion, and systemic inflammation could help clarify whether GI dysfunction is primarily a marker of illness severity or represents a modifiable causal pathway contributing directly to adverse outcomes. Second, interventional trials are needed to determine whether targeted therapies aimed at preventing or reversing GI dysfunction can improve clinical outcomes. Such interventions might include optimized enteral nutrition protocols, the selective use of prokinetic agents, management strategies for intra-abdominal hypertension, or novel therapies targeting gut barrier integrity. Third, studies examining the optimal timing and frequency of GI assessment, as well as the prognostic value of dynamic changes in AGI grade or the GIDS over time, could enhance the clinical utility of these tools. Fourth, validation studies in non-Asian populations and diverse ICU settings are essential to confirm the generalizability of our findings and establish the performance of GI dysfunction scores across different healthcare contexts.

Several limitations of this meta-analysis warrant consideration. First, the relatively small number of included studies (n = 8), although increased from our initial 6, still limits the statistical power for detecting publication bias and conducting extensive subgroup analyses. Although our funnel plot did not show obvious asymmetry, formal tests for publication bias have limited reliability with fewer than 10 studies. Second, although the inclusion of the study of Reintam Blaser et al (9) extends the evidence to European ICU populations, the majority of included studies were conducted in China, which may limit generalizability to other geographic regions with different patient populations, healthcare systems, or ICU practices. Third, and importantly, only 2 of the 8 studies (Shen et al [26] and Reintam Blaser et al [9]) provided adjusted effect estimates from multivariable models, whereas the remaining studies reported unadjusted ORs. This means that the pooled estimate may be subject to residual confounding by unmeasured variables such as illness severity (SOFA/APACHE II scores), the use of vasopressors, mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy, and other markers of disease severity. Without specific multivariable adjustments for these disease processes, it is difficult to determine conclusively whether AGI is an independent prognostic variable or primarily a marker of overall disease severity in which sicker patients develop more severe GI dysfunction. Fourth, there is a potential risk of exposure misclassification because sicker patients could be more likely to be graded as AGI III–IV, and the classification relies partially on clinician assessment, which could generate classification bias. Fifth, the assessment of AGI and the GIDS involves some degree of clinical judgement, particularly regarding symptoms such as feeding intolerance, which may introduce measurement variability across different practitioners and institutions. Sixth, we were unable to examine dose-response relationships across the full spectrum of AGI grades due to our dichotomized exposure definition, which may obscure more nuanced associations. Seventh, publication bias favoring positive results cannot be definitively excluded. Finally, the observational nature of the included studies precludes causal inference; severe GI dysfunction may be both a consequence and a cause of physiological deterioration, and the observed associations do not establish whether GI dysfunction directly contributes to mortality or primarily serves as a marker of overall disease severity. Future prospective studies with appropriate multivariable adjustment for illness severity and organ dysfunction are urgently needed to clarify the independent prognostic contribution of AGI.

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis provides strong evidence that severe AGI, assessed using standardized grading systems, is robustly associated with increased mortality risk in critically ill patients. The consistency of this association across different scoring tools, outcome definitions, study designs, and both Asian and European populations supports the clinical validity and prognostic value of systematic GI function assessment in the ICU. These findings underscore the importance of recognizing and monitoring GI dysfunction as a key component of organ function assessment in critical illness. Future research should focus on validating these findings in diverse populations with appropriate adjustment for confounders, elucidating mechanistic pathways and evaluating whether targeted interventions to prevent or reverse GI dysfunction can improve survival outcomes.
