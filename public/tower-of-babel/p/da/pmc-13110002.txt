Reply to Bayici et al.
Authors: Joshua G Crane, Mark S Slaughter, Jaimin Trivedi, Michele Gallo
Published: 2026

Thank you for your comments regarding our manuscript “Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting Following ST Elevation Myocardial Infarction Outcomes Are Associated with Time to Surgery.”1 Our findings were based on a retrospective review of a large administrative database of patients who had undergone coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) after ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). Our evaluation revealed that in-hospital mortality was greatest in patients who underwent CABG within the first 48 hours and that CABG in 2-15 days following STEMI had the lowest in-hospital mortality.

In their letter, Bayici et al. discuss the limitations of our study regarding the confounding of outcome data by the indication for the operation.2 They call for additional studies, stratifying the groups of patients by their risks and clinical courses. Unfortunately, as noted in the limitations of our article, this retrospective review from the National Inpatient Sample is limited in the data that it collects. Unfortunately, valuable data for risk stratification and reference of clinical status, such as troponin, haemodynamic data, SYNTAX, and STS risk score, are not available. However, other publications have reached similar conclusions for patients with acute myocardial infarctions and timing to CABG. In non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) patients, an operative window for CABG of 3-7 days following NSTEMI showed significantly lower odds of operative mortality.3 Additionally, NSTEMI patients who were operated on within the first 24 hours had significantly greater odds of in-hospital mortality than those who waited at least 3 days, similar to our findings.4 A potential pathophysiologic reason for delaying surgery in STEMI patients is the impending ischaemia-reperfusion injury to cardiac myocytes. While most of the damage to the myocytes is done within the ischaemic period and the first few minutes of reperfusion, the ischaemic area will continue to have necrosis and apoptosis for up to 3-days following reperfusion.5 STEMI patients who were operated on within the first 6 hours following presentation had 290% increased risk of in-hospital mortality compared to those who waited at least 3 days.4

In general, we agree with Bayici et al. that having additional clinical data would help further stratify the groups for the timing of CABG in patients with a STEMI. However, our findings are consistent with other publications and clinical experience that if patients are able to wait several days after a STEMI, the risk of CABG is reduced.
