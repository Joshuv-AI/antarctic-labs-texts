ITS phylogeny reveals a wide host range and a high genetic diversity of endophytic Phyllosticta capitalensis in Indonesia
Authors: Indriati Ramadhani, Yoshiaki Tsuda, Hiroki Yamanobe, Kaoru Yamaguchi, Izumi Okane
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhyllosticta capitalensis is a cosmopolitan endophytic fungus with a wide host range. We hypothesize that endophytic P. capitalensis originates in tropical regions, which have higher host plant diversity than subtropical and temperate regions. We tested this hypothesis by examining the host range and genetic diversity in Indonesia, and the phylogeographic distribution of haplotypes according to the climatic conditions of Indonesia and elsewhere. A total of 434 plant species were sampled in the Bogor and Cibodas Botanical Gardens in Indonesia. A total of 244 ITS sequences of endophytic P. capitalensis from Indonesia and 133 previously collected from tropical, subtropical, and temperate regions of Japan were analyzed to assess genetic variation across climatic zones. Endophytic P. capitalensis was isolated from the leaves of 217 species from 182 genera in 35 families. The two Indonesian populations had higher haplotype diversities (16 and 11 haplotypes, respectively) than those at high-latitude sites. Assessment of haplotype phylogeny revealed a star-like pattern; Hap 2 was the dominant haplotype, being detected in 30 plant families in Indonesia, suggesting a rapid radiation of the species. The results demonstrate that P. capitalensis has higher genetic diversity in tropical than subtropical and temperate regions, supporting our hypothesis regarding its phylogenetic origin.Keywords: endophytic fungus, haplotype distribution, latitudinal gradient, phylogeography, population structure

1. Introduction

Phyllosticta capitalensis Henn. (Phyllostictaceae, Botryosphaeriales, Ascomycota) is a cosmopolitan endophytic fungus with a wide host range. It has been associated with over 300 plant species worldwide as an endophyte or pathogen causing leaf spot (Norphanphoun et al., 2020; Wikee et al., 2013a, 2013b). This species is a homothallic fungus that can form ascigerous states in cultures derived from a single ascospore and a single conidium (Okane et al., 2001). Based on phylogenetic analysis, Okane et al. (2003) showed that P. capitalensis isolated from various plants has a wide host range. Previously, Baayen et al. (2002) reported that the nonpathogenic Phyllosticta isolates associated with citrus black spots (CBS) were P. capitalensis, which coincided with Guignardia mangiferae A.J. Roy. However, Glienke et al. (2011) later demonstrated through phylogenetic analysis that P. capitalensis was differentiated from G. mangiferae.

Phyllosticta paracapitalensis Guarnaccia & Crous, which can be distinguished from P. capitalensis by multi-gene sequence analysis, was proposed recently (Guarnaccia et al., 2017; Tran et al., 2019). Norphanphoun et al. (2020) resolved the species delimitation of the species complexes in the genus Phyllosticta based on multi-gene sequence analyses. The identity in the nucleotide sequences of P. paracapitalensis (CPC 26517T) and P. capitalensis (CBS 128856T) was approximately 99.5% in 2,410 bp (excluding gap sites) of combined multi-gene sequence data (Norphanphoun et al., 2020). By contrast, the P. capitalensis population, which formed a monophyletic group in a phylogenetic analysis based on rDNA internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region sequences, includes several species reported to be separate from P. paracapitalensis. Phyllosticta alliacea Motohashi, Jun. Nishikawa & C. Nakash., which was proposed as a new species by Motohashi et al. (2008), was also included. However, our analysis of ITS, large subunit (LSU) genes, tef1-α, rpb2, actA, and gapdh of the ex-type strain of P. alliacea (NBRC 102248T) showed that this species should possibly be assigned to P. capitalensis (ITS [AB454263]: 546/546 bp [100%], LSU [AB454178]: 504/504 bp [100%], tef1-α [PV647842]: 104/105 bp [99%], rpb2 [PV647839]: 617/617 bp [100%], actA [PV647841]: 232/232 bp [100%], and gapdh [PV647840]: 458/462 bp [99%]). Several other species have a similar taxonomic status to P. alliacea, including P. camelliae, P. conjac, P. fallopiae, P. harai, P. mangifera-indicae, P. miurae, and P. rhizophorae. These species form a monophyletic group in the ITS phylogenetic analysis (Norphanphoun et al., 2020).

Endophyte-plant associations are shaped by complex genotypic interactions between the host and the endophyte, as well as by local abiotic factors (Oita et al., 2021; Saikkonen et al., 1998). These interactions influence not only the ecological roles of endophytes but also their diversity and distribution across climatic regions. Consistent patterns of endophytic species diversity along latitudinal gradients have been reported, with higher species diversity typically observed in low- compared to high-latitude sites (Arnold & Lutzoni, 2007; Fröhlich & Hyde, 1999). Based on this pattern, it is reasonable to hypothesize that tropical populations of endophytic P. capitalensis, which tend to exhibit higher genetic diversity, may serve as sources for its global distribution. Indonesia, as a tropical archipelago with high levels of plant endemism (de Bruyn et al., 2014; Lohman et al., 2011) and recognized as a global biodiversity hotspot (von Rintelen et al., 2017), represents a valuable region for evaluating this hypothesis. However, previous studies on the genetic diversity of endophytic P. capitalensis have focused primarily on tropical plant species from India and Brazil (Pandey et al., 2003; Rodrigues et al., 2004; Wickert et al., 2012; Zavala et al., 2014), and data from Indonesia remain sparse. Addressing this gap is important for understanding how genetically diverse populations interact with different host plants and adapt to environmental conditions beyond their center of origin.

In this study, we focused on endophytic P. capitalensis species complex, forming a single clade using rDNA ITS sequence analysis in a relatively narrower sense than that of Norphanphoun et al. (2020), to assess the diversification and differentiation of endophytic P. capitalensis, which has an extensive host range and distributes widely. Although other similar studies are available based on the specific host plants (Baayen et al., 2002; Glienke et al., 2011; Wickert et al., 2012) or from a specific geographical region (Okane et al., 2003), the genetic variation among the isolates on various host plants across the climatic region is not known. The large-scale analyses of the genetic variation among populations within a P. capitalensis species complex to reveal the relationships with their host plants to provide insight into their parasitism and physiological specialization are required. Therefore, the ITS region evolves faster than the small subunit (SSU) and large subunit (LSU) genes (Nilsson et al., 2008; Schoch et al., 2012) and is considered to be under neutral selection (Chen et al., 2021) is analyzed as a phylogenetic and population genetic marker in this study. Furthermore, the host plant diversity and genetic diversity of endophytic fungi in Indonesia and surrounding areas are examined, to assess their distribution across tropical, subtropical, and temperate regions.

Ultimately, this study aims to investigate the host plant diversity of endophytic P. capitalensis in Indonesia; to estimate the level of genetic diversity of the Indonesian population of this fungus; and to assess the phylogeographic distribution of ITS haplotypes of this fungus between Indonesia and several regions in Japan with different climates. The findings will facilitate further work on introduction pathways, host plant expansion or specialization, and speciation among plant-associated fungi.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Sample collection and isolation

Sample collection was conducted between Apr and Aug 2021 in two botanical gardens: the Bogor Botanical Garden (BBG) and the Cibodas Botanical Garden (CBG), Java Island, Indonesia, managed by the National Research and Innovation Agency (BRIN) of Indonesia. BBG curates a diverse range of wet lowland plants, while CBG focuses on wet highland plants (Supplementary Table S1). BBG is located in Bogor City, West Java Province, Indonesia (6.36S, 106.48E; 215-260 m above sea level) and serves as the only remaining green and open space in the urban area. The landscape is mostly flat with slight elevation changes (Ariati & Widyatmoko, 2019). The average annual temperature is 26.9 °C, with the lowest monthly average of 25.9 °C in January and the highest of 27.5 °C in September. The annual rainfall amounts to approximately 4,307.7 mm (Sudiar et al., 2019). In contrast, CBG is located in Cianjur Regency, West Java Province, Indonesia (6.44S, 107.00E; 1,300-1,425 m above sea level). The garden is partly forested, with about 10% of its area adjacent to Mt. Gede Pangrango National Park, forming a transitional zone between natural forest and exotic plant habitats (Mutaqien & Zuhri, 2011). The landscape is diverse, ranging from flat and gently undulating areas to hilly and steep slopes. The average annual temperature is 19.3 °C, with the lowest monthly average of 18.4 °C in August and the highest of 20.1 °C in January. The annual rainfall averages 2,129 mm (Sudiar et al., 2019). Despite differences in elevation and vegetation, both BBG and CBG maintain year-round mean temperatures >18 °C, and are thus classified as tropical climates based on Trewartha climate classification (Trewartha & Horn, 1980).

Forty-two plant families that were previously reported to host P. capitalensis (Okane et al., 2003) were selected for sampling. The coordinates (latitude and longitude) and elevation of the collecting sites were recorded. Ten mature and intact leaves were collected from each plant and transported immediately to the laboratory at the Indonesian Culture Collection (InaCC), BRIN, West Java, Indonesia. All samples were processed within 24-48 h after collection. A total of 434 plant species belonging to 279 genera and 43 families were collected. Four pieces of intact leaves were selected from each plant species for isolation. The leaves were thoroughly washed under running tap water to remove dust particles, rinsed in 70% ethanol for 1 min, and sterilized with sodium hypochlorite (NaClO, 1% available chlorine) for 2 min. The leaves were rinsed twice for 1 min each in sterile distilled water. Subsequently, the leaves were placed between pieces of sterile paper to dry for 3-4 h. The leaves were cut into 5 × 5 mm samples, which were placed on half-strength malt extract agar (10 g malt extract, 2.5 g peptone, 15 g agar, and 1 L distilled water) in Petri dishes and incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 2 wk with daily monitoring. Single hyphal tips that formed at the edge of colonies growing from the plant samples were transferred to new Petri dishes with potato dextrose agar (PDA; 39 g potato dextrose agar and 1 L distilled water). The pure cultures obtained were photographed and preserved in 2 mL cryovials with 10% glycerol in combination with 5% trehalose and stored at −80 °C. The isolates were deposited at the InaCC, BRIN, Indonesia and Plant Parasitic Mycology Laboratory, University of Tsukuba, Tsukuba, Ibaraki, Japan. They were analyzed at the Plant Parasitic Mycology Laboratory, University of Tsukuba, Tsukuba, Ibaraki, Japan.

In addition to the newly collected isolates from Indonesia, we also examined 133 previously collected isolates from Japan, representing tropical, subtropical, and temperate regions (Supplementary Table S1). These isolates are working cultures of Izumi Okane housed at the Plant Parasitic Mycology Laboratory of the University of Tsukuba, collected between 1993 and 2015, and some of them are maintained at the Biological Resource Center (NBRC), National Institute of Technology and Evaluation, Kisarazu, Chiba, Japan. Of them, eighty-six isolates were collected from Tsukuba Botanical Garden (TBG), National Museum of Nature and Science, Tsukuba, Ibaraki, and from Takeda Garden for Medicinal Plant Conservation (KTG), Takeda Pharmaceutical Company Ltd., Kyoto, Japan. TBG (36.60N, 140.60E) simulates various Japanese ecosystems, including temperate and cold-temperate forests, grasslands, and moist habitats, and maintains a diverse collection of native plant species (https://tbg.kahaku.go.jp). KTG (35.20N, 135.48E) serves as a research facility focused on cultivating medicinal and useful plants for conservation and experimental purposes (https://www.takeda.co.jp/kyoto/). Both TBG and KTG experience eight months annually with mean temperatures >10 °C and four months <10 °C (https://www.data.jma.go.jp/), classifying them as temperate climates under the Trewartha climate classification (Trewartha & Horn, 1980).

The remaining 47 isolates were collected from natural vegetation on Amami Island (AIK), Kagoshima, Japan, and Iriomote Island (IRO) and Ishigaki Island (ISO), Okinawa, Japan. AIK (28.17N, 129.21E) is influenced by the Kuroshio Current and seasonal monsoons, with an average annual rainfall of 3,000 mm. The island is characterized by a mix of coastal and forested habitats (https://amami-whcc.jp/en/). AIK has mean monthly temperatures >18 °C for eight months and <18 °C for four months each year (https://www.data.jma.go.jp/), which corresponds to a subtropical climate according to the Trewartha climate classification (Trewartha & Horn, 1980). IRO and ISO (24.20N, 123.52E) are part of Iriomote-Ishigaki National Park, an ecologically rich region comprising dense evergreen broadleaf forests, Japan's largest mangrove forest, and extensive coral reef systems (https://iwcc.jp/english/iriomotejima/nationalpark/). Both IRO and ISO have mean temperatures >18 °C throughout the year (https://www.data.jma.go.jp/), and are classified as tropical climates according to the Trewartha climate classification (Trewartha & Horn, 1980).

2.2. DNA extraction, polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from fungal cultures using the rapid and simple microwave method (Izumitsu et al., 2012). The supernatant was used for PCR using ITS5 and ITS4 primers targeting the ITS1-5.8S-ITS2 region (White et al., 1990). The 15 μL reaction mixture contained 1 μL of DNA template (15 ng/μL), 2 mM each primer (1.5 μL), 7.5 μL of 2× Premix EmeraldAmp® PCR Master Mix (TaKaRa Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan), and 3.5 μL of nuclease-free water (NFW). PCR was performed using a TaKaRa PCR Thermal Cycler Dice® Touch (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan). The PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were purified using ExoSAP-ITTM (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) and sequenced by Eurofins Genomics (Tokyo, Japan). The sequences were edited and assembled using SeqMan software in DNASTAR version 5.0.6 (DNASTAR Inc., Madison, WI, USA). Finally, the sequences were aligned and matched with available sequences in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) program. The highest similarity (>98%) was chosen for assigning a species name according to the BLAST search results using the megablast option. All sequences were aligned using MAFFT version 7 (Katoh & Standley, 2013), and it was edited manually using MEGA version 11 (Tamura et al., 2021). To confirm the identity of the isolates compared to closely related species, a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using the online software W-IQ-Tree version 1.6.12 (Trifinopoulos et al., 2016; http://iqtree.cibiv.univie.ac.at/) with GTR+F+I+G4 option as a substitution model. Each node was evaluated with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. FigTree version 1.4.4 (https://github.com/rambaut/figtree) was used to visualize the phylogenetic tree. A pairwise distance matrix (Supplementary Table S2) was generated using Unipro UGENE version 47.0 (Okonechnikov et al., 2012) to estimate the monophyly threshold for all P. capitalensis isolates and their closely related species based on the phylogenetic tree (Supplementary Fig. S1). A threshold of 97% was determined to be appropriate for accurately delineating P. capitalensis populations and provided near-species-level resolution. The haplotype sequence data were deposited in NCBI GenBank under accession numbers PP589959-PP589996, PP892873, and PV082633-PV082640 (Supplementary Table S3).

2.3. Phylogenetic and population genetic analyses

The 377 aligned ITS sequences from Indonesia and Japan were subjected to population genetic analysis. The number of haplotypes, haplotype diversity (Hd), and nucleotide diversity (Pi) for each endophytic P. capitalensis population were determined using DnaSP version 6.12.03 (Rozas et al., 2017). Positions containing gaps were removed. Recent historical demographic events at neutral equilibrium in each population, and in the whole population, were estimated by conducting Tajima’s D neutrality test using Arlequin version 3.5. The significance of the Tajima’s D-values was assessed by running by 1,000 simulations under a model of selective neutrality (Excoffier & Lischer, 2010). To measure genetic variation within and among populations, analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) was conducted using GenAlEx version 6.5 (Peakall & Smouse, 2012). For the AMOVA, we calculated genetic distance among individuals based on haplotype and PhiPT (analogues to FST) and its standardized value, Phi’PT (analogues to F’ST; Weir & Cockerham, 1984), using GenAlEx. We also obtained pairwise PhiPT values; their significance was tested using 999 permutations. To evaluate the phylogenetic relationships among haplotypes, we constructed a median-joining network using a raw nexus file generated by DnaSP and PopART version 1.7 (Leigh & Bryant, 2015).

The Maturase K (matK) chloroplast gene was used as the universal DNA barcode for flowering plants (Lahaye et al., 2008) to evaluate the phylogenetic relationships among the host plants. All matK sequences of plant species representatives of the 40 host plant families of P. capitalensis were retrieved from NCBI GenBank (Supplementary Table S4). Alignment of matK sequences was conducted using MAFFT version 7, and they were trimmed using MEGA version 11. A neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was generated using MEGA version 11 with the Tamura three-parameter model (Tamura, 1992) and 1,000 bootstrap replications. A phylogenetic tree of the plant families was used to evaluate the relationships among the number of P. capitalensis isolates (Supplementary Table S1), number of host plant species (Supplementary Table S1), number of non-host plant species for P. capitalensis (Supplementary Table S5), Hd, and Pi (Supplementary Table S6), which were visualized by generating line and pie charts (Fig. 1).

3. Results

3.1. Sample collection and isolation

A total of 288 Phyllosticta isolates were obtained from leaf samples collected in BBG and CBG, comprising 244 P. capitalensis, 10 P. citribraziliensis, and 34 other Phyllosticta species (Supplementary Tables S1, S5). Based on the phylogenetic analysis of the ITS1-5.8S-ITS2 region, we identified a total of 377 endophytic P. capitalensis isolates. Of these, 244 were from Indonesia: 141 from samples collected from wet lowland plants at BBG, and 103 from wet highland plants at CBG. The remaining 133 isolates included in the phylogenetic analysis were collected from Japan (Supplementary Fig. S1; Supplementary Table S1).

Among the 434 plant species collected from the two botanical gardens, P. capitalensis was isolated from the leaves of 217 plant species belonging to 182 genera in 35 families (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S1). Phyllosticta capitalensis was isolated from angiosperms (34 families), including eudicots (rosids and asterids), magnoliids, and monocots; it was isolated less frequently from gymnosperms (conifers; 1 family; Fig. 1). Phyllosticta capitalensis was not detected in one angiosperm family (Myrtaceae), three gymnosperm families (Pinaceae, Cupressaceae, and Taxaceae), and one fern family (Equisetaceae). Fabaceae was the plant family with the highest number of P. capitalensis isolates (31 isolates), followed by Rubiaceae (21 isolates), Euphorbiaceae (21 isolates), Lauraceae (16 isolates), and Malvaceae (15 isolates); the other plant families all yielded fewer than 15 isolates (Fig. 1).

To compare host range across regions, we examined the isolates from Japan, which were obtained from 84 plant species belonging to 69 genera in 41 families (Supplementary Table S1). As in Indonesia, P. capitalensis was mainly isolated from angiosperms (39 families) and less frequently from gymnosperms (2 families; Supplementary Table S1). Ericaceae yielded the highest number of P. capitalensis isolates (10 isolates), followed by Lauraceae and Fagaceae (8 isolates); with all other families contributing fewer than 5 isolates (Supplementary Table S1).

3.2. Phylogenetic and population genetics of endophytic Phyllosticta capitalensis

From 377 sequences, 30 polymorphic sites, 7 singleton-variable sites, and 23 parsimony-informative sites were found (Supplementary Data S1; Supplementary Table S3). A total of 27 haplotypes were detected with Hd of 0.55 and Pi of 0.00261 (Table 1). The BBG population had the highest Pi (0.00424). The KTG and CBG populations had higher nucleotide diversities (Pi = 0.00168 and 0.00166, respectively) than the other populations (Table 1). The haplotype diversities of the BBG and CBG populations (Hd = 0.59 and 0.52) were lower than those of the KTG and ISO populations (Hd = 0.68 and 0.62, respectively; Table 1). Tajima’s D neutrality test value for P. capitalensis populations was −0.52640. The Tajima’s D-value in the CBG population was negative and significant (P < 0.05; Tajima’s D-value = −1.98323; Supplementary Table S7).

We found nine host-specific haplotypes (Haps 9, 10, 17, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, and 27) in single-host plant families in Indonesia (Figs. 1, 3A, B). Smilacaceae had the highest Pi (0.01255). Rutaceae, Fagaceae, Lythraceae, and Podocarpaceae also showed higher nucleotide diversities (Pi = 0.00658, 0.00646, 0.00646, and 0.00646, respectively) than other plant families (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S6). Only 15% of the variation was among populations; 85% was among individuals in a single population (Table 2). In terms of pairwise PhiPT values, genetic differentiation was greater between Indonesian populations and KTG. Conversely, the genetic differentiation between the Indonesian populations and AIK was lower than between the other populations (Supplementary Table S9).

4. Discussion

4.1. Phyllosticta capitalensis species complex host-range breadth and diversity in Indonesia

The endophytic P. capitalensis species complex was frequently isolated from angiosperm species in two botanical gardens in Bogor and Cibodas, Indonesia. However, it was only isolated from Podocarpaceae among several gymnosperm families (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S1). It is suggested that the P. capitalensis species complex prefers angiosperms to gymnosperms as hosts, as reported by Okane et al. (2003). Further work is needed to clarify the existence of an inherent preference of endophytic P. capitalensis for angiosperms over gymnosperms as hosts.

The presence of four dominant haplotypes exclusive to BBG (Haps 18, 19, 20, and 26) and only one exclusive to CBG (Hap 14) suggests that local environmental conditions strongly influence the diversity and distribution of endophytic P. capitalensis species complex (Fig. 3A, B; Supplementary Table S8). Microclimatic and ecological differences between BBG and CBG appear to strongly influence the diversity and distribution of endophytic P. capitalensis species complex. Environmental factors such as elevation, temperature, and precipitation strongly influence fungal endophyte colonization and community structure in woody plants, with greater diversity typically observed at lower elevations and in warm, humid climates (Huang et al., 2020). BBG, located in a lowland tropical area, experiences consistently warm temperatures, high rainfall, and humidity, providing favorable conditions for endophyte colonization and diversity. In contrast, CBG experiences cooler temperatures, lower rainfall, and more variable humidity, which may reduce opportunities for colonization and partly explain the lower haplotype diversity observed compared to BBG. In addition to microclimate, surrounding vegetation may also influence haplotype diversity. CBG is located next to the natural forest of Mt. Gede Pangrango National Park, which likely brings in many fungal spores from the surrounding forest, possibly adding to haplotype diversity. In contrast, although BBG is surrounded by urban areas with limited natural vegetation. The presence of four dominant haplotypes suggests that its well-maintained and stable garden environment may help certain haplotypes persist over time. While differences in endophyte presence under controlled conditions may be statistically significant, they are often biologically negligible in natural settings, where trees are constantly exposed to endophytes and colonization is typically abundant and diverse, especially in older leaves (Saikkonen, 2007). While both BBG and CBG maintain greenhouse facilities, our study did not involve samples from these environments, as all samples were collected from non-greenhouse areas of the botanical gardens. In addition to environmental and ecological factors, anthropogenic influences may also play a role in shaping haplotype patterns. Instead, other factors may account for the occurrence of five shared haplotypes (Haps 11, 12, 13, 15, and 16) between BBG and CBG (Fig. 3A, B; Supplementary Table S8). One plausible explanation is human-mediated plant material exchange. As both gardens operate under centralized national management, the transfer of seeds, cuttings, or saplings for conservation and research purposes is common. Such practices can unintentionally introduce endophytic fungi like endophytic P. capitalensis species complex, which colonize host tissues asymptomatically, facilitating the co-dispersal and establishment of shared haplotypes across both sites.

The haplotype phylogeny revealed a star-like network centered on Hap 2, which was the most widely distributed haplotype across both Indonesia and Japan, suggesting a rapid radiation of the P. capitalensis species complex. However, several haplotypes were region-specific, with Haps 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 18, 19, 20, and 26 found only in Indonesia, and Haps 3, 4, 5, 7, and 8 exclusive to Japanese populations (Fig. 3A, B; Supplementary Table S8). In Indonesia, the combination of high plant diversity and stable tropical conditions likely facilitates the diversification of endophytic P. capitalensis species complex, promoting the emergence of multiple haplotypes with varying degrees of host association. According to Cannon & Simmons (2002), the frequent isolation of identical endophytic strains from unrelated plant species indicates that tropical leaf endophytes show low host preference. In contrast to the more specialized interactions seen in temperate regions, the high plant diversity and structural complexity of tropical forests likely promote broader and less defined host associations. The widespread occurrence of Hap 2 across 30 plant families in Indonesia indicates a wide host range, characteristic of a generalist lineage (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S1). In contrast, the detection of nine haplotypes (Haps 9, 10, 17, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, and 27; Figs. 1, 3A, B), each associated with a single plant family, suggests a pattern of host preference, where certain haplotypes tend to be more frequently associated with particular host plant families. These patterns reflect variation in host association, where some haplotypes exhibit stronger preferences for specific hosts, even though they are not strictly limited to them. In Japan, where plant diversity is lower than Indonesia and climatic conditions are more seasonal, such host preferences may be more pronounced, potentially contributing to the development of distinct haplotypes associated with specific host plants. Consequently, regional variation in environmental conditions and host availability may shape the formation of unique haplotype assemblages. However, due to differences in sample size between regions, comparisons of haplotype diversity should be interpreted with careful consideration, and expanded sampling in Japan is needed to more accurately assess host association patterns and uncover potentially overlooked lineages.

Endophytic fungi can behave as latent pathogens, remaining symptomless in their host plant for some time. When introduced to a new environment, latent pathogens likely infect new hosts without resistance, potentially causing severe damage (Burgess & Wingfield, 2017; Slippers & Wingfield, 2007). Motohashi et al. (2009) reported that P. capitalensis clade based on the rDNA ITS and LSU regions was composed of endophytic species and other plant pathogenic Phyllosticta species. Moreover, some Japanese species showed pathogenicity in a specific host (Motohashi et al., 2009). In addition, P. capitalensis causes leaf spot disease on Japanese privet (Ligustrum japonicum) in Iran (Sabahi et al., 2022) and rubber trees (Hevea brasiliensis) in Colombia (Guevara et al., 2022), and brown spot disease on guava fruit (Psidium guajava) in Mexico (Cruz-Lagunas et al., 2023). Phyllosticta capitalensis from Indonesia and Japan clustered with seven pathogenic Phyllosticta species (P. camelliae, P. conjac, P. fallopiae, P. harai, P. mangifera-indicae, P. miurae, and P. rhizophorae) in the ITS phylogenetic tree (Supplementary Fig. S1). There is no report that P. capitalensis had become pathogenic to the host plants examined in this study.

Endophytic P. capitalensis isolated from Citrus spp. (Guarnaccia et al., 2017; Tran et al., 2019) often co-occur with its endophytic sister species, P. paracapitalensis. The occurrence of these two endophytic Phyllosticta species has potential implications for citrus plant management, particularly in the presence of the citrus pathogen P. citricarpa (Nicoletti, 2019). Although P. capitalensis has been reported as an endophyte in Citrus spp., this study focused exclusively on its occurrence in non-Citrus hosts (Supplementary Table S1).

4.2. Genetic diversity and structure of endophytic Phyllosticta capitalensis species complex

Canopy cover and leaf age have been identified as factors influencing endophyte colonization in tropical forest trees; however, colonization appears to be more strongly determined by the duration of leaf exposure to airborne propagules than by absolute leaf age (Arnold & Herre, 2003). In endophytic P. capitalensis species complex populations from BBG and CBG, such ecological differences may contribute to the observed genetic variation. BBG showed the highest nucleotide diversity, while KTG and CBG also exhibited relatively higher values (Table 1), suggesting that local environmental conditions shape fungal genetic diversity. One contributing factor may be plant age, which is positively associated with endophyte infection frequency due to cumulative colonization over time (Arnold & Herre, 2003). BBG, founded in 1817, is the oldest botanical garden in Southeast Asia and hosts a dense and diverse plant collection. It covers an area of 87 hectares and, as of 2017, maintained 12,370 living specimens, excluding unidentified and glasshouse collections (Ariati et al., 2019). Given its longer history, BBG likely includes many older and more established plant specimens compared to CBG, which was founded later in 1852 and covers an area of 85 hectares. CBG houses 8,054 living specimens of Pteridophyta and Spermatophyta, excluding Bryophyta, which remain uncatalogued (Sujarwo et al., 2019). Higher plant density in BBG may also result in greater canopy cover, which enhances spore dispersal opportunities and helps maintain humid microclimates, further facilitating endophytic colonization and diversity. Although the duration of leaf exposure could not be directly assessed, its effects may be indirectly reflected in the combined influence of plant age, canopy cover, and microclimate. In addition to ecological factors, particular host plant families may significantly affect the genetic diversity of P. capitalensis species complex in Indonesia. Smilacaceae, for example, exhibited the highest nucleotide diversity in this study (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S6), suggesting its potential role as a reservoir of genetic variation for P. capitalensis species complex in Indonesia.

Haplotype diversity of P. capitalensis species complex varied by region, with lower values in BBG and CBG compared to the ISO population in Japan (Table 1). This difference may reflect variation in forest structure and management. BBG and CBG are managed gardens, where regular human intervention can reduce ecological complexity and microhabitat diversity. In contrast, ISO is located in a natural old forest with little disturbance and high structural complexity, conditions that tend to support more diverse endophytic communities. For example, Helander et al. (2006) found that natural old forests harbor the highest fungal diversity, while managed mature forests support the lowest. These findings align with the higher haplotype diversity observed in ISO (Table 1). Similarly, the KTG population also had higher genetic diversity (Table 1), suggesting that specific host plants in this region enhance genetic variation. However, local abiotic factors and land use also influence fungal abundance and diversity. For example, wet microclimates in temperate zones may support unexpectedly high fungal diversity, whereas hosts with long-lived foliage, such as evergreens, can sustain diverse endophyte communities regardless of latitude (Arnold & Lutzoni, 2007). Whether these interactions are stable over time and across environments requires further investigation.

AMOVA revealed low overall genetic differentiation among P. capitalensis species complex populations from Indonesia and Japan (Table 2), indicating weak population structure at the ITS level. Pairwise PhiPT values showed strong differentiation between the Indonesian populations and KTG. In contrast, genetic differentiation between the Indonesian populations and AIK was very low compared to IRO and ISO, even though IRO and ISO are geographically closer to Indonesia (Supplementary Table S9). This pattern may reflect overlapping host plant diversity or sampling bias due to the limited number of isolates from AIK (Table 1). These findings indicate the need for higher-resolution markers to capture fine-scale structure and potential local adaptation that ITS region cannot resolve.

In conclusion, ITS phylogeny revealed that endophytic P. capitalensis species complex exhibits a wide host range in Indonesia, being associated with diverse plant families across Bogor and Cibodas botanical gardens. Genetic diversity within Indonesian populations was relatively high, particularly at BBG, which may be shaped by local ecological conditions such as plant age, canopy cover, and microclimate. The haplotype network analysis showed population differentiation across geographic and climatic regions, with region-specific haplotypes in both Indonesia and Japan. However, differences in sample sizes and the limited resolution of the ITS region suggest caution in drawing definitive conclusions about phylogeographic distribution between Indonesia and Japan. To address this limitation, further studies using genome-wide SNP analysis are needed to clarify local adaptation, identify potential adaptive genes and haplotypes, and examine their relationships with host plants and local environmental conditions.
