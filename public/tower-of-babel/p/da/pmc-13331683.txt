GSH-responsive polyurethane/PBAT blend films: degradation kinetics, ecotoxicity screening, and governance–carbon coupling via three-tier mapping
Authors: Jie Zeng, Mingcong Zhang, Boyuan An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a transferable three-tier mapping framework translating laboratory degradation kinetics and ecotoxicity data of stimulus-responsive biodegradable materials into evolutionary game parameters and life cycle assessment (LCA) scenarios. Using glutathione (GSH)-responsive polyurethane (PUs)/poly(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) (PBAT) blend films (20 : 80 w/w) as proof-of-concept, the films achieved 27.3 ± 1.8 MPa tensile strength with ∼90% disulfide embedding efficiency. An iso-crystallinity control (HDO-extended, disulfide-free, matched ∼20% crystallinity) confirmed >96% of acceleration derives from disulfide cleavage (99.2% vs. 19.5% mass loss at 498 h under 20 mM GSH, 313 K); 0.5 mM was the minimum effective GSH threshold (38.2% mass loss; 2.8-fold k-based acceleration). Arrhenius analysis yielded Eα,GSH = 45.2 ± 2.1 kJ mol−1, 17.0 kJ mol−1 below the hydrolysis pathway (P = < 0.01; ΔEα 95% CI: 7.3–26.7 kJ mol−1). Ecotoxicity screening confirmed no significant adverse effects; LC-MS identified sulfur-containing products below ecotoxicological thresholds. A three-dimensional scoring scheme maps material data into game parameters; structural decomposition revealed public participation sensitivity of 45% at R/Ceco = 1.2, with Monte Carlo validation (∼72% equilibrium survival). Functional coupling from game equilibria to disposal fractions (five-benchmark calibration; leave-one-out cross-validation: 2.8 pp) yielded carbon footprints of ∼550–1100 kg CO2-eq per ton, with governance upgrading saving ∼540 kg CO2-eq per ton—56% of the Scenario A reduction relative to PE.

Introduction

Plastic pollution has emerged as one of the most critical challenges confronting global sustainability.1,2 With annual production exceeding 400 million tons, microplastics (MPs) have been detected across soils, freshwater, marine sediments, and the atmosphere,3,4 while their presence in human blood and placental tissue extends the issue to public health.5 China's upgraded “plastic ban” since 2020 has promoted biodegradable alternatives, yet governance practice has exposed two interrelated bottlenecks.6,7

Material bottleneck

PBAT typically requires industrial composting (≥58 °C) for effective degradation;8 under ambient conditions the process remains exceedingly slow. Intermediate stages may generate MP fragments posing ecological risks,9,10 and higher production-stage carbon emissions relative to PE mean material substitution alone proves insufficient.11

Structurally, PBAT is a random copolymer consisting of alternating aliphatic butylene adipate (BA) and aromatic butylene terephthalate (BT) segments. Its biodegradation proceeds primarily through enzymatic hydrolysis of ester bonds, catalyzed by microbial lipases and cutinases that preferentially attack the more flexible BA segments. Kijchavengkul et al.12 demonstrated that PBAT achieves >90% mineralization within 180 days under industrial composting conditions (58 °C, 50% moisture), but degradation rates decrease dramatically at ambient temperatures—soil burial studies report only 1–5% mass loss after 6 months at 25 °C. Crystallinity plays a regulatory role: higher crystallinity reduces chain accessibility to enzymatic attack, slowing degradation kinetics. These limitations underscore the need for alternative degradation strategies capable of accelerating PBAT breakdown under milder environmental conditions.

Institutional bottleneck

Current standards (EN 13432, EN 17033, GB/T 20197, GB/T 35795) evaluate degradation only under industrial composting or passive burial,13 with no provisions for stimulus-responsive materials. A persistent disconnect between laboratory research and policy formulation impedes translation of advances into governance efficacy, while existing eco-design frameworks lack quantitative linkages to degradation data.14–16

Stimulus-responsive degradation offers a pathway beyond the material bottleneck.17 By incorporating cleavable bonds into polymer backbones, triggered accelerated degradation becomes achievable under specific stimuli. Glutathione (GSH)-mediated thiol–disulfide exchange, validated in biomedical delivery, may lower the activation energy barrier while preserving mechanical performance.18,19

Disulfide-containing polyurethanes represent a class of stimulus-responsive materials in which –S–S– bonds are incorporated into the polymer backbone as cleavable triggers. GSH (γ-Glu-Cys-Gly), a tripeptide ubiquitous in living cells, mediates degradation through a two-step thiol–disulfide exchange mechanism: the reduced cysteine thiol (–SH) of GSH performs a nucleophilic attack on the –S–S– bond in the PU backbone, generating a mixed disulfide intermediate (PU-S-SG) and a thiolate fragment (PU-SH); a second GSH molecule then attacks the mixed disulfide to release oxidized glutathione (GSSG) and a second PU-SH fragment, cleaving the polymer main chain. Xu et al.20 first demonstrated that disulfide-containing biodegradable elastomeric polyurethanes (PU-SS) synthesized with hydroxyethyl disulfide as chain extender undergo rapid degradation in the presence of 10 mM GSH, with degradation rates tunable by disulfide bond content in the backbone. This progressive chain scission, when the PU phase is blended with PBAT, disintegrates the blend morphology and dramatically increases the surface area of PBAT fragments available for hydrolytic and enzymatic attack—the PU phase functions as a “molecular zipper” that converts stimulus-responsive PU degradation into an accelerated cascade throughout the entire blend system.

However, whether material improvements translate into environmental benefits depends on collaborative governance efficiency.21 Evolutionary game theory (EGT), a mathematical framework derived from biological evolution, models strategic interactions among bounded-rational agents through replicator dynamics, where strategy frequencies evolve based on relative fitness (payoff differentials). Unlike classical game theory, EGT does not assume perfect rationality—agents adjust strategies through imitation and learning, making it particularly suitable for modeling the gradual adoption of biodegradable materials in multi-stakeholder governance systems. Life cycle assessment (LCA), standardized by ISO 14040/14044, quantifies environmental impacts across a product's entire lifecycle (cradle-to-grave); for biodegradable plastics, the end-of-life stage is critically sensitive to disposal pathways (composting vs. landfill vs. incineration), which depend on governance infrastructure—a dependency that existing LCA studies typically treat exogenously through fixed scenario assumptions. A critical gap persists: degradation kinetics determine what disposal pathways are technically feasible, ecotoxicity data determine what pathways are environmentally acceptable, and game equilibria determine what pathways are socially achievable; without connecting these three tiers, material innovations and governance policies remain disconnected.22

On this basis, this study proposes a three-tier mapping framework that links experimental degradation kinetics and ecotoxicity data to game parameters and LCA scenarios, thereby connecting material performance with governance outcomes. The methodological contribution is threefold:1 iso-crystallinity controls are used to distinguish disulfide-mediated acceleration from crystallinity effects in stimulus–responsive blends;2 a three-party evolutionary game model is parameterized using experimental kinetic and ecotoxicity benchmarks rather than fully assumed values; and3 game equilibria are coupled to calibrated disposal-fraction equations (LOO-CV = 2.8 pp), allowing end-of-life scenarios to be generated endogenously. In this study, disulfide-containing polyurethane (PUs)/PBAT blend films are used as a proof-of-concept system. Within this framework, Tier I comprises the experimental identification of disulfide-mediated degradation behavior, including iso-crystallinity controls and concentration-gradient experiments, which provide the dual-pathway Ea basis for subsequent mapping (Sections 3.1–3.5). Tier II consists of a three-party evolutionary game analysis with structural decomposition and Monte Carlo validation (Section 4.1). Tier III links equilibrium outcomes to disposal fractions for governance-coupled carbon-footprint assessment (Section 4.2), which further supports the policy discussion (Section 4.3). Because the three tiers are connected sequentially from material data to governance behavior and environmental outcomes, the framework can, in principle, be extended to other stimulus-responsive materials when comparable degradation and ecotoxicity datasets are available.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of GSH-responsive polyurethane/PBAT blend films

Disulfide-containing polyurethanes (PUs) were synthesized via a two-step procedure.23 In Step 1 (prepolymer synthesis), polycarbonate diol (PCDL, Mn = 2000 g mol−1, petrochemical-sourced) was reacted with isophorone diisocyanate (IPDI) at an –OH/–NCO molar ratio of 1 : 2.0, catalyzed by dibutyltin dilaurate (DBTDL, 0.05 wt% relative to total monomer mass), under N2 atmosphere at 80 °C for 2.5 h. Reaction completion was confirmed by real-time FTIR monitoring of the disappearance of the 2270 cm−1 –NCO peak. In Step 2 (chain extension), 2-hydroxyethyl disulfide (HEDS) was added at 0 °C with a stoichiometric ratio of OH(HEDS) : NCO(prepolymer) = 1.00 : 1.05 and stirred for 1.5 h. The 5% –NCO excess ensured complete HEDS consumption; residual –NCO content was 6.8% (theoretical 7.1%, conversion >95%). PUs were melt-blended with PBAT (BASF Ecoflex® C1200) at 20 : 80 w/w using a co-rotating twin-screw extruder (∅ 25 mm, L/D = 40, barrel temperature profile 160–180 °C, screw speed 150 rpm) and cast into films with thickness of approximately 25 µm (Fig. 1). An iso-crystallinity control replaced HEDS with 1,6-hexanediol (HDO) at equimolar OH content under identical synthesis and blending conditions, yielding disulfide-free blends with matched crystallinity (∼20%, XRD-confirmed). For blend-ratio screening, 10 : 90, 20 : 80, and 30 : 70 (w/w) PUs/PBAT films were prepared under the same extrusion and casting conditions and evaluated by tensile testing, XRD crystallinity, and 498 h mass loss under 20 mM GSH at 313 K; the comparative results are reported in Section 3.1.

Synthesis and fabrication of GSH-responsive PUs/PBAT blend films: prepolymer synthesis (Step 1), HEDS chain extension (Step 2), twin-screw melt blending (Step 3), and film casting (Step 4).

Material characterization

FTIR spectra were recorded on a Nicolet iS50 (4 cm−1, 32 scans). XPS (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, Al Kα) confirmed disulfide bonds via high-resolution S 2p analysis collected with 0.05 eV step size and 50 accumulations. Spectra were charge-corrected to C 1 s = 284.8 eV and deconvoluted with Gaussian doublets constrained to a 2 : 1 S 2p3/2 : S 2p1/2 area ratio, equal FWHM, and 1.2 eV spin–orbit splitting. XRD (Cu Kα, 2θ = 5–40°) determined crystallinity. SEM was performed on gold-sputtered cryo-fractured cross-sections. Tensile properties were measured on an Instron 5967 universal testing machine (50 mm min−1, GB/T 1040.3–2006 Type II specimen for films <1 mm thickness, gauge length 50 mm, n ≥ 5). Mw and PDI were determined by GPC (THF, PS calibration); PS calibration overestimates Mw for aliphatic polyesters by ∼20–50%.

Degradation kinetics and Arrhenius analysis

Film specimens were immersed in simulated soil extract (10 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.0, ∼30 mM ionic strength) with GSH (0.1–20 mM, eight levels) alongside GSH-free, neat PBAT, and iso-crystallinity controls at four temperatures (283 K, 293 K, 303 K, and 313 K, controlled within ±0.5 K in temperature-regulated incubators, three replicates; Table 1). Mass loss measurements were taken every 24 h for the first 120 h (induction phase), then every 48 h from 120–336 h (rapid degradation phase), and every 72 h from 336–498 h (deceleration phase). At each sampling point, triplicate specimens were removed, rinsed with deionized water, freeze-dried to constant weight, and weighed on an analytical balance (±0.1 mg). Concentration-gradient experiments (CG run) were conducted at 313 K with sampling at 0, 48, 120, 240, 336, and 498 h across eight GSH concentrations (0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 15, and 20 mM). GPC sampling was performed at 0, 120, 240, 336, and 498 h for Mw tracking. The Phase II window was defined by zero-crossings of the second derivative of dm/dt; apparent first-order rate constants k were extracted within this window (Section S2).24 The Arrhenius equation:1was fitted with slope standard errors and 95% CIs reported. pH effects were examined at 5.0, 7.0, and 9.0.

Ecotoxicity assessment

Seed germination assays25 (OECD 208) used wheat (Triticum aestivum) with 1% and 5% PE MP groups, 498 h endpoint and 240 h midpoint degradation product leachates, and blank controls (5 × 20 seeds, 7 days). Earthworm (Eisenia fetida)26 avoidance assays (ISO 17512-1) used 5% concentration over 72 h (4 × 10 adults). PE MPs (D50 = 75 ± 12 µm) were prepared by cryogenic grinding and sieving (150 µm). Sulfur-containing degradation products in the 498 h leachate were identified by LC-MS (Thermo Q Exactive Plus, ESI−, m/z 50–500) and confirmed by GC-MS (Agilent 7890B/5977B, DB-5ms column); quantification used external calibration against 2-mercaptoethanol and thiodiglycol standards (LOD = 0.1 mg L−1). Statistics: one-way ANOVA with Tukey HSD (α = 0.05); effect sizes as Cohen's d.

Soil microbial community analysis

Brown soil (pH 6.8, organic matter 2.3%, 60% WHC, 25 °C) was incubated with film specimens at Days 0, 15, 30, and 60 (n = 3). 16S rRNA V3–V4 amplicon sequencing used primers 341F/805R with PCR cycling of 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 28 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Libraries were sequenced on NovaSeq 6000 (2 × 250 bp) and processed with DADA2 within QIIME2 v2023.5 using truncation lengths of 230 bp (forward) and 200 bp (reverse), Silva 138.1 taxonomy assignment, ASV filtering below 0.005%, and PICRUSt2 v2.5.2 functional prediction with weighted NSTI < 0.15. At least 30 000 reads per sample were retained (Fig. S2). Alpha diversity (Shannon, Chao1), beta diversity (PCA, PERMANOVA), and PICRUSt2 functional prediction were assessed. No exogenous GSH was added. Detailed methods are in Section S1.

Evolutionary game model

A government (y)–enterprise (x)–public (z) three-party evolutionary game model was constructed.27 Parameters include technology premium R, transition cost Ceco, subsidy S, penalty P, remediation cost Cenv, public influence coefficient α, and synergy factor G. Results

Before the characterization results are presented, the rationale underlying the prepa-ration procedure (Section 2.1) is briefly discussed. The two-step prepolymer/chain-extension route was adopted so that the disulfide linkages introduced by HEDS are confined to the hard-segment junctions of the polyurethane backbone, where they remain sterically accessible to glutathione yet protected from premature hydrolysis during melt processing. The 1 : 2.0 –OH/–NCO ratio in the prepolymer step yields an isocya-nate-terminated prepolymer of controlled molecular weight, while the 5% –NCO excess in the chain-extension step drives HEDS to near-complete conversion (>95%, residual –NCO 6.8% vs. 7.1% theoretical) and minimises unreacted monomer. Solvent-free twin-screw melt-blending with PBAT at 20 : 80 (w/w) was chosen over solution casting to keep the process scalable, and the 160–180 °C barrel profile was deliberately kept below the disulfide thermolysis threshold to preserve –S–S– integrity, as later confirmed by the small (<5%) SOx fraction in the XPS S 2p spectrum (Section 3.1). These preparation choices establish the structural basis for the characterization, degradation, and mechanical results reported below.

SEM micrographs (Fig. 3a) of gold-sputtered surfaces (coating thickness ∼5–10 nm, far below typical phase-separation domain sizes of >100 nm) revealed homogeneous morphology without visible sea-island structures or discrete phase domains, consistent with good blend compatibility at the observation scale. This is corroborated by the monomodal GPC profile and the absence of additional crystalline diffraction peaks in XRD. However, SEM provides only surface morphological evidence; definitive miscibility assessment would require thermal analysis (e.g., DSC for Tg determination). XRD analysis (Fig. 3b) showed that crystallinity decreased from 28.5% (neat PBAT) to 14.5% at 30% PUs content—a 49% reduction (Fig. 3d). For reference, the pure PUs film exhibited only a broad amorphous halo centred near 2θ ≈ 20° with no detectable crystalline reflections (Fig. 3b), confirming that the disulfide-containing polyurethane is essentially amorphous and that all crystalline diffraction in the blends originates from the PBAT phase. The expanded amorphous regions are expected to facilitate GSH diffusion toward bulk-phase disulfide sites. Tensile testing of the PUs/PBAT 20 : 80 blend film (Fig. 3c) yielded a tensile strength of 27.3 ± 1.8 MPa and elongation at break of 538 ± 42% (n = 5, GB/T 1040.3 Type II specimens), satisfying the requirements of GB/T 35795-2017 for biodegradable mulch films.33 Blend ratio screening of the 10 : 90, 20 : 80, and 30 : 70 formulations showed that 10 : 90 films achieved only 67% mass loss at 498 h due to insufficient disulfide density, whereas 30 : 70 films exhibited reduced tensile strength (21.2 ± 2.3 MPa) and excessive brittleness (crystallinity ∼11%). The 20 : 80 ratio represents the optimal balance between mechanical performance and degradation acceleration, with crystallinity of approximately 20% providing adequate GSH mass-transfer channels while maintaining film toughness.

GSH-responsive degradation behavior

Under 313 K and 20 mM GSH, the blend film exhibited sigmoidal mass loss (Fig. 4a). At the 498 h endpoint, cumulative mass loss reached 99.2% for the 20 mM GSH group, while the GSH-free control showed only 13.8% and the neat PBAT + 20 mM GSH control reached 16.2% (Fig. 4a inset). The marginal increase of approximately 2.4 percentage points in the neat PBAT control indicates that the direct catalytic effect of GSH on PBAT ester bonds is limited. Because neat PBAT crystallinity (28.5%) exceeds that of the blend system (∼20%), its higher mass-transfer resistance makes 16.2% a conservative upper bound. GPC confirmed that apparent Mw plummeted from 85 kDa to 1.2 kDa in the PUs/PBAT + 20 mM GSH group, while the neat PBAT + 20 mM GSH control retained Mw = 72.1 kDa and the GSH-free PUs/PBAT blend control retained Mw = 78.5 kDa at 498 h, establishing disulfide bond cleavage as the dominant acceleration mechanism. Based on literature data for PBAT/PBSA systems, the 8.5 percentage-point crystallinity difference alone would contribute only an estimated 1.3–1.7-fold acceleration—far insufficient to explain the 99.2% vs. 16.2% disparity. To directly isolate the crystallinity confound, an iso-crystallinity control was performed: HDO-extended PUs/PBAT blends with matched ∼20% crystallinity but no disulfide bonds achieved only 19.5% mass loss (Mw = 48.3 kDa) under identical 20 mM GSH conditions, confirming that disulfide bond cleavage accounts for >96% of the total acceleration—calculated as (99.2% − 19.5%)/(99.2% − 16.2%) = 79.7/83.0 = 96.0%, where the denominator excludes the direct catalytic effect of GSH on neat PBAT ester bonds.34 This eliminates the crystallinity confound and validates the Ea-based parameter mapping in the governance framework (Section 4.1).

(a) GSH concentration-dependent mass loss profiles at 313 K; (b) pH-dependent mass loss curves; (c) Mw and PDI evolution over time; (d) degradation phase delineation via dm/dt inflection analysis.

Concentration-gradient experiments at 313 K revealed a clear dose–response relationship across 0.1–20 mM GSH. The minimum effective threshold was identified at 0.5 mM GSH, which produced 38.2% mass loss (2.8-fold k-based acceleration over the GSH-free control). At 1 mM, mass loss reached 52.1% (3.8-fold); at 5 mM, 82.7% (5.3-fold). Here, fold values refer to Phase II rate-constant ratios (kGSH/kcontrol), not mass-loss ratios. A log–log plot of k versus [GSH] yielded a first-order fit (R2 = 0.994), consistent with pseudo-first-order thiol–disulfide exchange kinetics. Crucially, 0.5 mM is comparable to intracellular GSH concentrations (1–10 mM) reported in plant root tissues; however, sustained extracellular GSH in bulk soil is orders of magnitude lower (∼10 µM), yielding an extrapolated half-life of ∼59 years (Section S2) that rules out chemical cleavage alone at field-relevant timescales. The material therefore serves as a methodological proof-of-concept for the three-tier mapping framework rather than a field-deployable product in its current form; the governance analysis would attain practical significance only when applied to materials with demonstrated field-scale degradation or when multi-mechanism synergy is empirically confirmed (Section 4.4).

Inflection-point analysis of dm/dt (Fig. 4d) delineated three degradation phases: induction, rapid degradation, and deceleration. Phase II was defined as 120–336 h; its 240 h midpoint—when oligomeric fragment release rates are expected to peak—was selected as the sampling point for ecotoxicity screening (Section 3.4). Weibull fitting yielded τ = 285 ± 12 h and β = 2.3 ± 0.2 (R2 = 0.998), where β > 1 quantitatively captures the post-induction acceleration. The Weibull model was not adopted as the primary descriptor becauseτ lacks Arrhenius compatibility; the Phase II first-order approximation (R2 ≥ 0.993) yields explicit k values required for Ea extraction. pH 7.0 was selected to simulate neutral agricultural soils. pH gradient experiments (Fig. 4b) showed fastest degradation at pH 5.0, as acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of exposed PBAT ester bonds (∼80 wt%) dominates following disulfide cleavage. GPC tracking (Fig. 4c) confirmed apparent Mw decreasing from 85 kDa to 1.2 kDa with bulk-erosion-consistent PDI rise-then-fall—over an order of magnitude faster than neat PBAT (∼63 kDa at 180 days).12

The implications of accelerated laboratory conditions for field-scale applicability, including extrapolation to environmental GSH concentrations and the proposed multi-mechanism synergy with microbial degradation, are discussed in Section 4.4.

Arrhenius kinetics and dual-pathway activation energy

Phase II apparent first-order rate constants increased monotonically across the 283–313 K range (Table 2): from 1.39 × 10−3 to 8.74 × 10−3 h−1 for the GSH pathway and from 1.21 × 10−4 to 1.53 × 10−3 h−1 for the hydrolysis-only pathway. Relative standard deviations across triplicate independent runs ranged from 5.0% to 9.8%, confirming good experimental reproducibility.

Phase II apparent first-order rate constants for both degradation pathways

Phase II window sensitivity analysis showed that ±10% window shifts produced k variations of <±9% and Ea changes of <2 kJ mol−1 across all temperatures, confirming that the first-order approximation is robust to window selection. Arrhenius plots (Fig. 5) exhibited excellent linearity (RGSH2 = 0.9993, Rhyd2 = 0.9987). The derived activation energies were Ea,GSH = 45.2 ± 2.1 kJ mol−1 (95% CI: 41.0–49.4) and Ea,hyd = 62.2 ± 2.8 kJ mol−1 (95% CI: 56.6–67.8), yielding ΔEa = 17.0 kJ mol−1 (difference SE = 3.5 kJ mol; 95% CI: 7.3–26.7 kJ mol−1). ANCOVA confirmed that the slope difference was significant (F(1, 4) = 28.6, P < 0.01, Cohen's f2 = 18.0, post hoc power 1 – β ≈ 0.85). With only four temperature points (df = 2 per regression), the broad 95% CI on ΔEa (7.3–26.7 kJ mol−1) should be interpreted as an order-of-magnitude indicator; the ±30% sensitivity envelope in the game model (Section 4.1) encompasses this propagated uncertainty. Ea,GSH falls within the literature range of 40–55 kJ mol−1 for GSH-responsive systems, and Ea,hyd is consistent with published PBAT hydrolysis values (55–75 kJ mol−1).

(a) Temperature-dependent mass loss curves at four temperatures; (b) Arrhenius plots with activation energy comparison for both pathways.

The kGSH/khyd ratio narrowed from 11.5 (283 K) to 5.7 (313 K), thermodynamically consistent with Ea,hyd > Ea,GSH: the GSH-triggered acceleration is relatively more effective at lower temperatures—relevant to temperate-climate agricultural film design.

Given the limited sample sizes (n = 3–5 per condition) and the four-temperature Arrhenius design, the activation energies and derived quantities are best interpreted within their reported uncertainty intervals rather than as exact point values. Each rate constant was obtained from a least-squares fit with propagated slope standard errors, and these were carried forward through the Arrhenius regression to the activation energies (Ea,GSH = 45.2 ± 2.1 kJ mol; ΔEa = 17.0 kJ mol−1, SE = 3.5 kJ mol−1, 95% CI: 7.3–26.7 kJ mol−1) and, in turn, to the kGSH/khyd ratio and the downstream game and LCA parameters. Accordingly, the numerical results in the following sections are presented and should be read as approximate ranges; the precise digits (for example, individual percentages or p-values) reflect the specific fitted dataset and are not intended to imply accuracy beyond what these intervals support. A formal global uncertainty propagation is provided by the Monte Carlo analysis in Section 4.1, which simultaneously perturbs all parameters within their estimated ranges.

Ecotoxicity screening and product identification

Seed germination assays (Fig. 6a) showed that 1% PE MPs reduced the germination rate to 82.0% (P < 0.05 vs. blank at 95.2%) and 5% PE to 74.5% (P < 0.01), with mean root lengths shortened by 18% and 31%, respectively. In contrast, the 498 h endpoint leachate group exhibited a germination rate of 92.5%, which was not statistically different from the blank control (Tukey HSD, P = 0.38). The 240 h Phase II midpoint leachate showed a germination rate of 90.8%, also not significantly different from the blank (P = 0.21), indicating that even during the rapid degradation phase when oligomeric fragment concentrations are expected to peak, no acute phytotoxicity was detected. Earthworm avoidance assays (Fig. 6b) yielded an avoidance rate of 55.8% for PE MPs versus only 5.2% for 498 h degradation product leachate, with the latter showing no significant difference from blank (P = 0.42). The 240 h midpoint leachate produced an avoidance rate of 7.5%, likewise not significantly different from the blank (P = 0.35). These results are consistent with the low-toxicity profile of PBAT degradation products in temperate soils: hydrolysis products, including terephthalic acid, adipic acid, and 1,4-butanediol, are further mineralized by soil microorganisms.

LC-MS/GC-MS analysis of the 498 h degradation leachate identified 2-mercaptoethanol (2 ME) as the primary sulfur-containing product at 2.3 ± 0.4 mg L−1—consistent with the stoichiometric upper bound of <5 mg L−1 and representing ∼2.3% of the aquatic LC50 (∼100 mg L−1). Two caveats remain:1 chronic and sublethal effects on soil fauna are uncharacterized;2 field-relevant soil matrix effects may alter product speciation and bioavailability.

Soil incubation (25 °C, no exogenous GSH) served to establish a microbial baseline for passive PBAT biodegradation rather than to directly test the GSH-responsive mechanism. The Shannon diversity index increased from 3.82 ± 0.15 (Day 0) to 4.56 ± 0.21 (Day 30, P < 0.05), and the Chao1 richness estimator rose from 412 ± 28 to 587 ± 35 (P < 0.01), with community restructuring concentrated within the first 30 days (Day 60 Shannon: 4.72 ± 0.18, P = 0.12 vs. Day 30). PICRUSt2 functional prediction indicated upregulation of esterase/lipase and thiol–disulfide oxidoreductase pathways, although these remain computational predictions pending metagenomic or metatranscriptomic validation. Discussion

Evolutionary game theory: equilibrium analysis

Before assigning parameters, it is important to situate this mapping within the existing literature and to make the causal chain explicit. Research that jointly links material properties, life-cycle assessment, and governance or behavioural models remains fragmented: evolutionary-game analyses of plastic governance typically derive payoff parameters from policy assumptions or survey data rather than from material measurements,22,27 whereas life-cycle and eco-design studies16,28 quantify carbon outcomes without coupling them to the strategic behaviour of governance actors, and sustainability-transition work14 treats material innovation as exogenous. The present framework targets this gap through an explicit three-tier causal chain: Tier I (measured degradation kinetics, mechanical properties, and ecotoxicity) constrains Tier II (a bounded, evidence-anchored range for each game parameter), which in turn drives Tier III (LCA disposal fractions that are made endogenous to the resulting equilibrium through eqn (5)–(8)). Each arrow in this chain is one-directional and traceable, so that a change in a measured material property propagates to a defined change in carbon outcome. We emphasise, however, that the chain transmits scenario structure rather than empirical causation: it shows how material data could shift governance and carbon outcomes under stated assumptions, not that such shifts will necessarily occur in any specific real-world jurisdiction.

The three-party evolutionary game model was parameterized through a three-tier material-data mapping scheme. The mapping logic proceeds as follows. First, the technology premium R encodes the marginal value that a biodegradable material provides over conventional PE across three normalized dimensions (Table 3). Each dimension is linearly rescaled to with explicit anchor points; the equal-weighted average (3.2) is conservatively rounded to R ≈ 3.0.

Second, the transition cost Ceco reflects the incremental manufacturing expenditure of HEDS chain extension and twin-screw melt-blending over standard PBAT processing; Ceco = 2.5 is set such that R/Ceco = 1.2, consistent with the 10–30% green-product price premium reported in industry surveys. Third, the environmental remediation cost Cenv is bounded by ecotoxicity contrast: PE MPs caused a 13.2 percentage-point germination reduction and 55.8% earthworm avoidance, whereas blend degradation products showed no significant effects; Cenv = 4.0 is calibrated to satisfy Cenv/R > 1 based on the ecotoxicity contrast and confirmed by LC-MS product identification (2-ME at 2.3 mg L−1, ∼2.3% of LC50), encoding the hierarchy Cenv > R > Ceco with sulfur-product concentrations verified below ecotoxicological thresholds.

It must be acknowledged that this mapping is semi-quantitative: the three-dimensional scoring does not constitute a formal cost–benefit analysis, and the equal-weighting assumption is a simplifying choice. Different weighting schemes would shift R within the ±30% perturbation envelope already explored in sensitivity analysis, without altering equilibrium type. Developing a stakeholder-elicited multi-criteria decision framework with empirical market data would substantially refine the mapping fidelity and is identified as a priority for future work.

Consistent with this, all game-theoretic and LCA results reported below should be read as scenario simulations that explore the consequences of material-informed parameter ranges, not as forecasts or approximations of actual policy outcomes in any specific jurisdiction. The technology premium, ecological cost, and transformation cost are ultimately market-, policy-, and behaviour-dependent quantities that cannot be fixed from a small set of laboratory indicators alone; the experimental data are used only to anchor plausible ranges and relative orderings, while the absolute equilibria and carbon values remain illustrative.

A further consideration concerns the boundaries of the tripartite abstraction. Real biodegradable-plastic governance involves many actors—farmers and other end users, recyclers, composting-facility operators, local regulatory authorities, consumers, and standard-setting bodies. The model compresses these into three aggregate strategic players according to their dominant decision role in the material life cycle: the government player (y) aggregates the public-authority and standard-setting functions (national and local regulators and GB/EN standards bodies) that set rules, subsidies, and penalties; the enterprise player (x) aggregates the supply-side producers, recyclers, and composting operators whose shared decision is whether to adopt the degradable technology and its associated disposal infrastructure; and the public player (z) aggregates consumers, farmers, and other end users whose decision is participation in correct sorting, use, and oversight. This aggregation is defensible when the actors within each group share a common payoff direction and respond to the same policy levers, which holds for the strategic question examined here—adoption of stimulus-responsive degradable film versus the conventional baseline. The abstraction necessarily omits intra-group heterogeneity and horizontal interactions (for example, recycler–composter competition), so the three-party model is intended for system-level qualitative insight into governance–carbon coupling rather than for resolving the behaviour of any individual stakeholder class; disaggregating selected actors into a higher-dimensional game is a natural extension where such intra-group conflicts become decisive.

Sensitivity analysis with ±30% perturbation (Fig. 8) identified the public influence coefficient α as the most critical lever for convergence time (∼45%), followed by R (∼30%). The baseline α = 0.3 is adopted from the literature range of 0.2–0.5 for government–enterprise–public evolutionary games; no empirical calibration specific to biodegradable-plastic governance exists, and the actual value may differ substantially—the ±30% sensitivity envelope (yielding α = 0.21–0.39) partially addresses this, but field surveys of public responsiveness to material-specific environmental information would be needed for rigorous calibration. The R/Ceco prerequisite was maintained across all perturbations (minimum R/Ceco = 1.05), and equilibrium type remained stable under single-parameter variation. Setting the replicator dynamics to zero (full derivation in Section S4) reveals two classes of stable equilibria: (i) full three-party participation (1, 1, 1), when R > Ceco and S < G; and (ii) enterprise–public autonomy (1, 0, 1), when S > G. S = G represents the bifurcation threshold. Evolutionary trajectories show government regulation following an inverted-U pattern (Fig. S3 and S4): under S > G, government enforcement initially intensifies as enterprise adoption accelerates, then declines toward zero as enterprise–public self-governance renders regulatory intervention unnecessary—a “regulatory sunset” rather than persistent institutional maintenance. In contrast, the S < G regime sustains government participation at equilibrium (1, 1, 1), because the low subsidy burden permits continued regulatory engagement. Increasing initial public participation z0 from 0.1 to 0.6 shortened cooperative convergence time by approximately 80% (Fig. S5).

The S/G bifurcation carries a critical policy design implication: when subsidy intensity exceeds the synergy threshold (S > G), the model predicts enterprise–public autonomy (1, 0, 1) with government exit; when S < G, full three-party participation (1, 1, 1) is the stable equilibrium. Because the composting disposal fraction depends strongly on government participation fcompost = 0.57x*y* + 0.01z*; eqn (5)), the (1, 0, 1) equilibrium yields dramatically lower composting (1% vs. 58%) and correspondingly higher landfill dependence (57% vs. 2%). This makes S < G a necessary condition for achieving the governance-coupled carbon savings quantified in Section 4.2. The sensitivity analysis (Fig. 8) uses S = 2.5 > G to explore convergence dynamics under the high-subsidy regime; the LCA scenarios below use the S < G equilibrium to evaluate carbon outcomes under sustained governance.

A structural decomposition analysis reveals that the ∼45% sensitivity of α is data-dependent rather than a structural artifact of the model. At the material-derived R/Ceco = 1.2, α sensitivity is 45%; however, at R/Ceco = 1.05 (early-stage market conditions), sensitivity rises to 58%, while at R/Ceco = 2.0 (mature market), it decreases to 31%. This transforms a single sensitivity number into a policy landscape: as production costs decline and R/Ceco rises, the optimal policy emphasis shifts from public mobilization toward subsidy–synergy optimization. At the current R/Ceco ≈ 1.2, public engagement campaigns represent the highest-leverage investment; however, the transition to subsidy-centric policy can be anticipated and calibrated against industry maturation indicators.

Full-range Monte Carlo simulation (10 000 iterations with ±30% uniform perturbation of all seven parameters simultaneously) yielded an approximately 72% (≈70–75% across resampling) probability of maintaining the cooperative equilibrium (x*, y*, z* > 0.5). The uniform perturbation assumption treats all parameters as equally uncertain; in practice, literature-derived parameters (α, G) likely carry narrower ranges than market-estimated ones (R, Ceco)—parameter-specific uncertainty quantification is identified as a priority for future refinement. Failure analysis identified that >85% of equilibrium collapses occur at R < 2.5 and Ceco > 2.8—precisely the conditions prevailing in early-stage markets where production volumes are low and costs are high.

Life cycle carbon assessment under governance scenarios

A central innovation of this framework is the explicit functional coupling between game equilibria and LCA disposal fractions. Rather than treating end-of-life scenarios as exogenous assumptions, disposal fractions are derived endogenously from the game model's equilibrium strategies (x*, y*, z*):5fcompost = 0.57x*y* + 0.01z*6frecycle = 0.20x* + 0.10y*z*7fincinerate = 0.10 + 0.12(1 − y*)8flandfill = 1 − fcompost − frecycle − fincinerate

These functional forms were calibrated against municipal waste composition data from five Chinese contexts (Shanghai, Shenzhen, national average, Chengdu, and rural baseline; Table S2), yielding deviations of ±2–4 percentage points (Section S5). To mitigate overfitting, the five route-specific coefficients were reduced to three free parameters: two structural constraints fix the incineration floor at 0.10 (governance-independent minimum infrastructure) and determine landfill residually (eqn (8)), while two coefficients—the recycling–public interaction (0.10 in eqn (6)) and incineration governance sensitivity (0.12 in eqn (7))—were preset from literature ranges. The resulting three free coefficients (0.57 and 0.01 in eqn (5); 0.20 in eqn (6)) were calibrated by constrained least-squares regression. Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV) yielded mean absolute errors of 3.5 pp (Shanghai held out), 3.0 pp (Shenzhen held out), 2.5 pp (national average held out), 3.0 pp (Chengdu held out), and 2.0 pp (rural held out), with a mean LOO-CV error of 2.8 pp (intermediate steps in Table S4). With five benchmarks and three free coefficients (df = 2), the LOO-CV constitutes genuine out-of-sample validation, confirming that the functional forms generalize across governance contexts spanning advanced urban to rural conditions. Coefficient sensitivity analysis (±20% perturbation) is presented in Section S5.

LCA employed a cradle-to-grave framework: raw material acquisition contributed 450 kg CO2-eq per ton (PBAT pellets 380 + PUs monomers 70) and processing added 200 kg CO2-eq per ton. Three governance scenarios were evaluated by substituting equilibrium strategies into eqn (5)–(8) (Table 5).

Policy implications

Based on the validated governance–carbon coupling, four policy recommendations emerge from the model outputs.

(1) Kinetic thresholds in national standards. Current standards (GB/T 20197, EN 17033) could incorporate activation energy benchmarks and stimulus–response rate criteria. The dual-pathway Ea analysis shows that the kinetic mechanism carries information relevant to processing and infrastructure planning—though specific thresholds require field trials and multi-stakeholder consultation.

(2) Life-cycle digital product passports. The ∼540 kg CO2-eq per ton governance-carbon sensitivity (56% of PE → A reduction) provides quantitative basis for evaluating digital traceability, enabling differentiated waste management that increases effective z* and y* in disposal fraction functions.

(3) Targeted fiscal instruments for early-stage markets. Monte Carlo failure analysis identified >85% of equilibrium collapses at R < 2.5 and Ceco > 2.8. Graduated subsidies and tax incentives could be phased out as market scale widens the R − Ceco margin.

(4) Dynamic public participation leverage. At R/Ceco ≈ 1.2, public engagement represents the highest-leverage investment (∼45% sensitivity); as R/Ceco rises toward 2.0, emphasis shifts to subsidy–synergy optimization (∼28%), suggesting governance planning calibrated against industry maturation.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations:

(i) Laboratory–field gap. All kinetics were obtained under laboratory-accelerated conditions (10–20 mM GSH, 283–313 K); extrapolation to environmental GSH (∼10 µM) yields ∼59 years half-life (Section S2), ruling out chemical cleavage alone at agronomic timescales. The material serves as proof-of-concept; field degradation requires untested multi-mechanism synergy. Ongoing ≥180 days burial trials are the first assessment.

(ii) Semi-quantitative parameter mapping. The scoring scheme uses equal-weighted dimensions; the broad 95% CI on ΔEa (7.3–26.7 kJ mol−1) propagates substantial uncertainty into downstream analysis. Stakeholder-elicited weighting remains a priority.

(iii) Ecotoxicity scope. Only two species, single concentrations, and acute endpoints at two time points were tested. LC-MS confirmed sulfur-containing products below ecotoxicological thresholds, but chronic and sublethal effects remain uncharacterized.

(iv) PCDL is petrochemical-sourced (15.6% of raw material emissions); bio-based polyols would alter the carbon profile.

(v) Disposal fraction geographic scope. Five-benchmark calibration (LOO-CV 2.8 pp; Section S5) provides genuine out-of-sample validation across Chinese governance contexts, but broader geographic coverage—particularly non-Chinese municipal systems—would strengthen generalizability.

The framework is mechanism-agnostic: photo-cleavable, enzyme-responsive, or pH-triggered systems could serve as alternative Tier I inputs given analogous kinetic and ecotoxicity benchmarks.

Conclusions

This study synthesized and characterized GSH-responsive PUs/PBAT blend films (20 : 80 w/w), systematically investigated their degradation kinetics under controlled conditions, performed ecotoxicity screening of degradation products, and developed a transferable three-tier mapping framework connecting material degradation data to evolutionary game parameters and life-cycle carbon assessment. The framework bridges the persistent gap between material science advances and governance policy formulation.

As a proof-of-concept under laboratory-accelerated conditions, the principal findings are:

(1) Mechanistic confirmation. An iso-crystallinity control (HDO-extended, disulfide-free, matched ∼20% crystallinity) confirmed >96% of acceleration derives from disulfide cleavage (99.2% vs. 19.5% mass loss at 498 h under 20 mM GSH, 313 K), validating the Tier I-based parameter mapping.

(2) Kinetic benchmarks. The GSH-triggered apparent activation energy Ea,GSH = 45.2 ± 2.1 kJ mol−1, 17.0 kJ mol−1 below the hydrolytic pathway (P < 0.01; 95% CI: 7.3–26.7 kJ mol−1), with 0.5 mM as the minimum effective GSH threshold (2.8-fold acceleration). GPC confirmed near-complete chain scission (Mw: 85 → 1.2 kDa) versus minimal reduction in controls (PBAT + GSH: 72.1 kDa; GSH-free blend: 78.5 kDa).

(3) Environmental safety. Acute screening (two species, two time points) showed no significant adverse effects. LC-MS identified 2-mercaptoethanol at 2.3 mg L−1 (∼2.3% of LC50), confirming sulfur-containing products below ecotoxicological thresholds. Soil microbial community analysis confirmed enrichment of PBAT-degrading genera (Pseudomonas 18.2%, Bacillus 15.8%), indicating biological capacity for processing degradation intermediates.

(4) Data-dependent governance sensitivity. Public participation sensitivity (∼45% at R/Ceco = 1.2) shifts dynamically with material economics: at R/Ceco = 1.05 (early-stage), sensitivity rises to 58%; at R/Ceco = 2.0 (mature market), it decreases to 31%, informing dynamic policy calibration. Monte Carlo: ∼72% equilibrium survival (>85% of collapses at R < 2.5 and Ceco > 2.8, indicating early-stage market vulnerability).

(5) Governance–carbon parity. Functional coupling (LOO-CV: 2.8 pp) yielded carbon footprints of ∼550–1100 kg CO2-eq per ton; governance upgrading saves ∼540 kg CO2-eq per ton—56% of the Scenario A reduction relative to PE (∼970 kg CO2-eq per ton). The S/G bifurcation threshold determines whether full three-party participation (1, 1, 1) or enterprise–public autonomy (1, 0, 1) is achieved, with profound implications for composting infrastructure utilization (58% vs. 1%). Future priorities: (i) intermediate GSH gradients (0.1–5 mM) under realistic soil matrices to validate multi-mechanism synergy with microbial degradation; (ii) chronic and sublethal ecotoxicity assessment across additional species and endpoints; (iii) ≥180 days field burial trials with simultaneous mass loss, molecular weight, microplastic, and soil indicator monitoring.

Looking ahead, several directions merit investigation. First, field-scale validation under real soil, moisture, and microbial conditions—together with seasonal temperature cycling—is needed to calibrate the laboratory kinetics against environmentally realistic degradation rates, building on the ongoing burial trials. Second, replacing the petrochemical PCDL soft segment with bio-based polyols and broadening the responsive chemistry (enzyme-, pH-, or photo-triggered linkages) would extend the Tier I input space while further lowering the cradle-to-grave carbon profile. Third, the three-tier mapping should be tested against empirical governance datasets and expanded, disaggregated stakeholder models to move it from scenario simulation toward predictive policy support. Finally, coupling the framework with dynamic, region-specific waste-stream data and longer-term, multi-species ecotoxicity testing would strengthen its value as a decision-support tool for sustainable polymer design and plastic-pollution governance.
