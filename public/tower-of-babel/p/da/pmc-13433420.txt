‌Coronary inflammatory imaging and clinical factors: associations with coronary artery aneurysm regression in pediatric Kawasaki disease
Authors: Yong Li, Zhiqiang Bai, Xuezhen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe factors influencing the regression of coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs) in Kawasaki disease (KD) remain incompletely understood. Recent studies suggest a potential role for coronary inflammation and fat attenuation index (FAI) of perivascular adipose tissue attenuation (PCAT) derived from coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA). This study aimed to investigate the differences in clinical, laboratory, and imaging characteristics, with a focus on pericoronary adipose tissue FAI, between patients with CAAs regression and non-regression. In this retrospective observational study, children diagnosed with Kawasaki disease accompanied by coronary artery involvement were divided into a Regression group and a Non-Regression group based on the outcome of coronary aneurysm progression. Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were collected and were comparable between groups. Key imaging parameters assessed included the maximum CAA diameter, Z-score, CAA size category (small, medium, giant), and the pericoronary fat attenuation index (FAI) measured by coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA). Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of CAA regression. This retrospective observational study analyzed 40 KD patients with documented CAAs, divided into a Regression group (R group, n = 20) and a Non-Regression group (NR group, n = 20). Proportion of white blood cell count (WBC) ≥ 30 × 10⁹/L in the regression group (10.0% vs. 60.0%, p < 0.001), pericoronary CT fat attenuation index (-71.9 ± 4.5 HU vs.-66.4 ± 3.7 HU, p < 0.001), aneurysm maximum diameter (4.28 ± 2.15 mm vs. 6.86 ± 2.20 mm, p < 0.001), and Z-score (4.6 ± 2.7 vs. 8.6 ± 4.0, p < 0.001) were significantly lower than those in the non-regressive group. In addition, the distribution of coronary aneurysm size grades was significantly different between the two groups (p < 0.001), with medium (55.0%) and giant aneurysms (30.0%) predominating in the non-regression group and small aneurysms (80.0%) predominating in the regression group. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified white blood cell (WBC) count ≥ 30 × 10⁹/L (OR 0.02, 95% CI 0.00–0.47, p = 0.016), higher pericoronary computed tomography fat attenuation index (CT FAI) (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.43–0.89, p = 0.009), and higher Z‑values (OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.58–1.00, p = 0.049) as adverse factors for coronary artery aneurysm (CAA) regression. Based on the Youden index derived from the receiver operating characteristic curve, the optimal cutoff values for predicting CAA regression were − 68.7 HU for the mean FAI and 4.6 for the Z‑score. In KD patients with CAAs, smaller initial aneurysm Z-score, the absence of extreme leukocytosis (WBC ≥ 30 × 10⁹/L), and lower fat attenuation index (FAI) are associated with a higher likelihood of CAA regression. These findings suggest that both the initial morphological severity of the aneurysm and the state of pericoronary inflammation, as potentially reflected by FAI, are critical determinants of vascular remodeling outcomes.Keywords: Kawasaki disease, Coronary artery aneurysms, Regression, Pericoronary adipose tissue

Background

Kawasaki disease (KD), an acute systemic vasculitis of unknown etiology predominantly affecting children, is the leading cause of acquired heart disease in developed nations [1, 2]. The most critical complication is the development of coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs), which occur in approximately 15–25% of untreated patients and can lead to long-term morbidity, including myocardial ischemia, thrombosis, and sudden cardiac death [3]. While intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy has significantly reduced the incidence of CAAs, a substantial proportion of patients still develop these lesions, making the prediction and management of their evolution a paramount concern in pediatric cardiology [2, 4].Consequently, accurately assessing the prognosis and regression potential of CAAs is crucial for risk stratification, guiding long-term surveillance, and optimizing therapeutic strategies [5, 6]. The natural history of CAAs is heterogeneous; some regress to a normal luminal diameter, others persist unchanged, and a subset progresses to giant aneurysms with a high risk of stenosis [7]. Identifying factors predictive of CAA regression is therefore of immense clinical importance [8]. Several baseline clinical and laboratory parameters have been investigated. Higher levels of acute-phase reactants, such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), prolonged fever duration, and IVIG resistance have been consistently associated with a more severe coronary outcome and a lower likelihood of aneurysm regression. Furthermore, initial echocardiographic assessment plays a foundational role [9, 10]. The maximal internal diameter of the coronary artery, particularly when classified as a giant aneurysm (≥ 8 mm or Z-score ≥ 10), is one of the most potent predictors of persistent dilation and adverse cardiac events [9, 11].

Beyond luminal dimensions, the pathophysiological process underlying CAA formation and evolution is driven by intense panvasculitis of the coronary artery wall. This inflammatory insult is not confined to the intima but involves the entire vessel wall, including the pericoronary adipose tissue (PCAT) [12]. PCAT is now recognized as a highly active endocrine and paracrine organ that modulates vascular tone and inflammation [13]. In the context of vasculitis, inflamed PCAT secretes pro-inflammatory adipokines and cytokines, contributing to a vicious cycle of vascular damage and impaired healing [14]. Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA)-derived perivascular fat attenuation index (FAI) has emerged as a novel, quantitative biomarker for detecting coronary inflammation by measuring changes in the attenuation of PCAT [15]. Higher FAI values indicate inflammatory remodeling and edema of the perivascular adipose tissue surrounding the coronary artery, serving as a direct indicator of active perivascular inflammation [16]. The role of this localized inflammatory milieu, as captured by FAI, in influencing the remodeling and regression potential of established CAAs in KD patients remains largely unexplored.

Currently, prognostic models for CAA regression in KD primarily rely on initial echocardiographic size and traditional serum markers [17]. However, these may not fully capture the ongoing vascular wall inflammation that critically determines vascular remodeling [18]. A comprehensive, multi-modal assessment integrating the established prognostic power of clinical-serological profiles and initial aneurysm size with a direct marker of coronary arterial wall inflammation (FAI) could substantially improve risk prediction. Therefore, this study aims to perform a multifactorial regression analysis to determine the independent and combined contributions of clinical characteristics, initial echocardiographic CAA size, and CCTA-assessed perivascular FAI to the likelihood of CAA regression in children with a history of KD. Such an integrative approach is necessary to move beyond anatomical descriptors and incorporate the pathophysiological state of the vessel, potentially leading to more personalized and effective long-term management for these patients.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective, single-center cohort study was conducted at Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital(No: SYSKY-2025-1052-01), which waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments.

We retrospectively screened the medical records of all patients diagnosed with Kawasaki disease (KD) complicated by coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs) who were admitted to our center between January 2018 and December 2023. The diagnosis of KD and CAA was based on the American Heart Association (AHA) diagnostic criteria and guidelines for echocardiographic assessment of coronary arteries [19]. Coronary artery lesions were classified according to the result of echocardiography: small aneurysm (Z-score ≥ 2.5 to < 5, or internal diameter 3–4 mm in children < 5 years; 4–5 mm in children ≥ 5 years), medium aneurysm (Z-score ≥ 5 to < 10, or internal diameter 4–8 mm), and giant aneurysm (Z-score ≥ 10, or internal diameter ≥ 8 mm) [20].

Inclusion criteria were: (1) confirmed diagnosis of KD; (2) confirmed presence of CAA by echocardiography at the time of KD diagnosis or before IVIG treatment and coronary CT angiography (CCTA) within one week; (3) availability of complete clinical, laboratory, and echocardiography follow-up data for at least 12 months after the acute phase. Exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of congenital heart disease or other acquired cardiovascular diseases; (2) incomplete medical records or loss to follow-up; (3) poor-quality CCTA images precluding quantitative analysis. The clinical indications for coronary CT angiography (CCTA) in our cohort were: (1) follow-up evaluation of known coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs) after Kawasaki disease (KD), (2) assessment of coronary artery morphology when echocardiography provided suboptimal visualization, and (3) pre-interventional planning for patients with giant aneurysms or stenotic lesions. The decision to proceed with CCTA was made by the attending pediatric cardiologist based on clinical need, patient cooperation, and renal function.

For each patient, the most severe coronary aneurysm was selected as the criterion for determining whether the coronary aneurysm was regressed or not at follow-up. Based on the evolution of CAAs on follow-up imaging, patients were divided into two groups: the Regression (R) group and the Non-Regression (NR) group. Aneurysm Regression meets either of the following conditions: Z-score < 2.0 or aneurysm maximum diameter < 3.0 mm (for younger children, Z-score reference value is limited). The judgement time point is as follows: comparison of baseline and follow-up echocardiography (interval ≥ 12 months), the above criteria continuously meet ≥ 2 consecutive follow-up visits [1, 9]. Given that follow-up data are unavailable for some lesions due to limited compliance with examinations in pediatric patients, we analyzed lesion evolution on a per-patient basis for the largest coronary aneurysm, rather than on a per-lesion basis.

Data collection

Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were systematically extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized data collection form. The following baseline variables were collected: Age, sex, body mass index (BMI), duration of illness before intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) administration, presence of IVIG resistance (defined as persistent or recrudescent fever ≥ 36 h after completion of initial IVIG therapy), diagnosis of incomplete KD, duration of fever (≥ 7 days vs. <7 days), and presence of specific cardiac findings on initial echocardiography (left ventricular dysfunction, mitral regurgitation, pericardial effusion), Hemoglobin (anemia defined as Hb < 80 g/L), platelet count (thrombocytopenia), serum albumin level (hypoalbuminemia), C-reactive protein (CRP; dichotomized at > 100 mg/L), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), procalcitonin (PCT), and white blood cell (WBC) count (dichotomized at ≥ 30 × 10⁹/L vs. <30 × 10⁹/L).

Coronary artery lesion characteristics: assessed via initial echocardiography

Variables assessed included: the number of aneurysms, their location (proximal, mid, or distal segments of the left anterior descending, left main, or right coronary arteries), the maximal diameter of the coronary artery aneurysm (CAA), the corresponding Z-score, and the presence of complications (such as thrombus formation, calcification, stenosis, or coronary wall thickening). CAA size was classified as small, medium, or giant according to the criteria specified earlier. Echocardiographic findings of children at the initial stage of Kawasaki disease and during follow-up for one year or more were analyzed.

CCTA imaging analysis and pericoronary fat attenuation index measurement

All patients underwent electrocardiogram-gated coronary CT angiography (CCTA) using a CT scanner (Discovery CT750 HD, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, Wisconsin) during the subacute phase (within one week of initial echocardiography).

After breath-holding training, ECG-gated coronary angiography was performed in elderly children using the following scan parameters: Tube voltage 80 kV, Tube current 60–100 mAs. The non-breath-holding little children underwent conventional spiral CT scanning under calm breathing conditions (during sleep or after training) with Tube voltage 80 kV, Tube current 18–45 mAs. The scanning parameters were delay 15–18 s, slice thickness 3.0 mm, scan time 5–8 s, reconstruction kernel B 31 f medium smooth+, reconstructed slice thickness 1.0 mm, and interval 0.4–0.8 mm.

The contrast medium used was Iohexol (350 mg I/mL), administered at an injection rate of 1–2 mL/s with a dose of 2 mL/kg body weight. Scanning was performed from the head side to the foot side, covering a range from 1 cm below the tracheal bifurcation to the basal plane.

In accordance with the ALARA principle, a low-dose scanning protocol was selected to minimize radiation exposure. The mean radiation dose was 0.46 ± 0.24 mSv.

The pericoronary fat attenuation index (FAI) was quantitatively assessed as a marker of pericoronary inflammation, following a validated methodology [21, 22]. Briefly, CCTA images were analyzed using specialized research software (uAI-CoronaryCTA, United Imaging). For each patient, the most severe coronary artery aneurysm detected both in initial CCTA and cardiac ultrasound examination was selected for subsequent follow-up via cardiac ultrasound. The ROI was defined as the segment of the coronary artery lumen with the largest diameter, extending 20 mm proximally and distally from the center of a fusiform aneurysm as the reference point. For fat attenuation index (FAI) measurement, a radial ROI was manually traced, extending 5 mm outward from the outer vessel wall (epicardial border) along the entire length of the aneurysm, while excluding areas adjacent to myocardium, pericardium, or large epicardial fat depots. This approach is consistent with methods previously described by Antonopoulos et al. and Oikonomou et al. [21, 22]. The mean attenuation (Hounsfield Units, HU) of all adipose tissue voxels within this ROI (defined as -190 HU to -30 HU) was calculated, yielding the perivascular FAI. Lower (more negative) FAI values indicate less inflamed pericoronary adipose tissue. The measurement process is shown in Fig. 1. The analysis was performed by two radiologists with > 5 years of experience in cardiovascular imaging, who was blinded to all clinical data and patient outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM Corp.) and R software (version 4.2.1). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median with interquartile range [Q1, Q3] and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Variables with a p-value < 0.10 in univariate analysis or deemed clinically relevant based on prior literature were entered into a multivariate binary logistic regression model (forward stepwise likelihood ratio method) to identify independent predictors associated with CAA regression. Results are expressed as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Results

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the 40 patients included in this analysis, stratified by their outcome status as non-regression group (NR, N = 20) or regression group (R, N = 20) of coronary artery aneurysms (CAA), are presented in Table 1. No significant differences were observed between the two outcome groups in terms of age (NR: 9.25 ± 1.92 years; R: 9.75 ± 3.33 years; p = 0.569), and the prevalence of IVIG resistance did not show statistically significant differences (p = 0.342), as 8 patients in NR group, 11 patients in R group. Laboratory and cardiac findings at presentation were also largely comparable between groups (Table 1). However, a white blood cell count of 30 × 10⁹/L or higher was significantly more prevalent in the non-regression group (60.0%) compared to the regression group (10.0%), with a p-value of less than 0.001. The characteristics of the coronary artery aneurysms themselves were shown in the Table 1. The mean pericoronary fat attenuation index (pericoronary FAI) was significantly lower (more negative) in the regression group (-71.9 ± 4.5 HU) compared to the non-regression group (-66.4 ± 3.7 HU; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). The mean luminal CT attenuation values in the coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs) non-regression group were compared with those in the regression group by branch location. The values were as follows: proximal left anterior descending artery, 406 ± 31 HU vs. 422 ± 47 HU (p = 0.217); proximal circumflex artery, 434 ± 45 HU vs. 429 ± 28 HU (p = 0.650); and proximal right coronary artery trunk, 464 ± 34 HU vs. 470 ± 29 HU (p = 0.562). The overall mean luminal CT attenuation values across the three branches were 435 ± 21 HU and 440 ± 16 HU (p = 0.364) for the non-regression and regression groups, respectively, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (Table 1). The correlation between fat attenuation index (FAI) and luminal attenuation was assessed using Pearson correlation, yielding a coefficient of r = − 0.047 (P = 0.774). This indicates no significant correlation between luminal CT attenuation values and FAI measurements.

The size of the aneurysms was a critical differentiating factor. The mean maximal aneurysm size was significantly larger in the NR group (6.86 ± 2.20 mm) than in the R group (4.28 ± 2.15 mm; p < 0.001). This was reflected in the CAA size categorization, where giant and medium aneurysms were overwhelmingly more frequent in the NR group (85.0% combined) compared to the R group (20.0% combined), while small aneurysms constituted 80.0% of the R group (p < 0.001). Correspondingly, the mean Z-score at diagnosis was significantly higher in the NR group (8.6 ± 4.0) than in the R group (4.6 ± 2.7; p < 0.001)(Fig. 2). In contrast, the number of aneurysms per patient (p = 0.256) and their anatomical location (p = 0.817) were not associated with the outcome. Features of chronic coronary artery changes, including the presence of thrombosis (p = 0.605), calcification (p = 0.197), stenosis (p > 0.999), and coronary wall thickening (p = 0.507), did not show statistically significant differences between the outcome groups at baseline.

Univariable logistic regression analysis for the prediction of CAA regression (Table 2) revealed that mean WBC count ≥ 30 × 109 /L (OR 0.07, 95% CI 0.01–0.41, p = 0.003), Z-value (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.51–0.89, p = 0.006), small aneurysms (OR 32.0, 95% CI 2.76–370.81, p = 0.006) and pericoronary FAI (OR 0.71, 95% CI 0.570–0.89, p = 0.0026) were predictors of CAA regression or not.

Discussion

This retrospective observational study investigated the factors associated with coronary artery aneurysm (CAA) regression one year after the acute phase of Kawasaki disease (KD). By comparing 40 patients with CAAs who experienced regression (R group, n = 20) with those who did not (NR group, n = 20), several key predictors of CAA outcome were identified. The principal findings indicate that patients with regressing aneurysms had significantly lower initial inflammatory markers, smaller aneurysm dimensions, and reduced peri-coronary inflammation as measured by computed tomography angiography (CCTA)-derived fat attenuation index (FAI). Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified white blood cell (WBC) count ≥ 30 × 10⁹/L, higher pericoronary FAI and higher Z‑values as adverse factors for CAA regression.

The study found that a white blood cell (WBC) count ≥ 30 × 10⁹/L during the acute phase was significantly less frequent in the regression group (10% vs. 60%). This aligns with the established pathophysiology of KD, where a more intense systemic inflammatory response is linked to greater vascular damage and poorer coronary outcomes [23]. Severe leukocytosis is a recognized marker of stronger inflammation and has been associated with treatment resistance and CAA development [23]. Our finding that it is also a negative predictor for subsequent regression reinforces the critical role of modulating the initial inflammatory cascade. This is consistent with studies emphasizing the importance of early, aggressive anti-inflammatory therapy to mitigate long-term sequelae [24].

The most robust anatomical predictors were the initial maximum aneurysm diameter and its corresponding Z-score. The regression group was predominantly composed of small aneurysms (80%), while the non-regression group had a majority of medium (55%) and giant (30%) aneurysms. Multivariate analysis showed a dramatically higher odds of regression for small aneurysms compared to giant aneurysms (OR 42.15). This finding is highly consistent with the natural history of KD CAAs, where smaller aneurysms have a well-documented higher propensity for resolution through adaptive vascular remodeling [10, 12]. Larger aneurysms, especially giant aneurysms (> 8 mm), are known to have a very low rate of complete regression and a higher risk of stenosis, thrombosis, and ischemic events [7, 9, 25]. Our study quantitatively confirms this gradient of regression probability across size categories.

A novel and significant contribution of this study is the evaluation of pericoronary adipose tissue inflammation via pericoronary FAI. The regression group exhibited a significantly lower (more negative) FAI value (-71.9 ± 4.5 HU) compared to the non-regression group (-66.4 ± 3.7 HU). A higher FAI indicates a higher lipid content and inflammatory state of the fat surrounding the coronary arteries, which is a recognized imaging biomarker of coronary inflammation in atherosclerosis [15, 26, 27]. The multivariate result (OR 0.62 for higher FAI) identifies peri-coronary inflammation as an independent adverse factor for CAA regression. This may suggest that the inflammatory process in KD may persist in the perivascular microenvironment, hindering vascular repair [28, 29]. While CCTA is not routinely recommended for initial KD assessment, this finding points to its potential utility as a prognostic tool in selected patients with CAAs, offering insight beyond luminal dimensions. Pericoronary FAI should not replace echocardiography but rather serve as an adjunctive tool when CCTA is clinically indicated. The incremental value lies in its ability to detect peri-coronary inflammation even before luminal changes become apparent, thus providing an earlier or more sensitive inflammatory marker. Recent studies in adult cardiology have validated FAI as a marker of plaque vulnerability, but its application in pediatric vasculitis like KD is pioneering [30–32]. Our study extends this concept, proposing that persistent local inflammation, detectable by FAI, may be a key determinant of vascular remodeling outcomes.

The primary innovation lies in the integrated analysis of a novel imaging biomarker (pericoronary FAI) with traditional clinical and echocardiographic parameters to predict CAA regression. While previous studies have firmly established the prognostic importance of initial aneurysm size, the incorporation of FAI provides a pathophysiological link. It moves beyond measuring the aneurysm’s lumen to assessing the inflammatory status of the vessel wall’s immediate environment. This offers a more comprehensive “phenotyping” of the coronary lesion, potentially identifying patients with smaller aneurysms but high peri-coronary inflammation who might still be at risk for poor remodeling.

This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective, single-center design and modest sample size. The findings, particularly the large odds ratio for small aneurysms, require validation in larger, prospective, multi-ethnic cohorts. Future research should investigate the evolution of FAI over time in relation to treatment and correlate it with histopathological markers of inflammation. Exploring whether targeted therapies can modulate perivascular inflammation (FAI) and improve regression rates is a crucial next step. Future studies with larger cohorts may benefit from per-lesion analyses with mixed-effects modeling to explore heterogeneity in aneurysm behavior within individual patients.

In conclusion, this retrospective study confirms that the initial size of a coronary aneurysm is the strongest predictor of its regression one year after Kawasaki disease. Furthermore, it identifies acute severe leukocytosis and, innovatively, a higher peri-coronary CT fat attenuation index as independent adverse factors associated with a lack of regression. These findings underscore the lasting impact of the initial inflammatory burden, both systemically and locally around the coronary artery. Assessing peri-coronary inflammation via FAI may enhance risk stratification, providing a more nuanced prognosis beyond aneurysm diameter alone. This could guide the intensity of long-term monitoring and inform future trials of adjunctive therapies aimed at promoting vascular healing in patients at high risk for persistent coronary lesions.
