A Multicentre Cross‐Sectional Survey of Non‐Wound Specialist Nurses' Role in Orthopaedic Incision Care: Training Needs and Implications for Clinical and Educational Practice in China
Authors: Kaiyan Fan, Huafen Wang, Jianli Lu, Kezhen Zhou, Mengwen Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

With global population ageing and the rising incidence of orthopaedic conditions, the demand for orthopaedic surgery has increased substantially (WHO, n.d.). However, surgical site infections (SSIs) remain a major concern, with large cohort studies reporting a 90‐day postoperative infection rate of approximately 4.2% (Shapiro et al. 2022; Tucci et al. 2019). SSIs impose a considerable burden on healthcare systems by increasing outpatient and emergency visits, prolonging hospital stays, and raising readmission rates and treatment costs (Barrés‐Carsí et al. 2022).

Registered nurses (RNs) play a central role in surgical wound care, often contributing to clinical decision‐making and coordinating multidisciplinary care (Gillespie et al. 2020). Evidence from China has shown that targeted nursing interventions can reduce SSI rates from 11.6% to 1.7% and increase the rate of optimal wound healing to 73.3%, underscoring the important contribution of nursing care to postoperative recovery (He and Hai 2024). However, orthopaedic procedures involving prosthetic implantation and extensive tissue manipulation are associated with an elevated risk of wound complications (Liu et al. 2025). These clinical complexities place specific demands on orthopaedic nurses and require competencies in incision care that may extend beyond the scope of general nursing education.

In response to evolving healthcare needs, the roles of nurses have expanded worldwide. The International Council of Nurses (ICN) defines Nurse Practitioners (NPs) and Advanced Practice Nurses (APNs) as professionals with advanced clinical preparation who are able to manage patient care with a high degree of autonomy (ICN—International Council of Nurses, n.d.). Within this broader framework, Orthopaedic Nurse Practitioners (ONPs), particularly those serving as Perioperative Nurse Surgical Assistants (PNSAs), are integral members of surgical teams and may help reduce complications and operative time while improving pain control and wound outcomes (O'Rourke 2022). These evolving responsibilities highlight the growing need for specialized perioperative wound‐management skills. Nevertheless, foundational nursing education does not always equip nurses with the competencies required for complex surgical wound care (Lin et al. 2019). Previous studies have consistently identified substantial knowledge and skill gaps between generalist nurses and wound care specialists, emphasizing the need for continuing professional development to support high‐quality wound management (Corriveau et al. 2020; Td et al. 2023; Pirani 2020).

The Knowledge–Attitude–Practice (KAP) framework provides a useful model for understanding and improving clinical behaviour, proposing that knowledge acquisition may promote positive attitudes and facilitate behavioural change (Li 2012). It has been widely used to develop questionnaires and guide targeted educational interventions (Sm et al. 2023). Existing KAP‐related research has mainly focused on non‐specialist nurses' practices in chronic wound care (Xie et al. 2022; Qin et al. 2020), pressure injury prevention (Li et al. 2022; Jiang et al. 2020), and the practices of wound care specialists (Yang et al. 2023). In contrast, relatively little attention has been given to orthopaedic nurses without wound‐care specialization. Timmins et al. (2018) investigated wound‐care practices among registered nurses, including orthopaedic nurses, in three hospitals in Haiti and identified a number of barriers, including resource shortages, heavy workloads, role ambiguity, and limited interdisciplinary collaboration. Similar challenges may also be relevant in China, where nursing autonomy, access to specialist support, and clinical role expectations may vary across institutions and practice settings. These issues remain insufficiently explored in the Chinese context and warrant further investigation.

Against this background, the present multicentre cross‐sectional survey aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to orthopaedic surgical incision care among non‐wound specialist nurses in China, to examine their perceived training needs, and to explore factors associated with total KAP scores. By clarifying these patterns, the study sought to provide evidence to inform the development of targeted and contextually appropriate training strategies for orthopaedic nurses.

Methods

This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE statement for observational studies (Data S5), the Consensus‐Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS), and the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E‐Surveys (CHERRIES). The corresponding checklists are provided in the Data S1 and S2.

Study Design and Setting

A multicentre cross‐sectional e‐survey was conducted among non‐wound specialist nurses working in orthopaedic departments in 15 hospitals across 10 provinces in eastern, central, and southwestern China. Data collection took place from March 1 to May 31, 2024, ensuring a uniform three‐month survey period across all participating hospitals.

Study Population and Sampling

A convenience sampling strategy was used. Data were collected through an anonymous electronic questionnaire. Participants were informed that all responses would remain confidential, and standardized instructions were provided to reduce variability in item interpretation. Because the survey relied partly on self‐report, social desirability bias could not be fully excluded. To partially mitigate this concern, the questionnaire included both knowledge items and self‐reported attitude and practice items, allowing comparison across domains.

Eligible participants were required to be (1) registered nurses, (2) non‐wound specialist nurses, (3) employed in orthopaedic clinical practice for more than 3 years, and (4) willing to participate voluntarily and provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria were (1) nurses who were not on duty during the survey period, such as those on leave or attending external training, and (2) trainee, probationary, or intern nurses.

The sample size was estimated using G*Power software to ensure adequate power for multivariable association analyses. Assuming a moderate effect size of 0.3, a statistical power of 0.80, and a significance level of 0.05, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 369 participants. To allow for incomplete responses, the target sample size was increased by 20% to 443. A total of 437 valid responses were included in the final analysis, which was considered sufficient for the multivariable models conducted in this study.

Questionnaires completed in less than 180 s were excluded according to the predefined data‐cleaning rule. Missing data were minimal (< 1%) and were mainly related to demographic variables. Because the extent of missingness was very low, complete‐case analysis was used for the relevant statistical procedures.

E‐Survey Administration and Reporting

The survey was administered as a closed electronic survey through participating departments. Eligible nurses were invited locally by departmental coordinators at each participating site and accessed the questionnaire using the survey link or QR code distributed through institutional communication channels. Participation was voluntary, and no financial or material incentives were provided. To minimise duplicate entries, participants were instructed to complete the closed survey only once using the locally distributed access link or QR code.

Before entering the questionnaire, participants were presented with an electronic study information page describing the study purpose, eligibility criteria, estimated completion time, confidentiality arrangements, and the voluntary nature of participation. Electronic informed consent was obtained before the questionnaire could be completed. The final analytic dataset contained no personally identifying information, and the data were stored in password‐protected files accessible only to the research team.

Because the survey was distributed locally at participating sites and the final dataset available to the research team was anonymized and aggregated, complete site‐level invitation counts and response rates were not retained. Consequently, institutional response rates, source population denominators, and urban–rural classifications were unavailable, limiting assessment of site‐level non‐response bias and representativeness. After exclusion of questionnaires completed in less than 180 s according to the predefined data‐cleaning rule, 437 valid responses were retained for analysis.

Study Instruments

Three structured instruments were used in this study: (1) a demographic questionnaire, (2) a self‐developed questionnaire assessing knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to surgical incision care, and (3) a training‐needs assessment questionnaire for non‐wound specialist nurses.

Demographic Questionnaire

Participants provided information on sex, age, educational level, professional title, position, department, hospital grade, years of orthopaedic experience, specialist nurse status, whether a wound care specialist nurse was available in the department, and whether nurses in the department performed surgical incision dressing changes and had received relevant training.

Development and Validation of a Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices Questionnaire on Surgical Incision Care Among Orthopaedic Non‐Wound Specialist Nurses

A structured questionnaire assessing knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding surgical incision care was developed based on the KAP framework, current clinical guidelines, existing wound‐care instruments, and relevant empirical studies (Evans and Hedrick 2022; Anderson and Sexton 2022; Swanson et al. 2022; Tanner and Melen 2021; National Institute for Health and Care Excellence 2019; Kang et al. 2018; Huang et al. 2020). This process was intended to ensure both theoretical coherence and clinical relevance.

To establish content validity, the preliminary item pool was reviewed by a 12‐member expert panel comprising specialists in orthopaedic nursing, wound care, nursing education, and questionnaire development. Experts were selected according to educational background, professional rank, relevant clinical or academic experience, and willingness to participate. The panel evaluated item relevance, clarity, comprehensiveness, and applicability. Revisions were made through structured discussion and consensus.

A pilot test was then conducted among 15 orthopaedic nurses without wound‐care specialization to assess item clarity, comprehensibility, and acceptability. Based on pilot feedback, minor wording revisions were made to improve readability and logical flow. The final KAP questionnaire comprised 48 items across three domains: Knowledge (27 dichotomous items, scored 1 for a correct response and 0 for an incorrect response), Attitude (9 Likert‐type items), and Practice (12 Likert‐type items).

Because the Knowledge domain was designed to assess heterogeneous factual content rather than a single latent construct, factor analysis was not applied to the knowledge items. In contrast, the Attitude and Practice domains were psychometrically evaluated as latent constructs. Exploratory factor analysis was conducted for the Likert‐type items using principal axis factoring with oblique rotation, as the underlying constructs were expected to be correlated. Sampling adequacy and factorability were assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin statistic and Bartlett's test of sphericity. Item retention was based on conceptual relevance, factor loading, and corrected item–total correlation. Item‐level psychometric results for the Attitude and Practice domains, including rotated factor loadings, communalities, and corrected item–total correlations, are provided in Table S1.

The final instrument showed acceptable to very high internal consistency. Cronbach's alpha coefficients were 0.786 for Knowledge, 0.937 for Attitude, and 0.957 for Practice. However, very high alpha coefficients may also reflect substantial item overlap or redundancy and should not be interpreted as evidence of overall measurement validity in isolation. Higher scores indicated better knowledge, more positive attitudes, and more proactive practices regarding surgical incision care. The possible score ranges were 0–27 for Knowledge, 9–45 for Attitude, 12–60 for Practice, and 57–132 for the total KAP score. Confirmatory factor analysis was not performed, and test–retest reliability was not assessed in the present study; therefore, further evaluation of the instrument's structural validity and temporal stability is warranted. Although the observed ceiling effects suggest that some subscales may benefit from further refinement, instrument redevelopment was not feasible within the current study design. The questionnaire had been finalized before survey launch and was administered uniformly across all participating hospitals. Any substantive revision to item content, response options, or scale structure would have required a separate redevelopment and psychometric revalidation study in a new sample and would have reduced comparability with the data already collected. The full questionnaire and scoring rules are provided in the Data S3.

Non‐Specialist Nurses' Wound Care Training Needs Questionnaire

Training needs were assessed using the wound‐care training‐needs questionnaire developed by Fang et al. (2016). The full instrument and its scoring instructions are provided in the Data S3. This questionnaire consists of two components: training content needs (10 items) and training format needs (8 items), for a total of 18 items. Each item is rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not needed”) to 5 (“strongly needed”), yielding a total score range of 18–90, with higher scores indicating greater perceived training need. In the present dataset, the questionnaire showed very high internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha = 0.985). This value may indicate strong item homogeneity, although possible item redundancy should also be considered.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0, with supplementary item‐level re‐analysis procedures conducted to verify score construction and psychometric performance (Data S4). Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages. Continuous questionnaire scores were derived by summing the relevant item responses for each domain.

Normality was assessed using histograms, as well as the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Because several score distributions were skewed and showed marked upper‐end clustering, continuous variables were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), and non‐parametric methods were used where appropriate. Spearman's rank correlation was used to examine associations between Training Needs and KAP‐related scores.

Ceiling effects were quantified as the proportion of respondents achieving the maximum possible score on each subscale. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha, and corrected item–total correlations were calculated at the item level. For the Attitude and Practice domains, construct validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis based on principal axis factoring with oblique rotation.

To identify factors associated with KAP, the total KAP score was modelled as a continuous outcome. Given the skewed score distribution and the ceiling effects observed in several subscales, quantile regression was used as the primary multivariable modelling approach. As a sensitivity analysis, ordinary least squares regression with HC3 heteroskedasticity‐robust standard errors was also performed.

Based on clinical relevance and distributional considerations, the multivariable models included job title, position, hospital grade, the presence of a wound care specialist nurse in the department, whether nurses independently performed surgical wound dressing changes, and the total Training Needs score. Because some position categories contained sparse data, position was collapsed into staff nurse versus manager/other in the multivariable models. Variables related to dressing type, dressing‐change training, and standard procedures were analysed descriptively and in univariable comparisons only, because they were applicable only to the subgroup of departments in which nurse‐performed dressing changes had already been implemented.

Model diagnostics were performed to evaluate the adequacy and stability of the multivariable models. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). For the sensitivity model, residual plots, leverage values, and Cook's distance were examined to assess outlying and influential observations. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant Characteristics

After application of the predefined exclusion criterion based on questionnaire completion time, 437 participants were retained in the final analytic sample. Figure 1 presents a STROBE‐style flow diagram of participant inclusion and derivation of the final analytic sample. Most participants were female (99.5%, n = 435), aged 30–39 years (51.0%), and held a bachelor's degree (88.8%). More than half held the title of nurse‐in‐charge (54.0%), and most worked as staff nurses (82.4%). The majority were employed in tertiary class A hospitals (75.1%). Departments without a wound care specialist nurse accounted for 64.5% of the sample, and 84.2% had not implemented nurse‐performed surgical wound dressing changes.

STROBE‐style flow diagram of participant screening, inclusion, and final analytic sample.

Because the survey was administered locally across participating hospitals and the final dataset available to the research team was anonymized and aggregated, complete site‐level invitation counts and response rates were not retained. The study sample therefore reflects participating respondents rather than all eligible orthopaedic nurses in China. Although the participating hospitals were located across multiple provinces and administrative regions, the use of convenience sampling limits the representativeness of the sample and precludes a formal assessment of potential non‐response bias.

Training Needs and KAP Regarding Surgical Incision Care Among Orthopaedic Non‐Wound Specialist Nurses

Among the 437 nurses surveyed, the median Training Needs score was 90 (IQR 82–90) on a scale ranging from 18 to 90, indicating a generally high level of perceived need for wound‐care training. A total of 58.6% of respondents achieved the maximum possible Training Needs score, suggesting a substantial ceiling effect in this measure.

The total KAP score ranged from 57 to 132, with a median of 117 (IQR 106–122). The median scores for the Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice domains were 16 (IQR 13–19), 45 (IQR 41–45), and 60 (IQR 48–60), respectively. Ceiling effects varied across domains. The proportion of respondents scoring at the maximum possible value was 4.6% for Knowledge, 64.5% for Attitude, 55.6% for Practice, and 2.7% for the total KAP score. These findings indicate pronounced upper‐end clustering in the Attitude, Practice, and Training Needs subscales and suggest limited discrimination among respondents with higher scores on these measures.

Correlation Analysis Between Training Needs and KAP Regarding Surgical Incision Care Among Orthopaedic Non‐Wound Specialist Nurses

Spearman's rank correlation analysis showed that Training Needs was positively associated with the total KAP score for surgical incision care (ρ = 0.610, p < 0.001). At the subscale level, Training Needs was not significantly associated with Knowledge (ρ = 0.016, p = 0.732), but was positively associated with Attitude (ρ = 0.726, p < 0.001) and Practice (ρ = 0.696, p < 0.001).

Univariable Analysis of KAP Scores According to Participant Characteristics

Univariable analyses showed that total KAP score differed significantly according to job title (p = 0.001), position (p = 0.020), hospital grade (p = 0.001), implementation of nurse‐performed surgical wound dressing changes (p = 0.037), training in surgical wound care among departments implementing nurse‐performed dressing changes (p = 0.002), and Training Needs score (ρ = 0.610, p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed according to gender, age, educational level, the presence of a wound care specialist nurse in the department, dressing‐change type, or the existence of a standard procedure for surgical wound care (all p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Multivariable Analysis of Factors Associated With KAP Regarding Surgical Incision Care Among Orthopaedic Non‐Wound Specialist Nurses

In the multivariable analysis, total KAP score was treated as a continuous outcome. Given the skewed distribution of the score and the ceiling effects observed in several subscales, quantile regression was used as the primary modelling approach, with robust linear regression conducted as a sensitivity analysis.

In the primary quantile regression model, higher Training Needs scores were associated with higher total KAP scores (β = 0.889, 95% CI 0.833 to 0.945, p < 0.001). Compared with nurses holding the title of deputy chief nurse or above, lower KAP scores were observed among nurses (β = −4.703, 95% CI −7.915 to −1.491, p = 0.004), nurse‐in‐charge staff (β = −2.765, 95% CI −5.164 to −0.365, p = 0.024), and primary nurses (β = −3.320, 95% CI −6.057 to −0.584, p = 0.018). Nurses from level III (Class B) hospitals also had lower KAP scores than those from level II hospitals or below (β = −2.939, 95% CI −5.697 to −0.180, p = 0.037). Other covariates were not statistically significant in the primary model (Table 2).

Discussion

Effective wound management is important for recovery after orthopaedic surgery, and the present findings suggest continuing gaps in the preparedness of non‐wound specialist nurses for surgical incision care. By examining knowledge, attitudes, practices, and perceived training needs among 437 orthopaedic nurses from 15 tertiary hospitals across 10 provinces in China, this study provides multicentre evidence on current educational gaps and practice‐related challenges in this area.

The results indicated that respondents generally reported relatively favourable attitudes and practices regarding orthopaedic surgical incision care, whereas knowledge scores were comparatively lower. This pattern suggests that, in settings where wound care is not a primary specialist focus, nurses may rely more heavily on routine‐based or experience‐based practice than on consistently applied theoretical knowledge. This interpretation is broadly consistent with previous studies. For example, Balodimou et al. (2018) reported that only 39.2% of Greek nurses correctly identified the standard occurrence period for SSIs, despite many holding bachelor's degrees or above. A similar pattern was observed in the present study, in which only 26.5% of participants correctly identified this period, even though most respondents had relatively high educational qualifications. Likewise, Teshager et al. (2015) found that only 40.7% of nurses demonstrated sufficient knowledge regarding SSI prevention, suggesting that educational gaps in wound care remain common across settings.

Taken together, these findings suggest a disconnect between formal education and practical competency in surgical wound care. Although many participants had academic qualifications and reported relatively favourable practice scores, important knowledge gaps remained. This issue is further reflected in the high level of perceived training need observed in the present study. The median Training Needs score was 90, and 58.6% of respondents achieved the maximum score, indicating that demand for further education was substantial. At the same time, marked ceiling effects were observed in the Attitude, Practice, and Training Needs subscales. These findings indicate limited discrimination at the upper end of the score range and suggest that high scores in these domains should be interpreted cautiously rather than being viewed as evidence of uniformly high competence. Possible explanations for this clustering include restricted response options, social desirability, and limited item difficulty. It should also be noted that the very high Cronbach's alpha coefficients observed for the Practice and Training Needs scales indicate strong internal consistency but may also reflect substantial overlap or redundancy among items. These values should therefore be interpreted as evidence of item homogeneity rather than as proof of broader measurement validity.

The multivariable analyses further showed that total KAP scores were associated with job title, hospital grade, and perceived training needs. Higher KAP scores were generally observed among nurses with more senior professional titles and among those reporting greater training needs. These findings are broadly consistent with previous work showing that professional experience, institutional resources, and access to continuing education may shape wound‐care competence (Pirani 2020; Ielapi et al. 2022). In practice, senior nurses are often exposed to more complex cases, have greater responsibility for clinical coordination, and may have more opportunities to participate in professional development. Similarly, institutional context may influence access to specialist support, clinical protocols, and educational resources, all of which are likely to affect nurses' preparedness for surgical wound care.

At the same time, the hospital‐level findings should be interpreted cautiously. Although some differences were observed according to hospital grade, these associations were not entirely consistent across the primary and sensitivity models. This suggests that institutional grade may function as a proxy for broader contextual factors, such as training opportunities, staffing structure, and access to wound‐care expertise, rather than representing a stable independent determinant in itself. Future studies may benefit from examining site‐level characteristics in greater detail, including regional resource availability and urban–rural differences.

An important finding of the present study was that perceived Training Needs was positively associated with the total KAP score, as well as with the Attitude and Practice subscales, but not with the Knowledge subscale. This pattern may suggest that perceived educational demand is more closely related to professional engagement and self‐reported practice orientation than to factual knowledge alone. Nurses who perceive a stronger need for training may already be more engaged in improving their practice and more receptive to continuing education, even when their objective knowledge remains variable. However, because this study used a cross‐sectional design, these associations should not be interpreted causally. It cannot be determined whether greater perceived training need is associated with better KAP because more motivated nurses seek learning opportunities, or whether nurses with stronger KAP are more aware of their educational needs.

This pattern may also be understood in light of the Chinese clinical context. In practice, the implementation of wound‐related assessment, monitoring, and intervention may be influenced by local staffing structures, access to specialist support, and the extent to which standardized wound‐care guidance is incorporated into routine practice. Previous research has shown that nurses' adherence to surgical site infection prevention recommendations may be shaped by both facilitators and barriers within the clinical environment, including workload, team processes, and access to practical guidance (Lin et al. 2019). Evidence from China has also suggested continuing disparities in wound‐care education and competency development across nurses and institutions, as well as sustained demand for further professional training among wound specialist nurses themselves (Qiu‐ni et al. 2023; Jiang et al. 2025) In addition, post‐operative wound complication prevention relies on a broad range of nursing activities that may vary across settings according to local protocols, available expertise, and organizational support (Lin et al. 2019; Tobiano et al. 2024). Taken together, these contextual factors may partly explain why attitude and reported practice scores were relatively high, whereas knowledge remained less robust, and why training needs were strongly endorsed even among nurses with comparatively favourable self‐reported practices.

The findings also have practical implications for workforce development. Our data suggest that generalist orthopaedic nurses would benefit from structured, competency‐based continuing education that goes beyond routine wound‐care exposure. Given the knowledge gaps identified in areas such as wound classification, infection recognition, healing assessment, and dressing selection, educational content should be targeted and clinically relevant. Training strategies such as simulation‐based workshops, modular e‐learning, case‐based discussion, and supervised practical sessions may be particularly useful. Previous studies (Rasouli et al. 2023; Rotzoll et al. 2025) have shown that simulation‐based and blended learning approaches can improve clinical competence and learner confidence in healthcare education. In addition, regular interdisciplinary case discussion and clearer clinical protocols may help bridge the gap between theoretical understanding and day‐to‐day practice, especially in departments with limited access to wound‐care specialists.

These findings also support the broader need for stronger continuing education systems in orthopaedic wound care. In China, disparities in institutional resources, educator availability, and access to specialist mentorship may contribute to uneven competency development across settings. Previous studies have shown that limited access to structured wound‐care education remains an important challenge (Qiu‐ni et al. 2023; Jiang et al. 2025). Strengthening academic–clinical partnerships, expanding access to continuing education, and integrating flexible approaches such as tele‐education may help improve the accessibility and equity of training opportunities for nurses working in diverse hospital environments (Petges et al. 2020; Sebastian et al. 2018).

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias and reduced the representativeness of the sample. Second, because the final dataset was anonymized and aggregated, complete site‐level invitation counts, response rates, and source population denominators were unavailable, which limited the assessment of non‐response bias and generalizability. Third, the reliance on self‐reported data may have introduced social desirability bias. In addition, substantial ceiling effects were observed in the Attitude, Practice, and Training Needs subscales, limiting discrimination at the upper end of the score range and warranting cautious interpretation of the high scores observed in these domains. These findings suggest that further refinement and psychometric re‐evaluation of the relevant subscales may be needed. However, because the questionnaire had already been finalized and administered in a completed multicentre cross‐sectional survey, instrument redevelopment was beyond the scope of the present study. Although internal consistency and exploratory factor analysis were assessed, confirmatory factor analysis and test–retest reliability were not examined; therefore, further evaluation of the instrument's structural validity and temporal stability is warranted. In addition, because participants were recruited from multiple hospitals, responses may have been clustered within institutions, yet formal multilevel analysis was not feasible with the final dataset. Finally, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference.

Conclusion

This study assessed knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to surgical incision care among non‐wound specialist nurses working in orthopaedic departments in 15 tertiary hospitals across 10 provinces in China. Respondents generally reported favourable attitudes and practices, although important knowledge gaps remained, particularly in areas relevant to clinical wound assessment and management. These findings suggest a potential need for structured, competency‐based training that better integrates theoretical knowledge with practical skills. However, given the cross‐sectional design, convenience sampling, and marked ceiling effects observed in several subscales, the findings should be interpreted cautiously.
