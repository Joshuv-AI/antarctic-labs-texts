Identification of specific T-cell response and T-cell receptor targeting shared neoantigen for acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Weijun Zhou, Jinyi Yu, Fuyu Li, Yuting Huang, Jingwen Du, Anqin Wu, Kankan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccumulation of genetic mutations in malignant myeloid precursor cells leads to an extremely poor prognosis for patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Immunogenic neoantigens recognized by T cell receptor (TCR) can elicit effective immune responses against malignant cells with corresponding somatic mutations. To broaden the range of targeted treatments for AML, in this study, we explored the feasibility of immunotherapy targeting neoantigens arising from recurrent mutations, which are exclusively present on leukemic cells. We used data-driven methods to select seven neoantigens from four frequently mutated genes (NPM1, FLT3, TP53, and DNMT3A) associated with HLA-A*02:01-positive AML patients. Functional assays demonstrated that neoantigens derived from NPM1/W288fs, FLT3/D835H, and FLT3/D835Y were shown to induce specific T cell responses in AML patients. We further identified the specific TCR sequences from healthy donors capable of recognizing these neoantigens. In-depth studies of their specific T cells revealed the presence of dominant αβTCRs that could specifically recognize NPM1/W288fs and FLT3/D835H in an HLA-A*02:01-restricted manner. T cells engineered with each αβTCR selectively recognized and killed HLA-A*02:01-positive AML targets endogenously expressing corresponding mutations. Overall, our findings support the clinical translation of adoptive neoantigen-specific TCR-engineered T cells as a novel therapeutic strategy for treating AML.Subject terms: Acute myeloid leukaemia, Immunotherapy, Cancer immunotherapy

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is the most common type of leukemia in adults [1]. Standard treatment for AML typically comprises induction and intensive chemotherapy followed by hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) [2]. Although most patients initially respond to these therapies, refractory and relapse occur frequently, leading to an extremely poor prognosis [3, 4]. This poor prognosis is mainly due to the accumulation of genetic mutations in malignant myeloid precursor cells [5–7]. Molecular landscape studies reveal that AML carries an average of 10 − 13 coding mutations [8, 9]. Several small-molecule inhibitors targeting FLT3-ITD and mutant IDH1 are approved for the treatment of AML [10, 11]. However, not all recurrent genomic alterations have effective inhibitors, and drug resistance remains a significant issue due to the limited duration of therapeutic effect. Development of novel treatments to eradicate AML cells by targeting more mutations is therefore urgently required.

Recent research has highlighted the potential of immunotherapy against neoantigens that derived from somatic mutations. Neoantigens are exclusively expressed in malignant cells, making them more immunogenic and less likely to induce off-tumor toxicity [12]. The recognition of neoantigen by T-cell receptor (TCR) triggers specific immune response. TCR-engineered T cells targeting neoantigens have been demonstrated to induce persistent immune responses, leading to durable tumor regression [13]. Growing data shows that this approach broadens the range of targeted therapies. Indeed, T cell-mediated responses targeting various mutations, e.g., FLT3-ITD, NPM1c, IDH2R140Q, and PML‑RARA, have been detected in AML patients, indicating the potential of neoantigen-based T-cell therapy for AML [14, 15].

In this study, we combined a data-driven approach with immunogenicity analysis to identify shared neoantigens from recurrent hotspot mutations of AML, and demonstrated T-cell responses against these neoantigens in patients. We successfully isolated neoantigen-specific TCRs from healthy donors, and demonstrated the efficacy of these TCR-engineered T cells against AML cells carrying the corresponding mutations. This study indicated that T cells engineered with neoantigen-specific TCRs hold potential for clinical application in AML treatment.

Materials and Methods

Further information can be found in the Supplemental Methods.

Human sample and cell lines

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected from AML patients and healthy donors, and isolated by Ficoll-Isopaque separation and cryopreserved. PBMCs from healthy donors were used to generated dendritic cells (DCs), neoantigen-specific T cells, and TCR-engineered T cells, as described below. Marrow infiltrating lymphocytes (MILs) were isolated from bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMCs) of AML patients, as described below.

The commercial cell line T2 was purchased from ATCC. The cell lines OCI-AML3, THP-1, and U293T were maintained in our laboratory previously. OCI-AML3 cells transfected with FLT3/D835H were established and maintained in our laboratory, and refereed as OCI-AML3FLT3/D835H.

Data-driven analysis for potential neoantigens

High-throughput sequencing was performed to identify non-synonymous mutations. HLA class I alleles were determined by OptiType [16]. NetMHCpan 4.1 was used to predict immunogenic neopeptides [17]. The structural modeling of HLA-A*02:01 molecule in complex with neopeptides was constructed using AlphaFold and AutoDock CrankPep (ADCP) [18, 19]. Then the molecular dynamic simulations are performed using GROMACS version 2023.3 [20].

Detection of neopeptide-specific T cells in AML patients

The in vivo neopeptide-specific T cells were detected with tetramers labeled with two different fluorochromes to increase specificity and thereby sensitivity. PBMCs and BMMCs of AML patients were incubated with dasatinib to improve tetramer staining. CD8+ T cells were enriched and stained with indicated tetramers and analyzed in an LSR Fortessa cytometer (BD).

Generation of neopeptide-specific T cells from healthy donors

Induction of neoantigen-specific T cells was performed following the protocol we developed previously [21]. Briefly, CD8+ and CD14+ cells were isolated from PBMCs of HLA-A*02:01-positive healthy donors. CD14+ cells were cultured in fresh medium supplemented with GM-CSF, IL-4, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 and PGE2 to generate mature DCs. CD8+ T cells were co-cultured with neopeptide-pulsed DCs in fresh medium supplemented with IL-21, IL-7, and IL-15. Specific T cells were sorted with neopeptide-HLA tetramer and subjected to the following TCR sequencing analysis.

Construction of TCR-engineered T cells

TCR sequencing was performed using the methods described previously [22]. Obtained sequence reads were analyzed using the pipeline that we established previously. Full-length TCRA and TCRB sequences with modified mouse constant regions, linked by a furinSGSGP2A linker, were synthesized and cloned into a pLVX-EF1α-IRES lentiviral expression vector. PBMCs from healthy donors were pre-stimulated with cytokine cocktail, and then lentivirally transduced with TCR-expressing lentiviral vector. After rapid expansion using IL-7, IL-15, and IL-2, the TCR-engineered T cells were harvested and subject to further functional validation.

Results

Data-driven selection of neoantigen candidates for primary AML patients

Candidate neoantigens were selected using data-driven methods, including identification and binding affinity prediction of neopeptides, evaluation of HLA/neopeptide co-occurrences in AML cohorts, and 3D structure prediction and docking analysis (Fig. 1A). Due to its relatively high prevalence, HLA-A*02:01 was chosen to identify neopeptides in the following studies.

First, we utilized RNA-seq and a targeted DNA panel containing 100 common driver genes to analyze the hotspot mutations in AML patients. Bone marrow samples from 10 HLA-A*02:01-positive individuals with newly diagnosed AML were included. A total of 30 nonsynonymous mutations from 19 different genes were identified (Table 1). Among them, hotspot mutations in four genes, i.e., NPM1, FLT3, TP53, and DNMT3A, were detected (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, NetMHCpan 4.1 was used to predict the binding affinity of neopeptides covering substituted amino acid to the HLA-A*02:01 molecule. Seven neopeptides arising from NPM1/W288fs, FLT3/D835H, FLT3/D835Y, FLT3/N676K, DNMT3A/R899H, TP53/G266V, and TP53/H193H were found to exhibit strong affinities (ranked at %Rank ≤ 2) to HLA-A*02:01 (Table 2).

Candidate neopeptides with affinity to HLA-A*02:01.

Second, the co-occurrences of HLA-A*02:01/neopeptide of these seven neopeptides were evaluated in a combined AML dataset of 973 patients from three AML cohorts: the Ruijin Hospital datasets, aggregating both mutation and HLA class-1 annotation for 323 AML cases; the TCGA, containing data on 176 patients; and the Beat AML dataset for 474 patients. As shown in Fig. 1C, these seven neopeptides were recurring in the combined AML dataset, which is consistent with observations from the initial 10 AML patients. This suggests that immunotherapy targeting these shared neopeptides could bring considerable benefit to such patients. Consequently, these neopeptides were chosen for further studies.

Third, we utilized AlphaFold to predict the 3D structure of each neopeptide. The side chains of neopeptides were threaded onto the backbone of a previously resolved crystal structure of an NY-ESO-1 epitope in complex with HLA-A2 (PDB id: 1S9W) and sampled conformations by molecular docking using ADCP. Similar to the IMP positive peptide, each neopeptide displayed a strong affinity for the HLA-A*02:01 protein, as shown by the calculated Gibbs free energy of binding (ΔG binding) (Fig. S1A), indicating a consistent interaction between the candidate neopeptides and the HLA-A*02:01 molecule. The 3D graphical representation of the interaction was shown by Pymol in Fig. S1. We further verified the interaction of each neopeptide with HLA-A*02:01 by molecular dynamic simulation using GROMACS. Hydrogen bond analysis revealed that hydrogen binding between neoepitope and HLA-A*02:01 was mainly located at position 1, position 2 and the C-terminus. The middle residues, on the other hand, formed fewer hydrogen bonds with the HLA receptor and their side chains were therefore providing possibility for TCR binding (Fig. S2).

Experimental confirmation of the HLA-binding affinity and dissociation of neoantigens

To verify the binding capacity of these seven candidate neopeptides to the HLA-A*02:01 molecule in vitro, two different assays were performed: an UV-mediated peptide exchange-based and a T2 cell-based assay. The efficiency of peptide exchange in the UV-mediated approach was assessed by an ELISA assay, and the signal was normalized using the IMP positive control. Among the tested neopeptides, four of them, including NeoNPM1/W288fs (AIQDLCLAV), NeoFLT3/D835H (HIMSDSNYV), NeoFLT3/D835Y (YIMSDSNYV), and NeoTP53/H193D (GLAPPQDLI), displayed a strong binding affinity to the HLA-A*02:01 molecule, as shown by more than 70% of positive signal in the ELISA assay. Similarly, these four neopeptides exhibited a similar binding affinity for HLA-A*02:01 as the IMP positive control in the T2-binding assay, with the fluorescence indexes were between 1.0 and 1.5 (Fig. 1D).

The capacity of an antigen to present properly and elicit an immunogenic response in T cells may be reflected in the stability of the complex between an antigen and the HLA-A*02:01 molecule. A T2 cell-based assay was utilized to evaluate the stability of the pHLA-A*02:01 complexes formed of these four neopeptides. As shown in Fig. 1E, the dissociation complex50 (DC50) of NeoTP53/H193D was longer than 8 h, with the remaining percentages of pHLA-A*02:01 complexes at 8 h being 62.57%. The DC50 of NeoNPM1/W288fs, NeoFLT3/D835H, and NeoFLT3/D835Y were 7.33, 7.52, and 7.57 h, respectively. The stable complexes suggest that these four neopeptides are poised for interaction with TCRs on T-cell surface, supporting their differential immunogenicity toward the tested mutations.

Persistence and dynamics of neoantigen-reactive T cells in AML patients

Next, we identify the presence of neoantigen-specific T cells in AML patients to validate the natural immune responses toward the tested mutations. The precursor frequencies of circulating and bone marrow-infiltrating CD8+ T cells were measured with neopeptide-specific tetramers labeled with two different fluorochromes.

First, CD8+ T cells were isolated from PBMCs with immune beads and tested using APC- and PE-labeled HLA-A*02:01-tetramers. Circulating T cells specific to NeoNPM1/W288fs were found in three newly diagnosed AML patients harboring NPM1/W288fs (AML001, AML002, and AML003) with the frequencies ranging from 0.018% to 0.026% (Fig. 2A). CD8+ T cells specific for NeoFLT3/D835Y were detected in blood of patients AML006 with frequencies of 0.04% (Fig. 2A). These results indicate that these two neopeptides are directly recognized by the immune system of AML patients carrying corresponding mutations, in the context of HLA-A*02:01. Nevertheless, the presence of NeoTP53/H193D-specific T cells could not be detected in patient AML010 due to a lack of sufficient samples.

Second, we assessed the presence of neoantigen-specific T cells within the bone marrow microenvironment of AML patients. As shown in Fig. 2B, in the absence of any treatment, NeoNPM1/W288fs-specific CD8+ T cells were detectable in the MILs of AML001 (0.021%) and AML002 (0.014%), and patient AML006 had CD8+ T cells (0.013%) reactive to NeoFLT3/D835H, indicating a sustained immunological response against leukemia within the bone marrow.

Third, to comprehensively assess the dynamics of neoantigen-specific T cell frequencies throughout the disease course, we collected consecutive peripheral blood samples from AML004 carrying FLT3/D835H at three distinct time points: initial diagnosis, complete remission (CR) following chemotherapy, and post-allogeneic HSCT. As shown in Fig. 2B, the frequency of NeoFLT3/D835H-specific CD8+ T cells was higher during the CR stage (0.63%) compared to the initial diagnosis (0.013%), indicating enhanced immune clearance by T cells against leukemic cells carrying FLT3/D835H during the lower tumor burden stage. Of note, this patient subsequently underwent HSCT with an HLA-A*02:01-haploidentical family donor and maintained CR. The NeoFLT3/D835H-specific T cells were retained in circulating T cells at a frequency of 0.065% one year after HSCT, suggesting the advantage of neoantigen-specific T cells for leukemia-free survival. Likewise, CD8+ T cells against this neopeptide were also observed in circulating T cells of another patient carrying FLT3/D835H (AML005) during the CR stage (0.013%). These findings underscore the potential utility of neoantigen-specific T cells as a therapeutic tool throughout the course of leukemia therapy.

Notably, neopeptide-specific T cells were hardly detectable in either circulating or bone marrow-infiltrating lymphocytes when analyzing HLA-A*02:01-positive acute myeloid leukemia (AML) patients who lacked the mutations NPM1/W288fs, FLT3/D835H, or FLT3/D835Y, as quantified by the cognate tetramer staining (Fig. S3). This observation suggests that the immunogenicity of leukemia-specific neoantigens may be strongly dependent on the presence of these recurrent mutational signatures.

Additionally, to further confirm the T-cell immunoreactivity of AML patients against these neopeptides, an IFN-γ ELISPOT assay was performed. CD8+ T cells isolated from the peripheral blood of AML patients were co-incubated overnight with mitomycin C-inactivated T2 cells pulsed with individual neopeptide or corresponding wild-type peptide, in the presence of IL-2 (5 U/mL) and anti-CD28 antibody (1 μg/mL). PHA and DMSO were used as the positive and vehicle control, respectively. The spots counting showed that consistent with the tetramer staining results, significant IFN-γ secretion was induced by NeoNPM1/W288fs, NeoFLT3/D835H, and NeoFLT3/D835Y in T cells isolated from patient AML002, AML004, and AML006, respectively (Fig. 2C).

Taken together, these results indicate that among the seven synthesized neopeptides, NeoNPM1/W288fs, NeoFLT3/D835H, and NeoFLT3/D835Y, were highly immunogenic and are anticipated to be recognized by HLA-A*02:01-restricted T cells. We then continued our investigations on these three neopeptides.

Isolation of neoepitopes specific TCRs from HLA-A*02:01 healthy donors

Isolating an adequate number of neopeptide-specific T cells directly from AML patients is extremely challenging due to the disease-related immunological dysfunction and lymphopenia. Given that mutation-specific T cells have been demonstrated to exist in healthy individuals, and that we and other groups have successfully identified antigen-specific T cells from healthy donors previously [21, 22]. We therefore induced neopeptide-specific T cells from PBMCs of HLA-A*02:01-positive healthy donors. After the co-cultivation with autologous DCs that were pulsed with neopeptide or not, CD8+ T cells were harvested and subject to IFN-γ ELISPOT to assess the immune response. NeoNPM1/W288fs and NeoFLT3/D835H demonstrated a significant increased capacity to induce IFN-γ secretion from CD8+ T cells when co-cultured with neopeptide-pulsed DCs, as compared to DCs that were not pulsed (Fig. 3A). We then searched for neopeptide-specific T cells using HLA-A*02:01-tetramer complexes. The proportions of CD8+ Tetramer+ T cells were 0.12% (4765 cells) and 0.37% (3799 cells) for NeoNPM1/W288fs and NeoFLT3/D835H, respectively (Fig. 3B).

To characterize the neopeptide-induced T-cell rearrangements, total RNA was extracted from sorted CD8+ Tetramer+ T cells and performed TCRα and TCRβ sequencing with a previously established procedure [22]. The analysis of TCR repertoires revealed that dominant TCRA and TCRB sequences were observed in each neopeptide. The most frequent TCRA identified for NeoNPM1/W288fs and NeoFLT3/D835H were TRAV17−TRAJ54 (65.35%) and TRAV29−TRAJ34 (100%); and the dominant TCRB sequences were TRBV5-6 − TRBJ2-7 (50.37%) and TRBV27−TRBJ2-1 (45.55%), respectively (Fig. 3B). Additionally, no dominant TCRA and TCRB sequences were observed in response to NeoFLT3/D835Y with the detection of 1.21% (3221 cells) of CD8+ Tetramer+ T cells (data not shown).

Construction of TCR-engineered T cells and validation of their recognition toward neoantigens of AML

The dominant TCR was nearly or more than 50%, suggesting the stimulation of neopeptide expanded these T cells and T cells with these TCRA and TCRB pairs would be neoantigen-specific. To verify the functional potency of the identified TCRs, we constructed chimeric TCRs by replacing the constant domains of both TCRA and TCRB chains with modified murine counterparts, while retaining the human variable domains (Fig. 4A). The NeoNPM1/W288fs- and NeoFLT3/D835H-specific TCR (referred to as TCRNPM1/W288fs and TCRFLT3/D835H, respectively) were successfully transduced into T cells from healthy donors via lentiviral vectors. As shown in Fig. 4B, TCRNPM1/W288fs and TCRFLT3/D835H-engineered T cells bound to the HLA-A*02:01-tetramer loaded with the corresponding neopeptides, suggesting the mutation-specificity of these TCR-T cells. The immune function of these TCR-engineered T cells was then assessed using a range of biological experiments.

First, ELISA assays showed significant IFN-γ and granzyme B secretion in TCRNPM1/W288fs- and TCRFLT3/D835H-engineered T cells in a dose-dependent manner using T2 cells loaded with each neopeptide as target cells (Fig. 4C). In contrast, no responses were observed for the wild-type counterparts.

Second, TCR-engineered T cells were assessed for cytotoxic potential using an LDH-release assay after co-culturing with target cells. TCRNPM1/W288fs- and TCRFLT3/D835H-transfected T cells specifically lysed T2 cells loaded with NeoNPM1/W288fs and NeoFLT3/D835H, respectively, across a range of effector/target ratios. In contrast, they had less impact on T2 cells loaded with wild-type counterparts (Fig. 4D). These results were consistent with the findings of the cytokine release assays, confirming the neoantigen-specific reactivity of the tested TCRs.

TCR-engineered T cells kill neoantigen presenting AML cell lines and primary AML cells

Next, we determined the responses of our constructed TCR-engineered T cells to AML cells that endogenously process and present neoantigens on their cell surfaces with HLA molecules. The examinations were performed on AML cell lines and primary cells, respectively.

First, to determine the target specificity of the TCR-T cells, OCI-AML3 cells (NPM1/W288fs, HLA-A*02:01+) were employed as positive targets for T cells engineered with TCRNPMI/W288fs since they naturally carried NPM1/W288fs, whereas the THP-1 cells (NPM1/W288wt, HLA-A*02:01+) were used as a negative control. On the other hand, we transfected FLT3/D835H mutation into OCI-AML3 cells (FLT3/D835wt), and constructed the cell line OCI-AML3FLT3/D835H. The original OCI-AML3 cells were used as negative control in this context. The ELISA and LDH-release assays were then performed to assess the specific functional activity of TCR-engineered T cells against these AML cell lines.

TCRNPM1/W288fs-engineered T cells showed high levels of IFN-γ and granzyme B when cocultured with OCI-AML3 cells, compared to THP-1 cells (Fig. 5A). Similarly, OCI-AML3FLT3/D835H cells induced significant secretion of IFN-γ and granzyme B by TCRFLT3/D835H-engineered T cells, compared to primary OCI-AML3 cells (Fig. 5A; left and middle plots). Additionally, T cells transfected with TCRNPM1/W288fs or TCRFLT3/D835H exhibited significant killing activity against OCI-AML3 or OCI-AML3FLT3/D835H cells, while had no impact on their wild-type counterparts, as demonstrated by the LDH-release assay (Fig. 5A; right plot).

Second, to further confirm the cytolytic character of these TCR-transduced T cells, blasts from HLA-A*02:01 positive patients AML003 (NPM1/W288fs) and AML004 (FLT3/D835H) were used as targets cells. Additionally, three controls were incorporated: (i) carrying the same mutations but HLA-A*02:01 negative (AML011 and AML012), (ii) HLA-A*02:01 positive but without the tested mutations (AML013), and (iii) neither HLA-A*02:01 positive nor carrying the tested mutations (AML014).

The tested TCRNPM1/W288fs- and TCRFLT3/D835H-T cells significantly secreted IFN-γ and granzyme B in response to stimulation with HLA-A*02:01+ primary AML samples harboring NPM1/W288fs (AML003) and FLT3/D835H (AML004) mutation, respectively, at 20:1 effector/target ratio, whereas no response was observed when encountered AML blasts lacking the same mutations (AML013) or having the different HLA genotypes (AML011 and AML012; Fig. 5B; left and middle plots). Furthermore, these TCR-T cells lysed HLA-A*02:01 primary AML samples carrying the respective mutations (AML003 and AML004) in LDH-release assays at the 10:1 effector/target ratio, but showed minimal lysis against AML cells lacking either the identical mutations (AML013) or restricting HLA (AML011 and AML012; Fig. 5B; right plot). Patient AML014 who did not carry HLA-A*02:01 and tested mutations were not recognized by these TCR-T cells either. These results suggest that the anti-leukemia effects of the tested TCR-engineered T cells are HLA-A*02:01-restricted and mutation-specific.

Third, the in vivo efficacy of TCR-engineered T cells was evaluated in an immunodeficient NSG model engrafted with luciferase-labeled OCI-AML3FLT3/D835H cells. Mice were either left untreated or administered a single dose of 5 × 106 TCRFLT3/D835H-engineered T cells on day 9 after tumor inoculation (n = 10 per group). Treatment with TCRFLT3/D835H-T cells demonstrated a pronounced anti-leukemic effect, leading to significantly improved overall survival compared to untreated controls (Fig. 5C, left). Subsequent analysis of bone marrow and spleen samples collected a week after TCR-T cells infusion (n = 3 per group) revealed that single-cell suspensions from TCRFLT3/D835H-T cell-treated mice exhibited a marked increase in mTCR-positive T-cell infiltration alongside a significant reduction in leukemia burden relative to untreated mice (Fig. 5C, middle and right).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that NeoNPM1/W288fs and NeoFLT3/D835H are processed and presented on the AML cell surface through binding to HLA-A*02:01, rendering them susceptible to targeted elimination by primary T cells engineered with TCRNPM1/W288fs and TCRFLT3/D835H, respectively. Furthermore, TCRFLTD/D835H-engineered T cells exhibit potent in vivo cytotoxicity against AML cells, significantly prolonging overall survival in treated mice. These results suggest that adoptive T cell therapy using neoantigen-specific TCR-T cells represents a viable therapeutic strategy for HLA-A*02:01+ AML patients who carry the identical mutations.

Functional validation of TCR-T cells generated from AML patients

To investigate the feasibility of generating functional neopeptide-specific TCR-T cells directly from AML patients, we engineered AML patient T cells by lentiviral transduction with TCRNPM1/W288fs or TCRFLT3/D835H and subsequently evaluated their cytotoxic potential. Flow cytometric analysis using PE-conjugated mTCR antibody confirmed successful surface expression of both TCR constructs in transduced T cells, with proper heterodimer formation demonstrated by specific binding to HLA-A*02:01-tetramers (Fig. 6A).

Furthermore, functional characterization revealed that TCRNPM1/W288fs-engineered T cells exhibited potent and specific cytotoxicity against both NeoNPM1/W288fs-pulsed T2 cells and the OCI-AML3 cell lines that endogenously expressing NPM1/W288fs, while demonstrating no detectable activity against wild-type NPM1-expressing T2 cells or THP-1 cells (Fig. 6B, D). Similarly, TCRFLT3/D835H-engineered T cells selectively lysed target cells presenting NeoFLT3/D835H, including neopeptide-pulsed T2 cells and the genetically modified OCI-AML3FLT3/D835H cell line, but showed no cross-reactivity with wild-type counterparts (Fig. 6C, E).

These findings provide experimental evidence that patient-derived T cells can be successfully engineered to express neopeptide-specific TCRs with stringent neoantigen recognition and potent antitumor activity, supporting their clinical translation for adoptive T-cell therapy in AML.

Discussion

Neoantigens represent a promising avenue for immunotherapy, broadly classified as private or shared based on their origins and immunogenic properties. While private neoantigens offer tumor-specific targeting, their patient-restricted nature presents challenges for therapeutic development [23, 24]. In contrast, shared neoantigens arising from recurrent driver mutations demonstrated higher population prevalence and stronger association with oncogenic pathways, making them attractive targets for broader clinical application [15, 25–29]. Our study focused on AML, which exhibits a relatively low mutational burden with clustering of somatic aberrations in hotspot genes [8]. Through systematic evaluation of recurrent AML mutations, including NPM1/W288fs, FLT3-TKD variants (D835H, D835Y, and N676K), and TP53 mutations (G266V, H193D), we identified four neopeptides demonstrating high-affinity binding to HLA-A*02:01 and forming stable complexes efficiently recognized by specific TCRs. The detection of neopeptide-specific T cells in both circulating T cells and MILs across disease stages supports the therapeutic potential of targeting these shared neoantigens.

The development of effective immunotherapies for AML has been hindered by limited target availability, particularly for CAR-T cell approaches [30]. While neoantigens present promising targets for T-cell-based therapies, their predominantly intracellular localization necessitates TCR-mediated recognition through HLA presentation [13]. Leveraging the diverse TCR repertoire of healthy donors, we successfully isolated TCRs specific for shared AML neoantigens (i.e., NeoNPM1/W288fs and NeoFLT3/D835H). Functional characterization confirmed that engineered T cells expressing these TCRs selectively lysed mutation-bearing AML cells in an HLA-A*02:01-restricted manner.

NPM1c mutations represent early driver events in leukemogenesis and are present in approximately 30% of adult AML cases. The majority (>80%) of these mutations occur adjacent to the Trp288 codon, such as c.860_863dup observed in our patients AML001 and AML003. Our study also included the less frequent c.863_864insCTTG variant identified in AML002. These mutations generate C-terminal frameshift peptides (aa. CLAVEEVSLRK), from which multiple neopeptides, including CLAVEEVSL, AIQDLCLAV, LAVEEVSL, and DLCLAVEEV, have been identified with strong binding affinity to different HLA molecules [25, 31]. Of particular clinical relevance, we detected CD8+ T cells specific for AIQDLCLAV not only in peripheral circulation but also within MILs, providing direct evidence of neoantigen-reactive T cells in the leukemic microenvironment. This extends previous reports by demonstrating active immune recognition of NPM1c neoantigens at the disease site. Recent advances in NPM1c-targeted immunotherapy included the development of CLAVEEVSL-specific αβTCRs and CAR-T constructs targeting AIQDLCLAV/HLA-A2 [25, 32]. Our study contributes a novel αβTCR, TCRNPM1/W288fs, isolated from healthy donors and specific for the AIQDLCLAV neopeptide in HLA-A*02:01-positive contexts. The identification of this high-affinity TCR against a shared neoantigen represents a potentially broadly applicable treatment strategy for NPM1-mutated AML.

Of particular note, we identified and functionally validated the first TCR specific for the FLT3/D835H-derived neoepitope. FLT3-TKD mutations, particularly D835H and D835Y, are associated with treatment resistance and adverse prognosis in AML [33]. While previous work has described TCRs targeting FLT3/D835Y [34], our study makes two key contributions: first, we documented endogenous T-cell responses against both FLT3/D835H and FLT3/D835Y in AML patients; second, we identified TCRFLT3/D835H, the first αβTCR specific for the FLT3/D835H-derived neopeptide (HIMSDSNYV). This newly characterized TCR expands the repertoire of targetable FLT3 mutations and provides a new therapeutic option for patients with these high-risk disease variants.

Off-target toxicity represents a major barrier to the clinical translation of TCR-based therapies, as several neoantigen-induced TCRs have shown cross-reactivity to wild-type peptides, thus posing substantial risks of autoimmune complications [22]. In our study, both TCRNPM1/W288fs and TCRFLT3/D835H exhibited exquisite specificity for their cognate neoantigens without detectable cross-reactivity to wild-type counterparts. This specificity stems from the inability of wild-type peptides to form stable complexes with the relevant HLA alleles, thereby preventing their surface presentation and subsequent TCR engagement. Such fundamental differences in HLA binding capacity between mutant and wild-type peptides provide a safety mechanism for clinical application.

Additionally, our study highlights the feasibility of developing neoantigen-specific TCR-T cell therapies for AML using both healthy donor- and patient-derived autologous T cells. We demonstrated that primary T cells from AML patients can be successfully engineered to express TCRs targeting recurrent mutations, exhibiting potent and specific reactivity against neopeptide-pulsed targets and mutation-bearing AML cell lines comparable to healthy donor T cells. These findings support two complementary therapeutic strategies: allogeneic TCR-T cells for off-the-shelf applications, particularly in post-transplant settings, and autologous approaches that avoid graft-versus-host disease risks while maintaining broad patient applicability. The preserved functionality of patient-derived T cells suggests that AML-associated immune dysfunction does not preclude their use in adoptive cell therapy. This work advances the field by validating multiple clinically relevant approaches for targeting shared neoantigens in AML, with important implications for developing personalized immunotherapies. Future studies should explore combination strategies to enhance therapeutic efficacy in the bone marrow microenvironment.

Identifying neoantigen-specific TCRs presents significant technical hurdles. Current tetramer-based single-cell cloning methods are labor-intensive, while single-cell sequencing alternatives demand complex bioinformatics pipelines [25, 35]. We successfully isolated functional TCRs against NeoNPM1/W288fs and NeoFLT3/D835H by analyzing dominant TCR clones from tetramer-sorted CD8+ T cells, though failure to detect dominant TCRs for NeoFLT3/D835Y responses confirms the known disparity between clonal abundance and functional activity, highlighting the necessity for better TCR discovery tools. Computational prediction platforms [36], including our integrated analysis system [37], show potential to streamline discovery by supplementing experimental approaches and minimizing extensive cell sorting.

In conclusion, our study establishes a comprehensive framework for developing neoantigen-specific TCR therapies for AML, addressing key challenges from target identification to therapeutic validation. The isolation of high-affinity TCRs against shared neoantigens derived from clinically relevant driver mutations provides multiple translation-ready options for personalized immunotherapy. Future studies should focus on combinatorial approaches to overcome microenvironmental suppression and enhance therapeutic persistence, while expanding the repertoire of targetable neoantigens across diverse HLA types to benefit broader patient populations. These advances collectively represent significant progress toward effective TCR-based immunotherapies for AML, with important implications for treating other low-mutation burden malignancies.
