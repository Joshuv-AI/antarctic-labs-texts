Tumor-educated platelet RNA as a diagnostic biomarker for ground-glass opacity-related lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Mengqi Shao, Jieming Cao, Ruoyao Wang, Yazhuo Liu, Yan Hu, Weixuan Lei, Li Wang, Wenliang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGround-glass opacities (GGOs) are a common computed tomography (CT) imaging feature in various pulmonary diseases, including early-stage lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). The investigation of tumor-specific biomarkers for the diagnosis of early LUAD holds significant clinical importance. The aim of this study was to integrate highly specific tumor-educated platelet (TEP)-derived biomarkers to optimize preoperative evaluation.MethodsAfter identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs), six machine learning methods were applied to the sequencing results of cohort 1, generating a DEG-TEP data set. The DEGs in cohort 2 were considered to be the DEG-TISSUES data set. The intersection of these two gene sets was designated as the intersection-17 data set. Validation of the genes in intersection-17 was conducted in cohort 3 (tissues) and cohort 4 (platelets) via real-time reverse-transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). si-AHNAK cell lines were established in PC9 and H1299 cell lines to validate the role of AHNAK in LUAD in vitro. Finally, a single-gene gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out for AHNAK.ResultsA total of 7,753 DEGs were identified in cohort 1. After machine learning was applied, the DEG-TEP data set, consisting of 108 DEGs, was established, while the DEG-TISSUES data set contained 1,909 genes. The RT-qPCR results in cohort 3 indicated that the messenger RNA expression of AHNAK, LAD1, and TNFRSF17 in tissues was consistent with the sequencing results. In cohort 4, only AHNAK exhibited differential expression, with an area under receiver operative characteristic curve of 0.78 (95% confidence interval: 0.61–0.95; P<0.01). We found that AHNAK has a suppressive effect on the proliferation, invasion, and metastatic abilities of LUAD. The single-gene GSEA was applied to preliminarily characterize the biological functions of AHNAK in tumors, revealing that AHNAK is predominantly enriched in ribosome-related pathways.ConclusionsWe found that AHNAK is significantly downregulated in tumor tissues and TEPs and that the low expression of AHNAK promotes the proliferation, invasion, and metastatic abilities of tumor cells. We further discovered that AHNAK had a low expression in the tissues and TEPs of patients with LUAD presenting as GGOs, and we further validated its diagnostic efficacy and in vitro tumor-suppressive effects. The downregulation in AHNAK may be associated with changes in the ribosomal pathway, affecting the malignancy of tumors.Keywords: AHNAK, tumor-educated platelet (TEP), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), ground-glass opacities (GGOs), biomarker

Highlight box

Key findings

• The findings suggest that AHNAK may serve as a tumor-educated platelet (TEP) biomarker to facilitate the development of the early and noninvasive molecular individualized diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) with ground-glass opacities (GGOs) manifestations; it may exert a suppressive effect on the proliferation, invasion, and metastatic abilities of LUAD.

What is known and what is new?

• Imaging characteristics cannot accurately distinguish between benign and malignant GGOs. The use of TEPs is an effective means of detecting the condition of tumors. AHNAK has been reported in the literature to be related to the tumor progression.

• In our study, the expression level of AHNAK was significantly decreased in both the TEPs and tissues of patients with LUAD. AHNAK expression demonstrated good diagnostic efficacy for LUAD, with the downregulation of AHNAK in tumor cells being associated with tumor progression. The downregulation of AHNAK affects the ribosome pathway and thus contributes to the malignant progression of tumors.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• AHNAK has the potential to be a tumor marker and be used to assist clinicians in distinguishing between benign and malignant GGOs; its role in LUAD should be investigated further.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, and its high mortality rate is closely associated with a late diagnosis. In recent years, low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) has been widely adopted for the early screening of lung cancer (1). Ground-glass opacities (GGOs) in CT images often present diagnostic challenges to clinicians due to their potential association with a variety of conditions, including benign lung diseases and early-stage lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) (2,3). LUAD is the most prevalent subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), accounting for approximately 40% of all lung cancer cases (4,5). The survival rate in patients with lung cancer varies significantly across different clinical stages, and early diagnosis and prompt intervention can substantially improve patient prognosis (6). Consequently, investigating new molecular targets and biomarkers, particularly in the context of early-stage LUAD with GGO imaging manifestations, holds substantial clinical significance.

The most accurate method for assessing a tumor is biopsy; however, this approach can be traumatic for patients and often results in poor patient compliance. Furthermore, a single biopsy may not fully capture the genetic information of cancer. In this context, liquid biopsy, as a minimally invasive procedure, can effectively address these issues (7). Tumor patients frequently exhibit thrombocytosis (8); additionally, tumor cells can directly or indirectly stimulate platelets, which results in splicing events within the megakaryocyte-derived pre-messenger RNA (mRNA) found in circulating platelets, this RNA originates from megakaryocyte processing and undergoes splicing within platelets, leading to the formation of tumor-educated platelets (TEPs) (9). Platelets also carry genetic information secreted by tumors in the form of exosomes (10), and these pathways collectively contribute to the molecular reprogramming of TEPs. A study has established that TEP-RNA profiles can be used to develop effective diagnostic models and predict treatment responses in patients with tumor (11). In a study on NSCLC, TEP RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data were successfully converted into images to create the imPlatelet classifier, capable of accurately identifying NSCLC cases with a validation accuracy reaching 100% (12). Despite evidence supporting the role of TEPs in NSCLC, their function and mechanisms within the context of LUAD remain insufficiently explored.

AHNAK is a tumor-suppressor gene, and the protein it encodes is a very large protein (700 kDa) (13). The AHNAK-encoded protein is localized in the cytoplasm, cell nucleus, and plasma membrane (14). Given the extensive subcellular localization of AHNAK, it functions as a large scaffolding protein characterized by numerous binding sites, enabling it to interact with proteins to form a variety of complexes (15). AHNAK is involved in various physiological activities; for instance, it interacts with L-type Ca2+ channels to maintain Ca2+ balance (16) and participates in T-cell differentiation (17). Moreover, some reports have indicated that AHNAK is associated with the growth signaling pathways of tumor cells (18-20). Additionally, AHNAK has been implicated in the invasion and metastasis of tumor cells (21-23). In this study, we sought to validate AHNAK as a biomarker for TEPs to optimize preoperative assessment. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2025-747/rc).

Methods

Inclusion criteria and sample collection

The inclusion criteria for patients enrolled in this study were as follows: (I) age 18–80 years; (II) high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) showing GGO <3.0 cm in diameter; (III) consent to undergo surgery; (IV) availability of postoperative pathological diagnosis; and (V) signed informed consent; (VI) RNA integrity indicator RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥7. Meanwhile, the exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) history of other tumors; (II) combination with tuberculosis, hepatitis, or other blood diseases; (III) preoperative immunotherapy; and (IV) for cohorts 2 and 3, the presence of benign nodules.

Whole blood samples (10 mL) of patients with GGOs were collected from The Second Xiangya Hospital (2022–2024) and stored at 4 ℃ for static preparation. After pathological examination, patients were divided into an inflammatory-GGO group and a LUAD-GGO group (cohort 1). After surgery, tumors and adjacent normal tissues from 30 patients in the LUAD-GGO group (cohort 2) were collected for RNA-seq. All tissue samples were rapidly frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen. Subsequently, paired validation was conducted on 20 pairs of tumor and adjacent tissues from patients in the LUAD-GGO group (cohort 3), and platelet samples from 33 patients with GGOs (19 patients with LUAD and 14 patients with inflammation in cohort 4) were collected for validation. The flowchart of the study procedure is presented in Figure 1.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (No. 2020-107). All data and specimens were collected with the informed consent of the patients, who signed the relevant consent form prior to participation.

Platelet isolation

Platelets were isolated from whole blood within 48 hours after collecting the sample via two-step centrifugation. The first centrifugation was conducted at 120 ×g at 4 ℃ for 20 minutes to obtain platelet-rich plasma (upper layer); the second centrifugation was conducted at 360 ×g at 4 ℃ for 20 minutes to obtain platelets (sediment). The platelets were carefully kept in 30 µL of RNAlater (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

RNA extraction, complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, and platelet RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from isolated platelets and tissues with an RNA extraction kit (DP419; Tiangen, Beijing, China), and then SMARTScribe Reverse Transcriptase (639538; Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan) was used to reverse transcribe RNA into cDNA. All procedures were conducted according to the recommendations of the respective manufacturers. RNA-seq was conducted on the NovaSeq sequencing platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). RNA-seq library preparation, sequencing procedures, and raw fast-quality (FASTQ) data analysis were performed according to the standardized procedures.

Cell lines and culture

Human NSCLC cells NCI-H1299 [cat. no. CRL-5803; American Type Culture Collection (ATCC)] and PC9 (cat. no. SCSP-5085 China Center for Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences; Wuhan, China) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (BioChannel, Bury St. Edmunds, UK) at 37 ℃ in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. All cells were authenticated using mycoplasma detection and DNA fingerprinting.

Plasmids and cell transfection

For the knockdown experiments, three independent small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were used in two different cell lines to generate knockdown cell models, respectively. si-AHNAK-knockdown plasmids were constructed via molecular cloning. The sequences of siRNAs targeting AHNAK were designed on the siDirect website (https://sidirect2.rnai.jp//) and were as follows: AHNAK-siRNA#1, GCCUGACGUGGAGCUAAAUUUTT; AHNAK-siRNA#2, GCAUCCGAAGGCAGCAUUAAATT and AHNAK-siRNA#3, GUGCCAGAUGUUGACAUUAAATT. All oligonucleotide primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Plasmid transfection was performed with Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain assays

The real-time reverse-transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed with SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix (High Rox Plus) (Accurate Biolog, Changsha, China) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA and primer were mixed with SuperReal PreMix Plus Kits (Tiangen) to produce a 200 µL reaction mixture. The RT-qPCR analysis was conducted under cycling conditions of 30 seconds at 95 ℃ and subsequently 40 cycles of 5 seconds at 95 ℃ and 30 seconds at 60 ℃, followed by a melting curve. The primer pair sequences are listed in Table S1, and β-actin was used as an internal control. All oligonucleotide primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech. The average 2−ΔΔCt was used in unpaired samples (cohort 4), In the data processing of RT-qPCR for paired samples, the adjacent tissue was used as the control group. When calculating ΔΔCt, the value of all adjacent tissue was 0, resulting in no variance for adjacent tissue, which is unscientific. Therefore, the average 2−ΔCt was used in the data analysis of unpaired samples (cohort 3).

Immunoblotting

Total protein was isolated using WB/IP lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) with complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Protein supernatants were added to 4× protein sample loading buffer (Shanghai Wansheng Haotian Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), soaked in 70 ℃ water for 10 minutes, and subjected to 3–8% Tris-acetate gel (Shanghai Wansheng Haotian Biotechnology) electrophoresis. Subsequently, the protein bands were transferred to 0.45-µm polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Shanghai Wansheng Haotian Biotechnology) for 12 hours at a constant current of 20 V. Five-percent skim milk was used to block the membranes for 1 hour at room temperature. The protein bands were incubated with primary antibodies against AHNAK, including rabbit pAb (1:1,000 dilution) (16637-1-AP; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA) and β-actin mouse mAb (1:5,000 dilution) (66009-1-Ig; Proteintech), overnight at 4 ℃ and then incubated with secondary antibodies (1:5,000 dilution) (Proteintech) at 37 ℃ for 1 hour. Finally, the bands were detected via chemiluminescence.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8), colony formation, cell migration, and invasion assays

Cell proliferation and migration assays were performed when the cells were cultured in the logarithmic growth phase. In total, 1,000 cells were seeded into each well of a six-well plate, and after 1 week, aspirate the culture medium, wash with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fix with ethanol absolute for 30 minutes, and then stain with crystal violet (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 20 minutes, wash with PBS, photography for documentation after dry naturally. For the CCK8 assay, 1,000 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate, and CCK8 reagent (BeiJing DingGuochangSheng Biotech, China) was added every 24 hours. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Eastwin Scientific Equipment, Suzhou, China) to plot the cell growth, and the differences were statistically analyzed. Migration assays involved the addition of 1×105 cells to serum-free medium in the upper chamber, with 700 µL of complete medium being added to the lower chamber. After 24 hours, cells were observed via crystal violet staining. The procedures for staining and observation were the same as those in the above-mentioned cell colony formation assay. For the invasion experiment, 40 µL of Matrigel (Solarbio, Beijing, China) diluted with 2% serum-containing medium (dilution ratio of 1:4) was added before the cells were introduced into the upper chamber; subsequent experimental steps were the same as those in the migration assay.

Statistical analysis

The chi-squared test was used to examine the baseline characteristics. R package “DESeq2” was used to perform differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis on sequencing results, and multiple comparison correction was applied for DEG selection. Six machine learning methods, including Boruta, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), random forest, Rpart, support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVMrfe), and the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) algorithms, were used in this study. Boruta analysis utilizes the R package “Boruta” to calculate the z-scores of tumor-related factors in the model; XGBoost analysis employs the R package “xgboost” to compute gene gain values; Random forest analysis uses the R package “randomForest” to analyze gene IncMSE and IncNodePurity values; Rpart analysis applied the R package “rpart” to calculate the sum of “impurity reduction” for each gene across all split nodes; SVMrfe analysis utilizes the R package “e1071” to obtain information on DEGs at the model’s lowest error rate; and LASSO analysis uses the R package “glmnet” to identify genes significantly associated with LUAD. All these analyses identify and output results under the criteria of |log fold change (FC)| ≥1 and P value <0.05. These methods were applied to identify the significant features from the cohort 1 sequencing data. RT-qPCR validation of tumor tissues was conducted via paired t-tests, while RT-qPCR validation of platelets involved unpaired t-tests. RT-qPCR assays were performed with three technical replicates. DeLong’s test was used for statistical comparisons of the ROC curves. The online data analysis platform Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) was used to analyze the data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. Three RNA-seq datasets were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, and paired t-tests were performed. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were performed with the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) online analysis platform (https://cn.string-db.org/) (24). The Kaplan-Meier Plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=service) was used to evaluate the mRNA expression of AHNAK and the prognosis of patients with LUAD at different stages. Data analyses, image processing and visualization, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), were performed with GraphPad Prism v. 8.0 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA) and R v. 3.5.0. The baseline data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Cell counting was quantified with ImageJ v.1.52i (US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Moreover, each experiment for counting cell numbers involved three technical replicates. The P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We then applied the Boruta, XGBoost, random forest, Rpart, SVMrfe, and LASSO algorithms to screen the candidate genes for cohort 1, designated as the DEG-TEP data set. The DEG-TEP gene set consisted of 108 genes and is shown in Figure 2E. In cohort 2, we included the cancer tissues and adjacent tissues from 30 GGO-LUAD patients for RNA-seq, resulting in a data set of 1,909 DEGs, designated as the DEG-TISSUES data set. The intersection of the DEG-TEP and the DEG-TISSUES data set included 17 genes, designated as the intersection-17 data set (Figure 2F). We subsequently queried the expression profiles of all intersection-17 genes in the platelet-sequencing data set (Figure 2G).

Validation of intersection-17 in LUAD tissues

We collected cancer tissues and adjacent tissues from 20 patients with LUAD from cohort 3 and verified the mRNA expression via RT-qPCR. The expression levels of three genes (GPT2, GNG11, and FRMD3) in intersection-17 were below the detection limit, while the remaining 14 genes were validated. The mRNA expression of LAD1 (P<0.05) and TNFRSF17 (P<0.01) were significantly increased, while the mRNA expression of AHNAK was significantly decreased as compared to that of the corresponding precancerous tissues (P<0.05) (Figure 3A). The results of the other 11 gene expressions are shown in Figure S1. Furthermore, we used receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to assess the diagnostic efficacy of LAD1 [area under the curve (AUC) =0.91; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.82–1.00; P<0.001, DeLong’s test], TNFRSF17 (AUC =0.84; 95% CI: 0.72–0.97; P<0.001, DeLong’s test), and AHNAK (AUC =0.78; 95% CI: 0.63–0.94; P<0.01, DeLong’s test) in tumor tissues. All three genes demonstrated good diagnostic efficacy in cancer tissues (Figure 3B).

We further collected platelets from 33 patients with GGOs (19 LUAD-GGO patients and 14 inflammation-GGO patients) (cohort 4) and conducted RT-qPCR to identify the mRNA expression of AHNA, TNFRSF1, and LAD1 (Figure 3C) in platelets. Only the mRNA level of AHNAK was significantly decreased in the LUAD group (P<0.001). The ROC curve showed that AHNAK (AUC =0.78; 95% CI: 0.61–0.95; P<0.01, DeLong’s test) and TNFRSF17 (AUC =0.71; 95% CI: 0.53–0.89; P<0.05, DeLong’s test) had good diagnostic efficacy in platelets, while the diagnostic efficacy of LAD1 was not significant (AUC =0.66; 95% CI: 0.47–0.85; P=0.13, DeLong’s test) (Figure 3D). The decision curve analysis of AHNAK also demonstrated good diagnostic efficacy. Thus, AHNAK was selected for further investigation (Figure 3E).

Reduced AHNAK expression in LUAD

Analysis of the TCGA database on the online data analysis platform GEPIA showed that AHNAK mRNA expression was significantly downregulated in patients with LUAD (P<0.05) (Figure 4A). Subsequently, we obtained RNA-seq data sets from the GEO database for further validation (Figure 4B-4D). These data sets consist of RNA-seq data from patients diagnosed with early-stage (stage I/II) LUAD. In these three RNA-seq data sets, we found that the mRNA expression of AHNAK in LUAD was significantly lower than that in normal tissue (GSE75037: n=83, P<0.001; GSE197346: n=19, P=0.02; GSE233774: n=30, P<0.001).

Meanwhile, we used the Kaplan-Meier Plotter website to analyze the impact of AHNAK expression levels on the prognosis of patients with LUAD. The results indicated that low AHNAK expression levels were only associated with prognosis in patients with stage I LUAD (P<0.001) but not that of those with other stages of LUAD (Figure 4E-4G). Paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues from five patients were subjected to immunohistochemistry (IHC), which revealed that AHNAK expression was significantly downregulated in tumor tissues (Figure 4H).

Downregulation of AHNAK promoted the malignancy of LUAD

We searched in the online database HPA (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) and found that the H1299 and PC9 cell lines have high expression levels of AHNAK. We transfected si-AHNAK into H1299 and PC9 cells to establish AHNAK-knockdown cell models. The knockdown efficiency was confirmed via Western blotting (Figure 5A) and RT-qPCR (Figure 5B), with AHNAK-siRNA#1 achieving the highest knockdown efficacy. Subsequently, colony formation assays (Figure 5C,5D) and CCK8 assays (Figure 5E,5F) were performed to assess the impact of AHNAK expression on the proliferation of H1299 and PC9 cells. The results demonstrated that AHNAK knockdown significantly promoted the proliferation of LUAD cells. To determine the effect of AHNAK on LUAD cell migration and invasion, we performed Transwell assays (Figure 5G,5H). The Transwell results indicated that AHNAK knockdown significantly enhanced the migration and invasion capacities of H1299 and PC9 cells. These findings suggest that AHNAK plays a crucial role in the migration and invasion of LUAD in vitro.

Subsequently, we conducted single-gene GSEA on the sequencing data with the accession number GSE233774 to characterize the biological functions of AHNAK and performed KEGG analysis. We found that in the group with downregulated AHNAK, GSEA enriched many ribosome-related pathways and functions. Among the top 10 functions in the GO analysis, there were 5 ribosome-related functions. Meanwhile, in the KEGG pathway analysis, the ribosome pathway was the most enriched (Figure 6A,6B). The enrichment results showed that in the group with downregulated AHNAK were highly enriched the ribosome-related functions and signaling pathways (Figure 6C-6H). Discussion

In our study, we collected platelet RNA from patients with LUAD GGO and inflammation GGO (cohort 1). We found that in the sequencing results, the number of upregulated RNAs in platelets was far higher than downregulated RNAs, which is uncommon. We speculate that potential changes may have occurred in patients due to tumor activation of platelets, affecting the sequencing results. To determine which DEGs were genetically relevant to LUAD, we collected tumor and adjacent tissue samples from patients with LUAD from cohort 2, identifying a DEG set comprising 1,909 genes (DEG-TISSUES). We further screened and identified 17 DEGs present in both sequencing data sets, which were designated as intersection-17. Subsequently, we validated the differential expression of 17 genes using tissue samples (cohort 3) and platelet samples (cohort 4), ultimately identifying AHNAK as exhibiting significant differential expression and high diagnostic efficacy in both tissue and platelets. We confirmed AHNAK to be a diagnostic TEP biomarker for GGO LUAD. We further validated the function of AHNAK in vitro and identified its low expression as being associated with the occurrence and malignant behavior of LUAD.

In order to establish a noninvasive method to assist the in vitro diagnosis of LUAD via CT imaging characteristics such as GGOs, we collected blood components and integrated highly specific TEP-derived biomarkers to optimize a preoperative assessment system, thus enhancing the accuracy of early screening for LUAD. The primary CT imaging characteristic of early-stage LUAD is the presence of GGOs (25). However, GGOs can also arise from benign nodules, which exhibit image features highly similar to those of malignant GGOs. This overlap results in high sensitivity but low specificity when CT imaging features are relied upon as the sole feature in differentiating between benign and malignant pulmonary nodules. Consequently, clinicians often encounter cases that deviate from established guidelines when managing patients with GGO (26).

A noninvasive body fluid test can help clinicians arrive at a diagnosis. Compared with biopsy, body fluid test can be used to develop ELISA or qPCR detection kits, which can more conveniently assist clinicians in making diagnoses. However, the specific interactions between TEPs and lung cancer are still unclear. Previous studies have shown that there are differences in the RNA profile of platelets between patients with tumors and those without, with certain DEGs participating in tumor progression (12,27-29). Meanwhile, liquid biopsies can comprehensively reflect the genetic mutation information of lung cancer, guiding the selection of immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted molecular therapies (30-34). TEPs can be used for the early diagnosis of lung cancer can not only enhance the sensitivity and specificity of detection and may also serve as a long-lasting indicator for facilitating the guidance of therapies (35-37). In this study, we sought to identify diagnostic biomarkers via platelet-sequencing data to assist clinicians. After RNA-seq was performed on cohort 1, the results of GO analysis and KEGG analysis indicated that the DEGs were primarily enriched in cytokine-related cellular processes and signaling pathways.

We applied machine learning to the platelet-sequencing data and identified 108 DEGs, which we considered representative genes in TEPs. Given that platelets are susceptible to various influences, some DEGs might not be tumor-specific. Therefore, we cross-referenced these 108 genes with those differentially expressed in GGO-LUAD tissues and ultimately identified 17 intersecting genes, forming the intersection-17 gene set. After further validation, we found that 3 genes were below the detection limit of RT-qPCR. Consequently, we proceeded with the remaining 14 genes, with TNFRSF17, LAD1, and AHNAK exhibiting significant differential expressions in tumor tissues and adjacent tissues. ROC analysis indicated that all three genes possessed high diagnostic efficacy in tumor tissues. Subsequent validation in platelets confirmed that AHNAK exhibited particularly prominent expression differences and high diagnostic efficacy in TEPs. These results suggest that AHNAK has the potential to serve as a novel TEP biomarker for diagnosing GGO LUAD.

There is currently no reported evidence of AHNAK gene involvement in the impact of blood components on tumorigenesis. Our results indicate significant changes in the mRNA expression of AHNAK in TEPs of patients with early GGO LUAD, suggesting variations in AHNAK levels in TEPs are associated with tumor presence, but other systemic factors such as inflammation may also contribute to these changes, warranting further investigation into their specificity.

Our findings initially confirmed the role of AHNAK in suppressing tumors in LUAD. We further evaluated the differential expression of AHNAK using data sets from TCGA and GEO databases. The Kaplan-Meier plotter result indicated that only the prognosis of patients with stage I LUAD was worse when AHNAK expression was low, suggesting the importance of AHNAK in the early diagnosis for these patients. Additionally, IHC was employed to validate the significantly low expression of AHNAK in LUAD tissues, reinforcing its significance as a potential biomarker. Knocking down the expression of AHNAK in lung cancer cells not only affected their invasive capacity but also significantly impacted their proliferative ability. Downregulation of AHNAK levels has been reported in various tumors, such as ovarian cancer and liver cancer (19,38), where its expression is significantly reduced. Research on the physiological roles of AHNAK has primarily focused on cell signaling contacts (39,40), calcium channel regulation (41,42), and membrane repair (43). A study has indicated that the downregulation of AHNAK enhances the metastatic potential of tumor cells (44), which aligns with our results. Additionally, AHNAK has been reported to be involved in drug resistance processes in bladder and breast cancers (45,46), while AHNAK has been experimentally shown to mediate drug resistance in breast cancer (44), its association with drug resistance in bladder cancer (45) is primarily based on correlative transcriptomic analyses and requires further functional validation. These suggest AHNAK can be a biomarker for monitoring tumor drug resistance.

GSEA indicated that AHNAK was enriched in ribosome-related pathways. The association between ribosome-related pathways and tumors has also been reported (47,48). The ribosome is responsible for protein synthesis in a cell. We found that along with the decreased expression level of AHNAK, a scaffold protein, ribosome-related pathways are also inhibited. We speculate that this process ultimately affects the synthesis of proteins associated with tumor suppressors, thereby promoting tumor progression. Taken together, these findings suggest directions for further research and support the potential of AHNAK levels in platelets for diagnosing LUAD.

This study involved certain limitations which should be addressed. Due to the limited sample size, only AHNAK showed significant differential expression. Additionally, due to the short period for sample collection, we were unable to obtain survival data of patients with low AHNAK expression. Larger and more diverse studies will be conducted in the future to further investigate the distribution of the AHNAK gene in the population. To strengthen the association between AHNAK and clinical practice, in future studies, we will further explore the relationship between different AHNAK expression levels and CT imaging findings. Nonetheless, the results of this study shed light on the role of AHNAK in the progression of LUAD and provide new insights into the development of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets for LUAD.

Conclusions

AHNAK holds promise as a TEP biomarker in facilitating the development of the early molecular individualized noninvasive diagnosis of LUAD with GGO manifestations. Given the convenience of liquid biopsy, developing clinical detection kits is feasible after conducting multicenter prospective studies to verify the detection efficacy of AHNAK.
