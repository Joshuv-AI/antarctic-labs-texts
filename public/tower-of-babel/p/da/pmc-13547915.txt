Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis update, comparing the Australasian Interstitial Lung Disease Registry to the Australian Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis Registry
Authors: Michelle Chee, Yet Hong Khor, Dan Jackson, Wendy Cooper, Jason D’Costa, Sally de Boer, Adrienne Edwards, Samantha Ellis, Eli Gabbay, Henry Gallagher, Ian Glaspole, Nicole Goh, Christopher Grainge, Peter Hopkins, Gregory Keir, Benjamin Kwan, John Mackintosh, Yuben Moodley, Irene Moore, Vidya Navaratnam, Chong Ong, Paul Reynolds, Karen Royals, Alan Teoh, Lauren Troy, Elizabeth Veitch, Margaret Wilsher, Jeremy Wrobel, Chris Zappala, Tamera Corte, Francis Thien, the AILDR Steering Committee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsReal‐world evaluation of clinical characteristics and treatment profile of patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is needed to establish areas for improvement according to evidence‐based recommendations. This study evaluated the clinical characteristics and treatment profile of patients with IPF from the Australasian ILD Registry (AILDR), comparing them to the Australian IPF Registry (AIPFR) cohort, to identify differences in baseline characteristics among IPF patients treated with antifibrotics and immunosuppressants.MethodsConsecutive patients with IPF enrolled between May 2016 and December 2023 from the AILDR and data of patients enrolled in the AIPFR between 2012 and 2016 were included.ResultsA total of 842 and 647 patients with IPF were included from the AILDR and the AIPFR respectively. Both cohorts were male predominant (AILDR: 72.0%; AIPFR: 67.7%) of similar mean body mass index (AILDR: 29.2 ± 4.9 kg/m2; AIPFR: 28.7 ± 4.8 kg/m2). With regard to disease‐targeted treatment, 572 (67.9%) and 108 (12.8%) participants of the AILDR cohort were on antifibrotic medications and immunosuppressants respectively. There were a total of 35.6% (n = 300) IPF patients on pirfenidone and 40.4% (n = 340) on nintedanib in AILDR. Only 146 (23%) of the AIPFR cohort were receiving antifibrotics. Of newly registered patients in 2016 (n = 18), 2020 (n = 71) and 2023 (n = 151), 50.0%, 18.3% and 15.2% respectively had received immunosuppressive therapy.ConclusionBaseline demographics from the IPF cohorts for both AILDR and AIPFR are largely comparable, with increasing antifibrotic and decreasing use of immunosuppressants since 2016. Immunosuppressive therapy in IPF remains relatively higher than expected despite guideline recommendations.

Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is the most frequent idiopathic interstitial lung disease (ILD) in many Western cohorts, often accounting for approximately 20%–25% of all ILD cases. 1 Its incidence is increasing worldwide, with associated high morbidity burden and mortality rates. 2 Over the past decade, there has been a growing number of long‐term observational studies worldwide, contributing to improved understanding of disease behaviour, clinical traits and management profile of IPF patients. Pharmacological therapies for IPF have evolved from immunosuppressants to antifibrotic medications since the early 2010s. 3

The Australian IPF Registry (AIPFR) was a national initiative, led and managed by the Lung Foundation Australia. Established in 2012, it was funded through philanthropic pharmaceutical contributions via unrestricted educational grants and competitive research grants from the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC). The registry was established as a multi‐centre, prospective, observational study that included both newly diagnosed and existing IPF patients across Australia. A multidisciplinary steering committee, consisting of pulmonologists from each state alongside sub‐specialist radiologists and histopathologists, oversees the registry. The AIPFR promoted site and patient recruitment through national and international conferences, direct mail campaigns to professional society members and outreach efforts beyond tertiary referral centres. All referred patients diagnosed with IPF by their treating pulmonologist in Australia were included, with no specific exclusion criteria, thereby minimising selection bias and creating a representative cohort of IPF cases seen in specialist practices nationwide. Longitudinal data from 867 IPF patients, including patient‐reported outcomes, pulmonary function metrics, high‐resolution computed tomography (CT) scans and blood samples, remains available for research. AIPFR recruitment commenced in February 2011 and concluded in December 2020, with data collection finalised on 31 December 2021.

The Australasian Interstitial Lung Disease Registry (AILDR), by comparison, is a bi‐national prospective observational clinical registry which captures patient data with all ILD subtypes across Australia and New Zealand. The objective of the AILDR is to establish a clinically relevant database reflecting real‐world practice in Australasia and to enhance the standardisation of diagnostic and treatment pathways through collaborative research efforts. 4 The registry, commencing with four pilot sites in 2016, has now grown to 23 participating centres, inclusive of data from more than 4500 recruited participants. In this study, we sought to evaluate the clinical characteristics and treatment profile of patients with IPF from the AILDR, compared to the previous cohort from the AIPFR. We also aimed to ascertain whether there are any differences in baseline characteristics among IPF patients treated with antifibrotic medications versus immunosuppressants. We hypothesised that AILDR IPF patients treated with immunosuppressants have a higher prevalence of concurrent autoimmune diseases.

A previous report from the AIPFR, published in 2017, described real‐world patient characteristics and clinical presentations before the widespread availability of antifibrotic medications through subsidised government‐funded programmes in mid‐2017. 5 A previous survey of Australasian specialists revealed a widespread deviation from guideline‐based practices in the diagnosis and management of IPF. 6 Recent Australian data demonstrated age‐standardised estimates of IPF prevalence and mortality of 35.1 and 6.3 per 100 000 population respectively. 7 These data mandate further research to improve current understanding of clinical presentations and contemporary disease management.

Methods

Study design and participants

This observational study included consecutive patients with IPF from the AILDR between May 2016 and December 2023. The diagnosis of IPF was primarily made through regular ILD multidisciplinary meetings at each participating site, often involving respiratory physicians, pathologists and radiologists. The inclusion criteria for this study included participants aged 18 years and above with a diagnosis of IPF according to ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT criteria. 8 , 9 , 10 All participants with diagnoses other than IPF and AIPFR participants were excluded from this study. The ethics approval for this study was granted by the Sydney Local Health District (HREC/16/RPAH/345) and the Western Australian South Metropolitan Health Service (RGS11/ILD1) Human Research Ethics Committees, with each participating site being responsible for obtaining local governance approval. Historical data of patients enrolled between 2012 and 2016 from the AIPFR (protocol number X14‐0264; ethics approval 2019/ETH06869) were used as the comparator.

Data collection

This study included comprehensive data inclusive of patient demographics (gender, age, body mass index (BMI), smoking history), lung function (forced vital capacity (FVC, % predicted), diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO, % predicted), 6‐min walk distance (6MWD)), antifibrotic and immunosuppressant medications that were used at any time during the study period, as well as domiciliary oxygen use. The antibody status was recorded as binary variables in both registries, determined by laboratory ANCA positivity.

Statistical analyses

Between‐group comparisons for the AILDR cohort were performed using t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables depending on data distribution, and chi‐squared tests for categorical variables. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 842 participants from the AILDR were included in this study from respective participating centres (Table S1). The baseline characteristics of AILDR patients with IPF together with IPF cohorts from other international registries and clinical trials are summarised in Table 1. There were 647 patients included from the AIPFR. Both cohorts were male‐predominant (AILDR: n = 606 (72.0%); AIPFR: n = 647 (67.7%) of similar mean BMI (AILDR: 29.2 ± 4.9 kg/m2; AIPFR: 28.7 ± 4.8 kg/m2). The AILDR cohort was older at time of registration than the AIPFR cohort (mean age 74.3 ± 8.7 vs 70.9 ± 8.5 years), with fewer ever‐smokers (62.8% vs 71.7%). Both cohorts had similar mean FVC % predicted (AILDR: 83.4 ± 19.0%; AIPFR: 81.0% ± 21.7%, while mean DLCO % predicted was higher in the AILDR cohort compared to the AIPFR cohort (59.4% ± 18.7% vs. 48.4% ± 16.7%). Oxygen therapy use for the AILDR IPF cohort was 14.3% (n = 120).

Comorbidities

In the AILDR cohort, the three most common comorbidities were cardiac disease (n = 339, 40.3%), previous surgery (n = 230, 27.3%) and gastro‐oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) (n = 194, 23.0%) as shown in Figure 1. There were 28.1% IPF patients in the AILDR on anti‐acid pharmacotherapy (proton pump inhibitors or histamine H2‐receptor antagonists). Among patients with the diagnosis of GORD, 88 (45.4%) were on anti‐acid medications, whereas in AIPFR the commonest comorbidities were GORD (n = 233, 41%), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (n = 197, 36%) and coronary artery disease (n = 161, 28%).

Antifibrotic medications and immunosuppressants

Compared to the AIPFR cohort, there were more patients from the AILDR receiving antifibrotic medications (68% vs. 23%) and fewer receiving immunosuppressants (13% for AILDR cohort vs 46% on prednisone, 7% on azathioprine for the AIPFR cohort). A total of 35.6% (n = 300) IPF patients in the AILDR were on pirfenidone and 40.4% (n = 340) were on nintedanib. Baseline characteristics for IPF patients on antifibrotics (n = 572) compared to those not on antifibrotics (n = 270) in the AILDR are shown in Table 2. Patients receiving antifibrotic therapy tended to be younger, have higher BMI, lower FVC and better exercise capacity.

In general, the use of immunosuppressants decreased across the patient registration years as demonstrated in Figure S1. Common immunosuppressants used in the AILDR cohort were prednisone 11.9% (n = 100), mycophenolate 1.8% (n = 15) and azathioprine 1.0% (n = 8) (Fig. 2). Of newly registered patients in 2016 (n = 18), 2020 (n = 71) and 2023 (n = 151), 50.0%, 18.3% and 15.2% respectively were reported to be or have been on immunosuppressive therapy. Compared to patients who did not receive immunosuppressants (n = 734), those who received immunosuppressants (n = 108) had a higher prevalence of documented autoimmune disease, obstructive sleep apnoea and pulmonary hypertension as comorbidities (Table 3). Antinuclear antibody (ANA) (30%), antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) (9%), myositis antibodies (6%), anti‐double‐stranded DNA (anti‐dsDNA) (6%) and rheumatoid factor (5%) were common antibodies reported to be positive in the AILDR IPF cohort who received immunosuppressants (Fig. 3).

Discussion

We report findings comparing IPF patients from the AILDR and AIPFR cohorts, which have been essential clinical research tools for providing epidemiological and treatment profile data across Australasia. There are limited real‐world observational demographic and treatment data available for IPF. Our findings provide in‐depth contemporary insights into demographic and treatment data in Australian and New Zealand patients with IPF recruited from a wide range of geographical locations and clinical settings.

The year 2014 was pivotal when two antifibrotic medications, pirfenidone and nintedanib, were shown in randomised controlled trials to be effective in reducing disease progression 11 , 12 and all‐cause mortality in patients with IPF. 13 Consistent with the change in international clinical practice guidelines since then, the prevalence of antifibrotic therapy use in AILDR IPF patients increased to 67.9% from the 23% reported in the AIPFR cohort. When this registry was activated, antifibrotic medications were mainly available through restricted compassionate access schemes. 8 Subsequent availability of subsidised Nintedanib and Pirfenidone for IPF in Australia and New Zealand has supported broad access to evidence‐based therapy. The increased use of antifibrotic therapy is also consistent with recommendations included in the 2017 IPF treatment position statement by the Thoracic Society of Australia and New Zealand and Lung Foundation Australia. Local prescribing restrictions for nintedanib and pirfenidone for IPF are summarised in Table S2, with some impact on eligibility depending on disease characteristics. On the other hand, despite there being no prescribing conditions for antifibrotic medications for IPF in the United States, the prohibitively high out‐of‐pocket cost of medications has led to low uptake, at only 26.4%, since their approval in 2014. 14 Further cost effectiveness studies and global policy reviews will be helpful in addressing these issues to improve affordability and accessibility for patients who need the medications.

Recent years have seen significant paradigm shifts away from corticosteroids and combination immunosuppressants, which were conventional IPF therapy for many decades. The PANTHER‐IPF study published in 2012 showed increased hospitalisation and mortality rates in patients with IPF who received combination low‐dose corticosteroids, N‐acetylcysteine and azathioprine compared to those receiving placebo. 15 However, despite these findings, a relatively high, albeit decreasing, prevalence of ongoing immunosuppressant use remained in the AILDR IPF cohort. Further analysis is needed to understand the indications for prescription of immunosuppressants in these patients and detailed evaluation of the practice and rationales of IPF pharmacotherapy across Australasia. Of note, those receiving immunosuppressive therapy had a higher prevalence of autoimmune disease, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and family history of connective tissue disease. Pulmonary hypertension is a common manifestation of connective tissue disease, in addition to being a complication of ILD. 16 Interstitial lung disease and OSA have a complex and potentially bidirectional relationship, supported by multiple proposed pathogenic and physiological mechanisms. 17 This includes systemic corticosteroid administration for the treatment of ILD side effect risks such as metabolic syndrome. 18 , 19 Unfortunately, data on the indication for prescription were not captured. Compared to the 13% immunosuppressant use rate in Australasia, 23% of an IPF cohort in the United States were using or had recently used an immunosuppressant. 20 In Denmark, 18% of patients with IPF received at least one prescription of prednisolone or other immunosuppressive treatment prior to referral, 21 whereas in Russia, 42.8% of the IPF cohort were on systemic corticosteroids. 22 In this study, patients with IPF who received immunosuppressants had higher rates of co‐existing autoimmune conditions. This raises several unanswered questions, including whether IPF was the correct diagnostic label for these patients, as well as whether the use of short‐burst corticosteroids for acute exacerbations was a relevant factor. The observation is a prompt for future studies, as concurrent autoimmune features in other ILD subtypes, notably hypersensitivity pneumonitis, have been shown to exhibit different disease behaviours. 23

We investigated baseline characteristics of the AILDR cohort compared to AIPFR, which were broadly similar but with fewer ever smokers in the AILDR. Demographics from the two cohorts were also comparable to other clinical registries, including INSIGHTS‐IPF, 24 FinnishIPF 25 and Swedish IPF, 26 as well as the landmark ASCEND 11 and INPULSIS‐2 12 trial populations. However, the IPF cohorts in INSIGHTS‐IPF, ASCEND and INPULSIS trials were younger, with lower mean FVC % predicted and DLCO % predicted. INPULSIS and ASCEND trials recruited mild to moderate disease, with exclusion of asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, connective tissue disease and recent immunosuppressant use.

Our data showed significant antacid therapy use at 28.0% among AILDR IPF participants. In previous international clinical practice guidelines for IPF in 2011 and 2015, 8 , 27 antacid therapy was conditionally suggested to improve respiratory outcomes. These recommendations stemmed from observational studies indicating a high prevalence of acid reflux, 28 , 29 and hiatus hernia 30 in patients with IPF, as well as a retrospective study suggesting a survival benefit with antacid use. 31 Subsequent updates to the guidelines showed insufficient evidence to support a beneficial role of antacid medications for the sole purpose of improving respiratory outcomes in IPF. 9 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 However, the effects summarised in the guidelines are rated as very low‐quality evidence, predominantly derived from observational studies with susceptibility bias and possible unmeasured confounding factors. To highlight the need for further research to assess the benefits of antacid therapy in IPF management, at the time of writing, there is an in‐progress multicentre randomised controlled trial in the United Kingdom (TIPAL) with estimated study completion in 2025, which aims to assess whether IPF progresses slower if treated with PPIs. 37

While this study provides invaluable latest insights into baseline characteristics and treatment profiles of patients with IPF, it is essential to acknowledge several study limitations. First, when one adheres to the current guidelines set forth by the international specialist societies, the accuracy of ILD diagnosis is notably enhanced through a multidisciplinary team approach. Within this framework, diagnostic accuracy plays a pivotal role in influencing the quality and reliability of the collected data and subsequent analysis. Facilitating multidisciplinary discussions to reach a consensus will build confidence in the diagnostic process and support the development of appropriate treatment plans. While there is no central validation process for the AILDR, all participating sites are required to hold ILD multidisciplinary discussion for consensus diagnostic labelling. Thus, our findings represent real‐world clinical cohort diagnoses and patient care. Second, although AILDR endeavours to comprehensively capture data to improve its interpretive accuracy, we are faced by the real‐world challenges of dealing with missing data. In addition, as only binary data on antibody status were collected, the differences in testing methodologies and thresholds across participating centres could not be evaluated.

Conclusion and future directions

The baseline demographics from these two IPF cohorts recruited during a period of dramatic evolution in disease management are largely comparable. As would be expected, antifibrotic medication use has increased since 2016, while immunosuppressive agent use in IPF has reduced over this time. The reasons for persistent prescribing of these medications in some IPF patients need further characterisation. Our results demonstrate the importance of AILDR as a platform to enable benchmarking and quality assurance in IPF disease and its management. As more pharmacological and non‐pharmacological interventions become available for IPF, registry data will help to provide insights into real‐world therapeutic practices and implementation of consensus guideline recommendations.
