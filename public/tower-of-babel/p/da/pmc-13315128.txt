Paclitaxel Noninferior to Paclitaxel: Registry‐Scale Evidence for an Urea‐Excipient Drug‐Coated Balloon in a Real World Population: A Commentary on the SCAAR Nationwide Comparative Registry Analysis

Every interventionalist choosing a drug‐coated balloon (DCB) today faces a quiet contradiction. European guidelines endorse DCB for in‐stent restenosis (ISR); however, the majority of the cases where a DCB is used are de novo lesions with a very variable grade of complexity. 1 , 2

Moreover, the pivotal trials behind these recommendations were largely conducted with an iopromide‐excipient paclitaxel‐coated balloon (PCB) that is no longer the sole or necessarily dominant platform in contemporary catheterization laboratories. The balloon in the drawer may carry urea, acetyl tributyl citrate, or butyryl‐trihexyl citrate as its excipient, and could be crystalline or amorphous, with an invariably different pharmacokinetic of the drug and resulting uncertain clinical implications. Whether such technical differences in newer devices reflect a different clinical performance, specifically whether one specific urea‐excipient PCB performs comparably to current alternatives, is what Von Koch and colleagues now address in the largest comparative DCB data set assembled to date in this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA). 3

THE STUDY AND ITS FINDINGS

Using SCAAR (Swedish Coronary Angiography and Angioplasty Registry), a nationwide database with virtually complete follow‐up, the investigators analyzed 6488 DCB procedures (8054 devices) from August 2021 to May 2024. The study was framed as a target trial emulation, 4 , 5 with prespecified eligibility, treatment strategies, and outcome definitions, and addressed confounding through propensity score‐weighted Cox regression adjusting for a prespecified set of clinical, angiographic, and procedural variables.

One feature of the design is critical. The primary comparison, urea‐excipient PCBs (Prevail, Medtronic; 1797 procedures) versus other contemporary DCBs (4691 procedures), is essentially paclitaxel versus predominantly paclitaxel: at the device level, the comparator group was 95.6% paclitaxel‐based and only 4.4% sirolimus‐coated (253 devices). The study therefore cannot adjudicate the excipient question or compare drug classes. The answerable question is narrower but clinically relevant: does the urea‐excipient PCB perform on par with other paclitaxel DCB in everyday practice?

The cohort reflects genuine, real‐world complexity: approximately 32.4% had diabetes, 64.2% acute coronary syndromes, 31.5% ISR, and 22.8% involved bifurcation lesions. At 2 years, after adjustment, outcomes were similar across all end points. Major adverse cardiovascular events occurred in 16.4% of urea‐PCB patients versus 18.4% in the comparator group (adjusted hazard ratio [HR], 1.01 [95% CI, 0.84–1.22]). All‐cause mortality was 7.2% versus 8.2% (HR, 1.05 [95% CI, 0.80–1.39]), myocardial infarction 6.1% versus 8.0% (HR, 0.81 [95% CI, 0.59–1.11]), target lesion revascularization 7.4% versus 8.7% (HR, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.70–1.29]), and definite target‐lesion thrombosis 0.8% versus 1.3% (HR, 0.93 [95% CI, 0.41–2.11]). Results were broadly consistent across prespecified subgroups, including acute coronary syndromes (2‐year major adverse cardiovascular events 20.5% versus 20.0%), diabetes (24.1% versus 24.2%), ISR (25.4% versus 25.7%), and small vessels (12.7% versus 14.7%).

One methodological detail matters when interpreting myocardial infarction (MI) rates. The MI end point used in this study derives from RIKS‐HIA hospitalization codes (International Classification of Diseases [ICD] I21–I22), with periprocedural MI being explicitly excluded. This end point captures only clinically manifest MI requiring hospital admission, unlike the biomarker‐based definitions in most PCB trials, and does not reflect eventual differences among the formulations of DCB.

WHY IT MATTERS

Three things make this study stand out. First is size: at >6000 procedures, it is far larger than the earlier studies evaluating this balloon platform, including the 50‐patient, prospective, single‐arm PREVAIL study 6 and the 757‐patient FALCON registry of the predecessor IN.PACT Falcon DCB, which shared the same urea‐based paclitaxel‐coated balloon, at a different drug dosage. 7 Second, the study period (2021–2024) captures current DCB practice, including acute coronary syndromes and complex anatomy that were underrepresented in earlier studies. Third, the target trial emulation framework and directed acyclic graph lend methodological rigor well beyond that of a conventional registry comparison.

These results are consistent with broader evidence for DCB, which demonstrates good safety and performance in the long term, 8 also as compared with stents. 9 The SCAAR analysis adds what no trial has provided for this urea‐based platform: real‐world reassurance across a broad, unselected population.

Also, emphasis should be put on the apparent lower risk of MI in the urea‐based DCB in case of hybrid (we still prefer to rephrase it as “blended”) stent+DCB approach. This practice is used more and more, especially in the complex lesion setting, and further investigations are required, aiming at finding the perfect DCB platform to get along with stents. 10 , 11

WHY RESTRAINT IS NEEDED

Target trial emulation is powerful, but it does not remove the possibility of residual confounding. It assumes that, after adjustment, the treatment groups are sufficiently comparable, an assumption that is difficult to fully meet in interventional cardiology, where device selection is influenced by lesion morphology, operator judgment, and procedural context. SCAAR lacks granular lesion‐level variables (lesion length, minimum lumen diameter, diameter stenosis) that are among the strongest drivers of both device choice and target lesion revascularization. If operators preferentially used urea‐PCB in shorter or simpler lesions, residual confounding could make the groups look more similar than they are. Additionally, the study protocol was not prospectively registered, and the urea‐PCB entered Sweden at the start of the study window, raising the possibility of an early‐adopter effects that propensity models cannot fully capture. Another element that is difficult to capture in such a study is the deliverability of the device, one major contributor of the fate of a DCB, with newer generations of devices having improved from the initial bulky systems.

Specifically, sirolimus‐coated balloons (SCB) have an improved deliverability as compared with paclitaxel‐DCB; however, their performance (which was outside of the scope of this study) was mediocre here, and the results should be interpreted with caution. The 22.1% 2‐year target lesion revascularization and 18.4% MI observed in 253 SCB‐treated devices likely reflect substantial confounding by indication: patients treated with SCB had 41.1% ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction and 45.5% ISR, compared with 11.2% and 28.0% respectively in the urea‐PCB group. These are not comparable populations. The all‐comer EASTBOURNE (The All‐Comers Sirolimus‐Coated Balloon European Registry) registry (2123 patients) documented a 2‐year clinically indicated target lesion revascularization rate of 7.0%, 8 and a Cox‐regression‐adjusted comparison 2 two prospective registries found no clinical difference between SCB and PCB in de novo or restenotic coronary lesions. 12 , 13 The SCB results here highlight a broader challenge: evaluating a newly introduced device in a registry where patient selection is uncontrolled, a problem that only randomized trials can fully resolve.

WHAT THIS MEANS IN THE CATHETERIZATION LABORATORY

The practical message is straightforward. When a urea‐excipient PCB is selected for appropriate lesions after adequate vessel preparation, these data provide meaningful, if not definitive, reassurance: 2‐year outcomes across a broad case mix including acute coronary syndromes and ISR were similar to those treated with other contemporary paclitaxel DCB. Operators choosing among available paclitaxel platforms can draw on these data with reasonable confidence. They should not, however, be read as evidence that all DCB are interchangeable and that excipient biology is irrelevant.

One finding buried in the supplements of this article deserves emphasis. Intracoronary imaging remained underused across the cohort, even in ISR, where accurate balloon sizing is most consequential for both drug delivery and dissection avoidance. The European Association of Percutaneous Cardiovascular Interventions consensus endorses imaging for complex lesion subsets including ISR, 14 and this is a modifiable quality gap that likely influences outcomes more than platform selection.

THE PATH FORWARD

The currently ongoing Prevail Global program will provide an important evaluation of this urea‐based PCB in ISR and de novo small‐vessel disease, including a randomized comparison against an approved coronary DCB for ISR and a prospective comparison against historical drug‐eluting stent data in small‐vessel disease. 15 Another important piece of the puzzle derives from the reduced MI rates in the blended DCB + stent group, and if a tailored dual antiplatelet regimen is needed after DCB angioplasty is still to be understood. 16

Von Koch and colleagues have produced a large and methodologically rigorous real‐world comparative DCB assessment to date (Figure 1). Their finding, no large detectable difference in 2‐year outcomes between urea‐excipient PCB and contemporary paclitaxel DCB, constitutes registry‐scale reassurance, not proven equivalence, and does not speak to sirolimus platforms. Taken together, these data provide the most credible real‐world benchmark currently available and help define the questions that only randomized trials can resolve. For sure, this is another challenge for the old and now questionable motto “there is no class‐effect for DCB.” 17
