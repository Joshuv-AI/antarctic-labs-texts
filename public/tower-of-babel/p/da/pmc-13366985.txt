The STK33/mTORC1/S6K1/HIF-1α signalling axis drives glycolysis and progression in pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours
Authors: Bo Zhou, Canyang Zhan, Zhenzhen Gao, Fei Xiang, Sheng Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs) are characterized by metabolic reprogramming, but the underlying mechanisms driving their progression remain poorly understood. Therefore, we investigated whether serine/threonine kinase 33 (STK33) promotes PanNET growth and aerobic glycolysis via mTORC1/S6K1/HIF-1α signalling.MethodsPanNET specimens and adjacent nontumorous pancreatic tissues from 102 patients who underwent curative surgery for PanNETs were subjected to immunochemical staining for STK33 and HIF-1α. The relationships among STK33 expression levels, HIF-1α expression levels, clinicopathological parameters and clinical prognoses were statistically analysed. The effects of STK33 on PanNET growth and glycolysis were examined via shRNA-mediated knockdown and overexpression in vitro and in vivo.ResultsIncreased STK33 expression in PanNETs correlated significantly with shorter disease-free survival (P < 0.001). STK33 knockdown suppressed cell proliferation, glucose uptake, lactate/ATP production, and S6K1 phosphorylation, whereas STK33 overexpression enhanced these effects. Inhibition of S6K1 (PF-4708671) reversed STK33-driven glycolysis and tumorigenesis. In vivo, STK33 increased tumour growth concurrent with enhanced S6K1 phosphorylation. Mechanistically, HIF-1α transcriptionally upregulated STK33, whereas STK33 promoted increased HIF-1α protein levels via mTORC1/S6K1 signalling, enhancing the expression of the glycolytic enzyme LDHA. Clinically, STK33 and HIF-1α were strongly coexpressed in PanNET tissues (ρ = 0.771; P < 0.001), and both predicted poor prognoses.ConclusionsOur results support a model in which STK33 functions as a key regulator of the mTORC1/S6K1/HIF-1α signaling-metabolic axis in PanNETs, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08279-8.Keywords: Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours, Glycolysis, Tumour progression, STK33, mTORC1/S6K1/HIF-1α axis

Background

Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs) are a heterogeneous group of neoplasms arising from pancreatic islet cells, with variable malignant potential and clinical behaviours [1, 2]. While surgical resection offers curative potential for localized disease, advanced PanNETs remain challenging to treat because of the limited therapeutic options and frequent resistance to agents such as everolimus and sunitinib [3, 4]. The 5-year survival rate for patients with high-grade PanNETs is dismal (< 30%), underscoring the urgent need to identify novel molecular drivers [5, 6]. Cancer cells undergo profound metabolic alterations to meet the biosynthetic and bioenergetic demands of rapid proliferation, a phenomenon known as metabolic reprogramming. A key alteration is the preference for aerobic glycolysis via the cytosolic metabolic pathway that converts glucose to pyruvate, generating ATP and NADH; under aerobic glycolysis, pyruvate is preferentially converted to lactate even in the presence of oxygen [7]. This metabolic shift promotes tumour growth and metastasis [8, 9].

Unlike pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), PanNETs frequently retain metabolic plasticity, with aerobic glycolysis (the “Warburg effect”) emerging as a key adaptation supporting their growth and survival in nutrient-poor microenvironments [10]. Increased uptake of the glucose analogue ¹⁸F-FDG on PET scans is correlated with high tumour grade, high metastatic potential, and poor prognosis in PanNET patients [11]. This glycolytic phenotype enables PanNET cells to thrive in hypoxic, nutrient-deprived tumour microenvironments and contributes to extracellular matrix acidification, which facilitates invasion and immune evasion. However, the regulators orchestrating this metabolic shift in PanNETs remain largely unexplored.

Serine/threonine kinase 33 (STK33) belongs to the calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase (CAMK) family and was initially linked to spermatogenesis and fertility. Recently, STK33 has emerged as a significant participant in DNA replication, signal transduction, cell differentiation, and cancer development. In KRAS-driven cancers, STK33 is essential for cell survival through its regulation of critical oncogenic pathways [12]. Several studies have shown that STK33 is overexpressed in a variety of tumours, and may promote tumorigenesis and disease progression [13, 14]. Furthermore, the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) serves as a central integrator of growth factor signalling, nutrient availability, and cellular energy status. When activated, mTORC1 phosphorylates downstream effectors, including ribosomal protein S6 kinase 1 (S6K1), which in turn promote protein synthesis, cell growth, and metabolic reprogramming [15]. S6K1 enhances glycolytic flux by activating HIF-1α translation, phosphorylating glycolytic enzymes, and promoting glucose transporter expression [16–18]. Notably, despite these emerging roles in various cancer types, the function of STK33 in neuroendocrine tumours—particularly in relation to metabolic reprogramming—remains entirely unexplored.

The potential interactions of STK33 with mTORC1/S6K1 and HIF-1α signalling, both central to PanNET progression and metabolism, represent a significant gap in our understanding. Hence, in this study, we sought to determine whether STK33 serves as a critical regulator connecting oncogenic signals to metabolic reprogramming in PanNETs, which could provide important insights into molecular drivers and aid in the identification of novel therapeutic targets for this challenging cancer type.

Materials and methods

Patients and immunohistochemical staining

PanNET specimens and adjacent nontumorous pancreatic tissues were obtained from 102 patients who underwent curative surgery for PanNETs at the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, from 2015 to 2024. The diagnosis of PanNETs was based on standard histological criteria. Pathological analyses were conducted according to the criteria issued by the World Health Organization (WHO), and the tumour stages were classified according to the 9th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system. The clinicopathological parameters of all the patients in this series were collected. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All methods and research activities were performed in accordance with the hospital’s guidelines and regulations.

Immunohistochemistry was performed using the standard avidin-biotin-peroxidase complex procedure. Briefly, 5-µm-thick formaldehyde-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections were transferred onto adhesive slides and dried at 65°C for 30 min. Then, they were subjected to immunohistochemical staining with anti-STK33 (1:100; Proteintech, USA) and anti-HIF-1α (1:100; Proteintech, USA) antibodies at 4°C overnight and then incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Staining was performed using a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit. The same PanNET tissue sections were stained sequentially for STK33 and HIF-1α using consecutive sections that were obtained from the same paraffin-embedded tissue blocks. Images were taken from morphologically matched regions on the two slides to ensure that they represented the same tumour area.

For HIF-1α, only nuclear staining was considered for scoring, reflecting its functional role as a transcription factor. For STK33, both cytosolic and nuclear staining were evaluated as it localizes to both compartments. Cytosolic or nuclear staining of yellowish or brownish granules was graded using the following scale: 0 for background staining, 1 for faint staining, 2 for moderate staining, and 3 for strong staining. In addition, the ratio of positive cells to total cells (expressed as a percentage) in each tissue section was graded according to the following scale: 0 for < 5%, 1 for 5–25%, 2 for 26–50%, 3 for 51–75%, and 4 for 76–100% [10, 13, 14]. These two parameters were multiplied to produce a weighted score for each tumour sample; scores of 0–2 and ≥ 3 were considered indicative of negative and positive staining, respectively. Immunohistochemical staining was independently analysed by 2 investigators who were blinded to the clinical and survival data. The Cohen’s κ coefficient was 0.82, indicating almost perfect agreement.

Cell lines

The human PanNET cell lines BON and QGP-1 were purchased from Bluefbio (Shanghai, China). All cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin. The cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere. Cells were treated with 20 µM PF-4,708,671 (S6K1 inhibitor; dissolved in DMSO) for 48 h.

Plasmids and small interfering RNAs

shRNAs targeting STK33 (shSTK33-1, 5′-GGTAGTGCAGAATCTTTAA-3′; shSTK33-2, 5′-AGTTCAAGTTTCACATCTA-3′; and shSTK33-3, 5′-GAAGCGACAGACAAGGAAA-3′) were cloned and inserted into a pLKO.1 vector. For overexpression, the CDs region of STK33 was inserted into the pLVX-Puro vector. The recombinant plasmids and the psPAX2 and pMD2G vectors were cotransfected into 293T cells using Lipofectamine 2000. After 48 h, the supernatant containing viral particles was collected and used for the transduction of PanNET cell lines. The siHIF1α-1 (5′-CGAUGGAAGCACUAGACAAAG-3′) and siHIF1α-2 (5′-GCUAUUCACCAAAGUUGAAUC-3′) sequences were transfected into PanNET cell lines. Negative controls included cells that were transduced with either a pLKO.1 plasmid containing a scrambled shRNA sequence (shNC) or an empty pLVX-Puro vector or transfected with a control siRNA sequence (siNC). Non-transduced parental cells served as an additional control (Control).

CCK-8 assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well. Afterwards, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. After the cells were incubated for 1 h, the optical densities at 450 nm were measured using a microplate reader to quantify cell viability.

Colony formation assay

The cells were treated and cultured for 2 weeks. Afterwards, the colonies were first fixed with paraformaldehyde for 15 min. The cells were subsequently stained with a 0.5% solution of crystal violet for 30 min to visualize the colonies. The number of colonies (> 50 cells) was quantified.

Measurement of glucose uptake

Cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were cultured in six-well plates at 37 °C for one day. Forty-eight hours after treatment, the cells were cultured in DMEM for 3 h and then washed with Krebs–Ringer bicarbonate buffer supplemented with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA), followed by incubation with DMEM supplemented with 100 µM 2-NBDG (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA) for 45 min. Glucose uptake by cells was measured using flow cytometry.

Measurement of lactic acid and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels

The lactic acid content in the cell supernatants was quantified using a lactic acid assay kit (A019-2; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). The ATP content within the cells was determined using an ATP assay kit (A095; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute).

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) measurement

Cellular glycolysis was assessed by measuring the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) using a Seahorse XF-96 Analyzer (Agilent Technologies). Briefly, cells were seeded at 3 × 10³ per well in an XF-96 culture plate and incubated overnight. Following baseline measurement, glucose, oligomycin, and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) were sequentially injected into the wells at specified time points to perform the glycolysis stress test. Data were collected and analysed using the Seahorse Wave software.

qRT‒PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cell lines using TRIzol. Following DNase treatment, cDNA was synthesized using a cDNA First-Strand Synthesis kit (AE311-02; TransGen Biotech). qRT‒PCR was conducted using the Green qPCR SuperMix (AQ601; TransGen Biotech) on an ABI 7300 system (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression levels were analysed using the 2− ΔΔCt method and normalized to that of the reference gene ACTB. The following PCR primers were used: STK33-F, 5’-GGGAAATGTCCCTGATGCCA-3’; STK33-R, 5’-GTTGCTGTTCCTTGGCTTGG-3’; HIF1α-F, 5’-CCCAATGTCGGAGTTTGGAA-3’; HIF1α-R, 5’-TGGCTGCATCTCGAGACTTT-3’; ACTB-F, 5’-CCGCGAGAAGATGACCCAG-3’; and ACTB-R, 5’-GATAGCACAGCCTGGATAGCA-3’.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (89901; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for protein extraction. A BCA protein assay reagent kit (Beyotime, China) was used to quantify the protein concentrations. Following denaturation, the samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE) and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was subsequently incubated with antibodies against STK33 (12857-1-AP; Proteintech), S6K1 (Abs146388; Absin), p-S6K1 (Abs18182; Absin), HIF-1α (20960-1-AP; Proteintech), LDHA (66287-1-Ig; Proteintech), and β-actin (66009-1-Ig; Proteintech). Protein expression was detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (Bio-Rad, USA). For phosphorylated proteins, the band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. The grey-value of each target band was normalized to that of β-actin (internal control) to obtain a relative expression level.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays

ChIP assays were conducted using a magnetic ChIP kit (Millipore) in accordance with the manufacturer’s procedures. Cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde and fragmented in the presence of MNase by sonication. HIF-1α (ab1; Abcam) or negative control IgG (30000-0-AP; Proteintech) antibodies were used for immunoprecipitation assays. After the crosslinks were washed and reversed, the precipitated DNA was amplified using primers and quantified by PCR. Enrichment was calculated as a percentage of the input DNA.

Plasmid construction and dual luciferase reporter assay

Cells were transfected with the pGL3-basic plasmid containing the wild-type or mutant promoter region of STK33 and transfected with HIF1α siRNA, along with the pRL-TK plasmid, using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) at 37 °C for 6 h according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Afterwards, a luciferase assay system (Promega) was used to measure luciferase activity according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Luciferase activity was calculated as the Firefly/Renilla ratio.

Establishment of stable cell lines and xenograft study

The flanks of 4- to 5-week-old male mice were injected subcutaneously with 2 × 106 BON-1 cells transduced with the STK33 expression vector, and the mice were injected with 50 mg/kg PF-4,708,671 (dissolved in vehicle: 5% DMSO + 30% PEG300 + 5% Tween-80 + 60% saline) every 2 days. Tumours were measured every three days, and tumour volumes were calculated. Thirty-three days after the PF-4,708,671 injection, the mice were sacrificed, and the tumour xenografts were collected, photographed and weighed (n = 5 mice per group). Laboratory animal care and animal experiments were performed in accordance with the animal ethics guidelines and protocols approved by the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine.

Immunofluorescence staining and microscopy

The samples were incubated overnight with anti-Ki67 antibodies (27309-I-AP; Proteintech) at 4 °C. The cells were subsequently incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (A0428; Beyotime) at room temperature for 1 h. The cell nuclei were labelled with DAPI (C1005; Beyotime). Microscopy was then performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany) to analyse immunofluorescence staining.

Statistical analyses

The experimental procedures were performed three times unless otherwise specified. The data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) unless otherwise specified. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using the independent Student’s t-test (two-tailed). For multiple-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test. For tumour growth curves measured over time, repeated-measures two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s post-hoc test was used to assess the effects of treatment and time. Correlations between STK33 and HIF-1α expression levels were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and values are indicated as follows: 0.01< *p < 0.05, 0.001< **p < 0.01; and ***p < 0.001. Significance symbols are defined in each figure legend, with precise comparator groups indicated.

Results

Patient and tumour characteristics

A total of 102 patients with primary PanNETs who underwent curative resection and lymphadenectomy were enrolled. No lymph node (LN) metastases were observed on preoperative chest CT, abdomen-enhanced CT or liver-enhanced MRI. Surgical procedures included distal pancreatectomy (n = 62), pancreaticoduodenectomy (n = 33), and middle pancreatectomy (n = 7). Five patients with solitary liver metastases underwent combined distal pancreatectomy, liver metastasectomy, and lymphadenectomy. The median age was 55 years (range 25–82). Forty-six tumours (45.1%) were incidentally detected during health examinations. Symptoms included abdominal pain (n = 24, 23.5%); abdominal discomfort (n = 18, 17.7%); jaundice (n = 9, 8.8%); and diarrhoea (n = 5, 4.9%). The median tumour size was 3 cm (range 1–10.2). Finally, 28 (27.5%) patients had LN metastases on pathology. All tumours were non-functional. According to WHO grading, 27 (26.5%) were G1, 65 (63.7%) were G2, and 10 (9.8%) were well-differentiated G3. The median postoperative follow-up was 65 months (range 7–105). During follow-up, 29 patients (28.4%) experienced recurrence and 16 (15.7%) died. The 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival rates were 100%, 92%, and 84%, respectively, and the 1-, 3- and 5-year DFS rates were 93%, 74% and 71%, respectively.

STK33 expression correlates with PanNET progression and shorter DFS and OS

We first investigated STK33 protein expression in 102 PanNET specimens and corresponding normal adjacent pancreatic tissue specimens using immunohistochemical staining to determine the role of STK33 in PanNET pathogenesis. As shown in Fig. 1A and B, elevated STK33 expression was associated with a higher WHO classification (P < 0.001). Furthermore, we observed that STK33 protein expression was much higher in PanNET specimens than in normal tissue specimens (Fig. 1C, P < 0.001). The clinicopathological characteristics of the PanNET patients and STK33 immunostaining results are summarized in Table 1. STK33 IHC scores were correlated with tumour size (P < 0.001), lymph node metastasis (P = 0.008), perineural invasion (P = 0.002), and AJCC stage (P < 0.001). No significant correlations were detected between STK33 expression and the remaining clinicopathological parameters, such as age, sex or T stage.

We further evaluated whether STK33 expression and clinicopathological parameters correlated with survival among the 102 patients with PanNETs. The results of the univariate analysis of DFS and OS for each of the clinicopathologic variables are shown in Tables 2 and 3. STK33 increases the viability and colony formation ability of PanNET cells

On the basis of our previous research findings, QGP-1 cells expressed higher levels of STK33 mRNA and protein than BON cells did [16]. Therefore, QGP-1 cells were transfected with either shSTK33 or shNC, while BON cells were transiently transfected with either oeSTK33 or the corresponding vector. STK33 expression was evaluated after 48 h. STK33 mRNA and protein expression were markedly decreased in the shSTK33 groups (shSTK33-1, shSTK33-2 and shSTK33-3) compared with the shNC group and control group (both P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A and C), whereas STK33 mRNA and protein expression were markedly increased in the oeSTK33 group compared with the control and vector groups (both P < 0.001) (Fig. 3A and C). Furthermore, the cells were incubated for 72 h; CCK-8 assays were performed every 24 h, and the OD490 values for each group were recorded. As illustrated in Fig. 2D, cell viability was lower in the shSTK33 groups (shSTK33-1 and shSTK33-2) than in the shNC group at each time point (P < 0.001). Additionally, cell viability was significantly greater in the oeSTK33 group than in the vector group at each time point (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3D). Compared with that in the shNC group, cell proliferation in the shSTK33-1 and shSTK33-2 groups was significantly inhibited, as determined by a cell colony formation assay (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2E and F), whereas the overexpression of STK333 promoted colony formation (Fig. 3E). These findings indicate that STK33 plays a key role in PanNET cell proliferation.

STK33 promotes intracellular glucose uptake and lactate production in PanNET cells

Glucose uptake and lactate assays were conducted to investigate whether STK33-induced molecular changes that affect intracellular glucose uptake and lactate production influence aerobic glycolysis. Glucose uptake was assessed using 2-NBDG (2-(N- (7-nitrobenz-2-oxa-1,3-diazol-4-yl) amino)-2-deoxyglucose)). Our flow cytometry results clearly revealed that STK33 knockdown inhibited glucose uptake through the expression of 2-NBDG, as shown in Fig. 2G. Similarly, ATP (p < 0.001) and lactate (p < 0.001) levels displayed similar trends (Fig. 2H and I). Glycolysis is a process of oxidative glucose metabolism in which glucose is broken down into lactate, which produces a small amount of ATP. STK33 overexpression increased glucose uptake and ATP and lactate production (Fig. 3F and H). To directly assess the regulatory role of STK33 on glycolytic flux, we performed real-time dynamic monitoring using ECAR assay (a measure of glycolysis). The results showed that knockdown of STK33 significantly suppressed glycolysis and glycolytic capacity (Fig. 2J). Conversely, overexpression of STK33 markedly enhanced cellular glycolytic activity (Fig. 3I).These results indicate that STK33 effectively promotes aerobic glycolysis, potentially increasing the energy supply required for the cell growth and survival of PanNETs.

STK33 promotes glycolysis by enhancing mTORC1/S6K1 signalling output in PanNET cells

To elucidate the effects of STK33 on glycolysis, we knocked down or overexpressed STK33 and conducted WB assays to measure the levels of the glucose metabolism-related proteins, S6K1, HIF-1α and LDHA. STK33 knockdown significantly inhibited the phosphorylation of S6K1 (a downstream effector of mTORC1) but did not affect the expression of S6K1 (Fig. 2K and L), whereas STK33 overexpression had the opposite effects (Fig. 3J and K). The p-S6K1/total-S6K1 ratio was quantified and is shown in the corresponding bar graphs. The knockdown of STK33 expression significantly inhibited the expression of the HIF-1α and LDHA proteins (Fig. 2M and N). However, STK33 overexpression had the opposite effect (Fig. 3L and M). Furthermore, we treated oeSTK33 cells with the S6K1 inhibitor PF-4,708,671 to verify the role of the mTORC1/S6K1 signalling pathway in STK33-mediated glycolysis. Overexpressing STK33 in BON-1 cells increased cell viability and cell colony formation, and these effects were attenuated by treatment with an S6K1 inhibitor (PF-4708671) (Fig. 3D and E). STK33 overexpression increased glucose uptake and ATP and lactate production, changes that were partially repressed by the S6K1 inhibitor (PF-4708671) (Fig. 3F and I). Moreover, the expression of metabolism-related proteins whose expression was upregulated by STK33 decreased upon treatment with the S6K1 inhibitor (PF-4708671) (Fig. 3J and M). These findings indicate that the effects of STK33 on PanNET are mediated through enhanced mTORC1/S6K1 signalling output. Collectively, STK33 plays important regulatory roles in PanNET cell proliferation and glycolysis via positive regulation of mTORC1/S6K1 signalling and may be an effective molecular target for PanNET treatment.

Experimental validation of STK33-mediated regulation of glycolysis and tumour progression through mTORC1/S6K1 signalling in animal models

We further validated that STK33 affects glycolysis and tumour progression by promoting mTORC1/S6K1 signalling by establishing a xenograft model through the subcutaneous injection of stable STK33-overexpressing BON-1 cells into nude mice. Afterwards, we monitored the volume and weight of the tumours in the mice. The volume (p < 0.001) and weight (p < 0.01) of the tumours were considerably increased after the upregulation of STK33 expression, but were partially reduced by treatment with the S6K1 inhibitor (PF-4708671) (Fig. 4A and C). Tumour growth curves were analysed using repeated-measures two-way ANOVA, which confirmed the significant effects of STK33 overexpression and its partial reversal by PF-4,708,671. Furthermore, the immunofluorescence results indicated that the upregulation of STK33 expression increased the level of Ki67 in tumours, which was attenuated by treatment with an S6K1 inhibitor (PF-4708671) (Fig. 4D). WB analysis revealed that the expression of glycolytic-related proteins within the tumour (p-S6K1, HIF-1α and LDHA) increased concurrently with the upregulation of STK33. Quantification of the p-S6K1/total-S6K1 ratio confirmed enhanced S6K1 phosphorylation. However, these changes were partially reversed by the S6K1 inhibitor (PF-4708671) (Fig. 4E). These findings indicated that an increase in STK33 expression significantly promoted the glycolytic capacity of PanNETs. In summary, the results of in vivo experiments confirmed that STK33 affects glycolysis and tumour progression in PanNETs by enhancing mTORC1/S6K1 signalling output.

Close relationship between HIF-1α and STK33 expression in PanNETs

We investigated the effect of altering the expression of HIF-1α on the expression of STK33 in PanNET cells by infecting QGP-1 cells with shHIF1α-1, shHIF1α-2 or shNC. HIF-1α mRNA and protein expression levels were significantly lower in the shHIF1α groups (shHIF1α-1 and shHIF1α-2 groups) than in the shNC group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5A and C). Moreover, knockdown of HIF-1α in QGP-1 cells markedly downregulated the expressions of both the STK33 mRNA and protein (Fig. 5D and F). We subsequently performed an immunohistochemical analysis of HIF-1α expression using specimens obtained from the same PanNET patient cohort used for STK33 staining. Immunostaining for HIF-1α and STK33 revealed a close correlation between the protein expression levels of STK33 and HIF-1α (Fig. 1E). Importantly, Spearman correlation analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between STK33 and HIF-1α expression in PanNET tissues (ρ = 0.771, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1F). Moreover, similar to the effects of STK33 expression on patient survival, the elevated expression of HIF-1α correlated with shorter DFS for the patients in this study (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1G).

Transcriptional regulation of STK33 expression by HIF-1α in PanNET cells

We subsequently used the Pscan website (http://159.149.160.88/pscan/) to investigate the vital transcription factors that regulate STK33 expression and to determine whether HIF-1α regulates STK33 expression at the transcriptional level. These results suggested that HIF-1α may bind to the ACGTGC sequence within the STK33 promoter (Fig. 5G), which is consistent with the typical hypoxia response element (HRE) sequence (A/G) CGTG [19, 20]. Therefore, we cotransfected the deletion mutant reporters with or without HIF-1α expression vectors into 293T cells. The luciferase reporter assay results revealed that knockdown of HIF-1α markedly reduced the promoter activity of STK33 (Fig. 5H). Furthermore, we used ChIP‒qPCR to quantitatively analyse HIF-1α occupation of the genomic region of the STK33 promoter, which revealed that HIF-1α was enriched in the STK33 promoter region (Fig. 5I). These results indicate that HIF-1α directly binds to hypoxia response elements in the STK33 promoter region and activates its transcription.

Discussion

This study establishes STK33 as a novel oncogenic driver in PanNETs through its dual role in promoting cellular proliferation and aerobic glycolysis. We found that elevated STK33 expression is strongly correlated with aggressive clinicopathological features and shorter disease-free survival in patients, indicating that STK33 is a valuable prognostic biomarker for PanNET patients. Furthermore, STK33 knockdown significantly suppressed cellular proliferation and glycolytic metabolism (reduced glucose uptake, lactate production, and ATP generation) both in vitro and in vivo, whereas STK33 overexpression had the opposite effects. Through mechanistic studies, we elucidated that STK33 specifically promotes mTORC1/S6K1 pathway activation by increasing the phosphorylation of S6K1 without altering total S6K1 protein levels. Furthermore, our data suggest a positive feedback loop in which HIF-1α directly binds to hypoxia response elements in the STK33 promoter region and activates its transcription, whereas STK33 in turn stabilizes the HIF-1α protein through S6K1-mediated mechanisms.

The Warburg effect, characterized by increased glucose uptake and lactate production even under normoxic conditions, represents a hallmark metabolic adaptation in cancer cells that supports rapid proliferation and biomass accumulation [7, 21]. Our study revealed that STK33 plays a central role in the metabolic reprogramming of PanNETs, particularly through its regulation of glycolytic metabolism. The observed STK33-mediated increases in glucose uptake and lactate production create a self-reinforcing metabolic program that provides several advantages to PanNET cells. First, compared with oxidative phosphorylation, accelerated glycolytic flux provides rapid ATP generation to meet the energy demands despite the lower ATP yield per glucose molecule [22, 23]. Second, glycolytic intermediates are shunted into various biosynthetic pathways, including the pentose phosphate pathway for nucleotide synthesis and serine/glycine metabolism for one-carbon units and redox homeostasis [23]. Third, lactate secretion acidifies the tumour microenvironment, promoting extracellular matrix remodelling, angiogenesis, cell invasion and metastasis, and immune evasion through the suppression of cytotoxic T-cell function [23–25]. Therefore, the metabolic dependencies created by STK33 activation may explain the aggressive clinical behaviour observed in STK33-high PanNETs.

mTORC1 and its downstream effector S6K1 constitute a critical signalling axis that integrates environmental cues with cellular metabolic programming [18, 26, 27]. In our study, STK33 promoted cellular proliferation and glycolytic metabolism (increased glucose uptake, lactate production, and ATP generation) via enhancement of mTORC1/S6K1 signalling output, indicating that STK33 is a novel positive regulator of this pathway in PanNETs. This discovery provides several important insights into PanNET metabolism and therapeutic opportunities. (1) The observation that STK33 depletion phenocopies S6K1 inhibition in the suppression of glycolytic metabolism reveals a unique metabolic dependency in STK33-high PanNETs. This vulnerability suggests that combining STK33 targeting (through novel kinase inhibitors) with existing mTORC1 inhibitors (such as everolimus) could overcome the therapeutic resistance that frequently develops in PanNET patients treated with mTOR inhibitors alone [28]. The synthetic lethal interaction between STK33 and S6K1 inhibition provides a strong rationale for developing combination therapies targeting this axis. (2) Our findings demonstrate that in addition to classic hypoxia-mediated stabilization, HIF-1α can be regulated through STK33/S6K1 signalling under normoxic conditions. This expanded understanding of HIF-1α regulation suggests that targeting the STK33/S6K1 axis might effectively modulate HIF-1α activity even in well-oxygenated tumour regions and disrupt multiple aspects of glycolytic metabolism. This finding is particularly relevant given that cancer cells often develop metabolic flexibility and compensatory mechanisms when single metabolic enzymes are targeted [29–31]. The coordinated regulation of upstream signalling (STK33/S6K1), transcriptional control (HIF-1α), and enzymatic activity (LDHA) creates a vulnerable node whose disruption could have profound effects on PanNET cell viability.

Importantly, while our data demonstrate reciprocal correlations and functional dependencies between STK33 and HIF-1α—including HIF-1α-driven transcriptional upregulation of STK33 and STK33-mediated upregulation of HIF-1α protein via mTORC1/S6K1 signaling—the proposed ‘positive feedback loop’ remains a mechanistic model that is strongly supported by correlative and loss-/gain-of-function data but has not yet been validated by formal rescue experiments. Definitive causal proof would require studies such as overexpression of HIF-1α in STK33-knockdown cells to assess restoration of glycolytic phenotypes, or reconstitution of constitutively active S6K1 in STK33-deficient cells to establish pathway hierarchy. While our data demonstrate that STK33 enhances HIF-1α protein levels via the mTORC1/S6K1 pathway, the precise molecular mechanism (e.g., direct phosphorylation of HIF-1α by S6K1, regulation of upstream translation factors, or interference with the VHL/proteasome degradation pathway) is not yet defined. Future studies using cycloheximide chase, proteasome inhibition, and assessment of HIF-1α hydroxylation status will be required to fully elucidate this regulation. Further investigation should include examination of additional mTORC1 substrates (e.g., p-S6 and p-4EBP1) and orthogonal validation via rapamycin rescue experiments to fully characterize the signaling network.

Several limitations should be considered when our findings are interpreted. First, the retrospective design and single-centre nature of our study may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the results. Second, as our cohort exclusively consisted of patients who underwent surgical resection, the clinical significance of STK33 in advanced, inoperable tumours remains to be elucidated. Third, the BON-1 and QGP-1 cell lines used in our functional studies may harbour TP53 mutations, which could influence the observed phenotypic effects. Fourth, while glucose uptake, ATP, lactate, and ECAR measurements collectively support glycolytic reprogramming, they do not capture metabolic flux or distinguish aerobic glycolysis from other metabolic pathways. Future studies using stable isotope tracing (e.g., ¹³C-glucose) and Seahorse real-time flux analyses would provide deeper insight. Furthermore, the limited sample size in the G3 subgroup may influence the statistical power of the survival analyses. Future larger, multicentre prospective studies incorporating patients across all disease stages, including those receiving systemic therapies, will be essential to validate the roles of STK33 in PanNET progression and metabolic reprogramming.

Conclusions

The results of the current study reveal the important role of STK33 as a key regulator of PanNET metabolism and progression through positive regulation of the mTORC1/S6K1/HIF-1α signalling axis, opening new avenues for understanding the molecular pathogenesis of these tumours and developing novel targeted therapies for patients with advanced disease.
