Response of cyanobacterial blooms to climate warming: evidence from satellite observations and long-term trends in Lake Taihu in China
Authors: Xinyi Li, Xin Hang, Shihua Zhu, Liangxiao Sun, Yachun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is still a lack of fundamental understanding of how climate warming drives the expansion of cyanobacterial blooms in freshwater ecosystems. This paper explores the impacts of climate warming on cyanobacterial blooms on the basis of long-term (2003–2022) satellite images and meteorological observation data, with Lake Taihu in China taken as an example. These results indicate that over the past two decades, with climate warming, cyanobacterial blooms have significantly expanded. For every 1 °C increase in the annual average temperature, the cumulative area of cyanobacterial blooms will increase by approximately 5,377 km2. The date of the first observed blooms is negatively correlated (R = -0.73) with the average winter–spring temperature, which increased by 39 days per decade on average. The date of the last observed blooms is positively correlated (R = 0.6) with the average temperature from November–December, with a delay of 18 days per decade. Furthermore, the temperature response curve of cyanobacterial blooms derived from satellites also conforms to an “S-shaped” pattern. The evidence provided by this study demonstrates that climate warming promotes the expansion of cyanobacterial blooms in this lake and that the synergistic effects of climate change should be incorporated into strategies for controlling cyanobacterial blooms.Keywords: Climate warming, Cyanobacterial blooms, Synergistic effects, Controlling strategy, Lake TaihuSubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Limnology

Introduction

Over the past centuries, nutrient over-enrichment associated with human activities has accelerated the eutrophication of freshwater ecosystems and promoted the periodic proliferation of cyanobacterial blooms1–3. The continuous expansion of cyanobacterial blooms is of great societal concern because harmful cyanobacteria can pose a threat to fisheries and aquaculture, entertainment, and tourism activities and even adversely affect safe supplies of drinking water4–7. Although extensive studies have been conducted to prevent this growing threat, the response mechanism of cyanobacterial blooms to various environmental factors and the crucial factors driving their competitive advantage are still unclear or controversial, resulting in difficulties in effectively predicting the occurrence, duration, and severity of cyanobacterial blooms8–10. These unresolved issues have weakened the effectiveness of strategies aimed at controlling cyanobacterial blooms and reducing their harm11.

Considerable research efforts have been devoted to exploring the environmental drivers of cyanobacterial blooms in geographically diverse freshwater ecosystems of varying sizes, depths, shapes, salinities, and hydrological conditions1,5,12. For example, increasing salinity levels can affect the structure and function of planktonic and benthic microalgal communities, leading to an increase in vertical density stratification, which will be beneficial for cyanobacteria that can utilize such stratification1. Larger and stronger precipitation events may mobilize nutrients on land surrounding waters, increase nutrient enrichment in water bodies and ultimately promote the cyanobacterial bloom potentials1,12.There is a consensus that the competitive traits of cyanobacteria are further amplified and that blooms are triggered under favorable environmental conditions3,13, which include increased anthropogenic nutrient loading, climate warming and increased atmospheric CO2 supplies8,14. Importantly, it is generally believed that the formation of cyanobacterial blooms is a rather complex process that is typically not caused by a single environmental factor but rather by multiple factors occurring simultaneously3,8,15,16.

There is a growing recognition of the crucial role of climate change in this continuous expansion of cyanobacterial blooms globally17,18. Climate change and eutrophication, which are influenced mainly by human activities, are two processes that promote the proliferation and expansion of cyanobacterial blooms3. Under the regulation of local and large-scale climate change, the cyanobacterial biomass in different locations often exhibits significant inter-annual variations19. Increasing evidence suggests that climate change may be the main driver of the increasing frequency and severity of cyanobacterial blooms in eutrophic freshwater ecosystems20–22.

Climate warming is the most significant feature of global change, with average temperatures rising at an unprecedented rate in human history in recent decades23,24. Climate warming accelerates the eutrophication of freshwater ecosystems by altering rainfall patterns, soil warming, glacier melting, and other non-point sources that increase nutrient loads25. The warming effect may promote cyanobacteria growth and more adapted to higher temperature environments, increase the primary productivity of phytoplankton, and accelerate the formation of algal lakes25,26. Climate warming will promote the rapid reproduction of omnivorous fish to become dominant populations, replacing planktonic animals, thereby reducing the control ability of lake ecosystems over algae and indirectly promoting excessive proliferation of algae25,26. In addition, climate warming can lead to changes in precipitation patterns, altering the flux of nutrients into lakes and the hydraulic retention time of water bodies, ultimately resulting in eutrophication of water bodies25,27.

Climate warming coupled with eutrophication may promote the expansion and severity of cyanobacteria by increasing their dominance, growth rates, duration, geographic distribution and activity3,9,28–30. Cyanobacteria generally exhibit higher growth rates at relatively higher temperatures (usually above 25 °C) compared to eukaryotic algae such as diatoms and dinoflagellates1,3. Climate warming may alter the structure of microbial communities in water bodies, making them more conducive to the growth of cyanobacteria9. Observations and experimental studies have confirmed a positive correlation between the elevated mean water temperature and the cyanobacterial biomass level24,31,32. This is due to the increased stability of the water body caused by rising temperatures, which prolongs the duration of thermal stratification and thereby enhances the growth rate of cyanobacteria33,34. Rising temperatures lead to a shift to an earlier occurrence and a later disappearance of cyanobacterial blooms, thereby extending their annual lifecycle19,24,35. In temperate lakes, some cyanobacteria species can form winter blooms and even persist throughout the year30,35. Climate warming can strongly influence cyanobacteria dynamics at different stages of their life history, all the way from benthic nutrient supplementation to cyanobacteria proliferation and from establishment in the population community to bloom formation24.

However, the interaction mechanism between climate warming, cyanobacterial blooms, and eutrophication of water bodies is currently not fully understood36–40. In particular, most of the current research on the link between climate warming and cyanobacterial blooms is based on limited and often contradictory experimental and observational data36,41. Therefore, there remains an urgent need for more empirical research on this issue to understand how climate warming affects cyanobacterial blooms and to what extent it exacerbates them. This information is crucial for adopting scientific strategies to control cyanobacterial blooms and manage water resources.

Lake Taihu, located in the Yangtze River Delta of China, is a large, shallow, and eutrophic inland lake. In recent decades, due to the synergistic effects of eutrophication and climate change, the lake has suffered from frequent and severe cyanobacterial blooms12. This study aims to quantitatively assess the impacts of climate warming on cyanobacterial blooms in this lake by utilizing long-term meteorological and satellite observation data, thereby expanding the understanding of the synergistic effects between climate change and cyanobacterial blooms, and also providing a basis for adjusting the current control strategy that focuses on nutrient reduction.

Materials and methods

Study area and data

Lake Taihu (30◦50–32◦80 N, 119◦80–121◦550 E), the third-largest freshwater lake in China with an area of approximately 2338 km2 and an average depth of 1.9 m, is located in the most economically and socially developed Yangtze River Delta region (Fig. 1). The Lake Taihu Basin falls in the mild and moist northern subtropical monsoon climate zone, with an annual average temperature of 15–17 °C. Southeast winds prevail in summer, and northerly winds prevail in winter. Since the 1990 s, climate change, rapid urbanization and human activities have exacerbated eutrophication accompanied by cyanobacterial blooms (Microcystis spp.) in this lake. In particular, large-scale cyanobacterial blooms broke out in Lake Taihu in 2007, which directly affected the drinking water supply of Wuxi city and caused a drinking water crisis12. Although water environment management strategies in recent years have achieved remarkable results in alleviating eutrophication, frequent cyanobacterial blooms are still the main ecological problem facing Lake Taihu. It is noted that the eastern coastal area of Lake Taihu is a clean water area with abundant of aquatic plants42,43, and this study does not cover this region (yellow part in Figure.1).

Locations of Lake Taihu and surrounding national basic meteorological stations.

The meteorological observation data used in this study were obtained from the Jiangsu Climate Center. The hourly temperature observation data during 2003–2022 were collected from the five national basic meteorological observation stations around Lake Taihu, including Dongshan, Wujiang, Suzhou, Wuxi and Yixing stations (Fig. 1). The daily, monthly, seasonal, and annual average temperatures of the five stations were calculated according to the method specified in the national standard 《Specifications for surface meteorological observation—Air temperature and humidity》(GB/T 35226 − 2017).The satellite images were obtained from the data reception stations of the National Satellite Meteorological Center and the Jiangsu Climate Centre. A total of 14,600 MODIS/AQUA and MODIS/Terra images from 2003 to 2022 were collected in this study to extract information on cyanobacterial blooms.

Cyanobacterial bloom retrieval methods

Before retrieving cyanobacterial blooms, preprocessing such as cloud detection and atmospheric correction is required for MODIS images. In this study, the MODIS cloud products (MOD/MYD35) were used to perform cloud masking on satellite images to eliminate the interference from clouds44. To eliminate the atmospheric influence on inland lakes, the Second Simulation of the Satellite Signal in the Solar Spectrum (6 S) model was used to perform atmospheric correction on the Top of Atmosphere (TOA) reflectance measured by the satellites45.

The theoretical basis for inverting cyanobacterial blooms via remote sensing technology is that dense cyanobacterial blooms have spectral characteristics similar to those of land vegetation39. The spectral reflectance curve of dense cyanobacterial blooms obtained from field measurements in Lake Taihu revealed lower reflectivity in the red band and higher reflectivity in the near-infrared band with increasing chlorophyll concentration, which is also known as the “red edge effect”45,46. On the basis of this spectral feature, cyanobacterial blooms can be identified via several indicators, such as the band ratio, normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), and FAI, which involve the red and near-infrared bands45. The NDVI threshold was determined via a commonly used method of integrating visual analysis with statistics45,47. A total of 100 pairs of MODIS RGB and NDVI images with cyanobacterial bloom were randomly collected as samples. Firstly, visual analysis was conducted on each RGB image to determine the boundaries between cyanobacteria and water, and the average NDVI value of all pixels along this boundary in the corresponding NDVI image was calculated as the identification threshold for cyanobacterial bloom in this image. Then, using the same method, the NDVI thresholds for the cyanobacteria -water boundaries of the 100 NDVI images were calculated, ranging from − 0.0086 to −0.0325 with an average of −0.02. Finally, we determined − 0.02 as the threshold for identifying cyanobacterial bloom in this study. Using this method, a total of 1973 images with bloom areas of ≥ 1 square kilometer were obtained from satellite images of Lake Taihu from 2003 to 2022 for research purposes.

The area of cyanobacterial bloom in an image is defined as the sum of the pixel areas where cyanobacterial bloom is detected and the cumulative area refers to the total area within a specific time period. Here, the frequency refers to the number of cyanobacterial bloom occurrence time within a specific time period. In addition, the first day of each year when cyanobacterial blooms are detected by satellite is defined as the first observed date (FOD) of cyanobacterial blooms, the final day of detection within the same year is termed the last observed date (LOD), and LOD-FOD can represent the duration of cyanobacterial blooms.

Results

Increase in temperature in lake Taihu

The annual and seasonal average temperature from 2003 to 2022 was calculated from the temperature observation data of the national basic meteorological stations around the Lake Taihu (Fig. 2). The annual average temperature is 17.1 °C, showing a fluctuating upward trend, with an average increase of 0.4 °C per decade (). In particular, in recent years, the annual average temperature has repeatedly broken historical extremes. Like the annual average temperature, the average temperature in each season also shows a fluctuating upward trend, with an average increase of 0.2~0.7 °C per decade. The average temperature increase rate in spring and winter is 0.6 °C per decade, significantly higher than the 0.2 °C in summer and autumn. It is believed that this seasonal warming feature is more conducive to the overwintering and survival of cyanobacteria in winter, as well as their recovery and growth in spring, providing sufficient cyanobacterial biomass for the formation of more and larger areas of cyanobacterial blooms in the future. This occurred because the average temperature in summer and autumn is relatively high in the Lake Taihu area and is within the temperature range suitable for the formation of cyanobacterial blooms, whereas the average temperature in winter and spring is relatively low, and the relatively low temperature often limits the formation of cyanobacterial blooms.

Spatiotemporal distribution of satellite-derived cyanobacterial blooms

Using the aforementioned method, 1,973 images with cyanobacterial bloom areas ≥ 1 km² were obtained from clear or cloudy satellite imagery between 2003 and 2022. The cumulative areas are calculated for each month, season, and year on the basis of the area of cyanobacterial blooms in each image, and then the cumulative frequencies are determined. In the past 20 years, the monthly cumulative area of cyanobacterial blooms has fluctuated, with May to November each year being the period of frequent occurrence of cyanobacterial blooms (Fig. 3a). The relatively serious periods of cyanobacterial blooms in Lake Taihu were 2006—2007, 2017 and 2019—2020, and the area was significantly greater than that in other years (details in the red box marked in Fig. 3a). In the past 20 years, the cumulative annual area of cyanobacterial blooms has increased by 3566 km2/decade on average. The areas in 2006, 2007, 2017, 2019, and 2020 were 30% greater than average, while the areas in 2003, 2004, 2009, and 2014 were 30% lower than average, indicating significant inter-annual differences in cyanobacterial bloom areas (Fig. 3a and 6). Unlike the area, the cumulative frequencies of cyanobacterial blooms presented a continuous increasing trend over time (Fig. 3b). Figure 3b shows that although the frequency was also high in some months of 2010, 2014, and 2016, the annual cumulative frequency from 2003 to 2022 showed a significant positive correlation over time (, =0.92). Considering that the nutrient levels of a specific lake are usually unlikely to undergo severe changes in a short period of time, the significant inter-annual or monthly differences in cyanobacterial blooms may be the result of changes in climate, hydrology, and other factors.

The time trends of annual cumulative cyanobacterial bloom area and annual average temperature from 2003 to 2022.

Figure 4 shows the spatial distribution of the annual cumulative frequencies of cyanobacterial blooms in Lake Taihu in some years, reflecting changes in the spatial coverage of cyanobacterial blooms since 2003. As depicted in the figure, the distribution of blooms in 2003 was relatively limited and was primarily confined to the northwestern lakeshore regions. By 2007, large-scale and high-frequency outbreaks had occurred, with cumulative coverage extending to nearly the entire lake area, except for a small unmonitored zone in the southeastern corner. Despite the severity of blooms being mitigated through enhanced water pollution control measures, their spatial extent has persistently covered most of the lake, and the occurrence frequencies remained at elevated levels. As evidenced by the observational findings, cyanobacterial blooms in Lake Taihu have exhibited greater frequency, longer duration, and expanded spatial coverage over the past two decades.

Response of cyanobacterial bloom on temperature

Current studies on the temperature dependence of algae are mainly from laboratory research, while investigations focusing on satellite-derived cyanobacterial blooms remain limited28. Figure 5 depicts the curve of the cumulative area and cumulative times of cyanobacterial blooms with air temperature in the interval of 5 ℃.We found that the relationship curve between cyanobacterial blooms and air temperature formed a relatively typical “S-shaped curve”. This figure depicts the response process of cyanobacterial blooms to temperature. The area and frequency of cyanobacterial blooms initiate a progressive increase above average temperature of 5 °C, whereas they are rarely observed prior to which. When the temperature exceeds approximately 15 °C, the area and frequency of cyanobacterial blooms increase rapidly. At 15 °C, both metrics account for over 10% of the total, rising to more than 20% at 20 °C. The peak occurs at 30 °C, with the area and frequency proportion reaching 32% and 28%, respectively, followed by a sharp decline beyond this temperature. These findings suggest that relatively high temperatures within 30 °C may favor cyanobacterial proliferation and bloom formation, whereas elevated temperatures above 30 °C can suppress bloom development.

Response curves of cyanobacterial bloom area and frequency to temperature in Lake Taihu, which are roughly exhibited an “S-shaped curve”. The values are the percentages of the cumulative area and frequency within a temperature range of 5 °C to the total area and frequencies.

Influence of warming climate on cyanobacterial blooms

The above analysis indicates that the Lake Taihu region has experienced significant warming over the past two decades, with an average annual temperature increase of 0.4 °C per decade. Accompanying this persistent warming, cyanobacterial blooms have shown a substantial expansion trend in the lake. Overall, there was a positive correlation between annual cumulative bloom area and annual average temperature (S) (Fig. 6). The regression slope indicates that for every 1 °C increase in the annual average temperature, the annual cumulative area of cyanobacterial blooms will increase by approximately 5377 km2. Moreover, both the annual average temperature and the area of cyanobacterial blooms exhibited distinctive phase characteristics during this period. The annual average temperature can be roughly divided into three distinct phases: 2003–2007 (Phase Ⅰ), 2008–2012 (Phase Ⅱ), and 2013–2022 (Phase Ⅲ), corresponding to three distinct periods of temperature trends: rising, falling, and rising, respectively. Similarly, cyanobacterial blooms exhibited a similar pattern, with significant expansion during the warming phase and contraction during the cooling phase. The correlation coefficients between the annual average temperature and cyanobacterial bloom area in these three stages were 0.74 (Phase Ⅰ), 0.77 (Phase Ⅱ), and 0.37 (Phase Ⅲ). Our findings confirm the increasing ecological risk of cyanobacterial bloom expansion under climate warming.

Figure 7 shows the temporal trends of the first observed date (FOD) and last observed date (LOD) of cyanobacterial blooms from 2003 to 2022 in Lake Taihu. In the past two decades, with increasing temperature, the timing of the first observed cyanobacterial bloom has generally advanced, whereas the last observed date has progressively delayed. On average, the FOD in the second decade was 41 days earlier than that in the first decade, whereas the LOD was 23 days later. Correspondingly, the duration of blooms significantly increased from 238 days in the first decade to 302 days in the second decade, an increase of 27%. This means that two decades ago, in 2003, cyanobacterial blooms were observed only within a few months (primarily in the warmer summer and autumn seasons), whereas they are now observed nearly year-round.

Discussion

Here, we provide empirical evidences that climate warming regulates cyanobacterial blooms in inland lakes. Against the backdrop of global warming, the climate in the Lake Taihu region of China has exhibited a persistent warming trend over recent decades, with the annual average temperature increasing by 0.4 °C per decade. Particularly in recent years, the annual average temperature has repeatedly reached record-breaking levels. Our results also suggest that relative warming amplitude in winter and spring are significantly greater than that in autumn and summer. This seasonal warming feature is more conducive to the overwintering and survival of cyanobacteria in winter, as well as their recovery and growth in spring24. Corresponding to the characteristics of regional climate warming and seasonal warming, cyanobacterial blooms in Lake Taihu have shown a distinct expansion trend in the recent two decades. Specifically, the cumulative annual area of cyanobacterial blooms has increased by 3566 km2/decade on average. There is a positive correlation between cyanobacterial bloom area and annual average temperature, with a correlation coefficient of 0.44. For every 1 °C increase in the annual average temperature, the annual cumulative area of cyanobacterial blooms will increase by approximately 5377 km2. Winter–spring warming advances bloom onset by 39 days/decade, whereas autumn warming delays dissipation by 18 days/decade. Thus, the duration of blooms significantly increased from 238 days in the first decade to 302 days in the second decade, an increase of 27%. This finding is consistent with the results of Kim et al.38, who also reported the linkage between climate warming and the earlier onset and extended duration of cyanobacterial blooms in temperate rivers. However, in contrast to Kim et al.38, we found that climate warming significantly delayed the end date of cyanobacterial blooms, which is similar to the results observed by Chen et al.49.

Furthermore, our research reveals the temperature-dependent characteristics of cyanobacterial blooms obtained from real lake environments rather than laboratory settings. We found that the area and frequency proportion of cyanobacterial blooms at 5 °C intervals exhibit a characteristic S-shaped curve in response to air temperature changes. This indicates that cyanobacterial blooms increase slowly at lower air temperatures, experience accelerated increase when the average temperature exceeds 15 °C, peak around 30 °C, and subsequently decline rapidly thereafter. This process is similar to the temperature-dependent growth rate variation process of Microcystis, as reported by Paerl et al.28. The relevant experimental results indicated that at relatively high temperatures, Microcystis communities typically exhibit optimal growth rates and demonstrate the most effective competitive dominance against other species (e.g., Chlorophyta, Bacillariophyta, and Dinophyta)24. Considering that Microcystis is dominant in Lake Taihu50, it is reasonable that cyanobacterial blooms primarily composed of Microcystis exhibit similar temperature‒response characteristics to those of the species itself. This finding demonstrates that satellite-derived cyanobacterial blooms in natural lake environments exhibit temperature-dependent behavior similar to that of laboratory-cultured Microcystis.

The evidences suggest that it is time to incorporate the synergistic effects of climate warming into strategies for mitigating cyanobacterial blooms in lakes. The current cyanobacterial bloom management approaches mainly focus on reducing nutrient inputs, while the compounding effects of climate change have not been factored into mitigation strategies for cyanobacterial blooms11,34,51. As a result, climate warming is weakening the effectiveness of strategies aimed at controlling cyanobacterial blooms and will seriously affect our ability to control cyanobacterial blooms in the future34,51. Especially, it can be anticipated that the future climate will further increase the intensity and frequency of cyanobacterial blooms due to the expected global average temperature increase of 1.5 °C or more in the coming decades28,36,52. Therefore, it is urgent to adjust the existing strategies for controlling cyanobacterial blooms and managing water resources. First, reducing nutrient inputs is an obvious target because they have been demonstrated to be effective in improving water quality, they can be manipulated and should therefore form the core component of any bloom mitigation strategy. However, measures aimed at reducing nutrient inputs to curb eutrophication and potential cyanobacterial blooms require to be careful evaluated and potential adjusted to accommodate higher cyanobacterial bloom broke potentials due to warming8. Second, given that climate warming has led to earlier onset, delayed dissipation, and prolonged duration of cyanobacterial blooms, management authorities need to respond in advance by extending control periods throughout the year. Controlling Microcystis during winter helps reduce the magnitude of cyanobacterial blooms during the warm seasons, while control efforts can be appropriately relaxed in summer due to the inhibitory effects of high temperatures. Additionally, it is necessary for scientists to seek more compelling evidence to demonstrate that climate warming contributes to the prevalence of cyanobacterial blooms, further elucidate the synergistic mechanisms between climate change and these blooms, and provide a more reliable basis for effectively mitigating cyanobacterial blooms.

Accumulating evidences have suggested that the persistent expansion of cyanobacterial blooms under global changing is primarily due to rising temperatures24,38,40. Consequently, most studies on the impact of climate change on cyanobacterial blooms have focused on temperature variations23,24,29,30. However, since cyanobacterial blooms are the result of the synergistic effects of various climatic factors, no single climatic factor such as temperature, wind, precipitation, or sunlight can comprehensively describe the potential linkages between climate change and cyanobacterial blooms41,53. We note that in Fig. 6, although the average annual temperatures in 2004 and 2016–2020 are relatively close, there is a significant difference in the annual cumulative area of cyanobacterial blooms. This also indicates that, besides temperature, changes in other climatic factors, nutrient levels, and human intervention may influence cyanobacterial blooms. Current research on the impact of climate change on cyanobacterial blooms primarily relies on the control variable method to quantify the contribution rates of individual climatic factors, which has obvious limitations36,54, and this paper is no exception. It is necessary to focus on the influence of other climatic factors as well as the comprehensive impacts of climate change. Additionally, atmospheric effects, cloud cover, and aquatic plants can interfere with the accurate acquisition of cyanobacteria information in inland waters, and there is no universally applicable remote sensing inversion algorithm for cyanobacterial blooms55. Future research should primarily focus on improving algorithms to eliminate the aforementioned interferences and developing a generalized algorithm for cyanobacterial bloom inversion.

Conclusion

In this study, we provide the empirical evidences that local climate warming promotes the expansion of cyanobacterial blooms in Lake Taihu in China. Our research reveals that the climate in this region has exhibited a persistent warming trend and rising temperatures have exacerbated the scale and severity of cyanobacterial blooms in this lake, which are characterized by increased coverage area, increased frequency, earlier onset, delayed dissipation, prolonged duration, and expanding spatial distribution. Concurrently, we also find that the temperature response curve of satellite-derived cyanobacterial blooms exhibits an “S-shaped curve”, confirming that moderate temperature increases can promote the formation of cyanobacterial blooms, whereas high temperatures above 30 °C can suppress their development. This research advances our understanding of how climate warming and eutrophication may synergistically affect cyanobacterial blooms in inland lakes, providing a foundation for future studies focused on quantitatively assessing the combined impacts of climate warming on cyanobacterial blooms.
