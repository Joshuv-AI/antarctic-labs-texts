Comparison of the accuracy of removable partial denture frameworks fabricated using conventional and digital impressions: a clinical study
Authors: Myeong Ah Yoon, Minji Sun, Jeongho Jeon, You-Jung Kang, Jee Hwan Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSERemovable partial dentures (RPDs) require metal frameworks for support, stability, and retention. Conventional impression methods are time-consuming and may introduce inaccuracies that affect framework fit. Intraoral scanning (IOS) offers a digital alternative that may improve accuracy and efficiency; however, few studies have quantitatively compared frameworks fabricated using IOS data with those fabricated using conventional methods. This study aimed to compare the accuracy of RPD frameworks fabricated using conventional and IOS-based methods.MATERIALS AND METHODSThis study included 15 arches from 13 patients requiring RPDs. Each arch received two metal frameworks, one fabricated using the conventional impression method (CON group) and the other using IOS (IOS group). Qualitative evaluations included visual inspection and pressing with a plugger, whereas quantitative evaluations were performed using three-dimensional superimposition and gap measurements at the rest-seat areas. Paired t-tests were used to compare the accuracy, and two-way analysis of variance was used to evaluate the interaction between the fabrication method and tooth position.RESULTSAll frameworks met the qualitative evaluation criteria. In the quantitative evaluation, the IOS group exhibited a significantly smaller mean gap (201 ± 78 µm) than the CON group (239 ± 83 µm) (P = .015). Furthermore, the IOS group demonstrated significantly greater accuracy, particularly at the terminal abutments of distal-extension RPDs.CONCLUSIONClinically acceptable RPD framework accuracy was achieved in both groups. However, accuracy was significantly better in the IOS group, especially at the terminal abutments of distal-extension RPDs.Keywords: 3D deviation analysis; Accuracy; Digital removable partial denture; Framework, Fully digital workflow; Intraoral scanning

INTRODUCTION

Removable partial dentures (RPDs) play an important role in restoring oral function and aesthetics in patients with missing teeth. The metal framework of an RPD serves as the foundation for the prosthesis, ensuring adequate support, stability, and retention. Therefore, its significance cannot be understated, as an accurate and well-fitting framework determines the success and longevity of the RPD.1,2,3 With the introduction of computer-aided design-computer-aided manufacturing (CAD-CAM) technologies, numerous clinical applications of digital technologies have emerged in dentistry. Nonetheless, owing to the complexity of combining metal frameworks with resin bases, much of the manufacturing process of RPDs still relies on conventional techniques, such as impression-making and framework fabrication using the lost-wax technique. However, the conventional method is time-consuming, and the complexity of the framework may compromise accuracy.4

With technological advancement, the application of digital technologies for manufacturing of RPDs has increased, and several strategies have emerged to streamline the process. Particularly, three-dimensional (3D) printing techniques, such as selective laser sintering, are becoming increasingly popular for the fabrication of RPD frameworks. This method can compensate for the limitations of the conventional technique by enhancing fracture resistance through prevention of void formation and distortion, and simplifying the overall laboratory process for fabrication of metal frameworks.5,6,7,8,9 Recent studies have demonstrated that frameworks fabricated using additive manufacturing have acceptable accuracy.10,11,12 Furthermore, intraoral scanning (IOS), can be used to obtain detailed 3D images of partially edentulous dental arches. Moreover, digital impressions can provide the same information as that obtained using conventional impression materials. By combining the additive manufacturing method with digital impressions, a modeless environment can be established for manufacturing RPDs. This method provides several advantages such as increased patient comfort, streamlined workflow, and reduced material costs. Furthermore, few studies have reported excellent clinical outcomes using frameworks fabricated by additive manufacturing.2,4 However, quantitative analyses and clinical studies on the accuracy of frameworks fabricated using IOS and additive manufacturing are lacking.

The accuracy of RPD frameworks can be evaluated using various methods, including visual inspection, microscopy, or conventional approaches involving silicone impressions.2,4,10,11 Although these methods are widely used, they often have limitations in terms of reproducibility and potential damage to the prosthesis during evaluation. Furthermore, evaluations are restricted to specific sections or measurement points, making comprehensive and multidimensional assessment challenging. In contrast, multidimensional analysis facilitated by superimposition of 3D scan data provides distinct advantages for evaluating RPD frameworks.13,14 Moreover, this technique enables non-invasive and precise evaluations while preserving the structural integrity of the definitive prosthesis intended for clinical application. By offering a comprehensive multidimensional analysis of each framework component, these digital methods significantly enhance the accuracy and reliability of fit assessments, effectively addressing the inherent limitations of conventional techniques.

This study aimed to compare the accuracy of frameworks fabricated using additive manufacturing with conventional impression-making methods, against those fabricated from IOS data. The null hypothesis was that there is no significant difference in the gap between the rest and rest seat between metal frameworks fabricated through additive manufacturing using conventional impressions and those fabricated using IOS data.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This clinical study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yonsei University Dental Hospital (IRB no. 2-2021-0045). Arches requiring RPDs were selected from patients visiting the Department of Prosthodontics at Yonsei University Dental Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment in the study. The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.4). With an effect size of 0.70, α = 0.05, and power (1-β) = 0.80, the minimum number of participants required was 12. After tooth preparation including rest-seat preparation and insertion of surveyed crowns, each participant received two metal frameworks — one each fabricated using the digital workflow and conventional impression method — and the clinical accuracy of the two frameworks was compared (Fig. 1).

Flowchart showing the procedures in each group. CON, conventional impression method; IOS, intraoral scanning method.

In the conventional impression group (CON group), an individual (custom) tray was fabricated for each case. Border molding (Peri compound, GC, Tokyo, Japan) was performed in the edentulous regions to ensure accurate capture of the soft tissue contours. A silicone impression material (Aquasil, Dentsply Sirona, Charlotte, NC, USA) was then used to make the final impression, and a stone cast was subsequently fabricated (Die Stone Extreme, DK Munkyo, Daegu, Korea). The cast was scanned using a tabletop scanner (E3, 3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark), and the framework was designed using CAD software (Dental System, 3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark). The framework was fabricated by selective laser melting (SLM) using Cr-Co alloy (Mediloy S-Cp, BEGO GmbH & Co. KG, Bremen, Germany) and a metal printer (Dual 150, Riton, Guangzhou, China). The print orientation was aligned with the survey direction, and the layer height was set at 30 µm. Post-processing was performed in a sintering furnace at 950℃ for 240 minutes. The framework was fitted to the stone cast by a dental technician (M. S. W).

In the experimental group (IOS group), intraoral scanning was conducted using a 3D scanner (Trios 3, 3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark). The scanning path was standardized based on the Kennedy classification. For Class I and Class II distal extension edentulous cases, the scan was initiated from the anterior teeth, followed by the remaining dentition, and subsequently extended into the edentulous region. In Class IV cases, the scanning process was initiated from the posterior teeth, proceeding with the occlusal surfaces and followed by the buccal and palatal surfaces in sequence. The STL file obtained from the scan was imported into CAD software (Dental System, 3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark) for framework design. Unlike the conventional group, which utilized physical impressions and stone casts, the experimental group bypassed these steps by directly using IOS data. However, framework fabrication was performed by selective laser melting (SLM) using a cobalt-chromium alloy (Mediloy S-Cp, BEGO GmbH & Co. KG, Bremen, Germany) and a metal printer (Dual 150, Riton, Guangzhou, China), consistent with the conventional group’s manufacturing process. To evaluate the fit, the arch STL file was fabricated by additive manufacturing using photopolymer resin (Sindoh, Seoul, Korea), and the framework was pre-fitted on the resin cast by the same dental technician.

At the framework-fitting appointment, both conventionally and digitally fabricated frameworks were seated intraorally. Minimal clinical adjustments were performed on the fitting surfaces of the removable partial denture (RPD) frameworks when necessary. Following a standardized protocol applied to both groups, minor interferences were relieved to facilitate complete seating. These adjustments were limited to minor surface modifications and excluded major reshaping, clasp bending, or connector alterations. As most frameworks exhibited adequate fit at the initial try-in and required only minimal modification, the influence of these adjustments on the final fit assessment was considered negligible.

The fit of the frameworks was evaluated qualitatively by the clinician through visual inspection and a pressing test. Visual inspection included confirming the complete seating of all occlusal rests, ensuring that all rigid components adapted accurately to the abutment surfaces, verifying that the major connector did not impinge upon underlying soft tissues, and confirming that the visible relief space between the framework and supporting tissues was less than 1 mm.10 The pressing test was performed by placing a plugger perpendicular to the occlusal plane on each rest seat and applying firm but controlled pressure. The clinician looked for any detectable movement of the framework during pressure application. A stable framework without movement was interpreted as indicative of accurate adaptation and intimate fit. This clinical technique has been previously described as an effective method for confirming proper engagement of the rest seats and evaluating the passive fit of the RPD frameworks.15

Quantitative evaluation was performed for frameworks that met the qualitative evaluation criteria. Scan powder (Scan spray, VITA, Bad Säckingen, Germany) was applied as thinly and evenly as possible from a distance of 3 cm.16 Each framework was three-dimensionally scanned using a table-top scanner (Freedom UHD, DOF, Seongnam, Korea) to obtain STL files of the frameworks fabricated using the conventional method (CF) and IOS method (IF). Subsequently, vinyl polysiloxane (VPS) adhesive (VPS tray adhesive, 3M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) was applied to the rest areas of the framework and allowed to dry thoroughly. The patient’s oral cavity was dried, and a VPS impression material (Aquasil XLV, Dentsply Sirona, Charlotte, NC, USA) was applied to the rest areas of the framework before seating it in the patient’s mouth (Fig. 2A). Hand pressure was applied to the rest areas for 5 min until the impression material set. After setting, excess fin-like impression material around the framework was trimmed using scissors. Scan powder was sprayed again, and the framework was scanned again using the tabletop scanner. Thus, STL files of the framework showing the gap between the patient’s tissues and the conventional framework (CF+D) and IOS framework (IF+D), recorded as the VPS thickness, were obtained. For the quantitative fit analysis, scans included all surfaces of the RPD frameworks, both internal and external. This thorough scanning approach enabled precise superimposition and gap measurement by comparing the scanned framework surfaces with those of the corresponding cast. Evaluating the entire framework structure provided a comprehensive assessment of fit accuracy, avoiding the limitation of focusing on isolated areas.

Quantitative evaluation of frameworks. (A) Rests with vinyl polysiloxane, (B) Measurement of the gap distance using metrology software, (C) Diagram showing the division of the rest into central and peripheral areas.

Data were analyzed using metrology software (GOM Inspect 2018, Carl Zeiss GOM Metrology GmbH, Braunschweig, Germany). CF+D and CF were superimposed using the best-fit alignment to measure the gap between the rest and rest seat (Fig. 2B). The entire rest area (Zone C+P) was divided into eight segments to measure the central (Zone C) and peripheral (Zone P) regions separately (Fig. 2C). The minimum, mean, and maximum gap between the rest and rest seat were measured for each selected area. The same method was used to compare IF+D with IF. Based on the obtained measurements, the most clinically appropriate framework was selected for tooth arrangement and RPD fabrication.

The primary evaluation criterion was the mean thickness of the VPS impression material in different areas. A thickness ≤ 50 µm was defined as contact.17 Prior to performing parametric statistical analyses, normality testing was conducted using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to assess whether the distributions of dependent variables followed a normal distribution at a significance level of α = 0.05. When normality was confirmed, paired t-tests were used to compare mean gap distances between the conventional and digital groups. For variables that did not meet the normality assumption, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied. The significance level was set at α = 0.05 for all the analyses. Additionally, paired t-tests were used to compare accuracy across different areas, and repeated-measures two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate the interaction between the framework fabrication method and the location of the abutment teeth.

RESULTS

A total of 15 arches from 13 patients (eight men and five women), aged between 66 and 89 years (mean age: 75.7 years), were included in the study. Among the RPDs evaluated, six were in the maxilla and nine in the mandible. Based on Kennedy classification, the distribution of edentulous arches was as follows: Kennedy Class I, 13 arches (88%); Class II, one arch (6%); Class III, none; and Class IV, one arch (6%). A total of 54 rests were analyzed, comprising 40 occlusal rests (74%) and 14 cingulum rests (26%). These rests were distributed across different tooth positions: 14 (26%) in anterior teeth, 30 (56%) in premolars, and 10 (18%) in molars. Additionally, 26 rests (48%) were located on the terminal abutments of distal-extension RPDs, while the remaining 28 rests (52%) were on the other abutment teeth. Among the 54 rests analyzed, 45 (83%) were placed on surveyed crowns, while 9 (17%) were prepared on natural teeth.

The qualitative evaluation of all frameworks was satisfactory. All rests were adequately seated in the rest seats with no visible gaps, and retention and stability were satisfactory. Thus, quantitative evaluation was performed for all frameworks. In the CON group, among the 54 rests, 20 (37%) were in contact. The number of contacts was greater in Zone P (37%) than in Zone C (19%). In contrast, in the IOS group, 31 (57%) of the rests were in contact. The number of contacts was greater in Zone P (54%) than in Zone C (37%).

The mean gap in the total area (Zone C+P) of the 54 rests was 239 ± 83 µm in the CON group and 201 ± 78 µm in the IOS group (P = .015). This indicates that the accuracy of frameworks fabricated using the IOS method was significantly better than that of those fabricated using the conventional method. Nevertheless, accuracy was within the clinically acceptable ranges in both groups.18,19 Furthermore, the independent t-test revealed no significant differences in the mean gap between Zones C and P (Table 1).

Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant differences in the mean gap for the total area (Zone C+P) between the groups, but not between the arches (i.e., maxillary and mandibular). Furthermore, the interaction between the impression method and the arch was not significant. A one-way ANOVA to evaluate differences according to the impression method for each arch showed no significant differences for the maxilla but significantly lower mean values for the mandible with the IOS method (Fig. 3A). No significant interaction between the impression method and abutment-tooth position (anterior, premolar, or molar) was observed. However, the IOS group showed significantly lower mean values for the rests in the premolar region (Fig. 3B). Additionally, the interaction between the impression method and terminal abutments of distal-extension RPDs was not significant. Regarding the gaps between the rests and rest seats in zones C and P, significant differences were observed between the terminal abutments of distal-extension RPDs and other teeth. The IOS group showed lower mean values in all zones (Zones C+P, C, and P) for the terminal abutments of distal-extension RPDs, indicating higher precision. Even for rests on teeth that were not terminal abutments, the IOS group showed better results than the CON group, with lower mean values in all zones (Table 2). Regarding the interaction between the impression method and whether the tooth was the terminal abutment, significant differences were observed between the groups (P < .05), particularly for the mean values in Zone C and P. This suggests that the IOS method tends to achieve better accuracy than the conventional method at the terminal abutments of distal-extension RPDs.

DISCUSSION

Digital technology has been widely applied in various fields of dentistry; however, its application to RPDs is relatively slow. Previous studies have reported the fabrication of frameworks using IOS data;7,20,21 however, to the best of our knowledge, no clinical study similar to this study has been reported. This study used IOS data to fabricate frameworks that were clinically evaluated. In patients with limited mouth opening or severe gag reflex, conventional impression methods are not feasible; however, IOS can be performed.

In this study, a VPS impression material was applied between the framework and the tooth, and its thickness was measured using metrology software. Stern et al.17 and Dunham et al.22 proposed methods to evaluate the accuracy of RPD frameworks by applying a VPS impression material and measuring its thickness. Recently, quantitative evaluations have been performed using software. Few studies have used best-fit measurements, whereas others have measured vertically using Geomagic Control X.12,23 In in vitro studies, silicone impression material can be left on the cast to measure the thickness after removing the framework. However, in clinical studies, the silicone impression material cannot be left in the mouth and must be removed with the framework. In this study, the thickness of the silicone was measured by overlapping the STL files of the framework alone with those of the silicone-covered framework. Previously, the thickness of the silicone was primarily measured microscopically; however, this study used metrology software.

According to Almufleh et al.,1 patient satisfaction with frameworks fabricated using SLM was higher than that with frameworks fabricated using conventional methods. However, this study assessed only short-term patient satisfaction; therefore, further research is required to evaluate long-term patient satisfaction. Additive manufacturing reduces time and cost and offers excellent biocompatibility. In this study, metal frameworks were fabricated using SLM in both groups. However, in the IOS group, the framework was applied to the resin cast, whereas in the CON group, the framework was applied to the stone cast. Because stone casts are relatively more rigid than resin-printed casts, more adjustments might have been made to the frameworks fitted to stone casts. Furthermore, in the conventional impression method, slight movement of teeth with minor mobility can occur during impression making.

Although quantitative differences in the gap distance were observed between the two groups, the clinical relevance of this difference must be interpreted cautiously. Qualitative assessment confirmed that all frameworks demonstrated clinically acceptable fit without requiring major adjustments, aligning with previous literature. The digital group (IOS) exhibited a significantly smaller mean gap distance (201 ± 78 µm) than the conventional group (239 ± 83 µm), suggesting improved adaptation. However, this value still falls within a clinically acceptable range and overlaps with previous studies that report varied results depending on fabrication methods and evaluation techniques. Compared to previous studies, the gap distance values observed in this study showed both similarities and differences. The mean gap distance of the IOS group (201 ± 78 µm) was greater than the 150 ± 13 µm reported by Soltanzadeh et al.12 and the 174 ± 117 µm reported by Ye et al.10 However, it was lower than the 327 µm median gap distance reported by Muehlemann et al.24 Additionally, the contact rate of the IOS group (57%) was greater than the 42.5% observed in Ye et al.’s10 study, while the conventional group (37%) demonstrated a similar value. In a clinical study evaluating RPD frameworks, Tregerman et al. found that the digital method (IOS + SLM additive manufacturing) provided significantly smaller gap distance than the conventional method.2 These findings indicate that while digital impressions generally reduce the gap distance, differences in the study methodologies, fabrication techniques, and evaluation criteria may contribute to variations across studies. Furthermore, the results of this study support the notion that digital impressions can enhance consistency by enabling more precise data acquisition and streamlining the manufacturing process.

Previous studies also evaluated the entire rests by dividing them into zones. Stern et al.17 reported better accuracy at the marginal ridge, which they attributed to less shrinkage during the casting process in the thicker marginal-ridge area. Similarly, Dunham et al. reported that occlusal rests had slightly better contact in the peripheral areas than in the central areas. However, Ye et al. divided rests into areas closer to the occlusal center and the marginal ridge instead of central and peripheral zones and found no significant differences between the zones using ANOVA.22 This study found differences only in the minimum values between the central and peripheral zones, with no significant differences in the mean or maximum values.

This study has several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. First, the quantitative evaluation was limited to the occlusal rest areas. While these are critical for providing vertical support and are considered reliable indicators of passive fit during clinical try-ins, they do not represent the overall fit of the framework. Other components—such as minor connectors, guide planes, and clasps—also contribute significantly to the adaptation and functional performance of removable partial dentures. The focus on rest seats was intentional, aiming to ensure consistency and objectivity in intergroup comparisons, particularly given the common use of secondary impressions to refine tissue adaptation in clinical practice.

Second, the study did not include a quantitative assessment of the framework’s adaptation to soft tissue surfaces. Although no obvious tissue impingement was observed during clinical try-ins, objective measurements of tissue contact or pressure distribution were not performed. This limitation is especially relevant considering that most included cases were Kennedy Class I arches, in which soft tissue support is essential for prosthesis stability. Previous studies, such as that by Chen et al., have emphasized the importance of accurately capturing soft tissue contours for digital denture fabrication.25 However, intraoral scanners may struggle to record mobile or compressible tissues, potentially compromising adaptation and limiting the generalizability of the findings to tissue-supported regions.

Finally, the accuracy of the frameworks was not evaluated using a single, standardized master model. In the conventional group, frameworks were assessed on stone casts derived from PVS impressions, whereas the digital group used 3D-printed resin casts generated from intraoral scan data. While both casts originated from the same clinical anatomy, differences in the fabrication methods—manual pouring versus 3D printing—could have introduced minor dimensional discrepancies. Although all frameworks were fabricated using the same SLM process, the two groups varied in their upstream data acquisition: conventional impression-taking and cast scanning versus direct intraoral scanning. These distinct clinical and digital workflows likely influenced the fit outcomes and must be considered when interpreting group differences, beyond the fabrication method or material alone.

This study evaluated data acquired immediately after the frameworks were fabricated and fitted. However, changes in the frameworks may occur during fabrication of the resin base, necessitating an evaluation of the accuracy at that stage. Furthermore, long-term evaluation of the framework accuracy after extended use in the oral cavity is necessary. Post-processing is crucial for preventing framework deformation; therefore, evaluating potential deformations in the oral environment is necessary. In this study, SLM-printed frameworks fabricated using both IOS data and conventional VPS impressions had clinically acceptable accuracy. However, further research is needed on the application of digital technology to the subsequent stages of RPD treatment, such as denture-base impression methods, software to simulate mucosal displacement under pressure, and methods for recording interocclusal relationships in patients with insufficient posterior occlusal support.26

CONCLUSION

Both IOS and conventional impressions combined with SLM printing can achieve clinically acceptable RPD framework accuracy. However, RPD frameworks fabricated using IOS data have a significantly better accuracy than that of those fabricated using conventional impression techniques. Particularly for terminal abutments of distal-extension RPDs, the IOS method tends to provide better accuracy. Moreover, although frameworks fabricated using both methods are clinically acceptable, digital dentures might be a more comfortable and efficient alternative for patients. Further research is required to evaluate the framework accuracy in various partially edentulous situations.
