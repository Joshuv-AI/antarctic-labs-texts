Evaluation of the circular RNA Pum1_0014, miRNA-146a, and miRNA-141-3p as biomarkers in PCOS
Authors: Marwa S. Abdel-Tawab, Hamada A.A. Ali, Eman M. Khalil, Wafaa A.A. Atris, Manal E. Hassan, Hoda R. Reyad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe molecular mechanisms underlying polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), a multifactorial disorder, remain unclear. Altered serum microRNAs (miRNAs) and circRNAs (circRNAs) in PCOS can serve as biomarkers in this disease. This study was aimed at assessing the serum levels of circRNA Pum1_0014, miRNA-146a, and miRNA-141-3p, as well as their potential as biomarkers in PCOS.Patients and methodsThis study examined 50 patients meeting PCOS criteria and 50 apparently healthy women as controls. Serum levels of metabolic and hormonal parameters were investigated in all participants. Real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction was used to assess the expression of the three studied genes.ResultsThe gene expression of miRNA-141-3p and circRNA Pum1_0014 was downregulated in the PCOS group vs. the control group. Additionally, miRNA-146a was significantly (p < 0.05) overexpressed in the PCOS group vs. the control group. In the PCOS group, miRNA-146a showed a highly significant negative correlation with circRNA Pum1_0014, whereas both RNAs showed highly significant correlations with FAI and SHBG. Moreover, miRNA-141-3p showed highly significant (p < 0.001) correlations with several metabolic parameters. Therefore, circRNA Pum1_0014, miRNA-146a, and miRNA-141-3p might serve as novel diagnostic tools with high sensitivity and specificity for PCOS prediction.ConclusionThe study proposes use of circRNA Pum1_0014, miRNA-141-3p, and miRNA-146a as new biomarkers in PCOS, and indicates their probable roles in the hormonal and metabolic imbalances in PCOS.

Introduction

The most prevalent endocrine disorder affecting fertile women globally is polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). In 1935, Stein and Leventhal first characterized this condition.1 Depending on the diagnostic standards, the prevalence varies from 5 % to 15 %. According to specialized society recommendations, PCOS diagnosis should be determined according to the manifestation of two of the following three features: polycystic ovaries, chronic anovulation, and biological or clinical hyperandrogenism.2

The pathogenesis of PCOS is unclear but is known to be associated with disturbances in endocrine and ovarian function manifesting in young women, including irregular menses, infertility, Hyperandrogenism, PCOM, and, if they conceive, a high risk of complications in pregnancy.3 These symptoms improve over time, and metabolic abnormalities decrease. Type 2 diabetes is four times more likely to be present in women with PCOS, who also have elevated likelihood of developing metabolic syndrome.4

Micro-ribonucleic acids (miRNAs) are small, non-coding ribonucleic acids that regulate the expression of genes by post-transcriptionally inhibiting specific messenger ribonucleic acids (mRNAs). When miRNAs bind their target mRNAs, they either encourage mRNA degradation or prevent translation. Because miRNAs circulate and are found in every bodily compartment, they are appropriate potential biomarkers.5 Some miRNAs associated with PCOS are highly expressed in the granulosa cells, serum, follicular fluid, whole blood, and theca cells.6 They control the growth and maturation of follicles, insulin signaling, the metabolism of lipids and carbohydrates, and even the production of steroid hormones. Therefore, miRNAs might potentially serve as therapeutic targets for PCOS treatment in addition to clinical biomarkers for PCOS diagnosis.6,7 Perturbed miRNA profiles might have a substantial role in the heterogeneity observed in women with PCOS and offer new opportunities for PCOS patient stratification.8 Controversial studies have reported the levels of miRNA-141-3p in PCOS.9, 10, 11 In addition, miRNA-146a has been reported to be a powerful modulator influencing human ovarian follicle atresia and degeneration.12

A recently identified family of endogenous noncoding RNAs with high transcript expression in eukaryotes is the circular RNAs (circRNAs).13 Because circRNAs lack a polyadenylated architecture at the 3′ end and a 5′-end cap structure, they form a covalently locked circular structure that resists nuclease destruction, in contrast to linear RNAs.14 CircRNAs are found in the ovaries and have expression patterns relevant to developmental stages throughout follicular development.15 Women with versus without PCOS also show differing circRNA expression. Through various processes, circRNAs have crucial regulatory functions in PCOS development.16 Therefore, circRNAs might serve as favorable and reliable diagnostic and treatment targets for PCOS. However, the precise association between circRNAs and PCOS remains to be determined, and how circRNAs might be used to diagnose and treat PCOS is unclear.17 A classic circRNA sponging mechanism, in which circRNA acts as an endogenous sponge for microRNA, might be involved in PCOS.18 In addition, circRNA Pum1_0014 has been reported to target miRNA-146a and consequently decrease cardiomyocyte apoptosis in myocardial infarction.19

This study was aimed at assessing the circulating levels of circRNA Pum1_0014, miRNA-141-3p, and miRNA-146a in the serum in women with PCOS; the relationships among them; and the potential for their use as novel non-invasive biomarkers in PCOS. By identifying the dysregulation of miRNA-141-3p, miRNA-146a, and circRNA Pum1_0014, this study goes beyond single-molecule analysis by proposing a mechanistically defined panel. This approach aligns with current trends in PCOS biomarker discovery, which emphasize using comprehensive panels to reflect the syndrome's multi-factorial pathology, thereby potentially addressing broad regulatory network dysregulation with clinically useful diagnostic tools.

Materials and Methods

Study design

The participants in this case–control study were chosen among outpatients at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Clinic at Beni-Suef University Hospital, Egypt, between April 2023 and December 2023. The study was performed at the Biochemistry and Molecular Biology Department of the Faculty of Medicine at Beni-Suef University, Egypt. Fifty patients with PCOS were selected according to the Rotterdam 2003 criteria.20 Fifty healthy women with no history of PCOS, average androgen levels, no hirsutism symptoms, regular menstrual cycles (menstrual periods as long as 7 days and menstrual cycles of 21–35 days), and normal androgen levels were selected as controls. All participants were assessed during the early follicular phase (days 2–5) of a spontaneous or progesterone-induced withdrawal bleed, which is the standard practice to control the acute fluctuation of hormones across the cycle. All patients were free of any medications known to affect ovarian or metabolic function (e.g., oral contraceptives, metformin, or anti-androgens) for a minimum of 3 months before enrollment and sample collection.

Participants meeting any of the following criteria were excluded from the study: congenital adrenal hyperplasia; prolactinoma; Cushing's disease, or other causes of hyperandrogenism or irregular menstruation; pregnancy or within the first year after giving birth; and use of hormone medication within 3 months of the start of the study.

Study procedure and sample collection

Venous blood (2 ml) was drawn, allowed to clot, and then centrifuged at 8000×g for 5 min to extract the serum, which was then stored at −80 °C until analysis. All participants underwent comprehensive clinical examinations and had their medical histories thoroughly reviewed. The patients and controls underwent biochemical profiling, including serum levels of fasting insulin, FSH, LH, estradiol (E2), prolactin (PRL), total testosterone (T), and sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), assessed with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays. The free androgen index (FAI) was estimated according to a previously reported formula.21 The lipid profile, including HDL-c, TAGs, and cholesterol, as well as fasting glucose, was measured through colorimetric estimation. LDL-c levels,22 the homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR),23 , and body mass index (BMI) were estimated.24

Molecular biology techniques

Total RNA extraction

The PARIS Kit (Thermo Fisher) can isolate both RNA and native protein from the same experimental sample, and allow for the separation of nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions before RNA isolation. With a PARIS Kit, we extracted RNA from the cytoplasm and nuclei of cells. According to the manufacturer's instructions, total RNA was extracted from 200 ml serum specimens from patients and controls with TRIZOL reagent (catalog number: 15596018CN, Invitrogen, USA). A NanoDrop 2100 spectrophotometer was used to measure RNA concentrations. The RNA specimens were then stored at −80 °C until further use. Repeated freeze-thawing was avoided, to help maintain sample quality during storage.

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription-PCR

For real-time quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR), reverse transcription was performed according to the guidelines of the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (catalog number: RR047A, Takara). Qiagen (Germany), supplied all primers created from GenBank RNA sequences with Tm of 60–63 °C as follows: miRNA-146a (F: 5′-CCGATGTGTATCCTCAGCTTTG-3′, R: 5′-TCCCAGCTGAAGAACTGAATTT-3′), miRNA-141-3p (F: 5′-UAACACUGUCUGGUAAAGAUGG-3′), universal RT primer (R: 5′-CGCAGTTGCAGGTCAGAATG-3′), U6 (F: 5′-ATTGGAACGATACAGAGAAGATT-3′, R: 5′-GGAACGCTTCACGAATTTC-3′), glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) (F: 5′- GGACGAAGTGTCGGTGTTGC-3′, R: 5- CAGGTTGGCTGTTCATCTGTC -3′), and circRNA Pum1_0014 (F: 5′-CCTCCTTCAAATCTCCTA-3′, R: 5′-ACTACAATTCTCAACAACA-3′).

qRT-PCR and data validation

Reference Gene Justification and Data Normalization: For quantification of circulating miRNAs (miR-141-3p and miR-146a), expression levels were normalized to U6 snRNA, the current standard for circulating miRNA analysis. However, on the basis of recent guidelines and the heterogeneous nature of circulating RNA, the expression of circRNA Pum1_0014 was normalized to G6PD.

Primer Validation and Quantification Efficiency: All primers, including the divergent primers used for circRNA Pum1_0014, were synthesized by Qiagen (Germany) and created from GenBank sequences with a targeted Tm of 60–63 °C. The specificity of all primers was validated with melt curve analysis and agarose gel electrophoresis. Furthermore, primer performance was assessed via standard curve analysis using serial dilutions of pooled cDNA. The slope, amplification efficiency, and linear correlation for all primer sets are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. All assays demonstrated an efficiency of 95 %–102 % (R2 > 0.99), thus confirming their reliability for quantitative analysis.

circRNA Circularity Confirmation: To confirm the circular nature of circRNA Pum1_0014 and exclude linear RNA contamination, we treated RNA samples with RNase, an exoribonuclease that degrades linear, but not circular, RNA molecules. Quantitative PCR confirmed that circRNA Pum1_0014 expression was resistant to RNase R digestion, whereas the control linear transcript (GAPDH mRNA) was degraded.

Preparation of reverse transcription reactions

The prepared reactions were thoroughly but gently mixed. After a brief centrifugation, the enzyme was incubated for 60 min at 37 °C, then inactivated for 5 min at 85 °C. Before qPCR, the reverse transcription product of the reaction was diluted 5–50 × in sterile water. The optimal mixtures' reaction conditions included incubation for 30 min at 16 °C, 30 min at 42 °C, 5 min at 85 °C, and an arbitrary length of time at 4 °C to synthesize cDNA.

Detection of circRNA miRNAs with qRT-PCR

The qPCR amplification was performed with SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara). According to the kit's instructions, PCR master mix was prepared from the following components: 12.5 μL 2 × SYBR Premix Ex Taq, 0.5 μL forward primer (10 μM), 0.5 μL reverse primer (10 μM), 2 μL template DNA, and nuclease-free water to a final volume of 25 μL. Quantitative PCR was performed with an Applied Biosystem 7500 RT-PCR instrument (Applied Biosystems), with reaction conditions of 95 °C for 5 min, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, and 60 °C for 60 s. Subsequently, qRT-PCR was used to determine the relative levels of circRNA Pum1_0014 in the cytoplasm and miRNAs in the nucleus. To determine the fold change in gene relative expression, we used the comparative threshold cycle (2−ΔΔCt) method.25

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25 (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) for Windows was used to analyze the data. Quantitative variables are described as mean, standard deviation (SD), and median. Normally distributed data are reported as mean ± SD, with P-values. Nonparametric quantitative variables were compared with the Mann–Whitney test, and differences are reported as P-values. The Spearman correlation test was used to perform binary correlation, and the results are reported as P-values and correlation coefficients (r). The established standards for calculating the correlation coefficient were as follows: a perfectly positive linear relationship is indicated by a value of +1: as one variable's value increases, so does the other variable's value, according to an exact linear law. A value of 0 denotes no linear relationship.

Ten-fold cross-validation was used on the predictive model to thoroughly evaluate the reliability and generalizability of the combined three-marker ncRNA panel (circRNA Pum1_0014, miR-146a, and miR-141-3p) and to decrease the possibility of optimistic bias or overfitting that is often present in short pilot studies. To ensure a consistent representation of the PCOS/control ratio across all subsets, we randomly divided the dataset into ten equally sized, non-overlapping, stratified folds. The model was evaluated on the final single fold after being trained on nine combined folds in ten iterative cycles. For each of the ten iterations, the area under the curve (AUC) was determined. Model stability was evaluated by computation of the SD of these values, and the final cross-validated diagnostic performance was calculated by averaging of the ten unique AUC values. The R statistical environment (version 4.x) with the caret and pROC packages was used to perform all cross-validation procedures.

Results

Participants’ demographic, laboratory, and clinical data

No significant differences were observed in mean age or BMI between the PCOS and control groups (Table 1). However, highly significant (p < 0.001) differences in mean ovarian volume, ovarian follicle count, and annual menstrual cycle count were found between groups, thus validating the correct selection of the patient cohort according to the established Rotterdam criteria. Highly significant (p < 0.001) differences in the mean levels of LH (higher in PCOS), E2 (higher in PCOS), total testosterone (higher in PCOS), free testosterone (higher in PCOS), SHBG (lower in PCOS), and FAI (higher in PCOS) were observed in the serum between patients with PCOS and controls. Compared with the control group, the PCOS group had significantly (p < 0.001) lower levels of HDL-c, and significantly (p < 0.001) higher levels of serum fasting insulin, HOMA IR, triglycerides, and cholesterol. Furthermore, the serum triglycerides were significantly higher in participants with obesity rather than normal weight in the PCOS group. In addition, the serum HOMA IR and the levels of fasting insulin significantly but gradually increased with increasing weight in both the PCOS and control groups. Mean levels of miRNA-146a, miRNA-141-3p, and circRNA Pum1_0014 in the studied groups

The PCOS group showed highly significantly (p < 0.001) higher mean levels of circulating miRNA-146a (1.46 ± 0.39), and highly significantly (p < 0.001) lower mean levels of both circulating miRNA-141-3p (0.87 ± 0.09) and circRNA Pum1_0014 (0.91 ± 0.07), than the control group (1.01 ± 0.07, 1.06 ± 0.09, and 1.03 ± 0.58, respectively). These findings suggested these parameters as non-invasive PCOS biomarkers. (see Figure 1).

Discussion

Circular RNAs and microRNAs are easily detected in the serum or plasma.26 Consequently, they might serve as noninvasive biomarkers for the detection of various disorders and contribute to the recognition of molecular processes involved in disease pathogenesis. Herein, we selected serum miRNA-146a, miR-141-3p, and circRNA Pum1_0014 for investigation in PCOS.

Although miRNA-146a was previously investigated in PCOS,27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32 conflicting results necessitated additional confirmatory studies.

The current study demonstrated significantly higher mean levels of circulating miRNA-146a in the PCOS group than the control group. These findings suggested the potential utility of the serum miRNA-146a as a non-invasive biomarker in PCOS.

Our findings matched those from Long et al. (2014)28 and Salimi et al. (2016)29, who have reported significantly greater circulating miRNA-146a in patients with PCOS than controls. The current findings were consistent with those of Cirillo et al. (2019)30 indicating higher levels of miRNA-146a in ovarian granulosa cells and follicular fluid in patients with PCOS than controls. Additionally, a previous study31 suggesting that miR-146a gene polymorphism might increase PCOS risk by enhancing miRNA-146a expression supported our current findings.

Soyman et al. (2022),32 in contrast to the present results, indicated lower circulating miRNA-146a in the PCOS group than the control group. The differences in results might be due to the diversity of the disease and differences in ethnicity.

In the current study, patients with PCOS, compared with controls, had significantly higher mean levels of circulating fasting insulin, HOMA IR, triglycerides, and cholesterol, thus indicating the insulin resistance status of patients with PCOS, particularly those with obesity. The elevated miRNA-146a in insulin resistance was consistent with findings from Lovis et al. (2008)33 and Kong et al. (2011)34 indicating elevated miRNA-146a levels under conditions of insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes mellitus.

These findings might be explained by a previous report indicating an association between miRNA-146a and insulin sensitivity.29,35,36 In contrast, Balasubramanyam et al. (2011)37 and Ghaffari et al. (2023)38 have reported down-regulation of miRNA-146a expression levels in patients with type 2 diabetes compared with controls.

In agreement with our current findings of upregulation of miRNA-146a in PCOS, previous studies have found similar results in other ovarian disorders.39, 40, 41

The role of miRNA-146a in PCOS and other ovarian disorders might be explained by its induction of inflammation and apoptosis during follicular genesis,42,43 through TNF-dependent modulation of follicular production and degeneration by activating caspase pathways.44 In addition, miRNA-146a promotes apoptosis in granulosa cells via tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) and interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase (IRAK1); these mediators control the activity of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and inhibitory kappa B α (IκBα), thereby affecting caspase pathway expression.45

To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate circRNA Pum1_0014 in PCOS. The serum of patients with PCOS showed highly significantly lower mean levels of circulating circRNA Pum1_0014 than the control group; therefore, circRNA Pum1_0014 could potentially serve as a biomarker to distinguish women with vs. without PCOS. We also observed a strong negative correlation between miRNA-146a and circRNA Pum1_0014, thereby suggesting the presence of underlying interacting molecular processes potentially contributing to PCOS pathophysiology.

Our findings strongly suggested a classic circRNA sponging mechanism, in which circRNA Pum1_0014 acts as an endogenous sponge for miRNA-146a. Failure to sponge leads to an increase in free, active miRNA-146a. This upregulated miRNA-146a then exerts greater inhibitory control over its target mRNAs, including components of key pathways such as the NF2/VEGF/PAK1 signaling cascade. Given that miR-146a has been independently implicated in inflammation and insulin sensitivity within PCOS, dysregulation of this circRNA axis provides a compelling molecular link among the genetic, inflammatory, and hormonal components that drive PCOS pathology, thereby offering a precise target for diagnostic and potentially therapeutic intervention.17, 18, 19

We found that miRNA-146a and circRNA Pum1_0014 showed highly significant correlations with FAI and SHBG in the PCOS group. These findings suggested their effects on steroidogenesis and potentially a new therapy target.

In contrast to these results, Long et al. (2014)28 have reported a negative correlation between serum miRNA-146a and serum testosterone in patients with PCOS. In addition, a previous study has indicated that miRNA-146a suppresses the release of androgens from human ovarian cells.40 Future research is required to clarify these controversial findings regarding the relationship between androgens and miRNA-146a, as well as the underlying molecular pathways.

The current findings indicated significantly lower expression of circulating miRNA-141-3p in the PCOS group than in the controls. Additionally, the significant negative correlations of miRNA-141-3p with BMI, fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, and serum TG suggested a possible role of altered miRNA-141-3p in the insulin resistance status in PCOS.

Similar results have been reported by Fan et al. (2021),11 who have revealed lower serum miRNA-141-3p in participants with PCOS than controls. In addition, they have indicated negative correlations between miRNA-141-3p and HOMA-IR. In contrast to the present results, He et al. (2018)10 have observed higher miRNA-141-3p in the granulosa cells of patients with PCOS than controls.

The metabolic link between miRNA-141-3p and PCOS might be explained by previous reports indicating the role of miRNA-141-3p in regulating insulin signal transduction by targeting phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), a regulator of insulin signaling, glucose synthesis, and gluconeogenesis.46, 47, 48, 49

Our ROC curve analysis of miRNA-146a, miRNA-141-3p, and circRNA Pum1_0014 as an accurate test for the early detection of PCOS suggested that these biomarkers might potentially be incorporated into clinical practice in two main ways. First, as a preliminary screening tool, the serum RNA panel could be used as a first-line, quantitative test for screening women presenting with non-specific symptoms (such as irregular periods) or those with a strong family history of PCOS. Second, for phenotypic subtyping, as indicated by the present results, miRNA 141-3p correlated with metabolic parameters, whereas the circRNA/miRNA 146a axis correlated with hormonal parameters. The panel might therefore aid in classifying patients with PCOS into distinct endotypes (such as metabolic-dominant versus hyperandrogenic-dominant) and directing individualized treatment (such as giving insulin sensitizers precedence over anti-androgens).

Similar results from Long et al. 201428 and Fan et al. (2021)11 have indicated the diagnostic efficacy of miRNA-146a and miRNA-141-3p in PCOS.

The present results demonstrated highly significant differences in the mean levels of LH (elevated), E2 (elevated), total testosterone (elevated), free testosterone (elevated), SHBG (decreased), and FAI (elevated) in the serum in patients with PCOS. Soni et al. (2022)50 and Zhu et al. (2019),51 in agreement with these findings, have explained the effects of androgens with increasing insulin resistance; moreover, hyperandrogenism itself can cause insulin resistance.52 In addition, the high levels of insulin observed in patients with insulin resistance can stimulate the thecal cells to synthesize more androgen, thus enhancing the already established hyperandrogenism.

We found that the PCOS group had significantly lower serum HDL-C and significantly higher serum fasting insulin, HOMA IR, triglycerides, and cholesterol than the controls. Furthermore, the serum triglycerides were significantly higher in obese than in normal-weight participants. In addition, the serum levels of fasting insulin and HOMA IR showed gradual, significant increases with increasing weight in both the PCOS and control groups. These findings suggested the metabolic risk in PCOS, in agreement with a meta-analysis of various metabolomic studies identifying many abnormal metabolites in patients with PCOS53.

Conclusions

The expression of circRNA Pum1_0014, miRNA-146a, and miRNA-141-3p is altered in PCOS; these RNAs might therefore serve as novel non-invasive biomarkers with high sensitivity and specificity in PCOS prediction. Our findings suggested roles of circRNA Pum1_0014 and miRNA-146a in aberrant steroidogenesis and miRNA-141-3p in insulin resistance in PCOS pathogenesis.

Study limitations

The current study, despite providing significant preliminary findings, has several important limitations that temper the immediate clinical application of the current results. First, the cross-sectional design precluded establishing a definitive cause-and-effect relationship between dysregulation, as indicated by the ncRNA panel, and PCOS onset or progression. Future longitudinal studies are therefore required to confirm whether these markers are antecedent causes, consequences, or simply correlations of the disease. Second, the modest sample size might limit the generalizability of the current findings to the broader, phenotypically diverse PCOS population. Therefore, the highly favorable diagnostic metrics (e.g., the high AUC), which might have been prone to optimism bias (as confirmed by the internal cross-validation), require cautious interpretation. Third, although the discussion provides strong mechanistic hypotheses linking the circRNA Pum1_0014/miRNA-146a axis and miR-141-3p to established PCOS pathways, direct functional validation demonstrating these interactions is lacking. Therefore, the findings should be considered a crucial biomarker discovery pilot study, which might serve as a foundation for mandatory future multi-center validation in larger, phenotypically stratified cohorts.
