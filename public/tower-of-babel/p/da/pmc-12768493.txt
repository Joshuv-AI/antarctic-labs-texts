Sensation kinetics identifies novel bladder sensation-capacity curve shapes during urodynamics in patients with urinary urgency
Authors: John E Speich, Annapoorani Narayanan, Mrudula Bandaru, Nyasia Jones, Devina Thapa, Rui Li, Zachary E Cullingsworth, Anna S Nagle, Andrew F Colhoun, Adam P Klausner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Overactive bladder (OAB) is characterized by urinary urgency. To better characterize the relationship between bladder sensation and urgency, a tablet-based “Sensation Meter” was developed to enable construction of bladder sensation-capacity curves. The objective of this study was to correlate sensation-capacity curve shapes during urodynamics (UDS) with urgency severity and bother.Methods:Individuals with moderate-to-severe urgency presenting for clinically indicated UDS were prospectively enrolled after urgency characterization using the International Consultation on Incontinence OAB questionnaire (ICIq-OAB). Throughout UDS filling, participants recorded sensation of bladder fullness (0–100 %) using the Sensation Meter. Sensation-capacity curves were constructed and area-under-the-curve (AUC) analysis was implemented to differentiate between three curve-shape patterns defined as r, l, and j-shape. Curve shapes were correlated categorically to urgency severity and bother, UDS capacity, and the presence/absence of DO.Findings:The study included 69 participants (52F, 17M). The distribution of sensation-capacity curve shapes was 7 (10 %) r-shape, 43 (62 %) l-shape, and 19 (28 %) j-shape. A j-shaped curve was significantly associated with severe urgency and with high urgency bother, but not with bladder capacity or DO.Conclusions:The key finding was the association between a j-shaped sensation-capacity curve, demonstrating a rapid acceleration of sensation near the end of filling, with both urgency symptom severity and bother. Curve shapes were not associated with bladder capacity or DO, suggesting that a j-shaped curve may potentially identify a separate driver of urinary urgency. Additional studies are needed to determine whether sensation-capacity curves can be used to identify novel OAB/urgency phenotypes or guide therapy.Keywords: Lower urinary tract symptoms, Overactive bladder, Sensation, Urinary bladder, Urodynamics

Introduction

Urinary urgency is the key symptom of overactive bladder (OAB), defined as “urinary urgency, usually accompanied by daytime frequency and/or nocturia, with urinary incontinence (OAB-wet) or without (OAB-dry), in the absence of urinary tract infection or other detectable disease.” [1,2] However, the diagnosis of OAB is mainly based on patient-reported symptoms which are not disease specific [3]. This problem is compounded by the subjective nature of bladder sensation. Researchers have been attempting to objectively study bladder sensation for decades [4–12]. The current gold standard for the objective characterization of bladder sensation is multi-channel urodynamics (UDS) in which patients report standardized verbal sensory thresholds of first sensation of filling, first desire to void, and strong desire to void [3]. However, this type of testing can be affected by investigator prompting as well as the non-physiologic nature of testing [13,14]. Our group has previously developed a real-time Sensation Meter (Fig. 1) that can be used during both non-invasive natural filling [15–19] and UDS [20].

(A) Screenshot of the Sensation Meter, with the slider bar participant interface (bottom) and graphical display of bladder fullness sensation from 0 to 100 % (middle). (B) Example Sensation Meter data for a participant that reported 21 increases in sensation during ~7 min of urodynamic filling.

The treatment of patients with OAB/urgency is further complicated by the low efficacy of mainstay treatments, such as antimuscarinic drugs, which are generally poorly tolerated in the long-term [21]. A key contributor to this issue is the algorithmic trial-and-error approach to the treatment of OAB/urgency [22]. Therefore, an important research goal is improved and more objective phenotypic classification of OAB/urgency based on symptom bother, sensation, and UDS findings in an effort to better guide treatment.

Importantly, there is currently no standardized method to characterize the different patterns of bladder sensation. A previous oral hydration study by Nagle and colleagues [15] suggested that individuals with OAB/urgency may have decreased bladder sensation with rapid filling in comparison to healthy controls. The group constructed sensation-capacity curves that demonstrated a leftward shift in healthy individuals suggesting increased sensation with faster filling. Paradoxically OAB/urgency participants showed a right shift with faster filling suggesting decreased sensation. The researchers hypothesized that the observed effect was due to predicted viscoelastic properties of the detrusor muscle [15]. Other studies have looked at sensation-capacity curves and used an area-under-the-curve (AUC) analysis [11,12]. these small studies focused only on repeatability of sensation during oral hydration protocols and did not evaluate phenotyping of urgency [11,12].

To improve the characterization of OAB/urgency, a sensation-kinetics approach was used to generate real-time sensation-capacity curves during UDS. Using this approach, the objective of the current study was to 1) develop a standardized method to objectively categorize sensation-capacity curves during UDS bladder filling, and 2) to correlate sensation-capacity curve shapes with urgency symptom severity and bother, bladder capacity, and the presence of detrusor overactivity (DO). The overarching goal of this investigation was to improve sub-typing of OAB/urgency.

Methods

Study Approval.

This cohort study was approved by the Virginia Commonwealth University Institutional Review Board (Study number: HM2000453), and all participants received written informed consent prior to participation. Participants were prospectively enrolled prior to completion of a single urodynamics study with use of the Sensation Meter.

Participants.

Adults at least 21 years of age scheduled for a clinically-indicated urodynamics study were recruited. Individuals with any neurologic conditions that could affect bladder sensation, those with cognitive dysfunction that might not be able to understand the nature of the project and individuals with inadequate hand function to use the Sensation Meter (see below) were excluded. Participants with urinary urgency were included in the study based on question 5a (“Do you have to rush to the toilet to urinate?”) of the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire on OAB (ICIq-OAB) where scores range from 0 to 4. Only participants with moderate to severe urgency (score = 2–4) were included as this definition has been used in prior publications to define symptomatic urinary urgency [15,23,24].

Participant Groups.

Participants were divided into two groups based on symptom severity: moderate urgency (5a score = 2) and severe urgency (5a score = 3–4). Next, the ICIq-OAB urgency bother score (question 5b with range of 0–10) was used to divide patients into groups with low/moderate urgency bother (5b score = 0–7) and severe urgency bother (5b score ≥8). Demographic data were also collected for all participants including age and biological sex.

Sensation Meter.

An established tablet-based Sensation Meter [15–17] was used to record real-time bladder sensation during urodynamics testing. The tablet enables patients to measure real-time sensation of bladder fullness (from 0 to 100 %) using a slide bar (Fig. 1). All patients completed standardized training on how to properly use the Sensation Meter prior to the study using an investigator-guided PowerPoint presentation which included practice sessions. The study was initiated when the patient demonstrated adequate understanding of the device. Sensation data were obtained at 10 Hz and time linked to UDS data.

UDS Protocol.

UDS was performed according to International Continence Society standards with Laborie equipment (Laborie, Portsmouth, NH) and air-charged catheters [25]. UDS procedures were performed for clinical indications with the only addition being the simultaneous use of the Sensation Meter. The UDS fill rate was determined by the urodynamicist, and was typically 50 ml/min or 10 % of estimated maximum bladder capacity per minute based on a void diary. Bladder capacity was considered the point at which the patient reported 100 % sensation or the point at which filling was concluded (whichever came first). Capacity was estimated as the end-fill infused volume. The presence or absence of DO was determined by a fellowship-trained urologist who was blinded to patient information, based on the printed UDS tracing.

Curve Shape Analytics.

Because UDS filling volumes vary widely, standardized sensation-capacity curves were created for each patient by sampling sensation data at every 5 % increment of cytometric capacity. To characterize curves into separate shapes, the %sensation at 50 % capacity (%S50) was initially determined. Participants with %S50 > 50 % were defined as the “early sensation” group (Fig. 2A–B, o indicates %S50) and participants with %S50 ≤ 50 % were defined as the “late sensation” group (Fig. 2C–D). Next, Microsoft Excel was used to calculate the area-under-the-curve (AUC) for the second capacity quartile (25 %–50 % capacity) and the third capacity quartile (50 %–75 % capacity) using the trapezoidal rule. Using an iterative process, three curve shapes were defined. The AUC under diagonal blue line in Fig. 2A is 1562.5 for the 2nd capacity quartile. Early sensation curves with AUC ≥1562.5 in this quartile were defined as “r” shaped (Fig. 2A, green). The AUC under diagonal blue line in Fig. 2D is 937.5 for the 3rd capacity quartile. Late sensation curves with AUC <937.5 in this quartile were defined as “j” shaped (Fig. 2D, pink). Curves not meeting either criterion were defined as “l” shaped (Fig. 2C and D). Total AUC was also calculated for each curve.

Examples of area-under-the-curve (AUC) analysis for sensation-capacity curves with “r” (A), linear “l” (B-C) and “j” (D) shapes during UDS on individuals with OAB.

Statistics.

Data are reported as means ± standard deviation. Comparisons were made between j-shape and non-j-shape (l and r) curves. Continuous variables were compared with Student’s t-tests and categorical variables were compared with Fisher’s exact tests with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The Bonferroni correction was used for multiple comparisons of j-shape and non-shape curves.

Results

Sixty-nine total participants were enrolled in the study, including 29 participants in the moderate urgency group (19F/10M) and 40 participants in the severe urgency group (33F/7M). Participant demographics, ICIq-OAB scores, and DO categorization are shown in Table 1. The median age of all participants was 52.7 years, and age did not differ between the urgency groups (p = 0.071). Overall, 29/69 (42 %) demonstrated DO during UDS, with a higher percentage noted in the severe urgency group (53 %) as compared to the moderate urgency group (28 %) (Table 1, p = 0.0498).

When categorized by urgency symptom severity (moderate vs. severe), j-shaped curves were significantly associated with severe urgency, with 15/40 (37.5 %) participants with severe urgency having j-shape curves compared to 4/29 (13.8 %) with moderate urgency (Fig. 4, Fisher’s Exact Test, p = 0.033). In addition, when categorized by urgency bother (low/moderate vs. high), j-shaped curves were significantly associated with high urgency bother, with 17/44 (38.6 %) participants with high bother having j-shaped curves compared to 2/25 (8 %) with low/moderate bother (Fig. 5, Fisher’s Exact Test, p = 0.01).

The median UDS bladder capacity of participants was 322 ml. Participants with bladder capacities <322 ml were defined as “low capacity” and those ≥322 ml defined as “high capacity.” In comparing low vs. high-capacity bladders, there was no significant association with curve shape (Fig. 6A, p = 0.59). The UDS fill rate was 50 ml/min for 67 % of the participants. For the small groups of participants with fill rates less than 49 ml/min (n = 15, with 2 “r”, 8 “l” and 5 “j” curves) and greater than 51 ml/min (n = 8, with 1 “r”, 5 “l” and 2 “j” curves), the distribution of “j” and “non-j” curves was not significantly different (Fisher’s Exact Test, p = 1.0). In addition, DO was identified in 10/19 participants with j-shape curves (52.6 %) and 19/50 (38.0 %) with non-j shape curves which was not a significant association (Fig. 6B, p = 0.29). However, DO was clearly associated with urgency bother, as 6/29 patients (20.7 %) with DO reported low/moderate bother compared to 23/29 (79.3 %) with high bother (p = 0.025).

Discussion

Using an established Sensation Meter [16,17], a sensation-kinetics approach was developed to generate real-time sensation-capacity curves. Applying this approach during UDS, distinct sensation-capacity curve shape phenotypes were defined with r, l, and j-shape curves. The key finding from the current investigation was that the j-shape curve, where individuals feel limited sensation and then experience a rapid sensory acceleration, was associated with a significant increase in patient-reported urgency severity and bother. Furthermore, curve shapes were not affected by UDS capacity and were more likely a function of sensory processing. The present study was not designed to examine the relationship between sensation and supraphysiologic UDS fill-rates, which might influence the sensation curve shape [16]. In addition, DO demonstrated during UDS was not significantly associated with the j-shape phenotype, even though DO was, itself, clearly associated with bothersome urgency. This suggests that sensation-capacity curve shape and DO may be acting through separate sensory pathways to affect the sensation and/or perception of urinary urgency. This is the first study to identify urgency phenotypes using a sensation-kinetics approach during UDS.

A previous study conducted by Blaivas and colleagues [26] attempted to determine whether urinary urgency was experienced differently in normal and pathologic states. Their results demonstrate different perceptions of urgency with one group reporting sensation as an intensification of normal urge and another group reporting a “different sensation.” The main conclusion was that urgency consists of at least two different types of sensation, a concept which provides strong rationale for the current attempt to sub-categorize real-time sensation based on sensation-capacity curve shapes. Moreover, the present finding that the j-shape curve is a significant driver of urgency severity and bother provides evidence in support of the International Continence Society definition urgency as “a sudden and compelling urge to urinate that’s difficult to delay [27].”

Current techniques to more objectively characterize bladder sensation and urinary urgency have relied on MRI-based or near infrared spectroscopy-based neuroimaging [28–30], sensation meters [15–19], validated questionnaires [31] and bladder diaries [32]. Bladder diaries and validated questionnaires gather information about fluid intake, frequency of urination, effects of medication and urgency episodes. Currently, UDS is the gold standard for assessing real-time bladder sensation. However, the standardized verbal sensory thresholds of first sensation of filling, first desire to void, and strong desire to void [27] are episodic, poorly defined, and add very little to our understanding of bladder sensation and urinary urgency. The key advance in the current study was the simultaneous use of UDS and an established Sensation Meter to generate real-time sensation-capacity curve shapes during filling.

Lowenstein and colleagues [33] developed an “urgeometer” recording real-time sensation data into urodynamics (UDS), which demonstrated differences in sensation between women with different incontinence types. However, no further studies were published with this device. Other methods for objective characterization of bladder sensation include electrical sensory perception threshold testing [34,35], but this is invasive and requires bladder catheterization. In another study using the Sensation Meter, OAB/urgency individuals, when compared to normal participants, experienced earlier sensation descriptors such as “tingling” and “tense” during slow filling. These findings suggest that these descriptors may better define cognitive triggers for early voiding at low volumes, a classic manifestation of OAB/urgency [18].

In the analysis of sensation-capacity curve shape, an area under the curve approach was chosen due to its ability to evaluate incremental changes in sensation over the UDS filling capacity range. An AUC technique was previously applied to small oral hydration study by Medina et al., but their study focused only on repeatability and did not evaluate urgency phenotyping [12]. In a similar oral hydration study, de Wachter et al., observed convex and “S-shaped” curves in a small cohort [11]. The present study focused on sensation during UDS. AUC analysis using the 2nd (25–50 %) and 3rd (50–75 %) capacity quartiles was selected over AUC analysis of the total curves for this particular study because it resulted in a clearer separation of curves with sensation acceleration near capacity (j-shaped) from those with more linear (l-shaped) curves. In addition, this study focused only on patients with moderate and severe urgency because urgency is the key symptom OAB.

Currently, OAB/urgency is diagnosed based on patient-reported symptoms, mainly using patient history, validated questionnaires, and bladder diaries. The sensation-kinetics approach to define r, l, and j-shape curves provides a simple and cost-effective method which may be useful in the ongoing work to improve OAB/urgency phenotyping. Future clinical trials may be able to recruit participants based on curve shape classifications to optimize therapies and improve treatment outcomes.

Limitations of the current investigation include the high stress environment of UDS and the number of participants. Erdem and colleagues [13] showed that catheter insertion can affect the perception of sensation during cytometric testing. Klevmark and colleagues [36] discussed how the rate of filling during UDS is often supra-physiologic which may also alter sensation. This was hypothesized as being due to an increase in vesical pressure and decreased bladder compliance, and these physiologic factors may have similarly affected sensation in our UDS participants. Further investigations with larger and multi-institutional groups will be required to confirm the results of this study. Numerous sensation studies during non-invasive oral hydration studies have been performed [12,15–19], and future studies with larger numbers of participants are needed to specifically evaluate this sensation-kinetics approach during both non-invasive natural filling studies and UDS.

Conclusion

This sensation-kinetics study created a novel analytic method to characterize real-time sensation-capacity curves as r, l and j-shape in patients with moderate to severe urinary urgency. The results showed statistically significant differences in sensation curves based on self-reported patient urgency severity and bother. This sensation kinetics approach provides a simple method to identify novel OAB/urgency phenotypes which may ultimately be useful to guide treatments.
