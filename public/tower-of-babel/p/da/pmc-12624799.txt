A Single-Reamer Technique is an Effective Strategy for Acetabular Preparation in Primary Total Hip Arthroplasty
Authors: Catelyn A. Woelfle, Geordie C. Lonza, Alexander L. Neuwirth, H. John Cooper
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffective acetabular preparation is a critical step of primary total hip arthroplasty (THA). Most techniques begin with a small reamer and subsequently utilize multiple larger reamers until the desired size is achieved. We reported the effectiveness of a more efficient, single-reamer technique by evaluating component success rates and determining its safety by reporting any intraoperative complications.MethodsAll primary, elective THAs performed through a direct anterior approach using fluoroscopic guidance by 2 fellowship-trained surgeons between October 2019 and May 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. Our inclusion criterion was use of a single-reamer technique and minimum 1-year follow-up. The single reamer was chosen as the largest one that fully seated in the native acetabulum without any anteroposterior translation between the walls.ResultsData for 836 THAs were reviewed. Ninety-six hips (11%) with significant acetabular deformity where a single reamer technique could not be effectively used were excluded, leaving 740 (89%) that met the inclusion criteria. There were no intraoperative acetabular fractures. One (0.1%) cup was explanted during revision for periprosthetic joint infection. Of the 739 remaining cups, 25 (3%) required acetabular screws while 714 were placed without supplemental screw fixation. Overall, 99.7% of acetabular components implanted using a single-reamer technique achieved successful radiographic osseointegration at 1 year. One cup (0.1%) failed to achieve radiographic bony ingrowth yet has stable fibrous fixation that has not required revision.ConclusionsWhen applied within a structured workflow to hips with normal acetabular morphology, a single-reamer technique is an effective and safe method for acetabular preparation.

Introduction

Fundamental principles of total hip arthroplasty (THA) include implanting prosthetic components in a manner that allows for durable fixation, achieving appropriate hip stability, and restoring the patient’s anatomy including leg length and offset, while preserving as much of the native surrounding soft tissue envelope as possible [1,2]. Effective acetabular preparation is a critical step in achieving these goals. Initial stability of the porous titanium cup is essential for bony in-growth and component survivorship, as aseptic loosening is among the leading causes of failure in THA [[3], [4], [5]]. Reaming accuracy is crucial for obtaining this initial mechanical stability of the press-fit acetabular cup in THA [[6], [7], [8]].

Conventional reaming techniques often describe a technique where the surgeon should begin by medializing within the acetabular floor with a reamer smaller than the femoral head, exposing the cancellous bone, and progressively increasing the size of the reamer until the desired cup size and position is prepared [1,2]. This is a commonly utilized surgical technique, best described by the traditional Charnley approach [9], that is widely taught to trainees and practiced across the world. While this method has some advantages in reproducibility and familiarity, it also has a few drawbacks. Notably, inadvertent eccentric placement of the initial small reamer within the larger bony cavity can lead to eccentric positioning of the final acetabular component as larger reamers tend to remain centered within the initially reamed hemisphere.

In contrast, in the anatomical reaming technique (also called the peripheral technique), the surgeon starts at the femoral head size and reams up slightly, usually with 2 or 3 reamers, referencing the acetabular rim. This reaming technique has been described in the literature as being beneficial in minimizing displacement of the hip center of rotation [[10], [11], [12]], which is important for optimizing function and prosthesis survivorship after THA [13]. A modified version of the anatomical reaming technique, which is less commonly utilized but potentially more efficient, is the use of a single reamer. The anatomical and single reamer techniques have similar goals, although the single reamer gets there with fewer steps [14].

In the experience of the authors, the single reamer technique is both very effective and very efficient for acetabular preparation. When 2 intraoperative criteria are met, this single-step technique allows reproducible concentric reaming within the native bony acetabulum and avoids the need to insert and remove multiple reamers in and out of the surgical field, which may improve workflow efficiency and decrease opportunities for iatrogenic soft tissue damage. Limited literature on the single-reamer technique has focused on evaluating hip alignment and functional outcomes [14], but, to the best of our knowledge, has not yet assessed the reliability of acetabular component fixation when this technique is used. The purpose of this study is to report the rate of acetabular component osteointegration success following of this technique to examine the technique’s effectiveness at achieving reliable acetabular preparation and to report any intraoperative complications to determine the technique’s safety.

Material and methods

All primary, elective THAs performed between October 2019 and May 2023 by 2 fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeons were retrospectively reviewed following institutional review board approval. The surgeons have been in practice for 14 and 6 years, respectively, and both adopted this single-reamer technique into clinical practice several years prior to the beginning of the study period. Although, neither surgeon appreciated a significant learning curve with this technique. All were performed manually through a direct anterior approach using standard fluoroscopic guidance. The single-reamer technique does not require additional imaging or fluoroscopy compared to any other reaming technique. The inclusion criterion was operative report documentation of a single-reamer technique used for acetabular preparation, as described below. Hips were excluded from analysis when the single-reamer technique was not utilized because of an acetabular deformity that increased the complexity of acetabular preparation, including Crowe III-IV dysplasia, severe acetabular protrusio, prior pelvic osteotomy, and Paprosky II-III acetabular bone loss. In these excluded cases, anatomical (also called peripheral) reaming was used with multiple reamers, as documented in the operative reports.

Thus, this study retrospectively reviewed 836 primary THA cases which utilized the single-reamer technique with a minimum 1-year follow-up. There were 83 cases that were nonelective due to fracture or conversion from prior surgery and were excluded from analysis. A total of 13 hips were also excluded for use of multiple reamers as follows: 9 cases with Crowe III-IV dysplasia, 2 cases for severe acetabular protrusio, and 2 cases for Paprosky II-III acetabular bone loss secondary to osteonecrosis. This left a total study cohort of 740 hips treated with elective, primary THA with a surgically straightforward native acetabulum where the single reamer technique could be appropriately applied.

For the single reamer technique, the femoral head was first used to estimate the size of the acetabulum and then the following criteria were used to select the final size. After acetabular exposure through the direct anterior approach, an adequate view of the full acetabulum can be visualized. Following removal of the labrum and any large peripheral acetabular osteophytes, the chosen reamer was placed in the native acetabulum and the fit was assessed before it was used (Fig. 1A). Using a “Goldilocks” analogy, the reamer was “too big” if it was not able to seat fully against the bony acetabular floor of the anterior and posterior cotyledons (Fig. 1B), “too small” if it seated against the floor but was able to translate in the anteroposterior plane between the anterior and posterior walls (Fig. 1C), and “just right” if it seated fully but couldn’t translate in the anteroposterior plane because it was captured by the walls (Fig. 1D). One of the main strengths of this technique is to minimize eccentric reaming, a common issue during acetabular preparation, by choosing a reamer size that does not translate and reduces the risk of anteroposterior asymmetric reaming when starting with a smaller reamer and continuously sizing up. This selected single reamer was then used to prepare the acetabulum in a continuous ream to the desired depth under fluoroscopic guidance (Fig. 2a and b). Once the acetabulum was prepared with this single-step approach, the final cup, 1-mm larger than this reamer, was impacted (Fig. 2c and d). With modern, high-friction porous implants, we found under-reaming by 1 mm under implant size allowed for a proper press-fit, ensuring adequate fixation while avoiding proud seating of the cup or excessive impaction force. Data from these surgical cases were collected by retrospective chart review and included patient demographics, the type of acetabular component implanted, use of supplemental screw fixation, any intraoperative or postoperative complications, any need for reoperation or revision of the acetabular cup, and the presence or absence of radiographic osseointegration at a minimum of 1 year follow-up.

Successful osseointegration of the acetabular cup was determined by radiographic interpretation by a fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeon. Patient demographics were analyzed using an independent sample t-test and chi-squared tests. Osseointegration outcomes with and without screw fixation were analyzed using a chi-squared test.

Results

Discussion

We observed excellent survivorship of cementless acetabular components when using a single-reamer technique for preparation of the acetabulum. While eccentric reaming is a common concern using any acetabular preparation technique that effects initial cup stability, this technique minimizes the risk by ensuring symmetric reaming and avoiding anteroposterior translation of the reamer through a well-centered, stable fit of the single correctly sized reamer. Initial acetabular component fixation was deemed excellent in 97% of cases and supplemental screws were only required in 3% of cases. Meanwhile, 99.7% of all cups implanted over the 4-year period successfully achieved radiographic osseointegration at the 1-year postoperative visit, with only one requiring component revision to date.

The rate of successful osseointegration and minimal need for supplemental screws is suggestive of excellent initial implant stability when preparing the acetabulum with the single reamer technique as described. Zuo et. al. 2021 used a finite element analysis to simulate loading of an acetabular component placed with either a conventional or an anatomical (also called peripheral) technique and measured micromotion of the cup between the acetabulum at 5 different points [12]. They demonstrated that an anatomical reaming technique provided better initial cup stability and decreased micromotion compared to a conventional reaming technique, with improved stress distribution at the bone–cup interface, specifically at the superior point [12]. Although micromotion was largest at the bottom of the acetabulum for both the conventional and anatomical techniques, the conventional technique had 9 μm more motion [12]. The single reamer technique uses a single reamer that first reams the acetabulum peripherally and then deepens the prepared surface, assuring rim press-fit and theoretically leading to improved acetabular stability [15].

Though our study does not directly compare our single reamer and conventional reaming techniques in vitro, our rate of radiographic osseointegration can be compared among similar studies. In our series, 99.7% of acetabular components achieved osseointegration by 1 year postoperatively. This finding is similar to that found in the study by Hussain et al, in which implant survival was examined in the anatomical reaming technique over an 8 year follow-up period, and they had no cases of aseptic loosening and 100% overall implant survival [16]. Hussain et. al. 2022 is the only study to our knowledge that examines cup position and implant survival, but instead utilizes a multiple reamer technique [16]. In the study, the surgeon chose not to ream to the acetabular floor to preserve the medial bone and obtain a more anatomically positioned cup, and they demonstrated 100% survivorship from aseptic loosening at a mean of 8.6 years [16]. In our study, we demonstrate 99.7% survivorship at 1 year with a single reamer technique.

We had a low rate of need for screws to improve acetabular component fixation in our study, which may point to an advantage of the single reamer technique. In a recent large cohort study by Chen et. al., they found that 47.4% of THAs with hemispherical acetabular components with ultra-porous surfaces were enhanced with acetabular screws; however, the risk of aseptic loosening was not statistically significant between the use of screws and no screws at 10 years, and their overall rate of aseptic loosening was between 0.1% and 0.2% [17]. Though their study population was much larger than ours with longer follow-up, our rate of aseptic loosening is similar, regardless of use of screws or no screws. Our single reamer reaming technique likely provides better perceived initial implant stability, as evidenced by our low rate of screw usage. With decreased micromotion, excellent initial cup stability, and reliable radiographic osseointegration at 1 year, we expect these clinical results to be durable.

The concept of our single reamer technique is very similar to that used with robotic-assisted total hip replacement, such as with Stryker MAKO SmartRobotics. With MAKO, a computerized tomography scan is obtained preoperatively and the desired 3-dimensional placement of the acetabular component is planned. Intraoperatively, the robotic arm is used with a single reamer, and reaming is constrained by a virtual haptic tunnel, preventing any deviation from the planned anteversion and abduction angles [18]. This robotic-assisted reaming technique with a single reamer has been shown to be more efficient than conventional reaming with less reamers used and less operative time spent on acetabular preparation [19]. Optimizing operative efficiency to decrease operative time and therefore reduce risk of infection has been a long-standing tenet in the arthroplasty literature.

Our study had a very low deep infection rate (0.1%), which is an important outcome when considering operative room efficiency. Previous literature has demonstrated shorter surgical time with a single reamer technique [14,19]. Viglietta et. al. demonstrated a reduction in operative time by 6 minutes with the use of a single reamer [14]. Caba et. al. revealed that with robotic-assisted reaming, the acetabular workflow could be reduced from 6.4 ± 3.1 to 3.3 ± 1.7 minutes as compared to conventional direct anterior fluoroscopically-assisted THA [19]. While our study did not directly assess operative time, the authors believe that having a single step for acetabular preparation using the single-reamer technique improves operative room efficiency by reducing preparation steps and may thereby contribute to lowering infection risks.

There are many strengths to this study. First, this was a multisurgeon study that utilized standard instrumentation, indicating this technique is reproducible and generalizable among surgeons. Both surgeons have been in clinical practice for several years and both adopted this technique years prior to the beginning of the study period; thus, while neither perceived a learning curve, it was well surpassed if one was present at all, minimizing any potential variability. This study also has one of the largest patient cohorts on the topic of a single-reamer technique. Furthermore, highly porous acetabular components from different companies were used with similar success.

The authors recognize the limitations of this study. Although the single reamer technique may reduce the risk of eccentric reaming when executed properly, we caution new adopters of the potential learning curve. While the learning curve of this technique has not been well-studied, it may increase the likelihood of improper positioning and eccentric reaming, particularly in unfamiliar surgical exposures. Regarding the limitations of the study methodology, this was a retrospective review. Secondly, the successful osseointegration was determined by the independent radiographic interpretation of a single surgeon, which may introduce an element of bias, and the radiographic analysis of osseointegration by an independent observer may strengthen our study. Third is the length of follow-up, as we only have radiographic outcomes on some patients of up to 1 year. Fourth, we did not perform radiographic measurement of changes in preoperative to postoperative global offset in the cohort. In addition to our findings on cup fixation with this technique, examining the change in center of rotation and hip offset or global offset, which is important for clinical function and biomechanics, from preoperative to postoperative imaging could further enhance the literature on this technique and should be evaluated in future studies. Finally, the argument that our single reamer reaming technique is as good as or better than conventional reaming could be bolstered by a conventional reaming comparison group.

Conclusions

The single-reamer technique is an effective method for acetabular preparation when applied within a structured workflow to hips with normal acetabular morphology. We observed excellent acetabular component survivorship at minimum 1 year postoperative and no intraoperative complications when using this technique in a large cohort, multisurgeon analysis. We believe that this technique can be utilized safely and efficiently to obtain reliable acetabular component positioning and fixation when performing fluoroscopically assisted direct anterior THA. Given the technique requires only a single step for acetabular preparation during reaming, the authors believe this may also potentially improve operative room efficiency during total hip arthroplasty, which deserves further study.
