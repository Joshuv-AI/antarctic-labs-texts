iPSC-based drug discovery identified the Hippo signaling pathway as a therapeutic target in the fibrosis of NPHP1-deficient nephronophthisis
Authors: Takefumi Suzuki, Koichiro Susa, Hiroaki Kikuchi, Yuta Nakano, Tomoki Yanagi, Yu Hara, Tamami Fujiki, Fumiaki Ando, Shintaro Mandai, Yutaro Mori, Takayasu Mori, Hiroaki Iwasa, Yutaka Hata, Shinichi Uchida, Eisei Sohara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNephronophthisis (NPH) is an autosomal recessive kidney disease, and NPHP1 is the most frequently affected gene. Tubulointerstitial fibrosis is the major phenotype of NPHP1-deficient NPH. The pathophysiology of NPHP1-deficient NPH is unclear because models representing the disease pathophysiology are lacking. Herein, we aimed to create a novel pathological model of NPH using 3D kidney organoids derived from human-induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and elucidated the pathophysiology while searching for therapeutic candidates.MethodsNPHP1-deficient kidney organoids were generated from iPSCs. Fibrosis was induced by treatment with IL-1β. The effects of the Hippo signaling pathway inhibitors as therapeutic candidates were assessed. Fibrotic status was evaluated using immunofluorescence and quantitative PCR.ResultsNPHP1−/− kidney organoids were generated from iPSCs. Fibrosis induction with IL-1β considerably increased the expression of fibronectin and transcription of fibrosis-related genes in NPHP1−/− organoids. Long-term culture of NPHP1−/− organoids induced substantial fibrogenesis compared with wild-type organoids. Co-immunoprecipitation analysis revealed the binding of NPHP1 to LATS1/2—the main constituents of the Hippo pathway. IL-1β administration increased the expression of the key Hippo pathway genes in NPHP1−/− organoids. By contrast, the Hippo pathway inhibitors ameliorated IL-1β-induced fibrogenesis in NPHP1−/− organoids. Because one of the inhibitors, verteporfin, is in clinical use, its practical availability is expected from a drug-repositioning perspective.ConclusionsHippo signaling pathway is involved in the fibrotic changes associated with NPHP1-deficient NPH and the Hippo pathway inhibitors could be therapeutic agents.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04567-0.

Background

Nephronophthisis (NPH), an autosomal recessive kidney disease, is a chronic tubulointerstitial disorder accompanied with fibrosis and formation of microcysts owing to tubular dilatation in the renal tubulointerstitium, which can eventually develop end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) [1]. No established therapy exists for NPH [2]. It is a pediatric kidney disease and the most common cause of ESKD in hereditary kidney diseases of childhood [3].

NPHP1 is the most frequently affected, accounting for almost 50% of all cases of NPH. Of these cases, homozygous full gene deletions of NPHP1 are the most common, accounting for approximately one-half of all NPHP1-deficient NPH cases [4].

NPHP1-deficient NPH has several characteristic phenotypes compared with NPH derived from other causative genes. The first is onset time. NPH is clinically categorized into infantile, juvenile, and late-onset variants, depending on the time of diagnosis. Compared with other forms of NPH, patients with NPHP1-deficient NPH exhibit delayed onset of kidney dysfunction, with the highest frequency observed in the late-onset group [2, 5]. In a study on adult patients with ESKD, patients with NPHP1-deficient NPH constituted 0.5% of the population. The median age at which the patients developed ESKD was 30 years (range: 18–61 years) [6]. Therefore, NPHP1-deficient NPH is important as a causative disease in adult patients as well as children with CKD.

The second is histopathological observation. The characteristic histopathological findings of NPH are disruption of the tubular basement membrane with irregular thickness [7], interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy, and cysts in the cortical-medullary border [8]. NPHP1-deficient NPH is distinguished by renal fibrosis compared to other forms of NPH [9, 10, 11]. In a histological study of renal biopsies from 45 pediatric patients with CKD, 67% of the patients with NPHP1-deficient NPH exhibited interstitial fibrosis and cysts were observed only in 22%. The prevalence of cysts was not different when compared with patients with non-NPH CKD (P = 0 .6) [12], indicating that fibrosis is the main pathogenesis of NPHP1-deficient NPH, unlike NPH caused by genes other than NPHP1.

There are many mouse models of NPHP1-deficient NPH [13, 14, 15, 16]. In contrast to human NPH patients, it took as long as 72 weeks for one of the models to show non-severe fibrotic changes and a mild elevation of serum creatinine (wild-type vs. Nphp1del2–20/del2–20, 20 µmol/l vs. 30 µmol/l) [15]. Moreover, none of the other Nphp1-deficient mouse models showed a fibrotic phenotype at all [13, 14, 16]. This discrepancy in renal phenotypes between humans and mice has posed a challenge in understanding the pathology of and developing therapies for NPHP1-deficient NPH.

Recently, we generated induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) deficient in NPHP1 using the CRISPR/Cas9 system [17]. Herein, we generated kidney organoids from NPHP1−/− iPSCs. We evaluated whether the organoid model could sufficiently replicate the fibrosis associated with NPHP1-deficient NPH. We found a link between NPHP1 and Hippo signaling, leading to renal fibrosis. Finally, we examined whether Hippo pathway inhibitors could act as therapeutic agents by suppressing fibrosis in NPHP1−/− organoids.

Materials and methods

Antibodies

The primary antibodies used for immunofluorescence (IF) studies comprised goat anti-PODXL (R&D, AF1658, AB_354920), mouse anti-CDH1 (Invitrogen, 13-1700, AB_2533003), biotinylated anti-LTL (Vector Lab, B-1325, AB_2336558), rabbit anti-α-SMA (Abcam, ab5694, AB_2223021), rabbit anti-fibronectin (Abcam, ab2413, AB_2262874), and rabbit anti-SIX2 (Proteintech, 11562-1-AP, AB_2189084). The secondary antibodies used for IF experiments comprise Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A21202, AB_141607), Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A21206, AB_2535792), Alexa Fluor 555 donkey anti-goat IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A11056, AB_2536180), Alexa Fluor 555 donkey anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A31570, AB_2536180), Alexa Fluor 555 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A31572, AB_162543), and Streptavidin, Alexa Fluor 633 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, S21375, AB_231500). The primary antibodies used in western blotting comprised mouse anti-Flag M2 (Sigma, F3165, AB_259529) and mouse anti-Myc-Tag (Cell Signaling Technology, 2276, AB_331783). The secondary antibodies used in western blotting comprised anti-mouse IgG (H + L), AP conjugate (Promega, S3721, AB_430871).

Cell culture

Human iPSC 1231A3 was provided by the RIKEN BRC through the National Bio Resource Project of the MEXT, Japan [18], and we generated NPHP1−/− iPSCs as reported [17]. All iPSCs were maintained in StemFit AK02N (Ajinomoto, RCAK02N, CVCL_LJ39) supplemented with 10 µM Y-27632 (Nacalai Tesque, 18188-04) and 100 ng/ml FGF2 (PeproTech, 100-18B). Cell culture plates were coated with hESC-qualified Geltrex (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A1413302). HEK 293T cells were maintained in DMEM (Nacalai Tesque, 0845935) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum.

Differentiation of 3D kidney organoids

We followed Morizane’s method for the directed differentiation of iPSCs into 3D kidney organoids [19, 20]. During differentiation, iPSCs were maintained in Advanced RPMI 1690 (Thermo Fisher, 12633020) supplemented with GlutaMax (Gibco, 35050-061). Initially, iPSCs were treated with 4.5 µM CHIR 99021 (R&D, 252917-06-9) from d 0 to d 4. Subsequently, 10 ng/ml Activin A (R&D, 338-AC) was added from d 4 to d 6, followed by 10 ng/ml FGF-9 (R&D, 273-F9) on d 7 to differentiate the cells into metanephric mesenchyme cells, including nephron progenitor cells, with 80–90% efficacy. On d 8, arising metanephric mesenchyme cells were transferred into 96-well ultralow attachment plates (Sumitomo Bakelite, MS-9096S). The cells were treated with 2.5 µM CHIR 99021 and 10 ng/ml FGF-9 from d 8 to d 10 and with 10 ng/ml FGF-9 from d 10 to d 13. After 28 d of culture, the organoids were used for experiments. On day 8, we evaluated the expression of SIX-2 as a marker of nephron progenitor cells by immunofluorescence staining for quality control. On d 21, we performed immunofluorescence staining of organoids using antibodies against CDH1, PODXL, and LTL to identify nephron-like structures. In all experiments, we compared the phenotypes of organoids derived from NPHP1−/− iPSCs with those of wild-type organoids derived from the parental 1231A3 iPSC line.

Immunofluorescence staining of kidney organoids

Organoids were rinsed once with PBS (Nacalai Tesque, 27575-31) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, washed three times with PBS, and immersed in 30% sucrose/PBS until they sank. Then, the organoids were submerged in cryomolds containing optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura Finetek, 4583) and frozen using liquid nitrogen. Frozen blocks were sectioned into 8-µm slices using Tissue-Tek Polar Cryostat (Sakura Finetek, POLAR-D). The sections were blocked with Blocking One Histo (Nacalai Tesque, 06349-64) for 1 h at room temperature, washed in PBS three times, and incubated with the primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Primary antibodies were diluted in 5% Blocking One Histo in PBS. Avidin/Biotin Blocking Kit (Vector labs, SP-2001) was used for staining biotinylated-LTL. The next day, the slides were washed three times with PBS and incubated with the corresponding combination of secondary antibodies. Finally, samples were mounted using ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant with NucBlue Stain (Invitrogen, P36981). All slides were examined under a fluorescence microscope (Keyence, BZ-X800) or confocal microscope (Leica, SP-8, and Nikon, AX80). Sections were scanned with a 10×, 20×, or 60× lens. Images used to compare each other were acquired with the same setting and the same size. The stained areas were quantified using ImageJ Fiji (https://imagej.net/software/fiji/downloads).

Quantitative PCR

Organoids were lysed using Sepazol-RNA I Super G (Nacalai Tesque, 09379). Total RNA was extracted using Direct-Zol RNA MicroPrep Kit (Zymo Research R2060), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured using NanoDrop OneC (Thermo Fisher, ND-ONE-W). RNA was reverse transcribed with ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (TOYOBO, FSQ-201). Quantitative PCR was performed using PCR Thermal Cycler Dice (TaKaRa Bio). Transcript levels were normalized to GAPDH expression.

The following primers were used. All primers could detect human genes.

GAPDH: 5′-GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCG-3′ and 5′-ACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA-3′, ACTA2: 5′-CTATGCCTCTGGACGCACAACT-3′ and 5′-CAGATCCAGACGCATGATGGCA-3′, FN1: 5′-ACAACACCGAGGTGACTGAGAC-3′ and 5′-GGACACAACGATGCTTCCTGAG-3′, COL1A: 5′-GATTCCCTGGACCTAAAGGTGC-3′ and 5′-AGCCTCTCCATCTTTGCCAGCA-3′, COL3A: 5′-TGGTCTGCAAGGAATGCCTGGA-3′ and 5′-TCTTTCCCTGGGACACCATCAG-3′, CTGF: 5′-CTTGCGAAGCTGACCTGGAAGA-3′ and 5′-CCGTCGGTACATACTCCACAGA-3′, CYR61: 5′-GGAAAAGGCAGCTCACTGAAGC-3′ and 5′-GGAGATACCAGTTCCACAGGTC-3′, ANKRD1: 5′-GGAGATACCCCGTTGCATGAT-3′ and 5′-GTAGCACCAGATCCATCGGC-3′.

Drug induction

We administrated 5 ng/ml IL-1β (Peprotech, 200-01B), 5 µM ethacridine (Selleck S4945), 5 µM peptide 17 (Selleck, S8164), and 1 µM verteporfin (Selleck, S1786) onto NPHP1−/− organoids. The concentration of these reagents used was as reported [21, 22, 23].

Cell fractionation

Plasmid transfection of HEK 293T cells was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 Reagent (Invitrogen, 11668500) and Opti-MEM (Gibco, 31985-070). After 3 d of culturing, the cells were fractionated in a centrifuge, and the total protein was extracted. For immunoprecipitation, the protein fractionation was treated with Anti-Flag M2 affinity gel (Sigma, A2220, AB_10063035) or Anti-c-Myc magnetic beads (Thermo Scientific, 88842), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Treated samples were retained using Tris SDS β-ME sample buffer (Cosmo-Bio, DCB-423437).

Western blotting

Protein samples were loaded onto sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) polyacrylamide gels, subjected to electrophoresis, and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes after SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The blots were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h, incubated with primary antibodies against Flag (1:1000) and Myc (1:1000) at 4 °C overnight, washed, and incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h.

Statistical analysis

R (version 4.3 .2) was used for data analysis. Welch’s t-test was used for comparisons between two means unless otherwise specified. We used Tukey’s multiple comparison test to compare three or more means. The results are shown as mean ± SEM, and statistical significance was set at *P < 0.05, **P < 0 .01, ***P < 0.001, n.s., not significant.

Results

Generation of NPHP1−/− 3D kidney organoids from human iPSCs

We used NPHP1−/− iPSCs to generate kidney organoids using Morizane’s method [17, 19, 20]. We assessed the optimal induction conditions for the organoids using SIX-2 staining at the 2D culture stage. Finally, we generated 3D kidney organoids from the NPHP1-knockout (NPHP1-deficient; NPHP1−/−) iPSCs (Fig. 1a).

To assess the embryological status and morphology of the nephrons, we cultured the organoids for 28 d and analyzed them using immunofluorescence. Co-staining with podocalyxin (PODXL), lotus tetragonolobus lectin (LTL), and E-cadherin (CDH1) revealed nephron-like structures in the organoids. We detected no specific changes, particularly in the structure of the proximal tubules, between wild-type and NPHP1−/− organoids (Fig. 1b).

We compared the potential fibrotic states of these early-stage organoids. We used immunofluorescence staining to evaluate the expression of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and fibronectin (FN) (Fig. 1c). No difference was observed between the two groups. We measured fluorescence intensity within the region of interest in each section and performed quantification. No statistical difference was observed (α-SMA: P = 0.303, FN: P = 0.468) (Fig. 1d). Finally, we extracted mRNA from the organoids and performed quantitative PCR. We found no significant change in the expression of ACTA2 (P = 0 .392), FN1 (P = 0.107), COL1A (P = 0.453), and COL3A (P = 0 .497) (Fig. 1e).

Therefore, we generated 3D kidney organoids from iPSCs lacking NPHP1. These organoids did not exhibit distinctive phenotypes. Generally, kidney organoids mimic fetal kidneys, and patients with NPHP1-deficient NPH do not exhibit kidney dysfunction during the fetal period. Consistently, our model did not exhibit specific phenotypes. However, a disease model is required to elucidate the pathology and aid in the development of therapeutic agents.

Indication of fibrosis stimulant to 3D kidney organoids revealed that the lack of NPHP1 promotes fibrotic change

After differentiation was induced, the NPHP1−/− organoids were similar to the wild-type organoids, indicating that they did not acquire a morphological or fibrotic phenotype at an early stage. Based on the clinical findings that most patients with NPHP1-deficient NPH develop ESKD in adulthood, we hypothesized that additional stimulation is required for NPHP1−/− organoids to develop fibrosis. A few models have already been proven to induce fibrosis in kidney organoids. Among them, we selected the well-established fibrosis model induced by IL-1β administration [24]. The concentration was set to a low dose that does not induce fibrosis in wild-type.

We treated wild-type and NPHP1−/− organoids with a low-dose of IL-1β. The control groups received an equivalent amount of D-PBS. After 7 d, the organoids were collected and evaluated for fibrosis (Figs. 2a-c).

Immunofluorescent staining of tissue sections showed that IL-1β administration did not induce fibrosis in wild-type organoids. However, in NPHP1−/− organoids, the expression of FN increased even at this low concentration of IL-1β (wild-type IL-1β vs. NPHP1−/− IL-1β P < 0.001) (Figs. 2a, b). Quantitative PCR indicated upregulation of FN1, COL1A, and COL3A (P = 0.003, 0 .036, and 0.024, respectively); however, no increase was observed in ACTA2 expression (P = 0.306) (Fig. 2c).

Interestingly, similar to IL-1β administration, fibrosis increased in NPHP1−/− organoids by extended culturing (Supplemental Figures S1 a-c).

Thus, we generated the first organoid model representing the fibrotic phenotype of NPHP1-deficient NPH. Using this model, we can investigate the pathophysiology of NPHP1-deficient NPH and develop therapeutic strategies.

NPHP1 binds to LATS1/2, a component of the Hippo signaling pathway

To elucidate the pathogenesis of NPHP1-deficient NPH, we investigated molecules that interact with NPHP1.

Initially, we investigated NPHP1-interacting proteins. We overexpressed Flag-NPHP1 and Flag-empty in HEK 293T cells. The proteins were isolated by immunoprecipitation and analyzed using mass spectrometry. Our results reconfirmed a strong binding of NPHP1 to NPHP4, consistent with other reports [25].

Of the molecules that interact with NPHP4, we focused on LATS1 and LATS2, which are the components of the Hippo signaling pathway [26]. The Hippo signaling pathway is involved in kidney fibrosis. We hypothesized that NPHP1 binds LATS. To verify the binding of proteins, Myc-LATS1 or Myc-LATS2 was expressed in HEK 293T cells with Flag-NPHP1. We performed co-immunoprecipitation, and the result of immunoblot confirmed binding of NPHP1 and the LATS proteins (Fig. 3a). Uncropped images of immunoblot in Fig. 3a are provided in Supplementary Fig. 3.

Then, we evaluated the role of Hippo signaling in NPHP1−/− organoids in a fibrosis model. We used qPCR to evaluate the transcription of CTGF, a representative fibrosis-inducing gene located downstream of Hippo signaling as well as CYR61 and ANKRD1 (Fig. 3b). We compared the fluctuations in response to IL-1β administration in wild-type and NPHP1−/− organoids. Gene expression changes in CTGF and ANKRD1 were observed only in NPHP1−/− organoids (P = 0.002 and < 0.001, respectively) (Fig. 3c). Although CYR61 was transcriptionally upregulated in wild-type organoids, fold-change was large in NPHP1−/− organoids (fold change = 1.849 and 3.072, respectively) (Fig. 3c). Similarly, the expressions of CTGF, CYR61, and ANKRD1 were increased in NPHP1−/− organoids with extended culturing (Supplemental Figure S1 d).

For further validation, we examined whether fibrosis was induced in NPHP1−/− organoids upon stimulation of Hippo signaling. Ethacridine, an enhancer of the Hippo signaling pathway, was administered to wild-type and NPHP1−/− organoids. The control groups received PBS. Ethacridine induced fibrosis in both types of kidney organoids. Moreover, sensitivity to ethacridine was increased in NPHP1−/− organoids (Supplemental Figures S2a, b).

These findings indicate that the Hippo signaling pathway plays a role in the progression of fibrosis in NPHP1-deficient NPH.

Hippo pathway inhibitor peptide 17 improved fibrosis in organoid models

Next, we examined the inhibitory effect of the Hippo pathway inhibitors on fibrosis using our organoid model to evaluate the potential of these drugs as therapeutic agents for NPH.

Based on the binding of NPHP1 to LATS, we hypothesized that insufficient inhibition of YAP-TAZ by the LATS complex promotes fibrosis through enhanced Hippo signaling. Therefore, we first assessed peptide 17, which specifically inhibits the binding of YAP/TAZ-TEAD, located downstream of LATS1/2 (and NPHP1) (Fig. 3b).

Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that peptide 17 significantly suppressed IL-1β-induced fibrosis in NPHP1−/− organoids (IL-1β vs. IL-1β with peptide 17, P < 0.001) (Figs. 4a, b). Quantitative PCR revealed transcriptional suppression of CTGF, CYR61, and ANKRD1, which are downstream of the YAP/TAZ-TEAD complex (Fig. 4c). The transcription of the fibrosis markers, FN1, COL1A, and COL3A, was significantly suppressed (IL-1β vs. IL-1β with peptide 17, P < 0.001, < 0.001, and 0.017, respectively) (Fig. 4d).

Drug repositioning

Some drugs in clinical use are known to inhibit Hippo signaling. We used these drugs for drug repositioning to confirm their protective effect on NPHP1−/− organoids. Because no effective animal models are available, drug repositioning has great meaning in establishing the treatment method. In addition, the result obtained with a single drug alone is insufficient to ascertain the efficacy of the Hippo pathway inhibitor. Therefore, we conducted this experiment.

We administered verteporfin to NPHP1−/− organoids. Verteporfin is used as a photosensitizer for photodynamic therapy to treat macular degeneration. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that verteporfin considerably reduced induction of FN by IL-1β (IL-1β vs. IL-1β with verteporfin, P < 0.001) (Figs. 5a, b). qPCR analysis confirmed the transcriptional inhibition of genes downstream of Hippo pathway and fibrosis marker genes (Figs. 5c, d).

Discussion

Since NPH was first reported in 1945 [27], several causative genes have been identified [2]. NPHP1 deficiency is the most common and represents an important target for intervention [28, 29]. Generally, fibrosis and microcysts associated with tubular dilation are observed in NPH [8]. However, unlike other forms of NPH, NPHP1-deficient NPH is characterized by early-stage tubulointerstitial fibrosis, and the incidence of cyst formation is less common in patients with NPHP1-deficient NPH [9, 10, 11]. Therefore, a model that emulates fibrosis is required to elucidate the pathophysiology of NPHP1-deficient NPH.

Although several reports have been available on mouse models [13, 14, 15, 16], replicating the fibrosis and renal failure of the human kidney has been difficult. Therefore, elucidating pathophysiology and developing therapeutic strategies have been challenging.

Recent advances in biotechnology, including in vitro direct differentiation protocols, have enabled studies in pathogenesis and iPSC-based drug discovery using kidney organoids generated from iPSCs [30, 31, 32, 33, 34]. Given the lack of suitable animal models, we generated 3D kidney organoids from NPHP1−/− iPSCs. We generated the first model that reproduced fibrosis in NPHP1-deficient NPH using human cells. Furthermore, we made progress in elucidating the pathogenesis of NPHP1-deficient NPH and in identifying therapeutic reagents.

A few studies have documented interactions between NPH-causing proteins, distinct from NPHP1, and the Hippo signaling pathway. However, no reports are available on the investigation of these interactions in the context of fibrosis [35, 36]. A recent report has shown that continuous hydrodynamic stimulation in rotational suspension culture increases cyst formation [37]. This suggests that physical stimulation of the cilia by the presence of urine flow is important for cyst formation.

On the other hand, our aim is not cyst formation but fibrosis. Our system uses stimulation by IL-1β, which is established as a fibrosis-inducing model in kidney organoids, and this system is more essential for evaluating fibrogenesis.

We have identified the involvement of the Hippo signaling pathway in the pathogenesis of NPHP1-deficient NPH. We confirmed the binding of NPHP1 and LATS1/2, a key component of the Hippo pathway. Furthermore, we have demonstrated that IL-1β administration leads to excessive activation of Hippo pathway, specifically in NPHP1−/− organoids, resulting in fibrosis. The administration of the Hippo pathway inhibitors ameliorated this fibrosis.

The Hippo pathway has a broad spectrum of functions, including cell growth, proliferation, tissue or organ regeneration, and wound healing [38]. In addition, the Hippo pathway plays an important role in fibrosis [26]. Dysregulation of Hippo pathway can cause diseases in various organs, such as fibrosis in the lung, liver, and kidney [39, 40, 41]. The contribution of the Hippo pathway dysregulation to fibrosis under the AKI-to-CKD transition in the kidney has recently been reported [42]. It has been extensively reported that the loss of LATS or overexpression of YAP/TAZ leads to the activation of the Hippo pathway resulting in renal fibrosis in mice [43, 44, 45, 46, 47].

The Hippo pathway inhibitor, peptide 17, considerably reduced fibrogenesis in NPHP1−/− organoids. Peptide 17 specifically and potently inhibits the binding of YAP/TAZ to TEAD, implying that the major etiology of enhanced fibrosis in NPHP1−/− organoids is situated upstream of the YAP/TAZ-TEAD interaction within the Hippo pathway. Considering the binding of NPHP1 to LATS, deletion of NPHP1 disrupted the LATS-mediated inhibition of YAP/TAZ, thereby inducing fibrosis.

We demonstrated an inhibitory effect of another Hippo pathway inhibitor verteporfin on fibrosis in NPHP1−/− organoids. Several drugs in the market have been reported to exhibit an inhibitory effect on the Hippo pathway, and research into their potential applications is ongoing [48]. In the case of diseases, including NPHP1-deficient NPH, for which in vivo models are unavailable, the drug-repositioning approach is beneficial in terms of human safety. Verteporfin was well-tolerated by humans. Our results suggest that administration of verteporfin could be effective for patients with NPHP1-deficient NPH.

NPHP1−/− organoids showed no phenotype when compared with the wild-type at the early maturation stage. Among NPH, NPHP1-deficient NPH shows no phenotype at birth and kidney function is progressively lost with growth [49]. Our group has previously reported that even twins with identical genetic backgrounds have a large discrepancy in the age they develop ESKD [50]. We hypothesized that an external factor, which could be described as a “third hit,” is involved in the disease progression in patients with NPHP1-deficient NPH.

Extended culturing resulted in a robust difference in FN expression between wild-type and NPHP1−/− organoids. The time-dependent progression of fibrosis indicated a common mechanism with age-related disease progression in NPHP1-deficient NPH.

Experiments with low-dose IL-1β induced fibrosis exclusively in NPHP1−/− organoids, whereas no change was observed in wild-type organoids. This finding suggests the importance of a third hit in promoting fibrosis in NPHP1-deficient NPH.

Because most causative genes of NPH are highly expressed in primary cilia, research efforts to elucidate the pathogenesis of NPH have become centered around “Cilia/Centrosome Hypothesis” [51, 52]. However, because NPHP1 is expressed outside cilia as well as inside cilia [53], further investigation is required to verify whether cilia are involved in functional disruption of Hippo owing to NPHP1 deficiency. In summary, the lack of NPHP1 induced fibrosis through the overactivation of the Hippo signaling pathway. In addition, the Hippo pathway inhibitors can potentially be used as novel therapeutic agents for treating NPHP1-deficient NPH.

Conclusions

iPSC-derived kidney organoids serve as a practical model for studying fibrosis in NPHP1-deficient NPH, enabling the elucidation of disease pathophysiology and the development of therapeutic strategies. The lack of NPHP1 induces fibrosis through the overactivation of the Hippo signaling pathway. Furthermore, Hippo pathway inhibitors show potential as novel therapeutic agents for treating NPHP1-related NPH.
