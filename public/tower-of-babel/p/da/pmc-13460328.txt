A Neural Circuit from the Pedunculopontine Tegmental Nucleus to the Central Medial Thalamic Nucleus for the Regulation of Nociceptive and Anxiety-like Behaviors in Male Mice with Chronic Inflammatory Pain
Authors: Shao-Jie Gao, Ya-Qun Zhou, Wen-Lu Song, Xiao-Ran Yang, Yi-Yao Jing, Long-Qing Zhang, Hong Wu, Xin-Yi Dai, Dai-Qiang Liu, Pei Zhang, Bo Tian, Wei Wang, Wei Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Chronic pain and anxiety exhibit a high degree of comorbidity, in which these conditions can mutually exacerbate each other. The authors investigated whether glutamatergic neurons in the pedunculopontine tegmental nucleus (PPTgGlu) and their projections to the central medial thalamic nucleus (CM) regulate nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors in a complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA)–induced chronic pain model.Methods:The authors used c-Fos staining, chemogenetics, and optogenetics to assess the role of PPTgGlu and the PPTgGlu–CM pathway in regulating nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors. Rabies-mediated retrograde tracing was used to map the anatomical connection from PPTgGlu to glutamatergic neurons in the CM (CMGlu). Optogenetics combined with electrophysiologic recordings was used to verify the functional connectivity of this pathway.Results:Plantar injection of CFA induced anxiety-like behaviors. This behavioral comorbidity was associated with the activation of glutamatergic neurons in the PPTg, as indicated by c-Fos staining. Chemogenetic inhibition of the PPTgGlu alleviated both evoked and spontaneous nociceptive behaviors, as well as anxiety-like behaviors in CFA-treated mice. Conversely, optogenetic activation of these neurons was sufficient to elicit both evoked and spontaneous nociceptive behaviors, as well as anxiety-like behaviors in naïve mice. To identify the underlying circuit, the authors first employed both anterograde and retrograde tracing to map the anatomical connectivity between the PPTg and the CM. The authors then verified the functional connectivity of this specific PPTgGlu–CMGlu pathway using optogenetics combined with electrophysiologic recordings. Finally, pathway-specific manipulations demonstrated that chemogenetic inhibition of the PPTgGlu–CM pathway relieved nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors, whereas optogenetic activation of this pathway was sufficient to induce these behaviors in naïve mice.Conclusions:The CM receives excitatory input from PPTgGlu, and this PPTgGlu-CM pathway modulates nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors.

Chronic pain is frequently comorbid with negative affective states, particularly anxiety. Such comorbidity can create a vicious cycle that increases and prolongs the pain experience. In this context, treatments targeting a single symptom are often inadequate.1–3 Although analgesics and anxiolytics are available, their long-term effectiveness is often limited by side effects and the challenge of managing both conditions concurrently. To develop novel therapies, it is therefore crucial to define the common, integrated brain circuits underlying both the sensory and affective dimensions of chronic pain.

Multiple brain regions have been implicated in the central mechanisms of chronic pain and emotional comorbidity processing, including the paraventricular thalamus, the lateral septum, the hindlimb primary somatosensory cortex, the dorsal raphe nucleus, and others.4–9 The pedunculopontine tegmental nucleus (PPTg) comprises diverse glutamatergic, cholinergic, and γ-aminobutyric acid–mediated (GABAergic) neurons, which are crucial for regulating wakefulness, cognition, and sensorimotor functions.10–13 Notably, recent findings indicate that the PPTg is the critical link between nociception and the affective component of pain. Optogenetic activation of PPTg cholinergic projections to the substantia nigra pars reticulata reversed hyperalgesia, suggesting a causative role in nociception.14 Moreover, activation of PPTg neurons has been shown to produce anxiety-like behaviors.15 These findings collectively implicate the PPTg as a structure that not only is involved in pain or anxiety but also may serve as a converging point for these comorbid states. Since glutamatergic neurons are the largest population in the PPTg and their role at this converging point has not been investigated, we hypothesize that these neurons are critical in mediating comorbid chronic pain and anxiety-like behaviors.

A prime candidate for receiving and integrating signals from the PPTg is the central medial thalamic nucleus (CM). As a key limbic thalamic structure, the CM is critically involved in processing both sensory (e.g., pain and itch) and affective information.16–19 This integrative role is supported by its anatomical position as a convergence zone. This region receives dense inputs from diverse sources, including the PPTg, locus coeruleus, periaqueductal gray, and dorsal raphe nucleus.16 Functionally, CM lesions reduce nociceptive behaviors.20 Separately, inhibition of the CM–to–medial prefrontal cortex circuit alleviates both scratching and chronic itch–evoked anxiety-like behaviors.17 Together, these findings demonstrate CM’s role in modulating sensory–affective outputs. However, despite the known involvement of both nuclei in pain–affect comorbidity and the existence of a PPTg–CM connection, a fundamental gap remains. First, it is unknown whether glutamatergic neurons of the PPTg project to the CM. Second, if this projection exists, its specific functional role in nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors is unclear.

Based on this evidence, we hypothesize that PPTgGlu regulate chronic pain and its associated anxiety-like behaviors. We further propose that these neurons form a functional projection to the CM. Consequently, this specific PPTgGlu–CM circuit is predicted to play a critical role in modulating these comorbid behavioral states. To test this hypothesis, we first used c-Fos expression to assess neuronal activation among glutamatergic neurons in the PPTg during chronic pain conditions. We further investigated their role by either chemogenetically inhibiting or optogenetically activating the glutamatergic neurons in the PPTg. To dissect the underlying circuit basis, we used rabies-mediated retrograde tracing to map the anatomical connectivity of the PPTgGlu–CMGlu neural circuit. Next, we combined optogenetics with electrophysiologic recordings to study the functional connectivity within this circuit. Additionally, we assessed c-Fos expression to examine the activation of glutamatergic neurons in the CM under chronic pain conditions. Finally, using chemogenetic inhibition or optogenetic activation, we examined how modulation of the PPTgGlu–CM circuit affects nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Only male C57BL/6J mice (n = 145) and vesicular glutamate tranporter 2 (Vglut2)-Cre mice (n = 10) aged 8 to 10 weeks (body weight, 20 to 27 g) were used in the current study (Supplemental Digital Content figure S1, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E517). C57BL/6J mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (certificate No. SCXK [Su] 2023-0004; China). The animal quality certificate numbers are provided in table S2 of the Supplemental Digital Content (https://links.lww.com/ALN/E515). Vglut2-Cre mice were provided by the Wuhan National Laboratory for Optoelectronics (Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, China) and had previously been backcrossed to the C57BL/6J background for at least seven generations before use. Only heterozygous Vglut2-Cre mice were used in the following experiments.

All animals were housed and experiments were conducted at the experimental animal center of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Wuhan, China; certificate No. SYXK [E] 2021-0057). Experiments involving viral injections were carried out in an Animal Biosafety Level 2 laboratory. All mice were housed in groups of four or five mice per cage under specific pathogen-free conditions, with a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on 7:00 am to 7:00 pm), ambient temperature of 22° ± 1°C (ranging from 21 °C to 23 °C), and relative humidity of 50 ± 5% (45%–55%). Food and water were available ad libitum throughout the experimental period. All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji Medical College, HUST (approval No. 2025-5060), and were conducted in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines. Animals were allocated to experimental groups using a computer-generated random sequence (www.random.org/lists). The behavioral tests were carried out by experimenters who were unaware of group assignments.

Chronic Pain Model: Chronic Inflammatory Pain Induced by Complete Freund’s Adjuvant

To induce chronic inflammatory pain, we injected complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA) into the left hind paw. This model was chosen because it reliably induces persistent peripheral inflammation and central sensitization, both of which are crucial for studying chronic pain–related neural circuits. Consequently, it has become a frequently used model in this field of research.21–24 The procedure followed standard protocols.6,7 Briefly, under anesthesia with 2.4% sevoflurane, 20 μl CFA (catalog No. F5881; Sigma-Aldrich; USA) was injected into the center of the left hind paw using an insulin syringe (BD Corporation; USA) on day 1. Age-matched controls received the same volume of sterile saline (0.9% NaCl). To sustain a persistent pain state, a second booster injection of 10 μl CFA (or saline for controls) was administered on day 12 after the initial treatment at the location of the initial injection (fig. 1A). This two-injection protocol was necessary. Previous research has shown that a single injection of 10 μl CFA results in the recovery of both mechanical pain threshold and anxiety-like behaviors by day 7.25 Furthermore, even when the initial injection dose was increased to 20 μl in our study, a rebound in the pain threshold was still observed on day 12. This finding collectively demonstrates the transient nature of the single-injection effect. Thus, this optimized CFA model reliably produces the sustained inflammatory pain required for our investigation.

Viral Injection

Mice were anesthetized with 2.4% sevoflurane and then secured in a stereotaxic device (RWD; China). Erythromycin eye ointment was applied to prevent corneal drying, and body temperature was maintained at 35° to 37°C with a far-infrared heating pad (Kent Scientific; USA). Viral injection was performed with a glass pipette (tip diameter, 15 to 20 μm; WPI; USA) controlled by an infusion pump (Stoelting; USA) at a rate of 30 nl/min. The pipette was left in place for an additional 10 min to minimize reflux of the viral solution.

Coordinates of the targeted brain regions were defined relative to bregma in three axes: anterior–posterior, medio–lateral, and dorsal–ventral. Based on the mouse brain atlas (The Mouse Brain26, Paxinos and Franklin, 2nd edition), the following stereotaxic coordinates were used: PPTg (−4.48 mm, −1.16 mm, −3.60 mm) and CM (−1.58 mm, 0 mm, −4.80 mm).

For retrograde monosynaptic tracing, helper viruses including adeno-associated virus (AAV) 2/9-EF1α-DIO-TVA-EGFP (2.0E + 12 vg/ml; PT-0021; BrainVTA; China) and AAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-rabies virus glycoprotein (2.0E + 12 vg/ml, 2:1, 50 nl; PT-0023; BrainVTA) were injected into the CM (−1.58 mm, 0 mm, −4.80 mm) of Vglut2-Cre mice. Three weeks after the virus expression, rabies virus (RV; RV-ENVA-ΔG-dsRed; 50 nl; R01002; BrainVTA) was injected into the same site in the CM. Ten days later, perfusion and slicing were conducted to examine the RV signal in the PPTg.

Mechanical Allodynia Measurement

As described in a previous article,27 mice were acclimated in individual chambers for 30 min. The mechanical paw withdrawal threshold (MPWT) was then assessed using a series of Von Frey filaments (0.008, 0.02, 0.04, 0.07, 0.16, 0.4, 0.6, and 1 g). Starting with the 0.16-g filament, each filament was applied perpendicularly to the left mid-plantar hind paw for up to 6 s. A brisk paw withdrawal or licking in response to Von Frey filament stimulation was considered a positive response. If a positive response was observed, the filament with the next lowest force was applied, and if no response occurred, the filament with the next highest force was used. The lowest force (in grams) required to elicit a positive response was defined as the MPWT. Testing was conducted between 8:00 am and 4:00 pm.

Spontaneous Nociceptive Behaviors Observation

To assess spontaneous nociceptive behaviors, mice were acclimated in individual transparent chambers (10 × 10 × 10 cm) for 40 min. Then a 10-min observation session was conducted, during which bouts of licking, lifting, or flinching of the left hind paw were recorded. Exploration, locomotion, rearing, or grooming behaviors were excluded from the analysis.28

Hot Plate Test

As described in a previous article,29 mice were placed inside a transparent acrylic cylinder on a hot plate (XR1700; Xinruan, China) maintained at 55°C. The latency to either lick a hind paw or jump was recorded as the endpoint. A cutoff of 60 s was applied to prevent potential damage to the animals. All animals were returned to their home cages after the test.

Tail-flick Test

Following standard procedures,30 the tail-flick latency (TFL) was measured. The distal 1 to 1.5 cm of the tail was immersed in a water bath maintained at 50° ± 0.5°C. TFL was defined as the time from immersion until the animal flicked or withdrew its tail. TFL was measured manually with a stopwatch. The trial was terminated if no response occurred within 10 s to prevent tissue injury.

Open Field Test

Twenty-four days after the virus injection, mice underwent the open field test (OFT). Two hours before the experiment, mice were placed in the test room for acclimation. Given that illumination intensity might influence anxiety-like behaviors in mice, the illumination in the test room was carefully controlled using energy-efficient light bulbs and monitored with an illuminometer (Testo 545; Testo, Germany). The illumination in the test room was set to approximately 20 lux.31 At the start of the experiment, mice were placed in the center of the open field (50 cm × 50 cm × 60 cm) and allowed to freely explore for 5 min. Concurrently, their movements were recorded using a video system (SuperMaze+; Xinruan, China). The test was performed as previously described, with a duration of 5 min.32

Elevated Plus Maze Test

Twenty-five days after virus injection, mice underwent the elevated plus maze test (EPM). Two hours before the experiment, mice were placed in the test room for acclimatization. The illumination in the test room was set to the same level as that used in the OFT. The EPM apparatus consisted of a cross comprising two open arms (35 cm long) and two closed arms (35 cm long and 10-cm-high walls) connected by a central platform. At the beginning of the experiment, each mouse was placed in the central area of the maze and allowed to freely explore. The test was performed as previously described, with a duration of 5 min.33 Throughout this period, the movements of the mice were recorded using a video system (SuperMaze+).

Light–Dark Box Test

The apparatus consisted of two side-by-side chambers connected through a doorway (7 × 7 cm): an open-top white box (27 × 27 × 27 cm; light level, 470 to 500 lux) and a black box with a closed top (18 × 27 × 27 cm). The entire apparatus was situated in a sound-attenuating chamber. At the beginning of the test, the mice were placed on the light side with their heads away from the dark chamber and permitted to explore freely for 5 min. The mouse movements were recorded using a video tracking system (SuperMaze+).

In Vivo Chemogenetic Manipulation

For the chemogenetic inhibition of glutamatergic neurons in the PPTg, AAV2/9–calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II α subunit (CaMKIIα)–human M4 muscarinic DREADD (hM4D (Gi))-mCherry (2.0E + 12 vg/ml, 100 nl; PT-0017; BrainVTA) or AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-mCherry (2.0E + 12 vg/ml, 100 nl; PT-1167, BrainVTA) was injected into the PPTg (−4.48 mm, −1.16 mm, −3.60 mm) (fig. 2, A and B). To chemogenetically inhibit the PPTgGlu-CM circuit, AAV2/9-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry (2.0E + 12 vg/ml, 100 nl; PT-0043; BrainVTA) or AAV2/9-DIO-mCherry (2.0E + 12 vg/ml, 100 nl; PT-0013; BrainVTA) was injected into the PPTg (−4.48 mm, −1.16 mm, −3.60 mm). Simultaneously, AAV2/R-CaMKIIα-Cre (5.0E + 12 vg/ml, 50 nl; PT-0220; BrainVTA) was injected into the CM (−1.58 mm, 0 mm, −4.80 mm). Clozapine N-oxide (CNO; 3 mg/kg; BrainVTA) was intraperitoneally injected into mice 1 h before the start of the behavioral experiments.

In Vivo Optogenetic Manipulation

For optogenetic activation of the glutamatergic neurons in the PPTg, C57BL/6J mice were injected with AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2)-mCherry (2.0E + 12 vg/ml, 100 nl; AG26975; Obio Technology, China) or AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-mCherry (2.0E + 12 vg/ml, 100 nl; A0V013; Obio Technology) to the PPTg (−4.48 mm, −1.16 mm, −3.60 mm). Two weeks later, an optical fiber (Fiber Core, 200 μm; Numerical Aperture, 0.37; Inper, China) was unilaterally implanted above the PPTg (−4.48 mm, −1.16 mm, −3.40 mm) and then secured with dental adhesive resin cement (C&B-Superbond; Sun Medical, Japan). For the optogenetic activation of the PPTgGlu–CM circuit, C57BL/6J mice were injected with AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-ChR2-mCherry (2.0E + 12 vg/ml, 100 nl; AG26975; Obio Technology) or AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-mCherry (2.0E + 12 vg/ml, 100 nl; A0V013; Obio Technology) into the PPTg (−4.48 mm, −1.16 mm, −3.60 mm). Two weeks later, an optical fiber (Fiber Core, 200 μm; NA, 0.37; Inper) was implanted above the CM (10°, −1.58 mm, +0.67 mm, −3.57 mm) and then secured with dental adhesive resin cement (C&B-Superbond). The light intensity at the tip of the optical fiber was measured and calibrated to 3 to 5 mW/mm2 using an optical power meter (PM20A; Thorlabs, Germany) before the behavioral experiments. It has been demonstrated recently that a rostrally localized glutamatergic subpopulation within the PPTg expresses the transcription factor Ceh-10 homeodomain-containing homolog (Chx10). Optogenetic stimulation of these Chx10-derived glutamatergic neurons in the PPTg induces an immediate cessation of all spontaneous locomotor behaviors in mice.34 To prevent sustained motor suppression during optogenetic activation, we employed an intermittent stimulation protocol (1 s light on/9 s light off per 10-s cycle) based on established parameters from previous literature.35 Specifically, a 473-nm blue light was delivered as 10-ms pulses at 10 Hz to stimulate glutamatergic neurons in the PPTg (see fig. 3F for the protocol). This paradigm helps minimize overall behavioral inhibition and facilitates the observation of specific neuronal effects on behavior. In behavioral experiments designed to activate the PPTgGlu–CM neural circuit, the intermittent light protocol (fig. 6D) was maintained continuously throughout the session to thoroughly investigate the circuit’s functional role. For all nociceptive behavioral tests (MPWT, spontaneous nociceptive behaviors observation, hot plate, and tail-flick tests), a 5-min blue light prestimulation was applied before formal testing to ensure sufficient neuronal activation. This prestimulation used the same parameters as the formal test. Thereafter, the identical intermittent light protocol was applied continuously throughout the testing period. For anxiety-like behavioral tests (including OFT, EPM, and light–dark box test), the intermittent light protocol was applied continuously throughout the entire test session.

In Vitro Electrophysiologic Recordings

Mice were anesthetized using 2.4% sevoflurane. Brain tissue was rapidly extracted and placed in ice-cold, oxygenated slice-cutting solution containing (in mM) 125 NaCl, 3.5 KCl, 25 NaHCO3, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 0.1 CaCl2, 3 MgCl2, and 10 glucose. Coronal slices (280 μm thick) containing the PPTg or CM were prepared using a vibrating microtome (VT1000S; Leica; Germany) and immediately transferred to oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid with the following composition (in mM): 125 NaCl, 25 NaHCO3, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 3.5 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, and 10 glucose. Slices were then incubated in a 28°C water bath for 30 min before recording. After being low-pass filtered at 2 kHz and digitized at 2 to 10 kHz, the signals were acquired via a MultiClamp 700B amplifier and pClamp 10 software (Molecular Devices, USA). The recording data were analyzed using Clampfit 9.0 (Molecular Devices).

To validate the effectiveness of AAV2/9-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry, we performed whole cell patch clamp recordings on transfected PPTg neurons. Recording pipettes (4 to 7 MΩ) were filled with a solution containing (in mM) 140 K-gluconate, 13.4 Na-gluconate, 0.5 CaCl2, 1.0 MgCl2, 5 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 3 Mg-ATP (magnesium adenosine triphosphate), and 0.3 Na-GTP (sodium guanosine triphosphate) (osmolarity, 280 to 290 mOsm/kg; pH 7.4). Baseline activity was recorded, followed by bath application of CNO (10 μM) for 30 s.

To validate the effectiveness of AAV2/9-ChR2-mCherry, an optical fiber (Fiber Core, 200 μm; Inper) was positioned 0.2 mm above the target brain region for light delivery. Neurons in the PPTg expressing AAV2/9-ChR2-mCherry were patched and subsequently stimulated with a blue laser (473 nm, 10 Hz, 10 ms, 5 mW).

To prove the functional connection in the PPTgGlu–CMGlu circuit, AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-ChR2-mCherry (2.0E + 12 vg/ml, 100 nl; AG26975; Obio Technology) was infused into the PPTg in C57BL/6J mice, and AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-EGFP (2.0E + 12 vg/ml, 100 nl; PT-0290; BrainVTA) was infused into the CM. We positioned an optical fiber (Fiber Core, 200 μm; Inper) above the surface of the target brain region to deliver optical stimulation (473 nm, 10 ms). Glutamatergic neurons in the CM expressing AAV2/9-EGFP were patched using pipettes (4 to 7 MΩ) filled with a solution containing (in mM) 130 Cs-gluconate, 2 NaCl, 6 CsCl, 0.6 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 4 Mg2+–adenosine triphosphate, 0.3 Na+-GTP, 10 phosphocreatine, and 1 QX-314 (pH 7.25). After the terminals originating from PPTgGlu within the CM were stimulated by 473-nm blue light, evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) were recorded from the CMGlu neurons, which could be blocked by α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptor antagonist 6,7-dinitroquinoxaline-2,3(1H, 4H)-dione (DNQX; catalog No. HY-15067; MCE; China).

Immunohistochemistry

After completing the behavioral experiments, mice were anesthetized with 2.4% sevoflurane and transcardially perfused with 25 ml phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) followed by 25 ml 4% paraformaldehyde. Brain tissue was then immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4°C, followed by gradient dehydration in 20% and 30% sucrose at 4°C. Brain sections were sectioned to a thickness of 35 μm using a freezing microtome (CM1900; Leica). The sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (AR0205; Boster, China) for 15 min, blocked with 10% goat serum (AR0009; Boster) for 40 min at 37°C, and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. After washing with PBS for 30 min, the sections were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. The specific antibody combinations used in each experiment were as follows. For colabeling of c-Fos and glutamatergic neurons, primary antibodies included mouse anti–c-Fos (1:200; catalog No. 66590-1-Ig; Proteintech, USA) and rabbit anti-glutamate (1:300; catalog No. G6642; Sigma-Aldrich; USA), followed by secondary antibodies: goat antimouse IgG conjugated to Coralite Plus 488 (1:100; catalog No. RGAM002; Proteintech) and goat antirabbit IgG conjugated to Coralite Plus 594 (1:100; catalog No. RGAR004; Proteintech). For colabeling of viral expression and glutamatergic neurons, sections were incubated with the primary antibody rabbit anti-glutamate (1:300; catalog No. G6642; Sigma-Aldrich) and the secondary antibody goat antirabbit IgG conjugated to Coralite 488 (1:100; catalog No. SA00013-2; Proteintech). For the experiments presented in figure 4L, the primary antibody rabbit anti-glutamate (1:300; catalog No. G6642; Sigma-Aldrich) was used in combination with the secondary antibody goat antirabbit IgG conjugated to Alexa Fluor 405 (1:100; catalog No. AS056; ABclonal, China). After incubation with secondary antibodies and an additional 30-min PBS wash, the sections were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; AR1177; Boster) for 15 min, washed with PBS, and covered with coverslips. Images of the brain sections were captured using a fluorescence microscope (BX53; Olympus, Japan), an automated slide scanning system (VS120; Olympus), and an upright single-photon confocal microscope (LSM800; Zeiss, Germany). To quantify c-Fos–positive cells, the PPTg and CM were precisely located with reference to a standard brain atlas (The Mouse Brain26). Cell counting was performed by an experimenter blinded to the experimental groups using ImageJ software (Fiji Distribution, National Institutes of Health, USA). For each mouse, two brain sections were selected for analysis, with a total of seven mice used in the study. The mean count per mouse was used for statistical assessment.

Statistical Analysis

Animals with viral misinjections (n = 24), incorrect fiber placement (n = 3), or unsuccessful model establishment (n = 4) were excluded from the analysis (Supplemental Digital Content figure S1, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E517). Sample sizes were chosen based on similar experiments using chemogenetic and optogenetic methods.9,17,32,36,37 All data are presented as mean ± SD. The normality of each dataset was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, normally distributed data were further assessed for homogeneity of variances using the F test. Data with equal variances were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t test, while those with unequal variances were analyzed using Welch’s t test. For data that were not normally distributed, the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was used. For comparisons among the four independent groups formed by the factors model (saline vs. CFA) and virus (mCherry vs. hM4D), the normality of the residuals was confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk test before analysis. For data meeting this assumption, a two-way ANOVA was performed to examine the effects of the virus and model factors, followed by Šidák post hoc tests for pairwise comparisons when significant. For data violating the normality assumption, we employed the Scheirer–Ray–Hare test, a nonparametric equivalent of two‑way ANOVA, to assess main effects and interactions.38–40 This test was conducted using the Scheirer–Ray–Hare function from the rcompanion package in R (https://www.r-project.org, accessed March 18, 2026) version 4.5.2. When significant effects were found, pairwise comparisons were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, with Bonferroni correction applied to control for multiple comparisons. Specifically, for the data presented in figure 5, F and G, the Scheirer–Ray–Hare test revealed no significant interaction between model and virus, but significant main effects were detected. To further explore these effects, we performed prespecified pairwise comparisons among the four groups using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Bonferroni correction, focusing on the following contrasts: saline‑mCherry vs. saline‑hM4D, saline‑mCherry vs. CFA‑mCherry, and CFA‑mCherry vs. CFA‑hM4D. The mechanical pain data in figure 1B were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, followed by Šidák post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Detailed statistical methods for all experiments are provided in Supplemental Digital Content table S3 (https://links.lww.com/ALN/E516). All analyses used a significance level of P < 0.05, with some data processed in GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA) and electrophysiologic data analyzed in Clampfit 9.0 (Molecular Devices).

Pedunculopontine Tegmental Glutamatergic Neuronal Hyperactivity Underlies Nociceptive and Anxiety-like Behaviors in Chronic Pain

To investigate chronic pain and its associated anxiety-like behaviors in mice, we used a CFA-induced model of chronic inflammatory pain in mice. The experimental timeline of the behavioral tests (Von Frey test, spontaneous pain test, hot plate test, tail-flick test, OFT, EPM, and light–dark box text) is shown in figure 1A.

We first assessed whether CFA successfully induced a persistent pain state. As shown in figure 1B, the MPWT was significantly decreased in the CFA group (saline vs. CFA, 0.91 ± 0.07 g vs. 0.16 ± 0.28 g; P < 0.0001; n = 8). CFA-treated mice also exhibited an increased frequency of spontaneous nociceptive behaviors (saline vs. CFA, 1 ± 1.60 vs. 19.13 ± 3.27; P = 0.0002; n = 8; fig. 1C). Moreover, the CFA group showed a significant decrease in response latency in both the hot plate test and the tail-flick test (hot plate, 9.73 ± 1.01 s vs. 7.39 ± 0.66 s; P = 0.0002; n = 8; tail-flick test, 1.94 ± 0.32 s vs. 0.84 ± 0.07 s; P < 0.0001; n = 8; fig. 1, D and E). These results indicate that CFA injection successfully induced a persistent pain state.

We next investigated whether these mice developed associated anxiety-like behaviors. In the EPM, the CFA group entered the open arms less frequently and spent less time there compared to saline controls (time in open arms [%], 21.10 ± 7.82 vs. 7.01 ± 4.82; P = 0.0007; n = 8; entries in open arms, 5.88 ± 2.23 vs. 2.25 ± 1.75; P = 0.0028; n = 8; fig. 1, F to H). In the OFT, the total distance traveled did not differ significantly between the CFA and saline groups, indicating no general locomotor impairment (saline vs. CFA, 39,772 ± 7,912 mm vs. 42,578 ± 4,603 mm; P = 0.4006; n = 8; fig. 1J). Compared to the saline group, mice in the CFA group spent a significantly lower percentage of total time and traveled a significantly reduced percentage of total distance in the center area (time spent in center [%], 16.47 ± 3.60 vs. 12.52 ± 2.56; P = 0.0241; n = 8; distance in center [%], 21.12 ± 3.37 vs. 16.34 ± 2.93; P = 0.0091; n = 8; fig. 1, I, K, and L). Similarly, in the light–dark box test, CFA-treated mice spent significantly less time in the light compartment than did the saline group (saline vs. CFA, 53.25 ± 4.55 vs. 35.75 ± 6.02; P < 0.0001; n = 8; fig. 1, M and N). However, the number of transitions between compartments did not differ significantly between the groups (saline vs. CFA, 13.88 ± 2.36 vs. 13.75 ± 3.06; P = 0.9283; n = 8; fig. 1O). These results demonstrate that anxiety-like behaviors are consistently elicited by the CFA-induced chronic inflammatory pain model.

To investigate the underlying neural mechanisms, we examined neuronal activation in the PPTg using c-Fos immunostaining (fig. 1, P and Q). We found a significant increase in c-Fos expression within the PPTg of CFA-treated mice compared to controls (saline vs. CFA, 29.77 ± 13.99 vs. 181.4 ± 69.89; P = 0.001; n = 7; fig. 1R). Importantly, this increase was largely colocalized with glutamatergic neuron markers (fig. 1S). Quantitative analysis revealed that 55.21 ± 11% (mean ± SD) of glutamatergic neurons in the PPTg region were c-Fos+ in the CFA group. These results indicate that the chronic pain state accompanied by anxiety is associated with increased activity of PPTgGlu.

Chemogenetic Inhibition of PPTgGlu Alleviates Nociceptive and Anxiety-like Behaviors in Chronic Pain

We next investigated whether the activity of PPTgGlu is necessary for the expression of chronic pain and its accompanying anxiety-like behaviors. For chemogenetic inhibition of PPTgGlu, AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry or AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-mCherry was injected into the PPTg (fig. 2, A and B). The experimental timeline is schematically illustrated in figure 2C. Three weeks after injection, extensive mCherry fluorescence was observed in the PPTg. The robust expression colocalized with a glutamatergic neuronal marker, confirming targeted expression in PPTgGlu (fig. 2D). The quantification confirms that the vast majority (94.20 ± 1.227%) of mCherry⁺ neurons co-express the glutamatergic neuronal marker, demonstrating high specificity (Supplemental Digital Content 4, figure S1, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E517). To validate the effectiveness of the inhibitory chemogenetic virus hM4D, we performed whole cell recordings and found that application of CNO (10 μM) significantly inhibited the activity of PPTgGlu (fig. 2E).

We then assessed whether inhibiting PPTgGlu activity could reverse established nociceptive behaviors. After CNO administration, the MPWT was significantly increased in CFA-hM4D mice compared with CFA-mCherry controls. In contrast, no such change was observed between the saline-hM4D and saline-mCherry groups (CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 0.01 ± 0.01 g vs. 0.70 ± 0.19 g; P < 0.0001; n = 8; saline-mCherry vs. Saline-hM4D, 0.85 ± 0.21 g vs. 0.95 ± 0.14 g; P = 0.5069; n = 8; fig. 2F). Moreover, in both the hot plate and tail-flick tests, CFA-hM4D mice showed significantly increased response latencies compared to the CFA-mCherry group, indicating a reversal of CFA-induced hyperalgesia. No such latency changes were observed between the saline-hM4D and saline-mCherry groups (hot plate test, CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 6.56 ± 1.05 s vs. 11.95 ± 1.14 s; P < 0.0001; n = 8; saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 11.66 ± 1.56 s vs. 12.14 ± 1.99 s; P = 0.8939; n = 8; tail-flick test, CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 0.78 ± 0.08 s vs. 1.47 ± 0.15 s; P < 0.0001; n = 8; saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 1.46 ± 0.20 s vs. 1.61 ± 0.19 s; P = 0.2319; n = 8; fig. 2, H and I).

Critically, the same intervention also reversed anxiety-like behaviors. In the EPM, compared to the CFA-mCherry group, the CFA-hM4D group made significantly more entries into and spent more time in the open arms. No such differences were observed between the saline-treated groups (time in open arms [%], CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 0.36 ± 0.54 vs. 11.41 ± 4.05; P = 0.0001; n = 8; saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 13.90 ± 6.34 vs. 13.28 ± 4.87; P = 0.9898; n = 8; entries in open arms, CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 0.38 ± 0.52 vs. 7.5 ± 3.46; P = 0.0034; n = 8; saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 7.00 ± 5.86 vs. 6.63 ± 3.89; P = 0.9966; n = 8; fig. 2, J to L). In the OFT, the total distance traveled did not differ among groups (CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 18,327 ± 2,926 mm vs. 19,726 ± 5,046 mm; P = 0.9713; n = 8; saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 21,408 ± 7,651 mm vs. 20,893 ± 10,264 mm; P = 0.9985; n = 8; fig. 2N). However, the CFA-hM4D group exhibited increased time spent in and distance traveled within the center zone relative to the CFA-mCherry group, an effect not seen in saline-treated mice (time spent in center [%], CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 3.35 ± 0.54 vs. 11.60 ± 3.41; P < 0.0001; n = 8; saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 9.82 ± 1.83 vs. 11.59 ± 3.00; P = 0.4098; n = 8; distance in center [%], CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 9.24 ± 1.77 vs. 18.78 ± 3.84; P < 0.0001; n = 8; saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 18.80 ± 2.14 vs. 19.83 ± 5.03; P = 0.9128; n = 8; fig. 2, M, O, and P). Consistent with these findings, in the light–dark box test, CFA-hM4D mice spent more time in the light compartment than CFA-mCherry controls, a difference absent between the saline-treated groups (CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 14.68 ± 3.50 vs. 28.65 ± 4.46; P = 0.0028; n = 8; saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 25.78 ± 5.38 vs. 27.86 ± 5.77; P = 0.9553; n = 8; fig. 2, Q and R). Compared with the CFA-mCherry group, mice in the CFA-hM4D group showed an increased number of transitions, whereas no change was observed between the saline-mCherry and saline-hM4D groups (CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 8.00 ± 2.56 vs. 11.38 ± 2.56; P = 0.0320; n = 8; saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 11.75 ± 2.38 vs. 10.75 ± 2.38; P = 0.8099; n = 8; fig. 2S).

Taken together, these results indicated that chemogenetic inhibition of the PPTgGlu alleviated both nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors in the chronic pain model.

To further characterize cell type–specific connectivity between the PPTg and the CM, we employed a retrograde trans-monosynaptic tracing approach with Cre-dependent viral tools. This approach allowed us to identify the subtypes of PPTg neurons that project to glutamatergic neurons in the CM. Helper viruses (AAV-EF1α-DIO-TVA-GFP and AAV-EF1α-DIO-rabies virus glycoprotein) were injected into the CM of Vglut2-Cre mice (fig. 4C). Three weeks later, RV-ENVA-ΔG-dsRed was delivered into the same site, selectively infecting helper virus–positive neurons in the CM. In the presence of these helper viruses, the RV could retrogradely traverse monosynaptic connections. Neurons in the CM exhibiting both red and green fluorescence were identified as starter cells (fig. 4D). We detected numerous dsRed-labeled neurons in the PPTg of Vglut2-Cre mice (fig. 4E). These dsRed-labeled neurons colocalized with glutamatergic neurons, suggesting that glutamatergic neurons in the PPTg innervate glutamatergic neurons in the CM (fig. 4F).

These results confirm the anatomical projection from PPTgGlu to CMGlu. To delineate the alterations in CMGlu activity during chronic pain accompanied by anxiety-like behaviors, we subsequently examined neuronal activation using c-Fos immunostaining (fig. 4, G and H). c-Fos expression was significantly increased in the CM of CFA-treated mice compared to saline controls (saline vs. CFA, 346.2 ± 123.9 vs. 836.8 ± 249.2; n = 7; P = 0.0005; fig. 4I). Importantly, this increase was predominantly colocalized with glutamatergic neuronal markers (fig. 4J). Quantitative analysis revealed that 47.23 ± 12.25% of glutamatergic neurons in the CM were c-Fos–positive in CFA-treated mice.

Next we assessed the functional connection of the PPTgGlu–CMGlu circuit by optogenetics combined with electrophysiology recordings. AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-ChR2-mCherry was infused into the PPTg in C57BL/6J mice, and AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-EGFP was infused in the CM (fig. 4K). After 3 weeks, mCherry+ terminals (red), EGFP+ signals (green), and glutamatergic neurons (blue) were observed in the CM region. The colocalization of EGFP+ signals with glutamatergic neurons appeared as cyan fluorescence (fig. 4L). After these terminals were stimulated by 473-nm blue light, CMGlu neurons receiving projections from the PPTgGlu were evoked EPSCs, which could be blocked by DNQX (ChR2–artificial cerebrospinal fluid vs. ChR2-DNQX, −148.4 ± 52.25 vs. −5.48 ± 9.42; n = 4 cells; P = 0.0286; fig. 4, M and N). In summary, the PPTgGlu–CMGlu circuit has a functional connection.

Mice in the CFA-hM4D group exhibited a significant increase in response latency in both the hot plate test and the tail-flick test (hot plate test, CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 8.19 s ± 0.99 s vs. 13.83 ± 2.62 s; P < 0.0001; n = 8; tail-flick test, CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 1.08 ± 0.05 s vs. 1.68 ± 0.23 s; P = 0.0001; n = 8; fig. 5, H and I). After CNO administration, no change in nociceptive behaviors was observed in the saline-hM4D group compared to the saline-mCherry group (MPWT, 0.95 ± 0.14 g vs. 1.00 ± 0 g; P > 0.99; n = 8; spontaneous pain behaviors, 1.00 ± 0.93 vs. 0.25 ± 0.46; P = 0.1555; n = 8; hot plate test, 13.99 ± 2.04 s vs. 14.41 ± 1.33 s; P = 0.9574; n = 8; tail-flick test, 1.92 ± 0.31 s vs. 1.77 ± 0.30 s; P = 0.5359; n = 8).

Similarly, the intervention robustly alleviated anxiety-like behaviors. In the EPM, the time spent in the open arms was significantly increased in the CFA-hM4D group compared to the CFA-mCherry group (CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 0.40 ± 1.13 vs. 11.20 ± 4.63; P = 0.0017; n = 8; fig. 5, J and L). A significant increase in open-arm entries was observed in the CFA-hM4D group relative to the CFA-mCherry group (CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 0 ± 0 vs. 3.88 ± 1.36; P = 0.0004; n = 8; fig. 5, K and L). Compared with the CFA-mCherry group, the total distance in the OFT was unaltered in the CFA-hM4D group (CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 8,284 ± 2,153 mm vs. 9,165 ± 1,827 mm; P = 0.9766; n = 8; fig. 5N). The CFA-hM4D group showed increased exploration in the central area of the OFT relative to the CFA-mCherry group (time spent in center [%], CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 2.11 ± 0.68 vs. 7.90 ± 3.5; P = 0.0028; n = 8; distance in center [%], CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 5.01 ± 1.82 vs. 15.03 ± 4.58; P < 0.0001; n = 8; fig. 5, M, O, and P). In the light–dark box test, mice in the CFA-hM4D group spent more time in the light compartment relative to the CFA-mCherry group (CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 25.66 ± 2.51 vs. 42.67 ± 5.21; P = 0.0028; n = 8; fig. 5, Q and R). Moreover, the CFA-hM4D group showed a significant increase in the number of transitions compared to the CFA-mCherry group (CFA-mCherry vs. CFA-hM4D, 3.38 ± 3.42 vs. 16.75 ± 2.38; P < 0.0001; n = 8; fig. 5S). After CNO administration, no change in anxiety-like behaviors was observed in the saline-hM4D group compared to the saline-mCherry group (time in open arms [%], saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 13.07 ± 6.70 vs. 12.93 ± 6.36; n = 8; P > 0.99; entries in open arms, saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 4.75 ± 1.91 vs. 4.50 ± 2.33; n = 8; P = 0.0098; total distance, saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 10,240 ± 2,799 mm vs. 11,027 ± 2,617 mm; n = 8; P = 0.9868; time spent in center [%], saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 6.72 ± 1.05 vs. 6.25 ± 1.45; n = 8; P > 0.99; distance in center [%], saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 15.07 ± 3.07 vs. 13.43 ± 2.01; n = 8; P = 0.8759; time spent in light side [%], saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 43.93 ± 2.97 vs. 42.26 ± 2.36; n = 8; P = 0.5677; transitions, saline-mCherry vs. saline-hM4D, 13.75 ± 4.17 vs. 14.00 ± 3.78; n = 8; P = 0.9989).

Taken together, these results indicated that chemogenetic inhibition of the PPTgGlu–CM pathway alleviated nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors.

Furthermore, mCherry+ terminals were observed in the CM and the placement of the optical fiber (fig. 6E, right). To validate the effectiveness of the ChR2 virus, we performed whole cell recordings and found that PPTgGlu expressing ChR2 were successfully activated by optical stimulation (fig. 6F).

Optogenetic activation of the PPTgGlu–CM circuit was sufficient to elicit nociceptive behaviors. Mice in the ChR2 group exhibited a significantly decreased MPWT (mCherry vs. ChR2, 0.9 ± 0.19 g vs. 0.02 ± 0.01 g; P = 0.0002; n = 8; fig. 6G). The frequency of spontaneous nociceptive behaviors was increased in the ChR2 group relative to the mCherry group (mCherry vs. ChR2, 0.75 ± 1.04 vs. 19.13 ± 6.18; P = 0.0002; n = 8; fig. 6H). Compared with the mCherry group, the ChR2 group showed a significant decrease in response latency in both the hot plate test and the tail-flick test (hot plate, 11.28 ± 1.33 s vs. 7.83 ± 1.46 s; P = 0.0002; n = 8; tail-flick test, 1.83 ± 0.36 s vs. 1.04 ± 0.17 s; P < 0.0001; n = 8; fig. 6, I and J).

Moreover, the number of transitions was also significantly reduced in the ChR2 group compared to the mCherry group (mCherry vs. ChR2, 16.13 ± 3.68 vs. 10.63 ± 3.62; P = 0.014; n = 8; fig. 6T).

Taken together, these results indicated that the PPTgGlu–CM pathway participated in nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors.

Discussion

Epidemiologic studies have shown that 20 to 40% of adults with chronic pain also develop comorbid anxiety or depression.41 Clinical evidence also indicates a reciprocal relationship between pain and anxiety, whereby each can exacerbate the other.42 Research has revealed a small-to-moderate positive correlation between pain-related negative affect states and pain intensity.43 Therefore, elucidating the neural pathway responsible for both chronic pain and associated anxiety-like behaviors is crucial for developing novel analgesic drugs. In this study, we identified a glutamatergic circuit connecting the PPTg to the CM that regulates nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors in mice. We showed that selective inhibition of either PPTgGlu or this specific pathway effectively alleviated both nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors. These results suggest that this circuit is a promising therapeutic target for chronic pain and its comorbid affective disorders.

The PPTg has also been implicated in descending pain modulatory mechanisms.44 The nucleus is composed of glutamatergic, cholinergic, and GABAergic neurons.12,13 Previous studies have shown that the PPTg contains neurons that are excited by noxious stimulation. Furthermore, these neurons are in close morphological proximity to neurons that are positive for NADPH-diaphorase.45 Moreover, cholinergic neurons in the PPTg have also been proven to be involved in pain modulation.14,46 In this study, we demonstrate a critical role for the glutamatergic population in a model of chronic inflammatory pain. We found that in a mouse model of chronic inflammatory pain, c-Fos expression was significantly increased in the PPTg, and its immunoreactive signals were largely colocalized with glutamatergic neurons. The observed colocalization was largely extensive. This suggests that under conditions of persistent inflammatory pain, glutamatergic neurons constitute a substantial proportion of the activated PPTg neuronal population. Subsequently, using chemogenetic inhibition of PPTgGlu, we found that it alleviated mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, anxiety-like behaviors, and spontaneous nociceptive behaviors in CFA-hM4D mice compared to the CFA-mCherry controls. In contrast, the same inhibition produced no significant behavioral changes in saline-treated mice. The lack of effect in saline-treated animals demonstrates that the chemogenetic inhibition does not produce analgesia under normal conditions. Together, these findings indicate that PPTgGlu primarily modulate the nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors in persistent inflammatory pain. Optogenetic activation of PPTgGlu using the parameters described induced mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and anxiety-like behaviors, as well as an increase in the frequency of spontaneous nociceptive behaviors in mice. We bidirectional manipulated PPTgGlu and found that this bidirectionally modulated nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors. This provides direct causal evidence for their essential role. It should be emphasized that previous studies have established the PPTgGlu as a key node in motor regulation.34 Consequently, the glutamate injection approaches used in earlier work47 nonspecifically activate these neurons. This nonspecificity often leads to significant changes in general motor activity. This confounding factor is inherent in stimulation techniques. It is therefore challenging to specifically attribute the observed behavioral responses to PPTgGlu activity. In our experiments, by employing optogenetics with precise control over stimulation parameters, we minimized these nonspecific motor effects. This approach not only allowed us to selectively interrogate the role of PPTgGlu in nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors but also avoided the confound of directly induced locomotor changes.

The CM, a major node within the intralaminar nuclei, serves as a key hub for integrating emotional and nociceptive information. The CM is implicated in various chronic pain conditions. For instance, in neuropathic pain, it receives nociceptive inputs from the ventrolateral periaqueductal gray. It then modulates nociceptive behaviors via its glutamatergic projections to the basolateral amygdala.20 Similarly, in a model of inflammatory bowel disease, enhanced functional connectivity between the CM and the anterior cingulate cortex correlates with visceral hyperalgesia.48 Furthermore, the CM is also involved in emotion-like behaviors. Its outputs to the medial prefrontal cortex specifically regulate the aversive affective reactions induced by chronic itch.17 Several previous studies have indicated that the CM receives inputs from many brain regions.16,49 Whether the CM receives projections from PPTgGlu remains unknown. To investigate whether the PPTgGlu project to the CMGlu, we employed anterograde and retrograde tracing strategies to delineate the downstream connections of the neurons. We found that PPTgGlu directly project to glutamatergic neurons in the CM. To determine whether the PPTgGlu–CMGlu neuronal circuit is functionally connected, we used optogenetics combined with patch clamp recording. We found that optogenetic stimulation reliably evoked EPSCs in CMGlu. This finding confirms the existence of a functional connection in this circuit, which was the primary objective of this study. A more detailed characterization of this connection, including confirmation of its monosynaptic nature and analysis of its presynaptic properties, constitutes an important goal for future studies. Having confirmed the existence of this functional monosynaptic circuit, we then investigated its relevance to chronic pain. We found that in a model of chronic inflammatory pain, glutamatergic neurons in the CM exhibit significantly elevated activation. A substantial proportion of this activation occurred specifically within the glutamatergic neuronal population. To directly interrogate the role of this circuit in chronic pain and comorbid anxiety-like behaviors, we employed both chemogenetic inhibition and optogenetic activation approaches. Using a projection-specific chemogenetic approach, we inhibited PPTgGlu that project to the CM. This inhibition significantly alleviated nociceptive behaviors (including mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and spontaneous nociceptive behaviors) and anxiety-like behaviors in the CFA-hM4D group. In contrast, the same inhibition produced no significant behavioral changes in saline-treated mice. Conversely, optogenetic activation of this circuit was sufficient to induce mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and anxiety-like behaviors, as well as an increase in the frequency of spontaneous nociceptive behaviors in mice.

It is important to consider our behavioral data in detail. In the light–dark box test, the number of light–dark transitions varied across experimental cohorts (fig. 1O, fig. 2S, fig. 3T, fig. 5S, and fig. 6T). Importantly, this variability in transitions did not impact the primary anxiolytic indicator (light compartment time), which was significantly increased or decreased in all relevant experiments. The variation in total distance traveled across experiments (fig. 1J, fig. 2N, fig. 3O, fig. 5N, and fig. 6O) arose from the use of different animal cohorts. Importantly, locomotor activity did not differ between experimental and control groups within any experiment. Therefore, center zone activity (time and distance) can be directly compared between groups. A potential limitation in interpreting our chemogenetic data is the off-target effects of CNO. We addressed this by including control animals expressing mCherry alone in parallel with all hM4D-expressing experimental groups. These mCherry-controls received the same CNO administration protocol. Importantly, in these mCherry-only control mice, CNO administration did not produce the behavioral effects observed in hM4D-expressing mice. This result strongly suggests that the behavioral effects we observed were mediated by the specific activation of hM4D rather than by off-target effects of CNO. Furthermore, we have considered the potential issue of CNO-induced functional desensitization. First, the behavioral effects (alleviation of nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors) remained stable throughout the 6-day CNO administration, showing no attenuation. Critically, after chronic CNO administration, we performed patch clamp recordings on day 27. These recordings demonstrated that an acute application of CNO could still rapidly suppress spontaneous activity in hM4D-expressing neurons (fig. 2E and fig. 5E). This confirms that the Designer Receptors Exclusively Activated by Designer Drugs system retained full functionality after prolonged CNO treatment, arguing against significant functional desensitization.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting our findings, and these limitations also outline important directions for future research. Our study focused on establishing the circuit’s behavioral role but did not define the underlying synaptic mechanisms. Future work should examine this circuit-selective neuroplasticity. Such studies will be critical to firmly link synaptic adaptation to the emergence of comorbid anxiety. Second, previous anatomical observations initially supported the prevailing view that cholinergic neurons in the PPTg broadly corelease acetylcholine with either glutamate or γ-aminobutyric acid.50,51 However, this notion has been challenged by emerging evidence from more specific molecular techniques. Recent studies demonstrate that the vast majority of PPTg cholinergic neurons do not express the transcripts essential for glutamate transport or γ-aminobutyric acid synthesis.13 Therefore, cholinergic cotransmission in the PPTg appears to be far less common than previously hypothesized. Third, high-frequency stimulation might induce antidromic activation. Whether our photo stimulation of the CM at 10 Hz causes retrograde depolarization of PPTg cell bodies remains a critical question for future exploration. Fourth, although we established a functional monosynaptic PPTgGlu–CMGlu projection, we did not directly manipulate CMGlu (e.g., inhibition or activation). Our experimental design was primarily focused on dissecting the circuit from the PPTgGlu to the CM. However, our finding that CMGlu exhibit increased c-Fos expression in the comorbid pain–anxiety state. This indicates their specific activation and supports their role as a key component within the PPTgGlu–CMGlu circuit. Future studies using cell type–specific manipulations of CMGlu will be crucial to define their precise role in mediating this PPTg-derived input and the associated pain and anxiety behaviors. Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge that the PPTgGlu we investigated likely have multiple downstream targets beyond the CM. Chronic pain and anxiety are complex behaviors regulated by distributed neural networks, and the PPTgGlu–CM pathway we have characterized may operate in parallel with other circuits. Our optogenetic activation and chemogenetic inhibition experiments demonstrate that manipulating this specific pathway is sufficient to bidirectionally modulate nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors in our model. Thus, our data establish a causal and functional role for the PPTgGlu–CM pathway. However, the specific contributions of other efferent projections (e.g., to the ventral tegmental area [VTA]) remain to be determined.10 In this regard, the VTA is a key node of the mesolimbic dopamine pathway, paramount for reward, motivation, and affect.52,53 It remains unclear whether PPTgGlu modulate nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors through direct innervation of particular VTA cellular targets or through modulatory effects on VTA microcircuits. Finally, a key limitation is the exclusive use of male mice. Sex differences in pain and affective comorbidities are well-documented. Sex hormones have also been recognized to play central roles in murine pain processing. Female mice also exhibit prolonged recovery in neuropathic pain models. This could be driven by estrogen metabolites promoting sensory neuron excitability. In males, testosterone has critical effects on pain circuitry.54,55 A recent study illustrated that testosterone induces pain hypersensitivity via androgen receptor activation in glutamatergic neurons of the medial preoptic area that, in turn, excite ventrolateral periaqueductal gray GABAergic neurons. This was not found in females, suggesting complex and sexually dimorphic hormonal mechanisms of pain regulation.56 Sexual variation in hormonal profiles and neural circuit organization may underlie differences in anxiety-related behaviors.57,58 Notably, corticotropin releasing factor receptor type 1 (CRFR1)-induced hyperexcitability of corticotropin-releasing factor neurons in the oval bed nucleus of the stria terminalis underlies female-specific vulnerability to anxiety states.59 Overall, these findings indicate that specific studies must be performed to elucidate the functional relevance of the PPTgGlu–CM pathway in female mice.

In this study, using a CFA-induced mouse model, we reveal that the PPTgGlu–CM circuit plays an essential role in the comorbidity of chronic pain and anxiety. Our findings identify a potential neural mechanism that may explain why anxiety disorders frequently co-occur with chronic pain. In a rat model of inflammatory bowel disease, positron emission tomography imaging revealed that visceral-noxious stimulation increases activity in the CM and enhances its functional connectivity with the anterior cingulate cortex.48 This provides strong evidence that thalamocortical sensitization, particularly within the CM–anterior cingulate cortex pathway, may underlie the development of visceral hypersensitivity. Clinical evidence also indicates that deep brain stimulation of the PPTg can alleviate both pain and motor symptoms.60 In conclusion, our work, together with clinical evidence, identifies the PPTgGlu–CM circuit and its key nodes as novel therapeutic targets. Furthermore, transcranial magnetic stimulation and deep brain stimulation have shown efficacy by targeting specific brain circuits in other disorders.61 For instance, research has shown that bilateral deep brain stimulation of the lateral habenula results in remission in patients with depression.62 Similarly, transcranial magnetic stimulation of the prefrontal cortex reduces cocaine use in subjects with cocaine use disorder.63 We have identified the PPTgGlu–CM circuit as a key regulator of pain–affect integration. This suggests that it could serve as a novel target for therapeutic interventions. If confirmed in humans, assessing activity in the PPTgGlu–CM neural circuit could aid in identifying subtypes of chronic pain patients at greater risk for anxiety, enabling tailored therapy.

In summary, our study demonstrates that the PPTgGlu–CM neural circuit plays an important role in chronic pain accompanied by anxiety. In chronic pain, enhanced excitability of the glutamatergic neurons in the PPTg evokes neuronal activation in the CM region, thereby promoting both nociceptive and anxiety-like behaviors.
