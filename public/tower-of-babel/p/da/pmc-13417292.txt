Programming Structural Flexibility for Tunable Gas Sorption via Hydrogen Bonding in Polyoxometalate-Based Porous Ionic Crystals
Authors: Shunsuke Mochizuki, Haru Hirai, Tomochika Kasagiri, Zhewei Weng, Masataka Ohtani, Sayaka Uchida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Porous ionic crystals (PICs) are porous molecular solids assembled through nondirectional Coulomb interactions, whose inherent structural flexibility during guest sorption and desorption creates adaptive pore environments that lead to unconventional gas sorption and separation selectivity. Although such flexibility is expected to be reflected in both thermodynamics and kinetic aspects of guest sorption, these two viewpoints have rarely been examined together. Herein, we apply a crystal engineering approach based on noncovalent intermolecular interactions to systematically tune the flexibility of PICs constructed from triol-ligand-modified (RC­(CH2OH)3, R = CH3, CH2OH, NH2) Anderson-type polyoxometalate (POM) anions as molecular building blocks. The number of hydrogen bonds formed per POM with adjacent ionic components is precisely controlled, ranging from zero for PIC-CH 3 to two for PIC-NH 2 , thereby enabling systematic control of structural flexibility. Despite their similar porosities, PIC-CH 3 and PIC-NH 2 exhibit distinct guest sorption behaviors. Thermodynamic analysis of guest (CO2 and CH3OH) sorption reveals that PIC-NH 2 displays a type-I isotherm characteristic of a rigid microporous framework dominated by physisorption, while PIC-CH 3 shows a continuous increase in uptake with pressure, indicating guest-induced structural transformation. Complementary kinetic analyses further reveal this contrast: PIC-NH 2 follows a single-step sorption process, while PIC-CH 3 exhibits multistep kinetics involving both guest diffusion and framework expansion. By combining thermodynamic and kinetic perspectives, we establish a hydrogen-bonding-guided crystal engineering strategy for programming structural flexibility to achieve tunable gas sorption in POM-based PICs.

Introduction

Porous crystalline compounds such as zeolites and metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), constructed by directional covalent or coordination bonds, have been extensively studied due to their well-defined porous structures and high surface areas, which are advantageous for gas sorption/separation, ion exchange/conduction, and heterogeneous catalysis. − In contrast, porous molecular crystals represent another important class of porous materials. These systems, which have been intensively investigated by Cooper, are composed of discrete molecular units assembled through noncovalent intermolecular interactions, such as hydrogen bonding, − π-π stacking, , van der Waals forces, − and Coulomb interactions. − ,,

Crystal engineering approaches, originally proposed by Zaworotko, utilize such noncovalent intermolecular interactions to enable the rational design of structures and functionalities. A seminal contribution in this field was reported by Ward, who synthesized an organic framework constructed from guanidinium cations and organosulfonate anions, in which the anions serve as molecular pillars supporting the cavities between complementary hydrogen-bonded sheets. Building on these concepts, Tohnai and co-workers reported porous organic salts assembled through Coulomb interactions between tetrasulfonate anions and halogen-substituted triphenylmethylamine cations, in which the pore environments can be tuned by halogen substitution, leading to enhanced CO2 adsorption. In a related direction, Konno and co-workers demonstrated that organometallic ionic components can assemble via Coulomb interactions and hydrogen bonds to form three-dimensional networks with open channels capable of transporting hydrated alkali metal ions.

We have been focusing on porous ionic crystals (PICs), which assemble primarily through Coulomb interactions between oppositely charged ionic components. , In particular, PICs based on polyoxometalate (POM) anions, − which are structurally well-defined metal-oxide cluster anions with various structures (e.g., Keggin-type [XM12O40] n−, Dawson-type [X2M18O62] n−, and Anderson-type [XM6O24] n−), offer diverse functionalities and exhibit structural flexibility arising from long-range isotropic Coulomb interactions, enabling adaptive responses to guest species. For example, K2[Cr3O­(OOCH)6(4-ethylpyridine)3]2[SiW12O40] selectively sorbs CO2 over acetylene due to framework expansion induced by interactions between CO2 and K+ and/or surface oxides of the POM. Beyond our work, Cronin and co-workers reported that Li9K7W1Co10[H2P8W48O186] exhibits large and reversible unit cell volume changes, ranging from −2170 to +1720 Å3, upon water, methanol, or ammonia sorption–desorption without loss of crystallinity. Despite these intriguing examples, the development of flexible POM-based PICs exhibiting unique guest sorption properties has largely relied on empirical knowledge or serendipitous discovery, highlighting the need for systematic design principles.

The above crystal engineering approaches led us to explore anisotropic intermolecular interactions, beyond Coulomb interactions, to achieve programmable control over the flexibility of PICs. To adjust anisotropic interactions between ionic components in PICs, designability of the surface of POMs is required. Thus, among POMs, we chose Anderson-type POMs as a platform, which consist of six edge-sharing [MO6] (M = W, Mo, V) units surrounding a heteroatom X and forming a planar structure with approximate D 3d symmetry. , Anderson-type POMs have been widely employed as building blocks in solid-state materials − not only because of their compact and planar shape, which differs from the spherical Keggin- and Dawson-types, but also because of the structural and functional diversity by organic functionalization. Covalent modification using tris­(hydroxymethyl)­methane-based triol ligands (RC­(CH2OH)3) allows capping of the POM core in δ- or χ-isomeric forms, depending on the coordination mode to μ3- and μ2-O atoms. − Recently, Li, Wu, and co-workers developed a series of POM-based ionic frameworks constructed from triol-modified Anderson-type POMs, in which Na+ cations act as linkers. The resulting porous ionic framework possessed rectangular openings derived from the planar POM geometry, with pore sizes tunable via the triol ligands, displaying selective CO2 uptake from N2, H2 or CH4 mixtures at room temperature. This example highlights the unique potential of functionalized Anderson-type POMs and motivates further systematic studies of this class of materials.

Based on these considerations, we systematically modulated hydrogen bonding in PICs to program their structural flexibility by varying the terminal functional groups of triol-modified Anderson-type POMs and designed a series of PICs (Figure ) composed of triol-modified anionic δ-isomers [MnMo6O18{(OCH2)3CR}2]3– (R = CH3, CH2OH, NH2) and the cationic complex [Cr3O­(OOCH)6(4-methylpyridine)3]+ (Cr-mepy), giving crystalline assemblies [Cr-mepy]3[MnMo6O18{(OCH2)3CCH3}2] (PIC-CH 3 ), [Cr-mepy]3[MnMo6O18{(OCH2)3CCH2OH}2] (PIC-CH 2 OH), and (mepyH)­[Cr-mepy]2[MnMo6O18{(OCH2)3CNH2}2] (PIC-NH 2 ), (mepyH = 4-methylpyridinium ion). Thermodynamic and kinetic analyses of guest sorption in these crystalline assemblies demonstrate that hydrogen bonding governs the structural flexibility of POM-based PICs and thereby controls their gas sorption behavior.

Results and Discussion

Classification of PIC Structures Based on Hydrogen-Bonding Ability of Triol Ligands

The PICs were synthesized by combining a CH3OH solution of each tetra-n-butylammonium (TBA) salt of the triol-modified Anderson-type POM with an acetonitrile solution of the perchlorate salt of Cr-mepy. The resulting solutions were left at room temperature for 1–3 days, allowing the PICs to crystallize (see experimental section for details). Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of the PICs showed characteristic peaks of the POMs and Cr-mepy (Figure S1). Elemental analyses using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES), CHN combustion analysis, and thermogravimetry (TG, Figure S2) confirmed the proposed compositions. Although CH3OH and acetonitrile were used as crystallization solvents, their volatility allows them to readily escape from the pores and are probably replaced by water upon exposure to ambient conditions.

The crystal structure of PIC-CH 3 is shown in Figure (see Table S1 for the crystallographic data). The negative charge of the POM (−3) is fully compensated by three Cr-mepy cations. PIC-CH 3 features a column of POMs with an inter-POM distance of 5.83 Å, indicating the absence of specific interactions between adjacent POM units (Figure a, left). Another column is formed by Cr-mepy cations, where adjacent mepy ligands are aligned parallel to each other, exhibiting π-π stacking (Figure a, right). These two types of columns assemble to form a porous structure. Void analysis reveals that PIC-CH 3 contains continuous one-dimensional channels, running along the a-axis with an opening of ca. 6 Å × 9 Å. The pore volume is 554 Å3 (Z = 1), accounting for 17% of the unit-cell volume (Figure b,c). The powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) pattern of PIC-CH 3 well agreed with that calculated from the crystallographic data, confirming successful bulk synthesis (Figure S3a).

The crystal structure of PIC-CH 2 OH is shown in Figure (see Table S2 for the crystallographic data). As in PIC-CH 3 , the negative charge of the POM (−3) is fully compensated by three Cr-mepy cations. PIC-CH 2 OH features a column formed by Cr-mepy cations, where the mepy ligands of adjacent Cr-mepy cations are aligned parallel to each other, exhibiting π-π stacking (Figure a, left). Another column is formed by POMs, with the distance between adjacent POMs (OPOM−OCH2OH) being approximately 2.98 Å, indicating the presence of hydrogen bonding (Figure a, right). This contrasts with the crystal structure of PIC-CH 3 , where no specific interactions between POMs were observed, highlighting the difference in hydrogen-bonding ability between the terminal functional groups (−CH3 vs −CH2OH) of the triol ligand. Void analysis indicates that PIC-CH 2 OH possesses large continuous channels running along the a-axis. These channels exhibit a bottleneck structure with minimum and maximum pore openings of ca. 6 Å and 17 Å, respectively, and a pore volume accounting for 25% of the crystal lattice (Figure b). The PXRD pattern of PIC-CH 2 OH showed reasonable agreement with the calculated pattern. However, the relative intensities the reflections of (010), (01–1), and (012) planes were weak, which can be attributed to the desorption of guest solvent molecules from the pores, reducing their contribution to the structure factor and/or to increased local structural disorder induced by solvent loss (Figures S3b, S4).

The crystal structure of PIC-NH 2 is shown in Figure (see Table S3 for the crystallographic data). Single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SXRD) analysis shows that the negative charge of the POM (−3) is compensated by only two Cr-mepy cations indicating that one cation is missing. TBA or mepyH, which may form by the partial decomposition of Cr-mepy, are potential candidates for the missing cation. The FT-IR spectrum (Figure S1c) shows no peaks attributable to TBA, and the small pore openings of PIC-NH 2 (see below) suggests that mepyH likely serves as the countercation. PIC-NH 2 consists of alternate layers of Cr-mepy and POM (Figure a). In the Cr-mepy layer, the mepy ligands of adjacent macrocations are aligned parallel to each other, showing π-π stacking. In the POM layer, the –NH2 group is within hydrogen-bonding distances with both the oxygen atoms of adjacent POM (N–OPOM = 2.84 Å) and Cr-mepy (N–OOOCH = 2.98 Å) (Figure b). As expected, two hydrogen atoms of the –NH2 group can participate in hydrogen bonding, and the multiple hydrogen bonds between the ionic components likely stabilize the crystal structure. Void analysis indicates that PIC-NH 2 contains zigzag channels running within the bc-plane, with minimum and maximum pore openings of ca. 3.3 Å and ca. 5.8 Å, respectively. The pore volume is 524 Å3 (Z = 1), which is comparable to that of PIC-CH 3 (554 Å3) and accounts for 21% of the unit cell volume (Figure c). The PXRD pattern of PIC-NH 2 shows good agreement with the calculated pattern, confirming successful bulk synthesis (Figure S3c).

Guest Sorption Thermodynamics and Structural Flexibility of PICs

Then, the guest sorption isotherms of two PICs, PIC-CH 3 and PIC-NH 2 , which have no and multiple hydrogen bonds between the ionic components, respectively, were investigated. These PICs exhibit similar void volumes (Z = 1) of 554 Å3 (PIC-CH 3 ) and 524 Å3 (PIC-NH 2 ) and serve as suitable models for understanding the effect of structural features, particularly hydrogen bonding, on guest sorption properties. The guest sorption behavior of PIC-CH 2 OH was not considered due to its tendency to form polymorphs that are difficult to remove.

The gas (N2 and CO2) sorption–desorption isotherms of PIC-CH 3 and PIC-NH 2 are shown in Figure a. Neither PIC exhibited N2 sorption (kinetic diameter: 3.6 Å, quadrupole moment: 5.11 × 10–40 C·m2), while CO2 (kinetic diameter: 3.3 Å, quadrupole moment: 1.43 × 10–39 C·m2) was sorbed. This behavior can be attributed to the smaller kinetic diameter and larger quadrupole moment of CO2, as well as to the presence of intrinsic electrostatic fields within the pores, which facilitate CO2 sorption. The CO2 sorption isotherm of PIC-NH 2 showed that the compound sorbed 2–3 mol·mol–1 (i.e., 2–3 mol of CO2 per mole of PIC-NH 2 ) at low pressure and reached 4 mol·mol–1 at higher pressures. The desorption branch closely overlapped with the sorption branch, indicating microporous behavior. This result is consistent with the microporous structure of PIC-NH 2 , which features zigzag channels within the bc-plane, with pore openings ranging from 3.3 Å to 5.8 Å. The CO2 adsorption isotherm of PIC-CH 3 exhibited a similar shape to that of PIC-NH 2 ; however, the uptake was smaller (3 mol·mol–1) and the desorption branch was positioned above the sorption branch, showing a hysteresis.

Solid and open symbols indicate the sorption branch and desorption branch, respectively. (b) CH3OH (298 K) sorption–desorption isotherms of PIC-CH 3 (red) and PIC-NH 2 (blue). Solid and open symbols indicate the sorption branch and desorption branch, respectively.

Figure b shows the CH3OH vapor sorption–desorption isotherms of PIC-CH 3 and PIC-NH 2 . While the CO2 sorption isotherms of the two PICs were similar, their CH3OH sorption isotherms differed significantly. Specifically, PIC-NH 2 exhibited a type-I isotherm, sorbing 3–4 mol·mol–1 of CH3OH and reaching saturation at very low relative pressure. The desorption branch closely overlapped with the sorption branch, showing no hysteresis, which is consistent with the CO2 sorption behavior (Figure a). In contrast, PIC-CH 3 showed a continuous increase in CH3OH uptake with increasing relative pressure, reaching approximately 9–10 mol·mol–1 at saturated vapor pressure. The three consecutive CH3OH vapor sorption–desorption measurements for both PIC-CH 3 and PIC-NH 2 were highly reproducible (Figure S6), confirming their structural stability under the measurement conditions. Unlike CO2, CH3OH is a polar molecule, while its size (3.6 Å) is comparable to that of CO2 (3.3 Å). Therefore, CO2 and CH3OH uptake is expected to be similar at high relative pressures, as indeed observed for PIC-NH 2 . However, in PIC-CH 3 , CH3OH uptake continued to increase with pressure, eventually reaching nearly three times the amount of CO2. A comparison of CH3OH uptake by representative porous crystalline materials is summarized in Table S4.

To investigate the possible structural changes during the sorption process, in situ PXRD measurements were performed at room temperature under vacuum, followed by exposure to a CH3OH vapor flow (Figure ) or a CO2 flow (Figure S7). As shown in Figure a, upon evacuation, the diffraction peaks of PIC-CH 3 shifted to higher angles, indicating contraction of the crystal lattice. For example, the as-synthesized PIC-CH 3 exhibited diffraction peaks at 5.00° and 5.33°, corresponding to (002) and (020) planes, respectively (Figure a). Upon evacuation, these peaks shifted to 5.23° and 5.53°, indicating that the c- and b-axes lengths were shortened. After exposure to CH3OH vapor (Figure a), the original diffraction pattern was recovered; the reflections of (002) and (020) planes appeared at 4.97° and 5.31°, respectively. Upon exposure to CO2 after evacuation, the diffraction peaks recovered to the positions characteristic of the as-synthesized state (Figure S7a), as observed upon exposure to CH3OH vapor.

For further analysis, the lattice parameters under each condition were estimated by fitting the diffraction patterns by the Pawley method. The lattice parameters of the as-synthesized PIC-CH 3 (Table S5) were comparable to those obtained by SXRD analysis (Table S1), confirming the reliability of the fitting procedure. Upon evacuation, the a-axis length of PIC-CH 3 remained unchanged (11.32 to 11.37 Å), while the b- and c-axes contracted from 32.80 to 31.90 Å and from 35.27 to 33.89 Å, respectively, indicating shrinkage of the pore aperture. The cell volume of as-synthesized PIC-CH 3 decreased by 5.7% upon evacuation and returned to its original value after exposure to CH3OH vapor. The same behavior was observed in the in situ PXRD measurement under CO2 flow; the cell volume decreased by 5.7% upon evacuation and returned to its original value after exposure to CO2. These reversible lattice contractions and expansions upon guest desorption and sorption are frequently observed in porous molecular crystals assembled primarily through nondirectional Coulomb interactions. ,, Although the CO2 uptake was smaller than the CH3OH uptake, the in situ PXRD results suggest that exposure to CO2 was sufficient to trigger recovery of the contracted lattice to the as-synthesized state.

For comparison, in situ PXRD measurements were also conducted for PIC-NH 2 as a reference (Figures b and S7b). In this case, the PXRD patterns showed only slight changes under different atmospheres (air, vacuum, CH3OH vapor, and CO2), and the lattice parameters estimated by the Pawley method indicated negligible structure change (Table S6). Based on the gas and vapor sorption results, PIC-NH 2 with multiple hydrogen bonds between the ionic components was found be rigid, while PIC-CH 3 with no hydrogen bonds exhibited a flexible structure. Therefore, we successfully demonstrated that structural flexibility in PICs can be effectively controlled by the presence or absence of hydrogen bonds to connect the ionic components.

The void volumes of PIC-CH 3 and PIC-NH 2 were 554 and 524 Å3 (Z = 1), respectively. Assuming a molecular volume of 66.7 Å3 for CH3OH, calculated from the molecular weight and the density of liquid at 298 K, the maximum uptakes are estimated to be 8.31 mol·mol–1 for PIC-CH 3 and 7.86 mol·mol–1 for PIC-NH 2 . For PIC-CH 3 , the estimated value is comparable to the experimentally observed uptake (9.16 mol·mol–1), suggesting that the flexible pore can adapt its structure to accommodate guest molecules efficiently. In contrast, PIC-NH 2 shows a significantly lower experimental uptake (3.58 mol·mol–1) than the estimated maximum. This discrepancy indicates that the void space is not effectively occupied during sorption. The rigid nature of PIC-NH 2 likely prevents the framework from adapting to the size and shape of CH3OH molecules.

To gain further insight into the thermodynamics of guest sorption, the CO2 sorption enthalpy of PIC-NH 2 , which does not undergo structural change, was estimated using a Clausius–Clapeyron plot derived from CO2 sorption isotherms measured at different temperatures (195, 205, 215, and 225 K) (Figure S8). The resulting sorption enthalpies (in absolute values) at 1, 2, and 3 mol·mol–1 were 26.7, 23.5, and 15.3 kJ·mol–1, respectively. Given that the condensation enthalpy of CO2 is 16 kJ·mol–1, and that alkali metal and alkaline earth metal ion-exchanged zeolites (M-BEA), which are commonly used as CO2 adsorbents, exhibit sorption enthalpies exceeding 50 kJ·mol–1 at low loading and remain above 20 kJ·mol–1 even at higher loading, , PIC-NH 2 exhibits relatively weak, physisorption-like CO2 adsorption. For comparison, CO2 sorption isotherms of PIC-CH 3 were also measured at different temperatures. However, the CO2 uptake did not follow the usual trend of decreasing adsorption with increasing temperature, and the corresponding Clausius–Clapeyron plot did not show linear behavior. This observation suggests that the CO2 sorption behavior of PIC-CH 3 is affected by structural changes of the porous framework, consistent with the in situ PXRD results shown in Figure S7a.

Molecular mechanics (MM) calculations based on Monte Carlo simulations were performed to optimize the positions of guest molecules in the PICs. Figure shows the typical optimized geometries of CO2 and CH3OH in the PICs. The guest molecules occupied the one-dimensional channels in PIC-CH 3 and the zigzag channels in PIC-NH 2 , respectively. For PIC-NH 2 , the distance between the carbon of CO2 and the oxygen of the POM is longer than 3.3 Å, indicating the absence of strong interaction. This observation aligns with the heat of sorption (15.3–26.7 kJ·mol–1, Figure S8) being comparable to the heat of condensation (16 kJ·mol–1).

Guest Sorption Kinetics and Structural Flexibility of PICs

In the previous section, the thermodynamics of gas and vapor sorption were discussed on the basis of sorption isotherms. To elucidate the kinetics of guest sorption, the time-dependent sorption behavior was investigated. Time courses for CH3OH and CO2 sorption in PIC-NH 2 were measured gravimetrically at a constant pressure (CO2 at 1 atm and CH3OH under a flow of 1 atm of N2 gas bubbled through liquid CH3OH) and 303 K to investigate the sorption kinetics (Figure ). The sorption amounts increased and nearly leveled off after approximately 20 s for CO2 (Figure a) and 50 s for CH3OH (Figure b). The shorter time required for CO2 to reach sorption equilibrium is probably due to its nonpolarity, which results in weaker host–guest interactions compared with those of CH3OH, facilitating faster and uniform diffusion of CO2 within the crystal lattice.

Time courses of (a) CO2 and (b) CH3OH sorption in PIC-NH 2 at 303 K. ln­(1 – M t/M e) versus t plots for (c) (a) and (d) (b). Black lines correspond to fitting results.

The simplest kinetic model for gas sorption is the pseudo-first-order (PFO) model, which assumes that the sorption rate is proportional to the difference between the equilibrium uptake and the amount sorbed at a given time t. The rate equation is given by 1 dMt/dt=k(Me−Mt) where M t is the uptake at time t, M e is the equilibrium uptake, and k is the rate constant. Integration of eq yields 2 Mt/Me={1−exp(−kt)} and a plot of ln­(1 – M t/M e) versus t is linear with a slope of −k. The experimental data for PIC-NH 2 were fairly reproduced with k = 9.10 × 10–2 s–1 for CO2 (Figure c, R 2 = 0.995) and k = 1.79 × 10–2 s–1 for CH3OH (Figure d, R 2 = 0.990).

Next, the time courses for CO2 and CH3OH sorption in PIC-CH 3 were measured and analyzed (Figure ). In both cases, rapid sorption was observed initially, followed by a gradual increase. The sorption profiles of PIC-CH 3 clearly differ from those of PIC-NH 2 , and cannot be described by the PFO model. Instead, we implemented the model commonly referred to as the double exponential model, which indicates that the sorption involves two independent rate-determining steps 3 Mt/Me=Me1{1−exp(−k1t)}+Me2{1−exp(−k2t)} This model has been used for alcohol sorption in flexible Ni-bipyridine MOF. For CO2 sorption in PIC-CH 3 , the fitted parameters were M e1 = 4.68 × 10–1 mol·mol–1 and k 1 = 6.87 × 10–2 s–1, M e2 = 6.58 × 10–2 mol·mol–1 and k 2 = 2.33 × 10–3 s–1. For CH3OH sorption in PIC-CH 3 , the fitted parameters were M e1 = 3.30 mol·mol–1 and k 1 = 3.91 × 10–2 s–1, M e2 = 6.94 × 10–1 mol·mol–1 and k 2 = 1.64 × 10–3 s–1. Among the two sorption processes characterized by the rate constants k 1 and k 2, the faster process (k 1) can be attributed to diffusion of guest molecules within the pores, while the slower process (k 2) is associated with framework expansion induced by guest sorption.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we achieved programmable control over the structural flexibility of porous ionic crystals (PICs) based on triol-ligand-modified Anderson-type POMs through a crystal engineering approach. The number of hydrogen bonds formed per POM with adjacent ionic components depended on the terminal group (R) of the triol ligand: 0 for R = CH3 (PIC-CH 3 ), 1 for R = CH2OH (PIC-CH 2 OH), and 2 for R = NH2 (PIC-NH 2 ). Combined thermodynamic and kinetic analyses of guest sorption revealed that PIC-NH 2 behaves as a structurally rigid framework dominated by physisorption, while PIC-CH 3 undergoes guest-induced structural changes upon guest sorption and desorption.

As our future work, we aim to expand the diversity of PICs by incorporating multiple functional groups into triol ligandsone for structural control and another for specific functionality. For instance, introducing a diamine could enable both hydrogen-bonding to maintain structural rigidity and provide chemisorption sites for CO2. Additionally, triol-ligand-modified Anderson-type POMs are known to undergo isomerization via redox of the central metal ion, protonation, or coordination with electron-withdrawing ligands. By externally triggering these transformations, we can induce structural changes that lead to novel functionality. Developing such responsive systems will be the core focus of our future work.
