Hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial transplantation multidirectionally modulates Schwann cell functions to repair peripheral nerve injury in rats
Authors: Jin Dong, Bing Yan, Xushen Zhao, Jinyu Bai, Huajian Shan, Xiang Gao, Lei Sheng, Jun Dai, Fengxian Jiang, Mingchao Zhang, Chaowen Bai, Xiaozhong Zhou, Shuai Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeripheral nerve injury (PNI) often leads to sensory and motor dysfunction. This study investigated the promoting effect of hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial transplantation on the structural and functional reconstruction of peripheral nerves and the molecular mechanisms involved.MethodsWe explored the effects of hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial transplantation on Schwann cell (SC) phenotypes, including proliferation, migration, cellular senescence, and mitochondrial membrane potential, and explored the underlying molecular mechanism through Western blotting. We assessed axonal and myelin regeneration, as well as motor function recovery, in injured rats through behavioral tests, morphological analysis, and electrophysiological detection.ResultsHypoxia preconditioning significantly increased the mitochondrial membrane potential in SCs without altering their ultrastructure or the normal expression of the COX IV and TOMM20 proteins. The uptake efficiency of SCs for exogenous mitochondria was significantly greater than that of neurons, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and other cells. Hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial transplantation activated the MAPK (Ras-ERK-c-Fos/c-Jun) pathway; promoted SC proliferation, migration, and dedifferentiation; inhibited H2O2-induced cellular senescence; and partially restored the mitochondrial membrane potential. Hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial transplantation significantly accelerated axonal growth, promoted axonal remyelination, led to significant recovery of electrophysiological function, and improved motor function.ConclusionHypoxia preconditioning enhances the mitochondrial membrane potential of SC-derived mitochondria. Mitochondrial transplantation modulates SC functions by activating the Ras-ERK-c-Fos/c-Jun pathway, significantly increases nerve fiber density and myelin thickness, alleviates gastrocnemius muscle atrophy, and promotes motor function recovery, providing a new strategy for the clinical treatment of PNI.Translational potentialWe established a hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial transplantation method that modulates SC functions, accelerates axonal and myelin regeneration, and promotes motor function recovery. This study provides a novel clinical strategy for treating peripheral nervous system injuries.Keywords: Dedifferentiation, Hypoxia, Mitochondrial transplantation, Nerve regeneration, Peripheral nerve injury, Schwann cell

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injury (PNI) is a common traumatic clinical condition that often results in motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunction [1,2]. Epidemiological studies indicate that traumatic nerve injuries account for approximately 2.8% of all trauma cases, with primary injury mechanisms including compression (30–40%), transection, and stretching [3]. Peripheral nerves possess a unique hierarchical three-dimensional architecture. This complex layered structure makes nerve repair challenging, especially when multiple layers are damaged simultaneously, often leading to failure of axonal regeneration and target organ denervation, ultimately causing irreversible functional impairment [4]. Therefore, developing new therapies to promote nerve repair has considerable clinical significance. For example, using bionic scaffolds combined with bioactive molecules or nanomaterials to mimic the natural extracellular matrix and synergistically regulate the regenerative microenvironment to guide the growth, migration, and differentiation of nerve cells [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]].

Schwann cells (SCs), which are specific glial cells of the peripheral nervous system, play crucial roles in nerve development, functional maintenance, and injury repair [10,11]. Following PNI, SCs immediately initiate a sophisticated repair program, with molecular mechanisms involving multilevel coordinated operation [11,12]. The reversal of mature SCs to an immature state, termed SC dedifferentiation, is a key step in nerve regeneration post-PNI. SC dedifferentiation primarily functions to promote nerve regeneration, clear myelin debris, and secrete neurotrophic factors, with key targets including Jun, YAP-TAZ, and β-catenin [13,14]. At the gene expression level, SCs in the distal nerve stump rapidly undergo phenotypic switching—downregulating myelin-associated genes and peripheral myelin protein 22 (PMP22) [[15], [16], [17]], while upregulating repair-associated markers such as glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and neurotrophin receptor p75NTR [18,19]. This reprogramming process is precisely regulated by the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and Notch signaling pathways [20]. Dedifferentiated SCs subsequently enter a proliferation phase, aligning in an orderly arrangement along the basal lamina to form Büngner bands, providing physical guiding channels for regenerating axons. Concurrently, SCs actively remodel the injury microenvironment by secreting matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [21], thereby degrading extracellular matrix components that hinder regeneration [22]. With respect to chemical guidance, SCs establish a comprehensive neurotrophic factor secretion system that includes brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), nerve growth factor (NGF), and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) [23,24]. These factors precisely regulate growth cone directional extension by activating members of the Trk receptor family on axonal terminals. Furthermore, adhesion molecules such as N-cadherin, which are expressed on SC surfaces, along with secreted extracellular matrix components such as laminin and fibronectin, collaboratively construct a molecular pathway network that supports axonal regeneration [25,26].

PNI triggers a series of microenvironmental disturbances, among which persistent endometrial hypoxia is a key pathophysiological feature that regulates regenerative responses and leads to pathological sequelae [27]. Following traumatic nerve transection or crush, disruption of the endometrial microvascular network, combined with the elevated metabolic demands of regenerating axons and SCs, creates a hypoxic microenvironment with oxygen concentrations <1% within the injured nerve segment [27,28]. Early studies suggested that this hypoxia drives anaerobic glycolysis to maintain ATP homeostasis but simultaneously impairs Na+/K+-ATPase function, inducing neuronal hyperexcitability and neuropathic pain [27]. However, recent evidence has revealed the dual role of hypoxia: long-term hypoxia exacerbates tissue fibrosis and neuronal dysfunction, whereas transient hypoxic signaling is crucial for activating regeneration programs. Hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α), the core regulator of the hypoxic response, is rapidly stabilized in SCs and dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons postinjury [29,30]. In SCs, HIF-1α upregulates myelin-associated genes (e.g., MPZ and MBP) and promotes SC migration, directly enhancing axonal remyelination; in DRG neurons, HIF-1α suppresses ferroptosis by upregulating SLC7A11 and GPX4, thereby maintaining neuronal viability and promoting neurite growth [30].

Mitochondria are central organelles for energy metabolism and cellular homeostasis regulation in SCs [31,32]. Their functional status directly affects the repair efficacy of SCs during nerve regeneration. Recently, intercellular mitochondrial transfer has emerged as a novel cellular communication mechanism that plays a key role in nerve regeneration [33,34]. In central nervous system injury repair, mitochondrial transplantation has been shown to have significant therapeutic effects: in spinal cord injury models, mitochondria derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) transferred to injured neurons via tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) significantly improved cellular energy metabolism and reduced oxidative stress levels [35], and in cerebral ischemia‒reperfusion injury, mitochondria transferred by astrocytes via an LRP1-dependent pathway significantly reduced neuronal apoptosis rates [36]. The hypoxic microenvironment following PNI regulates mitochondrial quality by promoting ATP generation, alleviating calcium overload, modulating mitophagy, and affecting mitochondrial biogenesis, fusion, and fission, thereby maintaining normal mitochondrial function and structure [37]. This study extends the strategy of hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial transplantation to the field of peripheral nerve repair. By establishing a hypoxia (5% O2) preconditioning model, we thoroughly explored the effects of hypoxia preconditioning on SC mitochondrial function and the role of mitochondrial transplantation in axonal regeneration and functional recovery. This research aims to overcome the limitations of traditional therapies that rely solely on structural reconstruction, providing novel strategies and perspectives for peripheral nerve repair from the perspectives of energy metabolism and cellular interactions.

Materials and methods

Animals

Female Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were obtained from the Medical Research Center of Soochow University. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines for ethical review of laboratory animals, and the study protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of blinded University (Approval No. blinded. All experimental procedures strictly followed international ethical norms and relevant national regulations for animal welfare. The rats were housed in the Experimental Animal Center of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, with a controlled ambient temperature of 25 °C and a 12-h light/dark cycle. Animals were group-housed at a density of three rats per cage, with ad libitum access to standard chow and water throughout the experiment.

Schwann cell culture and injury model construction

Rat Schwann cells (RSC96) were acquired from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The cells were maintained in DMEM medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Corning) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution, and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For subsequent experiments, Schwann cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL. Following 24 h of incubation, the culture medium in the H2O2 group and H2O2+Mito group was replaced with serum-free medium supplemented with 800 μmol/L H2O2, whereas cells in the Control group were treated with an equivalent volume of serum-free medium alone. After 24 h of intervention, the H2O2+Mito group received an appropriate proportion of mitochondria, while the Control and Injury groups received an equal volume of PBS. After another 24 h, β-galactosidase staining and JC-1 detection were performed.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

SCs were inoculated into 96-well plates. To minimize the potential interference of serum, the cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco) supplemented with 1% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution under routine incubation conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2).After cellular adhesion and stabilization, cells in different groups were exposed to gradient concentrations of H2O2 for 24 h to determine the optimal concentration for establishing an in vitro injury model. Meanwhile, hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondria at various concentrations were added to SCs for 24 h to screen the effective dosage for mitochondrial transplantation. Cell viability was quantitatively detected using a CCK-8 assay kit (CK04, Dojindo) in strict accordance with the manufacturer's protocol.

Isolation and identification of Schwann cell-derived mitochondria

Mitochondria were isolated from cultured Schwann cells using a commercial Mitochondria Isolation Kit (89,874; Thermo Fisher, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions with minor modifications. Following trypsinization and centrifugation, the collected cell suspension was subjected to centrifugation at 850g for 2 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded to obtain a cell pellet. Subsequently, 800 μL of Mitochondria Isolation Reagent A was added to the pellet, followed by vortexing at medium speed for 5 s and incubation on ice for 2 min. The cell suspension was then transferred to a pre chilled homogenizer and homogenized 50 times on ice to ensure complete cell lysis. The resulting lysate was returned to the original centrifuge tube, and 800 μL of Mitochondria Isolation Reagent C was added. The homogenizer was rinsed with 200 μL of Reagent A, and the rinse solution was combined with the aforementioned mixture, followed by gentle mixing via inversion. The mixture was centrifuged at 700 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was carefully transferred to a new 2 mL centrifuge tube for further centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet containing crude mitochondria was resuspended in 500 μL of Mitochondria Isolation Reagent C and centrifuged again at 12,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the mitochondrial pellet was retained. The protein concentration of the isolated mitochondria was quantified, and the mitochondria were either used immediately for subsequent experiments or stored at −80 °C until use. It should be noted that the storage time in the −80 °C freezer should be controlled within 1 week as much as possible. Refer to the operating procedure of adherent cells for the reconstitution steps and temperature. Simply put, reconstitute in a 37 °C warm-water bath, and then add normal saline to dilute to an appropriate concentration. Identification of extracted and purified mitochondria was based on transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and Western blot procedures (see sections 2.6, 2.7).

Mitochondrial labeling and uptake

Confocal microscopy was used to capture images of exogenous mitochondrial uptake by SCs, sensory and motor neurons, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages. Target cells were seeded on confocal dishes at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well. After 24 h of culture, prestained mitochondria (using MitoTracker) were added to the same confocal dish, mixed, and cocultured with target cells for 24 h. F-actin (R37110; Thermo) was used to label the cytoskeleton. After adherence, the cells were rinsed with PBS, stained with Hoechst 33342 for nuclear labeling, and finally imaged using a panoramic confocal scanner.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The cells were used to observe hypoxia-preconditioned SCs and identify extracted/purified mitochondria. SCs subjected to hypoxia for different durations were collected. The cell pellet was fixed in 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde for 24 h, followed by post-fixation in 2% (m/v) osmium tetroxide prepared with 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer. For subsequent assays, the isolated sciatic nerve tissue was also fixed in glutaraldehyde for 24 h. Grid-mounted sections were stained with a mixture of 1% uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and the ultrastructures of the stained cells and tissue samples were observed and imaged using a Hitachi HT 7700 transmission electron microscope.

Western blotting

Cell or mitochondrial lysis: RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors was added to the prepared samples, followed by lysis on ice for 20 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected for further analysis. Protein concentration was quantified by the BCA method, and all samples were adjusted to an equal concentration. Subsequently, 5× protein loading buffer was added at a ratio of 1:4, mixed well, and denatured in a metal bath at 100 °C for 5 min. After brief cooling on ice, samples were used immediately or stored at −80 °C. A total of 20 μg of protein per lane was loaded onto pre-cast gels. Electrophoresis was carried out at a constant voltage of 120 V until the bromophenol blue marker migrated to the bottom of the separating gel. For western transfer and blocking: Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes using a wet transfer system at a constant current of 300 mA for 90 min. The membranes were then blocked in TBST buffer containing 5% skim milk with gentle shaking at room temperature for 1–2 h. Antibody incubation and detection:(1) Primary antibody incubation: After washing three times with TBST, membranes were incubated with diluted primary antibodies (anti-COX IV, ab202554; anti-TOMM20, ab186735; 1:1000 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. (2) Secondary antibody incubation: Following three TBST washes, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000 dilution) was applied and incubated at room temperature for 1 h(3) Visualization: After extensive washing with TBST, protein bands were detected and imaged using ECL reagent according to the manufacturer's protocol.

EdU proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using an EdU proliferation detection kit (BeyoClick™, C0078S, Beyotime, China). RSC96 cells were seeded in confocal dishes. Different experimental groups were subjected to mitochondrial transplantation for 24 or 72 h. EdU reagent was then added to each well. Following 2 h of incubation, cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde for 20 min, rinsed twice with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS at room temperature for 15 min. After three additional PBS washes, nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Cat. No. 40732ES03, YEASEN) in the dark for 10 min. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope. Data quantification was performed using Photoshop 5.0 software to obtain the percentage of EdU-positive proliferating cells.

Cell migration assay

Transwell assays were performed to assess the effect of mitochondrial transplantation on SC migration. Briefly, SCs at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL were seeded into the upper chambers of Transwell inserts (Costar Corning) set in 24 well plates. The lower chambers were filled with complete medium in all groups. Mitochondrial suspension was added to the upper chamber in the experimental group, while an equal volume of PBS was applied in the control group. After 24 h of incubation, cells that migrated to the lower membrane surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet (G1062, Solarbio, China). Non migrated cells on the upper surface were gently wiped off with a cotton swab. Stained cells were photographed under a Nikon microscope (Japan), and migrated cells were quantitatively analyzed using Image J software.

Cell immunofluorescence

All the cell samples were pre-examined under a confocal laser scanning microscope to exclude endogenous fluorescence. Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100, cells were rinsed with PBS, placed in blocking buffer, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (anti-F-actin and anti-Tuj1, Abcam) diluted in immunofluorescence blocking buffer. The next day, after washing with PBST, secondary antibodies diluted in immunofluorescence blocking buffer were added and incubated at room temperature for 1–2 h. After incubation, cells were rewashed with PBST, and nuclei were finally stained with Hoechst dye. Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope after staining.

β-Galactosidase (β-GAL) staining

The H2O2 treatment protocol for SCs was described in detail in section 2.2. β-GAL staining was conducted following the manufacturer's instructions of a Senescence Detection Kit (C0602, Beyotime). Flow cytometry data were analyzed with Modfit LT 5.0 software to determine the percentage of senescent cells.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assessment

This assay was used to detect mitochondrial membrane potential (PE/FITC) in hypoxia-preconditioned SCs and H2O2-treated SCs. For hypoxia-preconditioned SCs: cells were cultured under hypoxic conditions (37 °C, 5% O2), and changes in mitochondrial membrane potential were measured at different time points (0, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h). The H2O2 intervention procedure for SCs was detailed in section 2.2. JC-1 staining was performed in accordance with the instructions of the JC-1 Staining Kit (M8650, Solarbio, China). For JC-1 monomers (green fluorescence), the excitation wavelength was set at 490 nm and emission at 530 nm; for JC-1 aggregates (red fluorescence), excitation was 525 nm and emission 590 nm. Baseline voltages were adjusted using unstained cells, and compensation was optimized using positive controls. The PE/FITC ratio was then calculated.

Transcriptomic detection

The SC intervention procedure was the same as that described in Section 2.2. Samples were collected for transcriptomic detection, with 3 biological replicates per group. Cells were first lysed with TRIZOL reagent (15596018CN; Thermo Fisher Scientific), then rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, preserved at −80 °C, and shipped using dry ice. Subsequent library construction, transcriptomic data acquisition, and analysis were performed by Shanghai Majorbio Biopharm Technology Co., Ltd. The brief experimental workflow was as follows: After total RNA extraction, RNA integrity was assessed using an RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). After RNA quality validation, cDNA libraries were constructed and verified. Qualified libraries were processed for high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. After data quality filtering, sequencing reads were mapped to the reference genome. Gene expression levels were quantified with DESeq2 software (version 1.20.0). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened using the thresholds of adjusted P-value ≤0.05 and log2-fold change≥1. Functional annotation including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment of DEGs was subsequently conducted using the clusterProfiler R package. Antibodies targeting key proteins identified by sequencing: anti-HIF-1α (ab179483), anti-Ras (ab52939), anti-ERK (ab184699), anti-p-ERK (ab201015), anti-c-Jun (ab40766), anti-p-c-Jun (ab32385), anti-c-Fos (ab208942), anti-p-c-Fos (ab308128).

Animal modeling

Sixty female Sprague–Dawley rats weighing 200 ± 20 g were used in this study. Animals were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 20 per group): a sham operation group (Sham), a sciatic nerve injury group (Injury), and a hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial transplantation group (Mito). After anesthesia, the right hindlimb was depilated and sterilized. The skin and right gluteus maximus were incised layer by layer, and the right sciatic nerve was carefully exposed under sterile conditions. In the Injury group, the sciatic nerve was clamped three times with non-toothed forceps (10 s each clamp) to establish a crush injury model. Rats in the Sham group only underwent sciatic nerve exposure without clamping. In the Mito group, immediately after model establishment, 10 μL of hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial suspension was injected beneath the nerve epineurium using a microinjector (According to relevant literature [38] and the volume of sciatic nerve crush injury in rats in this study (parameters such as nerve diameter and the width of the vascular clamp jaws), it is calculated that each rat is injected with 5 × 107 mitochondria (diluted to 10 μL with normal saline) into the nerve). Following surgery, the incision was closed in layers, and rats were housed under standard SPF conditions. All behavioral evaluations and sample collections were performed on postoperative day 14.

Sciatic functional index (SFI) assessment

Motor function was assessed using the Sciatic Functional Index (SFI) on postoperative day 14. A transparent PVC runway (50 cm in length, 8 cm in width) was prepared, with one end darkened and white paper placed flat on the bottom. Prior to testing, ink was uniformly applied to the plantar surfaces of both hind paws to capture footprints. To guarantee data reliability, rats received multiple adaptive training sessions before the formal experiment. For both the uninjured (E, healthy) and injured (N, experimental) hind paws, three parameters were measured: Print Length (PL, distance from toe tip to heel), Toe Spread (TS, distance between the first and fifth toes), and Intermediate Toe Spread (ITS, distance between the second and fourth toes). The SFI was calculated using the following formula based on the measured data: SFI = −38.3 × [(EPL - NPL)/NPL] + 109.5 × [(ETS - NTS)/NTS] + 13.3 × [(EITS - NITS)/NITS] - 8.8.

Electrophysiological detection

Under aseptic conditions, anesthetized rats were subjected to transcranial electrical stimulation. Two needle electrodes were inserted through the scalp, and transcranial motor evoked potentials (MEPs) were stimulated with parameters of 50 V and 100 mA. Two recording needle electrodes were inserted into the right gastrocnemius muscle, while two reference electrodes were placed in the contralateral healthy gastrocnemius muscle, and a ground electrode was positioned on the tail. Intraoperative evoked potentials were recorded using a NIM-Eclipse nerve monitoring system.

Gastrocnemius muscle assessment

The bilateral gastrocnemius muscles were fully exposed, dissected, and weighed. The wet weight ratio of the right (injured) gastrocnemius muscle to the left (uninjured/healthy) side was computed. After being weighed, the gastrocnemius muscle was immediately frozen in an acetone‒dry ice mixture and then transferred to a −80 °C freezer for storage. Frozen muscle tissue was consecutively sectioned at 30 μm thickness using a cryostat. Muscle tissue sections were subjected to Masson's trichrome staining using a commercial kit (Solarbio, G1120). Under a 20× magnification lens, the cross-sectional area of myofibers was measured on three separate muscle sections per rat using NIH Image J software. The average value of these measurements was defined as the final result for each animal.

Transmission electron microscopy analysis of the sciatic nerve

Two weeks postoperation, a 2 mm nerve segment was collected and postfixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde +2% paraformaldehyde. After fixation at 4 °C with 1% OsO4 for 2 h, tissues were dehydrated in an acetone gradient, embedded in resin, and cut into ultrathin sections. The sections were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate, then observed under a transmission electron microscope (H-7500, Hitachi). Three random images were captured per sample at 6000x magnification. Myelin sheath thickness was measured using ImageJ, and the average value was taken as the myelin thickness for each rat.

Toluidine blue staining

Two weeks postmodeling, a 2 mm segment of injured sciatic nerve tissue was collected. Semi-thin sections were prepared and immersed in toluidine blue staining solution (Solarbio, G3668) at room temperature for 20 min, followed by rinsing in 95% ethanol for 10 s. Finally, the slides were washed with distilled water, dehydrated directly with 100% ethanol, cleared, and mounted.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. The significance threshold was set at α = 0.05, and P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant; P > 0.05 was marked as “ns” (not significant). All the statistical tests in this study were two-sided, and corrections for multiple comparisons were applied. Method selection was based on data characteristics: Data normality was primarily examined using the Shapiro Wilk test (more sensitive for small samples), parametric tests were used for data with homogeneity of variance, and nonparametric tests were used when prerequisites for parametric tests were not met.

Results

Six hours of hypoxia preconditioning increases mitochondrial membrane potential in Schwann cells

A hypoxic microenvironment forms locally after PNI. Therefore, we established a hypoxic culture model and measured changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (PE/FITC) in SCs after different durations of hypoxia (0, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h) using flow cytometry. Compared with that in normoxic culture (37 °C, 21% O2), the mitochondrial membrane potential in hypoxic culture (37 °C, 5% O2) tended to first increase but then decrease with prolonged culture time (Fig. 1A). After 3 h of hypoxia, the JC-1 ratio was 1.19, whereas that in the normoxic group was 0.70, with an intergroup P = 0.0016, indicating a significant increase in the JC-1 ratio after 3 h of hypoxia. The JC-1 ratio peaked at 1.34 after 6 h of hypoxia. Although an increasing–then–decreasing trend was observed among 3, 6, 12, and 18 h hypoxia groups, the differences were not statistically significant. After 24 h of hypoxia, the JC-1 ratio was 1.03, which was clearly lower than those in the 6 h and 12 h hypoxia groups (Fig. 1D).

MitoTracker staining was used to visualize mitochondria. C. Under normoxia, most of the intracellular mitochondria appeared as elongated tubes with visible cristae structures. After 3 h and 6 h of hypoxia exposure, the mitochondrial morphology still appeared elongated. After 24 h of hypoxia, more fragmented mitochondria were observed. The results indicated that short-term hypoxia (≤6 h) caused no significant morphological changes in the mitochondrial structure in the SCs, whereas long-term hypoxia damaged the mitochondrial ultrastructure (Fig. 1B). TEM revealed that the mitochondrial cristae structures remained clear after 6 h of hypoxia. After 24 h of hypoxia, compared with those in the 6 h group, the mitochondrial cristae structures in the 24 h group appeared vacuolated (Fig. 1C). On the basis of these results, mitochondria from SCs exposed to 6 h of hypoxia presented the highest membrane potential, with no significant changes in overall morphology or crista structure. Therefore, mitochondria extracted from SCs after 6 h of hypoxia preconditioning were selected for subsequent experiments.

Identification of purified mitochondria and uptake efficiency by various cells in the PNI microenvironment

TEM revealed that the extracted SC mitochondria possessed a double-membrane structure and cristae (Fig. 2A). Western blot analysis revealed very low expression of the cytosolic reference protein GAPDH in the extracted mitochondria, while mitochondrial marker proteins—inner membrane protein (COX IV) and outer membrane protein (TOMM20)—were abundant. The opposite trend was observed in the extracted supernatant (Fig. 2B). On the basis of the above results, the extracted substance was identified as cellular mitochondria. The PNI repair microenvironment contains various cell types (e.g., SCs, motor neurons, sensory neurons, endothelial cells, macrophages, and fibroblasts). Therefore, this study investigated exogenous mitochondrial uptake by different cells in vitro. Tunneling nanotubes formed between SCs, which is important for intercellular mitochondrial transfer. Various cells could uptake a certain number of exogenous mitochondria (Fig. 2C). The level and proportion of mitochondrial uptake differed among cell types. Apart from SCs (relative fluorescence intensity of 7.69), endothelial cells took up relatively more exogenous mitochondria (relative fluorescence intensity of 2.87, intergroup P < 0.0001). Fibroblasts and macrophages took up the fewest exogenous mitochondria (relative fluorescence intensities of 0.25 and 0.09, respectively; intergroup P = 0.0016). In sensory neurons (DRGs), mitochondrial uptake was relatively low (relative fluorescence intensity of 0.47), whereas motor neurons took up more mitochondria (relative fluorescence intensity of 1.94; intergroup P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2D). The results indicated that SCs had the greatest relative uptake of exogenous mitochondria, which we attribute to the heterogeneity of mitochondrial sources. With respect to the phenomenon of increased uptake by endothelial cells, we speculate that mitochondrial transplantation might have proangiogenic effects.

Determination of mitochondrial transplantation concentrations and promotion of SC proliferation and migration by hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial transplantation

After PNI, the local microenvironment induces inflammation and oxidative stress. Thus, H2O2 was employed in vitro to mimic local oxidative stress injury. To identify the cytotoxic dose of H2O2 in cultured SCs, cells were treated with H2O2 at a concentration gradient of 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 μmol/L for 24 h. SC viability was then evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. The cell survival rate gradually decreased with increasing H2O2 concentration. On the basis of a comparison with similar literature [39] (where cell activity decreased by approximately 50% after H2O2 intervention), 800 μmol/L was ultimately selected as the optimal H2O2 intervention concentration in this study, resulting in a 42.95% decrease in SC viability (Fig. 3A). After 24 h of mitochondrial transplantation, cell viability was determined. It should be noted that the definition of 1-fold mitochondria is as follows: Mitochondria are isolated and extracted from a given number of donor cells and then transplanted into recipient cells of the same quantity, with a donor-recipient cell number ratio of 1:1. Similarly, the 10-fold mitochondria treatment group refers to extracting mitochondria from donor cells with 10 times the quantity and transplanting an equal amount into recipient cells with 1-fold quantity. It should be noted that due to the inevitable losses and differences in recovery rates during the mitochondrial isolation and purification process, the total amount of mitochondria actually introduced into the recipient cells cannot be strictly equal to the ideal value of the theoretical ratio. Compared with that of the control, the absorbance tended to first increase but then decrease with increasing mitochondrial transplantation concentrations (relative multiples: 2, 5, 10, and 25). Compared with the control, a two-fold concentration of mitochondria significantly increased cell activity (1.479 vs. 1.000, P = 0.0038). With a further increase in mitochondrial concentration, cell activity initially increased but then decreased; there were statistically significant differences between each experimental group and the control group. Based on the above experimental results, we selected the group with the highest cell viability, namely the group treated with a 10x relative concentration of mitochondria (Fig. 3B). Therefore, an appropriate concentration of mitochondrial transplantation can promote SC proliferation and viability, whereas an excessively high ratio has an inhibitory effect. In this study, a 10x relative concentration of mitochondria was ultimately selected for subsequent experiments. Post-PNI, SC proliferation capacity is particularly important for remyelination. The EdU assay results revealed that in comparison with the control group, one day following mitochondrial transplantation, the number of EdU-positive SCs significantly increased after 1 day of mitochondrial transplantation (15.85 vs. 26.60, P = 0.0051). After 3 days of mitochondrial transplantation, the number of EdU-positive SCs further increased compared with that in the control (24.82 vs. 35.27, P = 0.0045) (Fig. 3C and D). These results indicate that mitochondrial transplantation promotes SC proliferation.

Statistical analysis of the number of SCs on the bottom surface of the Transwell chambers; n = 4.

After PNI, SCs migrate to the injury site to guide axonal regeneration. Compared with that on the control group, the number of cells at the bottom surface of the Transwell chamber significantly increased after 1 day of mitochondrial transplantation (64.82 vs. 92.10, P = 0.0025). After 3 days of mitochondrial transplantation, the number of cells in the mitochondrial transplantation group further increased (74.07 vs. 206.11, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3E and F). These results indicate that mitochondrial transplantation significantly enhances SC migration ability.

Mitochondrial transplantation inhibits SC senescence, partially restores mitochondrial membrane potential, and promotes sensory and motor neuron growth

After PNI, the injured site and distal nerve stump undergo ischemia and inflammatory responses, which elicit oxidative stress. This cascade of events promotes lipid peroxidation, protein denaturation, and DNA damage, ultimately culminating in cellular apoptosis. Persistent oxidative stress significantly inhibits the activity of cells involved in peripheral nerve regeneration and disrupts cellular functions, and some SCs undergo senescence, hindering nerve repair. Therefore, oxidative stress injury is considered a major pathological obstacle to peripheral nerve regeneration. Compared with that in the control group, after 24 h of H2O2 treatment, the β-galactosidase level in SCs significantly increased (10590.30 vs. 12340.35, P < 0.0001). After mitochondrial transplantation intervention (H2O2+Mito group), the β-galactosidase level significantly decreased (12340.35 vs. 9210.88, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4A and B). These results indicate that mitochondrial transplantation significantly inhibits H2O2-induced SC senescence.

The flow cytometry (PE/FITC) results revealed that compared with the control, H2O2 significantly reduced the SC mitochondrial membrane potential (2.07 vs. 0.54, P = 0.0003). After mitochondrial transplantation intervention (H2O2+Mito group), the SC mitochondrial membrane potential was partially restored (0.54 vs. 0.94, P = 0.0091) (Fig. 4C and D).

Confocal microscopy observation of motor neuron growth in the control and mitochondrial transplantation groups revealed that after primary motor neurons were extracted and cultured for 4 days, the average axon length in the control group was 58.37 μm, whereas in the mitochondrial transplantation group, it increased to 80.32 μm (intergroup P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4E and F). These results suggest that mitochondrial transplantation promotes axonal growth in neurons. Furthermore, this study revealed internalized mitochondria within the axons and cell body clusters of DRGs (sensory neurons). Compared with the control, the axonal growth distance was greater, indicating the internalization and pro-axonal growth effects of mitochondrial transplantation on sensory neuron cell body clusters (DRGs) (Fig. 4G).

Hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial transplantation regulates the Ras-ERK-c-Fos/c-Jun pathway in Schwann cells

To explore the effects and molecular mechanism of hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondria on H2O2-damaged SCs, we used high-throughput RNA-seq combined with bioinformatics to analyze differential gene expression between the H2O2 injury group (H2O2 group) and the hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial treatment group (H2O2+Mito group). The clustering heatmap of the top 18 differentially expressed genes between the H2O2 and H2O2+Mito groups revealed good reproducibility between the groups, and the DEGs included Fos, Jun, etc (Fig. 5A). A volcano plot was constructed to show the types of key DEGs between the groups and to reveal the levels of Fos and Jun from the MAPK pathway (Fig. 5B). A KEGG pathway enrichment analysis bubble chart revealed signaling pathways associated with the differentially expressed genes: cell proliferation (PI3K-Akt) and cell dedifferentiation (MAPK)-related pathways (Fig. 5C). Following mitochondrial intervention, a total of 981 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 434 upregulated and 547 downregulated genes (Fig. 5D). To further verify the key regulatory pathways, we examined the protein expression levels of core components in the MAPK signaling pathway using Western blot analysis. The results demonstrated that compared with the H2O2-treated group, the H2O2+Mito group exhibited a significant increase in the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α). The expression levels of key proteins in the cell dedifferentiation-related MAPK pathway (Ras-ERK-c-Fos/c-Jun) significantly increased after mitochondrial intervention (Fig. 5E and F), promoting the dedifferentiation of mature SCs into a repair phenotype, which plays a crucial role in nerve regeneration post-PNI [40,41].

Hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial transplantation promotes morphological nerve repair in PNI rats

The animal model used in this study involved sciatic nerve crush injury in rats: After shaving and skin disinfection with iodine, the tissue and fascia were separated, the sciatic nerve was located within the intermuscular space, and the surrounding fat and connective tissue were bluntly dissected. The sciatic nerve was fully exposed (Fig. 6A). Afterward, a vascular clamp was used to crush the sciatic nerve at a point 5 mm proximal to its distal branches. The crush width was 2 mm, and the crush strength was 2 clicks on the clamp; this process was repeated 3 times, each for 10 s (Fig. 6B). In the mitochondrial treatment group, after modeling, 10 μL of mitochondrial suspension (quantified in pre-experiments using ink; Fig. 6C) was injected under the nerve epineurium using a 31G microsyringe (Fig. 6D). The solvent was normal saline. The control group was administered an equivalent volume of normal saline. The sham group underwent sciatic nerve exposure alone without any further manipulation. Mitochondria were extracted from SCs using differential centrifugation combined with a mitochondrial extraction kit. Mitochondrial pellets could be observed in the round-bottom centrifuge tubes (Fig. 6E).

A hemostatic clamp was used to crush the sciatic nerve at a point 5 mm proximal to its distal branches to construct the sciatic nerve injury model. C. Pre-experiment measuring the volume for epineurial injection using ink. D. Injection of mitochondrial suspension under the epineurium to repair nerve crush injury in rats. E. Hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial transplantation significantly improves motor function and alleviates gastrocnemius muscle atrophy in PNI rats

Footprint images of rats from each group at 2 weeks postoperation revealed that in the Injury and Mito groups, the toes on the operated right side were close together, showing obvious flexion contracture, with limited toe extension and abduction. The footprints of the Sham group had clear contours and intact intertoe structures. This finding indicates that at 2 weeks postoperation, limb function in the Mito and Injury groups had not fully recovered. The degree of foot splay was greater in the Mito group than in the Injury group (Fig. 7A). Analysis of the footprint morphology results (Fig. 7C) revealed that at 2 weeks postoperation, the SFI value of the Sham group (−16.65) was significantly better than those of the Mito group (−38.10, P < 0.0001) and the Injury group (−50.54). There was also a statistically significant difference between the Mito and Injury groups (P = 0.0017) (Fig. 7C). However, toe extension in the Mito group still lagged behind that in the Sham group. After PNI, the gastrocnemius muscle loses nerve innervation, resulting in a decrease in the number of muscle fibers and subsequent muscle atrophy. If nerve innervation is restored before irreversible muscle atrophy occurs, the reduction in the number of muscle fibers can be minimized, thereby promoting the recovery of motor function. At 2 weeks after surgery, the degree of muscle atrophy in the Injury group without mitochondrial treatment was more severe. The volume of the gastrocnemius muscle on the surgical side was significantly greater in the Mito group than in the Injury group. The muscle was ruddy in color and flexible in texture, and there was less proliferative collagenous connective tissue in the muscle interstitium, indicating that the degree of muscle atrophy was significantly reduced (Fig. 7B). Gastrocnemius muscle atrophy begins after nerve injury, and its weight is positively correlated with the degree of nerve recovery. At 2 weeks postoperatively, the wet weight ratio of the gastrocnemius muscle in the Sham group was 97.26%, which was significantly higher than that in the Mito group (71.07%, P < 0.0001) and the Injury group (51.25%). Additionally, a statistically significant difference was observed between the Mito group and the Injury group (P = 0.0002) (Fig. 7D). Meanwhile, the average cross-sectional area of myofibers in the Sham group was 1741.78 μm2, which was significantly larger than that in the Mito group (1284.58 μm2, P < 0.0001) and the Injury group (1006.83 μm2). The difference in myofiber cross-sectional area between the Mito group and the Injury group was also statistically significant (P = 0.0003) (Fig. 7E). These results indicate that mitochondrial transplantation treatment can alleviate denervation-induced skeletal muscle atrophy following PNI, maintain myofiber structural integrity, and thereby promote motor function recovery in rats.

Mitochondrial transplantation improves the density, diameter, and myelin thickness of newly regenerated axons and electrophysiology post-PNI

To further determine the electrophysiological function of reinnervated motor units, we performed electrophysiological experiments at 2 weeks postoperation and recorded compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs). The CMAP amplitude in the Mito group did not reach the same level as that in the Sham group (83.03 vs. 94.75, p = 0.0057); however, compared with the Injury group, the Mito group showed better CMAP recovery (83.03 vs. 64.25, p = 0.0016) (Fig. 8A and B), suggesting that mitochondrial transplantation improves electrophysiological parameters after PNI. Meanwhile, we recorded the latency of compound muscle action potentials (CMAP latency), which reflects the degree of myelination. The CMAP latency in the Mito group did not reach the same level as that in the Sham group (1.617 vs. 1.442, p = 0.0447). However, compared with the injury group, the Mito group showed a better CMAP latency (1.617 vs. 1.836, p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Fig. 4). We further verified that the hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial transplantation method promotes functional recovery of regenerating motor nerves. At 2 weeks postmodeling, toluidine blue staining was performed on the mid-portion of the crush site. In the Injury group, the area of the newly formed myelin sheaths was smaller, and the sheaths were sparsely and irregularly arranged. The mitochondrial treatment group had slightly larger and more densely packed newly formed myelin sheaths. The new myelin sheath in the sham operation group was darker in color, with thicker myelin sheath walls and tightly arranged fibers, indicating a certain degree of regional regularity (Fig. 8C). Compared with that in the Injury group, the density of newly formed myelin sheaths significantly increased in the mitochondrial treatment group (6955.35 vs. 7436.66, P = 0.0195) (Fig. 8D); however, compared with that in the Sham group, the density in the mitochondrial treatment group was still significantly different (7436.66 vs. 8108.01, P = 0.0041) (Fig. 8D).

Representative images of nerve electrophysiological conduction in the Injury, Mito, and Sham groups at 2 weeks postoperation. B. Statistical analysis of CMAPs in each group; n = 5. C. Toluidine blue staining of the mid-portion of the nerve crush site in each group at 2 weeks postoperation. D. Statistical analysis of the newly formed myelin sheath density determined by toluidine blue staining in each group; n = 5. E, G. After PNI, SCs undergo demyelination. After axonal regeneration is complete, dedifferentiated SCs wrap around axons to form new myelin sheaths. Myelin thickness is closely related to the quality of nerve regeneration. As shown in Fig. 8E and G, transmission electron micrographs at different magnifications reveal the number of regenerated nerve fibers, the state of myelin regeneration, and ultrastructural characteristics such as nerve diameter and myelin thickness.

Statistical results at 2 weeks postoperation showed that the nerve fiber diameter in the Sham group (8.75 μm) was significantly greater than that in the Mito group (5.46 μm). The nerve diameter in the Mito group was greater than that in the Injury group (1.77 μm, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 8F). The results suggest that the Injury group showed poor recovery without intervention, whereas the Mito group exhibited significant pro-axonal regeneration effects. Myelin sheaths not only provide physical support and electrical insulation for axons, but their regenerative state is also a key indicator for assessing nerve repair. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed that the myelin sheath thickness in the Mito group was 1.076 μm, which was significantly increased compared with the Injury group (0.318 μm, P < 0.0001); however, it remained substantially lower than that in the Sham group (2.048 μm, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 8H). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial transplantation markedly enhances the density, diameter, and myelin sheath thickness of newly regenerated axons following peripheral nerve injury (PNI).

Discussion

Clinical peripheral nerve injury is a common traumatic neurological disorder, with crush injuries accounting for approximately 30%-40% of traumatic nerve injuries. These are common complications of traffic accidents, industrial accidents, and sports injuries. Because crush injuries are clinically common and mitochondrial solution injected under the epineurium can directly fill the crushed nerve portion, we chose a nerve crush injury model. Its core pathological features include axonal degeneration, myelin damage, and ischemia‒reperfusion injury induced by mechanical stress, ultimately leading to sensory and motor dysfunction, muscle atrophy, and other clinical sequelae [42,43]. The prolonged recovery period (often months to years) and limited regeneration efficiency after PNI still severely affect patient rehabilitation and quality of life [44]. This clinical reality highlights the urgency and importance of strengthening research on PNI repair. As energy metabolism hubs and signaling centers of cells, mitochondria not only are involved in fundamental physiological processes such as ATP synthesis and calcium homeostasis but also play central roles in pathophysiological processes such as oxidative stress regulation, apoptosis pathways, and inflammatory responses [45,46]. Notably, mitochondrial transfer and exogenous mitochondrial transplantation have become cutting-edge scientific issues in regenerative medicine research [[47], [48], [49]]. Mitochondrial transfer can occur via tunneling nanotubes, extracellular vesicles, free mitochondria, and direct cell fusion [50]. Mitochondrial transplantation refers to the extraction and purification of mitochondria from cells or tissues followed by transplantation into recipient cells or tissues for therapeutic purposes [51,52].

Following PNI, a local ischemic–hypoxic microenvironment forms. Hypoxia is a common pathological factor, and mitochondria are key organelles that are sensitive to oxygen sensing. Recent studies have indicated that hypoxia affects both the morphology and function of mitochondria. Hypoxia induces extensive intermitochondrial contact and subsequent mitochondrial fusion, resulting in the remodeling of normal tubular mitochondria into giant mitochondria, which then interact with lysosomes—a novel form of mitochondrial quality control. Additionally, hypoxia can promote the overexpression of genes related to angiogenesis and nerve regeneration [[53], [54], [55]]. Li et al. reported that under chronic hypoxia, the mitochondrial VHL protein can reprogram cellular metabolism [56]. Tian et al. demonstrated that hypoxia facilitates the transfer of exosomal hemoglobin between endothelial cells and neurons, which plays a crucial role in maintaining neuronal mitochondrial homeostasis and protecting neurons against injury [57].

Therefore, on the basis of previous research on mitochondrial transfer and nerve injury repair, this study proposes a novel therapeutic approach in which mitochondria preconditioned under different oxygen concentrations (21% normoxic O2 vs. 5% hypoxic O2) are extracted, purified, and transplanted to treat PNI. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the therapeutic effects of this approach and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, providing a new theoretical basis and potential intervention targets for clinical treatment. In this study, differential centrifugation combined with a mitochondrial extraction kit were used to isolate and purify cell-derived mitochondria, which were then cocultured with SCs to observe their effects on cell function. It should be noted that, in order to minimize the influence of time and environmental factors on mitochondrial function, all the mitochondria used in this study were freshly extracted. Meanwhile, we also compared the freshly extracted mitochondria with those stored at −80 °C and found no significant difference in their biological activities, which is consistent with the research results of Liang et al. [58]. The results show that exogenous mitochondria can significantly promote the proliferation and migration ability of Schwann cells. Further research revealed that mitochondrial transplantation can reverse the degree of Schwann cell senescence induced by hydrogen peroxide and can partially repair the mitochondrial membrane potential. Previous studies have shown that mitochondrial dysfunction drives cell senescence and that cell senescence has a negative feedback effect on mitochondrial function through the secretion of SASP components and cell cycle arrest [[59], [60], [61], [62]]. Mitochondrial transplantation can replenish healthy mitochondria into damaged tissues and cells, helping cells restore the homeostasis of mitochondrial function and inhibiting cell senescence. The latest research indicates that the core reason why transplanted healthy mitochondria can rescue damaged cells lies in the functional mtDNA they carry. Transplanting a mitochondrial population lacking mtDNA has no improvement effect on respiratory function. Only functional mitochondria carrying intact wild - type mtDNA can restore cellular energy metabolism. Moreover, the transplantation of allogeneic mitochondria with active mtDNA can fuse with endogenous mitochondria and play a role in rescuing optic nerve damage [63]. The mitochondrial transplantation in this study is derived from rat cells, which belongs to allogeneic transplantation. Therefore, the mtDNA sequences of the donor and the recipient are highly similar, and the donor mtDNA can stably exist and function in the recipient cells after transplantation [64]. In addition, the latest research points out that the mitochondrial capsule technology and the MitoCatch targeting system can significantly improve the mitochondrial fusion efficiency [63,64]. We speculate that subsequent optimization based on such experimental technologies may lead to better nerve repair effects. Hypoxic preconditioning can significantly increase the mitochondrial membrane potential in the short term, and this phenomenon may be related to the remodeling of the mitochondrial dynamic balance in a hypoxic environment. Hao et al. suggested that hypoxia preconditioning might optimize energy metabolism efficiency by promoting the dynamic balance of mitochondrial fusion and fission [53]. This study did not observe consistent changes in mitochondrial morphology, which may be closely related to cell type, hypoxic concentration, or duration. Moreover, this hypoxia preconditioning effect, possibly through the optimization of mitochondrial quality control, may further enhance its ability to repair SCs. This finding aligns with the theory that mitochondrial functional status affects therapeutic efficacy [45].

Through RNA-seq analysis, Bai et al. found that mitochondrial transplantation activated the PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling pathways [65]. The transcriptome sequencing results of the present study are consistent with this finding. However, the previous study did not verify the mechanism verification on the sequencing results and differed from the present study in the method of nerve injury and in the animal model used. The results of the present study suggest that the effect of mitochondrial transplantation on nerves is related to energy metabolism, and we did not repeat the verification of this aspect. In this study, the expression of the c-Jun gene was significantly upregulated, which complements the Atf3 gene regulatory network proposed by Zhang et al. [38]. Moreover, we verified the MAPK pathway of the Ras-ERK-c-Fos/c-Jun through sequencing results. Combining with the results presented in the studies above, our findings reveal that c-Jun is the core transcription factor for the dedifferentiation of Schwann cells (SCs) and that the increase in the phosphorylation level of ERK is an important marker of dedifferentiation. These results suggest that hypoxic mitochondrial transplantation can promote the dedifferentiation of Schwann cells. Bioinformatics analysis was performed on the normoxic mitochondria and hypoxic mitochondria groups (Supplementary Fig. 1). The results showed that they were mainly enriched in the cell cycle and oxidative phosphorylation pathways, suggesting that hypoxia may participate in the processes of cell metabolic reprogramming, proliferation, and the occurrence and development of related diseases by regulating these pathways. We speculate that mitochondrial transplantation may activate multiple regeneration-related genes, resulting in the formation of a multilevel transcriptional regulatory network to synergistically promote PNI repair. In addition, we also found a very interesting experimental phenomenon. The expression of HIF-1α protein in the H2O2 + mitochondria group was upregulated compared with that in the H2O2 group alone. We speculate that the reason may be that hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) intervention can simulate cellular oxidative stress, which in turn indirectly induces functional hypoxia in cells. The activation of hypoxia-related signaling pathways will increase the expression level of HIF-1α. After the superposition of hypoxic induction and mitochondrial intervention, mitochondria with a higher membrane potential may further transfer the hypoxic stimulation signal to recipient cells. Of course, the specific in-depth molecular mechanisms mentioned above need further verification.

Through histomorphological, behavioral, and electrophysiological assessments in vivo, this study revealed the effects of mitochondrial transplantation on the repair of rat sciatic nerve crush injury. TEM revealed a more complete myelin lamellar structure and clearer axon‒myelin interface in the treatment group. We believe that this optimization of nerve ultrastructure may stem from the direct energy supply of mitochondria to SCs [66]. Mitochondria from hypoxic preconditioning enhance the migration ability of Schwann cells by increasing their mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby accelerating the formation of Büngner bands. Notably, the density of newly formed axons was greater in the treatment group, whereas obvious axonal atrophy was observed in the nerve injury group. This difference suggests that mitochondrial transplantation, by regulating SC dedifferentiation, promotes nerve regeneration. We also considered the possibility that neurons uptake exogenous mitochondria, promoting axonal growth. Gait analysis at 2 weeks postoperation revealed a significant improvement in the sciatic functional index in the hypoxia-preconditioned mitochondrial treatment group, indicating that motor function recovery is directly related to early axonal regeneration efficiency. In the mitochondrial treatment group, compared with those in the injury group, the newly regenerated nerve fibers in the control group were more orderly, and the degree of myelin thickness uniformity was significantly greater. Meanwhile, we also compared the therapeutic effects on normoxic mitochondria and hypoxic mitochondria groups (Supplementary Figs. 2–3). The results showed that the hypoxic group was superior to the normoxic group in terms of nerve myelin sheath thickness, recovery rate of gastrocnemius wet weight, and gastrocnemius muscle fiber area. The above results may be mainly attributed to the dedifferentiation of Schwann cells caused by hypoxic mitochondria and the potential angiogenesis effect. Furthermore, the mitochondrial treatment group showed reduced collagen deposition in the epineurium and increased vascular density, suggesting that mitochondrial transplantation not only promotes nerve regeneration but also improves the local blood supply at the injury site [67]. The clinical value of the approach described herein lies in overcoming the single-target limitation of traditional therapies, providing multidirectional regulatory functions after PNI. For the peripheral nerve compression model, 14 days after surgery is a critical time window for the early myelin regeneration and functional recovery of the peripheral nerve, which is crucial for revealing the early repair mechanism [10,12,68]. However, long-term follow-up is also an aspect that cannot be ignored. The myelin maturity and the functional recovery of the target organ related to it are the directions that we must evaluate in the later stage.

The PNI microenvironment also contains cells other than SCs. In this study, regarding the proportions of exogenous mitochondrial uptake among different cells, SCs and endothelial cells exhibited higher uptake, whereas macrophages and fibroblasts exhibited lower uptake. It should be noted that the higher uptake of mitochondria by endothelial cells may be related to the pro-angiogenic effect. There are significant differences in gene expression, cell structure, functional characteristics, and intercellular communication among endothelial cells in different systems [69,70]. The study by Lin et al. suggested that transplanting mitochondria into endothelial cells can enhance the formation of functional blood vessels in ischemic tissues [67]. The study by Wu et al. suggested that the sequential transplantation of engineered mitochondria can target the damaged heart and rescue myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury by replenishing energy to cardiomyocytes [71]. In the PNI model, the effects of mitochondrial transplantation on various cells in the nerve repair microenvironment require further validation. With respect to immunomodulatory effects, this study focused on the direct effects of mitochondrial transplantation on cell function and nerve repair and did not investigate immune-related effects. Recent studies have shown that mitochondrial transplantation can treat central nervous system injuries, including stroke and spinal cord injury. Zhou et al. reported that mitochondrial transplantation for the treatment of cerebral hemorrhage can regulate the immune function of microglia, reduce their proinflammatory properties, and increase the proportion of immunosuppressive microglia, thereby effectively promoting the functional recovery of cerebral hemorrhage [72]. Similarly, studies have shown that in myocardial infarction models, mitochondrial transplantation therapy can effectively induce macrophage polarization toward a reparative M2-like phenotype [73]. On the basis of these findings, we speculate that mitochondrial transplantation also induces an immune response in macrophages during peripheral nerve injury, inhibits inflammation that hinders nerve repair, and promotes nerve regeneration. Previous studies have indicated that mitochondrial transplantation can modulate autophagy in recipient cells, sometimes promoting [67] and sometimes inhibiting autophagy [74]. The effect of mitochondrial transplantation on SC autophagy in nerve injury warrants further study. In addition, it should be noted that the distribution and in vivo tracing of mitochondria after transplantation are important research directions that need to be evaluated. The research by Bai et al. shows that the results of small animal imaging experiments on mitochondrial transplantation for the treatment of nerve injury indicate that mitochondria are still distributed locally in the injected nerve tissue one day and three days after transplantation [65]. The research by Lin et al. suggests that the mitochondria transplanted in situ are only present in the subcutaneous gel graft in the transplantation area and the intramuscular injection area of the ischemic hindlimb, and no diffusion to other tissues is observed [67]. Similarly, Li et al. reported that in neurodegenerative diseases, exogenous mitochondria labeled with GFP could be detected in Purkinje cells as early as one week after transplantation and remained intact within four weeks. The improvement effect on ataxia symptoms lasted for up to three weeks [74]. It should be noted that in the field of mitochondrial transplantation research after intravenous injection, mitochondria will be distributed in different organs [75]. Therefore, the precise in vivo distribution (at the cellular level) and fate of mitochondria after transplantation are directions that must be evaluated in our later research.

Although this study has generated new findings, it has several limitations. First, the specific mechanism of action of hypoxia-preconditioned Schwann cells has not been fully elucidated, and further research on the relationship between hypoxic mitochondrial dynamics and function is needed. Second, the pro-angiogenic effect after mitochondrial transplantation and the long-term effects of in vivo experiments still need to be thoroughly evaluated. Finally, compared with the biosafety issues in cell transplantation, although mitochondrial transplantation has a certain degree of safety and there is no research reporting systemic immune rejection after mitochondrial transplantation at present [38,67], it is still necessary to improve relevant biosafety evaluation experiments when promoting the clinical application value of mitochondrial transplantation. The following problems still need to be solved for clinical translation: 1. Technologies for large-scale extraction and long-term preservation of mitochondria; 2. The optimal dosage and timing of administration for different types of PNI (such as transection injury and stretch injury); 3. Verification of the efficacy of long-distance nerve regeneration in large animal models. These issues can all serve as the focus of subsequent preclinical studies. Future research can combine multiomics sequencing technology to thoroughly analyze changes in the heterogeneity of Schwann cells after mitochondrial transplantation and explore new carriers to clarify appropriate mitochondrial delivery methods.

Conclusion

In summary, hypoxia preconditioning enhances the mitochondrial membrane potential of SC-derived mitochondria. Mitochondrial transplantation modulates SC functions by activating the Ras-ERK-c-Fos/c-Jun pathway, significantly increasing nerve fiber density and myelin thickness, alleviating gastrocnemius muscle atrophy, and promoting motor function recovery, providing a new strategy for the clinical treatment of PNI. This study confirms that the transplantation of hypoxic mitochondria can induce the dedifferentiation of Schwann cells, providing more clues for optimizing clinical treatment strategies for peripheral nerve injury.
