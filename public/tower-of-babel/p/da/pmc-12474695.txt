Different Polymorphisms of the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System Genes are Associated with Poorer Blood Pressure Control in Hypertensive Older Adults
Authors: Ivna Vidal Freire, Icaro J. S. Ribeiro, Cezar Augusto Casotti, Diego Andrade, Débora Diniz Bezerra, Jules Ramon Brito Teixeira, Ana Angélica Leal Barbosa, Luciene Cristina Gastalho Campos, Rafael Pereira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction Adequate blood pressure control (BPC) is crucial for preventing hypertension, as well as for mitigating the risks associated with inadequate control among hypertensive older adults. Identifying modifiable factors (i.e., dietary and biochemical patterns, sociodemographic characteristics, and health habits) and non-modifiable factors (i.e., genetic background) is crucial for improving control rates.AimThis study aimed to analyze the association between Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System gene polymorphisms, sociodemographic characteristics, health and lifestyle habits, and BPC in older adults.Methodsone hundred and forty-three older adults comprised the study population, and were genotyped for angiotensinogen (AGT) [M235T], renin (REN) [G2646A] angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) [InDel], angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1R) [A1166C] aldosterone synthase (CYP11B2) [C344T] gene polymorphisms. Sociodemographic characteristics, health, and lifestyle habits were recorded using questionnaires, and blood pressure was measured using standard methods. A Poisson multivariate regression was applied.ResultsOur finding indicated that, together, LDL-C, the A allele of the REN gene (G2646A), and genotype II of the ACE gene (InDel) were significantly associated with inadequate BPC in the community-dwelling older adults.ConclusionsDue to its non-modifiable nature, the genetic background has the potential to identify individuals with a greater risk of illness. The knowledge of the genetic profiles prone to impaired BPC and its interaction with modifiable factors could guide more effective behaviors and/or treatments aiming to mitigate the morbidity and mortality related to poor BPC among hypertensive older adults.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) plays a crucial role in blood pressure control (BPC) and the pathophysiology of hypertension by regulating vascular tone, sodium retention, vascular oxidative stress, vascular fibrosis, sympathetic tone, and inflammation [1, 2].

Hypertension has a multifactorial genesis, involving both modifiable factors (e.g., obesity, smoking, sedentary lifestyle) and non-modifiable factors such as genetic predisposition. Polymorphisms in several RAAS-related genes have been associated with many cardiovascular outcomes, such as hypertension [3]. In fact, imbalance or dysfunctions in the RAAS cascade contribute significantly to BPC impairment [4, 5].

Early identification of individuals with a genomic profile prone to developing hypertension can help target preventive therapies more effectively. Although genetic factors are not individually decisive, when combined with modifiable factors such as lifestyle habits (e.g., eating habits, smoking habits), they play a significant role in predicting the onset or worsening of hypertension.

The aging process is characterized by phenotypic changes favoring the development of chronic diseases [6, 7]. Changes in body composition (i.e., decreased muscle and increased fat mass), as well as mobility impairments, leading to sedentary behavior, also appear to underlie the main chronic diseases [6, 7]. Studies relating RAAS polymorphisms to BPC, or even hypertension in the older adult population, are still scarce.

Genetic polymorphisms of RAAS elements are commonly investigated to elucidate the genetic susceptibility to blood pressure impairments and/or hypertension [3, 8, 9]. Candidate genes have been identified, and the volume of scientific production is more significant for the polymorphisms M235T of Angiotensinogen, G2646A (Mbo I) of REN, InDel of ACE, A1166C of the angiotensin II type 1 receptor, and C344T of aldosterone synthase [8, 10–12].

Thus, this study aimed to analyze the association between RAAS gene polymorphisms, sociodemographic characteristics, health and lifestyle habits, and BPC in hypertensive older adults.

Methods

This is a cross-sectional study involving all community-dwelling older adults (aged 60 years or older) from Aiquara, Bahia, Brazil, who were home-visited and invited to take part in the survey. In the first stage, during a home visit, a total of 259 patients were interviewed, excluding those who were bedridden and those with severe cognitive impairment. Subsequently, a health assessment and blood collection were conducted, in which further losses occurred, resulting in a final sample of 210 older adults. Among them, 143 self-reported a previous diagnosis of hypertension, which was confirmed through consulting their medical records. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the local ethics committee approved all procedures in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (CAAE 56017816.2.0000.0055).

Data Collection

Data was collected in three stages: questionnaires, clinical tests, and biological material collection.

From the questionnaires, sociodemographic characteristics (i.e., sex, age, education, marital status, and income), dietary recall, smoking habits (current and ex-smokers), and level of physical activity using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) (> 150 minutes [sufficiently active] and <150 minutes [insufficiently active]) [13] were recorded. Medications currently used were also recorded. The 24-h dietary recall was used to extract the dietary patterns of micro and macronutrients [14]. The sodium content of each ingested food and beverage was calculated using the DietPro® version 6.1 software. For sodium, a cut-off value of ≤ 2,300 mg/day was considered normal [14].

After the questionnaires were recorded during the home visits, the participants were scheduled for a clinical assessment and venous blood sampling (10 mL from the antecubital fossa).

Blood Pressure (BP) was recorded using a HEM 7320 digital device (Omron®) [15] and in accordance with standard guidelines [15]. Two blood pressure measurements were taken from one of the upper limbs, and the average of the systolic and diastolic pressures was used for analysis purposes, with adequate BPC defined as < 140/90 mmHg. [15–17] In addition, weight and height were taken to calculate BMI (< 22 kg/m2 [underweight], between 22 and 27 kg/m2 [eutrophic], and > 27 kg/m2 [overweight]) [18] and were used as anthropometric measurements.

Identification of Polymorphisms

First, aliquots of whole blood were subjected to DNA extraction using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini kit (QIAGEN®) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was then amplified using Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) protocols specific to each polymorphism. All reagents were standardized, and each reaction contained 2 µl of DNA in a final volume of 25 µl. [19]. A negative control, consisting of all reagents without DNA, was included to ensure reliability and monitor for contamination. Statistical Analysis

After genotyping, each volunteer was classified according to the allele frequency for each studied gene. A chi-square test (χ2) was used to determine whether the observed genotype frequencies conformed to Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium [25], following the expected genotype distribution for this population and accounting for its miscegenation [20]. Normal distribution was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test.

Volunteers were grouped based on the genotype or allele associated with an increased risk of hypertension. For the M235T volunteers were stratified as MM vs. TT/MT, [21] for the G2646A as GG vs. AG/AA, [22] for ACE InDel as DD vs. ID/II, [7] for A1166C as AA vs. AC/CC, [23] and for C344T as CC vs. CT/TT [24].

Chi-square test was used to identify differences (significance level set at p <0.05) in the proportions distributed between groups (i.e., Adequate x inadequate BPC) of each predictor variables (i.e., gender, race, marital status, BMI, waist circumference [25], smoking habits [current and ex-smokers], level of physical activity [26], estimated sodium intake [27], antihypertensive medication, biochemical data [fasting glucose [28], creatinine [29], HDL-C, LDL-C [30], genotypes).

A Poisson regression with robust variance was applied to calculate the prevalence ratio (PR) and its 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for adequate and inadequate BPC among the studied participants. Poisson regression was applied twice for each gene, the first including only genotype and BPC, and the second, adjusting for the variables that presented a p < 0.1 in the bivariate analyses [31], as well as estimated daily sodium intake and use of antihypertensive drugs, based on their biological plausibility.

Following the procedure described, a Poisson multivariate regression analysis was conducted using the "Backward LR" method and robust variance, including all variables that reached a p-value of ≤ 0.1 in the bivariate analysis [31]. All described procedures were conducted using the statistical package STATA® (StataCorp LP, version 15.0).

The G*POWER software was used to evaluate whether we could find a meaningful reliability in our results from the Poisson regression through a power analysis procedure [7, 32]. We performed a post hoc power analysis to compute statistical power (1-β) using the sample sizes and achieved PRs obtained in the Poisson multivariate regression model [7, 31] and with a base rate exp (β0) of 0.5 and an α level of 0.05 [33].

Results

A total of 143 older adults were assessed, mean age of 73.4±7.8 years, predominantly women (64.3%), without a partner (52.4%), non-white skin color (88.1%), no formal schooling (51.0%), and a monthly income of ≤ 1 Brazilian minimum wage (84.4%).

The prevalence of inadequate BPC was 51.8%. In terms of health-related aspects, participants with inadequate BPC, 64.9% were sufficiently active, 70.3% reported as smokers (current or ex-smokers), 36.5% were diabetics, 22.9% reported a daily sodium intake > 2.3g/day, 58.1% reported using at least one antihypertensive drug, 70.0% were classified as overweight/obese and 51.9% exhibited a normal waist circumference. Regarding biochemical profile, 93.8% those with inadequate BPC exhibited normal levels of creatinine, and 67.7% normal fasting glucose, while 86.2% exhibited reduced HDL-C levels and 69.2% elevated LDL-C. The power analysis, with an α of 0.05 and a sample size of 143, indicated power of 0.95, 0.96, and 0.99, for A allele of the REN gene (G2646A), Genotype II of the ACE gene (InDel), and LDL-C, respectively. Then, based on our results, there was a 95%, 96%, and 99% chance of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis that carrying the A allele of the REN gene (G2646A), Genotype II of the ACE gene (InDel), and elevated LDL-C are not associated with poor BPC in hypertensive older adults.

Discussion

Our main finding in this study was that LDL-C, the A allele of the REN gene (G2646A), and genotype II of the ACE gene (InDel) were significantly associated with inadequate BPC among the community-dwelling hypertensive older adults.

BPC is regulated by different factors, including biological (i.e., non-modifiable), such as genetic factors and biochemical data, or environmental/behavioral (i.e., modifiable factors), such as sociodemographic and lifestyle variables. Thus, multivariate approaches appear to be the most suitable for evaluating the impact of these different factors on BPC.

In our bivariate analysis, smoking habits (smoker or ex-smoker) and LDL-C were associated with BPC. Also, in the final multivariate model, LDL-C remained as a predictor. Hypertension and dyslipidemia are well-known risk factors for cardiovascular disease (CVD). In patients with these conditions, adequate BPC and LDL-C values below pre-specified cut-offs can prevent cardiovascular events and improve prognosis [34, 35].

Previous studies have demonstrated the interaction between inadequate BP and elevated LDL-C. A randomized clinical trial involving 4,731 patients with recent stroke or transient ischemic attack demonstrated a cumulative effect of achieving optimal levels of LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides, and BP on the risk of recurrent stroke and major cardiovascular events [36]. Another study involving 2,380 patients treated for hypertension indicated that patients who did not reach treatment goals for both BP and LDL had a substantially increased risk for all-cause mortality [35].

Besides the "traditional" cardiovascular risk factors, such as those identified decades ago by the Framingham Heart Study [37], it is now possible to estimate the genetic risk of developing cardiovascular diseases, owing to the method known as genome-wide association studies [38, 39], including high blood pressure [40]. Despite over 1500 single nucleotide polymorphisms being associated with blood pressure through genome-wide association studies, they only explain about 27% of blood pressure heritability [41] and given the complexity of its homeostasis, it is likely that genes that influence different systems play a role in its control (i.e., BPC), which have been demonstrated for gene polymorphisms of the RAAS elements [10, 42, 43].

The immutability of the genetic background enables most studies testing this association to be conducted in healthy young individuals. Notwithstanding, when assessing genetic risk in older adults, certain aspects should be considered since the aging process impacts the efficiency of organic systems, which explains the need to include the environmental and lifestyle variables for investigating the relationship between genetic background and cardiovascular outcomes in older adults.

Due to the previously mentioned multifactorial characteristic of the phenotype BPC, which encompasses various genes, it was expected to observe a polygenic profile in the final model proposed here, as supported by the literature [39, 44, 45].

The results from Freire and collaborators (2018) [7] highlight this issue, contradicting most previous studies that indicated the D allele of the ACE (InDel) gene polymorphism as being associated with impaired BPC or hypertension in young people. The cited study did not confirm this association in older adults and, in addition, also indicated a hypothesis that II genotype could be associated with impaired BPC in older adults with high daily sodium ingestion.

The ACE InDel polymorphism, particularly the II genotype, has been associated with lower circulating levels of ACE [46]. The D allele is associated with increased ACE activity and higher levels of angiotensin II. Paradoxically, the II genotype has been implicated in altered vascular remodeling and endothelial dysfunction [47]. The lower ACE levels in individuals with the II genotype may lead to impaired compensatory mechanisms in response to hypertensive stress, thereby reducing their ability to effectively regulate blood pressure, especially in older adults with pre-existing vascular aging and stiffness.

Despite methodological differences, the A allele of the MBO I REN polymorphism has been identified as a risk factor for the onset of hypertension [12, 48]. The results of a case-control study conducted in China with 3090 participants indicate that, compared to the GG genotype, the GA (OR = 1.97 [95% CI 1.63–2.38]) and AA (OR = 3.63 [95% CI 1.96–6.71]) genotypes were statistically associated with an increased risk of hypertension [22]. In another study with 1975 participants, the AA/AG genotypes were significantly associated with an increased risk of HT in female subjects (OR = 1.49 [95% CI 1.07–2.09]). Additionally, these genotypes were linked to higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure levels in postmenopausal women with hypertension [49].

The G2646A polymorphism in the REN gene, which encodes renin, may influence hypertension by modulating renin synthesis and activity. The presence of the A allele has been associated with increased renin levels, resulting in higher angiotensin I production [48]. This, in turn, results in enhanced angiotensin II formation, a potent vasoconstrictor that contributes to elevated blood pressure and impaired BPC [50]. This mechanism may explain the association between the A allele and poor BPC in our study population.

This study assessed older adults with a self-reported medical diagnosis of hypertension. However, it was possible to see that among those who self-reported as hypertensive, some did not use any hypertensive drug class. This finding reinforces one of the two main factors for inadequate control of hypertension, non-adherence to drug therapy and self-medication, especially in the older adult population [51–53].

Patients with chronic diseases, such as hypertension, are highly prone to self-medicating [54]. This practice is usually the result of recommendations from friends, neighbors, and family members and is facilitated by the low cost of medication. However, medication treatments are individualized, and the use of therapies without a medical prescription can lead to inadequate BPC [55]. In contrast, even in cases where a medical prescription is present, ongoing treatments often suffer from low adherence, which also results in inadequate BPC [56–58].

Some methodological limitations are inherent to cross-sectional studies, most notably the inability to establish causal relationships. This limitation may lead to an overestimation or underestimation of associations between variables, as temporal sequencing cannot be inferred. Another potential limitation is the use of self-reported hypertension diagnoses, which could introduce reporting bias. Although self-reports are prone to inaccuracies, we mitigated this by cross-checking the diagnosis against medical records and corroborating it with data on antihypertensive drug usage. While this approach reduces the likelihood of misclassification bias, a potential residual bias remains, particularly if participants have incomplete or inaccurate records. Furthermore, despite the calculation made to assess the adequacy of the sample's power, potential selection bias may still influence the findings due to the reduced number of participants, suggesting that these results may inform future studies in larger populations and controlled studies. Despite these limitations, it is important to emphasize that identifying risk profiles is crucial for targeting health interventions, which can ultimately improve health outcomes, particularly for older adults, a rapidly growing segment of the global population.

Based on our findings, it was possible to demonstrate that genetic and behavioral factors are associated with inadequate BPC in hypertensive older adults. Genetic profiling for hypertensive patients holds significant potential in personalized medicine. It can help identify individuals at higher genetic risk for hypertension, allowing for early detection and targeted interventions. Genetic markers may also predict how patients respond to specific antihypertensive medications, leading to more effective treatment plans. Additionally, profiling enhances the understanding of BPC, aids in predicting disease progression and complications, and improves risk stratification for better management. Pharmacogenomics can optimize drug dosing, while considering gene-environment interactions can help in developing more comprehensive treatment strategies. Although promising, the clinical application of genetic testing for hypertension still requires further research and validation.

Conclusions

Owing to their immutable nature, genetic background can indicate individuals at greater or less risk of inadequate BPC. Understanding the genetic profile associated with inadequate BPC, along with other modifiable factors, has the potential to predict and even direct more effective behaviors or treatments that can mitigate mortality and morbidity related to inadequate BPC in hypertensive older adults.
