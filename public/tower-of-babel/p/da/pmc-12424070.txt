Introduction

UCF represents a rare yet clinically significant complication characterized by an abnormal connection between the uterus and the skin, most commonly following cesarean deliveries or other pelvic surgeries [1,2]. Although infrequent, this condition can lead to prolonged morbidity if not identified and managed appropriately. The estimated incidence of UCF is reported to occur in a minor percentage of cesarean deliveries, ranging between 0.1 % and 1 %, depending upon various clinical factors [3,4].

Patients diagnosed with UCF frequently present with discharge from the surgical site, with a subset of cases reporting cyclical bleeding due to the communication with the uterine cavity [5]. The establishment of a diagnosis requires a high degree of clinical suspicion, particularly in individuals exhibiting non-healing cesarean wounds and symptoms suggestive of sinus tract formation. Factors such as surgical site infection, inadequate wound healing, or pre-existing medical conditions may predispose patients susceptible to this complication. The application of imaging techniques and comprehensive wound exploration is crucial for precise diagnosis, while definitive treatment typically involves surgical resection of the fistulous tract. But more radical procedures, such as total abdominal hysterectomy, may be required to achieve complete resolution in more severe or recurrent cases [6].

Considering the rarity of UCF, it is crucial for healthcare practitioners to maintain a high index of suspicion following lower segment cesarean sections (LSCS). In accordance with SCARE guidelines [7], this case report highlights a rare occurrence of UCF following LSCS, indicating the importance of early identification appropriate management strategies to enhance patient outcomes.

Case presentation

A 22-year-old female presented to the Out Patient Department with a chronic wound at the site of a previous LSCS. Six months prior, she had undergone an elective LSCS at term for cephalopelvic disproportion. She had no prior surgical or medical history and no known comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, or anemia. Her nutritional status was adequate, with a normal body mass index. Approximately four weeks postoperatively, she developed localized pain and watery discharge from the surgical site, initially managed conservatively as a superficial surgical site infection, but over time, the patient developed a persistent sinus tract at the incision site, associated with watery discharge and pain. Following the cesarean section, the patient noticed the wound failing to heal properly. Approximately one-month post-surgery, she developed pain and watery discharge from the incision site. Despite conservative treatment for the infection, including wound care, the discharge persisted. Over the next several months, the wound continued to cause discomfort, and no significant healing was observed, leading the patient to seek further medical evaluation.

On presentation, her vital signs were stable. On physical examination, the patient appeared to be in fair general condition, with no active discharge observed from the wound dressing at the time of presentation. The sinus tract was visible at the LSCS scar, though the patient was not in acute distress. A full motor and sensory examination revealed no abnormalities, and the patient's vital signs were stable. Investigations were carried out, including a total leukocyte count, which was found to be 6200 cells/mm3, indicating no active infection. HIV and other serological tests were negative. A wound swab culture grew coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, and radiological imaging of the abdomen and pelvis revealed a sinus tract extending from the anterior abdominal wall without intra-abdominal collection. There were no additional findings apart from the sinus tract.

The patient underwent exploratory laparotomy with excision of the sinus tract. Intraoperatively, the fistula tract was identified as a 10 cm long fibrous tunnel lined with granulation tissue, extending from the anterior wall of the uterus to the overlying skin at the cesarean section scar (Fig. 1). Multiple branching tracts surrounded the main channel, consistent with chronic inflammatory remodeling. The fistula exhibited a mature epithelialized internal wall and a well-formed external opening, fulfilling classical anatomical criteria for acquired uterocutaneous fistula.

There was no significant discharge during the surgery. The sinus tract and fistula were successfully removed (Fig. 2).

Post-excision and repair of the uterocutaneous fistula during the surgical procedure.

Postoperatively, the patient's recovery was uneventful. She tolerated oral intake well, and her pain was managed with routine analgesics. The wound was clean and showed no signs of infection. She was discharged with instructions for routine wound care, including dressing changes every other day, and follow-up.

Discussion

UCF is an uncommon but serious complication following LSCS, which is frequently associated with SSIs [8]. Symptoms typically include surgical site infection, fever, and elevated inflammatory markers [9]. Diagnosis can be achieved through various imaging techniques, including hystero-salpingo contrast sonography, 2D/3D ultrasound, CT fistulography, and MRI [10]. Hysteroscopy can also be useful in visualizing the fistula tract. Treatment approaches vary, with some cases managed through surgical tract excision without hysterectomy, while others employ a combination of surgery and medical therapy, such as gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists [11]. In this case, the patient's initial infection was managed conservatively, but persistent symptoms, including non-healing wounds and a sinus tract with watery discharge, suggested a more serious underlying issue. About 3 % to 5 % of cases result in SSI following cesarean sections, and the risk is increased by conditions like obesity, diabetes, and inadequate wound care [12,13]. In this case, the development of a chronic, non-healing wound at the LSCS site was initially attributed to a postoperative SSI. Despite initial conservative management, the persistent sinus tract formation and watery discharge raised the suspicion of a more severe underlying pathology, which was confirmed as a UCF during exploratory laparotomy.

The pathophysiology of UCF usually consists of tissue necrosis, infection, and inflammation that prevent wounds from healing leading to erosion of tissue planes and resulting in abnormal connections between the skin and the uterus [4]. In this patient, these processes may have resulted in a 10 cm fistula with multiple branching sinus tracts. While infection appears to be the most immediate trigger, other contributing factors likely played a role in this case. Coagulase-negative Staphylococcus isolated from the wound culture is known to be associated with chronic wound colonization. Additionally, while the patient was not obese and had no diagnosed comorbidities, subclinical factors such as postoperative nutritional deficiency, wound tension, and delayed healing may have led to persistent inflammation and eventual fistula formation. Due to is rarity and non-specific symptoms, UCF can be challenging to diagnose, which often can be confused with other postoperative complications [6]. The typical presentations are persistent wound discharge, pain, and non-healing surgical sites, as seen in this case [14]. Imaging and wound cultures, which in this case grew coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, can help in confirming the diagnosis.

As the conservative approaches such as wound care and antibiotics are insufficient, the definitive treatment for UCF is surgical, which involves excision of the fistula tract, combined with debridement of necrotic tissue [2]. In this case, exploratory laparotomy with complete excision of the fistulous tract resulted in a successful outcome, with no postoperative complications. Surgical management should be considered early, especially when there is evidence of persistent sinus tract formation or infection.

Prevention of uterocutaneous fistula relies on adherence to meticulous surgical technique, effective postoperative wound management, and timely intervention in cases of infection or wound dehiscence. In patients who present with delayed wound healing, persistent discharge, or sinus tract formation following cesarean delivery, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for fistula formation. Early diagnostic imaging and prompt surgical assessment are essential to minimize complications and enhance long-term clinical outcomes [5]. In cases of non-healing wounds following cesarean sections, healthcare providers should consider the possibility of fistula formation, especially if there is persistent discharge or failure of conservative treatment. Surgical intervention is the mainstay for treatment, with postoperative care playing a vital role in ensuring successful recovery.

Conclusion

Despite being uncommon, UCF is a serious complication that can occur following cesarean sections, especially when there is SSIs. The rarity of this condition makes diagnosis challenging, as it is often overshadowed by more common postoperative complications. Early recognition is crucial, as timely surgical management can prevent further morbidity and improve patient outcomes. Healthcare providers should maintain a high index of suspicion in patients with non-healing cesarean wounds, especially those with persistent discharge or sinus tract formation.
