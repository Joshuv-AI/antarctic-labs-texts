NIPT-based prenatal screening of maternal Xq28 copy number variations in a cohort of 80,371 pregnancies
Authors: Lulu Meng, Yiyun Xu, Yixuan Liang, Ran Zhou, Jingyu Zhao, Haiqin Huo, Qinxin Zhang, Jie Gao, Tingting Liu, Min Dai, Peina Du, Yan Wang, Ping Hu, Zhengfeng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCopy number variation (CNVs) can result in various genomic diseases and variable clinical phenotypes. This study aimed to assess the feasibility and reliability of noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT) for prenatal screening of maternal copy number variation (CNVs) involving the Xq28 recurrent region.MethodsIn this retrospective, single-center study, we analyzed the NIPT data of 80,371 pregnant women to detect the maternal CNVs from 2017 to 2022. Maternal CNVs involving the int22h1/int22h2-mediated chromosome Xq28 recurrent region detected by NIPT were focused on. Chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) was then performed to validate NIPT results if the remaining maternal lymphocytes were available.ResultsA total of 48 (0.060%, 95% CI 0.045% ~ 0.079%) maternal CNVs involving int22h1/int22h2-mediated chromosome Xq28 recurrent region were identified by NIPT, including 29 (0.036%, 95% CI 0.025% ~ 0.052%) pathogenic deletions sizing from 128.1 kb to 93,244.3 kb, 17 (0.021%, 95% CI 0.013% ~ 0.034%) pathogenic duplications sizing from 442.5 kb to 2059.1 kb and two (0.002%, 95% CI 0.000% ~ 0.009%) variants of uncertain significance (VOUS) sizing from 215.2 kb to 326.1 kb. The detection rates of typical Xq28 recurrent microdeletions and microduplications were 0.005% (4/80,371, 95% CI 0.002% ~ 0.013%) and 0.006% (5/80,371, 95% CI 0.003% ~ 0.015%), respectively. Chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) was performed to validate NIPT results in 22 cases for which remaining maternal lymphocytes were available. The positive predictive value (PPV) of NIPT for maternal CNV’s detection in the 22 cases was 100.0% (95% CI 85.1% ~ 100.0%).ConclusionsThis study illustrated the feasibility and potential of NIPT to detect maternal CNVs involving the Xq28 recurrent region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00404-026-08482-4.Keywords: Noninvasive prenatal testing, Maternal copy number variant, Chromosome Xq28 duplication syndrome, Monosomy Xq28, X-linked genetic diseases

What does this study add to the clinical work

Int22h1/int22h2-mediated Xq28 deletions and duplications are associated with X-linked recessive embryonic/fetal lethality (in males) and chromosome Xq28 duplication syndrome, respectively. However, no population-based studies were performed, and the prevalence of int22h1/int22h2-mediated Xq28 deletions and duplications remains unexplored. Additionally, int22h1/int22h2-mediated Xq28 deletions and duplications are beyond the scope of the genome-wide fetal-CNV reporting framework of NIPT. Therefore, identification of maternal Xq28 CNVs by analyzing NIPT data would be a meaningful approach for prenatal screening and can provide useful information for reproductive decision making.

Introduction

The last decade has seen rapid advances in NIPT. Because of the robust performance characteristics of NIPT, it has been widely applied for prenatal screening of fetal trisomy 21, 18 and 13 [1]. With an increase in sequencing depth and improvement in bioinformatics analysis, the application of NIPT utilizing next-generation sequencing was expanded to detect sex chromosomal aneuploidies (SCAs), rare autosomal aneuploidies (RAAs), fetal CNVs and even single-gene diseases [2, 3]. However, it is notable that the PPVs of NIPT for SCAs, RAAs and CNVs were lower than those for common aneuploidies that may increase the anxiety of pregnant women and lead to unnecessary invasive prenatal diagnosis [4].

CNVs can result in various genomic diseases and variable clinical phenotypes. If offspring inherited maternal pathogenic variations, they can be symptomatic and harmful. Although NIPT is mainly applied to detect fetal aneuploidies, growing evidence exhibited that maternal chromosome aberrations can also be identified by NIPT, since more than 80% of cell-free DNA consists of maternal DNA fragment [5–7]. Brison et al. detected 16 maternal CNVs in DMD by analyzing NIPT data [8]. Duan et al. demonstrated that expanded NIPT can accurately evaluate maternal CNVs larger than 2 Mb [9].

For most X-linked disorders, females do not manifest as severe clinical features as males [10]. Females harboring X-linked deleterious variants may sometimes be ignored. The Xq28 recurrent region is a ~ 0.5 Mb region located on chromosome Xq28 and flanked by intron 22 homologous regions 1 and 2 (int22h1/2). Int22h1/int22h2-mediated Xq28 deletions and duplications are associated with X-linked recessive embryonic/fetal lethality (in males) and chromosome Xq28 duplication syndrome (OMIM # 300,815), respectively [11, 12]. The severity of phenotypes differs significantly by gender due to skewed X-inactivation in females. Females carrying int22h1/int22h2-mediated Xq28 deletions and duplications were reported to be unaffected or to have milder phenotypes [12, 13].

To date, a total of 34 living males have been reported with chromosome Xq28 duplication syndrome [11]. Most Xq28 recurrent CNVs were maternally inherited [4, 15]. However, no population-based studies were performed, and the prevalence of int22h1/int22h2-mediated Xq28 deletions and duplications remains unexplored. In addition, int22h1/int22h2-mediated Xq28 deletions and duplications are beyond the scope of the genome-wide fetal-CNV reporting framework of NIPT [16]. Therefore, if maternal Xq28 CNVs can be suggested when conducting prenatal screening, more useful information would be provided.

Up to now, no large cohort study was conducted to evaluate the efficacy of NIPT for maternal Xq28 recurrent CNV detection. Limited data have been reported to describe the value of NIPT for identification of maternal X-linked disorders [17, 18]. In this study, we reanalyzed NIPT data in a large cohort of 80,371 individuals to evaluate the feasibility and accuracy of NIPT to identify maternal CNVs involving the Xq28 recurrent region using a self-developed algorithm. In addition, this study exhibited the carrier rate as well as the spectrum of CNVs involving the Xq28 recurrent region.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

The flowchart of this study is shown in Fig. 1. Clinical information and sequencing data of 80,371 pregnant women who underwent NIPT in the Women’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University from 2017 to 2022 were retrospectively collected. The median maternal age of our cohort is 29 (range, 24 ~ 42). All the participants signed the written informed consent when pre-test counseling was provided by professional counselors. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanjing Women and Children’s Healthcare Hospital (2021KY-069).

NIPT procedures

About 5 ~ 10 ml maternal peripheral blood samples were collected using cell-free DNA blood collection tubes. The dual centrifugation method was performed to purify plasma from whole blood [19]. Cell-free DNA was then extracted from 0.6 ml of plasma according to the instruction of the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen Benelux B.V., Netherlands). The procedure of library construction mainly included end-repairing, adaptor ligation, and purification. The Illumina HiSeq2000 and BGISEQ platform were used for DNA sequencing.

All 80,371 NIPT data were reanalyzed using a locally developed algorithm, as previously described [18], to detect maternal CNVs larger than 100 kb. Briefly, sequencing reads were first filtered to remove duplicated reads and reads with uncertain genomic alignment, following the original analytical pipeline. A control reference set was constructed using whole genome sequencing data from 30 normal women. Genome-wide CNV detection was performed using a sliding window approach with a window size of 50 k band a step size of 5 kb. For each window, the read-depth ratio between each sample and the control set was calculated. This normalization against the control population allows for the identification of relative copy number changes by comparing read-depth signals across samples. Candidate CNV regions were defined based on consecutive windows showing consistent deviation from the expected copy ratio. Deletions with an average copy ratio ≤ 0.8 and duplications with an average copy ratio ≥ 1.2 were identified according to predefined thresholds, consistent with the previously published method [18]. Due to the window-based read-depth approach, the estimated CNV boundaries correspond to the affected genomic windows rather than precise nucleotide-level breakpoints. In this study, we mainly focused on CNVs involving the Xq28 recurrent region.

Data validation

Genomic DNA was isolated from maternal lymphocytes if available. CMA was performed taking maternal DNA as the sample to validate the reanalysis results of NIPT. CMA was conducted as the standard operating procedure of our laboratory as previously mentioned [20]. Chromosome analysis suite (ChAS) software was used for CMA data analysis. The results of CMA were compared with the reanalysis results of NIPT to determine the feasibility and accuracy of NIPT for maternal CNV detection.

Variant interpretation and classification

All the CNVs detected in this study were analyzed according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines [20]. In brief, public databases, such as PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), OMIM (https://omim.org/), ClinGen (https://www.clinicalgenome.org/), DECIPHER (https://www.deciphergenomics.org/), and DGV (http://dgv.tcag.ca/dgv/app/home), were searched for CNV scoring. CNVs were finally considered as pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), VOUS, likely benign or benign.

Results

Detection rate of maternal CNVs involving Xq28 recurrent region

All 80,371 NIPT data were successfully reanalyzed. A total of 49 maternal CNVs involving chromosome Xq28 were identified. Excluding one deletion that only partially overlapped the F8 gene rather than the Xq28 recurrent region, 48 maternal CNVs containing or overlapping the Xq28 recurrent region (int22h1/int22h2-flanked) were finally detected. The overall detection rate was 0.060% (95% CI 0.045% ~ 0.079%). NIPT reanalysis results of the 48 cases are listed in Supplementary Table 1, including 29 (0.036%, 95% CI 0.025% ~ 0.052%) deletions and 19 (0.023%, 95% CI 0.015% ~ 0.037%) duplications. All the deletions were classified as pathogenic. Among the 19 duplications, 17 were interpreted as pathogenic, and the remaining two were VOUS. As shown in Table 1, the detection rates of typical Xq28 recurrent microdeletions and microduplications were 0.005% (95% CI 0.002% ~ 0.013%) and 0.006% (95% CI 0.003% ~ 0.015%), respectively.

Size and distribution of the 48 maternal CNVs detected by NIPT

The size of the 29 deletions detected ranged from 128.1 kb to 93,244.3 kb (median size, 1086.3 kb). Twenty-four of the deletions were microdeletions, and five were partial aneuploidies. The distribution of the 24 microdeletions is exhibited in Fig. 2a. The median size of the 24 microdeletions was 880.4 kb (range, 128.1 kb ~ 8216.5 kb), with four microdeletions partially overlapping Xq28 recurrent region, four only containing Xq28 recurrent region and 16 involving Xq28 recurrent region as well as other dosage sensitivity genes/regions.

Spectrum of maternal microdeletions and microduplications detected by NIPT. a Spectrum of maternal microdeletions detected by NIPT. b Spectrum of maternal microduplications detected by NIPT. ChrX chromosome X, P/LP pathogenic or likely pathogenic, VOUS variants of uncertain significance

The size of the 19 duplications ranged from 215.2 kb to 2059.1 kb (median size, 516.7 kb). The distribution of the 19 duplications is displayed in Fig. 2b. The 17 P/LP duplications were consisted of four duplications partially overlapping the Xq28 recurrent region, five duplications only containing the Xq28 recurrent region, and eight duplications involving the Xq28 recurrent region as well as other dosage sensitivity genes/regions. Both two cases of VOUS were partially overlapping Xq28 recurrent region and did not involve the coding region of the critical gene (RAB39B gene) of the Xq28 recurrent region.

Data validation by CMA

Of the 48 cases with maternal CNVs detected by NIPT, maternal lymphocytes of 22 individuals were accessed to validate the results of NIPT reanalysis, including 12 cases with maternal deletions and 10 cases with maternal duplication. Among the 12 cases of maternal deletions, ten were microdeletions with the minimum size being 128.1 kb, and two were partial aneuploidies with maximum size being 15.3 Mb. The minimum and maximum sizes of the 10 cases of maternal duplications were 442.5 kb and 2.1 Mb, respectively. Overall, all 22 maternal CNVs were successfully confirmed by CMA (Table 2). The PPV of NIPT was 100.0% (95% CI 85.1% ~ 100.0%) in the 22 cases available. According to the results of CMA, the size of deletion ranged from 78.8 kb to 15.7 Mb, and the size of duplication ranged from 445.9 kb to 2.8 Mb. The interpretation of CMA data of all 22 cases was consistent with that of NIPT.

Discussion

NIPT was traditionally applied to identify fetal trisomy 21, 18 and 13. Considering the technical principles of NIPT, it is theoretically feasible to detect maternal chromosomal anomalies with existing NIPT data without extra experiment. Therefore, we developed an algorithm to realize maternal CNV detection by analyzing NIPT data. Given that the majority of patients with P/LP autosomal CNVs have clinical features, NIPT does not apply to this group of people. Hence, we focused on maternal X chromosome CNVs that may cause no phenotype or mild phenotype in female carriers. In this study, 80,371 NIPT data were retrospectively reanalyzed to identify maternal CNVs involving the Int22h1/int22h2-mediated Xq28 recurrent region. Overall, 46 P/LP maternal CNVs (29 deletions and 17 duplications) were detected. The carrier rate of typical and atypical Xq28 recurrent CNVs was 0.011% (9/80,371) and 0.010% (8/80371) in this study. Limited cases with microdeletions involving the Xq28 recurrent region were reported, because this kind of deletion was thought to be lethal in male. Our results suggested that the carrier rate of CNVs containing the Xq28 recurrent region might be higher than expected. Xq28 duplication syndrome was considered to be an ultra-rare disorder with prevalence lower than 1/5,000 which is comparable to our results [11, 21]. The current study constitutes the first large study using NIPT to realize prenatal screening of maternal Xq28 copy number variations.

It is meaningful to distinguish maternal CNVs from fetal chromosomal anomalies. On one hand, it can prevent confounding maternal CNVs with fetal CNVs that can avoid unnecessary invasive prenatal diagnosis and reduce the psychological burden of pregnant women and their families. Researchers have found that maternal CNVs had impact on fetal aneuploidy interpretation when performing NIPT. For instance, Zhang et al. suggested that 27/41 of the cases with false-positive or false-negative NIPT results were resulted from maternal CNVs [22]. Another study demonstrated that maternal CNVs contributed to 8.1% (6/74) of the false-positive fetal aneuploidies identified by NIPT [23]. On the other hand, distinguishing maternal CNVs from fetal chromosomal anomalies can help identify high-risk pregnant women harboring P/LP chromosome aberrations that can be harmful to offspring if inherited. In most cases, maternal CNVs were the incidental finding when using NIPT for prenatal screening [24, 25]. Brison et al. applied NIPT to profile maternal CNVs larger than 500 kb by SeqCBS analysis. In this single-center retrospective study, we successfully identified 48 maternal Xq28 CNVs by reanalyzing NIPT data and demonstrated the feasibility of NIPT for maternal CNV detection. These results expanded the clinical application scenario of NIPT and provided a new strategy for prenatal screening.

Few cases with deletion of the Xq28 int22h1/int22h2-flanked region were reported. It has been proposed that int22h1/int22h2-mediated Xq28 deletion can cause embryonic/fetal lethality in males, because female carriers were observed to undergo spontaneous abortions, and no male carrier has been reported [12, 13]. Because female carriers always have normal intelligence and have no severe clinical features, this kind of CNV can easily be ignored and lead to an adverse pregnancy outcome. With NIPT used for maternal CNV detection, more genetic information can be provided without extra investment. Up to date, which gene is associated with male embryonic lethality is still under debate. In this study, four typical microdeletions of the whole int22h1/int22h2-flanked region were identified in 80,371 pregnant women. There is a 1/4 probability that the offspring will be a female carrier, and also a 1/4 probability of spontaneous abortions or fetal death.

The int22h1/int22h2-mediated Xq28 duplication is one of the causes of X-linked intellectual disability (XLID). Other manifestations include sinopulmonary issues, atopic disease, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and so on. A recent study observed four unaffected male carriers and reported incomplete penetrance in males with int22h1/int22h2-mediated Xq28 duplication [14]. Skewed X-inactivation and incomplete penetrance made it more difficult to establish the diagnosis. It is hard to identify asymptomatic carriers if no proband was diagnosed in the family. In this study, the NIPT data were retrospectively reanalyzed. Thus, invasive prenatal diagnosis was not performed in patients who were diagnosed as carriers. We would accumulate more data to evaluate whether it is feasible and robust to integrate maternal CNV analysis into routine NIPT in the future.

Actually, the strategy of NIPT for maternal CNVs detection has been proposed in several previous reports [8, 18]. These studies exhibited the feasibility and reliability of NIPT for maternal CNVs detection. Tang et al. evaluated 13,156 pregnant women using NIPT and confirmed 19 deletions in Xp22.31 covering STS, with size ranging from 0.61 to 1.77 Mb [17]. Wang et al. assessed the efficacy of using NIPT to identify maternal CNVs in the DMD gene and revealed the PPV being 72.32% [18]. In this study, unselected 22 maternal CNVs involving the Xq28 recurrent region detected by NIPT were all proved to be true positive using CMA. Our results demonstrated the feasibility of NIPT for maternal CNVs detection again. As shown in this study, there were also some VOUS that would be detected by NIPT that may bring challenges to genetic counselors. The economic benefits also should be further evaluated.

To our knowledge, this study is by far the largest study focusing on using NIPT to detect maternal CNVs involving the Xq28 recurrent region. However, there were also some limitations. One limitation was that not all the cases with positive NIPT results were available to be validated by CMA. Another limitation was that we did not conduct follow-up. Last but not least, as a retrospective study, follow-up was not performed. In addition, phenotype information and family study were absent. If possible, a prospective study should be further performed to better evaluate the value of NIPT for detection of maternal CNVs.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we applied NIPT to realize prenatal screening of maternal CNVs in this retrospective, single-center study. Our results illustrated the feasibility of NIPT to detect maternal CNVs involving the int22h1/int22h2-mediated Xq28 recurrent region. Our findings suggested the potential value of NIPT for prenatal screening of maternal CNVs involving the Xq28 recurrent region.
