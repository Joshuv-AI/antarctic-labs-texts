Surplus boron nutrition enhances accumulation in plant residues, canopy radiation interception and yield of winter direct seeding rapeseed in Southwestern China
Authors: Rui Wang, Wenli Peng, Hui Teng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Boron (B) plays a critical role in the yield formation of direct-seeded winter rapeseed (Brassica napus L.). However, current management of this nutrient remains suboptimal. We conducted a split-plot field experiment in Bijiang County, Guizhou, China, between 2020 and 2022 to address this issue. The study evaluated how different B application rates (0, 3.75, 7.5, 15, and 30 kg boron ha−1) influenced yield formation, canopy radiation interception, and B allocation in two cultivars: the hybrid Huayouza 9 and the conventional Zhongshuang 11. Our results demonstrated that an application rate of 7.5–15 kg B ha−1 led to a 28–39% increase in seed yield. When application rates exceeded 15 kg B ha−1, 70.5–86.3% of the absorbed B accumulated in the stems and pericarps, which caused a 17% reduction in the B harvest index. The hybrid cultivar HZ9 demonstrated a superior remobilization efficiency that was 12.7–15.5% higher than ZS11 at the same input levels. A strong correlation was observed between canopy radiation interception during flowering and yield, explaining 82% of the variation. While optimal B rates improved interception by 18.3%, excessive B levels delayed senescence, which decreased pod-stage radiation capture by 8.3–11.7%. These findings indicate that returning plant residues to the soil could be an effective strategy to help maintain soil B levels in Southwest China and other B-deficient regions.

Introduction

As one of the world’s most important sources of vegetable oil, rapeseed also holds significant promise as a biofuel crop1,2. China is a major producer of rapeseed, contributing 19.7% of the global total2. Nevertheless, the country faces a critical yield gap, as its national average of 1.65 t ha−1 lags behind the global mean of 1.92 t ha−13. This problem is particularly acute in the boron-deficient regions of the southwest3. It is therefore crucial to increase rapeseed yield to meet national demands for edible oil3. While optimized fertilization strategies are known to be key yield-enhancing measures4existing research has a disproportionate focus on macronutrients (N-P-K). This has created knowledge gaps regarding micronutrient management, especially for boron (B) nutrition in winter rapeseed5–11.

Boron’s function is twofold: it is a structural component of cell walls and also regulates reproductive development. These roles make it especially crucial for rapeseed, a crop that demands 30–50% more B than cereal crops6,8. Field surveys have indicated that 68% of rapeseed fields in China have suboptimal available B levels (< 0.3 mg kg−1)3. In southwestern regions, this deficiency is particularly acute, with levels ranging from 0.14 to 0.16 mg kg−113. The deficiency leads to a “flowering without fruiting” syndrome8, which suppresses meristem activity and flower development and causes yield losses of 20–40%12. It has been demonstrated that B fertilization can significantly boost both the number of pods per plant and the number of seeds in each pod12. Advances in canopy physiology have recently highlighted how B mediates improvements in photosynthate partitioning from the pericarp to the seed3,4,13. The critical functions of boron in reproductive development and carbohydrate transport are well-established6–8; however, the accurate B rate-response relationships that govern yield formation and utilization in modern direct-seeded winter rapeseed systems have not been defined.

Direct seeding offers advantages by reducing labor requirements and being compatible with fully mechanized rapeseed production, which has led to its increasing adoption across China’s major rapeseed cultivation areas9. The rapid spread of this technology (currently covering 62% of China’s rapeseed area)3 has introduced new nutritional challenges. Direct-seeded rapeseed has an 18–25% higher nutrient demand during early establishment compared to its transplanted counterparts14. This is combined with a reduced capacity for root exploration15,16. Such physiological constraints could worsen the effects of B deficiency on crop development and yield stability6,8. According to recent work12current B recommendations may underestimate the needs of direct-seeded systems by 30–40%, which could be limiting potential yield ceilings. This suggests that the B uptake characteristics of direct-sown rapeseed may be different and could require adjusted fertilizer management strategies compared to transplanted seedlings. However, existing B management guidelines do not adequately account for the nutrient demands of direct-seeded rapeseed9,15.

Canopy architecture and radiation use efficiency in oilseed crops are modulated by B availability11. An increase in canopy radiation interception is one way to improve winter rapeseed yield15,16. The photosynthetically active period of the rapeseed canopy is prolonged by greater sunlight interception17–19. The crop’s photosynthesis and dry matter accumulation are driven by its green leaves and pods, which are the primary photosynthetic organs in the rapeseed canopy15,19. Masood et al.6 put forward the hypothesis that B fertilization boosts crop canopy photosynthesis in winter rapeseed. However, there have been no systematic studies analyzing how B nutrition affects radiation interception dynamics at critical growth stages for direct-seeded rapeseed20,21. An evaluation of the relationship between canopy radiation interception and B application levels is therefore imperative for exploring the growth patterns and yield formation of winter rapeseed.

To bridge these knowledge gaps, this study utilized a two-year field experiment. We evaluated three key areas: (1) the relationships between various B application levels (0–30 kg ha−1) and the yield components of a hybrid (Huayouza 9) and a conventional (Zhongshuang 11) variety; (2) the B-mediated regulation of canopy radiation interception during critical growth stages; and (3) the tissue-specific B allocation dynamics and utilization efficiencies in direct-seeded winter rapeseed. We believe our findings could offer mechanistic insights into B nutrition dynamics. They might also help establish evidence-based management strategies for the sustainable intensification of direct-seeded rapeseed systems in B-deficient regions of southwestern China and in 80 other countries worldwide6,11.

Materials and methods

Site description

We carried out field experiments over the 2020-21 and 2021-22 growing seasons in Bijiang County, Guizhou Province, China (109°13′E, 27°47′N, 470 m elevation). The location has an average annual temperature of 15.6℃ and an average precipitation of 1260 mm across the two winter rapeseed seasons. The wintering periods were represented by relatively low average temperatures (6.3℃ in 2020-21 and 7.4℃ in 2021-22) and limited rainfall (31.0 mm and 36.3 mm, respectively). An analysis of the soil (0–20 cm depth) indicated the following nutrient concentrations for 2020: 61.45 mg kg−1 of alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen, 11.76 mg kg−1 of available phosphorus, 92.88 mg kg−1 of available potassium, and 0.14 mg kg−1 of available boron. In 2021, the corresponding values were 66.96, 16.16, 103.83, and 0.16 mg kg−1, respectively.

Experimental design

A replicated split-plot configuration was utilized for the experimental layout, which spanned two consecutive growth cycles and included three replications. Two rapeseed cultivars constituted the main plots: Huayouza 9 (HZ9), a popular hybrid, and Zhongshuang 11 (ZS11), a popular conventional variety. Both cultivars see widespread cultivation across the major winter rapeseed production regions of China, especially in the Southwest. The subplots featured five different B application rates: 0 (B0), 3.75 (B1), 7.5 (B2), 15 (B3), and 30 kg boron ha−1 (B4). To ensure nutrients other than B were not limiting crop growth, 180 kg N ha−1, 90 kg P2O5 ha−1, and 75 kg K2O ha−1 were mixed into the topsoil of each plot. Urea was the source for N, single superphosphate for P2O5, potassium chloride for K2O, and borax for B. P2O5, K2O, and B were all applied as basal fertilizer prior to sowing. The total N was split, with half applied as basal fertilizer and the other half applied on the winter solstice of each season (December 21, 2020, and December 21, 2021). A planting density of 30 plants m−2 was maintained with 20 cm row spacing, which is a high-yield configuration for winter rapeseed. Each plot was 10 m2measured 5 m long by 2 m wide, and held 10 rows of plants. All other field management practices followed standard local conventions.

Sampling and measurements

For measurements of individual plant-level parameters, we sampled ten representative plants from every plot one day before harvest. We rinsed the samples with deionized water, blotted them dry, and then separated them into their respective plant tissues (seed, pericarp, and stem). The dry weight of each plant tissue was recorded after enzyme deactivation at 105℃ for 30 min, followed by drying to a constant weight at 65℃. We then ground and sieved the dried seed, pericarp, and stem samples to prepare them for B content analysis. The Azomethine H-colorimetric method was utilized to measure B concentrations in the different plant tissues22. At maturity, we harvested and threshed all plants in each plot by hand to determine the rapeseed seed yield and total aboveground biomass. A SunScan Canopy Analysis System (Delta-T Devices Ltd., UK) was utilized to measure crop canopy radiation interception, following the methods described by Wang et al.16.

Parameter calculation

The boron uptake, boron use efficiency and boron harvest index (BHI) were calculated referring to the model of Rathke et al.15.

Boron uptake by seed (Bup, s, g ha−1) = seed yield × boron content of seed (1).

Boron uptake by pericarp (Bup, p, g ha−1) = pericarp yield × boron content of pericarp (2).

Boron uptake by stem (Bup, t, g ha−1) = stem yield × boron content of stem (3).

Boron uptake in aboveground (Bup, g ha−1) = Bup, s + Bup, t + Bup, p (4).

Boron harvest index (BHI, %) = Bup, s/Bup (5).

Reciprocal internal efficiency (RIE, g t−1) = (Bup/Y) × 1000 (6).

Internal use efficiency (IUE, kg g−1) = Y/Bup (7).

Agronomic efficiency (AE, kg g−1) = (YF –Y0)/F (8).

Physiological efficiency (PE, kg g−1) = (YF – Y0)/(Bup, F − Bup,0) (9).

Where F expresses the level of B application to rapeseed (kg ha−1), and Y denotes the seed yield (kg ha−1), YF and Y0 represent the seed yield in supplied B plot and in non-supplied one, respectively. Bup, F and Bup,0 indicate the boron uptake of the aboveground tissues of rapeseed plant each plot with and without B application, respectively.

Statistical analyses

To evaluate the main effects of B application levels and rapeseed cultivars on B uptake, radiation interception, yield, and yield components, along with their interaction, we performed an independent ANOVA. Growing season and block were treated as random effects. We further analyzed any significant effects through multiple comparisons utilizing the least significant difference (LSD) test at a significance level of P ≤ 0.05. SPSS Statistics 25.0 (SPSS, Armonk, NY, USA) was utilized for conducting both Pearson’s correlation analysis and ANOVA. All figures presented in this study were created with SigmaPlot software (SigmaPlot 10.0, United States).

Results

Yield response to Boron fertilization

Seed yield was significantly affected by the varying levels of B application and the different rapeseed cultivars across both experimental seasons (Table 1). In comparison to the control (B0, 0 kg B ha−1), applying B at levels from 3.75 to 30 kg B ha−1 resulted in seed yield increases of 17.2–38.5% in 2020-21 and 6.2–38.1% in 2021-22 (Fig. 1). The B-efficient cultivar, HZ9, produced its peak yields of 2367 kg ha−1 (2020-21) and 2513 kg ha−1 (2021-22) at the B3 level (15 kg B ha−1). These yields were 12.7–15.5% greater than the maximum yields achieved by ZS11 at the B4 level (p < 0.05). The use of quadratic models identified optimal economic application rates between 13.2 and 17.8 kg B ha−1. This finding demonstrates a potential to reduce application from the maximum rate (B4) by 21–41% without a yield penalty in direct-seeded systems.

Boron partitioning dynamics

Direct-seeded rapeseed demonstrated a preferential allocation of boron to the seeds, with cultivar HZ9 demonstrating superior partitioning efficiency (Figs. 2 and 3). For instance, HZ9 allocated 68.3% of its total B uptake (2.68 kg ha−1) to the seeds at the B3 level, an amount significantly higher than ZS11’s 61.7% (p < 0.05). A positive linear correlation exists between B accumulation in the aboveground biomass and the level of B application, as presented in Fig. 4. Between 38 and 43% of the absorbed B accumulated in non-seed organs such as the pericarp and stem at the B4 application rate (30 kg ha−1). Stem became the major sink for surplus B, particularly in ZS11 where stem Bup, m reached 24.6 kg ha−1 (vs. 18.3 kg ha−1 in HZ9). An associated 17% reduction in BHI at B4 relative to B3 (p < 0.05) corresponded with this imbalanced allocation.

Boron use efficiency

The ANOVA results indicated significant interactions between boron and cultivar (p < 0.01) for several key efficiency parameters (Table 1). While HZ9 displayed a 16.8% higher reciprocal internal efficiency at the B3 level, it also demonstrated a more rapid decline in internal use efficiency (from 15.2 to 6.7 kg g−1) than ZS11 (Table 2). Agronomic efficiency followed contrasting patterns between the cultivars, with HZ9 peaking at B1 (33.8%) whereas ZS11 peaked at B2 (40.2%). These differing efficiency dynamics highlight the fundamentally different strategies for B utilization that cultivars employ in direct-seeded systems.

Canopy radiation interception

Boron application led to an improvement in radiation interception during the reproductive stages, an effect that was particularly noticeable at flowering (Fig. 6). At the B3 application level, HZ9 achieved 92.4% interception, an increase from the 18.3% interception observed at B0. The B2 treatment also accelerated radiation interception during the seedling stage by 22–26% over the B0 control. However, the application of excessive B (B4) was detrimental, reducing pod-stage interception by 8.3–11.7% due to prolonged vegetative growth (Fig. 6). A quadratic relationship between the B application level and flowering-stage interception was found to underpin 82% of the total yield variation (Fig. 7). This result emphasizes that radiation capture during the flowering phase is the critical determinant for yield formation in direct-seeded systems.

Discussion

Surplus B supply level enhanced accumulation in non-seed tissues of rapeseed

Our observation of 6.2–38.5% yield improvements with B fertilization is consistent with recent reports that identify B deficiency as a limiting factor for rapeseed productivity in acidic soils3. However, these gains surpassed the 15–28% increases previously documented in transplanted systems9. The accelerated phenological development of direct-seeded rapeseed, which creates an intense demand for B during early reproductive transitions, is the likely cause of this heightened responsiveness19. While our identified optimal B rate of 13.2–17.8 kg ha−1 supports model predictions for direct-seeded systems in Southwest China12it also signifies a 32% increase compared to recommendations for transplanted systems3. This suggests a unique effect of varying B fertilizer rates on the yield of direct-seeded rapeseed, which is likely connected to the crop’s boron-intensive physiology and its high nutritional demand for various physiological processes. When B supply is low, the plant must direct its B uptake primarily toward essential physiological functions in an effort to balance minimum nutritional requirements with reproductive growth needs23. The B acquired in these limited conditions has to support basic functions while accumulating, as much as possible, the B needed for seed production20. This dynamic likely explains the slower increase in seed yield observed at low B application rates in our study; whereas, under an ample supply of B fertilizer, the plant’s B uptake surpasses what is required for general physiological functions. The resulting surplus B is then allocated preferentially to reproductive organs and seeds, which promotes rapid gains in seed yield.

These results also confirm previous reports which indicate that B utilization efficiency typically declines as B supply increases24,25. For current direct-sown rapeseed cultivation in Southwest China, a boron application rate between 7.5 and 15 kg ha−1 appears to be suitable. As we increased B application rates, the accumulation of B in seeds rose significantly at first then reached a plateau. With higher B application levels, more B accumulates in the pericarp and stem tissues, while its deposition in the seeds becomes limited due to the stabilization of B accumulation in reproductive organs (seeds) once a specific concentration is reached. It then prevents any further influx of B into the seeds. This suggests that excess B, once a certain threshold is crossed, is sequestered primarily in non-seed tissues. The preferential sequestration of B in the stems (24.6 kg ha−1 at B4) and pericarps (118.5 kg ha−1) indicates a novel nutrient buffering mechanism that is different from the luxury consumption of macronutrients26. In comparison to an over-accumulation of nitrogen mainly in the leaves5the vegetative storage of B in rapeseed aligns with recent findings of stem-specific BOR4 transporter up-regulation under conditions of high B supply21.

In our study, we observed B luxury consumption specifically in vegetative organs (non-seed tissues) instead of reproductive tissues. This may represent a stress avoidance mechanism related to winter rapeseed’s interaction with its environment. By storing surplus B in non-seed tissues, the plant may reduce the negative effects of excessive B in seeds on germination, seedling establishment, and growth under high-boron conditions. Previous research has presented that high concentrations of B can be detrimental to both crop growth and seed yield6. Across a range of crops, boron toxicity thresholds are known to occur between 10 and 130 mg B kg−1 of dry weight26. Such tissue-specific partitioning appears to serve a dual function. It prevents phytotoxic accumulation in the reproductive organs27 and also establishes legacy nutrient reserves that can be accessed through residue recycling, as stem tissues retained 28–33% of the applied B10. The 17% reduction in BHI at excessive application rates, however, highlights the metabolic cost of this strategy20a finding consistent with Shi et al.’s work on carbohydrate diversion to B detoxification pathways22. It should be acknowledged that even the highest B level in our experiment (30 kg ha−1) did not produce any observable toxicity in the rapeseed. This lack of toxicity might be attributed to the potentially higher B requirements of direct-sown winter rapeseed compared to other crops, which suggests the highest tested B level (30 kg ha−1) may not have reached the toxicity threshold for rapeseed seeds.

B application levels significantly affected canopy radiation interception during early growth and flowering-stage

Highlighting boron’s role in sustaining photosynthetic capacity during reproductive transitions17 radiation interception at the flowering stage explained 82% of the variation in yield (Fig. 7). Significant effects of B application levels on canopy radiation interception were evident during the early growth and flowering stages. Our results at the canopy scale extend the leaf-level observations of Masood et al.6 indicating that the B3 treatment led to an 18.3% improvement in radiation capture during flowering. This enhancement is similar to the 15–20% increases achieved through optimized nitrogen regimes5. In addition, a B-mediated acceleration of canopy closure during the seedling stage, marked by a 22–26% increase in interception at the B2 level, offers particular benefits to direct-seeded systems where early vigor is critical for yield potential11. Significant differences in radiation interception among B application levels first appeared at the seedling stage (Fig. 8). The restrictive effect that B deficiency imposes on early canopy development is closely tied to the accumulation of aboveground biomass in direct-sown rapeseed during its vegetative phase15. Our findings confirm that varied B application rates at the plant population level influenced canopy radiation interception in early growth, which created differences in above-ground biomass (Fig. 8). This relationship is further supported by the increased dry matter and greater B uptake we observed at higher application rates. Previous research has also established that adequate B nutrition can enhance leaf emergence rate, leaf number, and photosynthetic activity, boosting total dry matter production11,12. The differential responses in canopy radiation interception under varying B levels drive the development of the canopy’s photosynthetic area, which may be the final contributor to the observed differences in seed yield3,14. However, an optimal threshold for B appears necessary for source-sink balance, as excessive application (B4) induced vegetative prolongation and an 8.3% decline in pod-stage interception21. This phenomenon has not been previously reported in rapeseed systems8.

B absorption dynamics mechanistic model and its implications for direct-seeded rapeseed in B-deficient regions

This study demonstrates the critical role of B nutrition in rapeseed. Boron was presented to be important for the growth of seedlings, the capacity of the crop canopy to obtain light energy during the flowering stage, and the final yield formation. To clarify the relationship between B fertilization, rapeseed growth, and yield, we constructed a dynamic model of nutrient allocation (Fig. 8). The model was developed utilizing growth dynamics data from direct-seeded rapeseed under a wide range of boron fertilizer gradients (0–30 kg ha−1), supplemented by findings from existing literature. The effects of different B fertilizer supply levels on rapeseed growth can be categorized into four stages. At the first level, where the supply of boron fertilizer is very low, the applied boron is absorbed by the roots and is utilized to meet the basic physiological needs of the plant. This initial supply, which we term “physiological B absorption,” is essential for maintaining basic rapeseed growth. For both HZ9 and ZS11 cultivars in this experiment, fertilizer applications below 7.5 kg ha−1 corresponded to this physiological absorption stage. The second level begins when the boron fertilizer supply surpasses the plant’s basic physiological needs. Any surplus B beyond this point is allocated mainly to the production of reproductive organs and more seeds. Supplying B fertilizer at this level therefore has a positive effect on increasing rapeseed yield, a stage that can be termed “yield-increasing B absorption.” Fertilizer application rates between 7.5 kg ha−1 and 15 kg ha−1 in our experiment represented this yield-increasing absorption stage for B supply.

When the supply of B fertilizer surpasses the upper limit required for the second level, the third level of B absorption is reached. After meeting the demand for yield-increasing absorption of B (the second level), any surplus B is absorbed. This excess is mainly utilized for the surplus growth of nutrient organs, which produces larger nutrient organs and more assimilates for rapeseed plants. Since the supply of B fertilizer is sufficient at this level, the absorbed B elements are mainly stored in non-seed organs, a process that does not significantly increase seed yield. The utilization efficiency of B elements will, accordingly, further decrease. This supply of B fertilizer in the third level is defined as luxury B absorption in rapeseed, which in this experiment occurred at a B supply level of 15–30 kg ha−1. Exceeding the upper limit required for luxurious B absorption in the third level initiates the fourth level. Once excessive B absorption reaches the maximum amount that can be stored by the nutrient organs, damage to the growth of rapeseed plants can occur. The supply of B fertilizer in this fourth level is therefore called toxic B absorption. Finally, existing studies have reported this phenomenon of toxic B absorption.

The application of B as a base fertilizer produced two benefits. It enhanced radiation distribution and interception in the crop canopy during early development, and it also significantly improved the yield of direct-sown rapeseed. This yield improvement was most apparent at the first two levels of B absorption (physiological and yield-increasing). Moreover, higher application rates resulted in increased B concentrations in plant tissues such as the pericarp and stems (an effect corresponding to the third level known as luxury B absorption). This accumulation suggests that higher B application rates may enrich soil fertility through the eventual return of these plant residues. Based on this evidence, incorporating rapeseed plant residues back into the soil presents a viable strategy for maintaining soil fertility in B-deficient regions27. A full analysis into the long-term effects of B cycling under such residue retention practices is therefore a necessary direction for future research.

Conclusions

This study identifies an optimal B application range of 7.5–15 kg ha−1 for direct-seeded winter rapeseed in the B-deficient soils (0.14–0.16 mg kg−1) of Southwest China. Applying B in this range increased yields by 28–39%, primarily by enhancing photosynthate partitioning and improving flowering-stage canopy radiation interception by 18.3%. At application rates above 15 kg ha−1, a portion (70.5–86.3%) of the absorbed B accumulated in stems and pericarps, which reduced the harvest index by 17% but created an opportunity for nutrient recycling. The hybrid HZ9 demonstrated superior performance, outperforming the conventional ZS11 by 12.7–15.5% due to a higher remobilization efficiency, which highlights the need for genotype-specific management. Excessive B application delayed canopy senescence and caused an 8.3–11.7% reduction in pod-stage radiation capture, supporting the importance of maintaining a proper source-sink balance in direct-seeding systems. These findings report that the effective return of non-seed rapeseed tissues to the soil, achieving 62–68% B recovery, could offer a valuable strategy for sustaining B levels and overall soil fertility.
