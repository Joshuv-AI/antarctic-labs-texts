Rural–Urban Differences in Excessive Alcohol Use and Select Mental Health Conditions
Authors: Taryn Farrell, Cassie Odahowski, Emma Boswell, Christina Andrews, Peiyin Hung, Elizabeth Crouch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposePrevious research demonstrates that excessive alcohol use behaviors, alcohol‐related harms, and the prevalence of mental health conditions disproportionately impact rural communities, with mixed findings on rural–urban differences in behavior uptake. This study aims to examine rural–urban differences in the prevalence of excessive alcohol use among individuals with selected self‐reported mental health conditions.MethodsThe 2021 and 2022 National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH) data was used to obtain prevalence estimates for self‐reported past‐year any mental illness (AMI), lifetime depressive symptoms (DS), past‐month excessive alcohol use alone, and past‐month excessive alcohol use among individuals reporting either past year AMI or lifetime DS using survey‐weighted descriptive statistics. Bivariate associations and survey‐weighted multivariable logistic regression models were used to determine the odds of having the selected outcomes across levels of residence (large urban, small urban, and rural).FindingsAround half of all individuals reported past month excessive alcohol use; rates were slightly higher among small urban residents compared to rural and large urban residents. Prevalence for past year AMI, lifetime DS, and past month excessive alcohol use alone were highest among small urban residents. After adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics, no significant differences were observed by residence for AMI and excessive alcohol use or for DS and excessive alcohol use.ConclusionsThese results add to a limited availability of updated and generalizable research examining differences in self‐reported mental health conditions and excessive alcohol use behaviors across a comprehensive rural–urban continuum and can be used to inform targeted policy solutions and intervention strategies.Keywords: alcohol use, alcohol use disorder, depressive symptoms, mental illness, rural

Introduction

Excessive alcohol use continues to remain a critical public health concern as a leading cause of preventable death in the United States [1]. Prior research has demonstrated that excessive alcohol use, alcohol‐related harms, and mental health conditions are more prevalent in rural communities compared to urban counterparts [2, 3, 4]. Nationally, the mortality rate for deaths attributed to alcohol use increased by approximately 70% from 2012 to 2022 with the number of deaths broadly characterized as alcohol‐related deaths exceeding the number of deaths attributed to opioids and suicide [5, 6, 7]. There is evidence demonstrating polysubstance use, opioid use disorder, and other behavioral health conditions presenting independently or co‐morbidly, disproportionally impact rural communities and economically disadvantaged areas [8]. With deaths attributed to alcohol use rapidly increasing during the last two decades in the United States, it is imperative that population specific drivers of the uptake of excessive alcohol use behaviors be better understood [1].

Prior research on excessive alcohol use in rural areas is limited and has revealed mixed findings. There are a limited number of studies that have examined alcohol use behaviors and self‐reported mental health conditions both alone and together, across three levels of residence (urban, suburban, and rural). Findings from nationally representative studies ranging from 1997 through 2011 report that urban residents more likely to report past‐year alcohol use, excessive alcohol use, and lifetime alcohol use compared to rural residents [9, 10, 11]. However, a 2007 study reports rural residents more likely to report past‐year AUD, lifetime AUD, and excessive drinking compared to urban and suburban residents in the United States [11]. It is also reported that rural residents were also more likely to engage in excessive alcohol use behaviors if criteria for past‐month major depressive disorder were also met and that rates of abstinence were lowest among suburban residents compared to urban and rural residents (31.3% vs. 35.4% vs. 41.7%) [11, 12]. Recent evidence examining excessive alcohol use behaviors before and during the COVID‐19 pandemic have also yielded mixed findings regarding rural–urban differences in alcohol misuse with one state‐specific study noting rural residence was associated with lower odds of increased drinking behaviors during the pandemic. Both studies note a need to examine behavioral and social drivers as well as needs unique to rural communities to better understand pandemic and post‐pandemic alcohol use behaviors.

Examining excessive alcohol use behaviors and deaths attributed to alcohol use alone do not sufficiently capture the myriad drivers for the uptake of these behaviors and the subsequent impact on individual and population health [13]. Prior research has presented evidence of a strong relationship between excessive alcohol consumption and mental illness [14, 15, 16]. However, there is a significant research gap regarding the intersection of excessive alcohol use and mental illness as well as the prevalence estimates across levels of residence [14, 15, 16]. A study using data from 1990 to 1992 found that rural residents were not only more likely to report past‐month alcohol misuse or dependence if criteria for major depressive disorder were met in the past month but were also less likely to seek treatment than urban counterparts [12]. It has been posited that restricted access to integrated behavioral health services in rural communities and non‐Medicaid expansion states serves as a significant driver of variation in the prevalence of co‐occurring behavioral health conditions and the uptake of excessive alcohol use behaviors [17]. In addition to geographic accessibility barriers, rural communities are characterized by a number of complex and interrelated social and cultural factors that influence the decision to seek treatment including stigma, cost and affordability barriers, and perceptions of perceived need [18, 19, 20].

Current data on prevalence estimates are limited, as prior studies examining differences in excessive alcohol use and co‐morbid mental health conditions are dated, have limited generalizability, explore variation across dichotomized levels of rurality, or only consider the two conditions independent from one another [12, 18, 19, 20]. Failing to examine the prevalence of these conditions across a complete rural–urban continuum limits opportunities to develop a robust, population‐specific alcohol‐related risk profile for each level of residence and potentially masks risk factors and drivers for behavior uptake unique to each level. This knowledge is necessary to identify populations in need of tailored, community‐specific, and culturally appropriate intervention and prevention strategies. Data are also needed to support efforts to enhance behavioral health care infrastructure in rural areas. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to examine geographic differences in the prevalence of past month excessive alcohol use among individuals reporting any mental illness (AMI) in the past year or lifetime depressive symptoms (DS), using the 2021 and 2022 National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH). We hypothesized that past month excessive alcohol use would be higher among rural residents, that there would be significant differences observed across levels of residence, and would be higher among those self‐reporting AMI in the past year and/or lifetime DS compared to those without [21, 22, 23].

Methods

Data Source

Data for this analysis were drawn from the 2021 and 2022 NSDUH. NSDUH is a publicly available, nationally representative survey that captures information on tobacco, alcohol, and illicit drug use behaviors; the prevalence of substance use disorders; substance use disorder treatment utilization; self‐reported mental health information; and behavioral health care utilization services from an estimated 70,000 individuals aged 12 and older each year [24]. Samples are obtained from all four quarters of each year and are completed either in‐person or via the web. Weighting is used to achieve a nationally representative sampling across all 50 states and Washington DC. More information regarding NSDUH data collection procedures can be found elsewhere [24].

Population Studied

NSDUH data include 141,219 total respondents—69,850 in 2021 and 71,369 in 2022. Our study sample was restricted to individuals with complete demographic information and to individuals aged 18 or older. The final unweighted data sample used for this study was approximately 94,391.

Study Variables

Independent Variable

The independent variable for this study was residence type. Residence was defined as large metropolitan (large urban), small metropolitan (small urban), and nonmetropolitan (rural) at the county level calculated by NSDUH based on the 2023 Rural Urban Continuum Codes with large metropolitan serving as the referent group [24].

Outcome Variables

The primary outcomes of interest were self‐reported past year AMI, lifetime DS, past month excessive alcohol use alone and past month excessive alcohol use among individuals reporting either past year AMI or lifetime DS. Excessive alcohol use was measured using self‐reported engagement in excessive alcohol use in the past 30 days. NSDUH defines excessive alcohol consumption or binge drinking as having 5 or more drinks on the same occasion for males and 4 for females on at least 1 day in the past 30 days [24]. Only survey participants aged 18 or older were asked to report experiencing any mental illness (AMI) in the past year [24]. Adult respondents were classified as meeting AMI criteria if they reported experiencing any mental, behavioral, or emotional disorder in the past year in alignment with criteria outlined in the DSM, fourth edition [24]. Experience of depressive symptoms was classified using a measure for ever having experienced “a period of time lasting several days or longer when most of the day you felt sad, empty or depressed.” To examine excessive alcohol consumption and AMI or DS together, variables were created where participants responded “Yes” for both past year AMI and past month excessive alcohol use and another for those responding “Yes” to both lifetime DS and past month excessive alcohol use. Responses to each type were dichotomized as yes or no.

Study Covariates

Covariates were identified based on Andersen's Behavioral Model (BM), which includes predisposing (demographic), need (such as health status), and enabling (such as income or insurance) characteristics [25]. This multi‐level model has been leveraged extensively in studies examining the factors that influence health service utilization, health conditions, and health systems [26]. Andersen's BM offers a conceptualization of better understand how mental health conditions may influence perceived need for treatment and subsequently influence the uptake of excessive alcohol use behaviors in the absence of or alternate to seeking mental or behavioral health care. While mental health (self‐reported past year AMI or lifetime DS) was conceptualized as an outcome measure for this study, overall health status was included as the primary need variable in this model. Predisposing or demographic characteristics including age and sex. For sex, male served as the referent group. Age was grouped as 18–25, 26–34, 35–49, 50–64, and 65 or older with 65 or older serving as the referent group. Perceived need was measured using self‐reported general health status categorized as excellent, very good, good, or fair/poor with excellent serving as the referent group. Enabling factors included education level, total family income, employment status, marital status, and health insurance status. Educational attainment was categorized as less than high school, high school graduate, some college, and college graduate with college graduate serving as the referent group. Total family income was categorized as less than $20,000, $20,000–$49,999, $50,000–$74,999, and $75,000 or more with $75,000 or more serving as the referent group. Employment status was categorized as employed full time, employed part time, unemployed, or other with employed full time serving as the referent group. Marital status was categorized as married, widowed/divorced/separated, or never been married with married serving as the referent group. Health insurance status was categorized as private insurance, public insurance, or uninsured with private serving as the referent group.

Analytic Methods

Survey weighted descriptive statistics were used to provide prevalence estimates of the mental health conditions (AMI and DS), excessive alcohol use, and combined mental health conditions among individuals reporting excessive alcohol use across levels of residence. Bivariate associations were calculated using Rao–Scott chi‐square tests to examine differences in combined substance use behaviors and mental health conditions behaviors across levels of residence. Survey weighted multivariable logistic regression models were performed to compare the prevalence of substance use behaviors among individuals reporting either past year AMI or lifetime DS. Weighting was leveraged in the analyses in accordance with instructions from NSDUH on clustering, strata, and weighting divided by two for pooled estimates to generate nationally representative estimates across two years of survey data [24]. Analyses were completed using Statistical Analysis Software (SAS; Version 9.4) and used a significance level of α = 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

This study was deemed exempt by the institutional review board at the University of South Carolina due to the use of publicly available, de‐deidentified data.

Results

Survey Participant Characteristics

The final unweighted data sample used for this study was 94,391 (Table 1). Approximately 15.9% (n = 14,989) of respondents reported residing in a rural area compared to 39.2% (n = 37,006) in small urban and 44.9% (n = 42,396) in large urban areas. All characteristics examined were significantly different by residence, with the exception of gender. In large urban areas, most participants were between 35 and 49 years old (23.2%), non‐Hispanic White (53.7%), college graduates (34.1%), came from a household with a total family income of $75,000 or more (47.0%), report full time employment (43.4%), are married (48.5%), have private health insurance (63.2%), and report very good health status (35.9%). For small urban areas, most participants were in the 35–49 (21.6%) and 50–64 (21.6%) year age groups. Most small urban participants were non‐Hispanic White (67.8%), had some college education (29.0%), had income of $75,000 or more (36.0%), were employed full time (40.6%), were married (47.7%), on private insurance (58.2%), and reported very good health status (34.5%). In rural areas, most participants were between 50 and 64 years old (24.4%), non‐Hispanic White (80.7%), high school graduates (33.8%), came from a household with a total family income of $20,000–$49,999 (34.6%), report “other” employment (42.2%), are married (49.8%), have private health insurance (55.7%), and report good health status (34.9%).

Prevalence of Combined Excessive Alcohol Use and Mental Health Conditions

Around half of adults reported excessive alcohol use in the past 30 days for all types of residence (Table 2). A slightly higher percentage of adults living in small urban and rural areas reported excessive alcohol use in the past 30 days compared to adults living in large urban areas (51.1% vs. 50.6% vs. 48.3%, p = 0.0058). A higher percentage of adults residing in small urban areas report experiencing AMI in the past year compared to adults living in rural and large urban areas (24.8% vs. 22.8% vs. 22.1%, p < 0.0001). A larger proportion of adults living in small urban areas report experiencing both AMI in the past year and excessive alcohol use in the past 30 days compared to adults living in rural and large urban areas (15.9% vs. 14.7% vs. 13.8%, p < 0.0001). Compared to adults living in rural and large urban areas, more adults living in small urban areas report ever experiencing DS (28.8% vs. 29.6% vs. 31.9%, p = 0.0002).

Adjusted Models for Combined Excessive Alcohol Use and Mental Health Conditions

In adjusted analyses (Table 4), no significant differences were present by residence for combined past‐year AMI and excessive alcohol use. The slightly higher unadjusted odds of combined past‐year AMI and excessive alcohol use for small urban residents were attenuated after adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics (Model 1: aOR 1.09; 95% CI 1.00–1.18). Similarly, in Model 2 examining adjusted odds of lifetime DS and past month excessive alcohol use together, increased unadjusted odds among small urban residents were no longer significant after adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics (Model 2: aOR 1.04; 95% CI 0.97–1.10). On the contrary, rural residents had slightly lower odds of lifetime DS and past month excessive alcohol use when compared to their large urban counterparts after adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics (Model 2: aOR 0.91; 95% CI 0.83–0.99).

Discussion

This study is one of the first to examine a nationally representative sample for variation in the prevalence of excessive alcohol use and self‐reported past year AMI or lifetime DS across three levels of geographic residence. Our findings revealed similarly high prevalence rates and statistically significant differences for each self‐reported condition alone as well as together across all three levels of geographic residence. Of concern, around half of adults reported excessive alcohol use in the past 30 days for large urban, small urban, and rural areas. These results add to a limited availability of updated and generalizable research examining differences in self‐reported mental health conditions and excessive alcohol use behaviors across a comprehensive rural–urban continuum, minimizing a significant research gap for policy makers, clinical providers, and public health practitioners.

Prevalence for past year AMI, lifetime DS, and past month excessive alcohol use alone were highest among small urban residents. Similarly, small urban residents had the highest prevalence of individuals reporting past year AMI and past month excessive alcohol use as well as individuals with past year AMI reporting past month excessive alcohol use. Urban residents comprised the majority of individuals reporting lifetime DS and past month excessive alcohol use as well as individuals with lifetime DS reporting past month excessive alcohol use. Our results deviated slightly from previous findings reporting rural residents as more likely to report past‐month alcohol misuse among individuals meeting criteria for major depressive disorder and urban residents being more likely to report higher rates of past‐year alcohol use, excessive alcohol use, lifetime alcohol use, and past 12‐month AUD [9, 13].

Our findings for small urban residents differ from previous findings reporting rural residents being more likely to report exceeding daily drinking limits and urban residents being more likely to report exceeding monthly drinking limits compared to small urban/suburban residents. Our findings contribute to a significant knowledge gap regarding updated variation in excessive alcohol use behaviors and self‐reported mental health conditions across an expanded rural–urban continuum and contribute key insights to better refine population‐specific alcohol‐related risk profiles for individuals living in rural, small urban, and large urban communities.

Levels of excessive alcohol use were similarly high across all levels of residence but were highest among small urban residents indicating there is not only a need for additional investigation of potential mediators and protective factors for each group but targeted interventions to reduce prevalence rates through community‐specific policy solutions. Policy interventions commonly explored to mitigate alcohol misuse and alcohol‐related harms include regulatory levers to reduce the density of alcohol retail outlets, policies limiting the number of liquor licenses issued per population, increase in taxes on alcohol purchasing, increased regulation of operating hours of government controlled alcohol wholesale and retail systems, and enforcement of social host ordinances [27]. Updated prevalence rates for the behaviors and self‐reported mental health conditions reported in this study can better position policymakers and community leaders to make more informed decisions regarding where tailored policy interventions are most needed to mitigate alcohol‐related harms. Further research is needed to better understand drivers of excessive alcohol use behaviors, barriers to accessing comprehensive behavioral health treatment, and cultural factors unique to small urban/suburban communities.

While community‐specific interventions designed to regulate the alcohol environment at the local‐level are critical in preventing alcohol misuse and alcohol‐related harms, it is imperative that increasing access to quality behavioral health care services also be prioritized. Behavioral health conditions are not only severely undertreated in the United States but are highly comorbid with each other as well as other chronic conditions. These comorbidities are mediated by complex biological mechanisms and shared risk factors and despite their increasing prevalence, individuals with behavioral health conditions are still subjected to fragmented specialized care operating outside of general health care. It has been widely documented that access to comprehensive behavioral health care has been limited among historically marginalized racial/ethnic groups and individuals in rural areas [28]. These individuals face myriad barriers to accessing treatment beyond geographic barriers including factors such as acceptability of available care, stigma, discrimination, lower insurance coverage, lower socio‐economic status, and lower perceived need [28]. While residents in rural areas face heightened barriers to accessing comprehensive behavioral health care potentially exacerbating the potential downstream effects of excessive alcohol consumption, findings from this study underscore the need for updated interventions and policy solutions that are responsive to community‐specific needs among rural, small urban, and large urban communities.

It has been demonstrated that integrated behavioral health care models have improved treatment delivery and subsequent health outcomes in underserved populations with many patients reporting improvements in symptoms and functioning, increased satisfaction with care, and a reduction in stigma and cultural barriers [29]. Strategies to more effectively facilitate patient‐centered care include advancing the expansion and financing of integrated behavior health models like the Collaborative Care Model (CoCM), expanding telemental/telebehavioral health services, strengthening the integration of behavioral health and primary care services in co‐located models, and scaling and expanding the Screening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment (SBIRT) model [30, 31, 32]. To increase access to comprehensive, integrated behavioral health care, it is imperative that payment models and reimbursement mechanisms are aligned to incentivize and support adoption, clinical and social support environments are conducive to interdisciplinary collaboration, and that evaluation measures are implemented to foster shared accountability for patient‐centered care. As rates of alcohol use, alcohol‐related harms, and comorbid behavioral health conditions continue to increase, policymakers, clinical providers, and public health practitioners are in a position to implement evidence‐based interventions to not only shape regulatory environments to reduce harm but improve clinical environments to promote patient‐centered care and meet patients where they are.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, NSDUH is subject to both recall and social‐desirability bias. Second, this study uses cross‐sectional data and therefore we cannot determine the temporality of AMI, DS, and excessive alcohol use to make a causal inference and therefore cannot classify them as co‐occurring or co‐morbid conditions in the interpretation of our findings. Third, NSDUH excludes some populations who may experience higher rates of substance use disorders and mental illness; incarcerated individuals or individuals experiencing homelessness not living in shelters potentially underestimating the prevalence of the conditions used. Fourth, self‐reported status of mental health and engagement in substance use behaviors may not align with clinical diagnostic criteria. Fifth, the complexity of behavioral health conditions such as substance use disorder and mental health conditions, calls to question the efficacy of using “ever having” as a primary indicator to evaluate the prevalence of these conditions. Lastly, leveraging large survey data sets like NSDUH, increase the statistical power, making it easier to detect statistically significant differences for nationally representative estimates, and making it important to critically examine the practical implications of the findings.

Conclusions

Around half of Americans reported excessive alcohol use in the past 30 days across all levels of residence with excess alcohol use rates being exacerbated by among individuals also reporting past year AMI or lifetime DS. In addition to having the highest prevalence of AMI among individuals reporting excessive alcohol use, AMI, DS, and excessive alcohol use were highest among small urban residents. These results warrant further investigation to identify community and population‐specific psychosocial stressors, drivers of excessive alcohol use behaviors, and barriers to accessing comprehensive behavioral health treatment needed to better inform targeted policy solutions and intervention strategies.
