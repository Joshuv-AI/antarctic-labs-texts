Accuracy of non-invasive electrocardiographic imaging in scar-dependent ventricular tachycardia: relationship to arrhythmogenic substrate and imaging defined scar

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsAccuracy data for non-invasive electrocardiographic imaging (ECGi) in defining the arrhythmogenic substrate for scar-dependent ventricular tachycardia (VT) is lacking. Introduction

Ventricular tachycardia (VT) entrainment and local activation timing (LAT) maps are gold standard techniques to identify the critical isthmus.1–5 Non-invasive mapping techniques, such as electrocardiographic imaging (ECGi), may enhance VT ablation procedures by providing additional spatial localization data of the arrhythmogenic substrate. This may facilitate a more targeted invasive mapping strategy by guiding operators to the relevant myocardial segments.

The primary aim of ECGi is to identify the electrical activity on the surface of the heart using data obtained from electrical activity on the body surface. This is known as the ‘inverse problem’, and solving it forms the basis of non-invasive ECGi. Finding a solution to the inverse problem is challenging because it is an example of a mathematically ill-posed equation, where multiple solutions may exist and small arbitrary variations in the body surface recordings can result in significant changes in the computed cardiac surface potentials.6–8 Therefore, steps must be taken to stabilize the equation and account for these challenges using regularization algorithms.6,7

There are two methods of defining the cardiac electrical activity from the body surface electrical activity in ECGi systems. These can be divided into a potential-based approach and a surface electrical layer source method (dipole-based approach such as the equivalent double layer [EDL]).7,9 A key difference is the ability to define the electrical activity on different cardiac surfaces, with the potential-based approach limited to epicardial electrical activity only, whilst the EDL can map electrical activity on both endo- and epicardial surfaces.

View into ventricular onset (VIVO, Catheter Precision, SC, USA) is a non-invasive mapping system utilizing the custom-made cardiac isochrone positioning system (CIPS)- based ECGi methodology and a standard 12-lead electrocardiograph (ECG).10 This ECGi system is made of several components that include; (i) the cardiac current source model based on the EDL approach, (ii) proximity and orientation of nine standard ECG leads, (iii) definition of the electrical conductor properties using the boundary element method, and (iv) patient-specific geometry from cross-sectional imaging.10

Despite the reported accuracy of ECGi in mapping premature ventricular complexes (PVCs), data specific to scar-dependent VT is lacking.11–15

Methods

Study design and patient population

From 2022 to 2024, 31 patients undergoing VT ablation at the University Hospital Coventry (UK) were included in this prospective, validation study. The inclusion criteria were patients with structural heart disease, implantable cardiac defibrillator (ICD) with appropriate therapies, inducible VT, and either Multislice Cardiovascular Computed Tomography (MSCT) or Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance (CMR) imaging data performed within 6 months prior to the procedure. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all patients provided written informed preprocedural consent. Study approval was provided by University Hospital Coventry Research Department and the local research ethics committee. Data supporting study findings are available from the corresponding author upon request.

Electro-anatomical mapping procedural workflow

Study participants underwent VT ablation in accordance with the local procedural workflow previously described.16–18 EAM was performed using the Advisor HD Grid mapping catheter and Ensite X mapping system (Abbott, MN, USA). Omnipolar EGMs were recorded with bandpass filters set at 30–300 Hz. The WorkMate Claris EP system (Abbott, MN, USA) was used for surface ECG acquisition. Substrate maps were generated during sinus rhythm or right ventricular pacing, if no underlying rhythm. Epicardial access was achieved as previously described.19 Conventional bipolar scar definitions were used: <0.5 mV representing dense scar, 0.5–1.5 mV borderzone scar, and >1.5 mV healthy myocardium. Following substrate mapping, pulsed ventricular stimulation (PVS) was performed with two drive trains (600/500 ms) with ≥3 extra-stimuli from two ventricular sites to induce VT.20 If haemodynamically stable VT was induced, activation mapping was performed to define the VT-ESR and critical isthmus. The VT-ESR was defined by identification of late diastolic potentials. Inclusion of VT termination with ablation and non-inducibility was not included limiting the precise location of the VT exit site. VT was classified as endo- or epicardial using the EAM, with endocardial VTs defined as those with high frequency (>400 Hz) late diastolic potentials on endocardial activations maps or successfully terminated with endocardial ablation at the VT-ESR.21 Low frequency or absence of late diastolic potentials were considered an epicardial breakout. VT activation maps were constructed using LAT maps with a first deflection algorithm. If VT was associated with haemodynamic instability, VT was terminated with electrical cardioversion and pace mapping was performed to identify the VT-ESR. The definition of a pace map–defined VT-ESR was a > 95% 12-lead QRS morphology match to the clinical VT. Radiofrequency ablation targeting functional substrate, VT-ESR, and isthmus was delivered using an externally irrigated 3.5-mm-tip ablation catheter that allowed contact-force assessment (TactiFlex, Abbott, MN, USA). Temperature control was set at 45°C and power settings ranged from 40 to 50 W, with a minimum force of 8 g, aiming for an impedance drop of 10–15 Ω. The post-ablation endpoint was VT non-inducibility using the PVS protocol.

View into ventricular onset (VIVO) ECGi mapping workflow

The commercially available VIVO ECGi mapping system was integrated within the procedural workflow summarized in Figure 1. The ECGi system components include proprietary VIVO software, a camera to acquire a three-dimensional (3D) image of the torso and single-use positioning patches. At the start of the procedure, patches were placed over the sternum and shoulders in standardized positions. The 3D camera image of the patient torso was acquired with all standard 12-lead ECG and VIVO ECGi patches in situ and subsequently uploaded into the VIVO software, to ensure accurate localization on the constructed 3D model. Previous MSCT or CMR images were uploaded into the VIVO software in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. The images were segmented by an experienced operator using a standardized workflow to create the patient-specific model of the heart and torso.

Following VT induction, a 12-lead ECG of the VT was extracted from the WorkMate Claris EP system (Abbott, MN, USA) and uploaded into the VIVO software. Callipers were manually placed at the onset/offset of the QRS complex and end of the T wave. The VIVO software generated an isochronal map of earliest ventricular depolarization based on the surface QRS to identify the VT-ESR (Figure 1). VIVO ECGi maps were not used to guide ablation.

Cardiovascular CT workflow

MSCT was performed on a GE Revolution Apex 256-slice scanner (GE Healthcare, USA) with a detailed protocol description in the supplementary data file. The following key sequences were acquired: (i) Prospectively gated non-contrast axial cardiac imaging (Agatston score-based coronary calcification assessment), (ii) first-pass contrast-enhanced thoracic axial imaging (coronary artery [stenosis, plaque characterization], cardiac structural and functional [left ventricular ejection fraction], myocardial hypoattenuation [hypoperfusion, scar], thoracic assessment) and (iii) delayed iodine hyperenhancement cardiac axial imaging 10-min post contrast (scar assessment) were performed. To ensure appropriate integration with VIVO ECGi mapping system, full chest coverage was required to generate the required cardiac and thorax geometry. Clear opacification of both the right and left ventricles was required to ensure clear delineation of the interventricular septum. MSCT analysis was performed using AW Server Version 3.2 software (GE Healthcare, USA).

Cardiovascular MRI workflow

CMR was performed on a Siemens Aera 1.5T scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Germany). The following key sequences were acquired: (i) Balanced Steady-State Free-Precession (bSSFP) cine imaging (volumetric, functional assessment), (ii) Modified Look Locker (MOLLI)-based T1 mapping (fibrosis, scar, extracellular volume assessment), (iii) bSSFP-based T2 mapping (oedema assessment), and (iv) Inversion Recovery T1-weighted bSSFP Late Hyperenhancement imaging at 10 min post 0.1 mmol/kg intravenous injection of Gadobutrol contrast. CMR analysis was performed using CVi42 software (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Canada).

EAM and VIVO ECGi map comparative analysis

Post hoc map comparisons of the VT-ESR identified with EAM and VIVO ECGi maps were performed by two blinded electrophysiologists (T.D., A.A.). The VT-ESR, critical isthmus, and successful ablation sites were identified within the electroanatomical maps and designated segments (T.D.), blinded to the VIVO ECGi map, in accordance with the 17-segment AHA model of the left ventricle (LV) and a 15-segment model of the right ventricle (RV).10–12 A second electrophysiologist (A.A.) blinded to the results of the EAMs, reviewed the VIVO ECGi map and identified the VT-ESR in accordance with the AHA segment model.

Comparison of EAMs to VIVO ECGi maps were classified as a complete match, if both maps identified the same AHA segment, or a partial match, if AHA segments identified were adjacent to one another. If the AHA segments were neither the same nor adjacent, then the result was classified as no match (see supplementary material online, Figure S1). The AHA segment classification system was additionally used for ECGi map comparisons to imaging-based scar, critical isthmus, and successful ablation sites.

Calliper sensitivity analysis was performed for cases where the initial assessment resulted in segment discordance between the EAM and VIVO ECGi map. Onset/offset callipers were adjusted in 10 ms increments and the VIVO ECGi map VT-ESR was re-evaluated.

Statistical analysis

To objectively determine the accuracy of VIVO ECGi in scar-dependent VT, complete match, partial match, and no match ratios and percentages are expressed. For continuous variables with normal distribution, mean ± standard deviation (SD) is reported. For those with non-normal distribution, median with 25 to 75% inter-quartile range (IQR) are expressed. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used for normality testing. Categorical variables are reported as counts and proportions and displayed as percentages (%). Categorical variables were assessed using Fisher’s exact test or χ2 test. For multiple comparisons, ANOVA was performed if data were normally distributed or Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed data. P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) was used for statistical analysis.

A post hoc non-inferiority analysis was performed to compare the performance of VIVO ECGi against EAM. The benchmark accuracy for EAM was set at 90%, with a non-inferiority margin of 15%, corresponding to a non-inferiority threshold of 75%. By defining success as complete or partial match, VIVO achieved a success rate of 87.5% (95% CI [74.8–95.3%]). A one-sided non-inferiority test demonstrated that the observed success rate of VIVO exceeded the non-inferiority threshold of 75% (P = 0.023), indicating that VIVO was non-inferior to EAM.

The spatial coherence of the ECGi map–defined VT-ESR to critical components of the VT circuit was investigated. In 13 VTs, diastolic potentials identified the critical isthmus with a complete match observed in 4 VTs (30.7%), partial match in 6 (46.2%), and no match in 3 (23.1%) VTs (see supplementary material online, Figure S6 for spatial distribution).

The proportion of complete, partial, and no matches observed between the EAMs and ECGi maps in epicardial VTs was analysed. In 11 VIVO ECGi-defined epicardial VTs, a complete match was observed in eight VTs (73%), partial match in two (18%), and no match in one (9%) VT (see supplementary material online, Figure S7 for spatial distribution).

A ‘search space reduction analysis’ was performed to quantify the extent ECGi may reduce the expected mapping search space. If EAM was limited to the VIVO ECGi-defined VT-ESR and adjacent segments only, ECGi may reduce the expected segment level mapping burden by ∼2.5 times (see supplementary data).

Relationship between ECGi map–defined VT-ESR and cross-sectional imaging-defined scar

Patients with cardiac cross-sectional imaging-defined scar (n = 27) were included in this analysis. A total of 41 VTs were evaluated to determine the spatial coherence between the ECGi-defined VT-ESR and imaging-defined scar. A complete segment match was observed in 20 (48.8%) VTs, partial match in 15 (36.6%) VTs, and no match in 6 (14.6%) VTs (Table 2).

Imaging-defined scar correlation to ECGi maps restricted to cases where the ECGi VT-ESR and EAM VT-ESR demonstrated a complete match, identified 28 VTs. The complete match between ECGi and cardiac cross-sectional imaging-defined scar increased from 48.8 to 60.7% (n = 17) and partial matches were observed in 8 (28.6%) VTs.

Relationship between the ECGi-defined VT-ESR and successful ablation lesion sites

Non-inducibility of clinical VT was achieved in all patients and a total of 45 VTs were targeted for ablation. A complete match between the ECGi VT-ESR and the successful ablation site was seen in 21 (46.7%) VTs (Table 2). A partial match was seen in 16 (35.6%) VTs, and no match in 8 (17.8%) VTs.

Accuracy of ECGi maps in ischaemic or non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy

The accuracy of ECGi was compared to EAM based on the presence of ischaemic cardiomyopathy (ICM) (n = 22 with 36 VTs) or non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy (NICM) (n = 9 with 12 VTs). In patients with ICM, a complete VT-ESR match occurred in 23 (63.9%) VTs and a partial match in 9 (25.0%). In patients with NICM, a complete match occurred in 9 (75%) VTs and a partial match in 1 (8.3%). In patients with ICM and NICM, a no match was observed in four (11.1%) and two (16.7%) VTs, respectively. There was no difference in accuracy between the two cardiomyopathy types (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.5328).

Impact of VT QRS morphology and cycle length on VIVO ECGi accuracy

Analysis was performed to determine the accuracy of VIVO ECGi in identifying the VT-ESR at different VT cycle lengths (VTCL), frontal plane QRS axis, and V1 RBBB vs. LBBB QRS morphology.

All 48 VTs were subdivided into 4 groups with VTCLs between 200 and 600 ms in 100 ms increments: VTCL 200–299 ms, VTCL 300–399 ms, VTCL 400–499 ms, and VTCL 500–599 ms (Table 3). There was no difference in accuracy with differing VTCLs (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.6196).

Accuracy of VIVO ECGi in identifying epicardial VT-ESR

The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of VIVO ECGi and 6 comparative 12-lead ECG-based algorithms for patients with ICM are summarized in Table 4. VIVO ECGi had the highest specificity for identifying epicardial VT-ESR (92.9%), with a sensitivity of 71.4%. In patients with NICM VIVO ECGi had a 100% specificity and 66.7% sensitivity, whilst a four-step ECG-based algorithm had a sensitivity and specificity of 50% and 0%, respectively.22,23

ECGi mapping of ventricular arrhythmias

A few studies have previously aimed to validate the accuracy of VIVO ECGi in identifying ventricular arrhythmia exit sites. Van Dam et al. assessed the accuracy in identifying the site of origin (determined by successful ablation site) for seven PVCs, of which five were correctly identified.10 The group published results of a second validation study demonstrating VIVO ECGi was able to accurately predict the PVC site of origin and specific location to the papillary muscle.12 Misra et al. reported the accuracy of VIVO ECGi in 20 patients undergoing PVC (n = 12) and VT (n = 8) ablation with 22 arrhythmias mapped. This included nine VTs in eight patients with structural heart disease.11 The combined cohort from previous studies totals 32 ventricular arrhythmias, of which only 9 structural heart disease VTs were included.10–12 The present study, therefore, represents the largest cohort of patients with structural heart disease, scar-dependent VT, to be investigated using VIVO ECGi.

Several non-invasive mapping systems are commercially available for ventricular arrhythmia mapping.15 This includes ECGi systems utilizing a potential-based cardiac source model, such as CardioInsight (Medtronic, MN, USA) and Amycard (EP Solutions SA, Switzerland). The use of the potential-based model in the CardioInsight system restricts the mapped cardiac electrical activity to the epicardial surface, limiting the accurate localization of VT-ESR from the interventricular septum or internal cardiac structures. The Amycard ECGi system also utilizes the potential-based model; however, constructs both the epicardial and endocardial cardiac potentials.6,24,25 This is achieved by using a more complex mathematical model, which is worse conditioned than the ‘inverse problem’ for only epicardial potentials.6,24–26 The role of potential-based ECGi systems in mapping ventricular arrhythmias has been investigated. Jamil-Copley et al. reported a prospective study using CardioInsight in 24 patients with outflow tract VT or PVCs.14 CardioInsight was able to accurately identify the ES and guide ablation in 96% of cases, with 18 VTs mapped to the RV outflow tract and 6 to the LV outflow tract.14 Hocini et al. assessed the feasibility of CardioInsight guided ablation in a prospective, multicentre study of 33 patients, of which 24 had symptomatic ventricular arrhythmias.13 The ventricular arrhythmia ES was accurately mapped in 23 of the 24 cases, with 96% patients remaining arrhythmia free at 1 month follow up.13 Parreira et al. assessed the accuracy of Amycard ECGi in 37 patients undergoing catheter ablation for ventricular arrhythmia and reported a 76% accuracy in predicting the exact AHA segment when compared to EAM.27 No statistically significant difference in accuracy was observed between the performance of Amycard compared to VIVO ECGi.27

Accuracy of VIVO ECGi in structural heart disease VT

The present study highlights the accuracy of VIVO ECGi in identifying the VT-ESR and critical components of the VT circuit in patients with scar-dependent VT. Combining complete and partial match accuracy suggests VIVO ECGi has the potential to guide operators to the ventricular region of interest during invasive mapping in 88% of VTs. Indeed, the majority of VIVO ECGi predicted VT-ESR was frequently co-localized with cross-sectional imaging-defined scar. Furthermore, mid-diastolic potentials highlighting the critical isthmus were more frequently seen in adjacent (partial match) segments rather than complete match segments. The VT diastolic isthmus can be subdivided into entrance, middle, and exit components. As a 3D structure, the isthmus can traverse the full thickness of the myocardium, thus only a minority of re-entrant VT circuits are identifiable on the endocardial or epicardial surface alone.28,29 Furthermore, with the diastolic isthmus traversing several myocardial segments, a higher degree of partial match is therefore expected, as early and mid-diastolic components are likely to be in proximity to, rather than at the VT-ESR. It should be noted that the EDL method used by VIVO ECGi only identifies the onset of ventricular activation and thus only the VT-ESR is represented within VIVO maps, with no information pertaining to diastolic wavefront conduction.

Together with the findings of this study, careful interrogation of both the ECGi defined VT-ESR segment and importantly, adjacent segments, is likely to co-localize the isthmus region. This additional information would be advantageous when dealing with unmappable VTs, as only a few beats of VT are required to construct the ECGi map. Factoring in the search space reduction analysis, implementing a combined ECGi-assisted/EAM approach to VT ablation may enhance the procedure workflow, reduce overall procedure time, with the potential to improve procedural safety.

Determinants and limitations of VIVO ECGi accuracy

The 12-lead ECG QRS morphology of VT reflects the re-entry circuit exit location and helps guide mapping, however, can be misleading in the presence of structural heart disease. In a recent study by Katsume et al. of 64 scar-dependent VTs, the AHA segment predicted using a 12-lead ECG QRS–based algorithm was discordant from the successful VT ablation site in 89% of cases.30 The authors concluded 12-lead ECG-based algorithms may incur error due to alterations in expected patterns of activation due to the presence of myocardial scar. The limitation of 12-lead ECG algorithms may be explained by the impact of non-uniform anisotropic wavefront conduction in scarred ventricles. Although ECGi systems may in part overcome the limitations of 12-lead ECG-based predictive algorithms through the inclusion of patient-specific data (cardiac and torso morphology), the presence or conductive properties of myocardial scar is currently not considered. The addition of this information in future development may further improve the accuracy of ECGi.

A key finding in this study was the accuracy of VIVO ECGi was not affected by underlying cardiomyopathy-type nor the VT cycle length. In patients with ICM and NICM, the ECGi system was able to identify the exact or adjacent segment in 89% and 83% of mapped VTs, respectively. Similar results were observed for slow compared to fast VTs, where the VT cycle length did not adversely impact the ECGi map accuracy. Interestingly, the accuracy of VIVO ECGi in defining the VT-ESR was significantly better with RBBB QRS morphology VTs which frequently originate from the LV, whilst those with LBBB morphology can originate from the RV or the interventricular septum.31 The difference in accuracy between the two morphologies may be due to how precisely the structure of the ventricles can be defined using cardiac cross-sectional imaging. A key component of the VIVO ECGi system is the patient- specific cardiac model constructed from cross sectional imaging, the quality of which is likely to impact map accuracy. CMR is considered the gold standard imaging modality for defining the RV structure.32,33 In our study cohort, the majority were imaged with MSCT due to presence of cardiac implantable electronic devices, which can result in significant artefact with CMR. MSCT images may have constructed a less reliable model of the RV and interventricular septum, which may explain these findings. Further assessment with a larger cohort of CMR patients is required to determine if the accuracy can be improved for LBBB QRS morphology VTs. The importance of careful QRS annotation was highlighted by the results of the calliper adjustment analysis performed on EAM/VIVO ECGi map discordant cases. In 31% of cases, a calliper adjustment of <20 ms resulted in improved accuracy of ECGi.

The ability to identify an epicardial VT-ESR prior to invasive EAM can influence procedural approach. In the 2025 joint societal consensus statement on epicardial ventricular arrhythmia ablation, a stepwise ECG-based algorithm is recommended for patients with NICM.22,23 In the present study, the accuracy of VIVO ECGi for identifying epicardial VT-ESR in patients with ischaemic and non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy was investigated and compared to previously reported 12-lead ECG-based algorithms. VIVO ECGi accurately identified the epicardial or endocardial location of the VT-ESR for both ICM and NICM, outperforming several 12-ECG-based algorithms. Timely use of VIVO ECGi may therefore aid operators to determine whether an epicardial approach may be required at an earlier stage during the procedure.

Finally, improved long-term outcomes have been reported with a substrate-guided ablation strategy rather than targeting clinical and mappable VTs.34 The positive outcomes from extensive substrate-guided ablation are probabilistic, such that the approach imparts a high sensitivity but sacrifices specificity. A more focused substrate-guided ablation strategy targeting the relevant arrhythmogenic ventricular segments may improve the specificity of substrate-guided ablation. A recent review has highlighted the importance of multi-modality imaging integration combining both invasive and non-invasive mapping with cross-sectional imaging data to provide patient-specific ablation strategies.35 Further studies are required to determine if procedural efficiency and safety can be improved by incorporating ECGi.

Study limitations

This is a non-randomized, feasibility study of patients with predominate ischaemic aetiology. A larger proportion of patients with NICM would improve accuracy assessment of VIVO ECGi in non-ischaemic cohorts. The majority of the mapped VTs were left ventricular in origin and likely driven by the presence of ICM. A greater proportion of patients with NICM may have increased the number of VTs originating from the RV. ECGi mapping was performed using a single system, and additional commercially available ECGi systems should be tested in patients with structural heart disease. Furthermore, there was no formal intra-operator reproducibility testing in this study. Simultaneous endocardial and epicardial mapping (SEEM) was not performed and therefore complete 3D VT circuit characterization was not available. VT termination with ablation and subsequent non-inducibility was not demonstrated to meet the strict definition for the VT exit site and thus VT-ESR was reported. Spatial co-localization was assessed using the AHA segment model rather than point-by-point distance metrics, which limits the spatial resolution with which true discordance between EAM and ECGi can be quantified. Finally, although EAM was used as the reference standard, it also has inherent technical limitations that mean that discordance with ECGi does not necessarily indicate inaccuracy of ECGi.

Future perspectives

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing multiple commercially available ECGi systems are required to identify any variability in accuracy for patients with structural heart disease VT. The findings of this study suggest that the use of ECGi may improve procedural efficiency. Future RCTs should be designed to determine if the use of ECGi results in a reduction in overall procedural time and procedural temporal metrics (mapping, ablation, and time in VT) which may translate into improved safety outcomes.

Conclusion

The ability of VIVO ECGi to accurately identify the VT-ESR, relevant myocardial scar segments, and critical components of the VT circuit, highlights the potential for integration within VT ablation workflows. Future studies should be designed to determine whether temporal metrics of the ablation procedure are improved which may enhance procedural efficiency and safety outcomes.
