Impact of a training program on gastroenterology health care professionals’ patient-centered shared decision-making: a pilot study
Authors: Inoek CM Koopmans, Kayleigh Gieliam, Paul AF Geerts, Wouter de Vos tot Nederveen Cappel, Paul LP Brand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient-centered care (PCC) and shared decision-making (SDM) are internationally recognized as the preferred approaches to patient care. However, implementation in daily clinical practice remains challenging as sustainable behavioral change requires a multifaceted approach. We developed and evaluated a training program consisting of an e-learning, skills training, deliberate practice and a group reflection session, aimed at improving gastroenterologists’ patient-centered attitudes and SDM skills.MethodsBefore and after training, we assessed preferred and self-reported actual patient care behavior (disease-centered vs. patient-centered) by visual analogue scale (VAS) and attitudes towards PCC with the Patient-Practitioner Orientation Scale (PPOS). Open-ended questions exploring participants’ concepts of patient-centeredness were analyzed using thematic analysis. Recorded patient consultations were analyzed using the OPTION5 and Four Habits Coding Scale (4HCS) to evaluate observed SDM and patient-centered communication behaviors.ResultsTwenty-three gastroenterology healthcare professionals completed the training program, showing significant improvements in attitudes towards patient-centeredness (baseline mean total PPOS 4.73 [SD 0.37], 95% CI of difference 0.04–0.44) and self-reported patient-centered behavior (baseline mean VAS 49 [18], 95% CI of difference 6.08–23.81) after the intervention. High levels of patient-centered behavior and SDM skills were observed in recorded consultations (mean 4HCS 72 [12], mean OPTION5 44 [16]). Thematic analysis of responses to open-ended questions highlighted the relationship between healthcare provider (HCP) and patient as a core foundation for PCC, emphasizing trust, clear communication, and understanding patients’ context. After the training, participants described increased attention to eliciting patients’ values and preferences and explicitly acknowledging decisions to be made.ConclusionA dedicated training program increased gastroenterology professionals’ patient-centered attitude and application of patient-centered SDM skills. These findings support the use of dedicated training to facilitate the implementation of PCC and SDM in clinical gastroenterology practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04949-5.Keywords: Faculty development, Shared decision making, Patient centered care, Gastroenterology, Communication skills

Introduction

Over the past decades, there has been increasing support for taking a patient-centered approach in medical care, as opposed to the traditional biomedical disease-oriented model of care[1, 2] or doctor-centered consultation style characterized by physician-dominated interactions and a primary focus on diagnosing and managing disease [3]. In 2000, the World Health Organization (WHO) emphasized the importance for healthcare professionals to be attentive and responsive to patients’ expectations and preferences, and to support them taking an active role in making decisions about their health and quality of life [4, 5]. This desire to empower patients to participate in their own medical care was fueled by changes in legislation, policies, healthcare systems and society [6]. Patients are now increasingly seen as experts regarding their own bodies, symptoms and situations. Their firsthand experience complements professionals’ knowledge [7] and is important for treatment success [8, 9].

Patient-centered care (PCC) has been defined as: ‘’Providing care that is respectful and responsive to individual patient preferences, needs, and values, and ensuring that patient values guide all clinical decisions.’’ [10]. This definition closely aligns with the definition of shared decision-making (SDM): ‘’A process in which both the patient and the healthcare provider contribute to the decision-making process, taking into account the best scientific evidence available, as well as the patient’s preferences, values, and circumstances.’’ [11].

While both PCC and SDM emphasize patient involvement and respecting their values, PCC is a broader concept aimed at providing personalized care [1, 6] whereas SDM is a more specific collaborative process integrating evidence-based medicine (EBM) with patient preferences to guide decisions [9, 12]. Essentially, SDM is a specific tool in the application of PCC. Optimal patient care requires systematic integration of EBM, SDM, and patient-centered communication [12]. A recent study showed that doctors who incorporate both PCC and SDM – patient-centered SDM – achieve considerably higher patient satisfaction rates compared to physicians using either one or neither [13]. This aligns with previous findings on the benefits of both PCC and SDM, including better informed patients with reduced uncertainty and decisional regret [14], higher patient satisfaction [13, 14], improved treatment adherence [15] and enhanced clinical outcomes [16]. Patients empowered to engage in SDM tend to favor less invasive treatments and diagnostic procedures, potentially contributing to efficient care and cost savings [17, 18].

Despite growing recognition of the benefits of PCC, and a general preference for SDM among physicians [19, 20], implementation of PCC and SDM remains slow [21, 22]. Healthcare professionals differ in their understanding of PCC [23–25]. Similarly, the concept of SDM is interpreted differently among physicians [20] and frequently confused with informed consent [26]. Physicians often believe they practice PCC or SDM, while applying these principles only partially or not at all [20, 27].

In gastroenterology, application of SDM also remains limited. In a study of 106 gastroenterologists in Texas, USA, 80% tried to use SDM, but only 12% had a standardized approach to its application [28]. This is noteworthy because SDM is highly relevant in the field of gastroenterology in view of the endoscopic procedures which are invasive and require careful consideration of patient preferences. Furthermore, for patients with chronic conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease, treatment decisions often have long-term implications for patients and should be closely aligned with their daily lives, values, and preferences [29, 30].

A disease-focused hospital culture is a barrier for SDM as well as PCC, while hospital culture shapes clinicians’ behaviors [31, 32] Numerous researchers have emphasized that education and training can play a crucial role in increasing physicians’ awareness of their limited application of PCC and SDM, as well as improving their communication skills to bridge this gap [25, 27, 33–35].

While training programs are known to positively influence short-term behavioral change [36–38], consistent application of this changed behavior in daily clinical practice has been less thoroughly explored. According to The Theory of Planned Behavior, attitudes play a crucial role in shaping professional behavior [39]. Given the importance of attitude in the adoption of patient-centered SDM, and the constraining role of prevailing hospital culture, we developed a group-based training program designed not only to provide SDM knowledge and skills, but also specifically aiming to influence attitudes towards PCC on individual and group level. The program therefore combines knowledge transfer with communication skills training, and feedback and reflection on its application in routine practice, taught in group setting. This study aimed to evaluate changes in attitudes and skills related to patient-centered SDM, in gastroenterology healthcare professionals following participation in the training program.

Materials and methods

We conducted a pre–post intervention study using a mixed-methods approach to evaluate the effects of the training program.

Development of the training program

A multidisciplinary team, including junior and senior doctors, communication and educational scientists, developed the training program. The training was based on the theoretical framework of deliberate practice and the theory of planned behavior [39, 40].

Description of the training program

The training program comprises an e-learning module and two in-person sessions: a communication skills training and a group reflection session. The 2-hour interactive e-learning module, completed prior to the in-person sessions, presents the evidence on the concept and core principles of PCC and SDM. The four habits of PCC and the four steps of SDM are introduced and described as the basis of patient-centered SDM (Table 1) [9, 41]. After passing a knowledge test on the e-learning content, participants can attend the 3-hour communication skills training session where they practice patient-centered SDM through simulated patient consultations with a trained role-play actor, and receive structured feedback on their application of these skills from the actors and the session moderators. Following the training, participants are encouraged to apply their newly learned skills in their daily practice and reflect on these experiences with their peers. Approximately 4–6 weeks after the session, participants are asked to record (on audio or video) an outpatient consultation. Selected fragments of these recordings are used during the group reflection session facilitated by two trainers, where participants receive feedback from peers and trainers on the application of the four habits of PCC and the four steps of SDM.

Participants and setting

In 2024, all 25 gastroenterologists, residents and nurse practitioners of the gastroenterology department at Isala, a large general teaching hospital in the Netherlands, were invited to participate in the pilot of the training program. They also were invited to take part in the study assessing the program’s effects. Data were collected on participants’ gender, age, professional roles, and years of experience in their current positions.

Evaluation of the training program

Questionnaire

At the beginning and the end of the training program, participants completed a questionnaire comprising three sections. In the first section, three open-ended questions captured participants’ attitudes and self-reported behavior in providing optimal patient care in their own words: (“What do you do in your consultations that makes you feel you are truly helping your patients?“, “What motivates you to do this?“, “What kind of healthcare professional (HCP) would you most like to be for your patients during consultations, and why?“). In the second section, we asked participants to rate their approach to patient care on a visual analogue scale (VAS) ranging from 0 (disease-centered) to 100 (patient-centered), both for their current practice and their ideal practice. The third section of the questionnaire comprised the validated Dutch-language version of the Patient-Practitioner Orientation Scale (PPOS) [42, 43], which assesses the attitude toward PCC divided into two subscales - sharing and caring. The sharing subscale focuses on providing information to the patient and their active involvement in the decision-making process, while the caring subscale pertains to incorporating patient’s preferences and values into the care process.

The PPOS comprises 18 items scored on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = strongly agree, 6 = strongly disagree), without a neutral option. The sum of the 18 items, divided by the total number of items, yields a total score of 0–6, with higher scores reflecting a more patient-centered attitude and lower scores a more disease-oriented attitude.

We used a constructivist research paradigm to analyze the qualitative data in this study, as the constructs of interest (participants’ attitudes and self-reported behaviors in applying patient-centered SDM) were considered socially constructed and subjective. Participants’ responses to the open-ended questions were anonymized before starting the analysis.

Thematic analysis was used to identify recurring patterns and meanings within the data [44]. An inductive approach was adopted, allowing themes to emerge from the data without imposing pre-existing theoretical frameworks.

Initially, two members of the research team (IK and KG) read all the responses multiple times to achieve data familiarization. Afterwards, all responses were coded independently. Codes were then discussed between these two researchers until consensus was reached. No new themes emerged in the later stages of coding, suggesting thematic saturation. After agreement on the final codebook, codes were grouped into candidate themes based on conceptual similarity. These candidate themes were discussed and categorized into main- and subthemes. The themes were subsequently refined in discussion with two additional members of the research team (PB and PG) until consensus was reached on the final set of themes. Data were managed and coded using Atlas.ti version 25.0.0.32864. As a form of member checking, one training participant was also member of the research team (WdVtNC), verifying the accuracy of the analysis results. His involvement in the study was limited to reviewing and editing the manuscript, in order to reduce the risk of bias related to insider involvement in data interpretation.

Recorded consultations

Consultations recorded by participants towards the end of the training program and used during the group reflection session, were scored independently by two observers. Participants patient-centered and SDM behavior were scored by respectively the Four Habits Coding Scheme (4HCS) and Observer OPTION5 score.

The 4HCS is a validated 23-item tool assessing physician communication behaviors across four domains. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = poor/absent behavior, 3 = moderate behavior, 5 = exemplary execution of the behavior, 2 or 4 points for in between). Details can be found in Appendix A. Higher scores reflect more effective, patient-centered behavior [41].

The OPTION5 is a validated instrument designed to assess clinician behavior in SDM. It comprises five core behaviors (alternating options, supporting deliberation / forming a partnership, giving information about options, eliciting patients’ preferences, integrating preferences into the decision) which are scored on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = no effort, 4 = exemplary effort, see appendix B for details). The sum of the scores is multiplied by 5, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicate more active facilitation of SDM [45]. Both researchers were trained in scoring the OPTION5 through an online module [46].

To calibrate their scoring, the researchers initially scored six complete recorded consultations independently using both the OPTION5- and 4HCS scoring systems. The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was assessed using a two-way mixed-effects model (absolute agreement, single measures), yielding an ICC of OPTION5 scores of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.27–0.97) and an ICC of 4HCS scores of 0.75 (95%CI: 0.84–0.96). ICC above 0.6 indicate reasonable to good agreement between observers [46].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using MedCalc Statisical Software version 23.2.8. As the distribution of the data was not significantly different from a normal distribution, as assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test, results are presented as means with standard deviations (SD). Differences in outcomes before and after the training program were analyzed with paired t tests, with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was declared exempt from formal medical ethical review under Dutch law by the Medical Ethics Committee United (MEC-U, reference number W24.091). All participants provided written informed consent.

Results

Participants

In 2024, all 25 gastroenterology HCPs (14 gastroenterologists, 5 residents, and 6 nurse practitioners) working in the gastroenterology department of Isala Hospital were invited to participate in the training program and the accompanying study.

A total of 23 participants (92%) participated in the training program, including 13 gastroenterologists, 4 residents and 6 nurse practitioners (Table 2). One gastroenterologist was not able to participate due to maternity leave, and one resident because her rotation at Isala hospital ended during the training program.

Baseline perspectives of participants

Participants’ perspectives on patient-centeredness

Participants considered a strong relationship between HCP and patient as a fundamental prerequisite for effective implementation of patient-centered SDM in clinical practice (Box 1). Key contributing factors to building a strong HCP-patient relationship included well prepared consultations, clear communication using language tailored to the patient’s level of understanding and transparency regarding patients’ medical information by enabling them to review their medical records. Overall, participants reported a positive attitude towards patient-centered SDM.

Many participants emphasized the importance of patients being well-informed about their condition and the available treatment options, with some also highlighting the relevance of understanding the patient’s background and the context in which symptoms arise. Participants described that establishing trust and encouraging an open dialogue facilitated the expression of patients’ concerns and expectations. This not only deepened clinicians’ understanding of the underlying causes of symptoms but also led to more personalized and appropriate treatment decisions. According to several participants, patients who feel heard and understood are more likely to take an active role in their own recovery, thereby improving adherence and treatment outcomes.

Motivations and barriers towards PCC in clinical practice based on open-ended questions

Respondents expressed a desire to move towards more PCC (Box 2). The most commonly mentioned motivations for a more patient-centered approach were enhancing patient satisfaction and improving treatment adherence. Participants also acknowledged challenges in consistently implementing a patient-centered approach, including time constraints, the perceived obligation to adhere to clinical practice guidelines, and the urgency of managing severe or acute conditions, which may necessitate a more directive approach.

While I am certainly attentive to other issues, there are times when, due to time constraints, I focus more on the deterioration of the condition and somewhat less on how that’s affecting the patient’s overall wellbeing.” – Nurse practitioner no. 5

Evaluation of the training program

Reflection on the training program by participants based on open-ended questions

Participants reported several intentions to change their practice and actual shifts in practice (Box 3). Specifically, they highlighted the intention to more explicitly discuss that a decision had to be made during consultations, and that the patient’s values, preferences, and perspectives are essential in that process. Several participants indicated that they planned to start consultations by actively exploring the patient’s needs and agenda to gain a clear understanding of their expectations and priorities. Finally, participants underscored the importance of providing patients with adequate time for deliberation. Some noted their intention to offer patients the opportunity to defer decision-making to a follow-up consultation, thereby supporting SDM in a more patient-centered and flexible manner.

Attitude about patient-centeredness

Following the training program, there were significant increases in both PPOS (0.22 points on a 1–6 scale, p = 0.022) and visual analogue scale scores (15 points on a 0-100 scale, p = 0.002), reflecting an increase in patient-centered attitude and self-reported behavior (Table 3). All but one participant expressed a desire to shift towards a more patient-centered approach than they currently practice, as reflected by the difference between the self-reported current and desired approaches to healthcare. The mean difference between actual and ideal PCC behavior decreased from 15 to 9 points (p = 0.055), suggesting that participants’ reported behavior is more closely aligned with what they consider ideal.

Discussion

In this pilot study, a structured training program aimed at fostering patient-centered SDM among gastroenterology healthcare providers resulted in a significant increase in positive attitudes toward PCC, accompanied by high levels of observed patient-centered behavior and SDM skills. These findings indicate that a dedicated educational intervention can effectively strengthen both attitudinal and behavioral components of patient-centered SDM in specialist care.

Baseline PPOS scores (mean 4.73) in this study were higher than those in a previous comparable study [43] (mean 4.24), implying that participants already endorsed relatively strong patient-centered values. Following the intervention, attitudes increased further, particularly in the “sharing” subscale which reflects transparency in sharing information with the patient and promoting their active involvement in decision-making. This attitudinal shift was reflected in the participants’ intentions and self-reported practical change, including more explicitly identifying decisions to be made and more actively engaging patients in the decision-making process.

Notably, the increase in self-reported actual patient-centered behavior exceeded changes in their ideal behavior, suggesting that our training program successfully reduced the gap between participants’ current and desired behavior, and supported translation of pre-existing patient centered attitudes into clinical practice. The program thus appears to shape attitudes and facilitate implementation of patient-centered SDM in routine care.

Although SDM is seen as the pinnacle of PCC and has proven to be beneficial [47–49], implementation in daily practice remains challenging [22, 49]. Barriers include insufficient (training in) communication skills [25, 28, 50], and a persistent disease-centered attitudes and hospital culture [31–33, 51].

Our training program was deliberately designed as a group training to address both communication skills and attitudes at individual and group level, potentially contributing to cultural change. However, successful implementation requires multifaceted strategies and organizational support [52]. Our program should therefore be seen as one essential element of the wider set of measures required for successful implementation of patient-centered SDM in routine care.

A notable strength of this study is the inclusion of a diverse group of gastroenterology professionals, including specialists, residents and nurse practitioners and the participation of the entire department, enhancing generalizability and supporting a shared culture of patient-centered SDM This might enable colleagues to reinforce each other in applying PCC and SDM principles, strengthening behavioral change, and allows for more reliable evaluation of the training’s impact on clinical practice and patient outcomes in future studies.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 23) may have reduced statistical power and increased the risk of Type II errors. As a result, potentially relevant differences may not have been detected.

Another limitation is the absence of a pre-intervention assessment of patient-centered SDM skills. A previous cross-sectional study in the same hospital using identical instruments can serve as a benchmark [13]. In this earlier study, the mean OPTION5 score was substantially lower (17.0 versus 44.0), suggesting a strong training effect, although it may also reflect an ongoing cultural change toward patient centered SDM within this hospital. Differences in 4HCS scores were smaller (69 versus 72), with the most notable change in Habit 4 “invest in the end” (32 versus 38) indicating improvements in summarizing, checking patients’ understanding, and outlining next steps, enhancing patient engagement and shared responsibility in the decision-making process.

In addition, an earlier Dutch study reported lower 4HCS and OPTION5 scores (mean 45 and median 25, respectively) [53]. Although these data were obtained in physiotherapist rather than physicians, they provide further context for interpreting the results of the present study.

The lack of long-term follow-up limits the possibility of drawing conclusions about sustainability of the observed effects. Future studies should adopt a longitudinal design (e.g., six- and twelve-months follow-up) combining self-reports and observations to assess sustained impact and identify factors such as workflow integration or ongoing coaching that support lasting behavioral change.

As a single-center pilot, generalizability is limited. However, the positive findings support broader implementation across specialties and clinical centers, allowing evaluation of contextual influences and scalability in future research. An ongoing expansion of this training program to additional settings is currently underway.

In this study, residents, nurse practitioners and medical specialists all followed the same training program together. We believe this approach may support the integration of SDM and PCC into postgraduate education. Previous studies showed that SDM training improves residents’ communication skills, but had limited impact in clinical practice due to organizational barriers and supervisors’ disease-centered attitudes [33, 51, 54]. Integrated training programs like ours address communication, professional identity, and reflective practice, aligning with identified educational needs [55]. Early exposure may facilitate normalization of SDM as a core clinical competency, particularly when embedded in curricula, supervision, and assessment structures.

Future research would benefit from incorporating patients’ perspectives by actively involving patient representatives in both the design and evaluation of interventions, thereby strengthening their relevance and impact and ensuring alignment with patients’ needs and priorities.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that a dedicated training program can significantly enhance both attitudes toward and practice of patient-centered SDM among gastroenterology HCPs. The substantial difference in observed SDM compared to an untrained reference group support its effectiveness. Joint training of residents and medical specialists in PCC and SDM skills appears a promising strategy to promote implementation of patient-centered SDM in postgraduate medical education and clinical practice.
