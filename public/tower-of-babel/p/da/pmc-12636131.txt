Association Between Functional Constipation and Growth Status in Children Based on Anthropometric Indices
Authors: Hanifah Oswari, Michelle Clarissa Junaidi, Jessica Sylvania Oswari, Regina Suriadi, Glen Lazarus, Muzal Kadim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the relationship between functional constipation (FC) and growth status in children using anthropometric indices (weight-for-height Z-score [WHZ] and height-for-age Z-score [HAZ]).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study at a pediatric primary care center in Jakarta, Indonesia, from December 2020 to December 2021. Medical records of 34 children with FC diagnosed using Rome IV criteria and 34 age- and sex-matched healthy controls were reviewed. Growth status was assessed using WHO WHZ and HAZ before and after polyethylene glycol treatment for at least six months. Data were analyzed using t-tests or nonparametric equivalents, with significance set at p<0.05.ResultsThe mean age of our cohort was 3.17±2.11 years at baseline. For children with FC, the mean treatment duration was 17.15±7.22 months. Children with FC had lower WHZ at baseline than the control group (–0.21±0.73, compared to 0.62±0.85, p<0.001). Following treatment, those with FC had increased WHZ (0.18±1.19, p=0.028). No changes were observed in the HAZ before and after treatment (p>0.05).ConclusionFC in children is associated with the WHZ. The completion of FC treatment improved the WHZ in children compared with that at baseline. However, no association was identified between FC and HAZ before and after treatment completion.Keywords: Constipation, Anthropometry, Nutritional status, Child growth, Height

INTRODUCTION

Functional constipation (FC) is characterized by hard, infrequent bowel movements (less than three times per week) that are often accompanied by difficult or painful defecation [1]. FC is most frequently identified following a thorough medical evaluation where no underlying intestinal or extraintestinal disorders are identified [1]. In the United States, FC affects 10–20% of children from infancy through adolescence, while in Asia, the prevalence varies from 0.5% to 29.6% [2]. FC typically occurs between the ages of 2 and 4 years, at the onset of toilet training [2]. Stunting is defined as a height-for-age z-score (HAZ) with the standard deviation (SD) below −2, and is a significant chronic malnutrition challenge [3]. Children with HAZ for which the SD is below −3 are classified as severely stunted [3]. The 2021 national survey revealed that 24.4% of Indonesian children had stunted height [4]. Stunted children are relatively likely to acquire infections, experience malabsorption, or develop cognitive impairment, all of which could lead to reduced lifetime productivity [4]. Moreover, delayed intervention for stunting could cause irreversible growth and cognitive development deficits, along with elevated morbidity and mortality risks that persist into adulthood [5].

However, the relationship between FC and growth status is inconsistent. A case-control study showed that constipation negatively affects children’s weight and height development, with evidence suggesting that effective constipation therapy could improve growth outcomes [6]. Furthermore, Vriesman et al. [7] suggested that chronic conditions such as FC may contribute to poor growth and stunting, with growth gains observed after successful management of FC, including adjustment for treatment effects. However, one study reported no significant association between FC and short stature [8]. A recent meta-analysis revealed that FC was associated with both overweightness/obesity and under-nutrition in children. However, the relationship between FC and short stature remains controversial [9].

Given these conflicting findings, the aim of this study was to investigate the association between FC and two key growth parameters in children: the weight-for-height z-score (WHZ) and HAZ. We hypothesized that FC was associated with both WHZ and HAZ, and that the successful completion of FC treatment would result in improved WHZ and HAZ compared to baseline measurements.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Kiddie Care Center, a pediatric primary care center in North Jakarta, Indonesia. We included all children diagnosed with FC who completed treatment between December 2020 and December 2021. This retrospective study was reviewed and approved by The Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, University of Indonesia (IRB No. KET-252/UN2.F1/ETIK/PPM.00.02/2023).

Data were extracted from electronic medical records, including age, sex, weight, height, treatment duration, WHZ, HAZ before and after treatment, age at FC onset, and FC duration. The control group comprised age- and sex-matched healthy children who visited the clinic for routine vaccinations during the same period.

Eligibility criteria

Children were eligible if they were under 18 years of age, were diagnosed with FC according to the Rome IV criteria, and had completed treatment with polyethylene glycol as an oral laxative. We excluded children with endocrine disorders (i.e., growth hormone deficiency, pituitary microadenomas, congenital hypothyroidism), genetic disorders (i.e., Down syndrome, Turner syndrome, Prader-Willi syndrome), bone diseases (i.e., dwarfism, achondroplasia, rickets), and chronic disorders (i.e., cystic fibrosis, Crohn’s disease, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, chronic renal insufficiency); along with those using medications or supplements affecting growth, treated for less than 6 months, and with incomplete medical records.

Sample size and sampling methods

Using the Kelsey formula, the minimum sample size required was 34 participants. For the control group, we included age- and sex-matched healthy children attending the clinic for routine vaccination, selected at a 1:1 ratio to the FC cases. Consecutive sampling was performed until the required sample size was obtained.

The diagnosis of FC was established using the Rome IV criteria [1] with age-specific definitions.

Growth assessment

Growth status was evaluated using the World Health Organization growth charts. WHZ was used to classify the nutritional status by comparing a child’s weight with that of a child of the same length, height, and sex. HAZ was used to assess linear growth and identify stunting by comparing the child’s height with the expected height of a healthy child of the same age and sex.

Statistical analysis

Demographic data and growth parameters were presented as the mean±standard deviation. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess data distribution. For between-group comparisons (FC vs. control), an independent t-test was used for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U-test for non-normally distributed data. For within-group comparisons (before vs. after treatment), a paired t-test was used for normally distributed data and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for non-normally distributed data. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0 (IBM Co.).

Baseline characteristics

No statistically significant differences were observed between the FC and control groups in terms of baseline characteristics including age, weight, and height (p>0.05).

Treatment duration and growth parameters

The mean duration of FC therapy was 17.15±7.22 months (range: 6–33 months). Children with FC had significantly lower WHZ than controls (−0.21±0.73 vs. 0.62±0.85, p<0.001). HAZ were similar between the FC and control groups (0.32±1.04 vs. 0.26±0.75, p=0.913) (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Constipation is common in children, accounting for 3–5% of pediatric primary care visits and 10-25% of pediatric gastroenterology consultations [10]. The prevalence of FC varies among geographic regions [2]. From infancy to adolescence, the prevalence in Asia is approximately 0.5–29.6% [10]. Beyond the neonatal period, FC accounts for more than 95% of constipation cases among children [2]. The onset of constipation often coincides with that of potty training, as it is most commonly diagnosed between 2 and 4 years of age [2]. This is consistent with our study findings, as the mean age of children with FC in our cohort was approximately 3 years (3.17±2.11 years).

Our study revealed that children who had FC had lower average WHZ (–0.21±0.73) than healthy children (0.62±0.85). This is consistent with earlier study findings indicating that FC might slow children’s weight and height growth trajectories when compared with children without FC [6].

Furthermore, positive changes in the WHZ were observed after FC therapy completion in our study, as there was an increase in the mean WHZ after the completion of therapy compared with the scores at baseline (p=0.028). The association between FC and undernutrition could be explained by discomfort from fecal impaction, which may cause abdominal fullness, anorexia, nausea, and early satiety, leading to decreased appetite and food aversion [11]. FC and undernutrition have the same risk factors, such as incorrect dietary practices, socioeconomic status, and parental education [9].

Certain studies have shown that constipation in children has significant impact on their weight and height, and that constipation treatment improves the children’s growth status [6,7]. However, the HAZ measurements in our study showed no statistically significant difference between the height of children with FC and that of healthy children. After therapy completion, the mean HAZ increased slightly; however, this increase was not statistically significant. Another study found no significant association between FC and short stature [8]. Many factors may have influenced these results. Most children in our study already had good HAZ at the time of diagnosis (before therapy). Moreover, most children in our cohort came from families with middle to high socioeconomic status (SES), whereas stunting was more often associated with low educational and economic status [12]. In addition, a higher income would provide easier access to healthcare facilities, as the children could receive immediate therapy and, therefore, have a shorter duration of constipation. We assumed that higher SES could influence HAZ in our cohort.

The findings of this study could also have been influenced by the shorter FC duration in our cohort. Pawlowska et al. [13] reported an average treatment duration of 39.6 months, whereas in our study, the average duration of treatment was 17 months. Khalili et al. [14] observed significant improvements in the weight, body mass index (BMI), and appetite status of children with FC after 3 months of intervention. Accordingly, children with FC showed improvements in appetite after 12 weeks of routine treatment, which was associated with increased BMI, weight, appetite, and other growth parameters. They concluded that therapeutic intervention in children with constipation could effectively improve their weight gain patterns and appetite as major influencing factors in the long term [14].

The limitations of our study include the fact that the sample did not represent populations with lower SES. Further studies on a larger scale and with a more heterogeneous population are needed.

Conclusion

FC in children is associated with the WHZ. The completion of FC treatment improved the WHZ in children compared with that at baseline. However, no association was identified between FC and HAZ before and after treatment completion.
