Experience With Surgical Correction of Retrocaval Ureter: A Report of Two Cases From Northern Tanzania
Authors: Donald Dominick Lema, Bartholomeo Nicholaus Ngowi, Frank Bright, Zerra Israel Cheyo, Phinihas Jackson Mwijumbe, Fredrick Anselim Kimaro, Musa Raymond Majura, Orgeness Jasper Mbwambo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA retrocaval ureter is a rare congenital anomaly caused by abnormal development of the inferior vena cava. It usually remains asymptomatic until middle age. A man and a woman aged 35 and 45 years, respectively, presented with right flank pain and hydroureteronephrosis. Computed tomography scans confirmed extrinsic compression by the vena cava. Both patients underwent successful surgical correction, and their postoperative recoveries were smooth. These cases emphasize the importance of early diagnosis and treatment to prevent long‐term kidney damage. A retrocaval ureter can mimic obstructive uropathy or coexist with ureteral stones. Symptoms may be sudden or persistent, and delayed diagnosis increases the risk of renal harm. Computed tomography reveals the typical “S‐shaped” loop of the proximal ureter behind the inferior vena cava. Surgery remains the definitive treatment. In areas with limited resources, open surgery is often preferred, although modern minimally invasive techniques are equally effective and less intrusive but less widely available.

Introduction

Retrocaval ureter, formerly known as circumcaval ureter or pre‐ureteral vena cava, is a congenital anomaly where the ureter is variably positioned behind the inferior vena cava (IVC) [1, 2]. It occurs when, around the sixth week, an abnormality causes the right posterior cardinal vein—normally situated lateral to the ureter—to persist as part of the renal segment of the IVC instead of the right subcardinal vein [1]. This condition is rare, more common in males than females, with an estimated prevalence of 0.13%, and usually remains asymptomatic until middle age [3]. Symptoms vary and are mainly due to a dilated ureter and calyceal system caused by compression of the retrocaval segment between the IVC and lumbar vertebrae [4].

We report a middle‐aged man and woman diagnosed with a retrocaval ureter who underwent open surgical correction. The report details their symptoms, diagnostic procedures, and treatment strategies, in accordance with the CARE checklist for case reporting [5]. Aside from their rarity, being the second report in Tanzania after that documented by Maro et al. [6], these cases illustrate the clinical spectrum of presentation and diagnostic challenges, and demonstrate the outcomes of open pyeloureterostomy in low‐resource settings, thereby contributing to the current literature.

Case Presentation

First Case

Case History/Examination

A 35‐year‐old man presented with right flank pain lasting two days. The pain was sudden, colicky, intermittent, and radiated to the lower abdomen, unrelieved by analgesics. It was accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and painful urination. He had no history of fever, hematuria, previous surgery, or trauma. On examination, he was clinically stable with normal vital signs. The abdominal contour appeared normal, with mild tenderness at the right costovertebral angle. Neither kidney was palpable, and no other organ was enlarged. Other systemic examinations were otherwise normal.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

The computed tomography (CT) scan revealed an abrupt narrowing of the right ureter at the L3/L4 level, with upstream dilation leading to right hydroureteronephrosis (Figure 1A,D–F). Although the cortex remained intact (Figure 1C), the ureter courses behind the IVC, indicating a retrocaval ureter. A non‐obstructive calculus was visible at the right ureterovesical junction (Figure 1B). Subsequent tests showed a normal metabolic panel, hemogram, and a negative urine culture. Both the echocardiogram and electrocardiogram results were unremarkable. The patient was scheduled for retrograde pyelography and right pyeloureterostomy.

The plain axial view shows markedly dilated right renal calyces and pelvis, with a normal contralateral kidney (A), and a non‐obstructing stone at the ureterovesical junction (B, blue arrow). The enhanced scans show preserved cortex, an abrupt narrowing of the right ureter at the level of L3/L4, and upstream dilatation of the ureter, resulting in right hydroureteronephrosis with a fishhook appearance, and the distal ureter remains undilated (C, D, E, F).

During the surgical procedure, the cystoscopy appeared normal. The retrograde pyelogram showed a patent distal ureter (Figure 2A) with medial rotation from the L3 vertebra, along with significant dilation of the renal pelvis and the proximal third of the right ureter (Figure 2B). Through a transverse lumbar incision, the retroperitoneum was opened, and the hilum was approached. The right renal pelvis and proximal ureter were severely dilated, with a looping segment behind the IVC (Figure 3). The right ureter was mobilized, transected, and repositioned anterior to the IVC; the right pyeloureterostomy was then completed with a water‐tight anastomosis. A ureteric stent was placed, and a retroperitoneal drain was inserted before closing the abdomen.

It shows a markedly dilated proximal ureter and renal pelvis (A, B, yellow arrows), a normal distal ureter secured with a vessel loop (B, white arrow), and a compressed section of the ureter behind the inferior vena cava (A, blue arrow), although it is not clearly visible in these images.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The postoperative course was uncomplicated, with feeding resumed after eight hours and mobilization after 24 h. The wound was assessed at 48 h; it appeared clean and dry, and the drain was removed. The urethral catheter was removed after 72 h, and the patient was discharged on day five. During follow‐up visits at six weeks postoperatively, he was recovering well with resolved flank pain. An ultrasound showed improved hydronephrosis, and renal function was normal; the DJ stent was subsequently removed. The patient reported significant improvement at the six‐month follow‐up.

Second Case

Case History/Examination

A 45‐year‐old woman presented with mild, intermittent colicky pain in her right flank that has persisted for ten years, accompanied by nausea and vomiting but no fever. Other systems were unremarkable. She had three Caesarean sections, the most recent ten years ago, and has been taking omeprazole for peptic ulcers for five years. On examination, she was clinically stable with normal vital signs. A Pfannenstiel scar was observed, and there was some mild tenderness in the right flank. Neither kidney was palpable, and no other organ was enlarged. Additionally, bimanual and systemic examinations were normal.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

The CT scan revealed a dilated right proximal ureter and renal pelvis, characterized by a fishhook shape caused by extrinsic compression from the IVC (Figure 4C–E). Mild calyceal dilation was noted, with no signs of cortical thinning (Figure 4B). The kidneys showed normal excretory function, and no calculi were present (Figure 4A,F). Further tests indicated a normal hemogram and metabolic panels, with a negative urine culture. Echocardiogram and electrocardiogram results were also unremarkable. The patient was scheduled for right pyeloureterostomy.

The findings from the computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen and pelvis in a 45‐year‐old woman. Inferior displacement of the right renal vessels by the head of the pancreas is observed (A)—a mild dilation of the right renal calyces without cortical thinning (B). A significantly dilated right renal pelvis, with a maximum anteroposterior diameter of 29.3 mm (C), along with proximal ureteral dilation and a compressed segment posterior to the inferior vena cava, exhibits a fishhook appearance (D, E), while the distal ureter remains undilated (F).

During the surgical procedure, a transverse lumbar incision was made to access the right retroperitoneum, followed by blunt dissection toward the renal hilum. The renal pelvis and the proximal ureteral segment appeared dilated. The middle ureteral segment was compressed behind the IVC, while the distal segment remained normal. The right proximal ureter was carefully mobilized and divided from the renal pelvis, then repositioned anterior to the IVC. An end‐to‐end, tension‐free anastomosis was successfully performed, completing the pyeloureterostomy. A double J stent was carefully inserted, and retroperitoneal drainage was placed before closing the abdomen.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The postoperative course was smooth, with feeding resumed after 8 h and mobilization after 24 h. The wound was inspected after 48 h and appeared clean and dry, and the drain was removed. The urethral catheter was removed after 72 h, and the patient was discharged on day five. During follow‐up visits at one and six months postoperatively, she experienced minimal discomfort from the indwelling ureteric stent, which was removed six weeks after surgery. The patient reported significant improvement at the subsequent follow‐up, with normal serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and abdominopelvic ultrasonography.

Discussion

Retrocaval ureter typically causes recurrent flank pain and hydronephrosis, often resembling obstructive uropathy from ureteric stones or stricture [7, 8, 9]. The symptoms can vary from sudden flank colic, as seen in our first case, to chronic intermittent pain lasting many years, as observed in our second case. It can also occur alongside ureteric stones, as demonstrated in the first case. Fola et al. and Maro et al. have reported cases diagnosed years after the onset of variable flank pain [6, 10]. A delayed presentation is often associated with reduced renal function. Both cases showed preserved cortical thickness and serum creatinine levels, indicating a gradual obstructive process with some compensation, although this is better assessed by renal scan [10], which was unavailable in our setting. Without timely intervention, complications from retrocaval ureter can develop [11]. Alwadai et al. discussed several complications following ureteral obstruction and urine stasis in retrocaval ureter, including recurrent urinary tract infections, renal impairment, and urinary stone formation [12, 13]. This case resembles our first case, which involved a distal ureteric stone identified on CT and removed endoscopically.

Traditional intravenous urography and/or CT urography reveal the ‘fishhook’ or ‘S‐shaped’ deformity of the proximal ureter caused by its retrocaval position [13, 14]. MRI may also be employed [13, 15]. Ancillary findings, such as distal calculi, can complicate interpretation if not carefully analyzed. The definitive treatment is surgical correction, with the procedure chosen based on the level of obstruction and renal function [16]. Typically, this involves mobilization of the ureter, transection, and anterior transposition, followed by pyeloplasty, pyeloureterostomy, or ureteroureterostomy [2]. Both of our patients underwent open pyeloureterostomies with successful outcomes. Although reports of laparoscopic and robotic repairs for retrocaval ureter are increasing, open surgery remains a practical option in many centers, particularly where resources are limited, and it yields favorable functional results [17, 18]. The benefits of minimally invasive techniques over open surgery include less bleeding, quicker recovery, and shorter hospital stays [19, 20, 21].

In these cases, apart from their rarity, they highlight a common pitfall in diagnosis, as the non‐specific symptoms in the second case were mistaken for peptic ulcer disease, leading to delayed diagnosis and definitive management. Additionally, they present the characteristic radiological findings and report outcomes of open pyeloureterostomy in resource‐limited settings. We believe that a six‐month follow‐up was sufficient to confirm immediate surgical success and initial recovery of renal function. Extending the follow‐up period for these cases and utilizing renal scans would provide more robust data on renal outcomes and recurrence rates; however, this was limited by patient adherence and the practical constraints of our setting.

Conclusion

Retrocaval ureter is a rare congenital anomaly that causes ureteral obstruction, often presenting with flank pain and hydronephrosis. Two adults with retrocaval ureter experienced flank pain. CT scans confirmed the obstruction. Open pyeloureterostomy produced excellent outcomes, emphasizing the importance of accurate diagnosis and surgical planning in resource‐limited settings. Modern minimally invasive surgical techniques are increasingly used to correct this condition as a superior alternative to open surgery, although they remain underutilized in developing countries.
