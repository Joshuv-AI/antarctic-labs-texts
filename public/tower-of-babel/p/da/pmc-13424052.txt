High intratumoral CD8 positive T cell infiltration associated with pathologic complete response after neoadjuvant immunotherapy in locally advanced colon cancer
Authors: Hiroki Okabayashi, Fuminori Teraishi, Ryohei Sumii, Koichi Okamoto, Tatsuya Toyokawa, Rika Omote, Ryusei Takahashi, Masashi Utsumi, Hideaki Miyaso, Masaru Inagaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeoadjuvant immunotherapy has shown high pathological response rates in mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) locally advanced colon cancer. However, clinically applicable predictors of response remain insufficiently defined.Case presentationWe report three cases of patients with locally advanced mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) colon cancer who received neoadjuvant immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy (ipilimumab plus nivolumab or pembrolizumab) and subsequently underwent curative resection. All patients achieved a pathologic complete response (pCR). Pre-treatment biopsy specimens consistently demonstrated dense CD8+ T-cell infiltration on immunohistochemistry. Using a microscope field number of 22 at x400 magnification, the mean combined intraepithelial and stromal CD8+ T-cell densities were 244.1, 370.4, and 433.5 cells/mm2 in Case 1, Case 2, and the present case, respectively, suggesting a potential association between an immune-inflamed tumor microenvironment and favorable response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy.ConclusionsThe findings of this case series support the feasibility and efficacy of neoadjuvant immunotherapy for locally advanced dMMR colon cancer. Dense CD8+ T-cell infiltration in pre-treatment biopsies may serve as a practical, low-cost, first-line biomarker for pCR; however, standardized quantitative thresholds and prospective validation are required before routine clinical application.

Introduction

Neoadjuvant immunotherapy has emerged as a promising treatment strategy for patients with locally advanced colon cancer, particularly those with mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) tumors. Recent prospective studies have demonstrated remarkable pathological response rates in this subgroup, including a phase II trial in which single-agent immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) achieved major or complete responses in a substantial proportion of patients undergoing curative resection [1, 2]. These findings support the feasibility of neoadjuvant immunotherapy and may redefine treatment paradigms.

The efficacy of neoadjuvant immunotherapy is largely attributed to the high tumor mutational burden and immunogenicity of dMMR tumors, which promote T-cell infiltration and anti-tumor immunity [3]. ICIs such as pembrolizumab and nivolumab plus ipilimumab have demonstrated high rates of pathological complete response (pCR) without compromising surgical outcomes [4, 5].

Despite this progress, most studies to date have focused primarily on dMMR tumors, while data regarding predictive biomarkers of response, particularly histopathologic features of immune cell infiltration, remain limited. The degree of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), especially CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, has been suggested as a potential correlate of response; however, standardized assessment and validation remain lacking in the neoadjuvant biopsy setting. Because diagnostic biopsies are routinely obtained before treatment, CD8 immunohistochemistry can be performed in most pathology laboratories, and CD8+ T cells represent the effector arm reinvigorated by immune checkpoint blockade, pre-treatment CD8 assessment may offer a clinically pragmatic rationale for risk stratification [6–8].

In this context, we report a case series of three patients with locally advanced dMMR colon cancer who received neoadjuvant immunotherapy and achieved pCR. We additionally provide a quantitative CD8+ T-cell assessment, radiological-pathological comparison, and discussion of perioperative monitoring and post-treatment complete clinical response assessment.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old woman was referred for further evaluation following a positive fecal occult blood test. Colonoscopy revealed a circumferential type 2 lesion in the ascending colon (Fig. 1a). Serum tumor markers were elevated, with a carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level of 28.15 ng/mL and a carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9) level of 8.61 U/mL. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated circumferential wall thickening of the ascending colon with enhancement, as well as an enhancing mass contiguous with the right Gerota’s fascia and extending to the right kidney (Fig. 1b). Histopathological examination of the biopsy specimen showed poorly to moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma. Mismatch repair protein analysis demonstrated loss of MLH1 and PMS2 expression, indicating dMMR (Fig. 1c).

The patient was diagnosed with locally advanced ascending colon cancer, stage IIIC (T4bN2bM0) according to the 8th edition of the Union for International Cancer Control classification. Because upfront surgery would likely require extended resection with right nephrectomy and carry a high risk of positive margins, the tumor was considered initially unresectable, and systemic therapy was initiated. The patient received one cycle of FOLFOX plus bevacizumab. After confirmation of dMMR status, the regimen was switched to ipilimumab plus nivolumab. After immunotherapy, the CEA level decreased to the normal range, and CT showed marked regression of the primary tumor and associated invasion (Fig. 1d). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and preoperative positron emission tomography/CT suggested a metabolic complete response (Fig. 1e–f).

The patient underwent robot-assisted laparoscopic right hemicolectomy with complete mesocolic excision and en bloc resection of the involved Gerota’s fascia (Fig. 2). No liver metastases or peritoneal dissemination were observed intraoperatively. Histopathological examination of the resected specimen showed complete fibrosis with lymphocytic infiltration and mucin lakes without viable tumor cells; all examined lymph nodes were negative. The response was graded as Grade 3, consistent with pCR (Fig. 3). The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 11. She declined adjuvant chemotherapy and remains recurrence-free at 6 months after surgery.

Retrospective analysis of three cases achieving pathological complete response after neoadjuvant immunotherapy

Radiological response assessment and CD8⁺ T-Cell infiltration

Radiological responses were retrospectively evaluated using contrast-enhanced CT, MRI (when available), PET-CT (when obtained), tumor marker kinetics, and endoscopic findings. Measurable target lesions were assessed according to RECIST 1.1. Immune-related responses were interpreted based on iRECIST and immune-related response criteria, considering that residual wall thickening or inflammatory changes after immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy may not indicate viable tumor [9, 10].

As all three cases achieved pathological complete response (pCR), statistical agreement between radiological and pathological findings could not be assessed using the kappa statistic due to lack of variability in pathological outcomes. Therefore, concordance was described qualitatively.

Clinicopathological features of the three cases achieving pathological complete response (pCR) after neoadjuvant immunotherapy are summarized in Table 1. All patients were female, aged 71–75 years, with clinically T4b disease and deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) status, defined by loss of MLH1 and PMS2 expression on immunohistochemistry.

Two patients received induction therapy with ipilimumab plus nivolumab followed by nivolumab maintenance, whereas one patient was treated with pembrolizumab. Immune-related adverse events (irAEs) were mild to moderate and manageable, including skin rash in one patient and adrenal insufficiency with hypothyroidism in another; the remaining patient experienced no irAEs.

Radiological assessment showed complete response in two patients and near-complete response in the present case, supporting conversion to curative resection. When radiological findings were compared with pathological outcomes, descriptive concordance was 2/3 (66.7%). Notably, the present case demonstrated near-complete radiological response despite achieving pCR, suggesting that residual post-treatment imaging abnormalities may overestimate viable disease.

Surgical procedures included right or left hemicolectomy. Adjacent organ resection was not required in one case, whereas omentectomy and en bloc resection of Gerota’s fascia were performed in the other two cases to achieve negative margins.

All three patients achieved pathological complete response (pCR) in the resected specimens. The interval between the last dose of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) and surgery was not consistently retrievable from the retrospective records and was therefore excluded from statistical analysis; this limitation is acknowledged.

No perioperative complications related to immune-related adverse events (irAEs), such as colitis, bowel perforation, adrenal crisis, or hyperinflammatory reactions, were documented in the available clinical records.

Immunostaining for CD8 (clone C8/144B; Dako, Denmark; prediluted) was performed on pre-treatment biopsy specimens, with hematoxylin counterstaining. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) were evaluated on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections according to the criteria of the International Immuno-Oncology Biomarkers Working Group [6]. CD8 expression was assessed separately in intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs) and stromal lymphocytes (STLs).

After low-magnification scanning of the entire tumor-bearing biopsy section, three representative high-density hotspots were selected, excluding areas of necrosis, crush artifact, and non-tumor mucosa. CD8⁺ cells were counted at ×400 magnification using a 40× objective. With a microscope field number of 22, the high-power field diameter was 0.55 mm and the corresponding area was 0.2376 mm²; counts per high-power field were converted to cells/mm² by dividing by 0.2376.

As no validated cut-off exists for CD8⁺ TIL density in pre-treatment biopsies of neoadjuvant dMMR colon cancer, dense infiltration was operationally defined in this exploratory series as a combined IEL + STL CD8⁺ density ≥ 200 cells/mm²; all three cases exceeded this threshold (Table 2).

The distribution patterns varied among cases. Case 1 demonstrated IEL-predominant infiltration, whereas Case 2 and the present case showed STL-predominant infiltration. In this small series, no clear association was observed between infiltration pattern and treatment regimen, occurrence of immune-related adverse events (irAEs), or radiological response, and all patterns were compatible with pathological complete response (pCR). These observations should be regarded as hypothesis-generating, as they do not support a validated spatial stratification framework for CD8⁺ T-cell distribution.

Discussion

In this report, we describe three cases of patients with locally advanced colon cancer who achieved pCR following neoadjuvant ICI therapy. All tumors demonstrated dMMR status, and immunohistochemical analysis of pre-treatment biopsy specimens revealed dense CD8+ T-cell infiltration in all cases. Our findings reinforce the therapeutic potential of neoadjuvant immunotherapy in dMMR colon cancer and suggest that readily assessable histopathological immune features may help identify patients most likely to achieve pCR.

Recent prospective studies have established a new benchmark for response in dMMR colon cancer. Early evidence from NICHE demonstrated that short-course neoadjuvant immunotherapy can induce marked pathological regression in resectable colon cancer [1]. More recently, the NICHE-2 study reported high rates of major pathologic response and pCR in locally advanced dMMR colon cancer treated with neoadjuvant nivolumab plus ipilimumab, without compromising surgical feasibility [2]. In parallel, pembrolizumab has shown substantial activity across localized microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H)/dMMR solid tumors, supporting its use in the neoadjuvant setting [4]. Neoadjuvant nivolumab plus relatlimab has also been reported as a promising strategy for locally advanced MMR-deficient colon cancer [5]. Recent rectal cancer trials and consensus guidance further indicate that perioperative and organ-preserving ICI strategies are rapidly evolving, although evidence from rectal cancer cannot be directly extrapolated to colon cancer because of differences in anatomy, local control strategy, and the feasibility of watch-and-wait approaches [11–13]. Our cases are consistent with this expanding literature and provide additional real-world surgical and pathological insights in patients initially considered at high risk of incomplete resection due to suspected adjacent organ invasion.

An important clinical challenge highlighted by our experience is the limited accuracy of conventional imaging for response assessment after immunotherapy. In the neoadjuvant setting, residual wall thickening, persistent lymphadenopathy, or inflammatory changes may reflect fibrosis and immune cell infiltration rather than viable tumor, potentially leading to discordance between radiological and pathological findings [14–16]. In the present series, two cases were considered radiological complete response and one case was considered near-complete response; nevertheless, all three achieved pCR. Thus, radiological-pathological concordance for complete response was descriptive only (2/3, 66.7%), and formal kappa analysis was not appropriate because all pathological outcomes were pCR. Post-treatment complete clinical response assessment should therefore be multimodal, including CT, MRI when adjacent organ involvement is suspected, PET-CT when clinically indicated, colonoscopy with biopsy, and tumor marker trends. However, unlike dMMR rectal cancer, for which nonoperative management is increasingly being investigated, watch-and-wait strategies for colon cancer remain investigational; therefore, curative resection remains essential for definitive staging and confirmation of pCR until validated colon-specific response criteria are established.

The key hypothesis generated by this case series is that pre-existing cytotoxic immune infiltration, reflected by high CD8+ T-cell density in baseline biopsies, may be a practical predictor of pCR to neoadjuvant ICI therapy. dMMR tumors are typically characterized by a high tumor mutational burden and abundant neoantigens, which facilitate T-cell priming and infiltration [3, 17]. However, not all dMMR tumors respond uniformly, and primary resistance has been reported. Candidate biomarkers such as tumor mutational burden, PD-L1 expression, interferon-gamma-related signatures, FOXP3+ regulatory T-cell infiltration, cytokine profiles, circulating immune cells, gut microbiome composition, and emerging modulators such as PCSK9 may improve prediction, but many require sequencing, multiplex assays, or specialized bioinformatics [12, 17–22]. In contrast, CD8 immunostaining is widely accessible, low cost, rapid, and can be applied to routine diagnostic biopsy tissue. Therefore, CD8+ T-cell density may be suitable as a first-line screening biomarker, while combined assessment with PD-L1, FOXP3, macrophage markers, TMB, and spatial digital pathology may provide higher predictive precision in future prospective studies.

Notably, we observed heterogeneity in the localization pattern of CD8 + cells, with one case showing IEL-predominant infiltration and two cases showing STL-predominant infiltration. These patterns can be interpreted within the framework of tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) phenotypes: immune-inflamed tumors show lymphocytes in direct contact with tumor cells, immune-excluded tumors retain lymphocytes in the surrounding stroma, and immune-desert tumors show minimal immune infiltration [23, 24]. IEL-predominant infiltration may reflect direct cytotoxic T-cell engagement with tumor epithelium, whereas STL-predominant infiltration may represent an inflamed stromal compartment that still permits rapid immune activation after PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade. Conversely, in other tumors, stromal restriction of CD8+ cells may represent immune exclusion driven by cancer-associated fibroblasts, transforming growth factor-beta signaling, abnormal extracellular matrix, or myeloid-mediated suppression [24]. Because all three cases achieved pCR, our data cannot determine whether IEL- or STL-predominant localization is superior. Incorporating digital pathology, spatial statistics, and complementary markers such as PD-L1, FOXP3, CD68/CD163, and TGF-beta pathway markers may help distinguish immune-inflamed responders from immune-excluded resistant tumors.

From a surgical perspective, the timing and safety of resection after neoadjuvant ICI therapy are critical. In our series, surgery was completed without perioperative immune-related complications, such as colitis, bowel perforation, adrenal crisis, or hyperinflammatory reactions. Perioperative monitoring in future cases should include systematic symptom screening for diarrhea/colitis, fever, rash, endocrinopathy, pneumonitis, and fatigue; laboratory assessment including complete blood count, liver and renal function tests, electrolytes, thyroid function, and cortisol when clinically indicated; and multidisciplinary review before surgery. Prospective studies such as NICHE-2 scheduled surgery after short-course immunotherapy and used timely surgery as a safety endpoint [2]. Nevertheless, the optimal interval between the final ICI dose and surgery remains undefined, and rare complications, including bowel obstruction precipitated by rapid immune-mediated tumor regression, have been reported [25]. The exact ICI-to-surgery interval was not uniformly available in this retrospective series, and no inference regarding optimal timing can be drawn. Standardizing the interval, monitoring irAEs, and ensuring endocrine replacement when needed may help mitigate perioperative risks. Furthermore, our case with suspected adjacent organ involvement illustrates that neoadjuvant ICI may convert borderline or initially unresectable disease to a resectable state, potentially avoiding more extensive multivisceral resection and improving the likelihood of R0 resection.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was small, and the results were derived from a limited number of patients, restricting generalizability and precluding formal statistical analysis. Second, although we added quantitative CD8+ T-cell counts per mm2 and hotspot analysis, the cut-off for dense infiltration was operational and exploratory; it should not be used for clinical decision-making without external validation. Third, exact ICI-to-surgery intervals, long-term recurrence-free survival (RFS), overall survival (OS), and baseline immune status indicators, including peripheral blood lymphocyte counts, CD4+/CD8 + ratio, and serum inflammatory cytokines, were not uniformly available from the retrospective records. Fourth, additional biomarkers such as TMB, interferon-gamma signatures, PD-L1, FOXP3, macrophage markers, ctDNA, and gut microbiome profiles were not systematically evaluated. Finally, follow-up remains relatively short, particularly for the present case, who remains recurrence-free 6 months after surgery and declined adjuvant chemotherapy. Although pCR after neoadjuvant immunotherapy is biologically encouraging, the safety of omitting adjuvant chemotherapy after pCR in locally advanced dMMR colon cancer remains uncertain and should be individualized according to pathological findings, patient preference, toxicity risk, and multidisciplinary discussion.

Conclusion

Our case series adds to the accumulating evidence supporting neoadjuvant immunotherapy for locally advanced dMMR colon cancer. The uniform achievement of pCR and the consistent finding of high baseline CD8+ T-cell density after quantitative conversion to cells/mm2 suggest that routine assessment of cytotoxic T-cell infiltration in pre-treatment biopsies may be a useful and pragmatic biomarker for predicting deep pathologic response. Prospective studies incorporating standardized quantitative thresholds, spatial TIME characterization, multimodal post-treatment response assessment, and long-term RFS/OS follow-up are warranted to validate these observations and guide personalized neoadjuvant strategies in colon cancer.
