Validity and Reliability of a Laser Pointer Device to Assess Knee Proprioception in Healthy and Active Individuals
Authors: Héctor González‐Pons, Elena Muñoz‐Gómez, Sara Mollà‐Casanova, Noemí Moreno‐Segura, Pilar Serra‐Añó, Marta Inglés
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and PurposeKnee proprioception offers valuable information about the state of the knee joint. The most widely used protocol to estimate it is the joint position reproduction (JPR), although the validated instruments for its measurement are expensive and complex. The aim of this study was to estimate the validity and reliability of a laser pointer device to assess knee proprioception in healthy and active subjects.MethodsA descriptive cross‐sectional study. The study was conducted at a Research Laboratory of the Faculty of Physiotherapy, University of Valencia (Spain). Twenty‐five healthy and active individuals (mean (SD) age = 27.24 (3.93) years) were assessed. Proprioception measurements were evaluated with 15° and 50° knee flexion in closed kinetic chain (CKC) and open kinetic chain (OKC), respectively, using a laser device and also a digital inclinometer (to validate the first), on two days by two different evaluators.ResultsValidity analysis indicated a moderate to strong correlation between the laser pointer device and the digital inclinometer in all the tests (Pearson's correlation = 0.40–0.55 (CKC) and 0.54–0.82 (OCK)). In the OKC 50° knee test, a moderate inter‐rater reliability (ICC = 0.54) and a strong intra‐rater reliability (ICC = 0.65) was shown, and in the CKC 15° test a strong inter‐rater reliability (ICC = 0.62) and moderate intra‐rater reliability (ICC = 0.58) was shown. The rest of the tests showed no intra‐rater and inter‐rater reliability (ICC = 0.10–0.24).DiscussionThough further research is needed, the results suggest that it could be considered as an affordable, simple and effective resource both in research and in clinical practice.

Introduction

Proprioception is defined as the ability to perceive movement, the position of a body segment, force, or tension (Busch et al. 2023). It plays a relevant role in motor control, posture and gait (Goble 2010; Henry and Baudry 2019). The knee joint is among the most extensively studied joints regarding proprioception (Hillier et al. 2015). It has been shown that knee proprioception is affected in athletes with injuries such as patellofemoral syndrome or anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears (Fleming et al. 2022; H.‐J. Kim et al. 2017), even months after the injury (Lee et al. 2015), and after surgery (Busch et al. 2021; Fleming et al. 2022). In ACL injuries, these alterations in knee proprioception are attributed to impaired muscle function and a decrease in physical activity level or balance capacity (Mehl et al. 2018).

The alteration of proprioception, represented as the impairment in the ability to reproduce specific joint position, assessed using joint proprioception reproduction (JPR) is related to the limits of stability variables and both are impaired in people with knee osteoarthritis (Raizah et al. 2023; Salamanna et al. 2023). It has been suggested that the deterioration of muscle spindles modifies the proprioceptive information sent to the central nervous system, thus weakening motor control (Salgado et al. 2015). In clinical practice, JPR is the most widely used proprioception measurement protocol (Bottoni et al. 2015; Munoz‐Gomez et al. 2025; Romero‐Franco and Jiménez‐Reyes 2015; Romero‐Franco et al. 2017; Salgado et al. 2015). This is mainly due to the capacity of this test to detect the subject's ability to recognize and reproduce a given position (Magalhães et al. 2010). To date, the most studied systems to perform this test have been the isokinetic dynamometer or photogrammetry; however, they involve expensive tools or a very laborious process (Romero‐Franco et al. 2017).

Other simpler and more affordable devices have been used to assess proprioception, the digital inclinometer being the most reliable one for the measurement of the knee JPR both in closed kinetic chain (CKC) (Romero‐Franco et al. 2017) and open kinetic chain (OKC) (Romero‐Franco et al. 2019). Considering closed and opened chains is important since there are significant differences in patterns of muscle activities and ligament forces between CKC and OKC (Mesfar and Shirazi‐Adl 2008). Assessing proprioception is also important for designing an effective intervention based on the evaluations. In this regard, a tool that serves both purposes—assessment and rehabilitation‐could be used. For this reason, another simple and cost‐effective system, such as laser pointers, could be utilized. Indeed, Laser‐based systems have been previously used to assess joint position sense in several anatomical regions, including the cervical spine (Ntenezakos et al. 2021), shoulder (Vafadar et al. 2016), and ankle (Olson et al. 2024), showing acceptable reliability and clinical feasibility (Balke et al. 2011; Vafadar et al. 2016). These methods typically quantify repositioning errors through the displacement of a laser beam attached to the body segment. Although some studies have incorporated laser devices in knee‐related contexts, these have primarily focused on rehabilitation or sensorimotor intervention protocols rather than on the evaluation of their measurement properties (Dengiz 2025). Consequently, evidence regarding the validity and reliability of laser‐based systems specifically for the assessment of knee joint position reproduction remains limited, warranting further investigation.

In this context, the validation of a laser pointer device for the evaluation of the JPR of the knee in CKC and CKA would be very advantageous, as it would allow this protocol to be applied quickly, affordably and easily and, subsequently, it could be used in rehabilitation. Thus, the aim of the present study was to determine the validity and reliability of a laser pointer device to measure knee JPR in healthy people.

Methods

Design

This was a cross‐sectional validation study in which inter‐ and intraobserver reliability and validity were compared with inclinometers.

Subjects

Twenty‐five healthy and active participants (i.e., participating in more than 150 min of moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity per week) aged between 18 and 40 years voluntarily participated in this study, according to previous studies (Vafadar et al. 2016). Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: cardiac, neurological or peripheral vascular diseases, acute infection, alcohol abuse, current treatment with analgesics, other injuries to the lower extremities or trunk, back pain, pregnancy, dementia, or other musculoskeletal disorders that would limit the successful execution of the test protocol.

Prior to examination, all participants were informed about the study objectives and signed an informed consent form according to the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Valencia (registration number: 1926087).

Procedure

Measurements were taken on the participants' dominant leg (the leg with which they normally kick a ball (Van Melick et al. 2017)). To assess laser reliability, a test‐retest was performed by two evaluators with no prior knowledge of the nature of the examination (Evaluator 1 and 2) over 2 days (8‐day apart). To determine inter‐rater reliability, both evaluators performed the measurements on the first day. The order in which the evaluators performed the assessments was counterbalanced, and participants had a 10‐min break between them.

On the second day, 8 days later, Evaluator 1 repeated the procedure. This allowed intra‐rater reliability to be determined. To assess laser validity, proprioception measurements were taken at the CKC and OKC by Evaluator 1 using both the digital inclinometer and the laser pointer device.

The tests were performed in OKC and CKC in two knee flexion positions (15° and 50°), according to previous studies (Vafadar et al. 2016) in a randomized order. Each test was performed three times, with a 2‐s rest between them. The absolute error (AE) of each position was calculated as follows: AE=[(targetposition–attempt1)+(targetposition–attempt2)+(targetposition–attempt3)]/3

Laser‐based measurements provide a linear displacement of the projected point rather than a direct measure of joint angulation. Therefore, for the laser device, the linear error was converted into angular error using the trigonometric relationship θ = arctan (e/L), where θ represents the angular error (in degrees), e is the measured linear error (distance between the target point and the participant's attempt), and L is the distance from the joint axis of rotation to the point where the laser pointer was positioned. This transformation allowed the error derived from the laser measurements to be expressed in angular units, thereby accounting for differences in limb length across participants.

Measurement of Knee Proprioception in CKC With the Laser Pointer Device

A protocol similar to that proposed by Romero‐Franco et al. (Romero‐Franco et al. 2017) was used, but using a laser pointer device (Motion Guidance Pro, Colorado, USA). First, the laser pointer was fixed with a strap on the anterior side of the thigh (two thirds distal the distance between the anterior superior iliac spine and the upper pole of the patella).

Then, the participant was asked to place the foot of their dominant leg on a 5 cm high wedge to reduce passive tension in the triceps surae (Magalhães et al. 2010), as well as to rest their contralateral hand on a stable high table to avoid balance problems (Stillman and McMeeken 2001). To prevent weight bearing on the non‐dominant leg, it was placed on a 7.5 cm high yoga brick (Figure 1A). To eliminate visual input, the participant was required to wear a blindfold.

Knee proprioception measurement in closed kinetic chain (A) and open kinetic chain (B).

Afterward, the evaluator fixed the laser pointer pointing toward the distal end of the second toe of the participant's dominant leg, with the leg in 0° extension at the hip and knee. A measuring tape was fixed to the floor to determine the distance of the laser from the baseline position as the participant flexed the knee.

Using a goniometer, the evaluator asked the participant to flex the dominant knee until it reached 15°, at which point the participant was instructed to “stop for three seconds and remember that position.” Then, the evaluator instructed the participant to “return to the starting position.” From that position (full extension), the participant was asked to reach the remembered position by his/herself, saying “here” upon reaching it and holding it for 3 seconds. Finally, the participant returned to the initial position.

This procedure was repeated for the 50° knee flexion position, which is referred to as the intermediate range of knee flexion, where muscle sensor mediation predominates in proprioception (Ribeiro et al. 2011). Subjects rested for 2 seconds between each of the three repetitions. All the participants conducted a familiarization session for both CKC and OKC, as described below, during the first day.

Measurement of Knee Proprioception in OKC With the Laser Pointer Device

The laser pointer device was placed with a strap on the lateral side of the lateral malleolus.

The participant sat on a stretcher, with a 90° hip and knee flexion position and a 5 cm wedge under the thigh of their dominant leg to obtain a horizontal position (Figure 1B). The participant was instructed to keep his/her trunk straight and perform movements only with the leg.

With the participant sitting at 20 cm to a cork panel, the evaluator initially measured the knee flexion positions of 15° and 50° with a digital inclinometer (target position), and placed a pin on the panel at the point where the laser pointed in each of these three positions.

Once the three positions were marked, the measurement procedure was identical to those described before. However, in this case, the evaluator placed another pin at the point marked by the laser pointer during each of the participant's performances, and used a measuring tape to measure the distance between the two pins. The average of the three repetitions of each position was calculated.

Measurement of Knee Proprioception in CKC With the Digital Inclinometer

A digital inclinometer (0.3° precision Limit mini digital inclinometer (50 × 50 × 32 mm) (Alingsas, Sweden)) was used (Romero‐Franco et al. 2017). It was fixed with a strap at the same point on the anterior thigh where the laser pointer device had been placed.

The procedure was similar to that described previously. Briefly, the participant was asked to adopt the same starting position (full hip and knee extension), at which point the inclinometer was calibrated to 0°. Then, identical verbal commands were used to instruct the participants to reach, remember and reposition the same knee positions (i.e., 15° and 50°).

Measurement of Knee Proprioception in OKC With the Digital Inclinometer

The inclinometer was fixed with a strap at the midpoint between the lower pole of the patella and the lateral malleolus, on the anterior side of the leg. The participant adopted the same position described before, and the measurement procedure was also similar.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v.29 (IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Standard statistical methods were used to obtain the mean and standard deviation (SD). Normality of the sample was assessed using Shapiro‐Wilk test. The convergent validity of the laser, compared to the inclinometer, was determined by calculating the Pearson's correlation analysis. Inter‐rater and intra‐rater reliability were determined using the intraclass correlation coefficient (2,1 ICC). Correlations greater than 0.8 were considered excellent; between 0.6 and 0.8 were strong; and between 0.4 and 0.6 were moderate. Values ranging from 0.2 to 0.4 or below 0.2 were considered indicators of weak or very weak reliability or validity, respectively (Wuensch and Evans 1996). Relationships were considered statistically significant at the level of p < 0.05.

Discussion

The evaluation of the knee proprioception in clinical practice provides a great deal of information to identify and control possible proprioceptive alterations (Bottoni et al. 2015; Romero‐Franco and Jiménez‐Reyes 2015; Salgado et al. 2015). Its quick and easy assessment using low‐cost tools may offer very valuable information to objectify a possible improvement after an injury and thus continue planning the recovery program in the most appropriate way (Arumugam et al. 2021; Kaya et al. 2019). Therefore, the purpose of the current study was to demonstrate the validity and reliability of a laser pointer device in measuring knee JPR in healthy active subjects compared to the digital inclinometer.

Due to the limited correlation between JPR measured during OKC and CKC movements (Herrington 2005), it was relevant to test the laser pointer device in both patterns. The CKC proprioceptive assessment has been shown to be more sensitive in detecting proprioceptive alterations in athletes (Romero‐Franco et al. 2017), while the OKC procedure requires the recruitment of more motor units and eccentric contraction (Romero‐Franco et al. 2019).

In the OKC, the results showed that, in the 50° knee flexion position, the laser pointer device showed a strong validity, as well as moderate inter‐rater validity and strong intra‐rater reliability for measuring knee proprioception. This finding is especially relevant, since this position represents the midpoint of the knee flexion range, where the mediation of muscle sensors predominates in detecting the knee position (Clark et al. 2016; Ribeiro et al. 2011).

On the contrary, in the CKC, the laser pointer device also showed a moderate validity in the 15° knee flexion, with strong inter‐rater reliability and moderate intra‐rater reliability. This position requires less physical and joint demands, which could disturb the results of the test at the proprioceptive level. In this sense, the population with knee joint pathology may be unable to successfully perform a single‐leg squat to reach greater knee flexion positions in CKC (Romero‐Franco et al. 2017). Therefore, the results of the current study show that 15° knee flexion position in CKC would be the position of choice to assess knee proprioception among this population.

The laser pointer device has already been validated to measure shoulder proprioception in healthy subjects, showing high inter‐rater (ICC = 0.92) and intra‐rater (ICC = 0.81) reliability in measuring shoulder proprioception in a low range of flexion (shoulder flexion of 55°) (Vafadar et al. 2016), as well as to measure ankle proprioception (inter‐rater (ICC = 0.91) and intra‐rater (ICC = 0.93) reliability) (Olson et al. 2024). Regarding the knee, this device has recently been used not to assess proprioception itself but to assess the validity of a squat test in healthy subjects and those who have undergone anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (Cardoso et al. 2021). it has been used as a component of sensorimotor training interventions focused on the knee (Dengiz 2025). However, to our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the validity and reliability of a laser pointer device to measure knee proprioception in healthy active subjects.

In this study, as justified above, the best predetermined positions to measure knee proprioception in healthy people were the knee flexion positions of 15° in CKC and 50° in OKC, the latter being the most used in clinical practice in previous studies (Ribeiro et al. 2011; Romero‐Franco et al. 2017). In these positions, the laser device is shown to be a valid and reliable instrument in the measurement of knee proprioception.

This is important because laser devices are used in clinical practice because of their reliability and versatility in providing proprioceptive feedback during rehabilitation exercises. Laser‐guided feedback has been successfully applied in the assessment and training of other joints and body structures. particularly for cervical proprioception in patients with chronic neck pain (Ntenezakos et al. 2021; Swanson et al. 2025). Similarly, laser‐based feedback has been used in populations with musculoskeletal pain, such as individuals with shoulder instability (Balke et al. 2011), subacromial pain syndrome (Favaro et al. 2023) and rotator cuff injury (Langer et al. 2020) In addition, laser pointer visual feedback has been incorporated into trunk stabilization exercises in patients with chronic stroke, demonstrating significant improvements in balance and gait (K.‐I. Kim et al. 2023) Given that knee JPR has shown to be significantly associated with joint stability (Raizah et al. 2023), and that proprioceptive and neuromuscular training are considered essential components in the prevention and rehabilitation of knee injuries (Arumugam et al. 2021; Kaya et al. 2019; Ogard 2011), he findings of the present study suggest that the laser pointer devices may also represent a useful tool for proprioceptive training within knee rehabilitation programs.

The laser pointer device has been proven to be a valid and reliable instrument to assess knee proprioception at 15° knee flexion in CKC and 50° knee flexion in OKC in healthy and active subjects. Although further research is needed to confirm our findings, the results shown here suggest that it could be considered as an affordable, simple and effective resource both in research and in clinical practice.

Limitations

Only healthy individuals were included, while the population with knee joint pathology may be unable to successfully perform a single‐leg squat to reach the different positions in CKC. Future studies should ascertain these results in other populations. A further limitation is the small sample size (n = 25), which may have reduced statistical power and widened confidence intervals around ICC and correlation estimates. Although this sample size is comparable or even larger than comparable preliminary reliability studies (Romero‐Franco et al. 2019, 2017; Vafadar et al. 2016), findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples are warranted to confirm these results and enhance their generalizability. Furthermore, intra‐rater reliability was assessed only for one evaluator; therefore, future studies should examine intra‐rater reliability for all evaluators to further strengthen the methodological robustness of the measurement procedure. An additional limitation is the use of a digital inclinometer with 0.3° accuracy as the reference device, as more recent models offer substantially higher precision. However, given the inherent biological variability of proprioception assessment, this difference is unlikely to have meaningfully impacted the validity estimates reported. Finally, the laser pointer device was validated against a digital inclinometer rather than against laboratory‐based gold‐standard systems such as isokinetic dynamometers or three‐dimensional motion analysis. However, the digital inclinometer has previously demonstrated good reliability and validity for assessing knee joint position reproduction and represents a practical and widely used tool in clinical settings (Romero‐Franco et al. 2019, 2017). Future studies should compare the laser pointer device with high‐precision motion analysis systems to further examine its measurement accuracy.
