Impact of Web-Based Medical Education on Rhythm Control in Atrial Fibrillation: Retrospective Case–Control Analysis
Authors: Margaret Harris, Katie S. Lucero, Nicholas Sidorovich, Sarah Bomba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntiarrhythmic drug (AAD) therapy remains underutilised in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF), despite guideline recommendations. Web-based continuing medical education (CME) may improve clinician knowledge and performance, buteffect on real-world practice is less clear. This study evaluated the impact of CME on knowledge, competence, and confidence in rhythm control with AADs, and assessed real-world AAD use among cardiologists, internal medicine (IM), and general practitioners (GP). The study included two phases. Phase 1 assessed CME outcomes in a pre/post study of 207 US physicians. Phase 2 examined practice patterns through a retrospective, matched comparison of 501 physicians with claims data. Phase 1 demonstrated a 22% improvement in guideline-based AAD knowledge (59% vs 72%, p < .001), a 57% increase in competence tailoring rhythm control strategies (30% vs 47%, p < .001), and a 79% increase in confidence with AAD therapy (19% vs 34%, p < .001). In Phase 2, the intervention group had a 3.20% increase in AAD use compared with 1.71% increase in the comparison group, with 6% more patients receiving AADs post-education. Web-based CME on guideline-based AAD selection improved physician knowledge, competence, and confidence, and increased real-world AAD utilisation.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia and affects up to 6.1 million people in the United States [1], with prevalence projected to increase to 12.1 million in 2030 [2]. AF is associated with an increased risk for stroke [3], heart failure [4], myocardial infarction [5], and chronic kidney disease [6].

Growing evidence indicates that initiating rhythm control early in patients with AF who have not developed long-standing AF could mitigate atrial remodelling, avert AF-related complications such as heart failure and stroke, and potentially alleviate symptomatic AF, therefore warranting broader adoption [7,8]. In addition, recent research and guideline recommendations suggest a paradigm shift away from rate control to rhythm control for patients with new-onset AF [9–12]. However, despite the publication of guidelines for the management of AF, there is considerable heterogeneity in AF management related to pharmacological treatment [13,14]. Pharmacotherapy with antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) remains a cornerstone of therapy for maintenance of sinus rhythm in specific types of patients with AF, but these therapies are underutilised in clinical practice and often not used in concordance with clinical practice guidelines; this can put patients at risk for suboptimal outcomes [15–18]. Nevertheless, non-adherence to guidelines may be reasonable in certain patients if careful consideration is given to the risk–benefit ratio of AADs and patients are closely monitored [19]. Even in the current era of ablation being suggested as first-line therapy in paroxysmal AF [20,21], appropriate use of AADs should be considered in the waiting period before ablation or for rhythm control after a few episodes of AF, particularly as “pill in the pocket” treatment for AF-related symptom control [19]. Moreover, AADs are a reasonable option as first-line therapy with ablation reserved for use in the event AADs fail [20].

Importantly, the results of a recent physician survey covering the most controversial topics related to AF management, such as screening, pharmacological treatment, ablation strategy, and management of comorbidities, suggest that a lack of adequate continuing medical education (CME) may be partly responsible for potential knowledge gaps regarding guideline content [14].

Continuing medical education is an effective tool to close physician practice gaps related to AF among cardiologists and primary care physicians (PCPs) and can potentially help improve patient outcomes, but the impact of web-based CME on knowledge, competence, confidence, and real-world outcomes (RWOs) for the treatment selection among clinicians who treat patients with AF has not been researched to our knowledge [15,22]. A PubMed search based on Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and the major search terms “Atrial Fibrillation”, “Continuing Medical Education”, “Antiarrhythmic Drugs”, “Real World Outcomes”, and the logical operator “AND” yielded no results. The aim of this study was to investigate the effect of a series of two web-based CME activities about AF rhythm control on physician understanding; competencies; confidence; and real-world selection of AADs in real-world clinical practice. We chose to focus on this class for this project specifically due to evidence indicating that AADs are often underutilised in clinical practice, particularly for patients who could benefit from them, such as those who have undergone ablation procedures [19].

Materials and Methods

Study Design

Each CME activity’s format included a roundtable discussion with three faculty experts. Primary outcomes included matched pre- and post-CME assessment results and the number of patients with AF who were given AADs. US cardiologist and IM/GP learners who participated in at least one of the two CME activities from 22 July 2022 to 16 November 2022 were considered for inclusion in the study. Learners were recruited via email and with drivers on Medscape. In addition, Medscape registered members (free registration) can browse and find content on their own. Participation was voluntary. Phase 1 focused on knowledge, competency, and self-efficacy outcomes from a paired, pre/post analysis based upon questionnaire data within 2 CME activities for the learners included in the real-world study. Learners had to complete all questions in at least one activity.

Cardiologists and IM/GP learners from 17 May 2022 to 16 November 2022 were considered for inclusion in the evaluation. Sample

Phase 1: CME Learning Outcomes

Phase 1 participants represented a subset of Phase 2 cohort, with outcomes assessment specifically localised to the real-world sample from Phase 2. Of the 501 participants in the Phase 2 cohort, 207 had complete pre-/post-assessment data and were included in the Phase 1 CME learning outcomes analysis (127 cardiologists, 80 IM/GP physicians). One hundred and two participants included in the study completed the first activity (56 cardiologists, 46 IM/GP physicians), and 105 completed the second activity (71 cardiologists, 74 IM/GP physicians). Ninety-eight participants completed both activities (74 cardiologists, 24 IM/GPs).

Phase 2: Real World

A total of 501 learners (225 cardiologists, 276 IM/GP physicians) who matched claims data and met the inclusion criteria were included in the evaluation. They coded for AF for 24,026 patients (18,488 patients for cardiologists, 5,551 for IM/GP physicians) in the 6 months prior to CME participation.

Measures

Phase 1 and Phase 2

Participation

For the purposes of this study, learners are defined as those who proceeded past the front matter and began 1 or more of the CME activities online. Their first participation date is herein their “index date”.

Demographics

Medscape member registration provided country of residence, profession, and speciality.

Phase 1: CME Learning Outcomes

Knowledge/Competence and Confidence

Data were collected for ≤12 weeks following each of the 2 online CME activities. A 3-item, multiple-choice, knowledge/competence questionnaire (knowledge based multiple choice and case-based multiple-choice questions) and 1 Likert-type confidence rating question (scale 1 to 5) were administered before and after each activity. A rating of 4 or 5 was considered “confident”.

Phase 2: Real World

Treatment Selection in the Real World

Time-aligned data were pulled from administrative pharmacy and medical claims. Claims were aggregated in the patient and then each healthcare professional (HCP) of interest. Patients with AF, indicating at least 1 code for AF in any of the patients (see Appendix for codes) and 1 AAD (amiodarone, dofetilide, dronedarone, flecainide, propafenone, sotalol) were considered to be treated appropriately. The number of patients who met these criteria and had the codes attributed to the HCP of interest indicated the number of patients with AAD treatment selection for AF. The primary endpoint was the proportion of patients with AF with an AAD 6 months after each HCP’s index date.

Statistical Analysis

Phase 1: CME Learning Outcomes

Physician learners who completed both pre- and post-assessment questions for the 2 CME activities were aggregated across activities, stratified by learning theme. A McNemar test was used to assess statistical significance of the paired proportions of questions correct responses and confident learners pre- versus post-CME with p < .05 considered statistically significant.

Phase 2: Real World

A 2-sample t test was conducted using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute) to examine the differences in between the CME and matched-comparison group in the mean change in proportion of patients with AF with AAD. The proportion of patients with AF with AAD prescribed by the HCP of interest was calculated 6 months before and 6 months after the index date. The change from pre to post was calculated to examine statistical significance in the mean difference in change for the CME and matched-comparison groups.

Ethical Considerations

This study was exempt under exempt from institutional review board approval under approval number 12,331 because it was compliant with the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) and Medscape privacy policy and used existing study data that were deidentified to the investigator under 45 CFR § 46.104(d)(4) and qualified for educational research exemption under 45 CFR § 46.104(d)(1).

Results

Phase 1: CME Learning Outcomes

Significant improvements were seen for knowledge/competence and confidence for each learning theme across all cardiologist and IM/GP physician participants. There was a 22-percentage point increase in correct responses from pre- to post-education (59% vs 72%, p < .001) for knowledge of the evidence and guideline-based use of AADs for the treatment of AF. There was a 57-percentage point increase in correct responses from pre to post (30% vs 47%, p < .001) for competence with tailoring rhythm control strategies in patients with AF in accordance with the latest evidence and guideline recommendations. There was a 79% relative increase in proportion who were confident tailing AAD therapy in patients with AF in accordance with the latest evidence and guideline-based recommendations from pre to post (19% vs 34%, p < .001).

Phase 2: Real World

Descriptive results show that cardiologists were providing better evidence-based care than were the IM/GP physicians. A higher proportion of their patients were on rhythm control with AADs at baseline than were the IM/GP physicians’ patients (Table 2). Pre-CME, there was a 3.19% difference in total number of patients with AF with an AAD between the CME and control groups, changing to 5.80% post-CME. Discussion

Principal Findings

Among learners who participated in these activities, relative percentage increases were seen from pre to post for knowledge of the evidence and guideline-based use of AADs for the treatment of AF and for competence with tailoring rhythm control strategies in patients with AF in accordance with the latest evidence and guideline recommendations. In addition, there was a relative percentage increase from pre to post for confidence in tailoring AAD therapy in patients with AF in accordance with the latest evidence and guideline-based recommendations after education.

The longitudinal and sequenced delivery of the 2 CME activities employed in this study was planned to facilitate optimal impact on physician knowledge, competence, performance, and real-world outcomes. CME that is delivered in a longitudinal or sequenced manner and that provides interactive methods to facilitate implementation in the practice setting has been shown to have a positive effect on knowledge, competence [23], and performance change in clinical practice [24,25].

Studies have shown that online CME is associated with increased use of evidence-based treatment selection [26] and appropriate prescribing in the real world [27]. Interestingly, this association with real-world clinical behaviours has been found for various CME formats. Lucero et al. evaluated the impact of an online, case-based, virtual patient simulation CME intervention on real-world use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) in patients with type 2 diabetes. Among endocrinologists and PCPs, the intervention was associated with a 24.8% increase in the use of GLP-1 RAs, compared with a 12.7% increase in the comparison group [26]. Whyte et al. evaluated the effect of targeted short-form messages or CME, or both, on inappropriate fluoroquinolone antibiotic prescribing among high prescribers. Targeted short-form messages and CME reduced new-prescription volume by 8.5% and 12.3%, respectively, while pairing targeted short-form messages with education reduced new-prescription volume by 21.7% [27].

In this study, outcomes data at 6 months post-education for the group participating in CME showed 6% more patients treated with AADs than in the matched comparison group. Our results demonstrate that CME is associated with positive real-world outcomes in clinician practice and patient outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, we were unable to determine clinicians’ rationale for and application of guideline-based use of AADs for AF, as well as the potential impact of the November 2023 AF guideline updates, which included new guidance for rhythm control. The retrospective nature of the study introduced potential confounding variables, and the lack of randomisation and sample size constraints further limit the interpretation of findings.

While AF diagnosis codes were used to identify the study population, relying on a single AF code may lack the specificity achieved by requiring two or more codes, as is standard in much of the research in this area. Additionally, our methodology does not allow us to fully assess whether the use of AADs adhered to guideline-directed recommendations. Critical factors such as QRS duration, QT interval length, or the presence of contraindicating comorbidities (e.g. coronary artery disease or heart failure when using class 1c agents) were not captured in the dataset. As a result, while we identified patients prescribed AADs, we cannot definitively determine if these treatments were aligned with guideline-based care.

It is also important to note that the clinical guidelines in effect during the study period were the 2019 AHA/ACC/HRS Focused Update of the 2014 Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of Patients with Atrial Fibrillation. Since then, the 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS Guideline for the Diagnosis and Management of Atrial Fibrillation has been published, introducing notable updates that may have implications for the interpretation of our findings. In particular, the 2023 guideline places greater emphasis on early rhythm control, with a Class I recommendation for rhythm control in patients with symptomatic AF and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), as well as a broader endorsement of rhythm control in selected patients with newly diagnosed AF. These changes may lead to increased use of antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) in certain populations. At the same time, the guideline strengthens the recommendation for catheter ablation in symptomatic AF – from a Class IIa to a Class I recommendation in patients with symptomatic paroxysmal or persistent AF refractory or intolerant to at least one AAD – potentially mitigating some of the anticipated rise in AAD prescribing by positioning ablation as a primary rhythm control strategy. Together, these evolving recommendations reflect a shift towards more aggressive rhythm control strategies overall, but with a more individualised approach to therapy selection, which should be considered when interpreting our findings within the context of current practice.

The selection of a 6-month follow-up period introduces an additional limitation. This period was chosen to align with an average clinical monitoring interval of 3 months, aiming to capture evidence-based AAD use during typical AF management. However, this timeframe makes it challenging to attribute changes to the CME interventions and assess the durability of observed prescribing behaviours over a longer period, as such effects may diminish over time.

Unmeasured confounders also warrant consideration. For example, clinicians’ motivation to complete the online CME intervention may have introduced selection bias, with this motivation itself contributing to observed effects. Although the study design minimised sampling bias, other unmeasured confounders, such as consumption of additional educational content (e.g. other CME activities, published studies, or collegial discussions) and clinicians’ motivation to adjust treatment practices, may have influenced results. Furthermore, the design and format of the CME activities could have served as an unmeasured confounder, as certain formats may align better with individual learning preferences.

The reliance on retrospective data further limits the study due to potential variability in data completeness across external databases. Lastly, the generalisability of the findings is restricted by the reliance on data that may not fully represent broader clinician populations.

Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into AAD usage patterns in clinical practice and highlights areas for future research to clarify the alignment of treatment decisions with guideline-based recommendations.

Conclusions

Employment of a CME-certified, 2-activity curriculum significantly improved knowledge, competence, and confidence and improved real-world rhythm control with AADs among cardiologists and IM/GP physicians treating patients with AF.
