Distinguishing Detectable From Clinically Relevant Immunogenicity With Subcutaneous Infliximab
Authors: Min‐Yuan Lu, Yu‐Ping Shu, Rong Shi
Published: 2026

Conflicts of Interest

The authors declare no conflicts of interest.

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the post hoc analysis by Colombel and colleagues examining the impact of anti‐drug antibodies (ADAs) on outcomes in patients with moderate‐to‐severe inflammatory bowel disease receiving subcutaneous infliximab maintenance therapy in the LIBERTY trials [1]. The authors should be congratulated for using a highly sensitive, drug‐tolerant assay and for exploring immunogenicity not only by ADA occurrence but also by titer.

Our concern relates to the central exposure definition. Classifying patients as “ADA‐positive” if they had at least one confirmed positive sample after treatment initiation creates a clinically heterogeneous group. Under this framework, a patient with a single transient, low‐titer ADA result is analyzed together with a patient who has persistent, high‐titer positivity across multiple visits. In intravenous infliximab therapy, however, persistent rather than transient immunogenicity is generally the phenotype most closely linked to secondary loss of response and treatment discontinuation [2, 3].

This distinction may be particularly important for subcutaneous infliximab. In the present analysis, ADA‐positive patients had lower infliximab concentrations from early follow‐up onwards; however, this pharmacokinetic disadvantage did not translate into statistically significant differences in week‐54 efficacy. One explanation is that the higher and more stable drug exposure conferred by the subcutaneous route [4] may blunt the short‐term clinical impact of transient or low‐level ADA, thereby diluting the effect of truly consequential immunogenicity within a broad ever‐positive category. Although the titer‐based analyses are valuable, particularly because higher ADA titers were associated with lower drug concentrations and a non‐significant pattern toward lower efficacy, titer magnitude and temporal persistence are not interchangeable.

Building on this acknowledged limitation, a clinically informative re‐analysis would be to classify ADA responses according to both temporal pattern and titer—for example, single/transient low‐titer positivity, persistent low‐to‐moderate positivity, and persistent high‐titer positivity—and to evaluate these phenotypes using landmark or time‐updated models. It would also be informative to explore whether such phenotypes are associated with subsequent dose escalation, drug persistence, or time to loss of response, and whether they modify the apparent benefit of concomitant immunomodulator use. These refinements may help move the field from merely detecting ADA to identifying which ADA responses truly matter for therapeutic decision‐making in the subcutaneous infliximab era.

We look forward to the authors' perspective on these questions.
