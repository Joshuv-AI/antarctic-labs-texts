Emergency pericardiocentesis as a bridge to surgical repair in left ventricular free wall rupture complicated by cardiac tamponade: a case report and literature review

Introduction

Left ventricular free wall rupture (LVFWR) is a rare but catastrophic mechanical complication of acute myocardial infarction (MI) [1]. Although reported in approximately 4% of patients with acute MI [1] and 0.2% of those presenting with acute coronary syndromes [2], its true incidence is likely underestimated because many cases result in out-of-hospital death before a diagnosis can be established [3]. Early recognition and timely intervention are essential for survival [4, 5].

Surgical repair remains the cornerstone of treatment and is associated with better survival than conservative management [6]. Nevertheless, in-hospital mortality remains substantial, reflecting the hemodynamic instability and critical condition of these patients [6]. Furthermore, management of patients with LVFWR complicated by cardiac tamponade before definitive surgical repair remains challenging, particularly when immediate operative intervention is not available [4, 5].

In this report, we describe a patient with delayed anterior ST-segment elevation MI (STEMI) complicated by LVFWR, hemopericardium, and cardiac tamponade who developed rapidly progressive circulatory collapse. Rescue fluoroscopy-guided pericardiocentesis provided temporary hemodynamic stabilization and facilitated successful transfer for definitive surgical repair. We used the CARE reporting guideline [7] to draft this manuscript, and the CARE reporting checklist [8] when editing, included in supplementary materials.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 62-year-old man presented to the Emergency Department of Rasool Akram Hospital, Tehran, Iran, with a 2-day history of persistent chest pain and epigastric discomfort. His medical history was unremarkable except for long-term tobacco use (30 pack in a year).

On admission, he appeared acutely ill, sitting upright with mild respiratory distress and ongoing chest discomfort. He was afebrile. Vital signs revealed a heart rate of 103 beats per min in sinus rhythm, blood pressure (BP) of 80/50 mmHg, and respiratory rate of 28 breaths/min. Jugular venous pressure was elevated to approximately 6 cm above the sternal angle. Cardiac examination demonstrated regular heart sounds with an S3 gallop and no audible murmur or pericardial rub. Pulmonary examination revealed bilaterally diminished breath sounds. Abdominal and neurological examinations were unremarkable.

The initial 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus rhythm with ST-segment elevation of up to 3 mm in leads I, aVL, and V2–V5, accompanied by reciprocal ST-segment depression in leads II, III, and aVF, consistent with an acute anterior STEMI (Fig. 1). The patient was immediately transferred to the catheterization laboratory for coronary angiography and primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) [9].

Initial 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrating acute anterior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, with ST-segment elevation in leads I, aVL, and V2–V5 and reciprocal ST-segment depression in the inferior leads

Coronary angiography demonstrated significant thrombotic stenosis in the mid-left anterior descending artery (LAD) and total thrombotic occlusion of the ostial first diagonal branch. In addition, the right coronary artery (RCA) exhibited significant stenoses in both the proximal and mid segments (Fig. 2-A). In accordance with current evidence favoring culprit-lesion revascularization in the acute setting [10], PCI was performed on the LAD and first diagonal branch, while the RCA lesions were managed conservatively. Two drug-eluting stents were successfully implanted, resulting in restoration of TIMI grade 3 flow (Fig. 2-B).

Coronary angiography and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). A Coronary angiography demonstrating significant thrombotic stenosis of the mid left anterior descending (LAD) artery and total thrombotic occlusion of the first diagonal branch. B Final coronary angiography results following PCI, demonstrating successful revascularization with restoration of TIMI grade 3 flow

Despite successful revascularization, persistent hypotension (SBP = 80 mmHg) prompted urgent transthoracic echocardiography (ECHO) to evaluate for mechanical complications of MI. ECHO revealed severe hypokinesis of the anterior and lateral walls, an approximately 2-cm echo-dense pericardial collection consistent with hematoma, and evidence of cardiac tamponade, including right atrial and right ventricular diastolic collapse. A defect in the apical left ventricular free wall was identified, consistent with LVFWR (Fig. 3).

Transthoracic echocardiography. Arrow demonstrates a large echo-dense pericardial collection consistent with hemopericardium, associated with cardiac tamponade physiology in the setting of left ventricular free wall rupture

During the examination, progressive enlargement of the pericardial hematoma was observed. Simultaneously, the patient developed worsening dyspnea, diaphoresis, confusion, and progressive hemodynamic deterioration. Repeat BP measurement demonstrated a systolic pressure of 60 mmHg. Intravenous fluid resuscitation and norepinephrine infusion were initiated immediately, and emergency cardiac surgical consultation was obtained. However, despite aggressive supportive therapy, the patient’s condition continued to deteriorate, with impending circulatory collapse.

Therapeutic intervention

Given the patient’s rapidly deteriorating hemodynamic status and the anticipated delay of approximately 3 h before definitive surgical intervention could be initiated, a multidisciplinary team decided to perform rescue fluoroscopy-guided pericardiocentesis as a bridge-to-surgery strategy, approximately 1 h after completion of primary PCI.

The patient was immediately transferred back to the catheterization laboratory, where pericardiocentesis was successfully performed under fluoroscopic guidance. Since the primary objective was controlled relief of cardiac tamponade rather than complete evacuation of the hemorrhagic pericardial effusion, drainage was guided by the patient’s hemodynamic response rather than a predetermined volume. Approximately 20 mL of hemorrhagic fluid was aspirated initially, followed by repeated incremental aspirations using a 20-mL syringe, with continuous reassessment of systolic BP, mental status, and overall hemodynamic status after each aspiration. After five incremental aspirations (cumulative volume, approximately 100 mL), systolic BP increased from 60 to 100 mmHg within minutes, mental status normalized, and the clinical signs of shock resolved. As adequate hemodynamic stabilization had been achieved and definitive surgical repair was imminent, further drainage was intentionally withheld (Fig. 4). The pericardial drainage catheter was left in situ until surgery to allow immediate access should recurrent cardiac tamponade or hemodynamic deterioration occur; however, the patient remained stable and no additional drainage was required.

Fluoroscopy-guided rescue pericardiocentesis. Fluoroscopic image demonstrating pericardial catheter placement for controlled drainage of hemorrhagic pericardial effusion in a patient with cardiac tamponade secondary to left ventricular free wall rupture

Following hemodynamic stabilization, approximately 2.5 h after pericardiocentesis, the patient underwent emergency cardiac surgery. After establishment of cardiopulmonary bypass, hemopericardium and organized thrombus were evacuated from the pericardial cavity (Fig. 5A). Inspection of the heart revealed an extensive anterior wall myocardial infarction and a 6-mm defect in the left ventricular free wall. Definitive repair was performed using a large Dacron patch covering the infarcted myocardium with an approximately 1-cm margin of viable surrounding tissue (Fig. 5B). The patient tolerated the procedure well, had an uncomplicated postoperative course, and was discharged following successful rehabilitation.

Intraoperative findings and surgical repair. A Evacuation of hemopericardium and organized thrombus following establishment of cardiopulmonary bypass. B Repair of the left ventricular free wall (LVFW) covering the infarcted myocardium with an adequate margin of healthy tissue

Follow-up and outcomes

At the one-month follow-up visit, the patient remained clinically stable and reported no chest pain, dyspnea, or other cardiovascular symptoms. Physical examination and vital signs were unremarkable. Follow-up transthoracic ECHO demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 35%, mild left ventricular enlargement, mild mitral regurgitation, and a minimal residual pericardial effusion without evidence of recurrent cardiac tamponade.

Patient perspective

The patient described significant anxiety and distress during the period preceding diagnosis and throughout the acute phase of hospitalization. At follow-up, he expressed satisfaction with the treatment outcome and reported a substantial improvement in his overall quality of life.

Discussion

Our patient exhibited several characteristic features of LVFWR, including delayed presentation after STEMI, persistent hypotension despite successful coronary reperfusion, progressive hemodynamic deterioration, and echocardiographic evidence of hemopericardium with cardiac tamponade. Prompt transthoracic echocardiography enabled early recognition of this mechanical complication before irreversible circulatory collapse occurred. Despite aggressive fluid resuscitation and vasopressor support, the patient developed rapidly progressive shock with impending cardiac arrest. Although definitive surgical repair was planned, immediate operative intervention was delayed because of logistical constraints. Under these circumstances, fluoroscopy-guided pericardiocentesis was performed as a temporizing bridge-to-surgery intervention. Incremental drainage of only 100 mL of hemorrhagic pericardial fluid resulted in immediate restoration of systolic blood pressure and clinical stabilization, allowing successful transfer for definitive surgical repair (Fig. 6).

LVFWR accounts for approximately 30% of in-hospital deaths related to acute MI and represents the third leading cause of early mortality following STEMI, after cardiogenic shock and heart failure [11]. Despite advances in reperfusion therapy, LVFWR remains associated with a dismal prognosis because many patients experience sudden hemodynamic collapse before the diagnosis is established. Consequently, a high index of clinical suspicion is essential, particularly in patients who develop unexplained hypotension or cardiogenic shock after MI [4]. Successful coronary reperfusion does not exclude the possibility of LVFWR, ventricular septal rupture, or papillary muscle rupture, and urgent bedside ECHO should be performed whenever these complications are suspected [12].

Most cases of LVFWR occur during the first week after MI, with nearly 40% occurring within the first 24 h [4]. Several clinical factors have been associated with an increased risk of rupture, including advanced age, first MI, delayed or unsuccessful reperfusion, and persistent hypotension [11, 13]. The underlying mechanism is thought to involve increased mechanical stress at the junction between infarcted and viable myocardium, resulting in progressive myocardial tearing and eventual rupture [5].

Clinical presentation is often nonspecific and may range from recurrent chest pain to abrupt circulatory collapse [5]. Common findings include hypotension, elevated jugular venous pressure, pulsus paradoxus, and cardiogenic shock that appears disproportionate to the extent of myocardial injury. In some patients, electrocardiographic monitoring may demonstrate pulseless electrical activity or electromechanical dissociation, reflect acute cardiac tamponade and impend cardiovascular collapse [14].

Definitive surgical repair remains the treatment of choice for LVFWR and offers the greatest chance of survival [15]. However, successful surgical management depends on timely diagnosis and the patient’s ability to survive long enough to reach the operating room [6]. In clinical practice, many patients experience rapid hemodynamic deterioration due to hemopericardium and cardiac tamponade before definitive repair can be performed, contributing substantially to the high mortality associated with this condition [16].

Initial management of suspected LVFWR focuses on hemodynamic stabilization through volume resuscitation, vasopressor support, and urgent surgical consultation [17]. However, despite aggressive supportive measures, many patients continue to deteriorate because cardiac tamponade imposes a critical limitation on ventricular filling and cardiac output [17].

The management of cardiac tamponade secondary to LVFWR remains particularly challenging since patients may experience rapid clinical deterioration before operative intervention can be initiated [18]. The role of pericardiocentesis in this setting remains controversial. While decompression of the pericardial space can improve ventricular filling and systemic perfusion [6], it may also theoretically promote further hemorrhage by eliminating the tamponading effect of accumulated blood. For this reason, routine or complete drainage of hemorrhagic pericardial effusions in patients with ventricular rupture is generally approached with caution [19].

A comparable therapeutic dilemma has long been recognized in patients with acute type A aortic dissection (TAAD) complicated by cardiac tamponade [20]. Although LVFWR and TAAD arise from distinct pathological processes, both conditions share a similar hemodynamic mechanism. In LVFWR, transmural myocardial infarction results in disruption of the ventricular free wall, allowing blood to accumulate within the pericardial cavity [3, 5, 21]. In TAAD, hemopericardium most commonly develops through transudation of blood across the ischemic false lumen, whereas frank rupture of the dissection into the pericardial space is less common but frequently results in life-threatening cardiac tamponade [22, 23]. In both settings, accumulation of blood within the pericardial sac impairs cardiac filling while simultaneously providing a tamponading effect that may temporarily limit further hemorrhage [18]. This shared pathophysiological principle forms the basis of controlled pericardial drainage (CPD).

Hayashi et al. demonstrated that intermittent, hemodynamically guided pericardial drainage targeting a systolic blood pressure of approximately 90 mmHg enabled successful definitive surgical repair in patients with TAAD complicated by refractory cardiac tamponade, without CPD-related mortality [24]. Similar results have been reported in centers without immediate cardiothoracic surgical capability, where CPD provided sufficient hemodynamic stabilization to permit safe transfer for definitive aortic repair [25, 26]. More recently, individual case reports have further shown that relatively small drainage volumes may produce substantial hemodynamic improvement, supporting titration of drainage according to clinical response rather than a predetermined volume [27].

Guided by these principles, we adopted a similar hemodynamically guided strategy in our patient. Hemorrhagic pericardial fluid was aspirated incrementally in 20-mL aliquots, with reassessment of blood pressure and clinical status after each aspiration. Drainage was intentionally discontinued after removal of approximately 100 mL, once systolic blood pressure increased to approximately 100 mmHg and mental status normalized, thereby avoiding complete decompression of the pericardial space before definitive surgical repair. Together with the limited reports describing pericardial drainage in patients with LVFWR [19, 27], , our findings suggest that the principles of CPD established in TAAD may be cautiously applicable to carefully selected patients with LVFWR-associated cardiac tamponade as a bridge-to-surgery strategy under close hemodynamic monitoring.

Our patient remained clinically stable at one-month follow-up after hospital discharge. Previous studies have demonstrated that patients who survive the acute phase of LVFWR and are discharged from the hospital may achieve favorable long-term outcomes, with survival rates approaching those of patients with MI without cardiac rupture [28, 29]. These observations underscore the importance of early recognition and successful stabilization during the critical acute phase [30].

Several reports have highlighted the consequences of delayed diagnosis and treatment in patients with VFWR. Ueno et al. described a patient who developed cardiac tamponade secondary to right VFWR during PCI and died despite pericardiocentesis, emphasizing the devastating impact of rapid hemodynamic deterioration and delayed recognition [31]. Similarly, another patient with LVFWR who underwent pericardiocentesis, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), and surgical repair did not survive because definitive treatment was initiated too late [32]. In contrast, Arai et al. reported successful survival in a patient with blowout-type LVFWR through close monitoring, early diagnosis, prompt initiation of ECMO, pericardiocentesis, and emergency surgery [33]. Continuous reinfusion of pericardial drainage blood to maintain adequate ECMO flow until surgery was considered a key factor contributing to the patient’s survival [33].

Transthoracic ECHO remains the imaging modality of choice (sensitivity: 93%; specificity: 98%) for the rapid diagnosis of LVFWR and its associated complications [14, 34]. Typical echocardiographic findings include pericardial effusion or hemopericardium, right-sided chamber collapse, echogenic material within the pericardial space, and, in some cases, direct visualization of the myocardial defect [14, 34]. Given its widespread availability and bedside applicability, ECHO plays a pivotal role in the evaluation of patients with persistent hypotension or unexplained hemodynamic deterioration following acute MI [35]. In our patient, prompt echocardiographic assessment enabled early identification of an expanding hemopericardium, cardiac tamponade, and a left ventricular free wall defect, facilitating timely intervention before irreversible circulatory collapse occurred.

Although emergency surgical repair remains the standard treatment for LVFWR, alternative or adjunctive strategies have been described in selected patients who are considered poor surgical candidates or who present with less severe forms of rupture. For example, intrapericardial fibrin glue injection therapy (PIFIT) has been reported as a potential treatment option for selected subacute or oozing-type ruptures. By promoting fibrin matrix formation within the pericardial space, this approach may facilitate sealing of the rupture site and support myocardial healing [36].

Mechanical circulatory support devices have also been used as adjunctive therapies in selected cases. Both the intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) [37] and percutaneous ventricular assist devices such as IMPELLA [38] may reduce left ventricular wall stress and improve systemic perfusion, potentially limiting further extension of the rupture while definitive management is planned. However, these strategies are generally reserved for carefully selected patients and often require prior stabilization of cardiac tamponade and restoration of adequate hemodynamics [38].

Conclusion

This case suggests that, in carefully selected patients with LVFWR complicated by cardiac tamponade and refractory hemodynamic instability, controlled, limited pericardial drainage may provide temporary hemodynamic stabilization as a bridge to definitive surgical repair when immediate surgery is not feasible. Early recognition of mechanical complications, prompt echocardiographic assessment, multidisciplinary decision-making, and timely surgical intervention remain essential for improving survival.
