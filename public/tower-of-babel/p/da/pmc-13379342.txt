Introduction

Self-reference mapping is a recently introduced mapping technique to localize the arrhythmogenic source of focal arrhythmias by sequentially advancing a multi-electrode catheter upstream toward the site of earliest activation.1,2 In this approach, the previously identified earliest-activation electrode is used as the reference, thereby eliminating the need for an additional reference catheter. The feasibility of this technique for identifying non-pulmonary vein triggers in patients with atrial fibrillation has been previously demonstrated.3 In contrast, delineation of reentrant circuits in atrial tachycardia (AT) relies on a stable reference electrogram to accurately define activation timing; therefore, it becomes challenging, particularly when a stable reference electrogram cannot be obtained.

Omnipolar electrogram-based mapping has been developed to characterize local myocardial activation by reconstructing the local electrical field from multi-electrode recordings independent of catheter orientation.4 This allows for the visualization of activation patterns without the need for a reference electrogram. We recently encountered a case of recurrent AT following pulmonary vein isolation, cavotricuspid isthmus ablation, and left anterior wall linear ablation, in which 2 distinct ATs coexisted within electrically dissociated right and left atria. Activation mapping was technically challenging because of marked right atrial enlargement, which precluded stable placement of a reference catheter in the right atrium (RA). This case highlights the clinical utility of a “pure” self-reference omnipolar electrogram-based mapping strategy, which enabled the identification of a localized reentrant circuit by visualization of local activation vectors, thereby overcoming a major limitation of conventional activation mapping in the absence of a stable reference.

Case report

An 82-year-old man with reduced left ventricular systolic function and a history of non-sustained ventricular tachycardia, who had previously undergone implantation of a cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillator (CRT-D), was referred for repeat catheter ablation because of drug-refractory AT accompanied by worsening heart failure. 2 years earlier, he had undergone pulmonary vein isolation, cavotricuspid isthmus ablation, and anterior wall linear ablation for persistent atrial fibrillation and AT. During follow-up, recurrent AT with a tachycardia cycle length (TCL) of 220 ms was documented. Progressive RA dilatation, predominantly attributable to severe tricuspid regurgitation, was associated with heart failure worsening, prompting a repeat ablation procedure.

A 12-lead electrocardiogram during AT demonstrated a biphasic P-wave morphology in lead V1 (Figure 1A). The TCL recorded in the coronary sinus (CS) and atrial lead of the CRT-D were 220 ms and identical. The CS activation sequence showed chevron activation pattern, and entrainment pacing from the distal CS and posterior wall yielded a post-pacing interval of 360 ms and 380 ms, thereby excluding mitral isthmus-dependent AT and roof-dependent AT. A low-amplitude continuous fragmented potential with a duration of 156 ms, covering approximately 70% of the TCL, was recorded by a grid mapping catheter (AdvisorTM HD Grid Catheter, Abbott Medical, Minneapolis, MN) and activation mapping demonstrated a focal activation pattern. Based on these findings, the tachycardia was considered to be most consistent with a localized reentrant AT originating from this region (Figure 1B). Radiofrequency energy application at the site of earliest activation (Figure 1C) successfully terminated the tachycardia; however, AT1 converted into a second AT (AT2).

In contrast, the 12-lead electrocardiogram exhibited a discordant TCL of 240 ms with negative P-wave polarity in lead V1, suggesting a RA origin (Figure 2A). During AT2, the TCL recorded in the CS was prolonged from 220 ms during AT1 to 300 ms. Intracardiac electrograms recorded from the RA lateral wall revealed a TCL of 240 ms, indicating the coexistence of distinct ATs in the RA and left atrium (LA, Figure 2B). Consequently, an activation map for AT2 in the RA using CS electrograms as a reference was not feasible. Although a decapolar catheter was positioned along the lateral tricuspid annulus as a reference, severe RA dilatation hindered stable catheter positioning, rendering conventional activation mapping to be technically challenging. To overcome this obstacle, omnipolar electrogram-based mapping, reconstructing the local electrical field from multi-electrode recordings, was adopted. This technique enabled real-time visualization of local activation vectors independent of catheter orientation which eliminated the need for a reference electrogram. The multi-electrode catheter was advanced upstream until local electrograms recorded on the inner splines encompassed the entire TCL (“pure” self-reference map; Figure 3). The activation vectors demonstrated a counterclockwise rotational pattern consistent with a localized reentrant circuit. Additional mapping around revealed centrifugal propagation of wavefronts away from the presumed reentrant core, further supporting a localized reentrant mechanism. Radiofrequency energy applications at sites exhibiting fractionated electrograms successfully terminated AT2 in the RA; however, another AT with a TCL of 300 ms persisted.

The TCL was identical in the right and left atria, and subsequent activation mapping was, therefore, performed in the LA. Detailed activation mapping combined with entrainment mapping confirmed a roof-dependent AT involving the Marshall bundle, and linear ablation across the LA roof resulted in termination of the tachycardia (Supplemental Material File). Finally, the patient returned to sinus rhythm, and right-to-left interatrial conduction was maintained during right atrial pacing at a cycle length of 480 ms.

During 1-year follow-up, no recurrence of AT was observed, and tricuspid regurgitation and heart failure symptoms markedly improved.

Discussion

Conventional activation mapping relies on a reference electrogram to define activation timing and sequence (Figures 4A). A previously reported self-reference mapping technique uses the previous earliest electrode site as the reference and does not require other reference catheter. Feasibility of this technique has been demonstrated in identifying focal arrhythmogenic sources but not in reentrant tachycardia. When this technique is applied to reentrant tachycardia, manipulation of the mapping catheter toward the upstream is facilitated but requires a reference electrogram to delineate reentrant circuit (Figure 4B). Both approaches are limited in patients with advanced atrial remodeling, extensive prior ablation, or unstable catheter positioning, in whom precise interpretation of activation timing and propagation may be compromised.5 Although anatomical inaccessibility of the CS is rare, failure to access or maintain the CS as a stable reference catheter has been reported in approximately 2%–4% of electrophysiology procedures, particularly in patients with marked RA dilatation or structural barriers to CS cannulation.6 The incidence may even become higher in patients with congenital heart disease. In this context, the present case highlights the clinical utility of a “pure” self-reference omnipolar electrogram-based mapping strategy for elucidating tachycardia mechanisms when conventional reference-dependent activation mapping is not feasible (Figure 4C).

In the present case, the RA was initially activated with a TCL of 220 ms, suggesting preserved 1:1 conduction from the LA to the RA. After successful termination of AT1 in the LA, AT2 with a TCL of 240 ms emerged in the RA, whereas roof-dependent AT with a TCL of 300 ms persisted in the LA, with functional block of right-to-left inter-atrial conduction. Once AT2 was terminated, the RA was again activated from the LA. After termination of the roof-dependent AT, right-to-left inter-atrial conduction at a cycle length of 480 ms was confirmed. These findings indicate that the electrical dissociation during AT2 was most likely caused by rate-dependent functional conduction block from the RA to the LA, rather than permanent disruption of the inter-atrial conduction pathway. This finding underscores the complexity of the electrophysiological substrate in severely remodeled atria. Advanced atrial remodeling can result in longitudinal and functional dissociation of atrial activation, such that atrial myocardium no longer behaves as a single, electrically unified chamber.7 In such settings, electroanatomical mapping systems capable of “pure” self-reference mapping may be particularly advantageous.

Omnipolar electrogram-based mapping has been developed to characterize local myocardial activation by reconstructing the local electrical field from multi-electrode recordings.4 This technique enables visualization of local activation vectors without dependence on catheter orientation or stable reference electrograms. “Pure” self-reference omnipolar electrogram-based mapping referred to a new mapping technique for identifying a tachycardia mechanism only from the acquired multiple local activation vectors when conventional activation map is technically challenging (Figure 4C). In the present report, the term “pure” self-reference mapping does not simply refer to conventional self-reference catheter manipulation; rather, it describes a mapping strategy in which sequential catheter advancement based on the self-reference concept is combined with interpretation of omnipolar activation vectors that are independent of a stable reference electrogram. In situations when conventional reference-based activation mapping cannot be constructed because of unstable catheter positioning or electrical dissociation between chambers, this approach may provide useful information for inferring the tachycardia mechanism. In the present case, the multi-electrode catheter was advanced upstream until local electrograms encompassed the entire TCL. Although a color-coded centrifugal activation pattern could not be demonstrated using “pure” self-reference omnipolar electrogram-based mapping, additional mapping of the surrounding area of the presumed localized reentrant circuit is absolutely necessary to confirm centrifugal wavefront propagation. Theoretically, “pure” self-reference omnipolar electrogram-based mapping can be used not only to diagnose localized reentrant AT but also to suggest a macro-reentrant mechanism. When the mapping catheter is advanced sequentially upstream and activation vectors demonstrate a consistent global rotation with returning to the previously mapped regions, a macro-reentrant circuit should be suspected. The diagnosis can, then, be corroborated using adjunctive entrainment mapping to confirm a mechanism. A key limitation of this approach is that direct assessment of mapped activation time relative to the TCL is technically challenging. In addition, local activation vectors are reconstructed from the local electrical field derived from multi-electrode recordings at each acquired point. Accordingly, operators must carefully review activation vectors across multiple mapping locations and integrate these findings with surrounding propagation patterns to accurately discriminate the underlying AT mechanism.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the utility of “pure” self-reference electrogram-based omnipolar mapping for elucidating the AT mechanism when conventional reference-dependent mapping is not feasible. This approach may serve as a valuable adjunctive strategy for the evaluation and treatment of complex AT in the setting of advanced atrial remodeling and unstable reference electrograms.
