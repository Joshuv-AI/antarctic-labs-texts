Privacy-aware deep vein thrombosis segmentation using a multi-model federated learning framework with the federated averaging algorithm
Authors: Pavihaa Lakshmi B, Vidhya S
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) is the formation of blood clots in the deep veins of the calf, requiring precise Computer Tomography (CT) scan segmentation for accurate diagnosis and treatment. We proposed and developed an efficient Federated Learning (FedL) architecture using the Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm. Seven distinct local models were designed and trained on non-independent and identically distributed (Non-IID) CT images to maintain data privacy and security, enhancing DVT segmentation efficiency and accuracy. The global model was progressively improved by aggregating the local model’s weights using FedAvg algorithm. Our algorithm was evaluated in three phases using datasets of 1000, 2000, and 3000 samples to assess the global model’s performance. Phase 1 involved three clients, each with unique local models (Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Sequential, and Semantic). While, Phase 2 expanded to five clients, incorporating additional models (U-Net and VGG Net-19). In Phase 3, scaled to seven clients with advanced models (Modified U-Net and Modified-Net). Notably, our framework proved consistent improvement across all phases, achieving a reduction in validation loss from 0.910 to 0.061 and a communication cost increase from 14 MB to 3279 MB with increasing model scales. Introduction

Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) is a serious condition where blood clots form in deep veins, typically in the legs, thighs, or arms. The clots can travel through the bloodstream and block arteries or the lungs, causing a Pulmonary Embolism (PE). If not early diagnosed, DVT can result in chronic venous disease, recurrent venous thromboembolism (VTE), post-thrombotic syndrome, heart attack, and even death1. Federated Learning (FedL) is an advanced machine learning (ML) approach that allows decentralized systems or servers to collaboratively train all the different local models, mainly focusing on segmentation and data privacy. This model is instrumental in medical fields such as tumor, DVT, PE segmentation, where patient privacy and data confidentiality is crucial. By training all local models on Computer Tomography (CT) images and sharing the updated global models, FedL is effective in tackling all the complex data and progressing in medical imaging without compromising data confidentiality.

A first and foremost advanced multi-model technique for DVT segmentation using a FedL model with the Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm on non-independent and identically distributed (Non-IID) data, has been developed. Our research introduces a FedL model to improve the reliability of DVT segmentation while preserving patient data. We designed a FedL technique with seven different local models for DVT segmentation: a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model, a Sequential model, a Semantic model, a U-Net model, a VGG Net-19 model, a Modified U-Net model created with residual and inception blocks, and a Modified-Net model integrate and constructed with elements such as: residual, inception, dilated convolution, spatial pyramid pooling (SPP), and attention blocks. The FedAvg algorithm manages data localization, ensuring its integrity and privacy. It improves the learning process by dynamically fine-tuning and scrutinizing the models in each phase and updating the global model based on local data features, addressing the heterogeneity of medical data on Non-IID data. This algorithm improves a model’s robustness, generalizability, segmentation performance, and data privacy confidentiality, making it a feasible approach for DVT segmentation using CT images and paving the way for futuristic artificial intelligence (AI) applications in the medical sector.

Handling independently and identically distributed (IID data) vs non-IID data in FedL

In FedL, IID data refers to samples that are independently and identically distributed across clients, producing stable and uniform training behavior. In contrast, Non-IID data exhibits significant variation across clients due to differences in acquisition methods, imaging protocols, and disease characteristics. In our network, Non-IID conditions arose naturally from heterogeneous clinical sources and were intentionally preserved to reflect real-world deployment scenarios. These variations introduced challenges such as biased local updates and unstable convergence; however, they were managed in the architecture through structured client heterogeneity rather than elimination. Training clients with varied frameworks on unequal dataset sizes enabled learning of complementary spatial, semantic, and boundary-aware features that counter local bias. FedAvg further expanded generalization by weighted aggregation of client updates, reducing the dominance of skewed distributions. Collectively, this design captured diverse feature representations while mitigating bias, thereby enhancing generalization and stability under realistic Non-IID settings.

The FedL framework is divided into three phases, each utilizing intricate dataset and multi-model architectures. In Phase 1, three clients train three different local models, which include CNN, Sequential, and Semantic. These are then aggregated using FedAvg to create an initial global model. In Phase 2, five clients use five different local models (CNN, Sequential, Semantic, and also including U-Net and VGG Net-19) using various datasets and improved model architectures with the integration of the earlier global model. Seven clients employ unique local models from Phase 3: CNN and Sequential, semantic, U-Net, and VGG Net-19, apart from two employing specialized models like Modified U-Net and Modified-Net, culminating in a final global model aggregation using FedAvg. This approach improves communication costs, reduces heterogeneity, and enhances the strength of the training efficacy. To overcome the heterogeneity of non-IID data, we trained seven local models in three phases with two scrutinizing stages. We updated the global model using a multi-model FedAvg algorithm that maintains the integrity and privacy of the data. This method enhances model robustness, generalizability, segmentation accuracy, and computational efficiency, offering promising outcomes for DVT segmentation and advancing medical applications by designing effective, robust models.

Historical development of theories and models

McMahan, Brendan, et al. pioneered the idea of FedL to tackle data privacy and distribution in ML. Recently, FedL gained prominence because of the potential to develop large-scale, decentralized data with privacy5. They investigated FedL; they used multiple models to train a model on a central server while protecting privacy threats. Moreover, they overcome communication overhead, heterogeneous data, privatization, security, and system heterogeneity through approaches like differential privacy, secure aggregation, personalized and federated transfer learning6. The study highlighted decentralized approaches in collaborative ML on the basis of image analysis. The focus was on developing cost-effective privacy mechanisms to resist bias and heterogeneity of data without a comprehensive privacy framework7. Guan, Hao, et al. discussed the evolution of FedL techniques in the assessment of medical images, emphasized the need for privacy mechanisms, identified the challenges of collaborative learning, and reviewed the state-of-the-art in terms of advances, benchmark datasets, and software platforms in the area8. FedL significantly improved performance in complex data settings through privacy-preserving, decentralized processing of data while also creating the FedL model with a pre-trained base model9.

Analysis of previous studies

Ma, Yuxi, et al. came up with a Heterogeneous Semi-Supervised FedL model incorporating consistency strengthening and fusion into it to make selective knowledge transfer easier. The model developed self-assessment in aggregation with reliable pseudo-label generation to overcome the annotation issues and to deal with unlabelled data. It outshone other heterogeneous approaches and showcased exceptional effectiveness even in homogeneous frameworks mainly in region-based metrics10. Wu, Huisi, et al. applied consistency and entropy-aware methods to construct unbiased pseudo labels based on aggregated models while improving their discriminative ability using classical contrastive learning11. The study presented a federated semi-supervised learning model in the application of medical imaging that utilized a federated pseudo-labelling approach towards models without their labels. This method achieved the Dice scores performance, which could lead to better patient care in medical applications12. A personalized FedL framework for medical image segmentation with learnable prompts, aggregation, multiple personalized data distribution prompts, a dual-decoder scheme, and comparison with fully supervised centralized training was computed by Lin, Li, et al. The results showed that minimized computation and communication overhead13. This method was extended to multi-source data, and this proved a more effective approach for practical data applications14. Accordding to Xia, Yingda, et al. Auto-FedAvg is a data-oriented approach that adaptively modified aggregation weights based on the data distribution and the improvement in model training. By splitting constraint sets into local and global models and iteratively updating them using a communication-efficient process, it outperformed the previous FedL methods when used with heterogeneous data distributions15. Adversarial Contrastive FedL enhanced model generalization through domain-invariant features and the added global discriminator for feature representation. This allowed the model to learn general features through contrastive learning and a global discriminator that provides efficient communication and privacy protection. Pioneering FedL Strategies for Segmenting Medical Images.

Overview of our local models in FedL algorithm

Venous thrombosis (VT) is a dreadful disease caused due to injury or gradual slowing in blood flow passing through the deep veins. To classify several kinds of VT, the model VGG Net-19 was proposed to reduce non-homogeneity and preserve edge consistency with sharp edges and smoothness besides orientation, intensity, and lesion variation. Its effectiveness was proven by the analysis of performance compared to other methods by referring to3. Hwang, Jung Han, et al. conducted a study comparing CNN-based and conventional ML algorithms to detect DVT in 659 participants by using contrast-enhanced lower extremity CT venography. The CNN-based VGG Net-16 model demonstrated the highest performance rate among all models, accurately identifying DVT on CT images18. A fully automatic CNN method for DVT was proposed using contrast-enhanced MRI (CE-MRI). A DL network with an encoder-decoder architecture segmented DVT in 1.5 seconds per MRI slice. For 58 patients, the mean Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) was 0.74 ± 0.17 and the median was 0.79. This DL method effectively worked for fully automatic DVT segmentation19. Contreras-Luján, et al. presented ML models for the early DVT diagnosis. They tested several approaches, including Decision Trees, Extra Trees, K-nearest neighbor (KNN), multi-layer perceptrons (MLP), Neural Networks, Random Forest, and Support Vector Machine. KNN achieved the highest accuracy of 90.4% and a specificity of 80.66%. All models trained on both computers as well as on the Raspberry Pi 4 showed an accuracy of 85%, with Area Under Curve (AUC) values ranging from 0.81 to 0.8620.

The Transformer-based Attention Guided Network enhanced semantic segmentation performance by perceiving non-local interactions among encoder features using multi-scale skip connections for feature aggregation, and reducing fine detail loss from convolution layers and sampling operations21. The CM-SegNet was a DL model for medical image segmentation, trained on six datasets. It employed MLP and convolution modules to connect various channels and spatial locations and efficiently segment different structural lesion regions in 3D images. It demonstrated better segmentation performance in shorter training time22. The Deep Neural Network used 2D U-Net, 2D VGG, and 3D U-Net for DVT segmentation and classification. Among these, VGG outperformed the CNN model without masks, while the 3D U-Net Inception model with masks delivered the best performance23. The PE-YNet model applied a multi-head attention mechanism to boost the precision of both segmentation and detection of images by focusing on key areas and discarding irrelevant information. It reached peak detection and segmentation accuracy of 99.89% and 99.83%, respectively24. The hybrid segmentation architecture of CellSegUNet was created by the inspiration from UNet++ and Residual UNet. It proved to be effective at tackling variable image intensities, overlapping cell nuclei, and noise. By evaluating horizontal and vertical features together, it reduced semantic gaps and data loss. The CellSegUNet surpassed UNet++ and ResUNet models working on the same depth level, achieving high accuracy, dice, and Jaccard metrics25. The UNet-ResNet architecture, which was applied using a pre-trained UNet with the ResNet-34 encoder for outlining the venous region, used the Intersection-over-Union (IoU) score and dice loss metrics to evaluate the training process of the model. The standard UNet resulted in an IoU score of 81.22% with a dice loss of 0.1341, whereas the application of the ResNet-34 encoder yielded improved performance metrics at 84.50% and 0.0857, respectively26. Researchers introduced a Modified U-Net incorporating residual and inception blocks for DVT segmentation in medical imaging. Their work examined CNN for semantic segmentation with a tailored architecture that was able to outperform traditional methods used in DVT edge detection. This model made the advancement of the art of medical image processing by the possibility of automation for DVT segmentation through training via binary cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimizer4.

Zhou, Jun, et al. developed a FedL model to evaluate the prognostic risk of acute pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE). This model incorporated logistic regression, CNN, and MLP, ensuring privacy and clinical information security. It allowed medical professionals to assess PTE prognosis accurately and efficiently16. Researchers introduced an ensemble framework for PE classification that combined ResNet50, DenseNet121, and the Swin Transformer to harness the complementary strengths of CNNs and Vision Transformers (ViT). They utilised autoencoder-based dimensionality reduction, data augmentation, discrete wavelet transforms, and Sobel filtering to attain robust feature extraction. When estimated on the RSNA-STR PE dataset, the model obtained 97.80% accuracy, 98.71% precision, 96.33% recall, 96.81% F1-score, and 0.95 Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC). These results outperformed existing pre-trained CNN and ViT models, validating its effectiveness for early PE detection and clinical decision support27. Sun, Guanqun, et al. introduced FKD-Med with knowledge distillation for improving efficiency in communication, combining FedL for privacy-sensitive data amalgamation in healthcare. They were designed specifically for medical computational problems and could be applied to various medical informatics tasks. FKD-Med amplified the volume of computational data, enhanced communication efficiency and accuracy in the segmentation of medical images by making TransUNet and ResUNet teacher models. Data was kept private and ensured reduced communication costs as well as enhanced segmentation accuracy17. The use of FedL significantly improved the performance of a segmentation model, enhancing its accuracy and addressing data privacy concerns. It minimized data breaches along with preserved patient data privacy through security measures like differential privacy and encryption. However, the model faced limitations such as computational and communication overhead, posing challenges when scaling to larger datasets. The model performance would largely be dependent on the quality and heterogeneity of data at diverse clients. Therefore, further research is required to assure data quality and diversity for FedL-based medical image analytics applications28.

Proposed methodology

Our research focuses on assessing the performance of FedL models on three available non-IID datasets containing 1000, 2000, and 3000 CT images respectively. The research is divided into three progressive phases, each phase incrementally involving a greater number of clients and models to enhance the accuracy and robustness of the global model. This performed as a federated validation stage to ascertain the baseline behavior of FedAvg under controlled architectures and client settings. Three models were chosen for their low parameter counts, uniform dynamics, and stable gradients, minimizing optimization variance. Limiting the network to three clients enabled precise assessment of aggregation consistency, communication synchronization, and convergence stability under Non-IID data, free from deep-model or heterogeneity effects. It delivered a reliable convergence baseline and confirmed correct parameter aggregation before scaling to deeper models and broader client contribution.

The FedL algorithm involves three stages: local training, data transmission, weight aggregation, and global model redistribution. Clients send the trained model parameters to a centralized FedL server, maintaining privacy. The FedAvg algorithm aggregates model weights from all clients, creating a unified global model that integrates learning from local models. The updated global model is sent back to clients, enriched with local knowledge, for further local training. Clients refine the model with their local data. The iterative process repeats over three rounds, allowing for continuous improvement through collective learning.

After the local training, clients transmit their model weights to the FedL server. The FedAvg algorithm aggregates these weights to create the updated global model, which is then redistributed to the clients. This iterative process of local training, data transmission, weight aggregation, and global model redistribution is repeated for seven rounds. With more clients and models involved, the global model is better able to capture a wider range of patterns and insights from the data.

In this research, the FedAvg algorithm was adopted as the aggregation strategy due to its architectural agnosticism, scalability, and stability under heterogeneous client settings. Our framework involves multiple clients employing distinct local model architectures trained on Non-IID datasets, which requires an aggregation mechanism capable of integrating diverse parameter updates without enforcing structural uniformity. FedAvg satisfies this requirement through straightforward weight averaging, enabling effective knowledge fusion across heterogeneous models while maintaining convergence stability. Centralized training using pooled datasets was not considered, as it contradicts the primary objective of preserving data privacy and simulating realistic distributed clinical environments. Advanced federated optimization methods such as FedProx and SCAFFOLD were not employed because they are designed for homogeneous model architectures and rely on proximal regularization or control variates that assume consistent model structures and synchronized optimization behavior across clients. These conventions are incompatible with the multi-model design and would introduce additional computational and communication overhead. Thus, FedAvg was selected as a robust, interpretable, and architecture-independent aggregation mechanism aligned with the goals of our study.

Overall, the process proved a FedL pipeline scalable, with iterative model refinement and robust evaluation, suitable for real-world deployment. This advanced multi-model approach was evaluated using diverse datasets and models to scrutinize performance at each stage. In Phase 1, datasets of sizes 1000, 2000, and 3000 were used to assess FedL performance. Three distinct local models were deployed across three clients: CNN, Sequential, and Semantic. Each model was trained on its respective dataset, and the resulting weights were sent to the FedL server, which then aggregated them using the FedAvg algorithm. The updated global model was then redistributed to local clients for further evaluation, to enable better performance of the global model over multiple cycles. In Phase 2, the approach scaled up to five clients with five various models: CNN, Sequential, Semantic, U-Net, and VGG Net-19. Each client trained its local model using datasets of sizes 1000, 2000, and 3000. The trained models’ weights were aggregated by the FedAvg algorithm on the server, generating an updated global model. This updated global model entered a scrutinizing Phase, where it was further evaluated by local models. This process was repeated, with the global model’s performance progressively enhanced through the Phase 2 pipeline. In Phase 3, the framework extended to seven clients with seven advanced and uniquely designed models: CNN, Sequential, Semantic, U-Net, VGG Net-19, Modified U-Net, and Modified Net. Again, datasets of sizes 1000, 2000, and 3000 were used. The global model obtained from Phase 2 was used as the basis for this Phase. After training on the clients’ local models and applying the FedAvg algorithm, the global model was further refined. The scrutinizing stage involved reevaluating the updated global model on local models, iteratively improving its performance across all datasets and client designs. This systematic, advanced multi-model approach ensured thorough testing and evaluation of the FedL framework. The inclusion of diverse model architectures and datasets of varying sizes highlighted the robust and versatile the approach. The performance metrics, evaluated at each Phase, provided valuable insights into the effectiveness of FedL under different configurations. This technique can serve as a strong foundation for optimizing FedL systems in segmentation tasks and beyond.

A hybrid processor system was crucial in Phases 2 and 3 to address the heightened computational and memory demands presented by modified architectures (Local Models 4–7) during federated training. The integration of U-Net, VGGNet-19, and their variants significantly amplified parameter counts, intermediate feature map sizes, and aggregation overhead, especially on larger datasets. To maintain training stability, reasonable convergence times, and consistent global updates without compromising federated privacy, effective parallel local updates and suitable FedAvg aggregation required a hybrid processing environment. Moreover, Phases 2 and 3 encompassed simultaneous training of heterogeneous deep models and frequent global aggregation cycles, further elevating synchronization costs and memory bandwidth requirements. It empowered effective handling of asynchronous updates, large tensor exchanges, and repetitive parameter averaging, ensuring numerical stability and preventing aggregation bottlenecks as both model depth and client count expanded.

Conclusion

The comparison here further shows the trade-off between complexity in the model, performance, and resources. FedL_3000 performs finest with respect to accuracy and convergence while FedL_1000 and FedL_2000 have potential concerning resource efficiency. In simple terms, the consistent performance of the same global model across all configurations specifies a strong potential for FedL collaborative training. Local model-4 was the most primitive performing model for segmentation, as its architecture was a well-known model for this task. Although global models have better metrics, this validates the use of the local model-4 structure in implementing FedL. During Phase 3, particularly in rounds 6 and 7, we increased our dataset gradually from 1000 to 3000, and we analysed significant improvement over the FedL segmentation performance but required additional communication resources that were incorporated from Phase 2. The enhancements in local models 4, 6 and 7 played a crucial role for the FedAvg algorithm, which fine-tuned the final global model despite the increased communication overhead. This shows that the FedAvg algorithm is quite robust in improving global model performance. This analysis provides a comprehensive understanding of the trade-offs in terms of model performance, and privacy preservation under various FedL configurations, offering a robust framework to select the appropriate model and dataset configuration. In addition, Local model-6 and Local model-7 could perform excellently on both FedL_1000 and FedL_2000, focusing on the need for state-of-the-art architectures for the segmentation tasks themselves. Metrics like communication cost and training time ascertain some scalability challenges of FedL, but the performance improvements affirm the effectiveness of the model such hints give valuable insights into future research flow.

Discussion and future direction

An extension analysis was performed to compare model complexity, performance, and resource utilizing the FedL approach across various datasets. The results show that FedL_3000 has achieved a better accuracy and converged better, while FedL_1000 and FedL_2000 are more resource efficient which is a necessity for limited computation resources in those scenarios. Consistent improvement in global model performance is yet another evidence that reinforces the FedL for collaborative training. Importantly, Local model-6, demonstrated consistent superiority over other local models because it is optimized precisely for segmentation. This provides an enlightenment of how our framework plays a vital role in superior performance, with importance on balancing model complexity with resource consumption in this specific case. The communication cost, average training time, and client heterogeneity are very significant metrics that help to indicate the scalability and efficiency of FedL models.

In future, our research will focus on several key areas: IID datasets to simplify aggregation and strengthen model performances, improvements of techniques like differential privacy and homomorphic encryption to ensure robust privacy without compromising its accuracy and reducing communication overhead by model compression and efficient update schemes. Also we will plan to develop techniques for detection and mitigation of adversarial attacks, scalability and generalization through scalable algorithms for diverse datasets; advanced model architectures for real-world data like medical imaging and autonomous driving; and ethical issues and ensure the fairness. These will indeed enhance FedL’s strength, efficiency, and usability in the medical domain.
