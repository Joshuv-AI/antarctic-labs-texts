Compatibility and stability of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate injection mixed with fructose and glucose diluents and pilot study of long-term storage impurities by LC-MS/MS
Authors: Zhenxia Hu, Yin Xu, Jia Li, Yujie Gong, Rong Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFructose injection is occasionally used to dilute ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate injection for patients who cannot receive glucose or sodium chloride injections in clinical settings. Since our PIVAS staff reported that discoloration occurred after ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate dissolved with fructose injection, this study aims to investigate the stability of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate in fructose and glucose solutions. Ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate was purchased and diluted with fructose or glucose injection for compatibility testing. The solutions were maintained under various conditions for different periods of time, after which the concentration of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate and other impurities was analyzed. The chemical structures of unknown impurities were further investigated by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. The analytical method was validated and was deemed to be suitable for the analyses in this study. Compatibility tests indicated that ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate is sensitive to temperature, illumination, and pH. When diluted with fructose or glucose, the concentration of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate decreased over time. The contents of impurities increased accordingly. Additionally, an unknown impurity was identified, speculated to be a hydroxyl oxidation product of ademetionine based on the LC-MS/MS results. Both fructose and glucose injections can be used as diluents for ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate, and the resulting mixture remains stable for up to 8 h after preparation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75392-3.Keywords: Ademetionine, Fructose, Compatible stability, High-performance liquid chromatography, Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometrySubject terms: Health care, Analytical chemistry

Introduction

S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAMe), is a versatile biological molecule, acting as a methyl donor, sulfur donor, and aminopropyl donor for multiple cellular reactions1. While it is widely expressed in mammalian cells, it is most abundantly found in the liver2. As the synthesis of SAMe is suppressed in chronic liver disorders including steatohepatitis and cirrhosis3, it is of great interest to use SAMe as a therapeutic agent to ameliorate the severity of these conditions.

Clinical studies have observed the beneficial effects of SAMe in various conditions. For instance, it modulates the symptoms of jaundice related to chronic hepatitis B3. SAMe is also effective in relieving pruritus and reducing elevated serum bilirubin levels due to cholestasis of pregnancy as well as other causes4. Extensive clinical evidence supports its efficacy in managing depression and osteoarthritis5,6. Interestingly, while SAMe has antiapoptotic properties for normal hepatocytes, it shows a contrasting proapoptotic effect on hepatocellular carcinoma cells, indicating its potential application in the treatment of other types of tumors7.

SAMe has been marketed worldwide and is used as a nutritional supplement in the United States2,8. Ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate, the butanedisulfonate salt of adenosylmethionine, holds particular significance in China. It was first registered by Abbott under the brand name Transmetil and has been approved for the treatment of intrahepatic cholestasis both before and during liver cirrhosis, as well as for treating intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy9,10. According to the drug’s instructions, injections should be used for the initial treatment in the first two weeks, after which a transition to oral medication is allowed. Ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate is available as a freeze-dried powder for injection, packaged in 500-mg bottles. An additional 5-mL vial of an injection solvent containing L-lysine, sodium hydroxide, and water is also included. As per the instruction manual, the freeze-dried powder must first be reconstituted with the provided solvent. After reconstitution, the solution can be further diluted with a 5% glucose injection or a 0.9% sodium chloride injection, and should be administered via slow intravenous infusion within 1 to 2 h after dissolution.

Fructose, a structural isomer of glucose11, differs significantly from glucose in terms of absorption, distribution, and metabolism12. Unlike glucose, fructose can bypass the phosphofructokinase regulatory step in glycolysis and can be rapidly absorbed by the liver13, resulting in poor response to blood glucose and insulin in both healthy individuals and those with diabetes14. In addition, fructose can be converted to glycogen independently of the phosphoglucomutase pathway and can be utilized at near-normal rates in patients with impaired glucose tolerance15. Fructose injection has been included in the pharmacopeias of many countries, including the United States and China. When administered in low doses for a short period of time, it does not affect blood glucose metabolism and insulin function. In clinical practice, it is often used as an energy source for patients with diabetes or as an injectable solvent for those who cannot receive 0.9% sodium chloride or 5% glucose injections. However, it should be noted that long-term or high-dose use may lead to complications such as hyperuricemia, lactic acidosis, and metabolic syndrome16.

The physicians at our hospital have prescribed ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate diluted in fructose injection for cholestasis patients who have diabetes and suffer from hypernatremia. However, the staff at PIVAS (Pharmacy Intravenous Admixture Services) of our hospital has reported incompatibility events in several batches of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate when mixed with 10% fructose injection, which were signaled by noticeable color changes. However, this finding was not observed when a 5% glucose injection was used as a diluent. This color change indicates that ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate might react with fructose under the preparation conditions. Hence, it is necessary to determine the compatibility and stability of these substances to guide drug preparation protocols in PIVAS. In this study, the concentration of SAMe was determined using an established high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method with a HILIC column8. Additionally, the liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method was employed to analyze impurities in the mixture.

Accuracy and precision

The results showed that the HPLC method is accurate and precise for analysis. The recovery rates of each standard, as well as the intraday and interday precision of the method, can be found in the Supplemental Information.

The limit of detection for ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate was determined to be 0.33 µg/mL, as the signal-to-noise ratio was approximately 3 at this concentration. The limit of quantitation for methylthioadenosine and adenine were found to be 0.0025 µg/mL and 0.00125 µg/mL, respectively, as the signal-to-noise ratio was approximately 10 at those concentrations.

Compatibility and stability test

In contrast, the fructose and glucose stock solutions of Ximeixin were colorless and transparent at 0 h. The fructose stock solution of Ximeixin gradually deepened and became light yellow and transparent after 12 h, while the glucose stock solution also deepened in color, becoming light yellow and transparent after 12 h. Both the fructose stock solutions of Transmetil and Ximeixin had a slightly darker color than their corresponding glucose stock solutions. Study of impurities

When ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate was diluted with a fructose injection, an unknown impurity peak appeared. On comparing this chromatogram with that of fructose injection, it was determined that this impurity was from the fructose injection and was suspected to be 5-hydroxymethylfurfural. We speculate that the impurity peak might be 5-Hydroxymethylfurfural for two reasons. First, 5-Hydroxymethylfurfural is a common impurity found in fructose injections, and according to the Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020 Edition, the content of 5-Hydroxymethylfurfural is monitored during the related substances limit test for fructose. Second, the crystals of 5-Hydroxymethylfurfural are a creamy yellow color, and we suspect it might be one of the reasons why the solution of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate appears darker when dissolved in fructose injections compared to when it is dissolved in glucose injections.

Ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate was diluted with a fructose injection solution and stored for different time periods. As shown in Supplemental information Fig. 1, the results showed that the color of the solution darkened as the storage time increased. The solution stored for one year showed larger impurity peaks for methylthioadenosine and adenine, as well as an unknown peak appeared, compared to the solution stored for one day.

The enrichment of the unknown impurity was performed using the same HPLC method but with an injection volume of 100 µL. The effluent of the unknown impurity was collected and subsequently concentrated using a rotary evaporator. The impurity was analyzed by HPLC (as described in analytical conditions, with an injection volume of 5 µL) and LC-MS/MS. The total ion current diagrams for both the positive and negative ion modes of the sample are shown in supplemental information Fig. 2.

The results showed that the m/z values of the excimer ion peaks [M+] at 4.0 min and 4.5 min were 298.0986 and 136.0625, respectively, which were consistent with the molecular weights of methylthioadenosine and adenine. For the unknown impurity, at 6.4 min, the m/z value of the quasi-molecular ion peak [M+] in the first-level mass spectrometry was 396.8043, and there was a [2 M + Na+] peak at 814.5797, which confirmed that the molecular weight of the impurity was 396. The primary fragmentation fragment in the secondary mass spectrometry was mainly at m/z 102.1288. The common cleavage pathways for ademetionine are shown in Fig. 5. The unknown impurity showed the same fragment ions m/z 102 in the secondary mass spectrometry as ademetionine.

Discussion

To study the compatibility and stability of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate injection when mixed with fructose injection, an HPLC method was developed and validated. Several studies have reported the analysis of S-adenosyl-L-methionine and its metabolites using reversed-phase or cation-exchange chromatography tandem UV or fluorescence detectors18–21. Reversed-phased ion-exchange chromatography employing sodium octanesulfonate as the ion-pair regent has also been applied for the analysis of S-adenosyl-L-methionine22. In this study, a hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography method employing Waters XBridge HILIC column was selected for its compatibility with mass spectrometry and its relative convenience. The acetonitrile-rich eluents of this hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography method assist spray formation and improve ionization efficiencies, which can improve the detection sensitivity23. The HILIC retention mechanism is a complex process involving liquid-liquid distribution, ion exchange, and hydrogen bonding, which can enhance the retention of polar analytes23. When the concentration of acetonitrile in the mobile phase is above 80%, the compound will be well retained. The recommended temperature range for Waters XBridge HILIC column is 20 ~ 45℃. A higher column temperature can reduce backpressure, however column temperature from 24 ~ 28 did not show significant difference in separation. Hence 26℃ was chosen as it is close to the room temperature. The pH of mobile phase can affect the protonation of ademetionine and hence the After tying several chromatography conditions, the obtained chromatograms were validated. The validation results showed that this method had good specificity, linearity, accuracy, and precision, and was suitable for the analysis. It should be noted that the HPLC method needs a steady flow rate to maintain the stability of the analysis.

Two formulations of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate for injection from two different companies were employed. These were diluted with fructose or glucose injection for further compatibility and stability study. The solutions were stored under different conditions for various time points, and the content of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate, as well as other impurities in the solution, was analyzed. The results of the compatibility and stability study showed that ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate is sensitive to temperature, light, and pH. There was a low and steady content of homocysteine in the ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate injection solution, which did not increase over time. The contents of methionine, adenosine, and adenine in ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate were higher and increased continuously as the storage time increased. In addition, the generation rate of methylthioadenosine and adenine in the solution varied depending on the choice of solvent/diluent, storage conditions, and storage environment.

In addition, an unknown impurity was found in samples subjected to long-term stability testing, which was further analyzed using LC-MS/MS. The unknown impurity had the same fragment ions at m/z 102 in the secondary mass spectrometry as ademetionine, suggesting that they may have similar fragmentation pathways. The molecular weight of the unknown impurity and ademetionine differed by only 2 units. This may be attributed to the oxidation of one of the hydroxyl groups in the adjacent diol structure to a carbonyl group during the long-term storage process, as shown in Fig. 6. Based on these results, it was speculated that the impurity could be a hydroxyl oxidation product of ademetionine. All the mass spectrometry graphs referred to in the text are presented in Fig. 3 of the supplementary Information.

Possible cleavage pathways of the unknown impurity.

A limitation of this study is that the structure of the unknown impurity was only preliminarily inferred according to the results of LC-MS/MS. For a more definitive structural characterization, nuclear magnetic resonance technology could be employed after the purification and enrichment of the impurity.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the compatibility and stability of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate when diluted with fructose/glucose injection. The results showed that both fructose and glucose injections can both be used as diluents for ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate for injection. However, it should be noted that, regardless of whether glucose or fructose was used as the diluent, the content of impurities in the solution increased to varying degrees after storage, and the concentration of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate decreased by nearly 5% within 12 h. Therefore, it is advised that ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate injection should be used as soon as possible after preparation, in clinical practice. We recommend that the injection be administered within 8 h of preparation.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate standard was purchased from Henan Yongan Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. Ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate for injection (Transmetil, specification: 0.5 g) was supplied by Abbott Laboratories. The medicines and standards were as follows: adenosylmethionine succinate for injection (trade name: Ximeixin, Zhejiang Haizheng Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, specification: 0.5 g); 10% fructose injection; 10% glucose injection; methionine reference substance (Sigma); adenine reference substance (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.); homocysteine reference substance (Sigma). Other reagents were analytical grade. Deionized water was prepared by Ultrapure Water Systems.

Instrumentation

LC analysis was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II HPLC system equipped with a G7114A UV detector and controlled through an Agilent OpenLab ChemStation chromatographic workstation. LC-MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography system equipped with an Agilent 6520 Q-TOF Accurate-Mass Q-TOF mass spectrometry detector for accurate mass measurements.

Analytical conditions

Chromatographic separation was performed using a Waters XBridge HILIC column, with dimensions of 4.6 mm × 150 mm and a particle size of 3.5 μm. The column temperature was maintained at 26 °C. A gradient elution program was employed, using mobile phase A (acetonitrile) and mobile phase B (0.01 mol/L ammonium formate solution, pH adjusted to 4.0 using formic acid) for chromatographic separation. The flow rate was set at 1.0 mL/min. Gradient elution was employed as follows. The initial condition was set as 10% mobile phase B for 5 min. Mobile phase B was then elevated to 35% between 5.0 and 5.1 min, followed by an increase to 45% from 5.1 to 13 min, and then reduced again to 10% between 13.0 and 13.1 min. Finally, 10% mobile phase B was maintained for 9 min. The proposed method was validated according to ICH guidelines for specificity, sensitivity, precision, linearity, accuracy, system suitability, and robustness. The preparation of standard and sample solutions were described in the in the Supplemental Information.

HPLC method validation

The method was validated for specificity, linear range, precision, accuracy, detection, and quantification limits. The validation of HPLC method follows the ICH HARMONISED TRIPARTITE GUIDELINE, Validation of analytical procedures Q2(R1).

Specificity and selectivity

Method specificity was assessed by comparing the chromatograms of blank control solutions with those of the sample to identify any detectable interfering peaks in the injection at the retention time of each standard. Fructose injection was diluted to an appropriate concentration to serve as the blank control solution. Standard solutions, described in supplemental information, were mixed to prepare the control solution. The sample solution was prepared as described in supplemental information and then diluted to a concentration of 100 µg/mL to create the test solution.

Linearity and linear range

Preliminary experiments revealed that the concentration of homocysteine in the sample was low; therefore, only ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate, methylthioadenosine, and adenine were considered as the analytes for quantitative analysis. Calibration curves were constructed using a linear plot of peak areas against different concentrations of the reference standards for ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate (80.64, 90.72, 100.8, 110.88, and 120.96 µg/mL), methionine (0.205, 0.5125, 1.025, 2.05, 5.125, and 10.25 µg/mL). and adenine (0.0999, 0.1998, 0.4995, 0.999, 1.998, and 3.996 µg/mL).

Accuracy and precision

Quality control sample solutions of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate (79.59, 100.54, and 119.86 µg/mL), methionine (0.53, 1.02, and 2.02 µg/mL), and adenine (0.21, 0.50, and 0.99 µg/mL) were prepared and analyzed under the previously described analytical conditions. Each sample was tested in triplicate on the same day and again for the next two days. The accuracy of the method was calculated based on the average drug recovery rate, whereas the precision was calculated as the relative standard deviation (%, intraday and interday).

Detection and quantification limits

Ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate solution at a concentration of 100 µg/mL was prepared and serially diluted 500-, 1000-, 2000-, 3000-, and 4000-fold to prepare standard solutions at different concentrations for LC analysis. The detection limit was determined as the concentration at which the signal-to-noise ratio was 3.

Methionine and adenine solutions were initially prepared at concentrations of 0.2 µg/mL and 0.1 µg/mL, respectively, and serially diluted 10-, 20-, 40-, and 80-fold to prepare standard solutions at different concentrations for LC analysis. The limit of quantification was established as the concentration at which the signal-to-noise ratio was 10.

Robustness

The robustness of the method was tested by making minor changes to the chromatographic method, and then a standard solution of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate mixed with impurities was tested. The acceptance limit for the relative standard deviation (RSD) of the peak area was set at less than 2%. Tests were conducted at flow rates of 0.95, 1.00, and 1.05 mL/min. Experiments were performed at column temperatures of 24 °C, 26 °C, and 28℃.

Compatibility and stability testing

Stock solutions of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate stock solutions were prepared by dissolving Transmetil or Ximeixin in fructose injection or glucose injection according to the procedure described in the Supplemental Information. The stock solutions were then diluted to 100 µg/mL for analysis. The stock solution and sample solution were prepared in duplicates. One set was placed at room temperature and exposed to light, and the other was stored in a refrigerator at 4℃ in the dark. The color and appearance of each solution were observed at 0, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h, and the pH of the solution was measured and recorded at each time point.

Parallel stock solutions at each time point were diluted to 100 µg/mL, as described in supplemental information, and then analyzed under the HPLC conditions described in analytical conditions to assess the content of each component.

Impurity studies

Sample preparation for impurity analysis

Ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate was diluted with fructose injection solution and stored at room temperature, exposed to light for one month, one year, two years, and three years. The solution stored for one year was analyzed and compared to the solution stored for one day. Impurity enrichment was conducted using the same HPLC method as described earlier, but with an injection volume of 100 µL. A fixed volume of the effluent was collected and then concentrated using a rotary evaporator.

LC-MS/MS method for impurity analysis

The LC method was the same as previously described, except that the flow rate was 0.5 mL/min and the injection volume was 5 µL. Both positive and negative ion scanning modes were used for product ion scan. The ion source was ESI. The atomizer pressure was 50 PSI. Curtain gas was set at 30 L/min. Ion source gas 1 (GS1) and ion source gas 2 (GS2) were set at 50 L/min and 60 L/min, respectively. Ion spray voltage and temperature were adjusted at 5500 eV and 550 °C, respectively. The collision energy for MS and MS/MS were 10 eV and 40 ± 15 eV, respectively. The scanning range for both MS and MS/MS was 100–1000 m/z.

The impurities were dissolved in methanol and diluted to appropriate concentrations for LC-MS/MS analysis. Standard solutions of ademetionine 1,4-butanedisulfonate, methylthioadenosine, adenine, and homocysteine were mixed and diluted to create the standard reference solutions.
