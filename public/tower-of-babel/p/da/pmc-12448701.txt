Staggered use of sodium nitroprusside and levosimendan in a patient with refractory cardiogenic shock: a case report
Authors: Andre Rodrigues Duraes, Yasmin de Souza Lima Bitar, Geonys Marlan Gonçalves Oliveira, Lilian Rosamar Vieira Franklin, Jesus Batista Vieira Lima Junior, Costantino Mancusi, Alessandro Galluzzo, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiogenic shock (CS) is a complex syndrome characterized by inadequate tissue perfusion due to reduced cardiac output resulting from a wide array of underlying causes. It is generally an acute and devastating condition with short-term mortality ranging from 30% to 40% and 1-year mortality around 50%.Case summaryA 31-year-old previously healthy male presented with progressive dyspnoea, fatigue, and signs of low perfusion, ultimately developing CS requiring ICU admission. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe left ventricular dysfunction (left ventricular ejection fraction 18%) with diffuse hypokinesia and moderate functional mitral regurgitation (Type IIIb). Despite high-dose inotropic support (dobutamine, milrinone) and vasopressors, the patient remained in refractory shock. A therapeutic trial with sodium nitroprusside led to transient haemodynamic improvement, followed by a secondary deterioration. Due to unavailability of immediate mechanical circulatory support (MCS), levosimendan was initiated at 0.1 μg/kg/min (without loading dose), resulting in rapid and sustained clinical improvement and weaning from vasoactive agents.DiscussionGiven the heterogeneous nature of CS and the various physiological derangements, several vasoactive medications may be attempted to try to stabilize or reverse the haemodynamic picture with or without the installation of MCS. These devices have evolved as novel treatment strategies to restore systemic perfusion to allow cardiac recovery in the short-term. But, this case demonstrates the potential of combining vasodilator and inotropic therapies—specifically dobutamine and milrinone with sodium nitroprusside and levosimendan—as a bridge strategy in severe CS when MCS is not feasible.Keywords: Case report, Heart failure, Cardiogenic shock, Levosimendan

This case highlights the successful management of refractory cardiogenic shock in a previously healthy young male using a combination of advanced inotropic and vasodilator therapies in the absence of mechanical circulatory support.

Introduction

A 31-year-old male patient who practiced daily physical activity (including weight training) presented with fatigue, malaise, and postprandial bloating with progression to dyspnoea at rest. He sought emergency medical attention and was transferred to the intensive care unit with a shock condition to be clarified. It was found to be cardiogenic shock with progressive worsening of perfusion, slowed capillary filling, drowsiness, tachycardia and hypotension. The patient had a previous history of flu-like syndrome 30 days before hospital admission. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe left ventricular dysfunction (left ventricular ejection fraction—LVEF 18%) with diffuse hypokinesia, left chamber dilation and moderate functional mitral regurgitation (Carpentier Type IIIb). Chest tomography showed moderate bilateral pleural effusion, and abdominal tomography showed moderate ascites. For a better understanding of the clinical case, we will divide this report into three moments.

Case presentation

Moment 1: refractory cardiogenic shock

The patient evolved with progressive worsening of hypoperfusion with progressive lactate elevation and signs of poor perfusion (Table 1). Norepinephrine 0.5 μg/kg/min, dobutamine 10 μg/kg/min, and milrinone 0.250 μg/kg/min were started. A VolumeView™ catheter (Edwards Lifesciences) was inserted into the right femoral artery and connected to the EV1000™ monitoring system (Edwards Lifesciences, software version 2.0).1 Inotropic agents were readjusted (dobutamine 15 μg/kg/min, milrinone 0.50 μg/kg/min, norepinephrine 0.8 μg/kg/min). The patient maintained hypoperfusion, high venous pressure, low cardiac index, high pulmonary vascular resistance, and high pulmonary free water levels (Summary figure, Panel A).

Moment 2: therapeutic test with sodium nitroprusside (SNP)

After 36 h and after interpretation of the profile C haemodynamic pattern (pulmonary congestion with increased filling pressures, vena cava distention, pulmonary B-lines, peripheral hypoperfusion, high systemic vascular resistance),2 and fine adjustments in vasoactive drugs, a bedside option was made for a therapeutic test with SNP. We cautiously and gradually managed to wean him off norepinephrine. It was an extremely stressful time for the care team. After weaning from Noradrenaline, thus tolerating a borderline mean arterial pressure (MAP), we started using SNP (0.1 μg/kg/min). At this point in time, dobutamine was already adjusted to high dose of 20 μg/kg/min and milrinone to 0.750 μg/kg/min. Subsequently, the perfusion was continuously reassessed, and the SNP was progressively escalated to higher doses up to the maximum level of 1.25 μg/kg/min, with significant improvement in perfusion and haemodynamic pattern (Summary figure, Panel B).

Moment 3: therapeutic test with levosimendan

However, after 36 h, the patient presented a new haemodynamic worsening (Summary figure, Panel C) without any sign of sepsis (leukogram, procalcitonin, and C-reactive protein at normal levels). A new echocardiogram maintained diffuse hypokinesia and LVEF 19%. The installation of a short-term circulatory support device was contemplated again. With the clinical worsening and unavailability of immediate short-term circulatory support, a therapeutic test (heroic) with levosimendan in a continuous infusion (without loading dose) of 0.1 μg/kg/min was chosen. The patient showed progressive improvement of shock (within 1–6 h after the start of the infusion) with progressive improvement after 24 h of haemodynamic parameters and hypoperfusion (Summary figure, Panel D). A few hours after the end of the infusion, the patient was already weaning from vasoactive drugs. Discussion

SNP is a potent dilator of veins and systemic and pulmonary arterioles. It has a rapid onset of action and a very short duration of effect (1–2 min) and can be used to increase cardiac output in patients with heart failure (HF).3 In addition to its widespread use as a fast-acting intravenous antihypertensive drug with a short half-life, SNP is a safe therapeutic choice in patients with advanced congestive HF with evidence of improving systemic, brain, and renal perfusion across several retrospective cohorts,4, 5 in addition to the use of SNP and dobutamine improve cardiac output, but the first seems to restore both atrial and ventricular pump function better.6 For cases of acute HF, patients with left ventricular dilatation and functional mitral insufficiency seem to be the subgroup of responders to the use of SNP.4,7

Levosimendan is a pharmacological agent with dual actions: it serves as a calcium sensitizer and opens KATP channels. By increasing the calcium sensitivity of the heart muscle, this drug effectively increases the force of cardiac muscle contraction and induces an increase in blood perfusion in key organs and a systemic vasodilation. This is particularly beneficial in HF, where the heart’s ability to contract effectively is compromised.8,9 There is recent evidence that this drug improves cardiac work, facilitating weaning from inotropes or even ventricular assist devices.10–14 The use of levosimendan in cardiogenic shock that maintains hypoperfusion (‘deteriorating’) requiring the escalation of intravenous therapies—Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) stage D—is even more challenging since the patient is worsening and a decision regarding the need for a circulatory support device vs. insisting on conservative treatment is imperative. There are few heroic reports of the use of levosimendan in refractory cardiogenic shock.15 The combination of the simultaneous use of dobutamine, milrinone, levosimendan and SNP in patients with refractory cardiogenic shock seems not to have been reported in the literature, making this case report something particular and that deserves future experiments, perhaps through a randomized clinical trial.
