The geometry of longitudinal ascending aortoplasty to effect aneurysmal diameter reduction
Authors: Alice L Zhou, Gabe Weininger, Y Joseph Woo
Published: 2025

Calculation of bite size required to achieve desired aortic diameter reduction.

Central Message

We present a simplified reduction ascending aortoplasty technique that uses basic geometry to ensure efficient and reproducible aortoplasty with adequate aortic diameter reduction.

The patient is a 78-year-old man with a history of hyperlipidemia and prostate cancer who was referred for surgical evaluation of aortic regurgitation (AR) and aortic root aneurysm. His height, weight, and body surface area were 1.93 m, 81 kg, and 2.08 m2, respectively, corresponding to an aortic size index of 2.1 cm/m2. Transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated a trileaflet sclerotic aortic valve with severe AR, no aortic stenosis, a 4.3-cm root and ascending aorta, and normal biventricular function. Given these findings, he was brought to the operating room electively for a bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement and intervention on his moderate ascending aortic aneurysm.

A preoperative transesophageal echocardiogram confirmed moderate-to-severe AR with holodiastolic flow reversal in the thoracic descending aorta and color jet diameter to left ventricular outflow tract diameter >50%, in addition to mild dilatation at the sinotubular junction up to 4.3 cm. The patient underwent median sternotomy, and cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was initiated via aortic and right atrial cannulation. The ascending aorta was crossclamped and the heart was arrested with retrograde cardioplegia. The ascending aortotomy was performed in longitudinal fashion linearly and along the long axis, after which direct antegrade cardioplegia was administered via the coronary ostia. The aortic valve leaflets were found to be calcified with multiple fenestrations. The leaflets were resected and the annulus was debrided. A 29-mm Edwards INSPIRIS valve (Edwards Lifesciences) was placed using supra-annular pledgeted sutures.

Given the patient's aortic dilatation to 4.3 cm at the sinotubular junction, we elected to address his aorta. Given his moderately advanced age and his moderate aneurysm, instead of graft replacement of his aortic root and ascending aorta, we performed an ascending aortoplasty to mitigate the risk of progressive aortic dilatation or rupture. The law of Laplace states that wall tension is directly proportional to vessel radius. Reducing aortic diameter thus decreases wall tension and therefore should reduce the likelihood of future dilatation or rupture/dissection. The ascending aortoplasty was performed by taking wide ∼1.5-cm plication bites of the ascending aorta on either side of the longitudinal aortotomy to consume the necessary 3.14 cm of aortic circumference, and hence 1 cm of aortic diameter, using double running 3-0 PROLENE suture with a running horizontal mattress layer (Video 1) followed by a second simple running layer as further reinforcement. A transesophageal echocardiogram demonstrated a well-seated bioprosthetic aortic valve with normal leaflet motion, no aortic stenosis, no AR, and a resultant aortic diameter of 3.2 cm (Figure E1). Crossclamp and CPB times were 51 and 68 minutes, respectively. The patient's postoperative course was uneventful. He was extubated on postoperative day 0 and was discharged on postoperative day 5.

Discussion

Reduction ascending aortoplasty (RAA) is a surgical management strategy for patients with borderline ascending aortic dilatation, particularly for high-risk patients who may not tolerate or require more extensive aortic surgery. RAA reduces aortic diameters, thereby lowering wall tension in accordance with the law of Laplace. This results in decreased likelihood of aortic dilatation or rupture without the additional crossclamp time or morbidity and mortality risks of more extensive aortic surgery, such as ascending aortic replacement. Studies have found decreased mortality and fewer complications among those undergoing RAA compared with ascending aortic replacement.1, 2, 3 Among 253 patients who underwent RAA with a median follow-up time of 3.8 years, redilatation occurred in 5.7% of cases.4

RAA is most commonly performed through an aortotomy along the greater curvature of the aorta, followed by excision of an oval segment of aortic wall.1 Our technique uses the same aortotomy incision that extends longitudinally across the length of the aneurysmal segment, but rather than excising aortic wall tissue, we take plication bites on either side of the aortotomy (Video 1). Regardless of technique, adequate reduction of transverse aortic diameter is critical, because larger diameters increase wall tension and predispose to progressive redilatation or rupture in accordance with the law of Laplace.5 Using our technique, the plication bite size taken is calculated using the aforementioned equation to ensure adequate consumption of aortic circumference and hence diameter. Our approach represents a simple modification of standard aortotomy closure, resulting in RAA with minimal additional crossclamp time compared with aortic valve replacement alone. Compared with ascending aortic replacement, our RAA technique requires just 1 anterior suture line that is easily accessible in case of any bleeding. In addition, the horizontal mattress plication bites serve to use the excluded aortic tissue to reinforce the suture line. The primary limitation of RAA is the potential risk, albeit low, of redilatation, and the limited utility of this technique in patients with connective tissue disorder or significant aortic fragility, for which ascending aortic replacement remains the safer choice.

In this case, we present a simplified RAA technique that uses basic geometry to ensure adequate aortic diameter reduction. This technique eliminates the need for oval excision and enables efficient, individualized, and reproducible aortoplasty with adequate diameter reduction.
