Developmental stage modulates the prognostic impact of RAS pathway mutations in AML patients undergoing Allo-HSCT
Authors: Xiaolin Yuan, Yibo Wu, Yanan Wang, Junqing Wu, Panpan Zhu, Luxin Yang, Xiaoyu Lai, Lizhen Liu, Jimin Shi, Jian Yu, Yanmin Zhao, Weiyan Zheng, Jingsong He, Jie Sun, Wenjun Wu, Yi Zhao, Zhen Cai, He Huang, Shanshan Pei, Yi Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe RAS pathway and developmental stage are known to influence leukemic cell proliferation and drug resistance. However, their impact on the prognosis of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) patients, particularly post-allo-HSCT, remains unclear. This study aimed to explore the effects of RAS-pathway mutations and developmental stage on the outcomes of AML patients who received allo-HSCT. A total of 364 consecutive adult AML patients who underwent their first allo-HSCT with myeloablative conditioning were enrolled in this study. The primary endpoint of this study was the cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) and secondary endpoints were OS and leukemia-free survival (LFS). RAS-pathway mutations were detected in 57 patients (15.7%). Subgroup analysis revealed opposite outcomes: RAS-pathway mutations were associated with a higher cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) (38.6% vs. 14.8%, P = 0.016) in the primitive AML subgroup and a lower CIR (3.4% vs. 26.8%, P = 0.013) and better leukemia-free survival (LFS) (93.1% vs. 66.9%, P = 0.013) in the monocytic subgroup, compared with AML patients without RAS-pathway mutations. Subgroup multivariable analyses in the primitive subgroup revealed that patients with RAS-pathway mutations had a higher CIR (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.55, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.21–5.40). Meanwhile, subgroup multivariable analyses in the monocytic subgroup showed that patients with RAS-pathway mutations exhibited a significantly improved LFS (HR = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.06–0.96). In summary, the prognosis of AML patients with RAS-pathway mutations who received allo-HSCT can be significantly influenced by the developmental stage at which the mutation occurs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06694-3.Keywords: Acute myeloid leukemia, RAS-pathway mutations, Developmental stage, Allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) — the most common form of acute leukemia in adults — is a molecularly and clinically heterogeneous disease with poor prognosis [1, 2]. Various disease-related features such as cytogenetics and mutational status have been identified in AML patients. Some of these features are valuable to risk stratification. The RAS signaling pathway plays an essential role in driving normal physiological cellular proliferation. Excessive activation of the RAS pathway leads to increased proliferation of hematopoietic progenitor cells and potentially plays a vital role in leukemogenesis and disease progression [3, 4]. Activation of the RAS pathway also mediates resistance to BCL-2 inhibitor, FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) or isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) inhibitors in AML [5, 6]. Mutations that activate RAS-pathway signaling, such as RAS gene mutations (NRAS, KRAS, or HRAS), as well as RAS-pathway regulators or downstream effectors (NF1, PTPN11, CBL, or BRAF), are among the most common drivers in hematological malignancies. About 30% of AML patients carry RAS-pathway mutations [1, 7, 8]. Among the RAS-pathway genes, NRAS, KRAS, and PTPN11 are the most commonly detected mutations in AML [9].

Findings from previous studies regarding the prognostic significance of RAS-pathway mutations in AML have been inconsistent. Previous studies reported that RAS mutations did not independently mpact the overall survival (OS) or disease-free survival (DFS) in adult AML patients, their impact is contextually modulated by therapy: cytarabine-based regimens improve outcomes in patients with RAS mutations, whereas homogeneous intensive chemotherapy neutralizes this effect [10, 11]. Better OS was observed among those with favorable karyotypes and those treated with intensive chemotherapy [12]. Kaburagi et al. found that NRAS mutations in pediatric AML were independent predictors of favorable outcomes. However, they found that PTPN11 and NF1 mutations were independent predictors of poor outcomes [8]. Eisfeld et al. demonstrated that NF1 mutations in adult AML were suggestive of an adverse prognostic impact in patients treated with standard chemotherapy [13]. Alfayez et al. also found that PTPN11 mutations were associated with an adverse prognosis in adults with AML [14]. However, the clinical relevance of RAS-pathway mutations in AML patients receiving allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) has not been revealed so far.

Recent studies showed that the leukemic cell maturation state was critical in determining biological and therapeutic responses in AML patients. The AML cell developmental stage has a significant impact on drug response and patient outcomes [15–18]. However, whether the differentiation stage of AML is prognostic in patients undergoing allo-HSCT remains elusive. Therefore, the present study analyzed RAS-pathway mutations and different developmental stages in AML patients receiving allo-HSCT to elucidate the genomic features, transplantation outcomes, and prognosis of RAS-pathway mutations.

Methods

Patients

A total of 364 consecutive AML patients who underwent their first allo-HSCT in the First Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University from June 2017 to June 2021 and received myeloablative conditioning regimen were retrospectively analyzed. Patients who had acute promyelocytic leukemia and lacked molecular data were excluded. The study was performed per the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University. All participants provided written informed consent authorizing the use of their personal information and clinical data for research purposes before allo-HSCT.

Definitions and outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) and secondary outcomes were OS and leukemia-free survival (LFS). CIR was calculated from allo-HSCT to relapse date. OS was defined as the duration from allo-HSCT to the date of death from any cause or censoring when the patient was last known to be alive. LFS was defined as the time from allo-HSCT to the date of relapse of disease or death from any cause. Complete remission (CR) was defined as < 5% leukemic cells in the bone marrow, and no leukemia cells in peripheral blood or extramedullary location. Relapse was defined as the reappearance of ≥ 5% bone marrow leukemia blasts and/or extramedullary lesions due to specific blast cell infiltration. Non-relapse mortality was defined as death without prior relapse. Complex karyotype was defined as ≥ 3 unrelated chromosome abnormalities in the absence of other class-defining recurring genetic abnormalities [19]. French-American-British (FAB)-M0/M1/M2 was defined as a primitive AML subgroup and FAB-M4/M5 as a monocytic AML subgroup [20–22].

Molecular characterization

Measurable residual disease (MRD) was determined based on multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC) and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). The cut-off for MFC-MRD positivity was >0.1% for CD45-expressing cells using either leukemia-associated immunophenotypes (LAIPs) or “different from normal” immunophenotypes. MRD positivity measured by RT-qPCR was defined as a cycling threshold (Ct) < 40. NPM1 or core-binding factor (CBF) fusions by qPCR >0.2% variant allele frequency (VAF) were considered MRD positive [23]. Patients with advanced disease at allo-HSCT were excluded from MRD assessment. After AML diagnosis, targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed using the 96-gene Myeloid Sequencing Panel (Illumina, San Diego, CA) on DNA extracted from a bone marrow sample (Supplementary Table 1).

Transplantation procedure

Transplantation procedures, including the conditioning regimen, graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis regimens, and supportive care, were performed as previously described [24]. All patients received a myeloablative conditioning regimen, consisting of busulfan and cyclophosphamide (BuCy) or busulfan, cyclophosphamide, and cytarabine (modified BuCy). All patients received mobilized peripheral blood stem cells as a source of the graft.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages) and compared using χ2 and Fisher’s exact tests. Continuous variables were expressed as the median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney test. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to estimate OS and LFS. Competitive risk analysis was conducted to calculate CIR and non-relapse mortality as competing risks. Multivariable analyses included variables with P < 0.15 in the univariable analysis or clinically essential variables. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis for survival and the Fine-Gray model for competing risk outcomes were used. Multivariable Cox regression analysis results were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. SPSS (Version 20.0, IBM Corp) and R (version 4.2.2, https://www.r-project.org/) were used for statistical analyses.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA was performed to determine the predominantly enriched pathways using the ClusterProfiler (version 4.9.3) package. The C5 GO biological process reference gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/human/collections.jsp) is utilized for performing GSEA analysis. GSEA results were expressed as the normalized enrichment score (NES) and its p-value. The GSEA profile of patients harboring RAS-pathway mutations and RAS-pathway wild type in primitive or monocytic AML was evaluated using the Cancer Genome Atlas database (TCGA) data set. TCGA-LAML raw RNA sequencing and mutational data were retrieved from the Genome Data Commons (GDC) data portal of TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/TCGA-LAML).

Results

Patient characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1. A total of 364 patients with de novo AML were enrolled. For the entire cohort, the median (IQR) follow-up for survivors was 1006 (648–1515) days and the median age at transplantation was 40 (IQR 29–49) years. RAS-pathway mutations were identified in 57 (15.7%) patients, with NRAS mutations observed in 40(70.2%) patients, KRAS mutations in 11 (19.3%) patients, HRAS mutations in 2 (3.5%) patients, PTPN11 mutations in 8 (14.0%) patients, CBL mutations in 4 (7.0%) patients, NF1 mutations in 5 (8.8%) patients, and BRAF mutations in 4 (7.0%) patients. Forty-four (77.2%) patients harbored a single RAS-pathway mutation, while 13 (22.8%) carried two or more mutations. The median age of patients with and without RAS-pathway mutations was 35 (IQR 26–47) and 41 (IQR 30–50) years, respectively (P = 0.014).

Association of RAS-pathway mutations with clinical and molecular features

The differences in hematologic and clinical parameters were compared between patients with RAS-pathway mutations and those with wild-type RAS-pathway (Table 1). Patients harboring RAS-pathway mutations were more likely to exhibit adverse risk according to the 2022 European LeukemiaNet (ELN2022) classification (P = 0.042) and higher white blood cell count (WBC) at the time of diagnosis (P = 0.042). No significant differences were observed between patients with and those without RAS-pathway mutations in terms of gender, hemoglobin, platelet count, bone marrow blasts, or developmental stage. The mutational landscape of the study population is depicted in Fig. 1. Results showed that patients with RAS-pathway mutations had a higher number of co-mutated genes (median: 3, IQR: 1–4) than those with RAS-pathway wild type AML (median: 3, IQR: 0–3) (P < 0.001). The most frequently co-occurring mutations in RAS-pathway-mutated AML were found in NPM1 (21.1%), DNMT3A (21.1%), KIT (19.3%), and TET2 (17.5%). Patients with RAS-pathway mutations exhibited significantly lower frequencies of CEBPA (P = 0.025) and FLT3-ITD (P = 0.025) mutations and higher frequencies of KIT (P = 0.002), IDH1 (P = 0.031), BCOR (P = 0.035), TTN (P < 0.001), and SMC1A (P < 0.001) mutations at the time of diagnosis than patients without RAS-pathway mutations. However, no significant difference in the occurrence of NPM1, DNMT3A, and TET2 mutations was detected between AML patients with and those without RAS-pathway mutations.

Impact of RAS-pathway mutations and developmental stage on clinical outcomes in the entire cohort

The 3-year CIR for the entire cohort was 21.0% (95% CI: 16.6–25.4%). As shown in Fig. 2 A, no statistically significant differences in CIR were found between RAS-pathway mutation and RAS-pathway wild type groups (21.6% vs. 21.0%, P = 0.740) in the entire cohort. Univariable analysis showed that NRAS, KRAS, or PTPN11 mutations did not significantly impact CIR (Supplementary Figure S1A, D, and G). KMT2A rearrangement, primary resistance, higher WBC count (≥ 20 × 109/L) at diagnosis, pre-MRD + and non-CR (nCR) status at allo-HSCT were associated with a higher CIR (Supplementary Table 2).

Prognostic value of RAS-pathway mutation status in the entire cohort. A, Probability of CIR. B, Probability of LFS. C, Probability of OS for AML patients received allo-HSCT

The 3-year LFS rate for the entire cohort was 72.9% (95% CI: 68.0–77.8.0.8%). The 3-year LFS was 74.9% (95% CI 61.6–88.2%) in the RAS-pathway mutation group and 72.4% (95% CI: 67.1–77.7%) in the RAS-pathway wild type group, and there was no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.436) (Fig. 2B). Additionally, no significant difference in LFS was found between patients with or without NRAS, KRAS, or PTPN11 mutation (Supplementary Figure S1B, E, and H). KMT2A rearrangement, primary resistance, BCOR mutation, pre-MRD + or ≥ CR2 (second CR), or nCR at allo-HSCT were associated with a lower LFS (Supplementary Table 2).

The 3-year OS was 77.4% (95% CI: 72.7–82.1%) for the entire cohort. The 3-year OS of RAS-pathway mutation and RAS-pathway wild type groups were comparable (76.5% vs. 77.2%, P = 0.475). No significant difference in OS was detected between patients with or without NRAS, KRAS, or PTPN11 mutation (Supplementary Figure S1C, F, and I). No significant differences in clinical outcomes were observed between patients with a single RAS-pathway mutation and those with two or more mutations. The 3-year OS was 75.1% (95% CI, 57.3–92.9%) in the single-mutation group and 79.1% (95% CI, 52.1–100.0%) in the multiple RAS-pathway mutation group (P = 0.926). Similarly, the 3-year LFS were 74.0% (95% CI, 57.7–90.3%) and 76.9% (95% CI, 54.0–99.8%), respectively (P = 0.818). The 3-year CIR was also comparable between the two groups: 21.5% (95% CI, 5.5–37.4%) versus 23.1% (95% CI, 0–47.0%; P = 0.527). Univariable analysis also revealed that KMT2A rearrangement, primary resistance, BCOR mutation, and pre-MRD+/nCR were associated with a shorter OS (Supplementary Table 2).

To assess the impact of leukemic cell developmental stage on patient outcomes, we initially analyzed the differences between different AML subtypes, that is, primitive and monocytic subtypes. One patient who was diagnosed with FAB-M6 and had no RAS-pathway mutations was excluded from the analysis. No statistical difference in RAS-pathway mutations distribution was observed between primitive and monocytic groups (P = 0.939). Univariable analysis showed that the developmental stage was not significantly associated with the CIR, LFS or OS following allo-HSCT (Supplementary Figure S2).

Multivariable analysis revealed that RAS-pathway mutations or developmental stages were not an independent predictor of CIR, LFS, or OS (Supplementary Table 3). CIR was significantly influenced by pre-MRD (HR = 3.47, P < 0.001), nCR (HR = 3.17, P = 0.003), and WBC count at diagnosis (HR = 1.93, P = 0.014). LFS was significantly influenced by pre-MRD (HR = 3.82, P < 0.001), nCR (HR = 4.59, P < 0.001), primary resistance (HR = 1.97, P = 0.007), FLT3-ITD (HR = 1.75, P = 0.017), KMT2A rearrangement (HR = 2.40, P = 0.002), and complex karyotype (HR = 3.29, P = 0.001). OS was significantly influenced by pre-MRD (HR = 3.59, P < 0.001), nCR (HR = 5.00, P < 0.001), primary resistance (HR = 1.86, P = 0.027), FLT3-ITD (HR = 1.85, P = 0.020), KMT2A rearrangement (HR = 3.28, P < 0.001), and complex karyotype (HR = 3.20, P = 0.005).

Impact of RAS-pathway mutations on clinical outcomes in different developmental stage subgroups

In the primitive AML cohort (FAB-M0/1/2), the 3-year CIR was significantly higher in the RAS-pathway mutation group than in the RAS-pathway wild type group (38.6% vs. 14.8%, P = 0.016; Fig. 3 A, blue line). LFS and OS were lower in patients with RAS-pathway mutation than in those with RAS-pathway wild type, although not reaching statistical significance (LFS: 57.8% vs. 77.9%, P = 0.102; OS: 61.9% vs. 80.5%, P = 0.321; Figs. 3B and C, blue line). In the subgroup of patients with the monocytic subtype (FAB-M4/5), the 3-year CIR was significantly lower in those with RAS-pathway mutations compared to those with the wild type RAS pathway (3.4% vs. 26.8%, P = 0.013; Fig. 3 A, red line). Moreover, patients carrying RAS-pathway mutations exhibited a significantly higher 3-year LFS compared to those with wild type RAS pathway patients (93.1% vs. 66.9%, P = 0.013; Fig. 3B, red line). The 3-year OS was markedly increased in patients with RAS-pathway mutations compared to those without, but the difference was not significant (93.1% vs. 74.7%, P = 0.068; Fig. 3 C, red line). Patients with RAS-pathway mutations in monocytic subgroup had the lowest CIR, and best LFS and OS among all subgroups. Univariable analysis of transplant outcomes of patients in primitive and monocytic subgroups are presented in Supplementary Table 4.

Impact of RAS-pathway mutation status on the post-HSCT outcomes in different development stages. (A) Probability of CIR, (B) probability of LFS and (C) probability of OS according to RAS-pathway mutation and development stages

Subsequently, we conducted multivariable analysis in both the primitive subgroup and the monocytic subgroup. Results showed that RAS-pathway mutations were associated with higher CIR in the primitive subgroup (HR = 2.55, 95% CI: 1.21–5.40; P = 0.014; Fig. 4 A). RAS-pathway mutations were associated with superior LFS in the monocytic subgroup (HR = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.06–0.96; P = 0.043; Fig. 4B), and there was a trend toward an improved OS (HR = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.05–1.05; P = 0.057) and a lower CIR (HR = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.02–1.15; P = 0.068) in RAS-pathway mutations, but with no statistical significance.

Association of RAS-pathway mutations with molecular features in different developmental stage subgroups

To identify co-occurring mutations, we conducted mutational profiling of RAS-pathway-mutated AML in different developmental stage subgroups. An overview of co-mutational distributions is depicted in Fig. 5. Notably, we found that only ASXL1 mutation was unevenly distributed between primitive and monocytic AML groups(P < 0.05), and we did not find an impact of ASXL1 mutations on prognosis (Supplementary Table 2). No sigcnificant differences were observed in the distribution of the other co-occurring genes (NPM1, DNMT3A, KIT, TET2, and FLT3-ITD). The co-occurring mutations are insufficient to explain the difference in outcomes between the two developmental stage subgroups.

Gene set enrichment analysis

We then hypothesized that the presence of RAS-pathway mutations in monocytic AML may synergistically interact with other pathways to facilitate the graft-versus-leukemia (GVL) effect. We subsequently examined AML data from TCGA. GSEA analysis showed that immune response pathways such as antigen processing and presentation, complement activation, interleukin 6 production and t cell mediated cytotoxicity are enriched in monocytic AML patients with RAS-pathway mutations (Fig. 6, Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

Here, we present findings indicating that both RAS-pathway mutations and the developmental stage of leukemic cells determine the clinical outcomes of AML patients undergoing allo-HSCT. Mutations in the RAS-pathway have a positive prognostic impact in the monocytic subgroup, while having a negative prognostic impact in the primitive subgroup. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate RAS-pathway mutations in AML patients undergoing allo-HSCT and demonstrate new associations between RAS-pathway mutations and different developmental stage subgroups.

In this study, 57/364 (15.7%) patients harbored RAS-pathway mutations, of which NRAS and KRAS were the most prevalent mutations. Compared to those with RAS-pathway wild type AML, those with RAS-pathway mutation AML were younger and had higher WBC counts at diagnosis. These results are consistent with the findings of previous studies [12, 25]. Additionally, patients with RAS-pathway mutations were more frequently categorized into ELN high-risk classes compared to patients with RAS-pathway wild type.

Previous studies found that RAS-pathway mutations were mutually exclusive with each other [8, 26]. Tyner et al. revealed that the PTPN11 mutation was mutually exclusive with the NRAS mutation [9]. However, other studies in myeloid neoplasms have challenged the dogma of the mutual exclusivity of RAS-pathway mutations [6, 27, 28]. Our study also found no evidence of mutual exclusion of RAS-pathway mutations. Multiple RAS-pathway mutations were observed in the current study, which could potentially be attributed to the existence of various subclones with RAS-pathway mutations. Several co-occurring and exclusive mutations have been detected in patients with RAS-pathway mutations. In our cohort of RAS-pathway-mutated AML, NPM1 was the most frequently observed co-occurring mutation. RAS-pathway mutations and FLT3-ITD were mutually exclusive in this study, which is consistent with findings from previous investigations [8, 9, 11]. Patients with RAS-pathway mutations were more likely to co-occur with KIT, IDH1 and BCOR mutation than RAS-pathway wild type patients at diagnosis. We did not find the prognostic impact of these co-occurring mutations in our series.

The FAB classification system has traditionally been utilized to categorize AML into M0-M7. Despite the incorporation of molecular categories into the World Health Organization (WHO) AML classification [29], FAB classification, which is an approximate of the differentiation stage of AML, remains a core feature of the large WHO category of AML. Data from the BEAT-AML study showed co-enrichment between FAB-M5 and RAS mutations [9]. Bowen et al. reported a relatively higher occurrence of KRAS mutations in FAB-M4 [11]. PTPN11 mutations were more commonly associated with the acute myelomonocytic/monocytic leukemia subtype FAB-M5 compared with wild-type PTPN11 [9]. However, Kaburagi et al. found that PTPN11 mutations were frequently detected in minimally differentiated FAB-M0 [8]. FAB subtypes exhibited similar patterns among patients with or without RAS-pathway mutations in our study. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the population included across studies.

We found that RAS-pathway mutations did not significantly influence clinical outcomes across our entire patient cohort regarding CIR, LFS, and OS, consistent with previous studies indicating that RAS-pathway mutation status alone may not dictate prognosis for all AML patients. Bowen et al. found that RAS mutations did not influence clinical outcomes (OS, DFS, CR, or CIR) in AML patients younger than 60 years [11]. However, Kaburagi et al. found that NRAS mutations were independent predictors of favorable outcomes, while PTPN11 and NF1 mutations predicted poorer outcomes in pediatric AML [8]. Our study examined the prognostic significance of single gene mutations in the RAS-pathway and found that NRAS, KRAS, and PTPN11 mutations were not associated with clinical outcomes.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the AML immune microenvironment is associated with the prognosis [22, 30–32]. Notably, GSEA analysis of AML data from TCGA revealed that immune response pathways were significantly enriched in monocytic AML patients harboring RAS-pathway mutations. This enrichment suggests a potential interaction between these mutations and immune mechanisms that could influence clinical outcomes. Specifically, pathways such as antigen processing and presentation, complement activation, and T-cell mediated cytotoxicity may enhance the graft-versus-leukemia (GVL) effect in these patients. Additional experimental investigations are necessary to substantiate this finding.

It is noteworthy that previous studies did not investigate the effects of RAS-pathway mutations in different developmental stages. Yang et al. reported no significant difference in OS between RAS-mutated and wild-type patients with FAB M4/M5 subtypes who did not receive allo-HSCT [33]. In contrast, our multivariable analysis stratified by developmental stage revealed divergent prognostic impacts between primitive and monocytic subgroups. This discrepancy may explain varying prognostic implications reported in previous studies regarding RAS-pathway mutations involvement in AML outcomes. Biologically, AML comprises primitive and differentiated cellular populations. Primitive AML cells — commonly referred to as leukemia stem cell-like cells — sustain the disease and exhibit stem cell characteristics (self-renewal and resistance to therapy). Differentiated AML cells exhibit a deficiency in self-renewal capacity but can affect the tumor microenvironment or hematopoietic function [34]. Moreover, pathway analysis demonstrated that immune response pathways were upregulated in monocytic AML patients harboring RAS-pathway mutations. These findings highlight the significance of cell differentiation effects on prognosis. For more primitively differentiated AML with RAS-pathway mutations, the GVL effect may not be sufficiently potent to eradicate these leukemic cells. Consequently, more frequent follow-ups and monitoring are essential for patients at high risk of relapse. Focusing on these patients for prompt interventions, such as donor lymphocyte infusions (DLI), demethylating agents or cytokine therapy, could decrease relapse rates and extend survival [35–38]. In this regard, we are currently engaged in a prospective clinical trial (ChiCTR2000033183) that investigates the efficacy of prophylactic interventions with azacitidine (AZA) and modified DLI in high-risk AML/MDS patients to prevent relapse after allo-HSCT.

Nevertheless, this study has some limitations. This is a single-center retrospective study with relatively limited sample size, leading to selection bias. There may have been imbalances in patient characteristics between the groups, including ELN risk classification, potentially affecting the study outcome. Nevertheless, we employed multivariable analysis to minimize the possible distributional imbalance and confounders on the outcomes, thereby enhancing the validity of the conclusions. Given the retrospective study design, complete immunophenotypic profiles were unavailable for some patients. Additionally, the FAB classification system may not comprehensively reflect the phenotypic heterogeneity of AML. Therefore, further large-scale multicenter studies and experimental validation should be conducted to ascertain the clinical significance of RAS-pathway mutations in AML patients undergoing allo-HSCT.

In summary, this study reveals a stark contrast in the outcomes of AML patients with RAS-pathway mutations, depending on the developmental stage of leukemic cells. Our findings suggest the use of both cellular developmental stage and molecular data for predicting outcomes in AML patients undergoing allo-HSCT.​
