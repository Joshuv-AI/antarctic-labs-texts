Early-third-trimester uterine rupture in an unscarred primigravida with a didelphic uterus: a case report
Authors: Tirusew Nigussie Kebede, Getaneh Dejen Tiche, Tebabere Moltot Kitaw, Birhan Tsegaw Taye, Moges Sisay Chekole, Tesfanesh Lema, Mulualem Sileshi, Girma Wogie Fitie, Mesfin Tadese, Kidist Ayalew Abebe, Legesse Demissie, Zekarias Taye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionUterus didelphys, one of the rarest types of Müllerian anomaly, is characterized by the presence of two separate uterine horns and/or two distinct cervices due to the failure of Müllerian duct fusion. It is associated with poor obstetric outcomes, including spontaneous abortion, preterm labor, fetal malpresentation, and, rarely, uterine rupture.Case presentationA 19-year-old primigravida from Sela Dingay woreda (resident of Sela Dingay town) in the North Shewa Zone of the Amhara Region was urgently referred to Debre Berhan University Hakim Gizaw Teaching Hospital from the woreda health center with a diagnosis of early third-trimester pregnancy (29 weeks, confirmed by a reliable last normal menstrual period) complicated by placental abruption. Clinical and instrumental evaluations revealed severe anemia (hemoglobin 5.5 g/dL), free fluid in the peritoneal cavity, absent fetal heartbeat, and a fundal placenta. Vaginal examination revealed a single cervix dilated to 1 cm. Given the suspicion of uterine rupture, preparations for an emergency laparotomy were initiated. After transfusing 2 units of fresh whole blood and obtaining informed consent, surgery was performed. Intraoperative findings included a rupture of the posterior aspect of the right hemi-uterus in a didelphic uterus. A newly deceased female fetus weighing 1.2 kg was extracted from the peritoneal cavity, and a subtotal hysterectomy of the ruptured hemi-uterus was carried out. Following postoperative stabilization in the intensive care unit, the patient was transferred to the maternity ward and discharged on the fifth postoperative day. She received counseling on future pregnancies, was administered Implanon NXT for long-acting contraception, and prescribed ferrous sulfate for 1 month. This case underscores the critical importance of comprehensive prenatal screening and vigilant management of pregnancies complicated by uterine anomalies to minimize risks and optimize maternal–fetal outcomes.ConclusionUterine didelphys is rarely associated with early-third-trimester pre-labor uterine rupture, but when it occurs, it results significant feto-maternal comorbidity. Therefore, it is advisable to screen every primigravida mother for uterine anomalies in addition to performing fetal anatomical scans in the early trimester. This is crucial because the complications associated with this anomaly can be unexpected and may lead to maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality due to delayed treatment.

Introduction

Müllerian anomalies are rare congenital malformations of the female reproductive tract [1]. These anomalies range from complete agenesis of the uterus and vagina to duplication of the uterus and vagina, as well as more subtle abnormalities affecting the uterine cavity [1, 2]. Although considered relatively benign, Müllerian anomalies have a reported prevalence of 4–7% in the general population [3] and are closely associated with various reproductive complications. These include increased rates of first- and second-trimester-pregnancy losses, challenges with natural and assisted conception, preterm birth, low birth weight, malpresentation, placental abruption, and elevated perinatal mortality [4]. Uterus didelphys (UD) is a specific type of Müllerian duct anomaly (MDA) caused by the failure of fusion of the paired Müllerian ducts during embryogenesis. It is characterized by the presence of two separate uterine horns, often accompanied by two distinct cervices and sometimes duplication of the vagina. UD affects approximately 1 in 3000 women and accounts for 8–10% of all Müllerian anomalies [5]. It is the second least common Müllerian anomaly, surpassed only by complete Müllerian agenesis [6].

There are two primary variations of uterus didelphys: uterus didelphys unicollis and uterus didelphys bicollis. The more common type, uterus didelphys unicollis, is characterized by two separate uterine bodies that share a single cervix, resulting in one common vaginal canal. In contrast, uterus didelphys bicollis involves two completely separate uterine cavities, each with its own cervix and vagina, with no communication between them [7, 8]. Uterus didelphys is frequently associated with renal anomalies, including renal agenesis and pelvic kidney, as well as other Müllerian duct anomalies. Notably, a longitudinal vaginal septum is present in approximately 75% of patients with uterus didelphys, which can cause difficulties with sexual intercourse and may complicate vaginal delivery [9]. Between 15% and 20% of patients with a didelphic uterus exhibit unilateral anomalies such as an obstructed hemivagina accompanied by ipsilateral renal agenesis or other renal anomalies. In approximately 65% of these cases, the anomalies are located on the right side [10]. Patients with an obstructed hemivagina and ipsilateral renal anomaly typically have regular menstruation, as menstrual blood can exit through the patent cervix and hemivagina. However, they often experience cyclic pelvic pain due to the accumulation of blood in the obstructed hemivagina. Additionally, a small fistulous connection between the patent and obstructed vaginal canals can lead to infections within the obstructed hemivagina. In rare cases, bilateral complete obstruction occurs, resulting in primary amenorrhea [11, 12].

UD is associated with a significant increase in preterm labor and fetal malpresentation at delivery. Studies have reported spontaneous abortion rates of up to 32% and preterm birth rates of approximately 28% in patients with UD [13, 14]. Additionally, there is evidence suggesting an increased risk of fetal growth restriction in this population [15]. Structural abnormalities of the cervix and uterus in UD also predispose affected women to cervical incompetence, which may further contribute to the elevated risk of preterm labor [16, 17]. However, the incidence of first- and second-trimester miscarriages does not appear to be higher than in the general population [18]. Approximately 45% of pregnancies in women with uterus didelphys reach full term [19]. Rupture of a uterus didelphys during pregnancy is exceedingly rare, with only two cases reported in the literature to the best of the investigators’ knowledge—one occurring in the second trimester and the other in the third trimester [20, 21]. This study aims to present a rare case of uterine rupture in a pregnant woman with unicollis didelphic uterus during the early third trimester.

Case presentation

A 19-year-old Amhara primigravida from Sela Dingay woreda (resident of Sela Dingay town) in the North Shewa Zone of the Amhara Region was referred to Debre Berhan University Hakim Gizaw Teaching Hospital from the Sela Dingay District Health Center, located 70.1 km away. The referral diagnosis was early third-trimester pregnancy at 29 weeks’ gestation (based on a reliable last normal menstrual period), complicated by placental abruption. She presented with vaginal bleeding and sudden-onset crampy abdominal pain lasting for the past 8 hours. Upon arrival, the patient appeared acutely ill. Her vital signs were as follows: pulse rate of 128 beats per minute, blood pressure of 88/50 mmHg, respiratory rate of 40 breaths per minute, temperature of 36.3 °C, and oxygen saturation of 80% on room air, which improved to 96% with nasal oxygen supplementation. She was semi-conscious, with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 14 out of 15. Abdominal examination revealed a tender, rigid abdomen, making accurate assessment of the uterine fundal height difficult. Genitourinary examination showed minimal active vaginal bleeding. Digital vaginal examination was deferred until placenta previa could be ruled out.

The patient was admitted to the emergency obstetrics unit, where two intravenous lines were secured. She was resuscitated with normal saline and Ringer’s lactate and received oxygen at a rate of 10 L/minute via face mask. The working diagnosis was third-trimester pregnancy in a primigravida complicated by hypovolemic shock, likely secondary to placental abruption. Basic laboratory investigations, including blood crossmatching and obstetric ultrasound, were ordered.

Obstetric ultrasound revealed free fluid in the peritoneal cavity, absence of fetal cardiac activity, and a fundal placenta; no uterine anomalies were identified. Laboratory results showed a hematocrit of 16.8%, hemoglobin level of 5.5 g/dL, platelet count of 170,000/µL, and normal liver and renal function tests. The patient’s blood type was B positive, and 4 units of fresh whole blood were prepared. After placenta previa was ruled out, a digital pelvic examination was performed, revealing a single cervix dilated to 1 cm, with bleeding noted on the examiner’s glove. On the basis of the clinical and investigative findings, the patient was urgently transfused with 2 units of whole blood. Given the suspicion of uterine rupture, an emergency laparotomy was planned. Informed written consent was obtained from the patient’s husband and mother after thoroughly explaining the findings and proposed procedure in the local language, Amharic. The patient was then transferred to the obstetric operating room with ongoing oxygen support.

Intraoperative finding

Following standard aseptic preparation with saline and povidone-iodine, the abdomen was draped with sterile covers. Under general anesthesia, a midline infra-umbilical vertical skin incision was made. Upon entering the peritoneal cavity, approximately 3000 mL of hemo-peritoneum was evacuated. A recently demised female fetus, weighing 1.2 kg, was found within the peritoneal cavity and delivered. Further exploration revealed a complete uterine rupture measuring approximately 10–15 cm on the posterior wall of the right hemi-uterus. Notably, an additional non-pregnant uterus, consistent in size with a 12-week gestation, was identified in the pelvis, located posterior and to the left of the ruptured uterus. Each hemi-uterus had a corresponding fallopian tube and ovary, consistent with a diagnosis of uterus didelphys. A subtotal hysterectomy was performed on the ruptured right hemi-uterus, with preservation of the ipsilateral ovary. Hemostasis was secured, and bilateral blood transfusions were continued intraoperatively. The patient was then successfully extubated and transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) in stable condition. After 2 days of intensive monitoring and supportive management, the patient was clinically stable and transferred to the gynecology ward. She continued to recover well and was discharged on postoperative day 5. Prior to discharge, she received comprehensive counseling on future reproductive health, including the risks associated with uterine anomalies. Discussion

Uterine rupture is a rare but serious peripartum complication, often associated with catastrophic outcomes for both the mother and fetus [22]. It represents a significant public health and clinical concern, particularly in low-income countries, where the reported incidence ranges from 0.4% to 3.98% [23–29]. This condition can result in massive maternal hemorrhage, severe fetal hypoxia, and high perinatal mortality, frequently necessitating extensive blood transfusion and, in severe cases, emergency hysterectomy [23]. While uterine rupture most commonly occurs during labor, particularly among women with risk factors such as a previous cesarean section, induction or augmentation of labor, fetal macrosomia, grand multiparty, and obstructed labor, it is exceedingly rare in non-laboring women, especially during the second or early third trimester [30–34]. Such early gestational ruptures are exceptionally life-threatening and are poorly characterized in the literature, with limited epidemiological data available. This underscores the importance of reporting and analyzing such rare presentations to better understand their risk factors, clinical course, and management outcomes.

Evidence suggests that uterine rupture during the second and early third trimesters of pregnancy is associated with several risk factors. These include multiple previous cesarean deliveries, a history of prior uterine rupture, classical uterine incisions, prior myomectomy, short inter-pregnancy intervals, placenta previa, morbidly adherent placenta (placenta accreta spectrum), and congenital uterine anomalies. Congenital anomalies alone account for approximately 16.3% of uterine ruptures occurring during this stage of gestation [35–38]. The prevalence of uterine anomalies in the general population is estimated to be 1 in 201 women (0.5%). Among these, the distribution is as follows: arcuate uterus (7%), septate uterus (34%), bicornuate uterus (39%), didelphic uterus (4–11%), unicornuate uterus (5%), and other forms, including hypoplastic, aplastic, or solid variants (4%). In such anomalies, the uterine walls may become abnormally thin and structurally compromised as pregnancy progresses. The myometrial thickness in these malformed uteri is often inconsistent, predisposing them to rupture even in early gestation. This highlights the critical need for early diagnosis and close monitoring of pregnancies complicated by uterine malformations to prevent potentially life-threatening outcomes [5, 39].

Additional evidence indicates that uterine rupture occurs in approximately 8% of women with congenitally malformed uteri, compared with just 0.61% in those with anatomically normal uteri attempting labor [40]. Rupture during the second and early third trimesters remains an unexpected and rare event. For instance, there are reports of uterine rupture occurring as early as 17 weeks of gestation in a didelphic uterus, initially misdiagnosed as a surgical emergency such as a ruptured appendix. The correct diagnosis was only made intraoperatively, highlighting the diagnostic challenge posed by such atypical presentations [20].

Our case reflects a similar diagnostic dilemma. Uterine rupture was not initially suspected at 29 weeks of gestation. The clinical presentation minimal vaginal bleeding and a tender, rigid abdomen suggested placental abruption. However, the presence of free intraperitoneal fluid on ultrasound raised the suspicion of uterine rupture. Definitive diagnosis was only confirmed during laparotomy. This delay underscores the diagnostic uncertainty in non-laboring women at earlier gestational ages and the risk of potential delays in critical interventions. Greater clinical awareness is necessary to ensure timely diagnosis and management in such rare but life-threatening scenarios. Another documented case involved a 26-year-old woman (gravida 4) with a history of three prior births: a vaginal home delivery, a cesarean section at term, and a subsequent vaginal home delivery. During her fourth pregnancy, at 39 weeks of gestation, she experienced spontaneous uterine rupture in a previously scarred uterus didelphys before the onset of labor [21].

Although women with a uterus didelphys have relatively favorable term delivery rates—approximately 45% compared with other types of Müllerian duct anomalies (MDA)—there remains a rare but significant risk of spontaneous uterine rupture either before labor or during the early third trimester, as illustrated by our case and others reported in the literature [13, 20].

The pathophysiology of uterine rupture in cases of uterus didelphys likely involves a combination of structural myometrial weakness, altered vascular supply, asymmetric uterine growth, and increased intrauterine pressure. These factors may act synergistically to predispose the uterus to rupture even in the absence of labor. Given these risks, comprehensive prenatal evaluation is essential particularly in primigravida women not only for fetal anatomical assessment but also to screen for uterine anomalies. If uterine didelphys is identified, vigilant antenatal monitoring and timely obstetric intervention are critical to reduce maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality. Early diagnosis can facilitate appropriate birth planning and emergency preparedness, thereby improving clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

Uterine didelphys is rarely associated with early-third-trimester pre-labor uterine rupture, but when it occurs, it results significant feto-maternal comorbidity. Patients with uterus didelphys are considered to be in a high-risk group, with increased complications associated with the malformation. Therefore, meticulous follow-up is crucial for such cases. Prompt and accurate diagnosis of uterine malformations, along with appropriate surgical interventions, are essential steps in preventing complications and ensuring the best possible pregnancy outcomes.
