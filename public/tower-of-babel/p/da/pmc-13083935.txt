Association between stress hyperglycemia ratio and all-cause mortality in neurocritical patients
Authors: Yuyang Pan, Wen Tan, Jianhua Peng, Kuangyang Yu, Yubin Yang, Jinwei Pang, Yong Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFluctuations in blood glucose during acute neurocritical illness are associated with poor outcomes, but the role of stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) in predicting outcomes across diverse neurocritical conditions remains unclear. This study evaluated SHR as a prognostic indicator for short and long-term mortality and its utility in machine learning-enhanced predictive models. Using data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) version 3.0 database, 2376 patients with acute neurocritical illness were grouped by admission SHR quartiles (Q1–Q4). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, Cox proportional hazards models, and restricted cubic splines assessed the associations between the SHR index and the mortality at 30, 90, 180, and 360 days. Machine learning algorithms optimized feature selection for 30-day mortality prediction, with SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values to visualize model contributions. A total of 2376 participants were included in the study. Higher SHR quartiles were associated with increased all-cause mortality across different time points compared to Q2 as shown by Cox proportional hazards models. Risk escalated when SHR ≥ 0.86, with stronger associations in non-diabetic patients. Higher SHR is strongly associated with increased all-cause mortality across different time horizons and may serve as a valuable prognostic tool for neurocritically ill patients, especially in non-diabetics. Introduction

Neurocritical care is pivotal in addressing the increasing burden of acute neurological emergencies, such as intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), acute ischemic stroke (AIS), and traumatic brain injury (TBI). These conditions are associated with high rates of morbidity and mortality1–3, often leaving survivors with long-term disabilities like cognitive impairment or paralysis. This poses significant challenges to healthcare systems worldwide4–6. Although these conditions differ etiologically, they share key pathological mechanisms—including oxidative stress, cerebral edema, and metabolic disturbances like stress hyperglycemia7. Yet existing research has largely neglected these commonalities, underscoring the need to identify prognostic factors that improve precision in patient management.

Stress hyperglycemia, defined as acute elevations in blood glucose due to physiological stress, is widely recognized as a marker of poor prognosis in acute neurological conditions8,9. Traditional glucose measurements have limitations; single-timepoint assessments fail to account for the dynamic interplay between acute glycemic spikes and chronic control, particularly in patients with pre-existing diabetes who may present with baseline hyperglycemia. The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), calculated as the ratio of admission blood glucose to glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), offers a more comprehensive metric by integrating both acute glucose levels and long-term glycemic status10.

Recent studies have demonstrated that SHR is also associated with adverse outcomes and complications across various critically ill populations. For instence, SHR showed significant associations with both short-term and long-term mortality in patients with chronic heart failure11, coronary artery disease12, and stroke13. In addition, SHR has been linked to an increased risk of adverse complications in critically ill patients in the intensive care unit (ICU). For example, SHR has been identified as an independent predictor of acute kidney injury in patients with acute myocardial infarction14, while another study reported a significant association between SHR and the development of new-onset atrial fibrillation in patients with sepsis15. Another study involving a broader ICU population of elderly patients admitted due to surgery or trauma, reported that SHR was independently associated with the occurrence of delirium and demonstrated superior predictive performance compared with established critical illness severity scores, such as the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), and Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II)16. These findings highlight the potential of SHR as a valuable biomarker for risk stratification across diverse disease states and underscore its complementary role alongside conventional severity scoring systems.

However, the utility of SHR as a universal prognostic marker for various neurocritical illnesses remains underexplored, particularly regarding long-term mortality and individuals without persistent diabetes. Most studies have concentrated on single diseases or short-term outcomes, neglecting the shared metabolic disturbances among these conditions. This knowledge gap hinders the development of universal risk-stratification tools for acute neurological emergencies. Given the potential of timely intervention for stress hyperglycemia to alter disability and survival trajectories, defining the role of SHR in prognosis presents substantial clinical potential. Establishing a link between this metabolic marker and patient outcomes could empower clinicians to identify high-risk individuals early and implement personalized strategies targeting stress-induced hyperglycemia.

Additionally, the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) remains a cornerstone tool for assessing consciousness levels in neurocritically ill patients. Its strong correlation with both short-term mortality and long-term functional outcomes aids in early clinical triaging and resource allocation17. Yet, GCS alone may not fully capture metabolic derangements or systemic comorbidities, limiting its predictive accuracy across diverse neurocritical populations18,19. Incorporating SHR into predictive models alongside traditional scoring systems like GCS could address the limitations of single metrics and enhance risk assessment in neurocritical care.

To address these issues, we conducted a retrospective analysis using the (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV) MIMIC-IV database. This study investigates the association between admission SHR and both short-term and long-term all-cause mortality in a heterogeneous cohort of neurocritically ill patients. By integrating data from various neurological emergencies, this research aims to bridge the gap between disease-specific insights and universal prognostic frameworks, ultimately improving risk stratification in clinical decision-making. Our findings reveal that higher SHR is strongly associated with increased all-cause mortality and demonstrates enhanced predictive value when combined with GCS scores, surpassing the predictive capacity of GCS alone in neurocritical illness outcomes.

Methods

Data source

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from the MIMIC-IV, version 3.0 database. MIMIC-IV is a large, freely accessible critical care database developed by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, comprising de-identified health-related data from over 360,000 patients and 90,000 ICU admissions at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2022. Access to the database was granted after completing the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) program. One author (Yuyang Pan) was responsible for data extraction (Record ID: 69101127).

Study design and population

We included patients diagnosed with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), acute ischemic stroke (AIS), or traumatic brain injury (TBI) based on ICD-9 or ICD-10 diagnostic codes. If a patient had multiple ICU admissions, only the first ICU stay was considered for analysis. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) Age < 18 years. (2) ICU length of stay < 24 h. (3) Missing blood glucose or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) values within the first 24 h after ICU admission. After applying these criteria, a total of 2,376 patients were included in the final cohort. Patients were subsequently divided into four quartiles according to SHR (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of patient screening.

Data extraction

The data extraction process was executed utilizing Navicat Premium16.3.2. The baseline characteristics of patients included the following: (1) Demographics: age and gender; (2) Vital signs: heart rate, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), respiratory rate, temperature, oxygen saturation (SpO2), and urine output; (3) Severity of illness scores: minimum Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI); (4) Comorbidities: congestive heart failure (CHF), peripheral vascular disease (PVD), peptic ulcer disease (PUD), malignant cancer (MC), chronic kidney disease (CKD), acute kidney injury (AKI) stage, diabetes, and hypertension; (5) Laboratory parameters: glucose, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), hemoglobin, platelet count, red blood cells (RBC), white blood cells (WBC), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, sodium, potassium, and international normalized ratio (INR); (6) Treatment: norepinephrine, antibiotics, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB), invasive intravascular catheterization (IIC), and ventilation. The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) is defined as the formula: SHR = (admission blood glucose (mg/dL))/(28.7 × HbA1c(%) − 46.7). All data was collected within the first 24 h after ICU admission.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at 30 days after ICU admission, while the secondary outcomes were all-cause mortality at 90,180,360 days after ICU admission.

Statistical analyses

As this was a retrospective study, no sample size calculation was performed. Variables with > 20% missing data were excluded; those with < 20% missingness were imputed via multiple imputation. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage. Group differences were assessed by Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), depending on the normality assessed by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Group comparisons were conducted using ANOVA for normally distributed variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test otherwise.

Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests were used to compare mortality across SHR quartiles at 30, 90, 180, and 360 days following ICU admission. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for the association between SHR and all-cause mortality at each time point. SHR was analyzed as both a continuous and categorical variable. Collinearity of variables was assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients. Variables that showed obvious statistical significance between groups or were considered to have particular clinical relevance were included in the multivariable model, and their variance inflation factor (VIF) were calculated. Two models were developed: Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 included age, gender, SBP, temperature, SpO₂, urine output, CCI, CHF, COPD, PUD, MC, CKD, AKI stage, diabetes, hypertension, hemoglobin, platelet count, red blood cell count, creatinine, and potassium.

Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to explore potential non-linear relationships between SHR and mortality. Subgroup analyses were stratified by age, gender, heart failure, diabetes, and hypertension.

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is a crucial tool for assessing consciousness levels and neurological dysfunction, offering significant prognostic value in patients under neurocritical care. In this study, we incorporated SHR into the GCS system to evaluate its impact on predicting mortality risk. The improvement in predictive accuracy was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

In this study, we developed machine learning models to predict 30-day mortality in neurocritical patients after ICU admission, aiming to support early intervention and improve outcomes. The study cohort was divided into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio. Given the significant class imbalance in our dataset (with survivors substantially outnumbered deceased patients), Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was employed to generate more samples of dead patients and make the outcome distribution ratio close to 1:1 to reduce the risk of overfitting. All covariables were incorporated into Boruta algorithm, and the variables that had an impact on outcomes were initially identified. To reduce the influence of multicollinearity, the selected variables were then included in the LASSO regression to obtain the final variables. Feature selection and SMOTE performed exclusively within the training set.

Five algorithms were used to construct the prediction model. Including eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), K Nearest-Neighbors (KNN) and Neural Network (NNET). Hyperparameter tuning was achieved through 5-fold cross-validation combined with grid search. Hyperparameter tuning performed exclusively within the training set. The performance of models was evaluated by area under the ROC curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and F1 score. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was employed to evaluate the clinical utility of the best model.

To overcome the ‘black-box’ nature of machine learning algorithms, the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method was implemented to interpret models’ prediction process by quantifying the contribution of individual features to the outcome. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Relationship between SHR and outcomes

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed significant differences in mortality across SHR quartiles at all time points (log-rank test, P < 0.001; Fig. 2). Patients in higher quartiles experienced lower survival rates at all time points compared to those in lower quartiles. Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess relationships between SHR and outcomes (Table 2). The Pearson correlation coefficients between variables (Supplementary Table 1), as well as the VIFs of the variables included in the multivariable model (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicated a low level of multicollinearity. Q2 was used as reference. In model1 (unadjusted model), SHR as a continuous variable was associated with increased 30-day mortality (HR = 1.29, 95% CI = 1.22–1.37, P < 0.001). When SHR was analyzed as a categorical variable, the 30-day mortality of Q1 (HR = 0.55, 95% CI = 0.4–0.75, P < 0.001) and Q4 (HR = 1.99, 95% CI = 1.57–2.54, P < 0.001) were significantly different from that of Q2. In model2 (multivariate-adjusted model), SHR as a continuous variable remained an independent predictor (HR = 1.35, 95% CI = 1.26–1.46, P < 0.001). When SHR was analyzed as a categorical variable, the 30-day mortality of Q1 (HR = 0.51, 95% CI = 0.37–0.71, P < 0.001) and Q4 (HR = 1.69, 95% CI = 1.32–2.16, P < 0.001) also showed significant difference from Q2. Similar associations were observed for 90, 180, and 360-day mortality. Notably, the protective effect of Q1 weakened over time. The adjusted HR and 95% CI of Q1 on 90, 180 and 360-day were 0.67 (95% CI = 0.53–0.88, P = 0.004), 0.76 (95% CI = 0.59–0.98, P = 0.032), 0.78 (95% CI = 0.62–0.99, P = 0.04).

Restricted cubic spine

RCS analyses revealed a nonlinear relationship between SHR and all-cause mortality (P for nonlinear < 0.001). Using SHR = 0.86 as the reference, we observed differing risk trends on both sides (Fig. 3). Considering the significant nonlinear relationship between SHR and outcomes, we conducted a piecewise Cox regression analysis to explore the relationship (Table 3). The results showed that when SHR < 0.86, SHR had no influence on the outcome. When SHR ≥ 0.86, it had a significant impact on the outcome as SHR increased.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis explored the relationship between SHR and 30-day mortality, stratified by age, gender, CHF, diabetes, and hypertension. No interaction effects were observed (P for interaction > 0.05; Fig. 4). Using Q2 as the reference, Q4 was not associated with a significant difference in mortality among patients with diabetes (HR = 1.1, 95% CI = 0.7–1.72). In all other subgroups, the 30-day all-cause mortality in Q4 was significantly higher compared to Q2. In addition, Q1 showed no significant difference from Q2 in patients ≤ 65 years (HR = 0.58, 95% CI 0.26–1.29), females (HR = 0.72, 95% CI 0.47–1.11) and non-hypertensive patients (HR = 0.49, 95% CI 0.21–1.16). We also conducted sensitivity analyses stratified by disease type to investigate whether the association between SHR and mortality remained consistent across different neurocritical care subtypes. The results showed that SHR was significantly associated with 30-day mortality in ICH, SAH, and AIS, whereas no significant association was observed in TBI (Supplementary Fig. 2). This may be due to the limited sample size of the TBI subgroup.

Subgroup forest plot for 30-day all-cause mortality. The adjusted covariates were consistent with Cox regression.

Incremental predictive value of SHR

We employed a combined SHR-GCS system to predict the mortality risk at different time points. The results showed that predictive performance of the combined SHR-GCS system was better than GCS alone at all time points (Fig. 5). The AUC for 30-day mortality increased from 0.798 to 0.806, for 90-day mortality increased from 0.784 to 0.791, for 180-day mortality increased from 0.773 to 0.778, and for 360-day mortality increased from 0.768 to 0.773.

Model evaluation

Table 5 presents the AUC, accuracy sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score of five models on testing set. NNET achieved the highest AUC of 0.868 and also demonstrated favorable accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score (0.802, 0.799, 0.806, 0.804). Figure 7C illustrated the ROC curves of the five models on testing set. The models ranked in descending order of AUC were NNET, RF, XgBoost, SVM and KNN. Discussion

Blood glucose management is a crucial aspect of caring for patients with neurocritical illnesses. Previous studies have demonstrated that acute stress hyperglycemia, a transient increase in blood glucose levels that occurs in response to acute stress conditions, are closely linked to an increased risk of mortality in specific patient populations20–23. Actually, in most patients with neurological critical conditions, intracranial pressure often rises rapidly after injury onset. The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, activated by intracranial injury, drives cortisol-mediated gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance7,24–26, creating a vicious cycle of hyperglycemia and neuroinflammation, a pathogenesis that particularly relevant in secondary brain injuries. This aligns with existing literature that links hyperglycemia to poor outcomes, highlighting the importance of metabolic control in the management of such patients. However, there are currently few studies focused on stress-induced hyperglycemia among patients with pan-neurological critical illnesses.

SHR is composed of blood glucose and HbA1c, reflecting the degree of stress-induced hyperglycemia. Several studies have confirmed the association between SHR and the risks of mortality and adverse outcomes in isolated neurocritically ill patients. In the current study, we conducted the first evaluation of the association between SHR and mortality across four heterogeneous neurocritical conditions: ICH, SAH, AIS, and TBI. This population had not been thoroughly studied in previous work. Our research extends previous investigations into the SHR by demonstrating its prognostic utility in patients with various neurocritical illnesses. In addition, while most prior SHR research focused on short-term mortality (e.g., ICU mortality or 30-day outcomes), our analysis demonstrates sustained associations of SHR with short-term mortality at 30-day and long-term mortality up to 360-day timepoints. This aligns with emerging evidence in acute coronary syndrome, where SHR predicts 1-year mortality via sustained metabolic dysregulation. Extending this to neurocritical populations, our findings suggest SHR reflects not only acute stress but also chronic brain injury progression, including delayed neuronal apoptosis or vascular remodeling—that impacts outcomes.

Subgroup analysis highlighted diabetes as a modifier of SHR’s prognostic value, a finding consistent with a previous observation23,27,28, where diabetic patients exhibit blunted SHR-mortality associations due to chronic hyperglycemia-induced tolerance. However, our study uniquely identifies non-diabetic patients as a high-sensitivity subgroup, underscoring the importance of baseline glycemic status in interpreting SHR. This contrasts with prior work, where diabetes often amplifies hyperglycemia-related risks and suggests neurocritical illness may represent a distinct clinical context where metabolic naivety exacerbates stress-induced damage29–31. This suggests that a one-size-fits-all approach for glycemia control in neurocritically ill patients may not be adequate, and tailored management strategies based on individual patient profiles could enhance outcomes.

To further confirm the predictive value of the SHR in the outcome of neurocritically ill patients, we analyzed the predictive strength of different parameters using a machine learning method. Closely following GCS scoring, results from the Boruta algorithm, LASSO regression, and SHAP algorithm indicated that SHR was significantly associated with the outcome, consistent with the results of Cox models. Moreover, the machine learning prediction models demonstrated good performance on the test set (all AUC > 0.79). Although the NNET model showed only a modest AUC improvement over LR (0.868 vs. 0.849), we consider this gain meaningful because NNET better captures nonlinear relationships and complex interactions in critically ill patients. Moreover, SHAP was used to improve interpretability. Nevertheless, the feasibility of clinical implementation should be further evaluated in future studies, taking into account model interpretability, implementation cost, and validation in multicenter prospective cohorts. The combination of SHR and GCS showed that SHR can enhance the predictive performance of GCS. In summary, this study demonstrates that SHR significantly influences the prognosis of critically ill patients with diverse neurological disease. Together, while earlier research identified SHR as a marker for adverse outcomes in isolated neurological diseases, our findings reveal a consistent association across diverse neurocritical etiologies.

The implications of these findings are profound for clinical practice. Recognizing SHR as a prognostic indicator could lead to more proactive management of blood glucose levels in neurocritically ill patients. Early identification of patients with elevated SHR may prompt clinicians to implement targeted interventions aimed at glycemic control, potentially improving survival rates. Moreover, the identification of SHR as a threshold marker can guide clinical decision-making. For example, an identical admission glucose of 200 mg/dL yields vastly different SHR values (1.29 vs. 2.22) in patients with HbA1c of 7% (moderate chronic hyperglycemia) versus 5% (normoglycemia), highlighting its ability to distinguish stress-induced dysregulation from baseline hyperglycemia. This nuance is critical in diabetes, where traditional glucose measurements fail to capture acute stress superimposed on chronic glycemic status. In terms of clinical application, some findings in our study should be interpreted with caution. For instance, we observed that in patients with SHR below 0.86, the risk of mortality was not influenced by SHR, suggesting that the 0.86 threshold may represent a potential inflection point in the stress-response continuum. A relatively higher SHR may reflect a more pronounced stress response and greater metabolic dysregulation, which may contribute to worse outcomes. However, the 0.86 threshold in our study was derived from a single-center retrospective cohort and should be considered a hypothesis-generating, cohort-specific finding. It is therefore premature to guide glycemic management based on this cutoff in routine clinical practice. Future prospective, multicenter studies are needed to validate this threshold and further elucidate its underlying biological significance.

The non-significant association in the small TBI subgroup may reflect limited power, but it also suggests that the prognostic value of SHR may differ by etiology. TBI is characterized by mechanical brain injury, often accompanied by diffuse axonal injury, blood–brain barrier disruption, and extensive neuroinflammation, which can drive stress hyperglycemia through catecholamine surge and insulin resistance32,33. In contrast, metabolic pathways of stroke differ depending on the subtype. Ischemic stroke is dominated by hypoxia-induced anaerobic metabolism, lactate accumulation and oxidative stress34,35, whereas hemorrhagic strokes involve hematoma-related inflammation, hemoglobin breakdown products and iron toxicity, which further exacerbate systemic inflammatory response and glucose dysregulation36,37. Thus, SHR may represent distinct pathophysiological processes in TBI versus stroke, and larger etiology-stratified and mechanistic studies are needed to confirm its predictive value across these conditions.

Although the implications of these findings are profound for clinical practice, there are several limitations should be addressed. First, the single-center, retrospective design introduces selection bias, as MIMIC-IV data exclude patients without HbA1c measurements, particularly in a significant number of patients with SAH and TBI. Second, the reliance on admission SHR neglects dynamic glycemic changes. While baseline SHR predicts outcomes, previous studies have also shown that glycemic variability (GV), reflecting glucose fluctuations during hospitalization, is associated with outcomes38–40.Third, mechanistic insights remain correlational. Future study with randomized controlled trials should focus on elucidating whether SHR-targeted glycemic control reduces mortality, addressing the mechanisms by which SHR interplay between metabolic dysregulation, inflammation, and oxidative stress in neurocritically ill patients are warranted.

Conclusion

Higher SHR is strongly associated with increased all-cause mortality across different time horizons and may serve as a valuable prognostic tool for neurocritically ill patients, especially in non-diabetic patients. The combination of SHR and GCS scoring exhibited a higher predictive value than currently GCS score alone in outcome prediction of patients with neurocritical illnesses.
