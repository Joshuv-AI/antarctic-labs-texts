Leptospirosis seroprevalence and risk factors among slaughterhouse workers in Burkina Faso
Authors: Sylvie Zida, Henri Gautier Ouédraogo, Tegwinde Rebeca Compaoré, Tani Sagna, Serge Théophile Soubeiga, Bienvenu Yalabola Banhoro, Abdou Azaque Zouré, Dinanibè Kambiré, Amadou Dicko, Elsio A. Wunder, Seni Kouanda
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Slaughterhouse workers are exposed to pathogenic Leptospira•Ten infectious serogroups have been implicated•Risk factors are bleeding station, contact with feces and urine, and agriculture

Introduction

Leptospirosis is a major zoonotic disease caused by infection with a pathogenic serovar of Leptospira spp. [1,2] with a worldwide distribution, but with a major burden in developing countries located in tropical regions [3,4]. Domestic animals harbor Leptospira in the kidneys and genital tracts where they can persist for a long period of time with intermittent shedding in urine. What constitutes the source of contamination of humans and other animals [5,6]? Transmission occurs directly through contact with the urine or body fluids of infected animals or indirectly through water or soil contaminated with urine from infected animals.

An annual incidence rate of one million cases of leptospirosis is estimated with a mortality rate of 60,000 individuals per year [4]. Occupation is a significant risk factor for humans, and direct contact with infected animals accounts for most infections in farmers, veterinarians, slaughterhouse workers, and sewer workers [7,8]. The highest risk is observed in slaughterhouse and meat industry workers, and previous studies have shown that the seroprevalence values on those workers are twice of those on other non-risk occupations [7,9,10]. Most human Leptospira infections are subclinical or mild, but severe cases may cause severe kidney, liver, and lung diseases resulting in life-threatening illness with mortality rate up to 50% [11].

Confirmation of a clinically suspected leptospirosis case in the laboratory also has many bottlenecks. The microscopic agglutination test (MAT) is the gold standard for serodiagnosis of leptospirosis but is complex and requires experienced operators [12]. Alternative methods include the indirect hemagglutination assay, rapid diagnostic test, molecular diagnostics (PCR), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). ELISA is generally recommended as a screening tool for suspect cases [13]. In sub-Saharan Africa, cases of leptospirosis are rarely diagnosed because it induces a febrile syndrome associated with non-specific symptoms also found in malaria and other common tropical diseases [2]. Other than the issues with diagnosis, one of the major challenges in accelerating awareness of the burden of leptospirosis in sub-Saharan Africa is the weakness of funds for research on this neglected disease.

Our previous study showed that 3.5% of patients with febrile jaundice in Burkina Faso were confirmed leptospirosis cases [14]. However, there are no data on the occupational risk for leptospirosis in farmers, veterinarians, and slaughterhouse workers. The objectives of this study were to determine the seroprevalence and risk factors of leptospirosis among slaughterhouse workers in Burkina Faso.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Health Research of Burkina Faso with “deliberation number: 2020-11-251”. All subjects were enrolled after obtaining their informed and written consent. Each participant was identified by an anonymous number, and the authors did not have access to information that could identify individual participants during or after collection.

Study design

We performed a cross-sectional survey among slaughterhouse workers in two major cities of Burkina Faso, Ouagadougou, and Bobo Dioulasso, between March and April 2021. The main slaughterhouses of Ouagadougou and Bobo Dioulasso were visited three times during the study. Twice before collection to obtain the administrative authorizations and to plan organizational and logistical aspects, and three days before data collection for sensitization and to explain the project objectives to the slaughterhouse workers. On the day of data collection, participants aged 16 and over were recruited after obtaining individual written informed consent. The interviewer explained the questionnaire and biological sampling procedures and highlighted that diagnostic tests would be performed on the samples. Participants were required to sign or apply a thumbprint to duplicated consent forms.

Data collection

Interviews were conducted in French and in the local language (Moore, Dioula) according to the language in which the participant was most comfortable. Questionnaire data were recorded with Census and Survey Processing System (CSPro) software. Data were collected on sociodemographic characteristics (such as age, gender, education, and number of years working); residence, personal history, and medical history for the past 3 months; occupation (occupational group, the specificity of the slaughtering work); knowledge of zoonosis; risk behaviors at the slaughterhouse (occupational practices and personal hygiene); and individual exposure factors outside the slaughterhouse (breeding, agricultural activity, and presence of a well at home). At the end of the administration of the questionnaire, each participant benefited from awareness of zoonosis and in particular leptospirosis.

Sampling procedure and laboratory analysis

A venous blood sample (5 ml) was obtained from each participant, labeled and transported, in a refrigerated cool box, to the laboratory of “Institut de Recherche en Sciences de la Santé (IRSS)” in Ouagadougou. Sera were obtained by centrifugation of the clotted samples (at 3000g for 20 minutes). The serum samples were then stored at −20°C until analysis. To determine seropositivity for leptospirosis, we performed two serological methods. The first was an ELISA test using “SERION ELISA classic Leptospira IgG/IgM”, (Institut Virion\Serion, Germany), following the recommendations by the manufacturer [15]. We also performed the microscopic agglutination test (MAT), golden standard assay for human leptospirosis, as previously described [12]. For the MAT, a panel of 32 Leptospira strains of different species and serogroups was used (S1 Table). The serological evaluation was performed using a dilution of 1:50 and 1:100. A seropositive case on MAT was determined when 50% or more leptospires for at least one serogroup strain were agglutinated at ≥ 1:50 titer. The threshold of 1:50 has been used in other studies [16], [17], [18]. This cutoff was a specific and more sensitive criteria for identifying prior infections [19]. All seropositive samples were titrated to determine the higher titer for each strain. The presumptive infecting serogroup was determined as the serogroup with the highest antibody titer. Subjects were classified as positive if they met any of the three following criteria: (1) ELISA immunoglobulin (Ig)G OD >0.788 x standard average; (2) ELISA IgM OD >0.792 x average of the standards; and/or (3) MAT ≥ 1:50.

The ELISA tests were carried out at the “Institut de Recherche en Sciences de la Santé” laboratory, and the MAT tests were carried out at Yale School of Public Health, Department of Epidemiology of Microbial Diseases.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with Stata 14 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Proportions for categorical variables were calculated. Bivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with leptospirosis serological markers. Associations were expressed as unadjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Results were considered statistically significant at the P <0.05 level. Risk factors with a P-value <0.10 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate analysis for each exposure.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics and occupational status

During our study, we enrolled a total of 172 participants in both cities. Among these participants, 66 were from Ouagadougou, and 106 were from Bobo Dioulasso slaughterhouses. The majority of participants were men (168/172, 97.7%) and the mean age was 35 years (range 16-68 years). The most represented age group was 30 to 40 years old with 31.4% (54/172). The different jobs in the slaughterhouses included slaughterers (40.1%), foreman (3.5%), offal cleaner (11.0%), slaughterhouse cleaner (4.7%), meat vendor (13.4%), livestock seller (2.9%), cattle carrier (2.3%), inspector/veterinarian (9.9%), director/administrator (5.2%), and other position (7.0%).

Discussion

This study presents a serosurvey for leptospirosis among occupationally exposed slaughterhouse workers and reports a seroprevalence of 16.3%. This high prevalence demonstrates that slaughterhouse workers constitute a professional group at higher risk for leptospirosis. The seroprevalence founded in this study was similar to those reported in other studies on slaughterhouse workers with a seroprevalence of 17.1% in Tanzania (95% CI: 7.1-32.1) [10], 14.9% in Morocco [20], 13.4% in Kenya (95% CI: 11.1-16.1) [7] and 10.41% (95% CI: 4.30-16.52) in Cameroon [21]. Furthermore, our result was predominantly higher compared to another study conducted in Burkina Faso in 2018 which saw a prevalence of 5.8% by ELISA test on a national sample of febrile jaundice cases [14]. This difference could be explained by the fact that slaughterhouse workers are more exposed to leptospirosis than the general population, but also because the prevalence found in the previous study only represents cases of severe leptospirosis.

The study population was primarily young, and the most represented age group was 30 to 40 years old (31.40%) with an average age of 35 years. Similar to a previous study conducted in 2018 [22], our analysis revealed that the age group with most seropositivity was age 21 to 30 years old, with 22.85% (8/35) of seropositive individuals, although it was not statistically significant (P = 0.95). This age group is the most active one in these slaughterhouses, which could explain the observation.

Although the seroprevalence was high, our participants were apparently healthy. This likely reflects previous infections or continued exposure to Leptospira, which potentially improves immunity. This could also be explained by the fact that antibodies (IgM and IgG) can persist for up to 6 months after infection [23].

The multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that residence, working at the bleeding station, contact with urine and feces, and the practice of agriculture outside the slaughterhouse were all risk factors associated with seropositivity for leptospirosis. Workers with contact with feces and urine were more likely to be seropositive to leptospirosis. It has been previously reported that contact with the viscera was a risk factor for leptospirosis [24,25]. Workers at the bleeding station were at increased risk of leptospirosis seropositivity compared to workers in other positions in the slaughterhouse workers. Indeed, a previous study has shown that being the first links in the slaughter chain increases the risk for exposure, because the splashes of urine and blood can be sources of contamination [26].

The high number of potential infecting serogroups of Leptopsira measured by MAT suggests a high degree of diversity of circulating strains and reservoirs. Mini, Canicola, Icterohaemorrhagiae, and Ballum have already been found in a previous human study in Burkina Faso [14]. The presence of rodents and stray dogs is common in the yards of slaughterhouses in Burkina Faso and the neighborhoods of the cities in general. Rodents and stray dogs are hosts for certain serogroups [27], therefore, leptospirosis prevention measures should take this into account in slaughterhouse sanitation. Other animals are likely to play an important role in the transmission cycle of leptospirosis but there are no data about the animal reservoirs of leptospires in Burkina Faso. A study of animal reservoirs can help us better understand the link between human cases and potential animal reservoir hosts.

Residence and the practice of agriculture are not related to the slaughterhouse and could be explained by exposure to leptospirosis in the general population, which is probably not negligible. Non-occupationally related exposures were collected in order to highlight the risk related to other risk factors related to leptospirosis exposure such as water exposure agriculture, and breeding which are common in the Burkina Faso. The fact that the practice of agriculture is linked to the seropositivity of leptospirosis was also reported in a 2021 Rwanda study where being a crop farmer was significantly associated with seropositivity for leptospirosis [28]. Further seroprevalence studies in Burkina Faso are necessary to understand the risk of infection and the epidemiology of leptospirosis in the country.

Conclusion

These findings indicate that a significant proportion of slaughterhouse workers are being exposed to pathogenic Leptospira. Public-health interventions against leptospirosis will need to target this occupational group. Risk factors for leptospirosis positivity included residence, working at the bleeding station, contact with urine and feces, and practice of agriculture outside the slaughterhouse. Slaughterhouse workers should take precautions during bleeding process, and the working environment of slaughterhouses might need to be improved by providing information about the disease and proper personal protective equipments for the workers. We intend to use this pilot study results as a baseline for studies in other occupationally exposed groups of leptospirosis with a “One Health” approach.
