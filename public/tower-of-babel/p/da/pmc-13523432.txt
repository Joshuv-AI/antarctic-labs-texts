Endovascular Management of Interlobolar Bronchopulmonary Arterial Fistula After Bronchial Stent Removal
Authors: Anand Mulji, Michael Sagatelian, Madeline Leo, Christopher Hannegan, Antony Gayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBronchopulmonary arterial fistulas are rare, life-threatening causes of hemoptysis, often occurring in patients with thoracic malignancy, radiation exposure, or repeated airway instrumentation.Case SummaryA 36-year-old woman with metastatic epithelioid sarcoma and radiation-induced bronchus intermedius stenosis underwent multiple bronchoscopic dilations and airway stent placements. Following bronchial stent removal, she developed massive airway hemorrhage. Computed tomography angiography demonstrated bronchopulmonary arterial fistula. Given prohibitive surgical risk, endovascular exclusion with a covered pulmonary arterial stent achieved immediate hemostasis. Recurrent hemoptysis due to incomplete stent apposition was successfully treated with repeat angioplasty. The patient remained clinically stable on follow-up.DiscussionSuccessful endovascular management of bronchovascular fistulas offers effective hemorrhage control while preserving pulmonary perfusion in nonsurgical candidates.Take-Home MessagesEndovascular therapy can serve as a salvage option for bronchopulmonary arterial fistulas in high-risk patients. Multidisciplinary coordination, postprocedural surveillance, and lifelong prophylaxis are essential for durable outcomes.Key words: complication, computed tomography, hemodynamics, hemorrhage, hemostasis, hybrid imaging, pulmonary circulation, stents, thoracic, treatment

History of Presentation

A 36-year-old woman with metastatic epithelioid sarcoma presented with worsening cough. She had a history of progressive airway symptoms requiring repeated bronchoscopic interventions. Flexible bronchoscopy demonstrated severe airway distortion with approximately 50% circumferential stenosis proximal to a bronchus intermedius stent, marked mucosal abnormalities at the level of the stent, and approximately 90% circumferential stenosis distal to the stent. Following stent removal, she developed brisk, nonlocalizable hemorrhage from the right mainstem bronchus requiring emergent airway control with endobronchial blockers.

Investigations/Management

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the chest demonstrated irregularity of the right main bronchus and adjacent pulmonary artery with bronchopulmonary arterial fistula (BPAF). Thoracic surgery was consulted, and surgical intervention was deferred due to prohibitive operative risk. Vascular and interventional radiology (VIR) was consulted for endovascular options (Figure 1).

Computed Tomography Angiography Chest (Left to Right): Axial, Coronal, and Sagittal Images Demonstrating Focal Irregularity of the Posterior Wall of the Right Mainstem Bronchus With Gas Extending to the Right Main Pulmonary Artery

A 6F sheath was placed in the right common femoral vein and upsized to an 8F × 50 cm destination sheath (Terumo Corporation). The pulmonary arterial system was catheterized using a 5F pigtail catheter (Terumo Corporation), stiff Glidewire (Terumo Corporation), Amplatz wire (Boston Scientific), 5F KMP catheter (Cook Medical), and marking pigtail catheter (AngioDynamics). Right pulmonary artery angiography demonstrated a pseudoaneurysm arising from the superior wall of the interlobar pulmonary artery with an adjacent filling defect corresponding to the site of injury identified on prior CTA. An 11 mm × 79 mm covered stent (GORE VBX, W. L. Gore & Associates) was deployed within the interlobar pulmonary artery, extending into the right lower lobe (common basal trunk) pulmonary artery to fully cover the defect. The proximal aspect of the stent was postdilated using a 16-mm balloon (Atlas Gold, Becton, Dickinson and Company). Angiography demonstrated occlusion of the middle-lobe pulmonary arteries with preserved perfusion to the upper and lower lobes. Following intraprocedural endobronchial deflation performed by pulmonology, repeat pulmonary angiography demonstrated flow around the proximal aspect of the stent without pseudoaneurysm or active extravasation. Flow around the proximal aspect of the stent was expected, with the intention of maintaining perfusion to proximal upper and lower lobar branches. Concurrent direct bronchoscopy revealed no active bleeding (Figure 2).

Covered stent deployment in the interlobar pulmonary artery extending to the right lower lobe common basal trunk with coverage of the defect and right middle lobe pulmonary artery and subsequent conventional angioplasty of the central aspect of the stent. Endobronchial blocker deflation without evidence of active extravasation and preserved flow of the upper and lower lobes.

Hemoglobin levels remained stable throughout the remainder of the hospitalization, with no further episodes of airway bleeding or additional interventions. Upon discharge, a 1-month follow-up was scheduled with VIR with a concurrent CTA of the chest to be performed to assess for stent patency. Antiplatelet therapy was not recommended at the time given presenting large volume hemoptysis.

She presented to the emergency department on postprocedural day 23 with small-volume hemoptysis, productive cough, and fevers/chills. CTA of the chest demonstrated persistent communication between the right mainstem bronchus and pulmonary artery with a tiny focus of gas noted in the pulmonary artery along the superior central portion of the stent and incomplete apposition of the central portion of the stent with continued filling of the right upper lobe pulmonary artery, compatible with a type 1a endoleak. VIR was consulted for endovascular options (Figure 3).

Computed Tomography Angiography Chest (Left to Right): Sagittal Image Demonstrating Tiny Focus of Gas Along the Superior Central Portion of the Stent

Coronal and axial images demonstrating incomplete apposition of the central portion of stent with the vessel walls.

A 6F sheath was placed in the right common femoral vein and upsized to an 8F × 50 cm destination sheath. The pulmonary arterial system was catheterized using a 6F × 100-cm angled pigtail catheter (Terumo Corporation), 100-cm Bernstein catheter (Merit Medical Systems), super stiff Amplatz wire, and stiff Glidewire. Main pulmonary arterial pressures were measured via a multisidehole catheter and sheath which demonstrated a systolic pressure of 30 mm Hg, diastolic pressure 13 mm Hg, and mean pressure 19 mm Hg. Right pulmonary angiography demonstrated brisk antegrade flow with opacification of the right lower pulmonary branches. Conventional angioplasty of the central and midportion of the covered stent was performed with 14 × 20 mm and 16 × 20 mm balloons (Atlas Gold, Becton, Dickinson and Company). There was improved apposition of the central portion of the stent to the vessel wall and improved opacification of the right lower pulmonary artery. Postdilation pressures of the main pulmonary artery demonstrated a systolic pressure of 38 mm Hg, diastolic pressure 18 mm Hg, and mean pressure 25 mm Hg (Figure 4).

Outcome and Follow-Up

Hemoglobin levels remained stable throughout the remainder of the hospitalization with no further episodes of airway bleeding or additional interventions. In the setting of BPAF, VIR recommended prolonged—potentially lifelong—antimicrobial therapy in accordance with ID guidance. A central venous catheter was placed on postprocedural day 7, and the patient was discharged the same day with a plan to complete a 4-week course of intravenous nafcillin. Follow-up CTA of the chest on postprocedural day 20 demonstrated nonocclusive thrombus within the central portion of the pulmonary artery stent, with >50% luminal stenosis. In response, antiplatelet therapy with low-dose Aspirin (81 mg daily) was initiated (Figure 5).

Axial image demonstrating findings of possible right-heart strain with mild flattening of the interventricular septum.

The patient remained asymptomatic, with no episodes of hemoptysis, chest pain, or dyspnea. She was subsequently transitioned to oral amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (Augmentin) for indefinite therapy, with outpatient follow-up using ID. CTA of the chest obtained on postprocedural day 85 demonstrated widely patent stent inflow and outflow, with interval reduction in nonocclusive thrombus and no evidence of pseudoaneurysm or recurrent fistula. Surveillance imaging at 6 and 12 months and outpatient clinic evaluations are scheduled, with plans for lifelong surveillance per VIR discretion.

Discussion

Bronchovascular fistulas are rare but potentially fatal, often presenting as massive hemoptysis in patients with prior thoracic surgery, malignancy, radiation, or airway instrumentation. In this case, repeated bronchoscopic interventions for radiation-induced bronchial stenosis culminated in acute hemorrhage and identification of a pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysm. Given prohibitive surgical risk, a covered stent was deployed endovascularly to exclude the defect achieving immediate hemostasis while preserving branch perfusion, highlighting the role of salvage endovascular treatment in high-risk patients.

The literature describes multiple endovascular strategies. One report detailed a 62-year-old man with squamous cell carcinoma presenting with massive hemoptysis due to a pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysm eroding into the bronchus. Coil embolization successfully occluded the fistula, with concurrent bronchoscopy confirming hemostasis and airway patency.1 Another case involved a 51-year-old man with a pulmonary artery–bronchial stump fistula following lobectomy, successfully treated with a covered stent graft, avoiding emergent thoracotomy.2 Similarly, a 55-year-old man with advanced lung cancer developed BPAF from endobronchial stent erosion; after failed bronchoscopic interventions, endovascular stent placement achieved durable hemostasis and clinical stability.3

Endovascular approaches can offer rapid hemorrhage control through minimally invasive access while preserving distal pulmonary perfusion—critical in patients with limited reserve. Covered stents provide immediate fistula exclusion, whereas coil embolization can selectively treat pseudoaneurysms or feeding vessels. Procedures can be performed under moderate sedation or local anesthesia, improving safety in critically ill patients, while real-time imaging enhances precision and reduces complications.1,2 These case reports suggest expanding roles for endovascular interventions. Innovations may include flow-diverting stents tailored to pulmonary vasculature, biodegradable embolic materials, or 3D imaging-guided preprocedural planning to optimize device selection and coverage.

It is also important to recognize that endovascular management carries notable limitations that must be carefully considered. Infections involving vascular grafts or endovascular prostheses are rarely eradicated without device explantation. In circumstances where prosthetic material must be retained within an infected field, lifelong suppressive antimicrobial therapy is frequently recommended, particularly in patients with an anticipated survival exceeding 6 months.4 In this case, the indwelling stent may act as a persistent nidus for infection, precluding definitive source control and necessitating lifelong antimicrobial suppression. Pulmonary arterial stenting is fundamentally a palliative/temporizing measure to address the fistula site. While literature is sparse regarding BPAF and endovascular management, there are analogous reviews. A review of 67 patients from 32 separate reports of aortobronchial fistula treated with thoracic endovascular aortic repair concluded a 30-day mortality rate of 1.5%, with 9% fistula recurrence reported, highlighting the risk of fistula recurrence, especially in cases where the fistula or cause thereof is not specifically addressed.5

Conclusions

BPAF is a rare but life-threatening complication that can occur in patients with prior thoracic radiation and repeated airway instrumentation. Endovascular covered stent placement provided effective hemorrhage control and preserved pulmonary perfusion in a high-risk surgical candidate.

Take-Home Messages

•Endovascular interventions, alone or in combination, offer a life-saving and minimally invasive option for the management of bronchopulmonary arterial fistulas, especially in high-risk patients who may be poor surgical candidates.
