Ultrafiltration–sodium profiles and their impact on thirst and xerostomia in hemodialysis patients
Authors: Kheizaran Miri, Morteza Arasnezhad, Amir Hoseein Mirzae, Alireza Sardashti, Mohammad Namazinia, Seyyed Reza Mazloum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic kidney disease affects 10–15% of the global population, with hemodialysis causing significant thirst and xerostomia due to fluid restrictions. These symptoms impair quality of life and treatment adherence. While ultrafiltration (UF) and sodium profiling have been studied separately, their combined effect remains understudied, particularly in Middle Eastern populations. We conducted a randomized crossover trial with 20 ESRD patients comparing: (1) ascending/descending UF with linear sodium reduction (150→138 mmol/L) versus (2) conventional HD with constant UF/sodium (140 mmol/L). Participants completed 16 sessions per protocol with washout periods. Outcomes were measured using validated Thirst Questionnaire (5-item) and Xerostomia Inventory (11-item), both showing excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α > 0.7/>0.8). The intervention group showed significant improvements versus conventional HD: 44% reduction in thirst scores (36.5 vs. 64.5, p < 0.001). 22% lower xerostomia (19.0 vs. 24.4, p = 0.002). 73% fewer hypotension episodes (15% vs. 55%, p < 0.05). These benefits are mechanistically linked to improved hemodynamic stability achieved through synergistic sodium and ultrafiltration profiling. This approach, which maintains plasma osmolality and intravascular volume, offers a practical, cost-neutral solution for symptom management. This approach significantly ameliorates patient-reported thirst and xerostomia and reduces intradialytic hypotension, offering a practical, cost-neutral strategy to enhance treatment tolerability. This approach should be adopted in routine practice, particularly for the growing ESRD population. Future studies should explore personalized strategies and long-term effects.Trial registration: This study was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (no. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a growing long-term condition that affects about 2 to 3% of the world’s population1. The kidneys play a central role in controlling water balance, and the function that is impaired in chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects 10 to 15% of the population worldwide2. The number of hemodialysis (HD) patients in Iran has increased significantly over the years, from 945 patients in 1997 to 30,882 in 20173. HD is the most common treatment for CKD and relies on the principles of diffusion and ultrafiltration (UF)4. Dialysis machines help filter waste products, excess fluid, and toxins from the blood of people with kidney dysfunction5. During UF, fluids are typically removed from the extracellular space (ECF), allowing patients to reach their dry weight and reduce total plasma volume6. But on the other hand, Fluid restriction causes patients with HD to experience dry mouth and increased thirst7. However, it is critical to understand that the primary physiological drivers of this symptom are the resulting disturbances in sodium balance and intravascular volume status. Fluid restriction leads to increased plasma osmolality (often due to sodium accumulation) and hypovolemia, which are the direct stimuli for the hypothalamic thirst centers8.

Thirst distress is defined as the subjective perception of dry mouth and the desire to consume fluids9. While thirst is a vital homeostatic mechanism for regulating body fluids, it can be caused by fluid deficiency, increased extracellular fluid concentration, or hormonal changes10,11. In patients undergoing hemodialysis (HD), thirst is one of the most common symptoms, experienced by 68 to 86% of patients, and has a significant impact on reducing quality of life and causing discomfort12. Because these patients require a fluid-restricted diet, excessive fluid intake leads to interdialysis weight gain (IDWG) and dangerous complications such as hypertension, pulmonary edema, heart failure, and death13,14. These restrictions, along with increased plasma osmolality due to sodium retention during the interdialysis period, exacerbate thirst14. Furthermore, in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), impaired urination and salt intake associated with TD can cause dry mouth, fatigue, and decreased energy, which interact to form a cycle of negative physical and psychological consequences15,16. The quality of life of these patients is severely affected due to constant thirst and the prohibition of drinking fluids, such that high levels of thirst are associated with a significant decrease in quality of life17. A deep understanding of these mechanisms is essential for designing thirst management strategies and improving clinical outcomes. Recent studies have supported the combination of UF and sodium profiles to reduce multiple complications of HD1819,. For example, in a study by Borzo et al., a linear UF-sodium profile improved BP in HD patients (2015)20. It is important to note that this study constitutes a secondary analysis of a broader clinical trial. While the patient population and intervention protocols are identical to those previously reported in our work focusing on hemodynamic outcomes21, the present research addresses a fundamentally different primary endpoint: patient-reported thirst and xerostomia. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to investigate the effect of combining an Ascending-Descending Ultrafiltration (A/D-UF) profile with a linear Descending Sodium profile on thirst and xerostomia in hemodialysis patients.

Methods

The research was conducted in the hemodialysis center of the 9-day Hospital in Torbat-e-heydarieh, Iran. This was a clinical trial that used a crossover method between December 2022 and June 2023 (Fig. 1).

Participants

Inclusion criteria were an age range of 18–75, being in the final stage of renal disease (ESRD), experiencing HD for 6 months, experiencing thirst in HD during the previous month; most importantly, they shouldn’t have had any disorder that causes fake thirst, such as diabetes. The exclusion criteria were: not meeting the inclusion criteria or inability to participate in the process due to unstable condition or dissatisfaction with attending the research further.

Intervention

The study employed a crossover design with two distinct hemodialysis (HD) protocols administered to both study groups. Each participant completed 16 HD sessions per protocol, with four wash-out sessions separating the two treatment phases. The experimental protocols consisted of: (a) an interventional approach featuring linear dialysate sodium concentration combined with ascending/descending (A/D) ultrafiltration (UF) profiling, and (b) a conventional HD protocol maintaining constant sodium levels and UF rates throughout treatment.

The A/D-UF profiling protocol spanned four hours and incorporated three distinct phases: ascending, intermediate, and descending. The UF process was carefully staged across ten sequential steps using B BRAUN Dialog plus dialysis equipment (Profile 1). The ascending phase removed 25.5% of the target fluid volume at reduced UF rates, followed by an intensive intermediate phase eliminating 51.2% of fluid at maximum UF rates, concluding with a gradual descending phase removing the remaining 23.6% at lowered UF rates (Fig. 2) (Table 1). Concurrently, the linear sodium protocol initiated at 150 mmol/L and progressively decreased to 138 mmol/L over ten 24-minute intervals (Fig. 3) (Table 1).

To evaluate the effects of these two protocols, patients’ thirst and xerostomia symptoms were assessed systematically during each dialysis session using validated questionnaires, as described in the Outcomes section.

Blinding

Due to the technical nature of the intervention, it was not possible to blind patients or dialysis staff to the treatment protocol. However, to ensure objective assessment, the research staff who collected all outcome data (questionnaires) were blinded to the group allocation. Additionally, the statistician remained blinded until all analyses were completed.

Outcomes

Data were collected using a two-part questionnaire assessing demographic characteristics (age, gender, education, income, residence, first hemodialysis date, and vascular access) and dialysis-related symptoms.

Thirst and xerostomia symptoms were evaluated using validated questionnaires at six different time points within each dialysis session: (1) immediately before the start of dialysis, (2) during the ascending UF phase, (3) during the descending UF phase, (4) immediately after the end of dialysis, (5) during the daytime after dialysis, and (6) at night after dialysis. Measuring symptoms at multiple time points enabled us to capture their dynamic course across different treatment phases and recovery periods, which is essential given the rapid intradialytic and postdialytic changes in plasma osmolality and intravascular volume. For symptom assessment, two validated tools were employed: a 5-item Thirst Questionnaire (Likert scale 1–5; total score 5–25) and a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for thirst (0–100). Both demonstrated strong validity (content validity verified by nephrology specialists) and reliability (Cronbach’s α > 0.7) 21.

Dry mouth symptoms were evaluated using an 11-item Likert-scale questionnaire (1–5; total score 11–55). This instrument showed excellent psychometric properties, including content validity (expert-verified), reliability (Cronbach’s α > 0.8), construct validity (confirmatory factor analysis), and convergent validity with clinical indicators22,23. These well-validated tools provide reliable measurement of dialysis-related symptoms suitable for both clinical practice and research applications. Intradialytic hypotension (IDH) was defined as a symptomatic drop in blood pressure requiring nursing intervention. Specifically, an episode was recorded if a patient experienced a decrease in systolic blood pressure by ≥ 20 mmHg or a decrease in mean arterial pressure (MAP) by ≥ 10 mmHg from their pre-dialysis baseline, accompanied by symptoms such as dizziness, nausea, or cramping, which necessitated an intervention (e.g., Trendelenburg positioning, reduction of ultrafiltration rate, or normal saline infusion). The number of IDH episodes per session was recorded for each patient24.

Hemodynamic and biochemical outcomes as mechanistic support

The implementation of the combined Ascending-Descending Ultrafiltration (A/D-UF) and linear descending sodium profile led to a significant improvement in hemodynamic tolerance (Table 1). To provide context for the primary outcomes (thirst and xerostomia) and elucidate the potential mechanisms behind this improvement, key supporting parameters were rigorously monitored. These included symptomatic intradialytic hypotension (IDH) episodes, ultrafiltration profiles, sodium kinetics, and volume status indicators. To ensure the accuracy of the study, the B BRAUN Dialog plus Dialysis Machine (Germany) was used for all samples. The dialysis solution was a sodium bicarbonate buffer, and its temperature was set at 37 °C for all patients. The blood flow rate was between 200 and 350 ml/min, and the dialysis fluid flow rate was set at 500 ml/min.

Sample size and randomization

A priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1.9.7) for repeated-measures ANOVA indicated that 20 participants would provide 80% power (α = 0.05, effect size f = 0.25) to detect differences in thirst scores, accounting for the crossover design’s efficiency. Of 62 screened patients, 42 were excluded due to diabetes (n ≈ 25), short dialysis vintage (n ≈ 8), lack of thirst symptoms (n ≈ 5), or declined participation (n ≈ 4), as detailed in the CONSORT flow diagram (Fig. 1). The remaining 20 participants were randomized into two sequence groups using an online tool (http://randomization.com). All participants completed all 32 sessions (16 per protocol plus washout) with no attrition.

Statistical methods

To minimize bias in data analysis, the statistician was blinded to the group allocations until all analyses were completed. Data was entered into IBM SPSS Statistics 20. Normality of the data was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Based on the results, repeated-measures ANOVA was used to analyze the effects of the intervention over time, and paired t-tests were employed for within-group comparisons, while independent t-tests were used for between-group comparisons. A confidence interval of 95% and a significance level of 0.05 were considered. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, median, or frequency.

Results

Patient characteristics and study flow

A total of 20 patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis were enrolled and completed this randomized crossover study. The cohort consisted of 12 men (60%) and 8 women (40%), with a mean age of 58.00 ± 14.54 years. The average time on hemodialysis was 54.55 ± 34.77 months. The majority of patients were treated with High-Flux Hemodialysis (85%, n = 17), and vascular access was primarily via a fistula (70%, n = 14). The most common cause of end-stage renal disease was diabetes (40%, n = 8). Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 3. Each patient completed 16 HD sessions (8 per study phase), resulting in a total of 320 sessions available for analysis.

Thirst and xerostomia outcomes

The combined A/D-UF and sodium profiling strategy had a profound and significant impact on patient-reported symptoms of thirst and dry mouth. At baseline, there was no statistically significant difference in the mean thirst scores between the two study phases (27.9 ± 12.5 vs. 36.4 ± 17.3; P = 0.085).

Following the intervention, the mean thirst score was significantly lower in the A/D profile phase (36.5 ± 11.4) compared to the routine treatment phase (64.5 ± 10.8; P < 0.001), as detailed in Table 4. A detailed analysis of individual thirst-related items revealed statistically significant improvements in the intervention group for sensations ‘before dialysis’ (1.5 ± 0.62 vs. 2.4 ± 0.58; P < 0.001), ‘during dialysis’ (1.6 ± 0.57 vs. 2.9 ± 0.50; P < 0.001), and ‘after dialysis’ (1.9 ± 0.50 vs. 2.9 ± 0.50; P < 0.001). No significant difference was found for the item ‘during the day’ (P = 0.712). The total thirst score was consequently significantly lower in the A/D profile phase (8.9 ± 1.8 vs. 12.9 ± 1.9; P < 0.001). Similarly, xerostomia scores were significantly improved. The intervention group reported a lower total xerostomia score (19.0 ± 4.8) compared to the control phase (24.4 ± 5.3; P = 0.002) (Table 4 , Fig. 4).

Discussion

The principal finding of this randomized crossover study is that the combination of an ascending-descending (A/D) ultrafiltration profile with a descending sodium dialysate profile significantly reduced thirst and xerostomia in hemodialysis patients compared to conventional fixed-profile treatment. Our previous analysis of this cohort demonstrated that the combined A/D ultrafiltration and descending sodium protocol effectively controlled blood pressure and reduced intradialytic hypotension21. The present study extends these findings, showing that this intervention also addresses a critical aspect of patient well-being by alleviating patient-reported thirst and xerostomia. Post-dialysis serum sodium levels were significantly higher in the intervention group (140.3 ± 2.2 vs. 138.5 ± 2.2 mEq/L, p < 0.001), supporting the notion that linear descending sodium with A/D ultrafiltration prevents abrupt plasma osmolality decline, reducing osmotic drive for thirst. Although IDWG did not differ significantly (2.7 ± 1.3 vs. 3.1 ± 0.9 kg), the downward trend in the intervention group aligns with self-reported symptom reduction. Longer-term studies with serial IDWG measurements are recommended to corroborate these findings.

The observed improvements can be mechanistically explained by the synergistic effect of sodium and ultrafiltration profiling on hemodynamic stability and plasma osmolality. Thirst in dialysis patients is primarily triggered by osmotic and volume changes. Rapid fluid removal can lead to increased plasma osmolality, stimulating thirst mechanisms15. The linear sodium reduction protocol (150→138 mmol/L) mitigated the rapid decline in plasma osmolality typically seen during conventional hemodialysis. By initiating dialysis with a higher sodium concentration, intravascular volume was better preserved, reducing osmotic stimuli for thirst and decreasing the risk of intradialytic hypotension. This effect was reinforced by the A/D ultrafiltration profile, which synchronizes fluid removal with plasma refilling, further enhancing cardiovascular stability18. Gradual changes in ultrafiltration, with an ascending phase followed by constant and descending phases, likely counterbalanced the solitary osmotic effects of sodium21. Compared to low-sodium profiles, high-sodium profiles were found to increase both maintenance and vascular refilling volume, thereby mitigating the risk of a blood volume drop during dialysis treatments25. It has to be noticed, if sodium concentration drops suddenly and with a large slope, it could cause other complications like seizures, which is might be one of the reasons that sodium concentration in this model can not be declined excessively26. Future research may further clarify these mechanisms.

On the other hand, understanding thirst regulation requires differentiating the contributions of osmoreceptors and baroreceptors. Although higher dialysate sodium increases sodium load, our data show that post-dialysis plasma sodium remained between 138 and 140 mEq/L, below the 140 mEq/L threshold for osmoreceptor activation27. This indicates that the intervention did not significantly trigger osmoreceptor-mediated thirst. Instead, baroreceptor-mediated mechanisms appear to dominate, particularly in patients with baseline fluid overload and low blood pressure. While higher sodium concentrations in dialysate are linked to increased thirst, individual responses can vary, highlighting the need for personalized sodium management in dialysis patients. However, some studies suggest that low sodium concentrations may not always effectively reduce thirst, indicating a complex relationship between sodium levels and thirst perception28. Interestingly, it has been reported that low-sodium dialysis can reduce fluid intake but may paradoxically increase thirst immediately after dialysis sessions29.

In conventional ultrafiltration, rapid intravascular volume contraction activates arterial baroreceptors and cardiopulmonary stretch receptors, stimulating the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) and ADH release, which intensifies thirst30–33. By combining sodium profiling with ascending-descending ultrafiltration, intravascular volume was preserved during the early dialysis phase, preventing abrupt hypovolemia, reducing baroreceptor activation, and limiting downstream hormonal signaling. Clinically, this translated into fewer patient-reported thirst episodes and improved xerostomia scores, especially among those with hypotension or volume overload at baseline. It has to be added that in our prior study about the effect of same setting, the hypotension in the intervention group occurred less than control group21. This signifies that RASS and baroreceptor mechanisms had been triggered less in the referred group.

Looking from another perspective, thirst in chronic hemodialysis is categorized as osmotic, natraemic, or volemic15. Osmotic and natraemic thirst arise from increased extracellular sodium or osmolality, whereas volemic thirst results from decreased extracellular fluid volume30–33. Rapid fluid removal during dialysis exacerbates thirst through volumetric and osmotic mechanisms34. Sodium and ultrafiltration are interdependent: high sodium shifts fluid from cells to vessels, improving ultrafiltration efficiency35. The combination of both strategies in our study likely represents an optimal approach for thirst control. Notably, since fluid volume decreases in ascending ultrafiltration and increases during descending one, the thirst feeling will be changed accordingly. On the other hand, in descending ultrafiltration, the concentration of sodium would be lower than in the ascending state, which drives the patient to have less thirst in that stage overall. So for future research suggestions, thirst and distress can be investigated during various stages, and it can be demonstrated if the changes are noticeable in the mentioned stages or not. In other words, it can be investigated if lower thirst distress in this type of dialysis setting during one session is stable or not.

There are many types of sodium profiles, including decreasing, increasing, and alternating. Decreasing itself is divided into stepwise, linear, and exponential profiles, and stepwise sodium profiling appears more effective in reducing intradialytic hypotension compared to linear methods. It necessitates investigating types of sodium profile and their effects on the signs and symptoms of patients. Since muscle cramps, dizziness, and lightheadedness have been claimed to vanish during a sodium profile, it would improve the authenticity of our research if we could measure them simultaneously. Even for ultrafiltration, some other methods exist, which implies the complicated impact of these settings18.

Other ultrafiltration methods and ion dynamics, such as potassium, also influence outcomes36. The findings of our research indicate there is less thirst at night in comparison with day in both the control and intervention groups. This is probably happening because of circadian thirst. In this definition, during the night, since a human being loses a bunch of water through sweating and urination, they tend to drink water excessively before sleep, which makes them less thirsty during the night, Align with this, Bots et al. reported significantly less thirst feeling during the night than the day, which was 47.8 and 29.3, respectively, for 94 dialysis clients15,37. Moreover, results imply that we have more thirst after dialysis. After dialysis, patients may experience hypovolemia, which can trigger thirst due to increased plasma angiotensin II levels38.

In our study, we used a questionnaire that subjectively measured thirst feeling, but there is an objective criterion, which is interdialytic weight gain (IDWG). When a patient becomes excessively thirsty, he drinks a lot of water during dialysis sessions, which increases his weight15. Therefore, for more confirmation, it is being proposed to consider this ruler for another research. Subjective questionnaires, such as the Dialysis Thirst Inventory, were supplemented by objective IDWG measurements, acknowledging that elderly patients may underreport thirst39. Salt taste dysfunction, prevalent among diabetic patients (≈ 58%) 15,36, may further impair thirst perception, with diabetic and heart failure patients reporting higher thirst scores40,41. Patients over the age of 65 demonstrated significantly lower thirst scores on the Dialysis Thirst Inventory (DTI)37. This evidence implies that it would be better to use IDWG for aged people. Another potential factor contributing to thirst is a dysfunction in salt taste perception, which may impair the ability to accurately detect salty flavors for instance, Tanaka et al. reported that the prevalence of salt taste dysfunction was 58%1542,. Patients with diabetes frequently report symptoms related to hyperglycemia, including excessive thirst, xerostomia, a sweet or unusual taste, and generalized weakness. Notably, the Dialysis Thirst Inventory (DTI) scores are significantly higher among diabetic hemodialysis patients compared to their non-diabetic counterparts40,43. Thirst is a frequently reported symptom among patients with heart failure, either41.

Xerostomia, closely linked to thirst and IDWG44 It was lower in the intervention group. Xerostomia risk is heightened by polypharmacy. Most xerogenic drugs exert their effects through anticholinergic activity on muscarinic M3 receptors or through centrally mediated mechanisms that inhibit fluid secretion by acting on brain regulatory centers45. Medications known to reduce salivary function—such as antihypertensives, diuretics, antihistamines, and antidepressants—are classified as potentially xerogenic agents46. Body mass index may also influence thirst47.

Overall, these confounding variables can cause bias in every study; therefore, it is recommended to control these intervening factors to get a good result in every research. Controlling all of these might be impossible, but by considering the main factors associated with thirst, conducting good research is possible and needed.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. The sample size was small and from a single center, potentially limiting generalizability. The short-term follow-up restricts assessment of long-term efficacy and sustainability. Blinding of patients and providers was not feasible due to the visible nature of the intervention, though assessors and statisticians were blinded. While frequent symptom measurements captured acute changes, they may not reflect full interdialytic burden and could be subject to recall bias. Importantly, we did not measure interdialytic weight gain (IDWG) after treatment sessions, so we cannot confirm whether reduced thirst translated to improved fluid adherence. Future larger, longer-term studies with multi-center recruitment and serial IDWG measurements are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusion

The new settings of modern dialysis devices might help clients cope with strenuous conditions and reduce the morbidity of the disease. Therefore, organizations should invest in these types of devices to improve the quality of life of the clients.
