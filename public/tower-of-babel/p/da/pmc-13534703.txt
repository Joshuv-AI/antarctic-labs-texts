Federated TinyML and digital twin framework for secure and resilient IoMT-based ICU monitoring
Authors: Umar Hayat Khan, Rahim Khan, Tahani Alsaedi, Samia Allaoua Chelloug, Fahad Alturise, Salem Alkhalaf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Resource-constrained medical sensing devices are increasingly expected to support local intelligence, privacy-preserving collaboration, and secure communication in Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) environments. However, deploying federated learning in ICU monitoring remains challenging because hospital data are often non-IID, model updates may be adversarially poisoned, and emerging quantum-security threats require stronger communication protection. This paper presents a federated TinyML framework with an edge-hosted patient-state Digital Twin layer for ICU monitoring. The Digital Twin component is implemented as a lightweight patient-state representation rather than a full physiological simulator. It maintains recent physiological observations, temporal risk trends, predicted status labels, and interpretable alert information at the hospital gateway. Patient-specific adaptation is therefore achieved through individualized temporal state tracking and risk-history synchronization, while the predictive model is collaboratively learned across hospitals. The suggested framework employs decision-tree ensembles due to their lightweight nature, efficient inference capabilities, and interpretability inherent to ESP32-class devices. As decision tree structures are not amenable to average aggregation like neural network parameters, the aggregation of local client models is done via the validation-based ensemble fusion. In order to enhance the resistance of the framework to attacks based on poisoning clients, the Performance-Based Filtering (PBF) method analyzes the performance of each local ensemble against a trusted validation set and excludes low-quality local ensembles from the subsequent global ensemble aggregation. The secure model exchange process utilizes ML-KEM-512 key encapsulation scheme in combination with AES-256-GCM authenticated encryption. The extra overhead of the crypto layer is 0.09 ms per update exchange. The framework is evaluated using a clinician-reviewed synthetic ICU monitoring dataset distributed across three non-IID hospital clients and externally benchmarked using a PhysioNet-based critical-care dataset constructed from shared physiological features. The prediction task uses five status categories: normal, mild, moderate, critical, and an outlier/anomaly category; these labels represent synthetic severity-status classes rather than prospectively validated ICU endpoints. Under a targeted label-flipping attack in which one client relabels critical cases as normal, the proposed PBF strategy achieves 86.79% accuracy, 0.782 macro-F1, 0.904 critical-class recall, 0.837 critical-class F1-score, 0.947 macro-AUROC, and 0.799 macro-AUPRC on the synthetic ICU evaluation. External benchmarking at \varepsilon = 2.0 achieves 98.04% accuracy, 0.959 macro-F1, 0.977 critical-class recall, 0.987 critical-class F1-score, 0.999 macro-AUROC, and 0.983 macro-AUPRC. Comparative evaluation against FedAvg, Krum, Trimmed Mean, and Coordinate Median shows that PBF provides stronger protection of critical-class recall under adversarial conditions, although full prospective clinical validation remains outside the scope of this study. Overall, the results indicate that a federated TinyML architecture with lightweight patient-state tracking, validation-based ensemble filtering, differential privacy, and post-quantum-secure communication can support privacy-aware and attack-resilient ICU monitoring experiments in resource-constrained IoMT settings. The study should be interpreted as a technical feasibility and robustness evaluation rather than a fully integrated clinical Digital Twin deployment.

Introduction

The physiological condition of patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) can become critical within a short span of time. Recognition of respiratory, hemodynamic, or sepsis deterioration, and other critical cases needs to happen early in order to provide medical help promptly. Traditional cloud-based AI systems may not be appropriate to use as the sole decision support layer in such environments due to the possibility of generating latency, dependency on bandwidth, risk of exposing privacy, and creating a single point of failure1. Edge computing takes care of one of the limitations by performing inference near the edge. Simultaneously, TinyML has made it possible to perform inference using lightweight models even on small microcontrollers like ESP322,3.

Federated learning (FL) is also attractive for multi-hospital healthcare systems because it allows participating institutions to collaborate without directly sharing raw patient records. However, applying FL to ICU-oriented Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) environments creates several technical challenges. First, model updates can still leak information about local data through membership inference or reconstruction attacks, making additional privacy protection necessary4–6. Second, differential privacy may reduce predictive performance, especially under non-IID hospital data distributions. Third, secure communication must account for future quantum threats because many existing IoMT systems still rely on classical public-key cryptography such as RSA or ECC, which may be vulnerable to large-scale quantum attacks7. NIST-standardized ML-KEM provides a lattice-based post-quantum key encapsulation mechanism, but its integration into low-latency federated healthcare pipelines remains insufficiently studied8.

Data heterogeneity further complicates federated ICU monitoring. Hospitals may differ in patient demographics, disease prevalence, sensor calibration, admission policies, clinical workflows, and monitoring frequency. Consequently, ICU datasets are usually non-IID across institutions. Figure 1 provides a generalized illustration of federated learning in IoMT. In such settings, Byzantine or malicious clients may intentionally poison local labels or model contributions to reduce the reliability of the global model. FedAvg remains a widely used baseline for federated learning9, but it can be vulnerable to adversarial client behavior. Robust aggregation methods such as Krum and Trimmed Mean have been proposed10; however, many of these methods assume access to aligned numerical update vectors, which is more natural for neural-network parameters than for heterogeneous decision-tree ensembles.Figure 1Generic structure of federated learning in IoMT.

Despite progress in federated learning, TinyML, differential privacy, post-quantum cryptography, and healthcare Digital Twins, most existing solutions address these technologies separately. Federated healthcare systems often overlook resource-constrained deployment and post-quantum-secure communication. TinyML-based monitoring systems typically focus on local inference and rarely support privacy-preserving multi-hospital collaboration. Healthcare Digital Twin research is frequently cloud-centric and often assumes richer computational resources than those available in practical edge-IoMT settings. Moreover, many Digital Twin studies focus on detailed physiological simulation, whereas resource-constrained ICU monitoring may require a more lightweight patient-state representation. These limitations motivate the need for a carefully scoped framework that jointly examines privacy, communication security, adversarial robustness, interpretability, and edge deployment constraints.

This paper presents a federated TinyML framework with an edge-hosted patient-state Digital Twin layer for ICU monitoring. The proposed system should not be interpreted as a fully deployed clinical Digital Twin or a complete physiological simulator. Instead, the Digital Twin component is defined as a lightweight patient-state representation maintained at the hospital edge gateway. It stores recent physiological observations, temporal trends, predicted status labels, risk trajectories, and interpretable alert information. Patient-specific adaptation is achieved through individualized temporal state tracking and risk-history synchronization, while the predictive model itself is collaboratively learned across hospitals. This scope is intentionally selected to support resource-constrained monitoring rather than detailed treatment-response simulation.

The proposed framework builds on our earlier Secure IoMT architecture11 and TrustEdge framework12, but differs from them in two important ways. First, it replaces blockchain-based consensus with a lightweight Performance-Based Filtering (PBF) mechanism designed to reduce latency and communication overhead. Second, it clarifies the federated aggregation process for decision-tree ensembles. Since decision-tree structures are not aligned across clients and cannot be directly averaged like neural-network weights, each hospital trains a local decision-tree ensemble, and the aggregation server performs validation-based client filtering followed by ensemble fusion and pruning. The resulting model is redistributed to hospital gateways and compiled for low-latency ESP32-class inference13.

Secure model exchange is implemented using ML-KEM-512 and AES-256-GCM. For each federated round, clients derive a round-specific shared secret through ML-KEM-512 and use it to protect serialized local ensemble contributions with authenticated encryption. At the aggregation server, decrypted client ensembles are evaluated on a held-out trusted validation set. Low-performing or suspicious contributions are excluded before global ensemble fusion. The PBF threshold is selected using a percentile-based rule, and its sensitivity is evaluated across thresholds from 10% to 50%. This design avoids gradient inspection, neural-network-style parameter averaging, blockchain consensus, and additional communication rounds. The framework is evaluated using a clinician-reviewed synthetic ICU monitoring dataset distributed across three heterogeneous non-IID hospital clients. The prediction task uses five status categories: normal, mild, moderate, critical, and an outlier/anomaly category. These labels are synthetic severity-status categories and should not be interpreted as prospectively validated ICU endpoints. External benchmarking is also performed using a PhysioNet-based critical-care dataset constructed from shared physiological features to assess generalization beyond the synthetic setting. Under a targeted label-flipping attack in which one hospital relabels critical cases as normal, the proposed PBF strategy achieves 86.79% accuracy, 0.782 macro-F1, 0.904 critical-class recall, 0.837 critical-class F1-score, 0.947 macro-AUROC, and 0.799 macro-AUPRC on the synthetic ICU evaluation. Comparative evaluation against FedAvg, Krum, Trimmed Mean, and Coordinate Median shows that PBF provides stronger protection of critical-class recall under adversarial conditions. Differential privacy is evaluated at multiple privacy budgets, with \varepsilon = 2.0 used as the main privacy–utility setting. Hardware profiling is reported separately for ESP32 inference and gateway-side security operations to avoid conflating microcontroller deployment with training or cryptographic processing.

The contribution of this work is not the invention of federated learning, differential privacy, post-quantum cryptography, TinyML, or Digital Twins as individual technologies. Rather, the contribution lies in the careful integration and experimental evaluation of these components within a resource-constrained ICU-oriented IoMT framework. The study specifically investigates how decision-tree-based federated TinyML, validation-driven Byzantine filtering, lightweight patient-state tracking, differential privacy, and post-quantum-secure communication interact under non-IID and adversarial healthcare conditions. The work should therefore be viewed as a technical feasibility and robustness study, while prospective clinical validation and full hospital deployment remain future work.

Literature review

The design of intelligent ICU monitoring systems needs to take into account a number of dependent requirements that include, but are not limited to, real-time inference, privacy preserving, secure collaboration among institutions, resilience to cyber attacks, interpretability and edge deployment of the system. Technologies such as federated learning, TinyML, Digital Twins, differential privacy, robust aggregation and post-quantum cryptography have been introduced to solve one aspect or another of this problem. Nevertheless, most research works focus on solving these issues independently or under restrictive assumptions that are hard to meet in constrained ICU-focused IoMT settings.

Federated learning in healthcare

Federated learning (FL) allows several healthcare organizations to jointly train ML models without exchanging the raw patients data. It is appealing for such an application because there are many legal and ethical restrictions in the area limiting centralization of patients’ data. FedAvg9 proposed by McMahan et al. shows that it is possible to use decentralized learning due to aggregation of parameters. However, FedAvg makes assumptions on clients’ compatibility that do not hold in real healthcare environment.

Healthcare data is generally considered to be non-IID due to variations in hospitals in terms of demographics of patients, prevalence of diseases, sensor setup, clinical processes, admission policies, and monitoring frequency14. Such diversity can negatively affect model convergence and generalization during federated learning. Federated learning has been utilized for different medical use cases such as sepsis prediction and privacy-enabled medical AI. Li et al.15 have shown the possible benefits of federated learning in sepsis prediction whereas Rieke et al.16 have considered FL for privacy-aware medical AI in general. Recent surveys such as Nasajpour et al.17,18 Several methods have been proposed to address non-IID learning, including clustered federated learning19 and SplitFed learning20. These approaches can improve model convergence under heterogeneous distributions, but many of them assume cloud-level computation, relatively stable communication, or neural-network-style update aggregation. Such assumptions are not always suitable for ICU-oriented edge systems, especially when low-power devices and hospital gateways must support inference, privacy protection, security, and robustness simultaneously.

Recent studies have also explored the combination of FL with blockchain and decentralized trust mechanisms in healthcare and related domains. For example, the study on revolutionising vaccine supply chains proposed a federated blockchain framework with smart contracts for improved scalability and security in healthcare logistics21. Fed-IoMT-Block introduced a privacy-preserving framework for secure federated learning in consumer-centric IoMT environments22,23. Similarly, Quantum-Secure HealthChain investigated the integration of quantum-secure mechanisms and blockchain for cloud-based healthcare security24. These studies show the growing importance of combining decentralization, privacy, and security. However, they primarily focus on blockchain-enabled trust, cloud-based healthcare security, or consumer-centric IoMT, rather than TinyML-compatible ICU monitoring with decision-tree ensemble fusion, ESP32-level inference, and validation-based Byzantine filtering.

Our previous HybridTrust framework13 addressed part of this challenge by introducing crypto-agile federated learning for heterogeneous medical devices. However, HybridTrust did not include a patient-state Digital Twin layer, nor did it focus on validation-driven aggregation of decision-tree ensembles for TinyML deployment. The present work extends this research direction by integrating federated TinyML, lightweight patient-state tracking, post-quantum-secure communication, and Byzantine-resilient filtering within a single ICU-oriented IoMT framework.

Privacy preservation in federated healthcare

Although FL avoids direct sharing of patient records, model updates may still reveal sensitive information. Attacks such as membership inference, gradient leakage, and reconstruction attacks can expose private characteristics of local training data. Nasr et al.6,25 showed that shared model updates can be vulnerable to privacy attacks, making additional privacy-preserving mechanisms necessary even in federated settings.

Differential privacy (DP) is still one of the leading mechanisms used in order to mitigate the problems related to the inference in machine learning. The theoretical framework of differential privacy was proposed by Dwork and Roth4. Also, the usage of DP in federated learning was elaborated on by Kairouz et al.5. Regarding the use of AI in healthcare, Kaissis et al.26 pointed out that there should be a combination of privacy-preserving, distributed learning and secure computation. However, the use of differential privacy creates a problem of privacy–utility trade-off due to noise addition.

Beyond FL-specific privacy mechanisms, broader privacy-preserving systems have also examined secure auditing, decentralization, and user-controlled data protection. For example, privacy-aware secure data auditing for cloud-based Intelligence of Things environments highlights the need to verify outsourced or distributed data without exposing sensitive information27. Similarly, privacy-preserved and decentralized smartphone recommendation systems demonstrate how privacy-aware computation can be combined with decentralized data management outside the healthcare domain28. These works reinforce the importance of privacy-aware distributed intelligence, but their objectives differ from ICU-oriented federated TinyML, where privacy protection must be evaluated together with model robustness, latency, and resource constraints.

Alsubaei et al.29 further emphasized the importance of encryption, authentication, and access control in IoMT infrastructures. However, many privacy-preserving healthcare FL studies remain server-centric and do not explicitly evaluate the computational implications of privacy mechanisms on constrained medical-edge deployments. In contrast, the present framework applies differential privacy at the hospital gateway level and separates gateway-side privacy operations from ESP32 inference, thereby avoiding the misleading assumption that microcontrollers perform all training and privacy-preserving computations.

TinyML and edge intelligence for IoMT

TinyML provides a practical way to execute machine-learning inference directly on microcontrollers and other resource-constrained devices. Prior work has shown that lightweight models can operate within limited memory and power budgets, making TinyML relevant for IoMT systems that require local, low-latency inference2,30. This is particularly important in ICU monitoring, where reliance on continuous cloud connectivity can introduce latency and availability risks.

Decision-tree ensembles are attractive for edge healthcare applications because they offer low computational complexity, fast inference, compact deployment, and native interpretability. Unlike deep neural networks, decision-tree models can provide feature-importance information and threshold-based decision paths that are more transparent to clinicians and system designers. These properties make them suitable for ESP32-class deployment, provided that model size and tree depth are carefully controlled.

The previous Secure Edge IoMT platform11 proved the viability of using TinyML and post-quantum cryptography to perform ICU monitoring tasks on ESP32-class devices. This previous research was however limited to secure edge inference, and did not provide federated collaboration across multiple hospitals, Byzantine-aggregation resistant algorithms, or Digital Twin synchronization based on the state of patients. Robustness is another vital feature in the healthcare sector, as wrong predictions, faulty communication, or even malicious updates could have a direct impact on patients’ safety31. Thus, reliability must be taken into consideration.

Digital twins in smart healthcare

Digital Twins (DT) have recently been attracting significant interest as virtual counterparts of physical entities that can stay synchronized with real-world measurements. In the field of medicine, DTs have been investigated to monitor patients, understand disease evolution, make decisions, and develop personalized medicines32,33. The most common structure of healthcare digital twin consists of physiological information about a patient, predictions, historical measurements, and simulations.

Elmisery et al.34 stressed the necessity of privacy-enabled Digital Twin framework for healthcare applications, whereas Rieke et al.16 pointed out the potential of federated intelligence in future healthcare AI ecosystems. Nevertheless, many DT frameworks developed for healthcare continue to be cloud-based and computationally expensive. Moreover, numerous DT systems have been built around physiology simulation and treatment response modeling that is impossible to perform on resource-constrained edge devices or gateways at hospitals with stringent delay constraints.

This is why the current work defines Digital Twin in a more narrowly defined way. Unlike previous efforts that aimed at constructing an elaborate physiological simulator that would model patient reaction to treatments and organ-level dynamics or clinical controls, the Digital Twin suggested in this work is implemented as a lightweight patient-state Digital Twin that keeps records about recent physiological states, their trends, predictions of patient state labels, trajectories of risks, and understandable alerts. Patient-level adaptation is accomplished using individual histories of patient states rather than training a new model for each patient. This definition aligns with constrained resource monitoring in ICU, where the first aim is patient-state modeling and tracking of patient risks.

Post-quantum security for medical IoT

The development of quantum computing poses major security challenges for conventional public-key cryptography. Conventional encryption algorithms like RSA and ECC might fall prey to potential quantum attacks using Shor’s algorithm7. Given the longevity of sensitive health information, it is necessary for the IoMT system to employ security measures that account for future cryptographic threats.

To mitigate these issues, NIST has developed ML-KEM, previously called Kyber, which is a key encapsulation mechanism based on lattices and is used to achieve post-quantum security8,35. There have been recent research works focused on the post-quantum lightweight cryptographic techniques for constraint devices. This separation is important for accurately interpreting latency, memory, and energy measurements.

Robust federated learning and Byzantine resilience

Federated learning systems are vulnerable to Byzantine clients that submit corrupted, poisoned, or malicious updates. This risk is especially serious in healthcare because degraded model performance may reduce the detection of clinically important patient states. Attacks may include targeted label flipping, random label flipping, Gaussian noise injection, sign flipping, model replacement, or other poisoning strategies.

Several robust aggregation methods have been proposed to mitigate Byzantine behavior. Krum selects updates that are close to the majority of client updates10, while Trimmed Mean removes extreme parameter values before aggregation. Coordinate-wise median is another commonly used robust baseline. Sybil attacks, in which malicious clients create multiple identities to manipulate aggregation, further complicate federated learning38. However, many of these robust aggregation methods were originally designed for neural-network parameter vectors. They are less directly applicable when local models are decision-tree ensembles with heterogeneous structures, since tree thresholds, feature splits, and branch layouts cannot be aligned or averaged in the same way as neural-network weights.

Blockchain-based trust mechanisms have also been explored for improving security and accountability in IoT and healthcare systems. Hilda and Thanarajan39 proposed blockchain-enabled authentication for intelligent networks, while other federated blockchain studies have investigated decentralized trust management for healthcare and supply-chain systems21,22. In our previous TrustEdge architecture12, blockchain was used to support self-healing federated TinyML in healthcare settings. However, blockchain consensus can introduce additional latency and communication overhead, which may be undesirable for resource-constrained ICU monitoring.

Machine-learning-based healthcare security systems have also been used to detect anomalies in IoMT environments40. These studies reinforce the need for reliable aggregation protocols in distributed healthcare intelligence. In the present work, blockchain consensus is replaced by a Performance-Based Filtering mechanism that evaluates each local decision-tree ensemble using a held-out trusted validation set. Accepted ensembles are fused and pruned to construct the global model, while suspicious client contributions are excluded. This approach avoids neural-network-style parameter averaging and provides a practical robustness mechanism for decision-tree-based federated TinyML.

Synthesis and research gap

The reviewed literature shows substantial progress in federated healthcare learning, privacy preservation, TinyML, Digital Twins, post-quantum cryptography, blockchain-enabled trust, and Byzantine-resilient aggregation. However, these areas are usually studied separately. Federated healthcare systems often do not consider microcontroller-level deployment or post-quantum-secure communication. TinyML studies usually focus on local inference rather than secure multi-hospital collaboration. Healthcare Digital Twin studies are frequently cloud-centric or simulation-heavy, making them difficult to deploy in constrained IoMT environments. Robust FL methods such as Krum and Trimmed Mean are commonly formulated for aligned parameter vectors, which limits their direct applicability to heterogeneous decision-tree ensembles.

Prior work has incrementally addressed some of these issues. Secure Edge IoMT11 demonstrated TinyML and post-quantum cryptography for ESP32-level ICU monitoring. TrustEdge12 introduced blockchain-enabled self-healing for federated TinyML. HybridTrust13 investigated crypto-agile federated learning for heterogeneous medical devices. Recent blockchain and privacy-preserving healthcare studies have further explored decentralized trust, secure FL, and cloud-based healthcare protection21,22,24,27,28. Nevertheless, limited work has examined how federated TinyML, lightweight patient-state Digital Twins, differential privacy, post-quantum-secure communication, and Byzantine-resilient filtering interact within one ICU-oriented IoMT framework.

This study addresses that gap by implementing and evaluating a federated TinyML architecture that combines edge-hosted patient-state tracking, decision-tree ensemble fusion, ML-KEM-512-based secure model exchange, differential privacy, and Performance-Based Filtering under non-IID and adversarial healthcare settings. The contribution is therefore positioned as an integration and robustness evaluation of carefully scoped components, rather than as the invention of FL, TinyML, post-quantum cryptography, or Digital Twins individually. The evaluation uses a clinician-reviewed synthetic ICU monitoring dataset and external benchmarking with a PhysioNet-based critical-care dataset constructed from shared physiological features41–43. Clinical claims are intentionally limited to technical feasibility, robustness, and experimental decision-support evaluation; prospective clinical deployment remains future work.

Methodology

This section describes the proposed federated TinyML framework with a lightweight patient-state Digital Twin layer for secure ICU-oriented IoMT monitoring. The framework is designed to examine four technical challenges jointly: (i) privacy-preserving collaborative learning among hospitals, (ii) robustness against adversarial model-poisoning attacks, (iii) post-quantum-secure model exchange, and (iv) low-latency inference on constrained edge devices. The proposed system is not intended to represent a fully deployed clinical Digital Twin or a complete physiological simulator. Dataset generation and clinical validation

The experiments involve the use of the Synthetic Dataset for Patient Vitals Monitoring with Scenarios43. This dataset was synthetically constructed by clinicians to replicate realistic ICU monitoring environments without compromising any actual patient data. The original dataset reviewed by clinicians consists of 50,200 physiological samples. Train–validation–test splitting and leakage prevention

To reduce the risk of data leakage, temporal trajectories were grouped before splitting. All records belonging to the same simulated patient trajectory were assigned exclusively to one split. No trajectory was allowed to appear simultaneously in training and testing data. This prevents repeated or temporally adjacent samples from the same simulated deterioration episode from being shared across training and test phases.

After trajectory-level separation, the training data were partitioned across three hospital clients to create the federated learning setting. A separate held-out validation set was used only by the aggregation server to evaluate local client models during Performance-Based Filtering. This validation set was not used for local model training, global model updating, or final test reporting. Final performance was measured on an unseen held-out test set.

External benchmarking was performed separately using a PhysioNet-based critical-care dataset constructed from physiological features shared with the synthetic ICU dataset. This external evaluation is used to assess generalization beyond the synthetic setting, but it does not replace prospective clinical validation.

Federated aggregation of decision-tree ensembles

Traditional Byzantine-resilient aggregation methods such as Krum and Trimmed Mean were developed mainly for neural-network parameter vectors. Krum selects updates close to the majority of client updates, whereas Trimmed Mean removes extreme coordinate values before aggregation. These methods require aligned update vectors and are therefore not directly suitable for decision-tree ensembles whose structures differ across clients.

In the proposed framework, each hospital trains a local decision-tree ensemble. Accepted local ensembles are fused into a global ensemble after PBF filtering:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {E}_{global} = \bigcup _{i \in \mathscr {S}} \mathscr {E}_{i}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {S}$$\end{document} denotes the set of accepted hospitals.

To improve resilience against malicious participants, the framework employs Performance-Based Filtering. A trusted validation dataset is maintained by the aggregation server. It therefore avoids gradient inspection, neural-network-style parameter averaging, blockchain consensus, and additional communication rounds.

Threshold selection

This is achieved using the PBF threshold, which helps regulate the balance between robustness and participation of the client. Lower thresholds may allow for poisoned clients data to enter the federation, while high thresholds may filter out useful but distributionally different clients. In order to measure the sensitivity of this threshold, values of 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50% were tested experimentally. The value of 50th percentile was selected for the main tests due to its stability.

Trusted validation dataset

The PBF approach relies on the existence of an independent validation dataset. The validation dataset in the current study is built after splitting based on trajectory levels, and it is not used for local training of clients. Validation is done only to assess the performance of the submitted local ensembles prior to their fusion. Parameters of the model are not optimized using validation examples.

This assumption seems to be valid for health care federations based on a consortium model, where a shared validation set can be used by the aggregation party. Nevertheless, it remains an issue to be considered. Future research needs to study decentralized trust calculation, rotating validation sets, and validation processes that do not depend on any trusted validation set.

Machine learning framework and security mechanisms

This section describes the machine-learning, privacy, security, robustness, and explainability mechanisms implemented within the proposed framework. To address concerns regarding the scope of the Digital Twin and the aggregation of decision-tree models, we clearly distinguish among three components: (i) the patient-state Digital Twin layer, (ii) the TinyML decision-tree inference model, and (iii) the federated ensemble-fusion and filtering mechanism.

The Digital Twin component is implemented as a lightweight patient-state monitoring layer rather than a comprehensive physiological simulator. For each patient, the Digital Twin maintains a sliding window of recent physiological measurements, updates risk trajectories using the latest federated TinyML model, and generates interpretable alert information. Therefore, patient status is estimated from recent physiological history rather than from a single isolated measurement. The Digital Twin can also perform limited counterfactual risk analysis by perturbing selected physiological variables, such as SpO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}, respiratory rate, or body temperature, within controlled ranges and observing the resulting change in predicted status. These outputs are intended for experimental monitoring support only and should not be interpreted as mechanistic physiological simulation, diagnosis, treatment-response prediction, or autonomous clinical decision-making. Local TinyML model: decision-tree ensemble

Each hospital gateway trains a local decision-tree ensemble using its private ICU monitoring data. In the main configuration, each local model contains 20 decision trees with a maximum depth of 8. The decision-tree ensemble is selected for three reasons. Low computational overhead: Decision trees require only threshold comparisons during inference, making them suitable for ESP32-class microcontrollers. Inference on the ESP32-WROOM-32 using ESP-IDF v5.1 takes approximately 6.3 ms. This measurement refers to TinyML inference only; training, differential privacy, cryptographic model exchange, and aggregation are performed at the hospital gateway or aggregation server.Native interpretability: Feature importance is computed from Gini impurity reduction across the ensemble. This supports transparent alert explanation, for example when a high-risk prediction is associated with abnormal respiratory rate, reduced oxygen saturation, abnormal blood pressure, or elevated temperature. These explanations provide model transparency but should not be interpreted as clinical causal validation.Suitability for heterogeneous data: Ensemble learning can improve stability compared with a single decision tree because multiple trees reduce sensitivity to local distributional bias. This property is useful in the proposed multi-hospital setting, where hospitals differ in the prevalence of normal, mild, moderate, critical, and outlier/anomaly cases.After pruning, the deployed model occupies approximately 142 KB, which is compatible with the memory constraints of ESP32-class devices. The compact model size supports low-latency bedside inference while leaving training and security operations to gateway-level infrastructure.

Federated TinyML training protocol

A synchronous federated learning protocol is implemented across three hospital clients, denoted as Hospitals A, B, and C. Each hospital holds a non-IID partition of the clinician-reviewed synthetic ICU dataset. Local training is performed at the hospital gateway, while the ESP32 device is used only for sensing and TinyML inference.

Let the local dataset of hospital k be defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} D_k = \{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N_k}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Each hospital trains a local decision-tree ensemble:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} M_k = \{T_{k,1},T_{k,2},\ldots ,T_{k,m}\}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where m is the number of trees in the local ensemble.

The fused ensemble is then pruned before redistribution to keep the deployed model within ESP32 memory limits.

Performance-based filtering for Byzantine resilience

Federated learning systems are vulnerable to Byzantine clients that submit poisoned, noisy, or fraudulent model contributions. This risk is more serious in non-IID medical settings because malicious behavior can resemble natural distribution shift across hospitals. In the main targeted attack scenario, Hospital C is treated as the Byzantine client and relabels critical-risk cases as normal before local training.

To reduce the influence of poisoned clients, the proposed framework uses Performance-Based Filtering. Post-quantum secure model exchange

The proposed framework protects federated model exchange using ML-KEM-512 as the post-quantum key encapsulation mechanism and AES-256-GCM as the authenticated encryption scheme. These operations are performed at the hospital gateway and aggregation-server level.

For each communication round, the aggregation server publishes an ML-KEM public key. Each hospital gateway encapsulates a shared secret and derives an AES session key. The encapsulation step is:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} (c,ss) \leftarrow \textrm{ML}\!-\!\mathrm {KEM.Encaps}(pk_{\textrm{agg}}), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where c is the encapsulated ciphertext and ss is the shared secret.

The aggregation server recovers the shared secret using:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} ss \leftarrow \textrm{ML}\!-\!\mathrm {KEM.Decaps}(sk_{\textrm{agg}},c). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The AES session key is derived as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} K = \textrm{HKDF}(ss,\textrm{round}\_\textrm{id}). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The protected local ensemble contribution is then encrypted using AES-256-GCM with a nonce derived from the round index and client identifier. This design provides confidentiality and integrity of model exchange and reduces replay risk through round-specific key and nonce derivation. Results and performance evaluation

The proposed federated TinyML framework with a lightweight patient-state Digital Twin layer was evaluated using a clinician-reviewed synthetic ICU monitoring dataset containing 480,000 samples distributed across three non-IID hospital clients. The evaluation focuses on four aspects: (i) robustness against Byzantine attacks under non-IID data distribution, (ii) status-prediction performance with emphasis on critical-class detection, (iii) privacy–utility trade-off under differential privacy, and (iv) external benchmarking using a PhysioNet-based critical-care dataset.

Unless otherwise stated, the reported synthetic ICU results correspond to Performance-Based Filtering (PBF) with differential privacy \varepsilon =2.0, 10 federated rounds, a 50th-percentile PBF threshold, and a targeted label-flipping attack from Hospital C. The prediction task uses five categories: Status 0 normal, Status 1 mild risk, Status 2 moderate risk, Status 3 critical risk, and Status 4 outlier/abnormal physiological pattern. Status 4 is not interpreted as a clinical severity endpoint.

Conclusion

This paper presented a federated TinyML framework with a lightweight patient-state Digital Twin layer for secure and resilient IoMT-based ICU monitoring. The framework integrates federated learning, differential privacy, post-quantum-secure communication, decision-tree ensemble fusion, and Byzantine-resilient Performance-Based Filtering (PBF). The objective was not to develop a fully deployed clinical Digital Twin or a complete physiological simulator, but to evaluate whether these components can operate together within a resource-constrained, privacy-aware, and adversarial multi-hospital monitoring setting.

The proposed architecture combines ESP32-compatible TinyML inference, hospital-gateway training, ML-KEM-512 post-quantum key encapsulation, AES-256-GCM authenticated encryption, differential privacy, and validation-driven client filtering. The patient-state Digital Twin layer maintains recent physiological observations, temporal risk trends, predicted status labels, and interpretable alert information. Patient-specific adaptation is achieved through individualized temporal state tracking rather than through a separate patient-specific model. Since decision-tree structures cannot be directly averaged across clients, the proposed aggregation strategy relies on filtering, ensemble fusion, and pruning rather than neural-network-style parameter averaging.

Experimental evaluation was conducted using a clinician-reviewed synthetic ICU monitoring dataset containing 480,000 records distributed across three non-IID hospital clients. The prediction task used five status categories: normal, mild, moderate, critical, and an outlier/abnormal physiological pattern category. These categories should be interpreted as synthetic severity-status labels rather than prospectively validated ICU endpoints. Under a targeted label-flipping attack in which a malicious hospital relabeled critical cases as normal, the proposed PBF strategy achieved 86.79% accuracy, 0.782 macro-F1, 0.904 critical-class recall, 0.837 critical-class F1-score, 0.947 macro-AUROC, and 0.799 macro-AUPRC. Additional experiments involving random label flipping, Gaussian-noise injection, sign-flipping, and model-replacement attacks showed that PBF maintained stable performance when the poisoned client contribution was rejected.

The proposed PBF mechanism was compared with FedAvg, Krum, Trimmed Mean, and Coordinate Median under the same targeted poisoning setting. Although FedAvg achieved higher overall accuracy, its critical-class recall was substantially lower. In contrast, PBF achieved the highest critical-class recall among the evaluated methods, indicating that validation-driven filtering can better preserve detection of critical-risk cases in this synthetic ICU monitoring task. This trade-off is important because critical-class recall is more relevant than overall accuracy in deterioration-oriented monitoring.

External benchmarking was performed using a PhysioNet-based critical-care dataset constructed from shared physiological features. At \varepsilon =2.0, the framework achieved 98.04% accuracy, 0.959 macro-F1, 0.977 critical-class recall, 0.987 critical-class F1-score, 0.999 macro-AUROC, and 0.983 macro-AUPRC. These results indicate strong performance on the selected retrospective shared-feature benchmark. However, they should not be interpreted as prospective clinical validation, because the external evaluation uses feature-compatible retrospective data and does not represent live deployment in hospital workflows.

Overall, the results suggest that federated TinyML, lightweight patient-state tracking, differential privacy, post-quantum-secure communication, and validation-based Byzantine filtering can be combined into a technically feasible ICU-oriented IoMT framework. The findings support the potential of secure and attack-resilient collaborative monitoring under experimental conditions, while full clinical deployment, prospective validation, and regulatory evaluation remain future work.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results.

First, the main evaluation is based on a clinician-reviewed synthetic ICU monitoring dataset. Although the synthetic data were designed to mimic plausible physiological deterioration patterns and were externally benchmarked using a PhysioNet-based critical-care dataset, the labels remain synthetic severity-status categories. They are not real ICU endpoints such as mortality, sepsis onset, mechanical ventilation, vasopressor requirement, or clinician-adjudicated deterioration events. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as experimental decision-support performance rather than clinical diagnostic validation.

Second, the suggested Digital Twin is a light-weighted monitoring layer for the patient s states as opposed to a fully-fledged physiological Digital Twin. The former retains recent observations, risk trends, predicted state labels, and understandable alerts; however, it does not simulate any dynamics at the organ level, treatment response, clinical control feedback, and patient-specific physiological simulation. Adaptation to individual patients is possible only at the state temporal level.

Thirdly, the federated scenario involves three hospital clients. Such an arrangement is helpful in assessing non-IID learning and byzantine filtering under controlled conditions; however, federated scenarios with more members might present other difficulties concerning communication efficiency, client availability, trust management, demography diversity, and representativeness of the validation set.

Fourth, the PBF approach relies on the availability of a trusted validation data set that is used for the assessment of client participation. It can be challenging to establish such a validation data set in practice within health care consortia due to limitations imposed by regulation, differing patient populations, and institutional governance issues.

Fifth, while the robustness framework was tested for targeted label flipping, random label flipping, Gaussian noise addition, sign flipping, and model substitution attacks, there are still several sophisticated attack types beyond the purview of this work. This set includes adaptive poisoning attacks, stealth attacks, backdoor attacks, colluding malicious clients, Sybil attacks, and optimization-based adversarial attacks.

Sixth, the hardware assessment distinguishes ESP32 inference from training on the gateway side, differential privacy, post-quantum cryptography, and aggregation. It is essential for interpretation purposes; however, the fact is that the current experiments cannot be considered a full-fledged implementation of hardware at the bedside, where there is real-time data collection, federated learning, Digital Twins, and secured communication.

Lastly, the analysis for explainability uses decision tree feature importance and correlation among the features. This approach offers transparency in the model but fails to establish any clinical causation and clinical readiness. Further testing will be needed in order for any future application to healthcare to be successful.

Future work

Future work will focus on moving from technical feasibility toward practical clinical evaluation.

First, the complete pipeline should be tested in a live hardware-in-the-loop environment using ESP32-WROOM-32 and ESP32-S3 devices connected to medical-grade or clinically realistic sensing modules. This would allow simultaneous measurement of sensing latency, inference latency, gateway processing time, secure communication overhead, Digital Twin synchronization delay, memory usage, and energy consumption.

Second, larger-scale validation should be conducted using real multi-center ICU datasets such as MIMIC-IV, eICU, PhysioNet Challenge datasets, and hospital-specific retrospective cohorts. Future studies should use patient-level splitting, temporal validation, external-site testing, class-wise metrics, calibration analysis, AUROC, AUPRC, and clinician-adjudicated endpoints where available.

Third, future work should investigate more advanced Byzantine-resilient aggregation methods for decision-tree and TinyML-compatible federated models. Possible directions include adaptive PBF thresholds, uncertainty-aware filtering, client reputation modeling, validation-free trust estimation, and privacy-preserving client evaluation.

Fourth, the post-quantum communication layer should be extended toward hybrid classical–post-quantum designs that combine ML-KEM-512 with currently deployed classical schemes such as ECC. Such hybrid designs may support gradual migration of hospital infrastructures toward quantum-safe communication while preserving compatibility with existing systems.

Fifth, the patient-state Digital Twin layer should be extended with stronger temporal modeling. Sequential models, streaming risk trajectories, uncertainty estimation, and patient-specific baseline adaptation could improve individualized monitoring. However, such extensions should remain clearly distinguished from full physiological simulation unless mechanistic modeling and prospective validation are added.

Finally, future clinical studies with healthcare practitioners are necessary to evaluate workflow integration, alert usability, explainability requirements, human factors, regulatory constraints, and patient-safety implications. Such studies are essential before secure federated patient-state Digital Twin systems can move from experimental IoMT prototypes toward real clinical decision-support deployment.
