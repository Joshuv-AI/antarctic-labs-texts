A cDC1‐NK/T‐Malignant Cell Circuit Drives Immunogenic Remodeling Under PD‐1 Blockade in Gastric Cancer
Authors: Takuya Nakatsuru, Hiroki Yamashita, Chiaki Mashima, Guoqiang Zhang, Hitoshi Saito, Tatsunori Minamide, Keita H Nagaoka, Ryo Kamata, Shunsuke A Sakai, Gaku Yamamoto, Tomoko Y Morita, Kazuki Nakai, Yumi Hakozaki, Kumi Kinoshita, Yui Takahashi, Izuma Nakayama, Akihito Kawazoe, Yuko Ishii, Sai M Pulukuri, Hiroshi Haeno, Yukie Kashima, Kohei Shitara, Tomonori Yano, Takeshi Saito, Akihiro Ohashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe gastric cancer (GC) tumor microenvironment (TME) constitutes a complex and dynamic ecosystem in which immune, stromal, and malignant cells (MCs) collectively shape therapeutic responses. The dynamics of GC TME remodeling in different treatment contexts remain unclear. We performed single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) of GC biopsy specimens from treatment‐naïve patients (TN) and patients treated with chemotherapy (C), chemotherapy plus nivolumab (CN), or chemotherapy plus trastuzumab (CT), and found that CN treatment enhanced natural killer (NK)/T cell‐associated immunogenicity in GC TMEs. In CN TMEs, MCs upregulate MHC‐I‐ and inflammation‐related genes. Conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1), one subcluster of dendritic cells (DCs), concurrently exhibited activation of antigen‐presentation and T cell‐stimulatory programs, indicating intensified communication between NK/CD8+ T cells and cDC1 in the CN TMEs. The cell–cell interaction analyses uncovered an intensified X‐C Motif Chemokine Ligand 1 (XCL1) ‐ X‐C motif chemokine receptor 1 (XCR1) chemotactic axis linking NK/CD8+ T cells and cDC1, highlighting the formation of “the MCs‐cDC1‐NK/CD8+ T cells circuit” to reinforce antitumor immunity in the CN TMEs. Furthermore, a reduced cytotoxic NK sub‐cluster, which showed high XCL1 expression, was enriched among CN responders, and its transcriptional signature was significantly correlated with favorable survival in the Cancer Genome Atlas GC cohort. Our findings delineate an immunostimulatory circuit driven by NK/CD8+ T‐DCs–MCs interactions to orchestrate the immune cycle under CN therapy in GC TMEs.Keywords: cancer immune cycle, combination with ICIs and chemotherapy, gastric cancer, single cell RNA‐seq, tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide, particularly in East Asian populations [1, 2, 3]. For patients with advanced GC, platinum plus fluoropyrimidine‐based chemotherapy regimens have been widely used as standard treatments; however, the prognosis remains poor due to frequent recurrence and metastatic dissemination [4, 5]. While molecular targeted agents, such as trastuzumab, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), like nivolumab, have improved survival when combined with chemotherapy, therapeutic efficacy varies among individuals, and the mechanisms underlying responsiveness remain unclear [6, 7, 8, 9, 10].

Tumor responsiveness to therapeutic agents is influenced by complex interactions within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [11, 12, 13]. Specifically, cellular components, including malignant, immune, endothelial, and fibroblastic cells, influence sensitivity or resistance to therapy. For instance, immunosuppressive myeloid cells and activated cancer‐associated fibroblasts promote chemoresistance and attenuate antitumor immunity, whereas increased infiltration of cytotoxic T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and dendritic cells (cDCs) correlates with favorable ICI responses [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. A detailed characterization of the GC TME under different therapeutic regimens is essential for understanding the mechanisms governing treatment outcome. However, how chemotherapy, alone or in combination with ICIs or anti‐HER2 therapy, reshapes the GC TME in patients remains poorly understood.

Single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) provides a powerful framework for delineating the transcriptomic landscape of the TME [19, 20, 21, 22]. We previously established an scRNA‐seq platform optimized for endoscopic biopsy specimens, enabling the characterization of diverse cellular components in the primary GC TME. This approach allows for the visualization of the TME ecosystem and facilitates the investigation of therapy‐induced immune remodeling in clinical samples [23, 24].

This study investigates how chemotherapy combined with ICIs or anti‐HER2 therapy shapes the GC TME using single‐cell transcriptomics. Using single‐cell RNA sequencing of endoscopically obtained GC biopsies [24], we revealed that the combination of chemotherapy and ICI induced inflammatory responses in tumor cells, and that MHC‐I‐related gene expression correlated with therapeutic responsiveness. In the immune compartment, chemotherapy with ICI induces qualitative alterations in dendritic cells (DC) function, activating acquired immunity and inducing metabolic remodeling of effector CD8+ T cells and NK cells. A distinct NK cell subset, characterized by low expression of classical cytotoxic molecules and high production of XCL1, has been implicated in cDC1 recruitment and in remodeling the immunogenic TME. Prognostic analysis using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset and NK cell‐derived differentially expressed genes (DEGs) suggested potential associations with clinical outcomes across multiple solid tumors. Metabolically adapted NK cells under combined chemotherapy and ICI treatment contribute to the formation of an immunogenic TME by inducing altered cDC1.

Methods

Ethics Statement

We enrolled patients with GC who underwent upper esophagogastroduodenoscopy at the National Cancer Center Hospital East between 2019 and 2020. All participants gave written informed consent for study participation. This study was approved by the institutional review board (registration number 2021–019) and performed in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical information was obtained from the electronic medical records. Clinical staging was determined according to the 8th edition of the TNM classification [25].

Single‐Cell Library Construction and Sequencing

Single‐cell libraries were prepared using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′GEM, Library & Gel Bead Kit v3.1 (10× Genomics, Cat# PN‐1000121) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After gel bead‐in‐emulsion (GEM) generation, cDNA amplification, and 3′gene expression libraries were generated. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000.

Processing Pipelines for Single‐Cell Datasets

Cell Ranger (version 3.1, 10× Genomics) was used to align reads to GRCh38 and quantify unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) [26]. Gene expression matrices were analyzed with Seurat (version 5.0.3) [27]. Low‐quality cells were removed, retaining cells with 250 to 5000 genes and < 20% mitochondrial transcripts. Data were normalized using the SCTransform [28]. Highly variable genes were identified (n = 2000). Principal components analysis (PCA) was performed on the scaled data. Graph‐based clustering and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) were performed using the top principal components [29].

Single‐Cell Data Integration and Subclustering Analysis of Major Cell Types

Datasets were integrated using Seurat by identifying anchors between datasets and merging them. Subsequently, the analysis pipeline described above was applied. Major cell types were analyzed separately by extracting cells from the integrated dataset. Subclustering was performed using dims = 1:20 and resolution = 1.0. Clusters were annotated based on canonical marker expression.

Calculate Cluster Proportion

For each patient, the proportion of each cell type was calculated: non‐immune cells (e.g., malignant cells) were normalized to the total number of cells, whereas immune cell subclusters were normalized to the total number of immune cells. Proportions were visualized as stacked bar plots and dot plots.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using escape (v1.12.0) [30]. Hallmark gene sets were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, version 2025.1.Hs) using msigdbr (version 25.1.1). Enrichment scores were calculated as z scores for each cluster and visualized as a heatmap using dittoSeq (version 1.14.3).

Subclustering

Subclustering analyses for Malignant cells, Dendritic cells, CD8+ NKT‐like cells, CD8+ T cells, and Natural killer cells were performed using Seurat (v5.0.3) [30]. Standard preprocessing (log‐normalization, identification of 2000 highly variable genes, scaling, PCA, and graph‐based clustering) was applied. For each cell type, the following parameters were used: Malignant cells (dims = 1:15, resolution = 0.1), Dendritic cells (dims = 1:20, resolution = 0.05), CD8+ NKT‐like cells (dims = 1:20, resolution = 5.0), CD8+ T cells (dims = 1:15, resolution = 0.3), and NK cells (dims = 1:15, resolution = 0.35). UMAP embeddings were generated with the random seed fixed at 777.

Differential Gene Expression Analysis (DEG) of scRNA‐Seq Data

DEGs were identified using Seurat's FindMarkers function with a two‐sided Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Effect‐size thresholds of |avg_log2FC| > 0.2 and |Δpct| > 0.1 were applied. P values were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, with significance defined at q < 0.05.

Detailed information can be found in Doc S1.

・Tissue processing.

The GC biopsies were enzymatically dissociated into single‐cell suspensions for scRNA‐seq analysis.

・Cluster characterization and gene expression visualization.

Cell clustering and marker gene identification were performed by the Seurat package.

・Calculate subclusters proportion.

Each cellular subcluster was compared across different tissue types and patient groups.

・Single‐sample gene signature enrichment analyses (ssGSEA).

The enrichment of specific biological pathways in individual cells was quantified by the ssGSEA algorithm.

Functional annotation of differentially expressed genes was conducted.

Pseudotime analysis was performed to reconstruct the developmental lineages.

Expression scores for the gene signatures were calculated.

Intercellular communication was inferred by analyzing the expression patterns of cognate ligand‐receptor pairs.

・Survival Analysis Based on Combined NK Subcluster Gene Signature.

The prognostic value of the NK subcluster signature was assessed with clinical data from public and internal cohorts.

・Gene Signature, Pathway Enrichment, and Correlation Analyses.

Correlations between different gene signatures and pathway activities were analyzed.

・Comparison of Gene Expression Levels and Positive Cell Fractions in CD8+ T Cells and NK Cells.

Key markers were compared between T cell and NK cell subsets.

・External Validation Using Bulk RNA‐seq Datasets.

Independent bulk transcriptomic data from external studies were analyzed.

・Estimation of RC‐NK Signature in Normal Tissues.

The RC‐NK signature was evaluated across various normal tissues.

Results

scRNA‐Seq of GC Treated With Chemotherapy Plus PD‐1 or HER2 Blockade

To delineate the gastric TME, we performed scRNA‐seq using 18 freshly obtained biopsy specimens: 17 from patients with stage III–IV GC and one treatment‐naïve specimen from a stage IB patient (Figure 1A). These included 3 treatment‐naïve cases (TN; 8343 cells) and 15 post‐treatment cases: 7 chemotherapy alone (C; 23,904 cells), 5 chemotherapy combined with nivolumab (CN; 11,986 cells), and 3 chemotherapy combined with trastuzumab (CT; 8973 cells) [24]. Clinical responses were classified as progressive disease (PD), stable disease (SD), and partial response (PR). The C group included three PD and four PR cases, the CN group comprised three SD and two PR cases, and all three CT cases were PD.

In total, 53,206 cells were collected from 18 biopsies (median 1273 genes per cell; Supplementary Figure S1A–C [24]). The initial clustering identified 17 distinct clusters visualized by UMAP (Supplementary Figure S1D). The 17 clusters were annotated by ScType [31] and validated manually by canonical marker expression: The identified cell clusters included basophils (975 cells, 1.83%), CD8+ NKT‐like cells (7,865 cells, 14.78%), classical monocytes (3,953 cells, 7.43%), monocytes (ISG‐expressing cells) (936 cells, 1.76%), macrophages (4,246 cells, 7.98%), CD4+ T cells (6405 cells, 12.04%), memory CD8+ T cells (733 cells, 1.38%), dendritic cells (1126 cells, 2.12%), naive B cells (1350 cells, 2.54%), naive CD8+ T cells (988 cells, 1.86%), neutrophils (11,646 cells, 21.89%), and plasma B cells (5,676 cells, 10.67%). These populations were defined as immune cells. Furthermore, malignant cells (2842 cells, 5.83%), endothelial cells (2,616 cells, 4.92%), erythroid‐like and erythroid precursor cells (30 cells, 0.06%), fibroblasts (735 cells, 1.38%), and unknown (1,084 cells, 2.04%) were detected Figure 1B, Supplementary Figure S2A,B and S3A,B. Notably, the proportions of individual immune cell types were calculated relative to the total immune cell population, while the proportions of non‐immune cells and unknown cells were calculated relative to the total cell count.

Next, the CD8+ T cell subsets (naïve, memory, and NKT‐like) were validated using marker expression Supplementary Figure S2B. Naive CD8+ T cells expressed IL7R, CCR7, and LEF1, while lacking GZMB and PRF1. Memory CD8+ T cells were characterized by CD44, CXCR3, and CD28 expression. In contrast, the CD8+ NKT‐like cluster exhibited GZMB, PRF1, and KLRG1 expression but the absence of IL7R and CCR7 expression. Although this cluster contained both CD8+ T and CD3− NK effector populations, they were collectively categorized as NKT‐like clusters due to the technical difficulty in separating them during the initial clustering Supplementary Figures S1D and S2A. Substantial inter‐sample heterogeneity was observed in TMEs (Figure 1C). Comparisons across treatment groups revealed treatment‐associated trends. Compared with the TN group, the C group exhibited fewer DCs. In contrast, the CN group showed an increasing trend in CD8+ NKT‐like cells and macrophages, whereas naïve CD8+ T cells decreased. The CT group did not display any alterations compared to the TN group Figure 1D, Supplementary Figure S3A,B. Due to minimal cell numbers, the clusters of unknown, erythroid‐like, erythroid precursor cells, and monocytes (ISG‐expressing cells) were excluded from further analysis Supplementary Figure S3A.

Given that CD8+ NKT‐like cells increased in the CN group, we performed ssGSEA to assess whether CN TMEs were immunologically activated. In ssGSEA, inflammation‐related hallmarks were upregulated in the CN group compared to the other groups, suggesting that CN enhanced the immunogenicity of gastric TMEs Figure 1E and Supplementary Figure S4A.

Chemo‐Immunotherapy Enhanced Inflammatory Signaling and HLA Expression in Responding Patients

Subcluster analyses of 2842 malignant cells (MCs) identified five distinct clusters (C0–C4) with unique gene signatures (Figure 2A). The five clusters were annotated as C0 (1,411 cells, high oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)), C1 (717 cells, high allograft rejection (Allo R)), C2 (418 cells, high interferon gamma response (Immune response)), C3 (277 cells, high E2F targets (E2F)), and C4 (19 cells, high WNT/β‐catenin (WNT/β‐catenin)) (Figure 2A–C). Pseudotime analyses of MCs revealed a trajectory originating from C0 and progressing sequentially through C3, ultimately reaching C1 and C2 (Figure 2D,E). Transcriptional profiles demonstrated that MCs formed distinct and segregated clusters in each treatment group (Figure 2F–H). The C0 (OXPHOS) cluster was more predominant in the TN group than in the other drug‐treated groups. In the C and CN groups, C0 clusters accumulated more in non‐responders (NR) than in responders (R). In contrast, the C1 (Allo_R) and C2 (Immune_resp.) clusters accumulated more in the R in the C and CN groups than in the NR group. C3 (E2F) was predominant in the CT group and consisted entirely of NR. These results suggest that the gene expression profiles of the MC clusters are associated with drug sensitivity.

We conducted an ssGSEA across each treatment group. In the C and CT groups, the TNFα signaling was remarkably upregulated among the inflammation‐related pathways. In contrast, the scores for the interferon‐gamma, IL6_JAK_STAT3 pathways were lower than those in the TN group. In the CN group, inflammation‐related gene signatures, such as inflammatory response, TNFA signaling via NFkB, IL6_JAK_STAT3 signaling, and IL2_STAT5 signaling, were upregulated compared to other treatment groups Figure 2I, Supplementary Figure S5A. The HLA gene signature scores of HLA‐related gene sets were markedly elevated in the R group, while downregulated in the NR across all treatment groups Figure 2J, Supplementary Figures S5B and S6A,B. IFN‐γ and the immunogenic cell death (ICD) gene signatures were also elevated in the responders of the C and CN groups Supplementary Figure S6C,E. The MHC‐I signature was positively correlated with the IFN‐γ signature (R = 0.484) Supplementary Figure S6D, but not with the ICD Supplementary Figures S6C–F and Supporting Information and Methods Doc S1. The CT group, all patients of which experienced PD, downregulated MHC‐I and IFN‐γ signatures. These data suggest that the IFN‐γ axis is expected to be the primary pathway associated with MHC‐I upregulation, and enhanced HLA‐associated antigen presentation may contribute to a favorable therapeutic response to CN.

Chemo‐Immunotherapy Promotes DC Activation

Subcluster analyses of 1126 DCs identified four clusters (C0–C3) (Figure 3A–C). The C0 cluster corresponded to cDC1 (BTLA, CLEC9A, BATF3, and XCR1), which are specialized for the cross‐presentation and activation of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells [32]. The C1 cluster corresponded to cDC2 (CD1C, CLEC10A, and SIRPA), which primarily mediate CD4+ T cell activation and support diverse helper T cell responses [33]. The C2 cluster corresponded to plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC) (CLEC4C, LILRB4, and IRF7), which are potent producers of type I interferons in response to viral and nucleic acid sensing [34]. The C3 cluster represented cDC3, which contributes to the induction of inflammatory responses in the TME via inflammatory mediators [35]. Antigen presentation and interferon signaling pathways were enriched in cDC1, cDC2, and pDC, whereas cDC3 showed enrichment of Toll‐like receptor and innate sensing pathways. In the TN group, the cDC1, cDC2, cDC3, and pDC clusters accounted for 6.8%, 36.5%, 45.5%, and 11.2%, respectively Figure 3D–F and Supplementary Figure S7A. In the C group, the cDC1, cDC2, and pDC clusters were reduced, while the cDC3 cluster was expanded, suggesting that adaptive immune activation was impaired in C‐treated TMEs. In the CN group, the cDC1, cDC2, and pDC clusters were enriched beyond the levels observed in the TN group, indicating that nivolumab plus chemotherapy activated DCs in gastric TMEs.

Gene signatures related to T cell differentiation, migration, and activation were upregulated in the CN group of the DC cluster Supplementary Figure S7B. The cDC1, cDC2, and pDC subclusters in the CN group also elevated the T cell activation pathway (Figure 3G). CellChat was also performed to compare ligand‐receptor interactions between the TN and CN groups. In the CN group, the cytokine‐mediated interactions of antitumor responses, such as IL‐2 and IL‐12, were markedly increased along with MHC‐I‐related interactions (Figure 3H and Supplementary Figure S7C). XCR1‐dependent interactions, which are involved in the recruitment of cDC1, were also enhanced. In the ligand–receptor (LR) analysis, XCL1‐XCR1 and XCL2‐XCR1 interactions were markedly increased between memory CD8+ T cells and CD8+ NKT‐like cells (Figure 3I). In addition, CCL5‐ and IFNγ‐mediated interactions were also increased (Figure 3J). These ligands were predominantly expressed in the CD8+ NKT‐like cluster, while minimally expressed in the naïve or memory populations Supplementary Figure S7D. This cluster‐specific expression suggests an important role of CD8+ NKT‐like cells in cDC1 recruitment, leading us to focus on subsequent analyses of this effector population.

Chemo‐Immunotherapy Expands Exhausted CD8 + T Cells While Impairing Cytotoxicity

Subclustering of this CD8+ NKT‐like cluster revealed four subsets: Effector CD8+ T cells (19.8%), NK cells (3.8%), γδ T cells (0.5%), and NKT cells (1.2%) Supplementary Figure S8A–C. Owing to their predominant populations and broad distribution across samples, effector CD8+ T and NK cell subclusters were selected for further analysis. A second round of sub‐clustering identified seven transcriptionally distinct subclusters C0 to C6 (Figure 4A,B), which were characterized by unique gene expression signatures: C0 (GZMK+ HLA‐DRB1+), C1 (GZMB+ TCF7+ IL7R+), C2 (FGFBP1+ TCF7−), C3 (EOMES+ TOX+ ENTPD1+ LAG3+), C4 (CD160+ KLRC2+ TRBV14+), C5 (GZMB+ PDCD1+ HAVCR2+ CXCL13+), and C6 (IFNG+ CD69+ SELL+ XCL2+). To determine the functional characteristics of each effector CD8+ T cell sub‐cluster, we performed ssGSEA based on the pathways associated with T cell activation. IFN‐γ signaling was upregulated in clusters C0, C3, and C6, whereas IFN‐α/β signaling was similarly enhanced in C3 and C6 but attenuated in C1. TCR signaling was most active in C0, C5, and C6. Cytokine signaling and chemokine response pathways were upregulated in C6 cells. In C5, signatures related to cell proliferation, including the G2M checkpoint, DNA repair, and mTORC1 signaling, were elevated, and C1 exhibited moderate enrichment of these pathways. C2 exhibited low enrichment scores across pathways, suggesting a functionally inactive population. C4 strongly activated pathways associated with interactions between lymphoid and non‐lymphoid cells and showed a high score for adaptive immune responses (Figure 4C).

Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed three major trajectories across the effector CD8+ T cell subclusters. Route 1 originated in cluster C2, passed through clusters C0 and C1, and finally reached cluster C6. Route 2 branched from cluster C0, passed through cluster C5, and terminated at cluster C3. Route 3 diverged early from cluster C0 and proceeded toward cluster C4, suggesting an alternative differentiation axis distinct from routes 1 and 2 (Figure 4D). We also projected the distribution of each treatment group onto the UMAP‐embedded effector CD8+ T cell subclusters to examine how drug treatments shaped the subcluster composition (Figure 4E). Routes 1 and 2 originated from TN effector CD8+ T cells, passed through the CT group, and reached clusters in the CN group. In contrast, route 3 diverged early from the TN trajectory and terminated in group C. Cluster 0 predominantly comprised cells from the C_NR (non‐response), C_R (response), CN_R, and CT_NR groups; Cluster 1 from the TN, CN_NR, and CN_R groups; Cluster 2 from the TN, C_NR, and CT_NR groups; Cluster 3 from the CN_NR groups; Cluster 4 from the C_R groups; Cluster 5 from the C_R and CN_R groups; and Cluster 6 from the C_NR group Figure 4F,G; Supplementary Figure S8D. In the CN responder group (CN_R), most cells were distributed within the C5 and C1 clusters, suggesting that these clusters may be associated with the therapeutic efficacy of CN. Since C3 and C5 shared similar transcriptional profiles but differed in their responses to CN treatment, DEG analysis was performed between C3 and C5. Immune‐promoting genes, including GZMA, GZMB, GNLY, CKLF, and FABP1, were upregulated in the C5 cluster, whereas immunosuppressive genes, including FOS, JD1, and DUSP1, were downregulated (Figure 4H,I, C5: response; C3: non‐response).

ssGSEA‐based pathway analyses defined the transcriptional profiles of T cell subclusters. C5 subcluster elevated cell cycle‐associated signatures, metabolism‐associated signatures, and inflammation‐associated signatures. The C5 subcluster is a metabolically active effector and/or an exhausted population with proliferative activity. The C3 cluster, conversely, elevated stress‐response gene signatures, suggesting negative regulation of the cell cycle under stress conditions. The C1 subcluster showed high scores for antioxidant response‐associated gene signatures, suggesting a stress‐adapted population with activated antioxidant responses in the TMEs (Figure 4J). In the module scores for exhaustion and cytotoxicity, the C3 and C5 clusters showed upregulated exhaustion scores compared to the other clusters, whereas C3 and C6 showed downregulated cytotoxicity scores Figure S8E, F.

Consistent with the cell–cell interaction (CCI) analysis, which identified CD8+ NKT‐like cells as signal senders on the XCL1–XCR1 axis (Figure 3I), gene expression profiling revealed that XCL1 was expressed in effector CD8+ T and NK cell clusters. Among these, NK cells exhibited higher expression levels and a greater proportion of XCL1+ cells, indicating that they may contribute more prominently than CD8+ T cells to the XCL1–XCR1 signaling axis (Figure 4K). Within the effector CD8+ T cell cluster, XCL1 and XCL2 were expressed in the C4 and C6 subclusters, representing terminally differentiated mature or early effector/quiescent populations Figure S8G, implying a potential role for these subclusters in cDC1 recruitment within GC TMEs.

Chemo‐Immunotherapy Enriches NK Cells With Reduced Cytotoxicity

Single‐cell analysis identified five transcriptionally distinct NK subclusters: CXCR4+ (C0), LINC02446+TNIP3+TRAT1+ (C1), LAIR2+ (C2), MYOM2+PTGDS+CX3CR1+ (C3), and KIT+AFF3+DLL1+ (C4) Figure 5A–C, Figure S9A. Based on DEGs, C0, C2, and C3 were defined as high‐cytotoxicity NK cells (HC‐NK) expressing effector molecules (GNLY, GZMB, and PRF1), while C1 and C4 were defined as reduced‐cytotoxicity NK cells (RC‐NK), characterized by lower expression of these effector genes (Figure 5A–C) [36, 37]. The ssGSEA analysis revealed that C0 and C2 were relatively quiescent in immunological activity compared with the other clusters, whereas C0 showed modest upregulation of the IFN‐γ signaling signature (Figure 5D). The RC‐NK clusters, C1 and C4, upregulated inflammatory‐related signatures: IFN‐γ and TCR signaling signatures were enriched in C1, and mTORC1, cytokine, and IFN‐signaling signatures were in C4. The C3 cluster upregulated the gene signatures of immunoregulatory interactions and adaptive immune signaling. A comparison between the HC‐NK clusters (C0, C2, and C3) and RC‐NK clusters (C1 and C4) revealed that immune‐active gene signatures, including cytokine, interferon, and adaptive immune signaling, and immunoregulatory interaction, were upregulated in the RC‐NK clusters, suggesting their potential role in antitumor immunity in the gastric TME rather than direct cytotoxicity (Figure 5E).

(M) Violin plots of DEGs between C1 and other NK cell subclusters. (N) Dot plot showing ssGSEA scores for molecular mechanisms related to metabolism and cytokine signaling across subclusters. (O) Kaplan–Meier plot showing overall survival of stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) patients from the TCGA database, stratified by the top 150 DEGs identified in C1 and C4 clusters. Error bars represent 95% confidence intervals.

Pseudotime analysis, with the immune‐inactive C2 cluster set as the root, revealed a transcriptional trajectory from C2 through immune‐inactive HC‐NK clusters (C3, C0) toward immune‐active RC‐NK clusters (C4, C1) (Figure 5F,G). The transition from HC‐NK to RC‐NK cells was enhanced by CN treatment, indicating that PD‐1 blockade facilitates the functional “re‐education” of NK cells within the GC TME. We evaluated the distribution of NK cell subclusters across the treatment groups and their response statuses (Figure 5H,I). RC‐NK clusters (C1 and C4) were enriched in the CN group; C1 was most enriched in responders within CN (red bar in CN_R) (Figure 5J,K). In contrast, the HC‐NK clusters (C0 and C2) were enriched in NR of the C and CT groups (C_NR and CT_NR, respectively) and in the TN group. The C3 cluster accumulated in the R of group C (C_R).

We characterized the C1 sub‐cluster through DEG analyses, comparing C1 with other NK clusters, as C1 was abundantly distributed in the CN_R group. In the volcano plots, the C1 subcluster showed upregulation of KIR2DL4, KLRC2, IL32, and GZMK, suggesting increased cytokine responsiveness and activation Figure 5L, Figure S9B. Notably, since RC‐NK cells expressed PDCD1, they could be direct targets of nivolumab. In contrast, low FCGR3A (CD16) expression in RC‐NK cells suggests their limited involvement in antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) (Figure 5M) [38, 39]. In the metabolic pathways, the RC‐NK clusters (C1 and C4) upregulated selenoamino acid and amino acid‐derivative metabolism, suggesting enhanced oxidative stress resistance and metabolic efficiency in nutrient‐limited TMEs (Figure 5N). The XCL1/2–XCR1 axis was also evaluated by the CellChat analysis between cDC1s and NK or effector CD8+ T cells Figure S9C, demonstrating significant communication (p < 0.01) across NK clusters, especially the strongest interactions in RC‐NK (C1 and C4) through the XCL1/2–XCR1 axis. Consistently, XCL1, XCL2, and another cDC1‐recruiting chemokine, CCL5, were highly expressed in these clusters Figure S9D. The contribution of effector CD8+ T (C6), however, appeared to be limited. These findings suggest that RC‐NK cells are the primary contributors to cDC1 recruitment in the GC TME.

The impact of RC‐NK signatures on clinical outcomes was also evaluated with TCGA datasets across multiple cancer types: Stomach (STAD), rectal (READ), colorectal (COAD), lung (LUAD), head and neck squamous cell (HNSC), pancreatic (PAAD), breast (BRCA), liver hepatocellular (LIHC), lung squamous (LUSC), and kidney renal clear cell (KIRC), as well as an integrated “all cancers” cohort. Patients were stratified into high and low RC‐NK signature (C1/C4 signature) score groups by cross‐validated concordance index‐based cutoffs. In the STAD cohort, patients with high scores exhibited improved overall survival (Figure 5O). Forest plot analysis confirmed that higher RC‐NK high scores were correlated with lower hazard ratios, specifically in the STAD (n = 314) and READ (n = 130) cohorts. In contrast, higher RC‐NK scores were associated with poor prognosis in the KIRC (n = 517) and LUSC (n = 422) cohorts. To further confirm these findings, we also used external bulk RNA‐seq cohorts of patients in treatment with anti‐PD‐1 therapy Tables S1 and S2 [40]. In the GC cohort (n = 44) [41], the RC‐NK signature‐high group exhibited a significantly higher objective response rate (ORR) (p = 0.0157, Odds Ratio = 7.93) Figure S10A. In contrast, an integrated NSCLC cohort of 2 independent studies (n = 46) [42, 43] showed no significant correlation between the RC‐NK signature and treatment response, suggesting that the RC‐NK‐mediated axis may be involved in antitumor immune response in a GC‐specific manner. Finally, we determined RC‐NK‐like cells in normal tissue data using the CellxGene database. RC‐NK signature score across 10,000 randomly sampled cells per tissue revealed RC‐NK‐like cells were more abundant in mucosal organs such as the stomach and colon Figure S10B. These findings suggest that their constitutive mucosal presence underlies the GC‐specific association between the RC‐NK signature and favorable prognosis.

Discussion

This study comprehensively characterized the cellular ecosystems of the GC TMEs using scRNA‐seq of endoscopically obtained microbiopsies across distinct therapeutic contexts (TN, C, CN, and CT). We gained insights into the drug‐induced remodeling of malignant and immune compartments within GC TMEs. Similar to previous studies [44], our data demonstrated that CN treatment induced a markedly inflamed GC TME, transcriptionally distinct from the TN, C, and CT. Our study provides a high‐resolution TME landscape of dynamic remodeling in distinct therapeutic contexts, which is challenging to capture using conventional bulk or surgical sample‐based analyses. Notably, our micro‐biopsy‐based scRNA‐seq yielded enriched immune cells, whereas stromal and malignant populations were relatively underrepresented (Figure 1B,C) [23, 24]. While the exact reasons are unclear, this distribution likely reflects a technical tendency inherent to small‐volume biopsy processing [23]. This enriched cell composition facilitated robust sub‐clustering, providing the high resolution for the detailed cell‐to‐cell interaction analyses.

A key feature of CN TMEs is the transcriptional reprogramming of MCs toward an antigen‐presenting phenotype. MCs upregulate inflammation‐related pathways, accompanied by increased HLA class I and II molecules, suggesting enhanced antigen presentation and immune recognition. This aligns with the established concept that interferon signaling enhances tumor immunogenicity by promoting MHC expression and antigen‐processing machinery [45], facilitating effective T‐cell priming and recognition in the TME.

We also observed enhanced crosstalk between cDC1, CD8+ T, and NK cells in the reprogrammed GC TME after CN treatment. The cDC1 subcluster in CN TMEs upregulated canonical markers (CLEC9A, XCR1, and BATF3) and costimulatory and chemokine molecules (such as CD80, CD86, CXCL9, and CXCL10), consistent with roles in antigen cross‐presentation and T‐cell priming [46, 47]. The XCL1–XCR1 axis between NK/CD8+ T cells and cDC1 cells was markedly enriched in CN TMEs, similar to reports showing that NK‐derived XCL1 and XCL2 recruit and sustain cDC1 populations [48]. The enrichment of LR interactions between cDC1 and T/NK cells, together with enhanced IFNγ signaling between tumor and immune compartments, indicates a coordinated activation circuit that amplifies cytotoxic responses in the CN TME. The CN TME increased activated CD8+ effector T cells expressing GZMB, PRF1, and IFNG, hallmarks of productive antitumor immunity.

The NK cell compartment exhibited remodeling under CN conditions, with an expansion of RC‐NK subsets expressing chemokines (e.g., CCL5 and XCL1/2) (Figure 5C). Further mechanistic analyses need to be conducted to explain how RC‐NK cells are expanded following PD‐1 blockade specificity in GC. Since RC‐NK‐like cells are inherently more prevalent in mucosal organs, their constitutive mucosal presence underlies the GC‐specific association between the RC‐NK signature and favorable prognosis. In addition, these cells specifically express PDCD1, serving as direct targets for nivolumab, and their metabolic fitness also facilitates selective survival and expansion in TME‐specific functional “re‐education”.

Our findings also provide mechanistic insights into combination therapies using ADCC‐inducing agents such as trastuzumab. Although RC‐NK cells exhibited low FCGR3A (CD16) expression and were unlikely to serve as primary ADCC effectors in our study, this does not negate the clinical utility of the combination. Instead, the efficacy of PD‐1 inhibitors plus trastuzumab in gastric cancer may be attributed to complementary mechanisms. Specifically, potent ADCC mediated by macrophages and canonical CD16+ NK cells may exert synergistic effects alongside the PD‐1 blockade‐mediated reactivation of T cells and RC‐NK cells. These NK subclusters interact with cDC1s to promote T cell‐oriented immune activation via IL‐12 and IFNγ signaling [48], suggesting that the CN TME fosters a cross‐regulatory DC–T–NK axis. These findings support a model in which chemo‐immunotherapy reactivates exhausted T cells and orchestrates broader immunological reprogramming involving antigen‐presenting tumor cells, cross‐priming cDC1s, and cytotoxic T and NK cells, establishing a self‐reinforcing inflammatory circuit (Figure 6).

Graphical Abstract: Remodeling in Gastric Cancer During Chemotherapy + Nivolumab Therapy. Chemotherapy combined with nivolumab reshapes the gastric cancer tumor microenvironment (TME) and is accompanied by an enrichment of reduced‐cytotoxicity NK (RC‐NK) cells. Rather than acting as classical cytotoxic effectors, these RC‐NK cells appear to shape the TME by promoting an immunostimulatory circuit that engages adaptive immunity. Notably, the RC‐NK transcriptional signature is associated with favorable patient survival, suggesting that this population plays an important role in antitumor immunity.

Although this study provides novel insights into the cross‐regulatory DC–T–NK axis, several experimental limitations should be considered. The sample size was small, preventing statistical validation, and the cross‐sectional design limited the ability to infer temporal dynamics. Comparisons were made across different patients rather than longitudinally within the same patient, and sampling times varied. We cannot rule out the influence of interpatient tumor heterogeneity on the results; nevertheless, comprehensive TME analyses provide valuable biological insights and generate hypotheses for future studies. Future studies integrating spatial transcriptomics and longitudinal sampling are essential to confirm spatial coordination and causal hierarchy among cDC1, NK, and CD8+ T cell subsets. Functional validation of RC‐NK cell subsets and their XCL1‐mediated recruitment of cDC1 in experimental models is critical for establishing their therapeutic relevance.

In conclusion, our study provides an understanding of the immune‐activated GC TME ecosystem shaped by chemoimmunotherapy. The concurrent enhancement of tumor antigenicity, cDC1‐mediated T cell priming, and NK‐DC‐T cell crosstalk offers mechanistic insights into the clinical efficacy of CN therapy. This highlights the potential of these immune‐interactive nodes as biomarkers or targets to optimize combination immunotherapy for GC.
