Increased functional connectivity of amygdalar-frontal pathways in patients with alcohol use disorder and childhood trauma
Authors: Elisabeth Jehli, Fabienne Burri, Niklaus Denier, Franz Moggi, Andrea Federspiel, Roland Wiest, Maria Stein, Tobias Bracht, Matthias Grieder, Leila M Soravia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildhood trauma (CT) often co-occurs with alcohol use disorder (AUD) and is associated with poor treatment outcome. We could demonstrated that patients with AUD and a history of childhood trauma showed reduced structural connectivity of the amygdala. Here, we additionally aimed to investigate fronto-limbic functional connectivity (FC) in patients with AUD with (AUD-CT) and without (AUD-noCT) CT. Based on findings in CT, we hypothesized reduced FC of the amygdala with the prefrontal cortex in AUD-CT and worse treatment outcome compared to AUD-noCT.Resting state fMRI scans were acquired in abstinent inpatients with AUD and healthy controls (HCs). We compared bilateral amygdala FC between AUD-CT (n = 21), AUD-noCT (n = 22), and HC (n = 27) using seed-based connectivity (SBC) and region-of-interest to region-of-interest (ROI-ROI) analysis. Sociodemographic and alcohol-specific variables (percent days abstinent, PDA) were assessed at treatment admission and three-month follow-up. The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) assessed trauma severity. SBC analysis revealed that AUD-CT showed increased FC of the left and right amygdala with the medial prefrontal cortex and left paracingulate gyrus compared to HC. AUD-CT showed increased ROI-ROI FC of the left with the right amygdala and the right amygdala with the medial prefrontal cortex. Moreover, AUD-CT exhibited a greater reduction of PDA at three-month follow-up compared to AUD-noCT.Increased FC of the amygdala, the medial prefrontal cortex, and paracingulate gyrus in AUD-CT might be a compensatory adaption to the reduced structural connectivity of the amygdala. Those specific alterations of FC in AUD-CT may represent a distinguishable neurobiological subtype of AUD, possibly underlying the complex clinical picture and worse treatment outcome.Keywords: Alcohol use disorder, Amygdala, Childhood trauma, Functional connectivity, Resting state fMRI

Introduction

Childhood trauma (CT) is a risk factor for several psychiatric disorders, including AUD (Anda et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2020), with child maltreatment such as physical and emotional abuse or neglect and or sexual abuse being the most common source (Hodgdon et al., 2019). The prevalence of CT in individuals with AUD is distinctively high, with values up to 55% (Huang et al., 2012) compared with 10% in the general population (Stoltenborgh et al., 2011). A history of CT is associated with an earlier onset of AUD (Schwandt et al., 2013), more comorbidities, and worse treatment outcome (Huang et al., 2012; Greenfield et al., 2002). More than half of the children experiencing some form of trauma report two or more types of adversity (Huang et al., 2012). Thus, the majority of maltreated children experience polyvictimization, which shows a graded relationship with the risk for psychopathology, especially for the development of substance use disorders (Anda et al., 2006). Behaviors such as smoking, alcohol, or drug abuse might be used consciously or unconsciously as they have an immediate pharmacological or psychological benefit in managing the stress of abuse, domestic violence, or other forms of family and household dysfunction (Felitti et al., 1998). Therefore, such risk behavior is often seen as a maladaptive emotion regulation strategy.

There is evidence that CT and AUD are associated with aberrant connectivity on a structural and functional level of brain regions involved in the processing and regulation of emotions. Reduced volumes of hippocampus, amygdala, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and anterior cingulate cortex were repeatedly found in cohorts with a history of CT (Cotter et al., 2001; Zaremba et al., 2018) as well as AUD (Tolomeo and Yu, 2022). We could previously show that patients with AUD and moderate to severe CT showed reduced structural connectivity of pathways projecting to and from the amygdala compared to AUD patients without CT (Soravia et al., 2022). The functional connectivity (FC) of the amygdala, as part of the fronto-limbic network, represents an intrinsic connectivity network involved in detecting, integrating, and filtering relevant interoceptive, autonomic, and emotional information, thus relevant for threat detection and response circuits (Seeley et al., 2007). The prefrontal cortex, on the other hand, is the central hub of executive control functions, including behavioral inhibition (Bari and Robbins, 2013; Lopez et al., 2020), and can be sub-divided into the medial prefrontal cortex (MPFC), dorsolateral PFC (dlPFC), and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC). As the amygdala shares extensive connections with the prefrontal cortex, they present central structures that process and regulate emotional stimuli. Enhanced amygdala response to emotional faces, particularly those seen as threatening or more salient, is the most consistent functional imaging finding observed in maltreated individuals during childhood and adulthood with and without psychopathology (Tottenham et al., 2011; Garrett et al., 2012; Dannlowski et al., 2012). These findings suggest that adaptation to childhood adversity is associated with augmentation of prefrontal-subcortical circuits specifically for negative emotional stimuli and threat detection (McLaughlin et al., 2015; Jenness et al., 2021; Herringa et al., 2016).

However, the literature yields mixed results regarding the direction of changes in FC of the amygdala with frontal regions in relation to CT. While most studies report a reduced FC of the MPFC with the amygdala, suggesting a dysfunctional top-down regulation (Teicher et al., 2016; Goltermann et al., 2022; Heyn et al., 2019; Cisler, 2017), a study with a sample of patients with major depressive disorder showed that maltreatment was associated with increased FC of the amygdala with the dorsolateral PFC (Goltermann et al., 2022). Comparable to the findings in CT, a decreased FC of the amygdala with the OFC has been discussed as a possible neural risk marker for the development of AUD (Crane et al., 2018; Peters et al., 2015, 2017; Hu et al., 2018). The OFC is crucially involved in reward processing, decision-making, and emotion regulation (Rolls, 2004), and it exerts top-down inhibitory control on the amygdala, which processes emotional information. Thus, a reduced top-down regulation is associated with reduced emotion regulation and impaired goal-directed behavior (Casey et al., 2008; Lee et al., 2012), repeatedly shown in AUD (Heinz et al., 2019; Galandra et al., 2019; Ghin et al., 2022). Another study using whole-brain analysis found that the amygdala-temporal fusiform cortex FC was related to AUD severity (Dean et al., 2020), highlighting the crucial role of amygdala connectivity in AUD.

AUD and CT often co-occur and may represent a subtype that differs regarding onset, progression, and treatment outcome of AUD (Huang et al., 2012; Greenfield et al., 2002; Schäfer et al., 2010), as well as recently shown regarding the structural connectivity of the amygdala (Soravia et al., 2022). While most findings yielded a reduced FC of the amygdala with the PFC in either AUD and CT, to our knowledge, this has not been investigated in patients with AUD with co-occurring CT so far. Thus, this study aimed to compare resting-state FC of recently abstinent AUD patients with and without CT using fMRI. According to our previous finding of reduced structural connectivity in AUD patients with CT, we hypothesize lower FC between the amygdala, the prefrontal (PFC), and orbitofrontal (OFC) cortex in patients with AUD and a history of moderate to severe CT compared to AUD patients without CT and healthy controls. We further assume that AUD with additional CT is associated with higher severity of general psychopathology, and poorer treatment outcome, the latter measured by percent days abstinent at the three-month follow-up.

As CT is an additional complicating aspect of AUD, differentiating these effects would contribute to a better understanding of the neural and behavioral correlates of this comorbidity, which, in turn, may help develop better treatment strategies for this high-risk population.

Materials and methods

Participants

All 49 patients were attending a twelve-week abstinence-oriented residential treatment program for AUD in a specialized treatment center in Switzerland (Clinic Suedhang). Inclusion criteria were 18–65 years of age, primary diagnosis of AUD according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5 (Association, 2013);), right-handedness, and abstinence from alcohol for at least four weeks prior to MRI measurement. Exclusion criteria were other severe substance use disorders (except nicotine; Drug Use Identification Test DUDIT ≥25 per substance (Berman et al., 2005),), current medical conditions preventing participation (e.g., acute infectious disease), diagnosed neurocognitive disorders (e.g., Korsakoff syndrome), contraindications to perform an MRI or inability to read and understand the participant's information. A control group was recruited, including 27 right-handed healthy adults. Low scores of psychopathology (Brief Symptom Check List, BSCL (Derogatis, 1993; Franke, 2000) GSIt-value ≤ 63) as well as non-problematic drinking behavior (Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Tests, AUDIT (Babor et al., 2001) < 8; Alcohol Use Disorder Scale, AUD-S (Wechsler et al., 1994) < 2) were inclusion criteria. Exclusion criteria were current or past diagnosis of substance use or disorder (Drug Use Disorders Identification Test, DUDIT (Voluse et al., 2012) (Voluse et al., 2012) < 8 per substance, except nicotine), current psychiatric diagnosis or treatment, and other neurocognitive complications. All participants provided written informed consent and received a reimbursement of 50 Swiss Francs for participation. The main study was approved by the local ethics committee (KEK-number: 2016-00988) and registered at Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT02968537) and the Swiss National Clinical Trials Portal (SNCTP000002043). MRI was usually performed by the end of the second week or at the beginning of the third week of the inpatient stay. The duration of the inpatient stay typically varied around eight to 12 weeks, depending on individual and institutional factors. However, the follow-up assessments including questionnaires and interviews took place three months after discharge of inpatient treatment and consisted of a questionnaire battery and a telephone interview. For more details on the procedure, tasks, materials, and questionnaires used in the main study, see Tschuemperlin et al. (2019). Five participants had to be excluded from the analysis due to technical problems during the MR scan, which led to incomplete data. One patient had to be excluded prior to FC data analysis due to strong cortical abnormalities. Therefore, the final patient sample comprised 43 patients (16 females, 27 males) with an average duration of problematic alcohol consumption of 13.8 years (SD 11.4) and an average age of 43.1 years (SD 9.3). Questionnaires and interviews

AUD diagnosis was verified using the Diagnostic Expert System for Psychiatric Disorders (DIA-X; (Wittchen and Pfister, 1997), the AUD part adapted to DSM-5) and the self-rating Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) to estimate the severity of drinking problems (Saunders et al., 1993). Alongside alcohol-specific variables, depressive symptoms were assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II (Beck et al., 2006);), and general clinically significant symptoms were assessed by the global severity index (GSI) of the Brief Symptom Check List (BSCL (Franke, 2017);). Post-traumatic stress symptoms were determined using the PTSD Screening Scale (PSS (Foa et al., 1997),), a self-report questionnaire to screen for posttraumatic stress disorder. A total score above 13 suggests a possibility of PTSD.

Assessment of childhood trauma

The participants were screened for a history of CT with the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, short-form (CTQ-SF (Bernstein et al., 2003);). The CTQ-SF is a screening tool of 28 items to assess a history of five aspects of childhood maltreatment: physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, physical neglect, and emotional neglect. These dimensions are represented by five items, each rating the frequency of maltreatment experiences on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (never true) to 5 (very often true). The classification of severity of experienced maltreatment ranges from none to low, low to moderate, moderate to severe, and severe to extreme exposure for each dimension (Häuser et al., 2011). The total score reflects the severity of overall trauma exposure. Patients who experienced moderate to extreme emotional, physical, or sexual abuse (CTQ subscales) during childhood were allocated to the AUD-CT group (n = 21.). In contrast, patients who reported none to low maltreatment in these dimensions were allocated to the AUD-noCT group (n = 22) (see Table 2, supplements Table S2).

Assessment of alcohol consumption

Assessment of alcohol consumption was assessed at baseline (assessing drinking in the 90 days prior to detoxification entry) and three-month follow-up (assessing drinking in the 90 days following treatment discharge) with the TLFB interview (Sobell and Sobell, 1995). From TLFB data, the percentage of days abstinent (PDA) was computed for baseline and three-month follow-up after correction for days spent in a protected environment (i.e., a residential treatment center or a somatic hospital). The reduction in PDA (ΔPDA, computed as PDA(3-month follow up) – PDA(Baseline)) was used as in those resting state fMRI analyses, which investigated the relation between the neurophysiology of alcohol-specific inhibition and drinking outcomes (Grieder et al., 2022).

MRI-data acquisition

MRI data were acquired at the Department of Diagnostic and Interventional Neuroradiology, University Hospital of Bern, with a 3 T S MAGNETOM Prisma scanner and a 64-channel head/neck coil. For high-resolution T1-weighted structural images, we used a bias-field corrected MP2RAGE sequence with the following parameters: 256 slices, field of view (FoV) = 256 × 256 matrix, voxel size = 1 mm3, repetition time (TR) = 5000 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.98 ms, inversion time T1 = 700 ms and T2 = 2500 ms. The MP2RAGE sequence generated two gradient echo images (INV1 and INV2) and a T1-weighted image (UNI).

Functional MRI (fMRI) resting state data was acquired using an EPI sequence with the following parameters: 60 slices, a field of view (FoV) with a 230 × 230, voxel size = 2.2 mm3, repetition time (TR) = 1300 ms and an echo time (TE) = 37.0 ms.

Resting state fMRI data preprocessing

Resting state fMRI data was preprocessed using the default preprocessing pipeline of the CONN functional connectivity toolbox (Nieto-Castanon and Whitfield-Gabrieli, 2021). Images were first realigned and unwrapped, followed by a slice-timing correction. The slice order was extracted from the functional JSON metadata. For functional outlier detection, intermediate settings (97th percentile in normative sample) were used. Functional and anatomical data were then segmented and normalized to MNI coordinates. Last, smoothing was performed using a Gaussian kernel of 8 mm full width half maximum (FWHM). Subsequently, scrubbing for motion and outliers correction, and denoising were done using a high-pass filter of 0.006 Hz and linear regression. Subsequently, denoising was done using a high-pass filter (0.006 Hz) and linear regression.

Computation and analysis of seed-based connectivity (SBC) and region-of-interest to region-of-interest (ROI-ROI) of functional resting state data

To investigate the temporal correlation in fMRI signal changes between spatially segregated brain regions, individual connectivity images were computed in the first step (1st level), whereas these images were statistically compared between the participant groups in the second step (2nd level).

1st level analysis

First level analysis was done using the “atlas” regions of interest (ROIs) including amygdala, hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, OFC, thalamus, striatum (caudate, putamen and pallidum), as well as salience (anterior insula, rostral PFC (RPFC), supramarginal gyrus and anterior cingulate cortex) and default mode (MPFC, posterior cingulate cortex and lateral parietal region) networks of the CONN toolbox. Subject-specific connectivity maps of the 1st level analysis were further analyzed in a 2nd level analysis comparing AUD-CT vs AUD-noCT (and AUD patients vs healthy controls (HC)) resting state connectivity data and in regression with covariates of interest (CTQ score) correcting for age and sex.

2nd level SBC analysis

First, to assess the hypothesis of altered FC in AUD-CT, a whole brain SBC analysis with the before mentioned ROIs as seed was calculated. 2nd group analysis of SBC between AUD and HC was performed with a general linear model for each predefined seed (ROI maps of 1st level) in regression with covariates of interest (CTQ score) correcting for age and sex. All effects were evaluated using a peak-voxel FWE-corrected p-value of 0.05. Significant clusters were converted into a binary mask, which was used to extract mean FC strength of subjects in all three groups (AUD-CT, AUD-nCT, HC). Extracted FC values between AUD-CT, AUD-nCT and HC were used for statistical comparison. Additionally, FC strengths of significant clusters of the SBC analysis were used to investigate the association with CTQ score, by applying the linear model fitting method in the ggplot2 package in R (R core Team, 2022; Wickham, 2009).

2nd level ROI-ROI analysis

Second, a ROI-ROI analysis to investigate FC alterations between the ROIs that included bilateral amygdala, salience, and default mode networks in AUD-CT was performed. Second level analysis of ROI-ROI resting state FC was done between AUD and HC and between AUD-CT and AUD-noCT.

All effects were evaluated using a cluster-level FDR-corrected p-value of 0.05.

Statistical analyses

Demographics and clinical variables between the two patient groups and HC were compared using T-tests for normally distributed variables, non-parametric tests for non-normally distributed variables, or χ2 tests for dichotomous variables. Neural data were analyzed with a 2 × 3 repeated measures ANCOVA with variables hemisphere (left/right) and group (AUD-CT/AUD-noCT/HC), corrected for age and sex, using Statistical Package for Social Sciences SPSS 27.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, Illinois).

All tests were two-tailed, and a probability of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (η2 = 0.01 small effect, η2 = 0.06 medium effect, η2 = 0.14 large effect) for interactions of FC with group variables, and Cohen's d (d = 0.2 small effect, d = 0.5 medium effect, d = 0.8 large effect) for between-subject tests. The effect of CT on treatment outcome was assessed by computing non-parametric ANOVA-type statistics using the nparLD package in R (Noguchi et al., 2012) with the dependent variable percent days abstinent (PDA) and the independent within-subject variable time (baseline; three-months follow-up (3mo FU)) and between-subject variable CT (CT; noCT).

Results

Study population

Patients with AUD differed in almost all sociodemographic and clinical variables from HC, as shown in Table 1. However, the two patient groups (AUD-CT and AUD-noCT) did not differ regarding sociodemographic or clinical variables except for significantly more PDA, higher scores in the global severity index of the BSCL (BSCL GSI), and higher CTQ total score in the AUD-CT group compared to AUD-noCT (see Table 2) and HC (supplements Table S2).

SBC analysis

AUD vs. HC

AUD patients with a higher CTQ total score showed increased FC of the left and right amygdala with a cluster comprising the MPFC and left paracingulate gyrus (F [2, 40] = 23.42; p-FWE = 0.018) compared to resting state fMRI data of healthy controls in the same clusters (see Fig. 1A and 1B and Supplements Table 1). The repeated measures ANCOVA revealed a significant group × hemisphere interaction effect (F [2, 65] = 4.3078, p = 0.018, η2 = 0.117). Post-hoc tests were conducted to calculate the correlation between CTQ scores and FC strengths for the left and right amygdala separately. AUD-CT patients showed increased FC of the left amygdala with the cluster including the MPFC and left paracingulate gyrus (AUD-CT vs. AUD-noCT (T [41] = 3.03, p = 0.004, d = 0.924; AUD-CT vs. HC (T [46] = 2.15; p = 0.037, d = 0.626) but not of the right amygdala with the MPFC and left paracingulate gyrus (AUD-CT vs. AUD-noCT: (T [41] = 1.09; p = 0.282, d = 0.333; AUD-CT vs. HC: (T [46] = 0.450; p = 0.655, d = 0.131) (see Fig. 1). AUD-noCT did not differ in any FC from HC (FC right: p = 0.527; FC left: p = 0.439). While AUD-noCT and HC FC did not differ between hemispheres (AUD-noCT: p = 0.906; HC: p = 0.624), AUD-CT showed significantly higher FC of the left amygdala with the MPFC compared to the right FC (T [20] = 2.916, p = 0.009, d = 0.636) (Fig. 1C).

The effect of childhood trauma on treatment outcome

The results of the nonparametric ANOVA revealed that AUD patients with CT showed worse treatment outcome measured by the percentage of days abstinent (PDA) compared to AUD patients without CT (interaction time x CT; ANOVA-type-statistics (ATS)[1] = 5.21, p = 0.023). Moreover, there was a main effect of time (ATS[1] = 133.97, p < 0.001), indicating that both groups benefit from specialized inpatient treatment program; AUD-CT to a lower degree than AUD-noCT (CT: ATS[1] = 34.21, p < 0.001; noCT: ATS[1] = 130.05, p < 0.001).

Discussion

The present study aimed to examine the effect of moderate to severe CT in patients with AUD on the FC in the amygdalar-frontal pathways and its association with treatment outcome at a three-month follow-up after inpatient treatment program.

Patients AUD and a history of moderate to severe CT showed increased ROI-ROI FC of the left with the right amygdala as well as the right amygdala with the MPFC compared to AUD patients without CT. This finding was further supported by the SBC analysis showing an association between the severity of CT and increased FC of the left and right amygdala with a cluster of the PFC comprising the MPFC and left paracingulate gyrus in patients with AUD compared to healthy controls. Post-hoc tests demonstrated that this effect was driven by a significantly increased FC of the left amygdala with frontal regions but not the right amygdala with frontal regions in the AUD-CT group compared to AUD-noCT and HC. Even though our finding of increased FC of the amygdala with the MPFC and paracingulate gyrus is not consistent with our hypothesis and most findings in CT, it is not contradictory yet rather novel and of interest, as it may reflect a compensatory adaptation to the reduced structural connectivity in AUD patients with CT.

Extensive evidence reports hemispheric asymmetries of the amygdala. An informative overview of the lateralization of amygdala function and structure by Ocklenburg and colleagues (Ocklenburg et al., 2022) shows that the right amygdala is dominant for processing unconsciously presented stimuli, automated emotion induction without explicit reflection processes, and short-duration responses. The left amygdala is dominant for more sustained long-time responses for cognitive and intentional mood regulation processes. Cognitive and intentional mood regulation may require more time than automated emotion induction without reflection processes.

Independent of the amygdala lateralization, the amygdala is an essential brain region for emotion generation and processing, while the prefrontal cortex is an emotion-regulatory region. The most consistent neuroimaging finding in individuals with a history of CT is an increased amygdala response to salient stimuli such as emotional faces (Tottenham et al., 2011; Bogdan et al., 2012), and many studies reported an impaired top-down regulation from the prefrontal cortex on limbic structures (Teicher et al., 2016; Goltermann et al., 2022; Heyn et al., 2019; Cisler, 2017). The enhanced threat detection and response might be an adaptive reaction to maltreatment and may help individuals avoid threats, but it also sensitizes individuals to subsequent stressors and increases the risk of pathology (Teicher et al., 2016). However, studies suggest that subjects with CT seem to be able to down-regulate the amygdala after negative affect to the same extent as subjects without CT, but they use regions involved in effortful control to a greater degree to do so. Thus, the increased FC of the left and right amygdala with the MPFC in AUD patients with CT might reflect the greater effort required to modulate heightened amygdala responses (McLaughlin et al., 2015). It may further be a compensation for the previously found reduced possibly developmental-related structural connectivity emanating from the amygdala in the AUD-CT group compared to the AUD-noCT group (Soravia et al., 2022). Furthermore, a review of the neural circuitry in substance use disorders suggests that the emotion regulation disturbance in this population might rather be related to impairments of prefrontal functioning to down-regulate amygdala activity than from increased amygdala reactivity to emotional stimuli (Wilcox et al., 2016). Thus, patients with AUD and additional CT might compensate for this impairment with increased FC of the MPFC with the left and right amygdala to regulate amygdala hypersensitivity.

In line with previous research, our findings showed that almost half of the patients with AUD experienced moderate to extreme trauma exposure during childhood (Huang et al., 2012; Schwandt et al., 2013; Greenfield et al., 2002). Sexual, physical, and emotional abuse, as well as physical and emotional neglect, often co-occur within individuals and show a dose-response relationship between the number and severity of childhood trauma types and the severity of AUD, worse treatment outcome, and increased risk of psychiatric comorbidities (Huang et al., 2012). This is backed up by our results showing that patients with AUD and CT showed worse treatment outcome shown in fewer percent days abstinent at the three-month follow-up after residential treatment (Huang et al., 2012; Schwandt et al., 2013; Greenfield et al., 2002). Furthermore, the AUD-CT group scored higher on the severity of general psychopathology, showed a tendency towards more depressive symptoms and more years of problematic drinking, which confirms the higher risk for psychiatric comorbidities and the more severe psychological burden and worse treatment outcome of this patient group (Huang et al., 2012). Thus, according to the high prevalence of CT in this patient population and the evidence of its association with poor treatment outcome, the history of CT should be systematically assessed in patients with AUD to tailor evidence-based interventions, such as specific trauma-focused psychotherapy for this high-risk population as proposed above.

This study has some limitations that need to be addressed. First, CT was only assessed with one questionnaire; thus, recall bias may affect the reporting of abuse incidents. We further did not assess the age and duration when trauma occurred, which might influence brain developmental processes. Third, although the covariate sex was not statistically significant, there are three times more male patients in the AUD-noCT group than female patients, while in the AUD-CT group, the sex ratio is equally distributed. However, we included sex as a covariate in all analyses.

Furthermore, the variables age, education, relationship, and employment status differed significantly between HC and AUD and might have confounded FC group effects. For example, no FC differences between AUD-noCT and HC were found in the SBC analysis. This was unexpected and might be related to confounds of uncontrolled variables, even though age and sex were included in the ANCOVA as covariates. Nevertheless, according to Miller and Chapman (2001), covariates might not eliminate confounding effects, a circumstance that should be considered when interpreting the results of this study.

Finally, the sample size was rather small. Additionally, our HC group reported no CT, thus, an HC group with CT should be included to verify the specificity of CT on FC.

In conclusion, our findings are in line with previous research demonstrating that CT is highly prevalent in patients with severe AUD. Furthermore, the subgroup of patients with AUD and CT did not only display a heightened higher severity of psychopathology, and worse treatment outcome but also neural alterations: the neural findings indicate that early traumatization in AUD may induce alterations in FC of the amygdala with prefrontal regions. Thus, AUD patients with CT might represent a distinguishable neurobiological subtype, which might need distinct treatment approaches. Therefore, it is of clinical relevance to assess and classify these subjects within AUD, which may contribute to a better understanding and treatment of the complex clinical picture that maltreated subjects often present.
