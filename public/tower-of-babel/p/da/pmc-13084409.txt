Death preparedness in patients with cancer: A concept analysis using Rodgers's methodology
Authors: Xi Zhang, Jingxin Wang, Xiaohong Liu, Tieying Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo analyse the concept of death preparedness in patients with cancer.MethodsRodgers' evolutionary method was used for the concept analysis. We systematically searched CNKI, Wanfang database, VIP database, CBM, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, PsycINFO online databases for studies related to death preparedness in patients with cancer. The relevant literature published between 2000 and April 2024 was thoroughly examined. Two researchers independently extracted data, with discrepancies resolved through discussion.ResultsA total of 21 articles were included, and the findings were categorized into antecedents, attributes, and consequences. Death preparedness in patients with cancer included five conceptual attributes: acceptance of death, emotional adaptation, interactive communication, expression of personal wishes, and end-of-life planning; antecedents included individual, interpersonal, and social factors; and consequences included patient-related consequences, family-related consequences, and social-related consequences.ConclusionsThis analysis elucidates the evolving characteristics of death preparedness in patients with cancer, clarifying its key attributes, antecedents, and consequences. The study provides a theoretical foundation for future research and clinical tool development. For clinical practice, this conceptual clarity can guide healthcare professionals in assessing and facilitating death preparedness, thereby improving patient-centered communication, advance care planning, and the overall quality of end-of-life care.

Introduction

With the change in lifestyle, environment, and population structure, cancer has become a major public health problem worldwide. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), cancer has become one of the leading causes of death worldwide.1 As a result, improving the quality of life at the end of life of patients with cancer has become a critical global action plan.2 According to the 2022 Global Cancer Statistics Report released by the International Agency for Research on Cancer, there were nearly 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million deaths worldwide in 2022.3

Death preparedness refers to the level of preparedness of an individual when facing death. It is regarded as a crucial prerequisite for enhancing the quality of life at the end of life of patients with cancer.4 Existing studies have found that death preparedness in patients with cancer is an important prerequisite for improving the quality of death in patients with cancer.5 The results of a qualitative study of patients with advanced breast cancer by Chunlestskul et al.6 showed that preparation for death was beneficial for patients to live a whole life and to face death calmly. Steinhauser et al.7 indicated that enhancing patients' preparedness to die was effective in improving quality of life and reducing anxiety and depression in terminally ill patients. Chan et al.8 also suggested that death preparation positively contributes to the reduction of death-related anxiety. Therefore, it is crucial for patients with cancer to prepare for death proactively and at their own pace. Such preparation can help them reduce anxiety and stress associated with death, promote a positive understanding of death, and improve the quality of end-of-life care.9,10

However, despite its suggested benefits, the concept of "death preparedness in patients with cancer" itself still lacks consensus at present. This conceptual ambiguity and inconsistency pose substantial challenges for related research, assessment, and clinical interventions. Some studies focus on the emotional acceptance component,11,12 while others emphasize practical arrangements or spiritual preparations.13 Consequently, this lack of conceptual clarity not only hinders the development of related theories but also creates practical obstacles in clinical practice, making it difficult to select effective measurement tools or design universally applicable support plans.

This study aims to elucidate the concept of death preparedness in patients with cancer, thereby providing a crucial foundation for subsequent research, tool development, and clinical practice.

Methods

Design

This study employs Rodgers' evolutionary method14 to define and clarify the concept of "death preparedness in patients with cancer". This method emphasises that concepts are dynamic and evolve over time, proving particularly suitable for research topics within nursing where no established definition exists. Identify the concept

To ensure the comprehensiveness of the literature review, a systematic examination was conducted on alternative and related concepts to the notion of "death preparedness" through preliminary searches. In the context of medical nursing, alternative concepts to "death preparedness" include “end-of-life preparedness”, “terminal preparedness”, “hospice preparedness”, “palliative preparedness”, “death readiness”, “end-of-life readiness”, and “terminal readiness”. These concepts are often used interchangeably or as substitutes in the literature. Although the expressions differ, they ultimately refer to death preparedness.

Data collection

On April 2, 2024, we searched the CNKI, Wanfang database, VIP database, CBM, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, PsycINFO online databases. In addition, we consulted dictionaries, thesauruses, and non-medical literature to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the application of the concept. We used Boolean operators and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) to search terms such as "death prepar∗," "end-of-life prepar∗," and "death readiness," as well as other possible similar terms. Throughout the entire search process, we iterated and tested repeatedly to ensure that the search strategy was the most appropriate. The search time range is from 2000 to April 2024. The PubMed database was used as an example, and the specific search strategy is shown in Additional file 1. The reference lists of the included studies were carefully reviewed, supplemented by manual searches, to prevent the omission of relevant literature. We used The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (http://www.prisma-statement.org), see Supplementary File 1.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the subjects were patients with cancer; (2) the research topic focused on death preparedness; (3) the language of publication was either Chinese or English. The exclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) the full text was not publicly available; (2) the publication type comprised grey literature, non-peer-reviewed journal articles, letters to the editor, or conference abstracts. Data extraction and analysis

Two researchers (X.Z and J.X.W) independently read through the included literature, understood the core content, and reassessed whether these studies were helpful in defining the target concept. The data analysis is based on Rodgers,15 the standard thematic analysis procedure was adopted until a coherent and comprehensive consensus was reached. For the selected literature, the research team extracted basic information such as authors, publication year, and country using an excel spreadsheet, while also recording specific details related to the attributes, antecedents, and consequences of the concept. If there were disagreements, the third researcher (T.Y.Z) was consulted until a consensus was reached. Definition of death preparedness in patients with cancer

“Death preparedness” originated from research and practice in hospice care. In 1971, Parkes discussed in detail the psychological and emotional reactions that individuals may have when faced with death in his classic book (Bereavement: Studies of Grief in Adult Life), laying the groundwork for the later development of the concept of "death preparedness".16 In 1980, Poss proposed that preparation for death would only really begin once the patient realized that death was imminent. Terminally ill patients consciously complete tasks to prepare for death before they die.17 It was not until 1995 that McCanse articulated the concept of "death preparedness" as an indicator of "healthy dying," which is considered measurable.18 In 2001, Steinhauser et al. conducted a study of death preparedness among patients, family members, and healthcare professionals to understand the different components they perceived to be included in death preparedness.7 In 2008, Chunlestskul et al. conducted a study on patients with cancer to explore their death preparedness; this included acknowledging their grief, preparing mentally, seeking information and support, preparing the family, and preparing for the end of life.6 In 2014, a conceptual analysis of death preparedness suggested it include six dimensions: communication with healthcare providers; awareness of end-of-life, acceptance of end-of-life, change in attitudes towards death; participation in end-of-life decision-making, and hospice care planning.5

Patients with cancer face the uncertainty of their disease, the challenges of treatment, and the possibility of death, and their death preparedness may differ from that of other end-of-life patients. Understanding death preparedness how patients with cancer face death, accept the reality of their disease, and prepare for death is essential to improving their mental health and enhancing the quality of end-of-life care.19,20 The concept of death preparedness in patients with cancer has not yet been unified; thus, its operational definition varies across studies. The study synthesized the existing literature on death preparedness in patients with cancer. Therefore, we combine the definition of death preparedness itself and various scholars' perspectives to define the death preparedness in patients with cancer as: the preparations made by patients with cancer in terms of cognition, emotion, social, and behavior when facing the impending death, aiming to reduce uncertainty, maintain self-efficacy. This definition offers added value over previous conceptualizations by integrating social and behavioral dimensions alongside cognitive and emotional aspects, thereby constructing a comprehensive multidimensional framework. Importantly, self-efficacy is manifested in observable behaviors such as active participation in medical decision-making, maintenance of daily autonomy, and proactive communication about end-of-life issues, which reflect patients' sense of preparedness and competence in the face of death.

Attributes of death preparedness in patients with cancer

Conceptual attributes are features, elements, or components that distinguish a concept from similar concepts and help deepen understanding and grasp of the concept.21 Through literature analysis and generalization, this study extracts the conceptual attributes of death preparedness in patients with cancer, which include (1) acceptance of death, (2) emotional adaptation, (3) interactive communication, (4) personal wishes, and (5) end-of-life planning.

Acceptance of death

For patients with cancer, acceptance of death is a complex and important process that is understood both as a dynamic psychological journey and as an eventual outcome.22 Death acceptance is a frank, open, and receptive attitude towards death, it reflects a person's positive attitude toward death.11,23, 24, 25 According to Kubler-Ross, "acceptance of death is a conscious rational acknowledgment of the prospect of one's inevitable demise accompanied by a positive emotional appraisal of that realization".26 Unfortunately, patients with cancer are often unable to accept their death.12,27 Acceptance of death is one of the core attributes of preparing for death as a cancer patient. By gradually accepting the reality of death through an ongoing process of cognitive and emotional adjustment, patients can better live with their emotions and ultimately reach a state where they can peacefully begin to process the end of life.24

Emotional adaptation

Emotional adaptation refers to a dynamic process in which patients with cancer navigate emotional fluctuations when confronting death.28 These fluctuations may include anxiety, frustration, anger, and fear. Through this process, patients seek appropriate ways to manage these emotions, thereby maintaining their psychological well-being and emotional stability.29,30 Studies have found that there is often a wide range of emotions in the face of the challenges of cancer diagnosis and treatment.6,10,11,24 Unlike general coping mechanisms, which typically target stress reduction or problem-solving, emotional adaptation here is fundamentally linked to meaning-making.4 As patients confront the finite nature of their lives, the emotional experiences they undergo, such as fear or sadness, can trigger deeper existential reflection on the meaning and value of life.5,10 This reflective process helps patients reframe their perspectives, integrate their experiences, and ultimately fosters a sense of peace, thereby better preparing them to meet the end of life.20 At the same time, emotional adjustment can help patients better communicate and build a support network with family, friends, and healthcare professionals to get through this challenging time together.25

Interactive communication

Interactive communication describes interacting, communicating, and exchanging ideas between patients with cancer, their families, friends, and healthcare professionals.30, 31, 32 Interactive communication plays a crucial role in death preparedness in patients with cancer.33 Intimate communication and interaction can help patients and their loved ones build a stronger bond and increase mutual understanding and support as they face the reality of cancer and death.34,35 Through interactive communication, patients can also express their care and appreciation for family and friends. In facing the challenges of death, patients may realize how important they are to others and want to be able to provide reflexive care for others.20,36

Expression of personal wishes

Expression of personal wishes, as one of the attributes of cancer patients' preparedness for death, influences treatment modalities, the quality of end-of-life care, and hospice care for patients with cancer at the end of life.6,30,37,38 Studies identified that the expression of a cancer patient's personal wishes to prepare for death might influence the healthcare team's and family members' decisions about treatment.39 Expressing personal wishes helps the healthcare team and family members better understand the patient's expectations for quality of end-of-life care. It may also guide hospice-related arrangements.40 The expression of personal wishes in the death preparedness of patients with cancer is of great importance to the patient. It promotes a feeling of respect and understanding and reduces anxiety and fear of death.

End-of-life planning

End-of-life planning is a crucial attribute of death preparedness in patients with cancer, where the patient develops and executes specific plans related to the end of life, including drafting a will, arrangements for medical care, distribution of the estate, etc.30,31,39 Research has shown that end-of-life planning helps patients achieve dignity and peace of mind in the final stages of their lives, reducing the burden on their families.6,25 It is also of great comfort to the patient's family, who often face great psychological stress and emotional distress at the end of a patient's life.41,42 End-of-life planning provides family members with guidance and support to help them better cope with the challenges at the end of the patient's life, as well as enabling them to manage loss and grief better after the patient has passed away.4,11

Antecedents of death preparedness in patients with cancer

According to Rodgers, antecedents are events or situations before the concept is created.21 In this study, the existing literature was summarized to classify the antecedents of death preparedness in patients with cancer into individual level factors, interpersonal level factors, and social level factors.

Individual level factors: disease characteristics, stage, diagnosis, and painful symptoms were closely associated with death preparedness in patients with cancer. Patients with advanced cancer were more likely to accept death and be prepared for death, whereas patients with some of the better healing cancers,43 such as breast cancer, showed significant differences in progression, healing, and treatment and were relatively less likely to think about death, and were more likely to pursue aggressive medical treatments.10,22 Painful symptoms of cancer would also directly affect a patient's death preparedness. Patients with severe symptoms tend to have a more realistic view, as mortality is prominent in their daily lives.44 Personal experience, death awareness, and death paradox could naturally influence a patient's preparation for death.45 It has been found that people who have experienced the death of a family member or friend have a deeper awareness and understanding of death and may face it positively, accepting it as an inevitable part of life.6,23 Death paradox involves a dual conflict of emotions and perceptions about death within an individual. On the one hand, patients with cancer might wish to die peacefully and with dignity, while on the other hand, they might fear death. This conflict might result in patients facing paradoxes when making death preparations.23,30

Interpersonal level factors play a particularly important role in death preparedness in patients with cancer, including attachment to security relationships, belief in other persons, handing over responsibilities, fears for the family, gratitude, and perceived burdens. Studies found that attachment security relationships and belief in other persons promote emotional support and enhance mental resilience.38 Handing over responsibilities is the patient's realization that they might need to switch their role to focus more on their health and end-of-life care.11 Both fears for the family and gratitude could influence the onset of death preparedness behaviors, causing the patient to commit to making suitable arrangements for family matters.31,32,35

Social level factors include social support, cultural atmosphere, social status, and resource services. The level of perceived social support for patients with cancer affected their psychological state, including their preparation to face death.10,44 Cultural context shapes cancer patients' perceptions, attitudes, and approaches toward death preparedness.6 In Chinese culture, death is often seen as a significant event at both the family and societal levels.46 Additionally, the concept of "filial piety" in Chinese culture may affect the patient's preparation for death, as patients may worry about the burden and pain their death may cause to their families, thus influencing their attitude toward death.47,48 In Thai culture, Buddhist beliefs have a significant impact on the view of death.49 In this cultural context, patients with cancer may more calmly accept the arrival of death because they believe that death is a natural part of life and should be faced with a peaceful mindset.23,50 By contrast, Western cultures tend to emphasize individualism and personal autonomy, making a patient's own wishes and decisions central to their death preparedness.13,31,51 In addition, patients with more resource services and support networks might be more likely to accept and cope positively with death, whereas patients with lower social status and limited resource services might face greater challenges.11,30

Consequences of death preparedness in patients with cancer

Consequences relate to events or situations that arise after the concept has been created.52 In the present study, Rodgers' evolutionary method was used to systematically analyze the literature and identify how the consequences of death preparedness in patients with cancer manifest across different levels of impact. This analytical approach revealed three categories: patient-related consequences, family-related consequences, and social-related consequences.

At the patient level, death preparedness enables patients to experience a sense of meaning and purpose, attain inner peace, and ultimately enhance their quality of death and dying.6,10,23,25,30 At the family level, the family's resilience could be strengthened so that family members would be more united and better equipped to face challenges, helping them to face their future lives better, thus achieving a healthy bereavement process for the family.6,38 Furthermore, at the social level, it helped to achieve tripartite cooperation among patients, their families, and healthcare personnel to achieve a "good death".4,10,23,44 In addition, evidence suggested that death preparedness helps patients avoid over-treatment,11,23 an outcome empirically linked to improved quality of end-of-life care and quality of death in patients with cancer.31,39

An exemplar

Patient A, a 72-year-old high school-educated male, lives alone in the city. He embraced Buddhism after his wife's death from ovarian cancer five years ago and enjoys playing chess with friends. His married son and daughter, both parents themselves, take turns visiting him on weekends. A year ago, he was diagnosed with advanced lung cancer and advised to have chemotherapy. Initially distressed, sad and unaccepting, he sought treatment actively but feared burdening his children. Admitted to hospital a week ago, tests revealed bone and liver metastases. His pain intensified, weight dropped daily, and he felt his days were numbered—conflicted between fearing death for his children's sake and longing to end his suffering. Influenced by his and his wife's illness and Buddhist reflections on life and death, his fear of death eased. He hoped to live his remaining days fully and with dignity to reunite with his wife. He joined a medical staff-organized family meeting, sharing wishes: watching the Tiananmen flag-raising, avoiding hospital stays/machines in his final moments, and thanking his children, medical staff and fellow patients. Post-discharge, he arranged his assets, desiring a peaceful home death, simple funeral, and joint burial with his wife. His children fulfilled his flag-raising wish, and he passed away peacefully at his son's home.

This model case encompasses all the attributes of death preparedness in patients with cancer. Patient A showed disbelief, distress, and sadness when he learned of his diagnosis of terminal cancer. Still, over time, he gradually came to accept the reality of death (acceptance of death), regulating his emotions through activities such as playing chess with friends and practicing Buddhism (emotional adaptation). He proactively participated in family meetings, communicating with his family and healthcare professionals (interactive communication), clearly expressing his wishes (personal wishes), and making arrangements for his affairs after death (end-of-life planning). The consequences are reflected in the patient's fulfillment of his personal wishes and attainment of inner peace, while his family, through their companionship, achieved emotional bonding and preparation for bereavement.

Discussion

Main findings

This study employed Rodgers' evolutionary method to analyze the concept of death preparedness in patients with cancer, tracing its development and clarifying its current meanings.14,21 Compared to prior definitions, which often emphasized primarily psychological readiness or acceptance, our evolved conceptualization reveals a more complex, multidimensional process that incorporates interpersonal, communicative, and practical dimensions. The analysis demonstrates that the concept has progressed from a predominantly internal, emotional state to a dynamic process involving active social engagement and planning.

The key evolution lies in identifying five decisive attributes, which together provide a more comprehensive description. The concept of “death preparedness” in patients with cancer has evolved from initially focusing on “acceptance of the fact of death” and “completion of end-of-life affairs” to a more complex, multi-dimensional and proactive process. “Emotional adaptation” and “interactive communication” have been highlighted as attributes, reflecting the modern palliative care and psychosomatic oncology's emphasis on patients' emotional health and social connection, which goes beyond the traditional pathological perspective.20,25,31,32 Additionally, the connotation of “expression of personal wishes” has been expanded, which not only concerns treatment choices but also extends to the pursuit of the meaning of life, life value, and personal legacy, which corresponds to the development of dignity therapy and narrative nursing.39,40,53 Finally, the entire conceptual framework has expanded from the individual level to the interpersonal and social level, emphasizing the crucial role of family involvement and social cultural support in the death preparation process. This evolution enables the concept to more comprehensively describe the complex experiences and needs of patients with cancer at the end of their lives.

Consequently, the new operational definition “cognitive, emotional, social, and behavioral preparations to reduce uncertainty, maintain self-efficacy, and control current and future losses” differs from prior definitions by explicitly integrating social and behavioral dimensions.37,54 This reflects an evolution from viewing death preparedness as an intra-individual phenomenon to recognizing it as an interactional and contextualized process influenced by individual, interpersonal, and societal factors.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate these evolved attributes into a coherent conceptual framework (Fig. 2) that also systematically specifies the antecedents and consequences associated with death preparedness in patients with cancer. The identified antecedents, including individual factors (e.g., disease status, personal experiences), interpersonal dynamics (e.g., family communication, social support), and broader societal and cultural influences, collectively shape how patients approach death preparedness. Meanwhile, the consequences extend beyond the individual level to encompass enhanced emotional peace and dignity for patients, reduced psychological burden and improved bereavement outcomes for families, and broader systemic benefits such as more appropriate end-of-life care resource utilization and reduced non-beneficial medical interventions.39,55

In summary, this evolved concept of death preparedness offers a broader, more applicable framework for supporting patients in clinical settings. By recognizing the active role of communication, emotional adaptation, and planning, healthcare professionals can better assist patients and families in navigating end-of-life challenges, ultimately contributing to improved care outcomes. By reviewing the literature and analyzing the concepts related to death preparedness in patients with cancer, this study proposed a new conceptual framework of death preparedness in patients with cancer to facilitate clinical implementation and evaluation and help healthcare professionals better understand patients' needs and provide appropriate support and care. Given the individual variability of death preparedness in patients with cancer and the lack of research on its key attributes and elements, the results of this study can serve as a basis for future research.

Cultural considerations in applying the concept

We incorporated literature from diverse cultural backgrounds, particularly East/Southeast Asian research. The analysis indicates that cultural context profoundly influences the manifestations and focal points of death preparedness. For instance, family responsibilities in Chinese culture (such as "filial piety") may prompt patients to focus more on alleviating the burden on their families; Thai Buddhist culture may facilitate a peaceful acceptance of death; whereas Western individualistic cultures may place greater emphasis on patient autonomy and the expression of personal wishes. This suggests that when applying this conceptual framework for clinical assessment or intervention, it is essential to consider the patient's cultural background and values, and to adopt culturally sensitive communication and support strategies.

Implications for nursing practice and research

The framework proposed in this study offers a more comprehensive perspective for clinical practice. Healthcare professionals can leverage it to assess patients' readiness for death more systematically. Beyond focusing solely on whether patients "accept" death, attention should also be given to aspects such as emotional adjustment, communication, expression of wishes, and planning. Based on this holistic assessment, personalized support can be provided, including facilitating doctor-patient communication, assisting with advance care planning, offering psychosocial support, and respecting cultural preferences. Furthermore, this framework provides a theoretical foundation for the development of multidimensional, cross-cultural assessment tools for death preparedness in patients with cancer.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Firstly, Rodgers' evolutionary method and the thematic analysis process both involve inherent interpretive subjectivity, which may influence the findings. To mitigate this potential bias, we engaged in regular team discussions and reached consensus through repeated deliberation during the coding and synthesis phases. Additionally, no formal quality assessment was conducted on the included literature. These factors may limit the rigor and replicability of the study's findings. Furthermore, with only 21 Chinese and English studies included, the relatively limited scope may not fully capture the theoretical breadth and cultural diversity of this interdisciplinary field. Future research should include studies in other languages and from diverse cultural contexts to enable cross-cultural validation and enhance the generalizability of the conclusions.

Conclusions

This study clarifies the evolving concept of death preparedness in patients with cancer, outlining its key attributes, antecedents, and consequences to provide a practical framework for clinical care. The findings offer medical professionals a structured approach to better understand and support patients, thereby enhancing end-of-life care quality. Future research should focus on developing standardized assessment tools and targeted interventions based on this refined conceptualization. Moreover, integrating this framework into palliative care guidelines and health policy could promote patient-centered end-of-life practices and inform resource allocation, ultimately improving outcomes for patients facing terminal illness.
