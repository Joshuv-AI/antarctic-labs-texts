Genomic characterization of carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacilli isolated from hospital wastewater in Niamey, Niger
Authors: Oumou Hamidou, Abdourahamane Yacouba, Ounoussa Tapha, Issoufou Noma Massir, Ismael Illa Salifou, Aminata Mamane Daouda, Harouna Moussa, Souleymane Brah, Saidou Mamadou, Lamine Baba-Moussa

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First genomic survey of carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria in Niger hospital wastewater.•Whole-genome sequencing identified 57 resistance genes and eight carbapenemase determinants.•blaNDM-1 was the most prevalent carbapenemase circulating across hospital sites.•Epidemic plasmids (IncFII, IncL, and IncX3) persisted throughout wastewater treatment.•Treated effluents remained reservoirs of high-risk clones and antimicrobial resistance genes.Keywords: Carbapenemases, Genes, Hospital, Wastewater, Sequencing, Bacteria

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a significant challenge to global health [1]. It has been reported that 700,000 people could die each year worldwide due to AMR, and by 2050, the threat of AMR will surpass that of cancer as the leading cause of death globally [[2], [3], [4], [5]].

The extensive use of antibiotics in the prevention and treatment of human infections has led to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in hospital settings. Hospitals are considered hotspots for AMR [6,7].

Hospital wastewater, often overlooked in the spread of AMR, constitutes a significant reservoir and conduit for ARB [8,9]. They are increasingly recognized as a route of dissemination of ARB and ARG [10,11]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the presence of hospital-derived ARB and ARG in wastewater. ARB and ARG are frequently detected in hospital wastewater, wastewater treatment plant effluents, and in the environment [1,7,[10], [11], [12]]. The characteristics of wastewater include high bacterial counts from various sources, high nutrient levels, and traces of antimicrobials and heavy metals. As a result, all these factors facilitate the circulation of ARG among bacteria through horizontal gene transfer and increase their frequency in effluents [6]. Consequently, the problem of antibiotic resistance is linked not only to the inappropriate and excessive use of antibiotics in hospitals but also to their discharge into wastewater [2,13]. Furthermore, carbapenems are last-resort antibiotics for treating infections caused by multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacilli (GNB). However, the effectiveness of carbapenems has declined considerably due to the global spread of carbapenemase genes. Several studies have reported the presence of clinically significant carbapenem-resistant GNB (CR-GNB), including Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, in hospital wastewater worldwide. These bacteria frequently harbor genes encoding carbapenemases, such as blaNDM, blaKPC, blaVIM, and blaOXA-48, which are associated with multidrug resistance and nosocomial outbreaks [11,[14], [15], [16]]. The global increase in CR-GNB in hospital wastewater is therefore considered a major public health problem, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where wastewater treatment infrastructure remains limited [17].

Nevertheless, the spread of carbapenem-resistant strains has alarmed hospital health officials, posing a threat to public and environmental health [18].

Thus, despite the increasing threat of CR-GNB, few studies have examined the spread of CR-GNB in hospital wastewater in Niger. Hence, this study aimed to characterize CR-GNB isolated from raw and treated hospital wastewater in Niamey, Niger.

Methods

Study design and settings

Thirty samples of raw wastewater (RWW) and treated wastewater (TWW) were collected from three tertiary hospitals in Niamey, including Amirou Boubacar Diallo National Hospital, Niamey National Hospital, and Reference General Hospital, from January 29 to March 30, 2025 (Figure 1).

Geographical distribution of carbapenemase genes in hospital wastewater in Niamey, Niger. HNABD: Niamey V; HNN: Niamey II; HGR: Niamey I (Niamey I to V refers to the communal distribution of the region of Niamey).

HGR, General Referral Hospital; HNABD, Hôpital National Amirou Boubacar Diallo; HNN, Niamey National Hospital.

Wastewater sample collection

Hospital wastewater collected in this study originated from healthcare facilities equipped with a wastewater treatment system based on the activated sludge process combined with membrane bioreactor technology. Wastewater generated from different hospital units was temporarily stored in collection tanks before being pumped to the treatment station. The wastewater was continuously conveyed through an automated pumping system for biological treatment. Variations in wastewater volume were observed depending on hospital activities and seasonal conditions. RWW and TWW samples were collected under sterile conditions and transported to the laboratory for microbiological analysis within a short period after collection.

Samples of raw (pre-treatment) and treated (final effluent) wastewater were collected weekly by composite sampling. Each sample was taken using a pole, at a depth of approximately 20 to 30 cm below the water surface. A volume of 200 mL of wastewater was collected at each sampling point. For each site, to ensure better representativeness of the bacterial load and to limit the impact of instantaneous flow rate variations, a final volume of 200 mL was created by combining three individual samples taken at 5 to 10 minute intervals. This procedure was standardized for all 30 collection points. The wastewater was collected in sterile 250 mL glass bottles, then placed in a cooler at 4°C and transported to the Hôpital National Amirou Boubacar Diallo biology laboratory within 2 hours of collection and processed within 4 hours. No pumping was performed; the samples were collected manually. To standardize collection conditions and maximize the likelihood of detecting resistant bacteria, all samples were taken during peak hospital activity, between 8:30 a.m. and 1:00 a.m. This time slot corresponds to peak water usage related to clinical care, ward cleaning, and patient activity, thus ensuring a biological load representative of the facility's wastewater discharges.

The water samples to be analyzed were filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane that retains the target microorganisms. This step enabled the concentration of bacteria in the water on the membrane surface [19].

Culture and colony identification

The membrane was then placed on MacConkey agar supplemented with 1 mg/l meropenem, brought into close contact with the medium, and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. After 24 hours of incubation, the presence of colonies on the membrane surface indicates that the bacteria are resistant to carbapenems. A single colony was subcultured onto MacConkey agar supplemented with 1 mg/l meropenem to obtain pure colonies. The purified isolates were identified using the API20E system (Biomérieux, Marcy-l'Etoile, France). In accordance with international standards Comité de l'Antibiogramme de la Société Française de Microbiologie/European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (CA-SFM/EUCAST), an antibiogram using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method was performed to test susceptibility to imipenem and meropenem. Only isolates with an inhibition zone diameter corresponding to the resistant phenotype (or high-dose susceptibility) were retained for subsequent steps.

This phenotypic resistance was then correlated with the presence of carbapenemase genes by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and confirmed by identifying genetic determinants via whole-genome sequencing.

DNA extraction and whole-genome sequencing

DNA from confirmed Gram-negative bacilli (BGN) isolates was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN Inc. GmbH, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration of the extracted DNA was determined using the Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA), and the DNA was diluted to 10-60 ng/µL in a final volume of 30 µL. DNA libraries were prepared using the Illumina DNA Library Prep–Tagmentation Kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA). The quality and concentration of the libraries were assessed using the 2100 Bioanalytical System (Agilent) and the quantitative PCR kappa Library Quantification Kit (Roche, CA), respectively. All libraries were normalized to 2 ng/µL and pooled. The pooled DNA libraries were then loaded onto a 2 × 150 cycle cartridge for sequencing on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 system (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA). Coverage depth was calculated as the ratio of the total number of bases sequenced to the estimated genome size for each isolate. The coverage depth was calculated for all 32 isolates the mean coverage was 52× (median: 48×; range: 23-128×). Two isolates with less than 20× coverage (15× and 18×) were resequenced to meet the required threshold.

Contamination control: The following measures were implemented during library preparation and sequencing: (i) negative extraction controls (biomolecular water) were included in each batch; (ii) negative library controls (without template) were added to each PCR reaction; (iii) a sequencing control (“blank”) was loaded onto the flow cell to detect reagent contamination; (iv) after sequencing, taxonomic identification using KmerFinder detected any cross-contamination (abundance threshold >1%). No significant inter-sample contamination was detected. The sequenced files were retrieved after 72 hours.

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw reads generated using the NextSeq 2000 platform were quality-checked using FastQC v0.12.1. Adapter sequences and low-quality bases (Q <20) were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.39. High-quality reads were assembled de novo into contigs using Unicycler v0.5.0, and assembly quality was assessed with Quality Assessment Tool for Genome Assemblies (QUAST) v5.2.0. Genomes with sequencing coverage below 20× or assemblies containing more than 300 contigs were resequenced. Only assemblies with coverage >20× and contigs ≥300 bp were retained for downstream analyses. Species identification was performed using KmerFinder v4.1. AMR genes were identified using Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) v3.0.9 and ResFinder v4.1. Virulence-associated genes were detected using VirulenceFinder v2.0 and the Virulence Factor Database. Sequence types (STs) were assigned using Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST) v2.0, and novel allelic profiles were submitted to PubMLST for assignment of new STs. Plasmid replicons were identified using PlasmidFinder v2.1, while plasmid reconstruction was performed using Mob-recon from MOB-suite v3.1.8. Extracted plasmid sequences were further screened for AMR genes using ResFinder. All analyses were performed using default parameters [20].

Phylogenetic tree

The top two strains isolated (Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae) were used for phylogenetic analyses.

The phylogenetic trees were constructed using eight Acinetobacter baumannii and six Klebsiella pneumoniae strains isolated in Niamey, Niger, and genomes from various countries in National Center for Biotechnology Information. This approach allows us to situate our local strains within the global epidemiological context. All genomes (Niger strains and National Center for Biotechnology Information references) were aligned to the A. baumannii strain American Type Culture Collection 19606 and K. pneumoniae HS11286 reference genome using Snippy, which finds single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) between a haploid reference genome and our next-generation sequencing sequence reads [21]. Core genome SNPs (positions common to all samples) were extracted using “Snippy-core” to combine multiple Snippy outputs into a core SNP alignment [21]. Recombination regions were detected and removed using Gubbins v3.2.1- Recombination detection in bacteria to avoid phylogenetic artifacts [22]. The final alignment (filtered_polymorphic_sites.phylip) was submitted to RAxML-NG for Maximum Likelihood-based inference of large phylogenetic trees [[23], [24], [25], [26]].

Tree’s robustness was assessed using ultrafast bootstrap (1000 replicates, option -B 1000) combined with the -bnni option to avoid overestimation of supports. Bootstrap values (as a percentage) are plotted on the branches of the final tree (significance threshold: ≥70%).

Results

Carbapenem-resistant bacteria isolated

A total of 32 different GNB species were isolated in hospital wastewater, of which 24 (24/32, 75 %) were isolated from raw sewage. The top three GNB isolated in RWW were Acinetobacter baumannii (9/24, 37.5%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (4/24, 16.7%), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (3/24, 12.5%). In the TWW (n = 8 isolates), the following species were identified: Klebsiella pneumoniae (2/8, 25%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (2/8, 25%), Escherichia coli (1/8, 12.5%), Citrobacter werkmanii (1/8, 12.5%), Pseudomonas asiatica (1/8, 12.5%), and Acinetobacter sp. (1/8, 12.5%) (Table 1).

Antimicrobial resistance genes

Analysis of resistance gene profiles in cultured isolates revealed a high diversity of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs), with 459 total ARG occurrences corresponding to 57 distinct resistance genes identified across all major antibiotic classes (Table 2). These findings highlight the complexity of AMR circulating in hospital wastewater environments. Among the detected ARGs, the sul1 gene, conferring resistance to sulfonamides, was the most frequently identified, accounting for 28 occurrences (28/459, 6.1%; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 3.9-8.3). Of particular concern was the detection of multiple carbapenemase genes. The blaNDM-1 gene was the most prevalent carbapenemase determinant, detected in 15 occurrences (15/459, 3.3%; 95% CI: 1.6-4.9), followed by blaNDM-5, blaOXA-395, and blaOXA-181. Overall, eight distinct carbapenemase genes were identified, representing 50 total occurrences (50/459, 10.9%; 95% CI: 8.1-13.7).

Carbapenemase genes

Based on carbapenemase gene sequencing, the highly active genes blaNDM-1, blaNDM-4, blaNDM-5, blaOXA-23, blaOXA-181, blaOXA-48, blaVIM-5, blaOXA-402, and blaOXA-58 were identified. blaOXA-58 and blaVIM-5 are detected in TWW. The five genes blaNDM-1, blaNDM-5, blaOXA-181, and blaOXA-395 are present in the wastewater of these three hospitals in Niamey. Venn diagram analysis showed blaNDM-1, blaNDM-4, blaNDM-5, blaOXA-48-like, blaOXA-181, and blaOXA-395 genes were detected in both RWW and TWW (Figure 2a).

The Sankey diagram showed the distribution of plasmid incompatibility groups (replicons) between the input (RWW) and output (TWW) of the treatment station (Figure 2b). The width of the bands was proportional to the relative abundance of each replicon. The results showed that high-risk plasmids, such as IncFII, IncL, and IncX3, pass through the treatment system without significant reduction. The stability of mobile genetic elements suggested a high potential for horizontal transfer of resistance genes between pathogenic strains and environmental bacteria after the wastewater is discharged into the receiving medium.

Direct connections are observed between TWW and critical genes, including blaNDM-1, blaNDM-4, blaNDM-5, blaOXA-48, and blaOXA-23. Discussion

Hospital wastewater management is a major public health challenge, particularly in developing countries where wastewater treatment systems are often inadequate. These effluents constitute complex reservoirs of emerging contaminants, including multidrug-resistant pathogens, pharmaceutical residues, and ARGs [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Our study demonstrates for the first time the extent of this contamination in the Nigerien context, revealing a remarkable diversity of priority pathogens and critical resistance mechanisms that persist despite conventional treatment processes.

Microbiological characterization revealed a marked predominance of Acinetobacter baumannii (37.5%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (16.7%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (12.5%). This profile differs from those reported in other regions. For example, Escherichia coli predominates in Mexico (73.1%) [31], whereas in Ghana it accounts for 30.6% of isolates [32]. These differences likely reflect the influence of local clinical practices, antibiotic use patterns, and the specific healthcare context of hospitals in Niamey.

Although this study did not include longitudinal monitoring, the high prevalence of A. baumannii in RWW may indicate significant epidemic pressure within the studied hospitals. Previous studies have shown that the detection of carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii in wastewater correlates with the prevalence of nosocomial Acinetobacter baumannii infections in healthcare facilities [33,34]. Therefore, studies integrating both environmental and clinical surveillance would be valuable to confirm this relationship in Niamey.

The predominance of Acinetobacter baumannii may also reflect the remarkable ability of this pathogen to adapt to local antibiotic selection pressures and hospital environmental conditions. Hospital wastewater may therefore represent a direct reflection of nosocomial infection pressure [34], while simultaneously serving as a potential environmental reservoir for epidemic clones between outbreaks.

Analysis of resistance gene profiles in cultured isolates revealed a high diversity of ARGs, with 459 total ARG occurrences corresponding to 57 distinct resistance genes identified across all major antibiotic classes. This diversity is consistent with global trends reported in hospital wastewater studies [35,36] and further supports the role of hospital effluents as hotspots for the accumulation and exchange of resistance determinants.

The predominance of the sul1 gene (6.1%) is consistent with findings reported by Shamsizadeh et al. [37] (2024), who observed an overrepresentation of sulfonamide and phenicol resistance genes in African and Asian regions, in contrast to European and North American profiles dominated by macrolide resistance genes. This distribution likely reflects historical differences in antibiotic usage practices.

One of the most concerning findings of this study was the detection of eight distinct carbapenemase genes, including blaNDM variants (blaNDM-1, blaNDM-4, and blaNDM-5) and blaOXA variants (blaOXA-48, blaOXA-181, blaOXA-23, blaOXA-58, and blaOXA-395). This remarkable diversity, also reported in other studies [15,34,38], suggests strong and prolonged selective pressure associated with carbapenem use. Six of these carbapenemases persisted after wastewater treatment, while four were identified in all three hospitals, indicating widespread and established environmental dissemination.

The exclusive detection of blaOXA-58 and blaVIM-5 in TWW may suggest that disinfection processes selectively favor these resistance mechanisms or promote their persistence through plasmid-mediated transfer from non-culturable bacterial species [12]. More broadly, our findings indicate that wastewater treatment may function more as a selective filter than as an absolute barrier against resistance determinants. The persistence of critical carbapenemases such as blaNDM-1, blaNDM-5, and blaOXA-48 in treated water suggests that these genes evade elimination processes, likely because of their association with stable mobile genetic elements or highly adapted bacterial hosts.

The presence of highly connected genes such as sul1 and aac(6′)-Ib-cr in both RWW and TWW suggests the existence of potential sentinel genes whose detection may predict the overall diversity of resistance determinants in cultured isolates. This observation is consistent with the findings of Le et al. [39], 2016 in Singapore, who also reported the predominance of these genes in hospital effluents.

Sankey diagram analysis further revealed the persistence of IncFII, IncL, and IncX3 plasmid replicons throughout the treatment process. These plasmids are recognized as highly efficient vectors for carbapenemase dissemination and possess characteristics that enhance their circulation of high-risk clones.

As demonstrated by Wu et al. [40], 2019 and Guo et al. [41], 2022, IncX3 plasmids exhibit high conjugation efficiency and strong biofilm-forming capacity, which may explain the persistence of blaNDM-1 after treatment and its efficient transfer to other environmental bacteria.

Phylogenetic analysis also revealed the persistence of identical clonal complexes, particularly among Acinetobacter baumannii isolates, in both RWW and TWW. This observation suggests that current treatment processes are unable to selectively eliminate the most established epidemic lineages.

Our findings also highlighted the consistent association of blaOXA-23 with Acinetobacter strains and the predominance of blaNDM-1 among Enterobacterales. This close association between bacterial species and resistance mechanisms corroborates the observations of Wang et al. [3], 2025, who reported the circulation of carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii clones co-harboring blaOXA-23 and blaNDM-1 during clinical outbreaks in the southwestern Indian Ocean region.

However, while Wang et al. [42] primarily described these hybrid clones (ST1/ST231) in intensive care units, our results demonstrate that these critical genetic associations are not confined to clinical settings but also persist in hospital wastewater in Niamey. This persistence underscores the role of wastewater as both a reflection of hospital microbial ecology and a potential environmental reservoir for the dissemination of highly resistant epidemic clones.

In contrast to European settings, where surveillance often focuses on specific genes such as mcr-1 or blaKPC-3 [42], our study revealed an exceptionally broad diversity of carbapenemase genes that persist despite wastewater treatment. These findings highlight the urgent need to strengthen hospital wastewater management and discharge regulations in Niger.

Overall, our results support the implementation of an integrated strategy combining hospital infection control and environmental surveillance. Wastewater monitoring could serve as a valuable sentinel system for the early detection of emerging resistance mechanisms and for evaluating the effectiveness of infection control interventions.

Finally, the persistence of mobile genetic elements suggests the need for complementary treatment strategies specifically targeting plasmids and other mobile resistance vectors, in addition to reducing bacterial load alone.

This study has several limitations. First, its temporal and geographic scope is limited to samples collected from three hospitals in Niamey during the dry season. Consequently, the results represent only a snapshot of the microbiological situation and do not capture the full annual dynamics of AMR in hospital wastewater across Niger. Seasonal factors such as rainfall, temperature, fluctuations in hospital activity, antibiotic prescribing practices, and variations in wastewater flow can influence bacterial abundance, the composition of microbial communities, and the dissemination of resistance determinants.

Second, the study relied exclusively on culture methods using meropenem-supplemented MacConkey agar (1 mg/l) for the isolation of carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria. Although this approach effectively selects for resistant Enterobacterales and non-fermenting bacilli, it may underestimate the diversity of resistant microorganisms. Fastidious, slow-growing, or viable but unculturable bacteria may not be detected under these conditions. Furthermore, some isolates may have lost resistance plasmids during subculturing, potentially leading to an underestimation of resistance genes. Third, culture-independent approaches, such as metagenomic sequencing, were not implemented. Consequently, unculturable bacterial species and the overall diversity of resistance genes could not be comprehensively assessed. The lack of metagenomic analysis also limits the quantification of the total genetic load released into the urban environment.

Finally, although epidemic plasmids such as IncFII, IncL, and IncX3 were identified, horizontal gene transfer was not experimentally demonstrated. Conjugation tests, transformation experiments, and longitudinal monitoring were not performed; therefore, the potential for the transfer of these mobile genetic elements remains hypothetical.

Despite these limitations, the combination of classical microbiology and whole-genome sequencing has provided valuable preliminary information on the diversity of carbapenem-resistant bacteria and resistance genes circulating in hospital wastewater in Niamey.

Future studies should incorporate high-frequency longitudinal sampling, automated samplers operating 24 hours a day, ultrafiltration concentration methods, metagenomic analyses, and experimental conjugation assays to better characterize the dynamics of resistance genes in wastewater and the dissemination of mobile genetic elements. Environmental investigations should also be extended to sediments of the Niger River and downstream irrigated soils to assess the transfer of clinically important resistance determinants into the environment and food chains. Particular attention should be paid to monitoring carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii carrying the blaOXA-23 and blaNDM-1 genes, due to its potential role in future difficult-to-treat nosocomial infections.

Conclusion

This study provides the first genomic insights into the dynamics of carbapenemase-producing bacteria in hospital wastewater in Niamey, Niger. The persistence of internationally disseminated high-risk clones (ST11 and ST773) and epidemic plasmids (IncFII, IncL, and IncX3) throughout the wastewater treatment process highlights the limited effectiveness of current sanitation barriers in preventing the spread of AMR. These findings emphasize the need for: (i) the implementation of environmental genomic surveillance as an early warning system for the detection of emerging high-risk clones and resistance determinants; (ii) the optimization of wastewater disinfection strategies to reduce the persistence and dissemination of mobile genetic elements; and (iii) the integration of wastewater-based genomic surveillance into national and regional AMR action plans in Niger and West Africa.

Overall, our results demonstrate that hospital wastewater constitutes an important reservoir of multidrug-resistant pathogenic bacteria and clinically relevant resistance genes. The persistence of these organisms despite treatment represents a significant public health concern and may facilitate the environmental dissemination of AMR. Strengthening surveillance programs, improving wastewater treatment technologies, and reinforcing AMR control policies are therefore essential to mitigate this growing threat.
