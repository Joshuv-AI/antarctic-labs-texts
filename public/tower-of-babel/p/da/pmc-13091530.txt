Illuminating the metabolic sink: Optical probes for in vivo thermogenic fat imaging
Authors: Zhuojia Chen, Xiwen Ye, Jiajun Wei, Minmin Peng, Linjie Ni, Xiaoyu Liu, Baisheng Chen, Jinde Zhang, Ronghe Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a metabolic sink, thermogenic fat plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of metabolic diseases, yet current techniques for assessing its location and activity in vivo face challenges, such as high costs and operational complexity. Optical imaging, encompassing label-free and labeled modalities, offers a relatively cost-effective and straightforward alternative. In comparison with the label-free modality, labeled optical imaging stands out for its superior performance, which is attributed to the utilization of advanced optical probes with enhanced specificity and sensitivity. Despite significant advancements in optical probes for thermogenic fat detection, systematic compilations related to these studies remain scarce. Hence, this review concentrates on four typical categories of optical probes and expounds their physicochemical properties, imaging benefits, and applications in thermogenic fat assessment. Their imaging targets, design strategies, translational challenges, and perspectives are also discussed. As a reference resource, this review will facilitate the development of more reliable and safer optical probes targeting thermogenic fat, thereby assisting in precise theranostics for metabolic diseases.Keywords: Optical probe, Design strategy, Thermogenic fat, Imaging, Clinical translation

Introduction

Thermogenic fat, including brown and beige fat, is essential for whole-body energy homeostasis by functioning as a highly active metabolic pool for glucose, lipids, and amino acids [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. In response to stimuli such as cold, it is activated to oxidize energy substrates for thermogenesis, which dissipates excessive energy in the body. This remarkable thermogenic capacity is structurally underpinned by its three hallmark features: densely packed mitochondria, multilocular lipid droplets, and extensive vasculature [5,6]. Besides, thermogenic fat is a secretory organ that releases batokines (i.e., brown adipokines) to mediate communication among organs and tissues [7,8]. The recruitment and activation of thermogenic fat exert profound protective effects against metabolic disorders (e.g., obesity and type 2 diabetes) and related cardiovascular diseases [[9], [10], [11]]. Conversely, in certain cancer settings, its high activity may accelerate tumor progression by supplying energy substrates [12]. Therefore, thermogenic fat is regarded as an attractive and promising target for therapeutic intervention in metabolic diseases. To harness this point, its accurate identification and assessment are crucial for understanding disease pathogenesis and evaluating treatment efficacy.

Various imaging technologies have been reported to detect thermogenic fat in vivo [13]. Traditional approaches include computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography (PET) [[14], [15], [16]]. However, they have limitations like high costs and operational complexity. In contrast, optical imaging emerges as a compelling alternative with cost-effectiveness and operational simplicity. There are two major forms of optical imaging: label-free and labeled. Label-free optical imaging utilizes the intrinsic optical properties of cells or tissues to generate contrast [17]. Labeled optical imaging relies on exogenous probes to achieve enhanced sensitivity and specificity in biological visualization. Although a multitude of optical probes, including dyes, luminescent proteins, nanoparticles, and radiopharmaceuticals, have been developed for in vivo thermogenic fat imaging, a comprehensive review regarding this field remains absent in the current literature.

Herein, we categorize and summarize relevant optical probes according to their signal generation mechanisms: fluorescence, bioluminescence, photoacoustic, and Cherenkov luminescence (Fig. 1), emphasizing their characteristics, imaging benefits and recent advancements in assessing thermogenic fat. Furthermore, we discuss their design strategies and imaging targets, as well as proffer insights into their prospects and challenges. This review promotes the development of cutting-edge optical probes and their integration with sophisticated imaging technologies. These advances further enable biologists to better understand the spatiotemporal activity of thermogenic fat and clinicians to more precisely assess its normal and pathological states.

Overview of diversiform optical probes for in vivo thermogenic fat imaging.

Fluorescence probes

Fluorescence probes undergo photoexcitation to emit light for fluorescence imaging (FLI). Their emission necessitates an external light source, which spans a broadband wavelength from the visible to the second near-infrared window. Long-wavelength excited probes are particularly appropriate for deep-tissue imaging due to enhanced penetration and reduced photon scattering and absorption by tissues. Moreover, fluorescence probes offer high sensitivity, selectivity, and rapid response. Thus, they are valuable tools for bioimaging. Various fluorescence probes have been used to image thermogenic fat, such as small-molecule dyes, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), polymer dots (Pdots), and genetically encoded proteins (Table 1).

Fluorescent dyes

Fluorescent dyes stand out for their ability to probe intracellular biochemical events with limited perturbation. Their low molecular weight is a key attribute that facilitates rapid permeation across cytoplasmic and organellar membranes to reach intracellular targets. Critically, their well-defined chemical structures are highly amenable to rational design and synthesis. This inherent engineerability allows for the precise tuning of their photophysical properties (e.g., wavelength and Stokes shift) and the optimization of their biological profiles. For thermogenic fat imaging, fluorescent dyes can either leverage their intrinsic structural features or be functionalized via conjugation with targeting ligands.

IR-786, a lipophilic cyanine dye, exhibits mitochondrial localization at low concentrations and endoplasmic reticulum localization at higher concentrations. Brown adipose tissue is characterized by abundant mitochondria, and its vascular perfusion increases upon thermogenic activation. Accordingly, Nakayama et al. [18] utilized IR-786 to quantitatively assess brown fat perfusion and mass. However, the practical application of this probe is limited by its unpredictable subcellular localization and notable cytotoxicity. SRFluor680, a lipophilic and uncharged fluorescent dye, also targets brown fat based on its structure. It can be rapidly and irreversibly sequestered into lipophilic intracellular organelles, which prevents its redistribution from brown fat to white fat. Rice et al. [19] demonstrated that SRFluor680 has superior biosafety and greater specificity for brown fat imaging in comparison with IR-786. Unfortunately, SRFluor680 cannot monitor dynamic changes in brown fat activity or mass, since its uptake is independent of tissue metabolic status. To overcome this limitation, researchers have turned to hemicyanine dyes, a class of near-infrared fluorophores renowned for their excellent optical properties and high structural modifiability [38]. Building on this scaffold, Xiang et al. [20] designed a series of probes named HCYBAT-1-3 by incorporating a trisubstituted amine group. These probes possessed large Stokes shifts that enhance FLI accuracy. Notably, HCYBAT-1 demonstrated optimal biocompatibility and mitochondrial selectivity, which made it well-suited not only for labeling brown fat but also for indicating its thermogenic activation.

Alternatively, fluorescent dyes can be conjugated with functional molecules to enhance their physicochemical properties or confer specific targeting capabilities. Jo et al. [21] developed BF800-AM by conjugating a tyramine linker to the IR-786 scaffold. Compared with IR-786, BF800-AM showed enhanced hydrophilicity and reduced toxicity, as well as greater specificity and a higher signal-to-background ratio in brown fat FLI. Similarly, Zhang et al. [22] introduced a julolidine ring into a curcumin analogue intermediate to create CRANAD-29 (Fig. 2). This modification minimized the simple diffusion capacity of the molecule by increasing steric hindrance and extended its excitation and emission wavelengths. Consequently, CRANAD-29 exhibited improved selectivity and imaging performance, enabling the separate visualization of brown fat mass and activation through spectral unmixing techniques [23]. In another approach, Panagia et al. [24] designed a free fatty acid (FFA) near-infrared fluorescent tracer called AlexaFFA, which consists of Alexa Fluor™ 647 linked to palmitic acid, a physiologically relevant FFA. This probe could mimic the kinetics of endogenous FFAs and directly assess FFA metabolism in activated brown fat. For targeted brown fat imaging, Azhdarinia et al. [25] functionalized IRDye800 with PEP3, a peptide (sequence: CPATAERPC) that specifically binds to the endothelium in brown and beige fat by recognizing distinctive vascular surface molecules. The resulting PEP3-IRDye800 has also been validated as a reliable FLI tool for tracking the dynamic changes of thermogenic fat in vivo.

CNTs

CNTs are compelling fluorescence probes for thermogenic fat imaging because they fluoresce in the second near-infrared region (1000-1400 nm), where light scattering and tissue autofluorescence are markedly reduced [39]. As a result, CNT-based FLI achieves deeper tissue penetration and a higher signal-to-noise ratio than many conventional fluorophores [40]. Moreover, owing to their sp2-bonded carbon framework, CNTs possess high structural stability and photobleaching resistance. While CNTs have been widely used for whole-body vascular imaging, coating them with various biocompatible materials further enables tissue-specific targeting, which makes them highly effective optical probes for precise adipose imaging.

Poly (2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphocholine-co-n-butyl methacrylate) (PMB) is a biocompatible and amphiphilic polymer that contains both polar phosphorylcholine groups and hydrophobic butyl groups within its side chains [41]. Probes coated with PMB behave like biogenic lipid compounds, such as serum lipids, with a high affinity for apolipoproteins. Under the guidance of apolipoproteins, the probes can localize to tissues capable of taking up and storing lipids or lipid compounds. Based on this, Yudasaka et al. [26] utilized PMB to modify CNTs. They observed that PMB-CNTs showed preferential accumulation in capillary endothelial cells (ECs) of adipose tissue, a phenomenon attributed to the presence of apolipoproteins on their surfaces. In brown fat, the intensity of fluorescence signals from PMB-CNTs correlated with capillary density, which indirectly reflects adipose thermogenesis and brownness. These findings indicate that PMB-CNTs are a robust FLI probe suitable for functional assessment of thermogenic fat.

Phospholipid polyethylene glycol (PLPEG) is another coating material with high bio-affinity. PLPEG-based nanomaterials are able to escape from the reticuloendothelial system, thereby achieving prolonged blood circulation [42]. This characteristic makes them more effective as imaging probes for the dynamic and long-term visualization of specific tissues. Yudasaka et al. [27] developed a conjugate of PLPEGs and CNTs to image the vascular system of brown fat (Fig. 3). In fasted mice, they found that PLPEG-CNTs extravagated from arterioles and capillaries into brown adipose tissue, as fasting caused the disruption or breakage of collagen bands in interstitial regions. Clearly, when combined with PLPEG-CNTs, FLI can visually indicate the metabolic state of brown fat by tracking fluid kinetics, particularly its activation in response to specific stimuli.

Fluorescent Pdots

Pdots are nanoparticles predominantly composed of π-conjugated polymers (>50%) [43]. They have excellent optical properties, including high brightness and good photostability, due to densely packed chromophoric structures, which endow them with large absorption cross sections, efficient energy migration/concentration, and fast radiative decay. Pdots also support versatile surface chemistries (e.g., amphiphilic coatings and PEGylation) for functional modifications. These modifications allow for the precise tuning of Pdots’ physicochemical properties, such as size, stability, and hydrophilicity, which in turn dictate their biodistribution in vivo.

To visualize brown fat activity at multiple scales via FLI, Guo et al. [28] synthesized a panel of amphiphilic near-infrared Pdots: MEH-PPV-COOH-30, MEH-PPV-COOH-50, MEH-PPV-NH2-30, and PFBT-COOH-30 (Fig. 4). Among these formulations, MEH-PPV-COOH-30 and PFBT-COOH-30 exhibited ultrasmall size (approximately 2.6 and 3 nm, respectively), amphiphilic structures, broad absorbance spectra, and good colloidal/fluorescence stability. They could circulate in the bloodstream for extended periods without being rapidly cleared by the liver or reticuloendothelial system. Notably, they enabled the direct visualization of vasculature in brown and beige adipose tissue with distinct imaging contrast. Similar to PEP3-IRDye800, MEH-PPV-COOH-30 and PFBT-COOH-30 proved suitable for assessing time-dependent alterations in thermogenic fat activity. Moreover, both probes achieved effective imaging as early as 5 min after intravenous injection, which is faster than PEP3-IRDye800 (1 h post-injection requirement).

Genetically encoded fluorescent proteins

As genetically encoded labels, fluorescent proteins enable the direct visualization of structures and processes in living cells and tissues. Various fluorescent proteins are available across the spectrum from blue to far-red [44]. Among them, infrared fluorescent proteins (iRFPs) have become widely used probes for non-invasive in vivo imaging, especially for the longitudinal tracking of gene expression. These iRFPs are engineered from bacterial phytochrome photoreceptors and utilize biliverdin as their endogenous chromophore. In mammalian cells, biliverdin is naturally abundant as a product of heme catabolism. Since their emission wavelength of iRFPs is also located in the near-infrared region, the resultant fluorescence can penetrate biological tissues with a high signal-to-noise ratio.

As a fundamental protein for heat production, uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) is exclusively expressed in thermogenic adipocytes. This specificity makes the Ucp1 gene an ideal locus for inserting iRFP reports to monitor thermogenic activity. Wang et al. [29] constructed a UCP1-luc-iRFP713 reporter mouse model, in which the expression of iRFP713 was driven by the Ucp1 promoter. Under cold exposure, significant iRFP713 fluorescence signals were detected in the interscapular brown adipose tissue (iBAT) of these mice. Moreover, signal intensity increased in a cold stimulus-dependent manner, which indicates that iRFP713 can be used to evaluate the activation status of thermogenic fat. However, iRFP713 exhibited certain limitations in this model, including diminished fluorescence at room temperature and poor tissue penetration. These may affect its high-quality FLI in adipose tissues with deeper anatomical localization or lower UCP1 expression.

In the aforementioned study, iRFP713 was positioned as the third gene in a tri-cistronic vector separated by two 2A sequences. Although 2A sequences enable a single mRNA to produce multiple independent proteins, they reduce translational efficiency for downstream cistrons. To overcome this, Fukuda et al. [30] generated UCP1-iRFP720 knock-in mice. The gene encoding iRFP720 was inserted directly at the translation initiation site of the Ucp1 locus and expressed as a single cistron. As a result, iRFP720 showcased stronger expression and higher imaging sensitivity than iRFP713 in visualizing brown fat activity. Additionally, after treatment with a β3-adrenergic receptor agonist, robust iRFP720 fluorescence was also detected in beige adipocytes within white adipose tissue. This implies that, compared with iRFP713, iRFP720 is capable of effectively tracking low-level UCP1 expression.

Bioluminescence probes

Bioluminescence imaging (BLI) is a technique derived from bioluminescence, a natural phenomenon wherein certain living organisms can convert chemical energy into light [45]. This luminescent reaction depends on three essential components: an enzyme (luciferase), a substrate (luciferin) and cofactors. Similar to genetically encoded fluorescent proteins, luciferase has been utilized as an optical reporter for bioimaging via genetic engineering. However, they have a notable distinction in light generation mechanisms. Luciferase can oxidize luciferin to generate light without external illumination, which eliminates issues associated with photobleaching and phototoxicity. Moreover, low background noise in BLI makes it a highly sensitive reporter for detecting gene transcriptional activity in vivo. In thermogenic fat research, two luciferases, firefly luciferase (Fluc) and Gaussia luciferase (Gluc), have been widely adopted (Table 1).

Fluc

Originally isolated from the North American firefly Photinus pyralis, Fluc is a 62-kDa globular protein and one of the most extensively studied bioluminescent enzymes. It catalyzes the oxidation of D-luciferin in the presence of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), magnesium ions (Mg2+), and oxygen. During the process, D-luciferin is initially activated through adenylation mediated by ATP and Mg2+. Then, under the action of oxygen, D-luciferin is oxidized to form oxyluciferin in an excited state. When oxyluciferin relaxes to its ground state, it gives off photons of yellow light (550-600 nm), which falls outside the spectral window of strongest tissue absorption [46,47]. As an efficient and stable enzyme-substrate pair, Fluc and D-luciferin serve as common bioanalytical tools for in vivo BLI, including the monitoring of biologic events in thermogenic fat.

A representative application of this system was demonstrated by Wang et al. [29], who employed Fluc as a bioluminescent reporter to visualize Ucp1 expression in mice treated with physiological and pharmacological stimuli. After the intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin, they observed high-intensity bioluminescence signals in the interscapular region, which corresponds to the anatomical location of brown fat. Similar bioluminescence was detected in the region of inguinal white fat, which indicates beige fat recruitment. Furthermore, they confirmed that the signal enhancement in these regions was linked to elevated UCP1 protein levels. These results clearly illustrated that the Ucp1-driven Fluc bioluminescence could effectively reflect thermogenic fat activation. Nevertheless, they also noted an unintended consequence. Ucp1 mRNA levels in knock-in mice were reduced to approximately 15% of wild-type levels. Inserting Fluc into the Ucp1 locus appeared to impair Ucp1 transcription.

Gluc

Gluc from the marine copepod Gaussia princeps is a relatively small (∼20 kDa) enzyme, which makes it highly suitable for gene construction in small vectors. Its compact structure also minimizes steric hindrance when it is used as a genetic fusion partner for other proteins [46,48]. Unlike Fluc, Gluc utilizes coelenterazine as its substrate and requires only oxygen as a cofactor to produce blue light. This ATP-independent process results in faster reaction kinetics. Moreover, as a naturally secreted luciferase, Gluc is released into the extracellular environment and detected in the bloodstream. For applications in thermogenic fat, these unique properties enable Gluc-based reporters to not only rapidly locate thermogenic adipose tissue but also dynamically evaluate metabolic flux via BLI.

Fatty acid synthase (FASN) is a vital enzyme for saturated fatty acid synthesis. In activated brown or beige adipocytes, it participates in a UCP1-independent thermogenic pathway that is driven by the biosynthesis and subsequent β-oxidation of monomethyl branched-chain fatty acids [49]. Leveraging this point, Li et al. [31] developed a FASN-2A-Gluc reporter mouse model (Fig. 5). Bioluminescence signals were predominantly detected in the iBAT region. After fasting or treatment with adipogenesis-blocking compounds, the signals declined significantly, which was consistent with the reduction in FASN expression. Serum Gluc activity measurements further corroborated these results. Although this model successfully visualized brown fat using BLI and indicated alterations in its metabolic activity, limitations exist in methodological specificity. FASN is also expressed in other metabolically active tissues, such as liver and white fat, which may contribute to off-target bioluminescence signals.

Photoacoustic probes

Photoacoustic imaging (PAI) is a hybrid imaging modality that synergizes the high contrast of optical imaging with the high spatial resolution and deep penetration of ultrasound imaging [[50], [51], [52]]. In a typical PAI process, short-pulsed laser light is absorbed by biological tissues and converted into heat, resulting in a transient temperature rise and subsequent thermoelastic expansion. This expansion generates ultrasonic waves, also known as photoacoustic waves, which are detected by ultrasonic transducers to reconstruct an image [53]. PAI image contrast actually emanates from the optical absorption of various endogenous tissue components (e.g., hemoglobin). In combination with ultrasonic penetrability, this property enables PAI to acquire molecular and functional information from deep tissues, such as brown and beige adipose tissue. Nevertheless, endogenous contrast lacks sufficient specificity and sensitivity. To address this issue, exogenous photoacoustic probes have been fabricated and applied to improve thermogenic fat PAI (Table 1).

Photoacoustic dyes

Multiple near-infrared fluorescent dyes have been demonstrated to possess excellent photoacoustic and photothermal conversion capabilities, which make them effective contrast agents for PAI [[54], [55], [56]]. Their strong absorption within the near-infrared window enhances imaging depth, resolution, and contrast. Moreover, they can be chemically modified or conjugated with targeting molecules to achieve tissue-selective accumulation. Among these near-infrared dyes, heptamethine cyanines like CyHF-8 are particularly attractive for thermogenic fat imaging due to their mitochondrion targeting and negligible cytotoxicity [57]. Li et al. [32] utilized CyHF-8 as a photoacoustic probe for monitoring dynamic changes in brown fat with multispectral optoacoustic tomography (Fig. 6). This probe exhibited a high molar absorption coefficient and moderate fluorescence quantum yield, which allows efficient conversion of absorbed optical energy into photoacoustic signals. Through spectral unmixing, PAI images revealed that significant CyHF-8 signals were localized in the brown fat region. Upon stimulation with the β-adrenergic agonist norepinephrine, the signals further intensified. These findings suggest that CyHF-8 can sensitively assess thermogenic fat activity.

In addition to heptamethine dyes, indocyanine green (ICG), a clinically approved tricarbocyanine dye, has been adapted for thermogenic fat PAI. Its optical absorption peak is near 800 nm, which offers high penetration through adipose tissue and boosts the imaging signal-to-noise ratio [58]. However, a primary limitation of free ICG is its non-specific biodistribution in vivo. To impart thermogenic fat selectivity, Gong et al. [33] conjugated ICG with the endothelium-targeting peptide CPATAERPC. The resulting probe, named Pep-ICG, highlighted vascular bundles in brown adipose tissue of normal mice with high photoacoustic contrast. Subsequently, it was used to assess brown fat activity in a hindlimb unloading model that simulates microgravity. With the assistance of Pep-ICG, PAI clearly visualized emblematic neovascularization accompanying brown fat activation.

Photoacoustic Pdots

Pdots, known for their exceptional photophysical properties, have been developed as versatile probes for FLI, PAI, and other imaging modalities [59,60]. As noted previously, lipophilic Pdots could detect thermogenic fat specifically via FLI within a very short time after intravenous injection [28]. To extend Pdots into the PAI field, Li et al. [34] synthesized a new formulation by the self-assembly of MEH-PPV and NIR775 (Fig. 7). The resulting Pdots exhibited a strong photoacoustic effect under pulsed laser irradiation at a wavelength of ∼770 nm. Based on multispectral PAI and spectral unmixing, they found that these Pdots rapidly and selectively accumulated in iBAT capillaries after intravenous injection. The uptake rate of capillary ECs reached 94.0% within 5 min and remained at 60% at 1 h, which is attributed to the high-affinity binding of Pdots to triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRLs) in the bloodstream. These results demonstrate that Pdots are sensitive photoacoustic probes for tracking iBAT microvascular dynamics in both short-term and long-term metabolic research.

Cherenkov luminescence probes

Cherenkov luminescence imaging (CLI) serves as an optical readout of radionuclide distribution by exploiting Cherenkov radiation. This phenomenon occurs when charged particles (especially beta particles) from radionuclide decay travel through a dielectric medium like biological tissue at a velocity greater than the phase velocity of light in that medium [61,62]. Because many established PET radiopharmaceuticals exhibit beta-emission characteristics, they can be directly utilized for CLI without modification. This compatibility means that CLI offers high imaging sensitivity comparable to PET. Moreover, CLI has unique application advantages, including low instrumentation cost and rapid imaging speed. Currently, several Cherenkov luminescence probes have been developed to track different aspects of thermogenic fat metabolism in humans and animals (Table 1).

Fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) is a classic radiopharmaceutical used clinically for PET. As a glucose analogue, it can be rapidly and irreversibly taken up by various tissues and organs with high glucose turnover, thereby acting as an indirect indicator of tissue glucose uptake capacity [63]. Zhang et al. [35] successfully utilized 18F-FDG for brown fat CLI in mice (Fig. 8). Within 2 h after 18F-FDG intravenous injection, they observed bright luminescence from iBAT, which was further enhanced by norepinephrine stimulation or cold exposure. Using three-dimensional reconstruction and spectral unmixing, they were able to quantify iBAT volume. Furthermore, their work revealed that a remarkable decrease in 18F-FDG uptake by iBAT under long-term anesthesia. This result implies that anesthesia suppresses normal mitochondrion activity, which is a valuable discovery for both clinical and animal research. However, 18F-FDG has a notable limitation in iBAT CLI. Because brown fat metabolism primarily relies on lipids as an energy source, glucose-based probes may fail to accurately reflect its true metabolic activity.

To overcome the limitations of glucose analogues such as 18F-FDG, alternative molecular targets have been investigated. One such target is the translocator protein-18 kDa (TSPO), which is associated with cholesterol metabolism and has been identified as a promising biomarker for thermogenic fat [64]. Following the report of [11C]PBR28, a TSPO-specific tracer, for iBAT PET in healthy volunteers [65], Moon et al. [36] designed deuterium-substituted [18F]fluoromethyl-PBR28 ([18F]fm-PBR28-d2), which has higher metabolic stability and longer half-life than [11C]PBR28. Subsequently, Lee et al. [37] used [18F]fm-PBR28-d2 for iBAT CLI in UCP1-reporter ThermoMice. As a TSPO targeting probe, it was superior to 18F-FDG in acquiring iBAT images under various conditions. Besides, the higher its molar activity, the better the resulting image quality. Since UCP1 and TSPO expression levels were correlated in iBAT, [18F]fm-PBR28-d2 mediated CLI could indirectly reflect endogenous UCP1 expression.

Imaging targets and design strategies for probes

The four optical probes above offer distinct advantages in thermogenic fat imaging. Their effectiveness depends not only on their optical properties but also on their ability to selectively accumulate in thermogenic fat and report signals. To achieve this selectivity, probe design is typically tailored to the unique structural, biochemical, and physiological characteristics that distinguish brown and beige adipose tissue from white adipose tissue. These distinguishing features primarily include multilocular lipid droplets for substrate supply, abundant mitochondria for thermogenesis, and highly vascularized networks for nutrient transport and heat dissipation (Table 2). By understanding the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying these characteristics, researchers can develop superior probe design strategies that enhance imaging performance for thermogenic fat.

Lipid droplets

Thermogenic adipocytes predominantly take up circulating FFAs and package them into multilocular lipid droplets via esterification. These lipid droplets serve as dynamic energy reservoirs that supply substrates for mitochondrial β-oxidation and thermogenesis. During thermogenic activation, they undergo a highly coordinated metabolic cycle that involves rapid FFA uptake, triglyceride synthesis, and subsequent lipolysis. Imaging strategies targeting lipid droplets in thermogenic fat generally focus on three molecular facets: FFAs, FFA transporters, and related metabolic enzymes. Based on FFAs, probe design strategies include developing lipophilic compounds structurally similar to FFAs (e.g., SRFluor680 [19]) or conjugating fluorophores to FFAs (e.g., AlexaFFA, an Alexa Fluor™ 647-FFA conjugate [24]). Probes are also designed to exploit transporters that facilitate the internalization of circulating FFAs into thermogenic adipocytes. A prime example is the fluorescent probe CRANAD-29, which is readily taken up by brown adipocytes via CD36/fatty acid transporter before accumulating in lipid droplets [22,23]. Finally, enzymes that govern FFA synthesis, such as FASN, can be targeted by genetically fusing fluorescent or bioluminescent reporters to their coding sequence. The FASN-Gluc model [31] exemplifies this strategy. Despite these advances, a potential limitation of current lipid-targeted probes is their susceptibility to off-target accumulation in white adipose tissue, which also stores abundant lipids. To achieve higher specificity, future design strategies can shift from passive lipid accumulation toward activatable probes that emit optical signals only upon interaction with specific lipases or distinct lipid droplet microenvironments in thermogenic adipocytes.

Mitochondria

Mitochondria are the bioenergetic core of thermogenic adipocytes. During thermogenesis, substrates like FFAs and glucose are transferred into mitochondria and then oxidized to generate heat [66]. Several optical probes are designed based on these substrate behaviors. For instance, 18F-FDG, a radiolabeled glucose analogue, can be preferentially sequestered by activated brown adipocytes and employed as a well-established CLI probe for brown fat metabolism [35,63]. A key molecular mediator of mitochondrial thermogenesis is UCP1, which triggers a proton leak across the inner mitochondrial membrane and thereby dissipates the proton gradient as heat rather than ATP synthesis [2]. This unique function makes UCP1 an ideal molecular target for probe development. Representative examples include UCP1-luc-iRFP713 [29] and UCP1-iRFP720 [30], two reporter constructs that express luciferase and/or fluorescent protein under the control of the Ucp1 promoter. Other mitochondrial proteins are also promising targets. TSPO, located on the outer mitochondrial membrane, primarily participates in cholesterol transport, mitochondrial permeability regulation, and energy metabolism [67]. Given its overexpression in brown adipocytes, TSPO-targeting probes like [18F]fm-PBR28-d2 have been successfully developed for iBAT CLI [37]. Beyond membrane proteins, negative mitochondrial membrane potential is exploited in probe design. As lipophilic cationic probes, IR-786 [18] and CyHF-8 [32] can label mitochondria in thermogenic fat. Coupling additional lipophilic groups can further enhance mitochondrial affinity and imaging contrast, as demonstrated by BF800-AM [21] and HCYBAT-1-3 [20]. However, UCP1-mediated thermogenesis actually decreases mitochondrial membrane potential, which implies that lipophilic cationic probes may paradoxically exhibit attenuated signals during the activation of thermogenic fat. Therefore, subsequent probe development should avoid over-relying on membrane potential as the sole driving force for mitochondrial accumulation.

Blood vessels

Dense vascular network within thermogenic fat is indispensable for transporting energy substrates and dissipating heat. The physiological properties of this network, particularly its transport capacity and permeability, offer crucial principles for designing efficient optical probes. As carriers, TRLs deliver lipids from circulatory system to brown and beige fat for utilization as thermogenic fuel. Moreover, they readily bind lipophilic compounds. These have inspired the creation of probes like Pdots [34] that hitchhike on TRLs to reach thermogenic adipose tissue. Vascular permeability is another targetable feature. Fasting can cause increased vascular permeability by damaging the collagen structure of tissue interstitium. PLPEG-CNTs have been designed to capitalize on this phenomenon, as they more easily extravasate from blood vessels into brown adipose tissue under fasting conditions [27]. In addition, a more specific strategy involves targeting unique biomarkers on the surface of capillary ECs within thermogenic fat. Its implementation relies on conjugating fluorescent materials to molecules with high affinity for these endothelial markers. Exemplary probes encompass peptide-modified fluorescent dyes (e.g., PEP3-IRDye800 [25] and Pep-ICG [33]), amphiphilic PMB-CNTs [26], and NIR Pdots [28] (e.g., MEH-PPV-COOH-30 and PFBT-COOH-30). Nevertheless, a critical bottleneck in current vascular targeting strategies is that endothelial binding does not guarantee successful extravasation and subsequent internalization by thermogenic adipocytes. To overcome this, future probe design can focus on sequential or multi-step targeting systems that not only recognize specific endothelial markers but also actively facilitate trans-endothelial transport and adipocyte endocytosis.

Perspectives and challenges

Although the aforementioned series of optical probes have demonstrated promising efficacy in tracking thermogenic fat, only two probes, ICG and 18F-FDG, have been translated into clinical practice. The former is approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for imaging-guided surgery, and the latter is one of the most widely used imaging agents [58,68]. As comparatively summarized in Table 3, despite differences in probe form and imaging modality, the clinical translation potential of current optical probes is largely determined by two shared considerations: tissue penetration and biosafety. In this context, different classes of probes exhibit distinct strengths and limitations.

Compared with animal imaging, human in vivo imaging requires optical probes capable of generating signals with deeper tissue penetration. This requirement poses a formidable challenge for most optical probes due to light-tissue interactions, such as absorption and scattering [69,70]. Weak bioluminescence and short-wavelength Cherenkov light undergo severe tissue attenuation, while fluorescence inevitably suffers from a two-way signal loss during excitation and emission. A notable exception is found in the probes designed for PAI. In this modality, these probes absorb excitation light but generate ultrasonic waves for detection [71]. This mechanism allows them to circumvent conventional optical constraints to some extent, thereby realizing superior imaging penetration. Given that long-wavelength light interacts less with biological tissues and exhibits stronger penetration capability, red-shifting the optical response band of probes into the near-infrared region is an effective strategy for overcoming the bottleneck of deep-tissue imaging [72,73]. Beyond spectral optimization, the construction of multimodal probes (e.g., PAI/FLI and PAI/MRI probes) can not only effectively address depth issues but also offer complementary anatomical, functional, and molecular information by integrating the advantages of different imaging technologies [[74], [75], [76]].

The other critical challenge focuses on biosafety, which also impedes the clinical translation of most optical probes for thermogenic fat imaging. Genetically encoded fluorescent/luminescent proteins rely on genetic engineering, a technique associated with genotoxic and theoretical risks [77,78]. Their use in humans is prohibited unless the safety of relevant models and tools has been fully demonstrated. Fluorescent/photoacoustic dyes are generally regarded as reliable optical probes, but they may induce cytotoxicity and phototoxicity under specific physiological conditions. Through molecule engineering, these dyes can be coupled with or encapsulated in highly biocompatible materials, which reduces their inherent toxicity and improves in vivo tolerability [[79], [80], [81], [82]]. Cherenkov luminescence probes also confront a fundamental limitation, that is radioactivity. This hazard can be minimized by utilizing radioactive tracers with short half-lives and low injection doses, as exemplified by 18F-FDG [83].

With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), optical probe development is ushering in a transformative new era. By leveraging powerful capabilities in data-driven learning and reasoning, AI is reshaping probe design paradigms [84,85]. Specifically, when the molecular structure of a candidate probe is input, trained models can systematically predict its physicochemical properties and imaging performance, which greatly streamlines and accelerates the process of probe design and screening [[86], [87], [88]]. Moreover, AI can simulate probe-biomolecule interactions to assess critical aspects like targeting efficiency and potential biosafety risks of optical probes [[89], [90], [91]]. In imaging applications, AI also becomes an indispensable analytical tool that improves image quality by effectively denoising and enhancing probe signals [[92], [93], [94], [95]]. Collectively, these AI-enabled strategies will expedite the iterative optimization of thermogenic fat probes and their eventual clinical translation.

Conclusion

Thermogenic fat is a linchpin in understanding pathological mechanisms of certain metabolic diseases. As a non-invasive approach, optical imaging holds tremendous promise for detecting thermogenic fat. To fully unlock this potential, contemporary research efforts have increasingly focused on developing advanced optical probes. In this review, we summarize four categories of optical probes, detailing their physicochemical characteristics and performances in tracking thermogenic fat. More in-depth, we discuss their imaging targets, design principles, barriers to clinical application, and future development trends (particularly integration with AI technologies). As a scientific reference, this review is expected to enlighten the design of next-generation optical probes and accelerate their clinical translation for thermogenic fat imaging.
