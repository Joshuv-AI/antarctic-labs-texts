Oculomotor chronometry of spoken word structure processing
Authors: Oren Kadosh, Benjamin Menashe, Yael Gera, Michal Ben-Shachar, Yoram S. Bonneh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Oculomotor inhibition (OMI) is the momentary inhibition of involuntary eye movements, such as saccades and blinks, following sensory stimulation. Higher-level cognitive processes, such as processing the structure of written words, have been shown to affect the duration and magnitude of the OMI. In this study we tested whether OMI measures are influenced by the processing of spoken word structures. Participants listened to Hebrew words and pseudowords presented auditorily while we recorded their microsaccades, eye blinks, and pupil dilation. Spoken pseudowords were divided into two groups based on their underlying linguistic structure, with one half containing real roots and the other half containing invented roots. Results show a greater OMI for Real-root pseudowords as compared to Invented-root pseudowords, replicating the morpheme interference effect found previously for written stimuli. OMI measures, including microsaccade and eye blink latencies, as well as pupil dilation peak latency, were consistently greater for real-root pseudowords compared to invented-root pseudowords. These findings demonstrate the sensitivity of OMI to the cognitive processing of spoken word structure, even in the absence of visual stimuli or visually directed task. The results highlight the potential role of oculomotor responses as a marker of higher-order linguistic processing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21869-8.

Introduction

Oculomotor inhibition (OMI) is the temporary suppression of involuntary eye movements, following sensory stimulation. This “oculomotor freezing”1 process was first reported for saccades, termed “saccadic inhibition”2–5 and has since been studied extensively in microsaccades which are miniature fixational eye movements6–11. However, recently, it has been described as a general effect, extending beyond saccades to include eye blinks12,13, smooth pursuit, catchup saccades during pursuit14, and very recently even ocular drift15,16. This suggests a coordinated modulation across different components of the oculomotor system following salient events.

The characteristics of OMI, including its timing and magnitude, are sensitive to various factors. They are known to be modulated by stimulus properties, such as visual saliency (e.g., manipulation of contrast or spatial frequency10,17, and auditory saliency (e.g., pure tone, environmental sounds18,19, as well as by top-down cognitive factors like attention and expectation7,11,13,20–23. Interestingly, deviant or unexpected stimuli, both visual and auditory, often lead to prolonged inhibition compared to standard stimuli9,24–27. These modulations impact not only eye movements but also associated behaviors like blinks and autonomic responses reflected in pupil dilation12,18,28,29. Moreover, the notion of a common neural input driving both saccades and pupil size has recently been supported by findings demonstrating pupil modulation according to task difficulty, reaction time, and behavioral performance, closely coupled with saccadic activity29–31.

We have previously reported that OMI in microsaccades and blinks is sensitive to the structure of written words32, using the morpheme interference effect (MIE), a well-known psycholinguistic phenomenon33,34. The MIE is demonstrated as the response difference between two types of stimuli: pseudowords composed of real morphemes (e.g., shootment) and pseudowords consisting of an invented morpheme (e.g., shootmant). During a lexical decision task where participants are instructed to judge whether a given stimulus is a word or not, participants take longer and make more errors when responding to pseudowords made up of real morphemes, compared to pseudowords containing invented morphemes. Hebrew words are formed using roots that provide the core meaning of the word, same as morphemes. Using this paradigm with written Hebrew stimuli35, it was shown that both microsaccade inhibition and blink inhibition were longer for Real-root Hebrew pseudowords compared to Invented-root Hebrew pseudowords32.

The link between microsaccades and higher-level cognition is not limited to the classic rate-inhibition pattern observed under forced fixation tasks8. Research indicates that overall microsaccade rates can decrease with increased task difficulty36,37 or working memory load rate38,39. Furthermore, microsaccade rate can reflect factors like name and face familiarity40–42, responses to unpleasant visual stimuli43, and even aspects of auditory processing like speech segmentation44. Notably, recent studies focusing on the latency of microsaccades following events like saccade completion found that these latencies are sensitive to stimulus features like saliency, spatial frequency, and deviancy17,27. This suggests that the timing of microsaccades can provide valuable indications of stimulus processing and cognitive engagement, regardless of whether the classic OMI rate profile is observed, potentially reflecting aspects of a generalized inhibitory or modulatory mechanism.

In addition, pupil dilation has been highlighted as an indicator of higher-level cognitive and perceptual processing, in particular, cognitive load45–47. Several studies have applied this measure to processing load in language tasks (see review48. For example, a recent study found a greater degree of cognitive effort, indicated by larger pupil dilation, for written pseudowords compared with real words49. Another study found increased pupil dilation and longer peak latency for low-frequency words using a lexical decision task50. Together with the OMI results, these findings establish the sensitivity of ocular markers to abstract linguistic features, but generalization beyond the visual domain remains to be tested.

The current study addresses the question of cross-modal generalization. We tested whether OMI measures are sensitive to higher-level word structure information presented auditorily, by utilizing the morpheme interference effect. Importantly, we focused on a later, secondary inhibition phase at variable latencies due to variable stimulus durations. We hypothesized that this secondary phase reflects a critical moment in speech processing when listeners can reliably discriminate Real-root and Invented-root pseudowords as well as real words. If a greater OMI is observed for spoken Real-root pseudowords compared to spoken Invented-root pseudowords, that is evidence supporting the sensitivity of microsaccades, blinks and pupil dilation to higher-level cognitive processes, across modalities.

Discussion

We measured the effect of pseudoword structure on oculomotor responses during auditory stimulus presentation. The results showed prolonged oculomotor inhibition to pseudowords containing a real root as compared to pseudowords containing an invented root, consistent with our previous findings using written words32. Both studies reproduce a well-established psycholinguistic phenomenon, the morpheme interference effect51, using oculomotor inhibition, a covert measure which is less susceptible to controlled processes.

The current results coupled with Yablonski et al.32, generalize the oculomotor-MIE across presentation modalities: reading and speech processing. A similar generalization across-modalities of the MIE has been demonstrated previously with overt responses and RT measures51, but this is the first time it has been tested using OMI measures. Although OMI following auditory stimulation, such as pure tones18,25,53,54 and salient environmental sounds19 has been previously reported, its generalization to speech is far from trivial and even surprising, given the distinct sensory and cognitive processing mechanisms involved in speech perception. Consequently, the results of this study are evidence that oculomotor inhibition is sensitive to abstract features, cross-modally.

Auditory speech processing is different than reading in many aspects, beyond the pure sensory requirements. An auditory stimulus is spread in time, while a visual stimulus is presented all at once. This allows readers to process written words non-linearly (i.e., not start-to-end), for example by processing the external letters first55, or by employing whole-word recognition methods56–58. However, for auditory stimuli, due to the constraint of the modality, processing occurs more sequentially as the stimulus unfolds over time. This also allows for dynamic, ongoing recognition of the stimulus, whereby the listener can compute a distribution for possible words and update accordingly with each new segment heard59,60. Thus, our results show that OMI generalizes from non-linear holistic visual processing to sequential dynamic processing.

Another key difference between auditory and visual presentation is that auditory stimuli vary in their Uniqueness Point (UP) and in their duration. The UP is the critical moment in time when there are no competing words that match the stimulus presented so far. At this point, the participant may recognize the stimulus with certainty as the sole matching lexical item or reject it as a nonword if there is no such match59,60. For time-sensitive measures, such as oculomotor responses, the UP offers a more cognitively meaningful temporal anchor than stimulus onset or offset. It serves as a cognitive breakpoint, a mental punctuation mark within the stream of auditory processing. This is particularly relevant given that oculomotor activity is modulated not only by external sensory input but also by internal cognitive states and active sensing strategies61,62. In the present study, we investigated the sensitivity of oculomotor inhibition (OMI) to such internal cognitive events, focusing on a secondary inhibition phase following the initial inhibition typically observed at stimulus onset. We hypothesized that this later inhibitory response would be time-locked to the UP, reflecting a top-down modulation of OMI by higher-order cognitive processes. In this view, the OMI phenomenon is not purely stimulus-driven but is influenced by ongoing lexical evaluation processes, thus offering a physiological correlate to the theoretical construct of the UP. Consequently, we measured all the ocular responses within a time window relative to the UP of each stimulus. The variation in stimulus duration may affect participants’ responses by extending the preparation time for eye movements or blinks in longer stimuli, potentially reducing latencies. As such, we included the stimulus duration and the UP as covariates in our statistical models. We also conducted a secondary analysis using subsets of the stimuli, balanced for their UP, which revealed similar results (see Supplementary Materials S2 and S3).

The results show a significant MIE using microsaccade reaction time (msRT) and using eye blink reaction time (blink RT), such that Real-root pseudowords had longer msRT and blink RT as compared to Invented-root pseudowords. The temporal dynamics of these robust effects show patterns similar to our previous study32, despite the difference in stimulus modality and the use of reaction times measured relative to the stimulus UP. Moreover, a positive correlation was observed between msRT and Blink-RT (see Figure S4 in the Supplementary Material), as previously reported12. Pupil measures were also examined in this study, namely the latency of peak dilation (pupil-RT). Results show a significant MIE for pupil-RT, as shown for msRT and Blink-RT despite excluding trials interrupted by blinks (additional analyses are available in the Supplementary Materials, see Figure S4). These findings further support the notion of a shared oculomotor inhibition mechanism29.

In sum, these findings demonstrate that oculomotor measures, such as microsaccade latency, blink latency, and pupil dilation, can reliably distinguish between pseudowords containing real morphological roots and those with invented roots. This sensitivity to morphological structure reveals that oculomotor inhibition is modulated not only by sensory properties but also by abstract linguistic features, reflecting higher-order lexical evaluation processes, even in the auditory modality. Importantly, auditory MIE may be implemented in younger populations, such as children who have not yet mastered reading, or in people with dyslexia whose reading abilities are impaired. Additionally, covert oculomotor measures without an overt manual response are better suited for clinical populations, such as stroke patients. As such, future work can build upon this study to further investigate higher-level cognitive processes such as morphological processing across development, in diverse populations, and in clinical settings.

Methods

Participants

A total of seventeen participants, aged between 20 and 40, were recruited; all of them were native Hebrew speakers studying in Bar-Ilan University. Two participants were excluded from the analysis due to poor eye-tracking recordings (less than 30% valid data). The experiment was approved by the Bar-Ilan University Ethics Committee and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Furthermore, all methods and procedures were conducted in compliance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Apparatus

The auditory stimuli were played through headphones at 70 dB, while participants were seated 0.6 m away from a 24-inch, 100 Hz computer screen with stabilized head using a chin and forehead rest. The experiment was conducted in dim light conditions using an in-house-developed psychophysical platform (PSY) developed by Y.S. Bonneh. We used a remote camera-based eye-tracking device (Eyelink-1000, SR Research) with a sampling rate of 500 Hz, and a 35 mm lens positioned 0.52 m from the participants. All recordings were conducted binocularly, but the analyses were performed monocularly using data from the left eye. This approach was chosen based on our experience with the accuracy of microsaccade detection, as reported in our previous studies10,12,32. A standard 9-point calibration was performed before each session.

Stimuli and procedure

For this study we used auditory stimuli developed in a previous study51, consisting of Hebrew words and pseudowords. The main manipulation of the study was between two different types of pseudowords: pseudowords consisting of a real root (Real-root condition) and pseudowords consisting of an invented root (Invented-root condition). Pseudowords were generated by inserting either a real or an invented Hebrew triconsonantal root into an existing Hebrew noun pattern in a balanced manner (patterns used: CaCiC, CCiCa, CiCuC, haCCaCa, hitCaCCut, and maCCeCa, where C indicates the root consonants). Overall, the stimulus list consisted of 100 real words and 100 pseudowords for each condition. The real-word list contained 65 root-derived words, with a mean (± SD) duration: 694 ± 120 ms, and number of phonemes: 5.87 ± 1.17. Pseudowords were matched on duration (Real-root: 796 ± 152 ms, Invented-root: 771 ± 142 ms, p = 0.23), number of phonemes (Real-root: 6.57 ± 1.47, Invented-root: 6.31 ± 1.45, p = 0.21), phonological mean log biphone frequency (Real-root: 3.54 ± 0.50 per million words, Invented-root: 3.52 ± 0.64 per million words, p = 0.78), phonological Coltheart’s N (Real-root: 4.24 ± 3.55, Invented-root: 4.64 ± 3.08, p = 0.40), and phonological OLD20 (Real-root: 1.85 ± 0.29, Invented-root: 1.83 ± 0.28, p = 0.61). However, pseudowords were significantly different in their uniqueness point (Real-root: 541.7 ms, Invented-root: 599 ms, t = 2.97, p = 0.003). The Uniqueness Point (UP) is the critical point in time when the participant can determine if a stimulus is a pseudoword with certainty, as there are no possible matching real words left in the lexicon63–65. This is an essential measure in auditory verbal stimuli, as the UP already provides all the information needed to complete the task (i.e., identify the stimulus type as a nonword). Thus, even though the UP often precedes the offset of the pseudoword, measuring reaction time from the UP rather than word offset is more appropriate for assessing the cognitive processing of a pseudoword, and is the standard method in auditory lexical decision51. Additionally, to account for the imbalance in UP between the pseudoword types, we conducted two separate analyses. In the main analysis, we included the uniqueness point as a covariate in the linear mixed-effects models. In a secondary analysis, we removed 18% of the stimuli from both conditions to balance the uniqueness point and re-ran the statistical analyses (see supplementary materials). All stimuli were recorded by a female native speaker of Hebrew, instructed to read each stimulus out loud with a neutral tone.

The Real words, Real-root pseudowords, and Invented-root pseudowords were presented in a pseudorandom order while participants performed a word detection task. The pseudo randomization was designed to ensure that no more than three consecutive stimuli shared the same response label (e.g., real word vs. pseudoword) or lexical condition. Participants were instructed to respond only to real words to eliminate the potential influence of button presses on the oculomotor measurements. Each pseudoword was presented two times and each real word was presented four times (producing a total of 800 trials), in order to ensure a 50%-50% balance between words and pseudowords presented. Participants were instructed to maintain fixation on a small dark circle (~ 0.5 degrees of visual angle, dva) which was consistently present at the center of the screen against a gray background with a luminance of 50 cd/m2. Half a second after the trial onset, a flashing green cross (0.5 dva) was presented for 300 ms inside the fixation circle, serving as a warning for the upcoming auditory stimulus. Following the auditory stimulus, the participants had an additional 2 seconds to determine and respond with a button press, only when a real word was presented (50% of the trials). Each participant completed five blocks, each block consisting of mixed trials of all three conditions.

Data analysis

Behavioral analysis.

The proportion of errors was recorded for each participant for each pseudoword condition. Since the task was to press a button upon perceiving a real word, errors in the pseudoword conditions correspond to erroneous button presses (false positives). A two-sample paired t-test was conducted to test for a difference in error rates between the pseudoword conditions. One participant was removed from the analysis because their error rate in the Real-root condition exceeded three standard deviations from the mean. A detailed illustration of the proportion of errors per condition per participant can be seen in Fig. S1.

Microsaccade and blink detection

For the microsaccade detection, we used the algorithm introduced by Engbert and Kliegl7, which is based on eye movement velocity and has been used in our previous studies10,17,18,27,32. The raw data were first smoothed using the LOWESS method with a window of 15 ms to optimize microsaccade detection, as this was found to be useful especially with noisy recordings7. Eye movements with a velocity range of 8–150°/s, amplitude range of 0.08-0.5°, and a duration longer than 9 ms were allowed. Eye blinks were detected as in our previous study12. We first defined periods with zero pupil size and then extended them by estimating the eyes’ closing and opening time periods, based on the vertical eye movement that typically precedes the blink32. Eye tracking data were divided into epochs triggered by the stimulus specific uniqueness point in a range of − 0.2 s to 2 s relative to this trigger, with one epoch per experimental trial. Periods of missing data within an epoch, for example during an eye blink, were discarded from the analysis with an additional margin of 50 ms, without discarding the entire epoch. The rejection rate varied across recordings with a mean of 23.6% ±10.3 SD, after the exclusion of two participants with ~ 70% bad recordings.

Calculation of the rate function

The rate modulation functions, MS and Blink rates, were calculated as in our previous studies32,40,66. The rate was calculated based on the raw onset times relative to the Uniqueness Point (UP) of each stimulus, within a window spanning from 200 ms before to 2 s after the UP. The data were baseline-corrected using the pre-UP period. The rate function was computed for each epoch, by convolving a 500/sec rate (the eye-tracker sampling rate) with a Gaussian window with a sigma value of 50 ms at the time of each microsaccade or blink onset (assuming an estimate of only one microsaccade or blink per sample duration for a sampling rate of 500 Hz). Finally, microsaccade rate functions were averaged across participants, including only time points within two standard deviations of each participant’s mean. In contrast, blink rates were averaged by pooling all trials across all participants. Importantly, the main purpose of these rate modulation plots is to illustrate the time course of microsaccade or blink occurrences, not to conduct a statistical analysis.

Microsaccade, blink and pupil reaction time calculation

We computed the first microsaccade or blink occurrence and calculated the latency of the pupil positive dilation peak (msRT, Blink RT, and Pupil RT respectively), relative to the uniqueness point (UP) of each stimulus. We used a time window starting at 100 ms post-UP to capture the release from inhibition and ranging up to each participant’s mean button-press response time (motor-RT). Although responses were recorded only for the real-word condition, the use of motor-RT as upper limit was sufficient for the calculation of the ocular RTs (approximately 800 ms). Only microsaccade and pupil dilation peak latencies that preceded a blink or without a blink in the time range of the analysis were allowed.

Statistical assessment

For the statistical assessment of the differences in oculomotor RTs across the different stimulus types, we employed a linear mixed-effects (LME) analysis67, which is particularly useful when dealing with repeated measures and nested data. Only correct trials were included in the analysis. Each oculomotor RT measure (MS, Blink and Pupil RT) was used as the dependent variable in a separate model. All models included multiple predictor variables: fixed effects of stimulus type (Word, Real-root pseudoword, Invented-root pseudoword), stimulus duration, and uniqueness point, and additionally random effects of stimulus type per participant:
