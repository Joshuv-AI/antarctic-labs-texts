Plasma proteome in LGI-1 autoimmune encephalitis

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeucine-rich glioma-inactivated antibody associated autoimmune encephalitis (LGI-1 AE) is a rare immune mediated neuroinflammatory disease resulting in seizures and cognitive impairment. LGI-1 AE can respond well to immunotherapy, however residual symptoms, particularly cognitive dysfunction may persist despite treatment.We have previously identified perturbations of peripheral innate immune cells (monocytes) in LGI-1 AE in the treated (chronic) phase of the disease. To further understand these peripheral changes, we performed a proteomic analysis of plasma from individuals with LGI-1 AE in the untreated (acute) and chronic phase of the illness.MethodsHigh-throughput proteomic profiling was performed on plasma samples from nine acute and 27 chronic LGI-1 AE participants and 16 age- and sex-matched controls using the SomaLogic 11 k proteomic panel. Differentially expressed proteins (DEP) were identified using limma with false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Bulk RNA sequencing was performed on isolated monocytes from ten of the participants with chronic LGI-1 AE and 28 controls. Differential gene analysis was performed using DESeq2 with FDR < 0.1. Pathway analysis was performed on the transcriptomic and proteomic datasets with gene set enrichment analysis using the Gene Ontology Biological Processes database and FDR < 0.05.ResultsThe plasma analysis identified 215 overexpressed and 1015 suppressed proteins in the acute cohort compared with the healthy cohort. Upregulated proteins included proteins related to innate immune activation, chemoattractants for myeloid cells, components of the complement cascade as well as lymphocyte signalling and activation molecules. Significantly downregulated pathways included those related to autophagy. There were no significantly differentially expressed proteins (DEP) between the acute and chronic or chronic and control cohorts. Monocyte transcriptomic analysis revealed 18 upregulated and 170 downregulated genes, highlighting interleukin (IL)-6 and downstream IL-6 pathway signalling as key dysregulated pathways.ConclusionsThis study identified IL-6 signalling and activation of the terminal complement cascade as potential pathophysiological immune mechanisms, highlighting opportunities for therapeutic repurposing using existing agents in LGI-1 AE. In addition, the observed suppression of autophagy- related pathways suggests a previously underappreciated mechanism that may contribute to disease pathogenesis, warranting further investigations into the role of autophagy dysregulation in CNS autoimmunity associated with LGI-1 AE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03851-x.Keywords: Autoimmune encephalitis, LGI-1, Biomarkers, Proteomics, Transcriptomics, IL-6, Complement, Autophagy

Introduction

Leucine-rich glioma-inactivated antibody associated autoimmune encephalitis (LGI-1 AE) is a central nervous system (CNS) antibody mediated disorder characterized by immune mediated inflammation of the brain parenchyma [1] and represents the most common form of autoimmune encephalitis in adults [2]. In the acute phase, patients with LGI-1 AE typically present with features of a limbic encephalitis including cognitive dysfunction, seizures, and psychosis [3]. LGI-1 AE is defined by antibodies against the neuronal cell surface antigen leucine-rich glioma-inactivated 1 (LGI-1), a component of the voltage-gated potassium channel complex that stabilizes ⍺-amino-3-hrdoxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazoleproprionic acid (AMPA) receptors [4] and is predominately expressed in the hippocampus and limbic structures [5]. LGI-1 AE can respond well to immunotherapy [6]. However residual symptoms particularly cognitive dysfunction can persist despite treatment [7] leading to impaired quality of life [8].

Cell based studies investigating CNS immune mechanisms in LGI-1 AE have demonstrated a clonal expansion of B cells, plasmablasts and plasma cells in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [9, 10] as well as the peripheral circulation [10]. Targeted enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)-based proteomic analyses have further demonstrated upregulation of a range of inflammatory and adaptive immune related cytokines including interleukin (IL)−6, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL)6, IL17A, CCL2, IL2, CXCL13, a proliferation inducing ligand (APRIL), and B-cell activating factor (BAFF) [11–13]. CSF mass spectroscopy analysis in a small cohort of nine patients with LGI-1 AE, four of whom were on treatment [14], demonstrated upregulated pathways related to the humoral immune response, T and B cell activation, and neurodegeneration, while pathways related to immune regulation were down-regulated.

We have previously identified perturbations of peripheral innate immune cells (monocytes) in LGI-1 AE in the treated (chronic) phase of the disease [15]. Analysing the peripheral circulation offers a less invasive method than cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) collection for identifying systemic biomarkers and allows for larger, more representative study cohorts across different disease phases. While LGI-1 AE is primarily a CNS disorder, investigating the plasma proteome is essential to determine if key immune processes are compartmentalized within the brain or if they involve a detectable shift in the circulating cells and proteins.

To further understand the systemic immune response in LGI-1 AE, this study utilized high-throughput plasma proteomics and monocyte transcriptomics to investigate whether peripheral immune perturbation, previously identified in the chronic phase, are also present in untreated patients in the acute phase of the illness acute patients.

Methods

Participant identification and recruitment

Patients diagnosed with LGI-1 AE between January 2018 to June 2025 were identified and recruited through the Australian Autoimmune Encephalitis Consortium (see Supplementary Table S1 for list of investigators and recruiting sites). Patients were required to have LGI-1 autoantibodies identified on serum samples via cell-based assay, and a clinical phenotype consistent with this diagnosis.

Ethics approval

The Human Research Ethics Committee at Alfred Health (HREC/17/Alfred/168) approved the study, with institutional review board (IRB) approval from all participating sites. All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Study design

Biological sample collection during the initial illness and prior to immunotherapy was considered ‘acute’. Sample collection at least 3 months after treatment initiation was considered ‘chronic’. All acute patients were untreated, and all chronic patients were treated. A cohort of age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers were recruited through local advertisement as an unaffected control cohort (n = 16). Healthy volunteers were required to have no previous or active neurological, autoimmune or neoplastic medical conditions and were not permitted to be on immunosuppressing medications. Plasma and whole blood for cell isolation were collected.

Monocyte isolation and RNA sequencing

Monocytes were isolated from whole blood using a negative selection method as previously described [16] and stored at −80C. Total ribonucleic acid (RNA) was extracted from stored monocytes using the Qiagen RNAeasy kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were prepared using the Illumina stranded mRNA library preparation protocol with poly-A mRNA enrichment. Paired-end whole transcriptome sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeqX Plus at a depth of 20 million reads per sample with a read length of 150 bp.

RNA sequencing data quality control was performed using FastQC and MultiQC. Adapter sequences were trimmed using bbduk. Sequencing data was filtered for ribosomal RNA using ribodetector. Alignment was performed using STAR with Gencode human genome v 37. Quantification was performed using salmon. Genes detected in at least three samples with a minimum count of ten were retained for principal component analysis (PCA) and differential gene expression (DGE) analysis. DGE between the LGI-1 and healthy cohorts was performed using DESeq2 in RStudio with R version 4.5.1 with the following design: ~ cohort + sex + age. False Discovery Rate (FDR) was set at 0.1. Gene pathway analysis was performed using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) with GOprofiler using the Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Processes (BP) database. Genes were ranked by using the formula logFC*-log10(p value).

Proteomic analysis

Blood was collected in 10 mL Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes. All samples were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min at 4 C within 90 min of collection and immediately frozen at −80C in 600 µL aliquots. High-throughput proteomic profiling was performed on the plasma samples using the SomaLogic 11 k proteomic panel. All proteomic analysis was done in RStudio with R version 4.5.1. Data preprocessing, including quality control, normalisation, and log transformation, was conducted using the SomaDataIO package. Proteins detected in at least three samples with a minimum count of ten were retained for principal component analysis (PCA) and differential protein expression analysis (DPE). DPE between healthy controls, ‘chronic’ LGI-1 and ‘acute’ LGI-1 cohorts was assessed using voom/limma with the following design: ~ 0 + cohort + sex + age and placed into blocks based on participant ID to account for paired sampling. FDR was set at 0.05. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using GSEA with GOprofiler with the GO BP database with a minimum cluster size of 25 and adjusted p value cut off of < 0.05. Proteins were ranked by log-fold change (logFC).

Clinical data and outcome analysis

Age, sex assigned at birth, immunotherapy, treatment delay (time from symptom onset to treatment), time since illness (time from symptom onset to serum testing), total follow-up time (time from symptom onset to last recorded follow-up), and symptoms during acute illness (seizures, faciobrachial dystonic seizures [FBDS]) were recorded. Rituximab exposure was considered current if given in the last twelve months. The nadir modified Rankin Scale score (mRS) and Clinical Assessment Scale in Autoimmune Encephalitis (CASE) score during the acute illness, at discharge and recorded during testing were used as the clinical outcome variables. Any relapses from treatment to final follow-up were recorded. Cognitive performance on psychometric testing performed at the time of testing for the chronic cohort was stratified into cognitive bands as previously described [17]. Neuroradiology reports extracted from the database of any MRI brain during the acute phase and last follow-up MRI brain were used to determine mesial temporal lobe abnormalities at these timepoints.

Categorical clinical data were compared with expression levels using regression analysis adjusting for age and time from symptom onset to testing, while correlations with continuous variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. For all clinical data analyses, statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 52 participants were included in the study; 16 healthy participants, ten males and six females with a median age of 61 (IQR 6.0), nine participants with acute LGI-1 AE and 27 participants with chronic LGI-1 AE. Demographic and clinical characteristics of the LGI-1 AE participants are shown in Table 1. In the chronic cohort several participants remained on immunotherapy at the time of sampling with medications including prednisolone (22%), mycophenolate (19%), intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) (11%), rituximab (30%) and azathioprine (4%). No participants had current malignancy. Three had a previous history of malignancy more than 5 years prior and in remission (one breast cancer, one both melanoma and prostate cancer, one renal cell cancer) and one participant in the acute cohort was diagnosed with chronic myeloid leukemia six months after diagnosis of LGI-1 AE (and sample testing). Five participants had actively managed co-morbidities (one controlled Human immunodeficiency virus [HIV], one asbestosis, one hypothyroidism, one diabetes and one chronic kidney disease) and two participants had previous CNS pathology (one traumatic brain injury, one cerebral infarct). Only two participants developed drug resistant epilepsy. Due to its rarity, this outcome could not be evaluated in subsequent analysis.

Proteomic analysis

High-throughout proteomic analysis was performed using the SomaLogic aptamer-based platform evaluating approximately 11,000 proteins. Of these, 10,755 proteins passed quality control. Following exclusion of proteins with insufficient expression across the cohort, 10,611 proteins were retained in the analysis. DPE analysis between three cohorts (acute LGI-1 AE, chronic LGI-1 AE and an age-matched healthy control cohort) was performed using limma, controlling for age, sex assigned at birth and repeated measures. Principal component analysis (PCA) plotting demonstrated substantial overlap between the three cohorts (Fig. 1A).

Acute LGI-1 AE vs Healthy controls

Of the 10,611 proteins evaluated, 215 were significantly overexpressed and 1015 significantly suppressed in the acute LGI-1 AE cohort compared with the healthy control cohort (Fig. 1B).

Overexpressed proteins included proteins related to innate immune activation (S100A9, TREM1, S100A8, IL6, FCAR), proteins that act as chemoattractants for myeloid cells (DEF1, DEFA4, CCL23, CCL15), acute phase reactants (SAA1, SAA2, ELANE), components of the complement cascade (C5, C9) as well as lymphocyte signalling and activation molecules (IL2, CCL19, CD79B).

Suppressed proteins included proteins related to innate activation (MIF, YWHAH), chemotaxis (CXCL3, LTB4R, CXCL2, CCL5, LECT2), NF-kB and inflammasome activation (IKBKG, RELA, TIRAP, IKBKB, PANX1, CASP6), immunoregulation (IRF3, LECT2, TOLLIP, TREML1, TANK, MANF) and lymphocyte chemotaxis, activation and antigen presentation (IL2RG, CCL22, TAGLN2, HLAB). Additionally, we identified proteins related to neuronal remodelling (YWHAG, BDNF, GAP43) and synaptic proteins (DNM1, SH3GL3, SH3GL2, SNX4, SNX9, STX4, SNX12, SNX32, SYAP1, SYNJ1, S100A5, GMFB, SYT4, CACNB3). Expression heatmaps detailing differentially expressed proteins are shown in Fig. 2.

GSEA using the GO BP gene sets identified 19 activated and 520 suppressed pathways in the acute LGI-1 AE group compared with the healthy controls (Fig. 3A, B). Hierarchical clustering of the activated pathways demonstrated five predominant themes: ‘humoral defence’, ‘disruption of other organisms’, complement regulation’, acute-phase inflammatory response’ and ‘lipoprotein complex remodelling’ (Supplementary Figure S1). A category net plot demonstrating the links between the top pathway from each theme (‘acute inflammatory response’, ‘complement activation’, ‘humoral immune response’, ‘killing of cells of another organism’ and ‘protein-lipid complex remodelling’) is shown in Fig. 3C. Within the ‘complement activation’ pathway the only core enrichment proteins that were also significantly differentially expressed were C5a and COLEC10. However, the other protein that make up the terminal complement cascade (C5bC6, C7, C8, C9) correlated strongly with C5a (Supplementary Figure S2). Notable supressed pathways include ‘macroautophagy’ and ‘endosomal transport’ (Fig. 3D).

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the impact of including participants in the chronic cohort who are within 12 months of acute illness we performed a sensitivity analysis using a 12-month post treatment cut-off for the chronic cohort. This analysis demonstrated minimal impact on the DPE, pathway analysis and clinical correlation (Supplementary Figures S5, S6, S7).

Transcriptomic analysis

DGE analysis between the LGI-1 and healthy cohorts identified 18 genes upregulated and 170 genes downregulated with an adjusted FDR < 0.1 out of 16,902 genes (Fig. 6A). The primary upregulated gene of interest was IL-6. Downregulated genes of interest included genes related to lymphocyte chemotaxis, activation and antigen presentation (CD226), innate activation (TMEM45A, TFPI, P2RY12), inflammation (CD109, PDE5A, NCOA4), NF-kB and inflammasome activation (ANO6, PANX1, LRRC8D, LRRC8B), chemotaxis and cell migration (CD99, CXCL5, AVPR1A, CYTL1) and immunoregulation (CD9, TRIM58).

Gene set enrichment analysis using the gene ontology biological processes gene sets identified activated and suppressed pathways in the acute LGI-1 AE group compared with the chronic LGI-1 AE cohort (Fig. 6B). Most notably ‘mature B-cell differentiation and immune response’, ‘acute-phase response’ and the ‘IL-6 signalling’ pathway were activated while pathways related to regulation of the neuronal apoptosis process were down-regulated. The activated pathways demonstrated very low gene counts and were driven largely by upregulation of IL-6.

We identified IL-6 to be the only protein overexpressed in both transcriptomic and proteomic analyses, while notable under-expressed proteins identified in both analyses were TPM4, TAGLN2, PANX1, NT5C3A and CAVIN1.

Discussion

In this study we characterised the plasma proteome of patients with LGI-1 AE across different phases of the disease to better understand the peripheral immune response associated with the condition. The acute phase of LGI-1 was marked by upregulation of innate immune and inflammatory pathways, particularly complement activation. In contrast, the chronic phase of LGI-1 AE was heterogenous. Both the acute phase, and to a lesser extent the chronic phase, were also characterized by suppression of autophagy pathways. Finally, a transcriptomic analysis of peripheral monocytes in the chronic phase identified IL-6 as a cytokine of interest.

Previous cytokine studies in LGI-1 AE have demonstrated a predominantly inflammatory serum profile in untreated patients including elevated levels of IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, CXCL8/IL-8, IL-10, IL-17A, IL-18, IL-35, GM-CSF, CCL2, CCL3 and CCL4 [11–13], although these findings have varied across studies. CSF proteomic studies have identified a broadly similar inflammatory profile with additional enrichment of B cell associated cytokines including CXCL13, BAFF and APRIL [13]. With our high-throughput proteomic approach using an aptamer-based technology our study did not demonstrate significant elevations in these proteins, though we did see elevation of a small number of lymphocyte related proteins (IL2, CCL19, CD79B) in the acute LGI-1 AE cohort. Conversely, we identified a reduction in proteins related to antigen presentation and lymphocyte chemotaxis (IL2RG, CCL22, TAGLN2, HLAB). Given LGI-1 AE is largely a CNS disorder, it is possible that key immune processes are compartmentalised within the CNS and therefore not adequately reflected in the peripheral circulation. Alternatively, these findings may indicate a phenotypic shift in peripheral immune cells away from antigen presentation towards a pro-inflammatory phenotype. We had previously demonstrated a reduction of HLADR expression in peripheral monocytes in AE, albeit in a treated population [15].

The acute phase inflammatory response was a characteristic of the acute LGI-1 AE cohort, demonstrating upregulated proteins related to innate immune activation (S100A9, TREM1, S100A8, IL6, FCAR), proteins that act as chemoattractants for myeloid cells (DEF1, DEFA4, CCL23, CCL15) and acute phase reactants (SAA1, SAA2, ELANE) while immunoregulatory proteins were suppressed (IRF3, LECT2, TOLLIP, TREML1, TANK, MANF). Interestingly proteins related to the NF-kB and inflammasome pathways (IKBKG, RELA, TIRAP, IKBKB, PANX1, CASP6) were suppressed in this same cohort.

Our study demonstrated elevated plasma IL-6 in the acute phase of LGI-1 AE and upregulation of IL-6 mRNA in peripheral monocytes in the chronic phase. IL-6 is a cytokine that is synthesized during the early phase of inflammation [18]. It subsequently induces the production of other acute phase reactants such as serum amyloid alpha (SAA) [18]. IL-6 can also promote differentiation of naïve CD4 + T cells into Th17 cells, which may disrupt immunological tolerance, and promotes differentiation of activated B cells into plasma cells [18]. In LGI-1 AE IL-6 has been demonstrated to be elevated in the serum and CSF in previous studies [12, 13] and has been correlated with disease severity (as defined by mRS) [12, 13]. It has been suggested that IL-6 may be a therapeutic target in LGI-1 AE as it is in neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) and there is an international clinical trial for the use of an IL-6R antagonist in LGI-1 AE currently recruiting [19].

The upregulated pathway with the highest percentage of proteins enriched in the acute LGI-1 AE cohort was ‘complement activation’. This was driven by increased expression of C5, C6, C7, C9 as well as C1q, C2 and C4a. C5 was also differentially expressed and significantly correlated with elevation in the other components of the terminal portion of the complement cascade. While altered expression of complement proteins can be seen during systemic inflammation[20], the complement cascade is an important part of the pathogenesis of other CNS and peripheral nervous system (PNS) antibody mediated diseases such as NMOSD [21] and acetylcholine receptor antibody related myasthenia gravis (MG) [22] and are key drivers of inflammatory activity in these diseases. The involvement of the complement system in LGI-1 AE is complicated, as the most common anti-LGI-1 antibody isotype is immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4) [23], which is thought to be a poor activator of the complement cascade [24]. However, some individuals with LGI-1 AE have additional IgG 1/2/3, which can activate the complement cascade [25]. Early histopathological studies in LGI-1 AE have also demonstrated complement deposition within the hippocampus of affected patients. Importantly, the presence of IgG1/2/3 subclasses in LGI-1 AE has been associated with hippocampal sclerosis even in the absence of clear clinical correlate [25], which is thought to be driven by complement mediated neuronal injury. If validated this could provide an important adjunct therapy in LGI-1 AE. Terminal complement pathway inhibitors have proved highly effective in NMOSD [21] and MG [22] and early use in LGI-1 AE may prevent neuronal destruction and the long-term sequelae of the disease. We did not characterize IgG isotypes in this study, but it would be of interest in future studies to evaluate if IgG isotype correlated with C5 and C9 levels in the plasma or CSF.

An entirely novel finding in our study was the suppression of ‘autophagy’ pathways in acute and chronic LGI-1 AE as compared with the healthy cohort. Autophagy is a fundamental ‘housekeeping’ and stress response process. Cells degrade and recycle their own components through the lysosomal system [26]. The consequences of dysregulated autophagy can include chronic or excessive inflammation and downstream inflammasome activation [27]. It can also lead to aberrant innate immune cell activation shifting towards an inflammatory phenotype [27]. Reduced autophagy has been identified as a component of disease pathogenesis in other autoimmune diseases such as the autoimmune connective disease systemic lupus erythematous (SLE) [28] and in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). In SLE failure to clear cellular debris results in increased autoantigen availability and presentation [28]. In IBD failure to clear damage signals increases damage receptor activation and increases inflammasome activity [29]. Both these mechanisms could contribute to the initiation of the immune response in LGI-1 AE.

Of note, were the clusters of participants with different patterns of protein expression in the chronic LGI-1 AE group, with some participants proteome more closely representing a ‘healthy’ individual, and others more characteristic of the acute LGI-1 AE proteome. The long-term outcomes of people who have been treated for LGI-1 AE is also heterogenous, with persistence of cognitive impairment [7] and fatigue [8] impacting their quality of life for a proportion [8]. Although we could not identify any clinical correlate of the clusters, this finding remains intriguing and it would be interesting to determine what drives the heterogeneity – is it related to intrinsic patient factors (e.g. genetic, epigenetic risks) or related to disease itself and response to treatment. The observed proteomic overlap between acute and chronic participants suggests that these signatures may serve as indicators of biological disease activity rather than purely temporal disease stages. Our LGI-RPIS successfully identified a subset of chronic patients with a persistent acute-like inflammatory profile. While this did not correlate with standard clinical scores in our current cohort, future longitudinal studies utilizing both peripheral blood and CSF are needed to determine if these signatures, particularly IL-6 and terminal complement activation, correlate with relapse activity or subtle markers of treatment failure. Stratifying patients by these molecular markers could eventually guide personalized treatment escalation in LGI-1 AE.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. As a cross-sectional cohort study, we are unable to infer any causal or temporal relationships and there are multiple potential confounders, most notably comorbid disease and previous or existing immunotherapy. As a rare disease the number of patients in this study, particularly in the sub-type analysis, are small. Our chronic definition of three months post treatment was used to best capture participants who were no longer in the acute phase of the disease. However, this design could include participants in the chronic cohort who are incompletely treated, or relapsing, and therefore better reflect acute pathology. Our sensitivity analysis demonstrated minimal difference using a cut-off of twelve months. Additionally, the median time since treatment in our chronic cohort was > 2.5 years, so it is likely most of this cohort appropriately represents the chronic phase of the disease. Future longitudinal study designs would enable paired samples to better account for potential confounders.

For the transcriptomic analysis there were several potential limitations. First the isolation procedure for the monocytes results in purity of 80—90%. RNA from other peripheral cells, while making up a small proportion, could add variability to the results. We also used the more lenient FDR cut-off of 0.1 for the RNA analysis which would result in more false positive findings. This is mitigated by the multi-omic validation through the proteome analysis.

We focussed on the peripheral compartment and did not sample the more CNS-approximate CSF compartment, so that our findings may not represent CNS-relevant changes. Further examination of the CNS, through CSF sampling, would be advisable to further characterize the interplay between the peripheral and central biological pathways. Despite these limitations, LGI-1 AE is a rare disease, and the challenges of recruiting and sampling adequately sized cohorts are intrinsic to research in this field.

Finally, this is an exploratory study, and the interpretation of the proteomic data requires caution. The SomaLogic technology measures relative change in protein concentrations and therefore only provides comparisons of protein expression across samples or cohorts but not ‘actual’ protein concentrations. It also does not allow for measurement of protein or pathway activity. Our pathway analysis, which is based on proteins that are relatively under- or over-expressed between the groups, identifies clusters of differentially expressed proteins that have been classified as being part of specific pathways or processes using the GO database. These factors mean that the true significance of the identified proteins and pathways is uncertain. For example, autophagy is difficult to measure in vivo. This study did not assess the contents of autophagosomes or autophagic flux within cells, and thus only identifies ‘autophagy’ as a pathway of interest, based on expression of plasma proteins, of which we encourage further evaluation.

Conclusion

Using integrated proteomic and transcriptomic analyses across different disease phases of LGI-1 AE, we identified IL-6 and the terminal complement cascade as potential pathophysiological immune mechanisms, highlighting opportunities for therapeutic repurposing using existing anti-IL-6 agents or complement inhibitors in LGI-1 AE. In addition, the observed suppression of autophagy- related pathways suggests a previously underappreciated mechanism that contribute to disease pathogenesis, warranting further investigations into the role of autophagy dysregulation in CNS autoimmunity associated with LGI-1 AE.
