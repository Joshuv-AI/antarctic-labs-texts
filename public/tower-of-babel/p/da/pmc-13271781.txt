Afirma genomic sequencing classifier performance in young patients with cytologically indeterminate thyroid nodules
Authors: Luz E Castellanos, Julian Bryan, Julio C Ricarte-Filho, Aime T Franco, Mohammed Alshalalfa, Joshua P Klopper, Richard T Kloos, Steven G Waguespack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextThe Afirma Genomic Sequencing Classifier (GSC) is validated in patients ≥ 21 years with a 96% negative predictive value for malignancy when GSC-(B)enign. Afirma GSC has not been formally studied in patients <21 years of age with indeterminate thyroid nodules (ITNs).ObjectiveTo evaluate Afirma GSC in young patients with ITNs.DesignRetrospective analysis of Afirma GSC testing.SettingITNs referred for molecular testing in a real-world settingParticipantsForty-nine ITNs from 49 patients < 21 years of age who had histopathology or 2 years' clinical follow-up data ascertained.InterventionNone.Main outcome measureAfirma GSC test performanceResultsIn 49 ITNs from patients aged 9 to 20 years (median 18.5 [interquartile range, 17.3-19.8]), 30 were GSC-B and 19 GSC-(S)uspicious, among which 14 (73.7%) were malignant (ie, true positive) and 5 (26.3%) were benign (ie, false positive). All 30 Afirma GSC-B cases were either histologically (n = 9) or clinically benign (n = 21) (ie, true negative). All 14 malignancies were GSC-S (sensitivity 100% [95% CI, 77-100]); 30/35 clinically or histologically benign cases were GSC-B (specificity 86% [95% CI, 70-95]). Negative predictive value for an Afirma GSC-B result was 100% [95% CI, 88-100]. Genomic alterations were not detected in the 30 GSC-B samples. Among the 14 malignant samples, there were 9 papillary thyroid carcinomas, 1 oncocytic carcinoma, 1 noninvasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features, and 3 follicular thyroid carcinomas.ConclusionIn young patients with ITNs, the Afirma GSC demonstrated an excellent negative predictive value when defining a thyroid nodule result by histology or clinical follow-up.

Compared with adults, thyroid nodules in the pediatric population are more likely to be malignant (∼20%-25% vs ∼12%) (1, 2) and can present a unique clinical challenge, particularly when classified as indeterminate on fine-needle aspiration (FNA) cytopathology. Based on The Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology (3), indeterminate thyroid nodules (ITNs) comprise 3 categories: Bethesda III (BIII; atypia of undetermined significance), Bethesda IV (BIV; follicular/oncocytic neoplasm), and Bethesda V (BV; suspicious for malignancy). In BV nodules, the risk of malignancy in children is sufficiently high (mean of 81%) (3) to warrant surgery. In BIII and BIV pediatric ITNs, the risk of malignancy ranges from 11% to 54% (mean 28%) and 28% to 100% (mean 50%), respectively, which is higher compared with adult patients who have equivalent cytopathologic findings (2-6). Due to the relatively high rates of malignancy in children with ITNs, the historical standard of care has been thyroid lobectomy (7). However, most children with ITNs have benign disease, and surgery not only exposes them to anesthesia and the inherent risks of thyroid surgery—which are higher than in adults—but also increases health care costs (8-10). Thus, a thoughtful but not overly aggressive approach to the evaluation and management of the Bethesda III and IV ITN in the pediatric population is needed.

Molecular testing via next-generation sequencing panels as an adjunct to cytopathology in the evaluation of ITN has become commonplace in adults because it improves diagnostic accuracy and better informs surgical management decisions, thus leading to fewer unwarranted surgeries (9, 11-15). The Afirma Genomic Sequencing Classifier (GSC) employs advanced machine learning models for nodule classification based on a whole transcriptome, exome-enriched RNA sequencing assay that sequences more than 21 000 genes in a CLIA-certified diagnostic laboratory. The assay allows for the creation of genomic classifiers (primarily to differentiate molecularly benign from suspicious ITN) and the detection of gene fusions, sequence variants, and other gene expression (16). Its primary objective is to classify ITNs as “benign” or “suspicious,” ruling out a cancer diagnosis in approximately two-thirds of ITN and reducing unnecessary surgeries (12, 15, 17). The Afirma GSC was previously validated in a cohort of patients aged ≥ 21 years and demonstrated a negative predictive value (NPV) for cancer of 96% for GSC-(B)enign results and a 47% positive predictive value (PPV) for GSC-(S)uspicious results from a cohort of patients with ITN with a baseline prevalence of malignancy of 24% (12). A meta-analysis of 13 independent studies showed an NPV of 99% and an observed PPV of 65% (18). Few studies have reported the use of Afirma GSC in pediatric and adolescent populations (6, 19, 20).

Given the paucity of high-level evidence regarding the role of molecular testing on FNA specimens in young patients with ITNs, contemporary guidelines from North America and Europe (7, 21) have not routinely recommended the use of molecular diagnostics to risk stratify pediatric ITN. Nevertheless, in recent years, the incorporation of molecular testing into the management of pediatric thyroid neoplasms has been increasing (5, 6, 22-25), although a prospective study in this population using Afirma GSC or another commercially available molecular test has not been performed to date. This study's aim was to evaluate the performance of the Afirma GSC in a retrospective consecutive case series study of a real-world cohort of patients younger than age 21 years with ITNs who had Afirma testing ordered as part of the clinician's practice.

Materials and methods

Study population

This was a retrospective case series that included consecutive patients younger than 21 years of age who had Afirma GSC testing for Bethesda III and IV ITNs between August 2017 (when the Afirma GSC became available) and June 2023 and who had follow-up data ascertained, defined as either surgical histopathology and/or clinical follow-up notes that were obtained under the institutional review board-approved research protocol. Testing was clinically motivated and ordered by more than 20 providers across various health care settings in the United States for the molecular evaluation of an ITN. Hence, this was a real-world cohort of patients. We studied patients younger than age 21 years because the Afirma GSC has not yet been validated in this population.

Study design

Thyroid nodule Bethesda classification was based upon what was reported on the Afirma test requisition form. Afirma GSC, including Xpression Atlas (XA) (26) data, was analyzed for all samples, although XA data are not reported clinically for Afirma GSC-B cases. The primary histopathology report or report of the final pathologic diagnosis in a clinic note (benign or malignant) was used as the gold standard for those patients who had thyroid surgery. For Afirma GSC-B cases managed without surgery, we required a minimum of a 2-year follow-up clinic note with physical examination description or a neck ultrasound report indicating no thyroid surgery, presumably because of no concerning index nodule changes. These data were used as a surrogate for histopathology.

Therefore, for this study, we used the following definitions:

True Positive (TP): GSC-S and malignant histology (including noninvasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like features; NIFTP)

True Positive (TP): GSC-S and malignant histology (including noninvasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like features; NIFTP)

True Negative (TN): GSC-B and benign histology or clinical follow-up of at least 2 years without subsequent surgery or a malignancy diagnosis

True Negative (TN): GSC-B and benign histology or clinical follow-up of at least 2 years without subsequent surgery or a malignancy diagnosis

False Positive (FP): GSC-S and benign histology

False Positive (FP): GSC-S and benign histology

False Negative (FN): GSC-B and malignant histology or a malignancy diagnosis on clinical followup

False Negative (FN): GSC-B and malignant histology or a malignancy diagnosis on clinical followup

Statistical analysis

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were determined using the definitions presented previously. Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to determine the association between continuous variables and categorical parameters. All statistical analyses were conducted with R version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Institutional review board approval

The data collection and research protocol were approved by the WCG IRB DHF 005-045P.

Results

Afirma GSC results

Of 169 357 ITNs undergoing Afirma GSC testing during the defined study period, 1386 (0.8%) were from patients with ages <21 years. Of these, we excluded 1337 (96%) because of no available histopathology or inadequate clinical follow-up (Fig. 1). Thus, the final study comprised 49 patients, 9 males and 40 females (age range 9-20 years, median 18.5 [interquartile range 17.3-19.8]) who had 30 GSC-B and 19 GSC-S cases from nodules ranging from 0.9 cm to 7.5 cm in size (Table 1, Fig. 2, Table S1 (27)). There was no statistical difference in nodule size between GSC-B and GSC-S nodules (P = .14). Histopathology reports or the clinic note indicating the final pathologic diagnosis (benign or malignant) were available for 28 patients. In the 19 GSC-S cases, 14 (73.7%) were malignant (TP), including 1 NIFTP case, and 5 (26.3%) were benign (FP). All 30 GSC-B cases were TN and either histologically (n = 9) or clinically benign (n = 21). There were no FN Afirma results. All 14 malignancies were GSC-S (sensitivity 100% [95% CI, 77-100]) with 10 (71%) and 4 (29%) classified as BIII and BIV nodules, respectively. Among the 35 clinically or histologically benign cases (BIII n = 25, BIV n = 10), 30 were GSC-B (specificity 86% [95% CI, 70-95])(Table 1, Fig. 3, Table S1 (27)). The NPV for a GSC-B result was 100% (95% CI, 88-100) and the PPV for a GSC-S result was 74% (95% CI, 55.4-86.3).

Expressed variants and fusions

Genomic alterations were not detected by XA in any of the 30 Afirma GSC-B samples. whereas there was a DICER1 alteration in 1 of the 5 FP nodules (Table 1, Table S1 (27)). Among the 14 TP samples, there were 7 papillary thyroid carcinomas (PTC; 1 each with a FGFR2::VCL and PPP1R21::ALK fusion; 1 oncocytic carcinoma [PAX8::PPARG fusion]; 1 NIFTP; 2 infiltrative follicular subtype of PTC; and 3 follicular thyroid carcinomas (n = 1 with PAX8::PPARG fusion and n = 2 with NRAS p.Q61 K).

Discussion

The results of this study suggest that Afirma GSC testing on an FNA specimen may be useful to risk stratify ITNs in older adolescents and to call benign disease with a high level of confidence. Given the higher prevalence of thyroid cancer in pediatric thyroid nodules relative to adult nodules, it is not surprising to see a higher PPV of 74% with Afirma suspicious pediatric nodules compared with 65% in adult patients (18). Of primary importance to clinicians, however, is that an Afirma benign result reliably rules out thyroid carcinoma in an ITN in a younger patient, which is demonstrated in this study by no FN results and thus a NPV of 100% (95% CI, 88.4-100).

Outside of the current study, additional real-world studies in younger patients have reported ITNs tested via Afirma GSC (6, 19, 20) and other commercially available platforms (24, 25). Heider et al (19) reported 3 BIII cases undergoing Afirma testing: 2 surgically treated GSC-S cases (1 benign; 1 malignant) and 1 GSC-B case that was nonsurgically managed. Rodriguez et al (20) conducted an institutional review of thyroid FNAs in patients ≤21 years; Afirma testing (that included both GSC and the older Gene Expression Classifier [GEC]) was done in 6 BIII (suspicious, n = 4 and benign, n = 2) and 3 BIV (suspicious, n = 2 and benign, n = 1) ITNs. In 4 cases with histologic follow-up, Afirma testing performed well with 3 TPs and 1 TN, although the type of Afirma test and the Bethesda classification for these cases was not reported. In a large single-institution series by Sanchez et al (6), Afirma testing was done in 34 nodules (GSC, n = 18; GEC, n = 10; XA, n = 3; and unknown, n = 3). Results were GSC/GEC suspicious in 16/31 cases (excluding the XA cases, all of which had the BRAF p.V600E pathogenic variant), GSC/GEC benign in 14/31 nodules, and nondiagnostic in one case. Correlative histology was available in 6 Afirma-benign (1 malignant; FN = 7%) and 14 Afirma-suspicious (9 malignant; TP = 64%) cases.

In Gallant et al (24), the molecular landscape of pediatric thyroid nodules was assessed using a multigene genomic classifier. The median patient age was 16.3 years, and the analysis was primarily performed on surgical formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues. Therefore, all patients had confirmed histopathology for analyzing the molecular correlation. However, as described, only 11 samples had Bethesda III-IV cytology, whereas most nodules were either Bethesda II (benign) (27%) or VI (malignant) (39%) lesions, which are rarely sent for preoperative molecular testing. Of all FFPE samples with molecular testing results, there were 23 matched, molecularly tested FNA samples with analysis on the cytology smears. Although there was perfect concordance of negative findings between negative FFPE and the matched FNA samples, the FNA had a discordant negative genomic classifier score relative to FFPE positivity in 1/19 cases, negative mutation analysis in 1/14, negative copy number alterations in 1/2, and negative gene expression alterations in 2/12 cases. It is unclear which Bethesda categories these discordant samples came from, and they cannot be tracked back to determine what the preoperative performance would have been in ITN samples.

More recently, Aekka et al (25) published the results of multiplatform and miRNA testing on pediatric thyroid nodules. In this study, the mean age was 13.9 years and 39 FNA samples were tested using cytology smears. Again, most cases were either from Bethesda II (33%) or Bethesda VI (33%) samples; only 8 cases were ITN. Of cases without strong driver alterations and negative miRNA profiles, 2/17 were malignant.

In the current study, of Afirma GSC-S samples with expressed molecular alterations identified via XA, 4/7 findings were gene fusions. The enrichment of oncogenic fusions in pediatric papillary thyroid cancers has been well described (28, 29). This study reports 2 relatively rare fusions in 2 GSC-S samples that were confirmed to be malignant: FGFR2::VCL and PPP1R21::ALK—both of which are oncogenic receptor-tyrosine-kinase fusions with U.S. Food and Drug Administration-approved tyrosine kinase inhibitors in other tumor types (cholangiocarcinoma [FGFR2] and non-small cell lung carcinoma [ALK]). The FGFR2::VCL fusion was recently reported in 4 PTCs from patients aged 34 to 74 years in a cohort of 5030 sequenced thyroid malignancies (30) and in a PTC from a 21-year-old with BIII cytology (31). The PPP1R21::ALK fusion has previously been reported in 6 additional thyroid tumor samples from adults without histologic correlate from the Afirma clinical database (32). The PPP1R21::ALK fusion has also been reported in a classic PTC in a 12-year-old female who had a Bethesda VI cytology result and in a second 57-year-old patient with BIII cytology but without histopathologic correlation (33). To our knowledge, this is the first report of detection of this fusion in a preoperative thyroid tumor specimen with BIII cytology and a histologic correlation.

Study strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study relative to other studies of molecular testing in young patients is that all samples were ITNs and were analyzed based on their management in routine clinical practice. All evaluated samples were cytologically BIII and BIV ITNs, and 9 Afirma GSC-B samples had histopathologic correlation, which appears to be the highest number of preoperative FNA samples studied of the 3 major testing platforms available in the United States. Additionally, these consecutively submitted samples were sent in for commercial testing at the discretion of more than 20 ordering providers, capturing a true real-world utilization of the test. However, given the retrospective nature of this study and the lack of clinical data (eg, physical examination, TSH levels, thyroid nodule ultrasound characteristics), the biases or important clinical considerations leading to molecular testing cannot be known. Additionally, there is no follow-up longer than 2 years or information about oncologic outcomes in these patients.

This study's main limitation is not having histopathologic confirmation on all Afirma GSC-B nodules. However, to attenuate the uncertainty of Afirma GSC-B nodules that were not removed, a minimum of 2-year follow-up with no documented surgery was used as a surrogate for a TN result. Another major weakness of our study is the inherent sampling bias that occurred due to only 3.5% of eligible ITN subjects having the requisite follow-up ascertained. Finally, the cohort tested is relatively old, with a median age of 18.5 years and the youngest age of 9 years; therefore, our observed performance of Afirma GSC may not generalize to younger pediatric patients.

In summary, this study suggests that the Afirma GSC can risk stratify ITNs in older teenagers with a similar degree of confidence as in adults, especially in the context of a GSC-B result. Notably, due to its unavoidable limitations, this study should not be considered as a formal validation of the use of the Afirma GSC in pediatric patients, in whom the decision for surgery should always incorporate other clinical data, such as sonographic findings (5), in addition to molecular findings. However, this study does represent the largest sample set of ITNs in patients aged < 21 years and the analysis was performed in the context of clinically motivated, real-world use of molecular testing in this population. Future prospective studies validating the performance of Afirma GSC in pediatric patients are warranted.
