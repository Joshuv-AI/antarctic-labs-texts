Changing Aetiology of Perforated Peptic Ulcers: Rising Impact of Non‐Steroidal‐Anti‐Inflammatories in Western Australia
Authors: Vidya Willis, Tamalee Wilson, David R. Fletcher, Amanda J. Foster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPeptic ulcer disease (PUD) continues to be a health issue, mainly due to Helicobacter pylori ( H. pylori ) infection and non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Despite better treatment of H. pylori and use of proton pump inhibitors, the rate of reduction in PUD complications is slowing down. This may be predominantly because of increased NSAID and aspirin use, especially among older adults and those in higher socioeconomic regions. Understanding changing causes of perforated PUD is important for prevention and care.MethodsThis retrospective audit reviewed patients who underwent surgical repair for peptic ulcer perforations at tertiary hospitals in Western Australia from 2010 to 2018. The study assessed patient demographics, ulcer sites, risk factors (including NSAID/aspirin use and H. pylori status), eradication, and follow‐up care to evaluate the relative impact of NSAID use and H. pylori infection on ulcer perforation.ResultsA cause for perforated PUD was identified in 78% of 359 patients; 72% had a history of NSAID or aspirin use and 42% tested positive for H. pylori . More gastric than duodenal perforations were observed. Practices around H. pylori testing, eradication, and follow‐up varied. Malignant gastric perforation was rare.ConclusionNSAID/Aspirin use may be surpassing H. pylori as the main cause of perforated PUD in Western Australia, mirroring global patterns. H. pylori remains important but identifying NSAID use as a modifiable risk and standardising testing, eradication, and follow‐up—including endoscopy—are needed. These findings should guide clinical practice for post‐operative and preventive care.

Introduction

Peptic ulcers are usually found in the stomach and duodenum though they can be present elsewhere. The main risk factors for peptic ulcer disease (PUD) are Helicobacter pylori ( H. pylori ) infection and non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) with other risk factors including malignancy, smoking, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, Crohn's disease, viral infections such as cytomegalovirus in immunocompromised patients amongst others [1, 2].

The global lifetime prevalence ranges from 5%–10% and incidence rates ranging from 0.1%–3% per year have been reported [2, 3]. There has been a decrease in age‐standardised prevalence and incidence rate of PUD from 1990 to 2019, a decrease in age‐standardised rate of disability adjusted life years and mortality rates [4].

In recent years however there has been a plateauing of this downward trend which is now being attributed to a change in aetiology of PUD from H. pylori to increasing NSAID use including aspirin especially in regions of high socioeconomic index [4, 5]. NSAIDs are one of the most commonly prescribed drugs globally accounting for 5%–10% of yearly prescribed drugs and increasing [4]. H. pylori infection rates have been lower in those above 70 years of age however there has also been an increase in age standardised incidence rate of PUD in older people which may be attributed to medications such as NSAID use especially aspirin in the setting of chronic diseases [4].

While H. pylori is an important risk factor that needs to be investigated and treated to prevent complications of PUD, it is essential to consider other risk factors, especially the increased use of NSAIDs when managing uncomplicated and complicated PUD.

The increasing role that NSAIDs and Aspirin are playing in causing peptic ulcer bleeding is becoming more apparent globally and in Western Australia (WA) [6, 7].

The aim of this study is to examine current trends in the aetiology of perforated PUD in WA, with a particular focus on the relative contributions of NSAID use and H. pylori infection. Through a retrospective audit of peri‐operative management in patients undergoing surgical repair of peptic ulcer at WA tertiary hospitals between January 2010 and December 2018, this study sought to update current understanding of causative factors and inform future clinical practice regarding post‐operative follow‐up and preventative strategies.

Methods

Ethical approval to conduct this study was obtained from the South Metropolitan Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee, Department of Health Western Australia on the 10th of December 2018, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki as revised in Brazil 2013 with approval for a waiver of consent given the retrospective nature and size of the study. ICD‐10‐AM and ACHI 6th to 10th Edition codes were used to identify specific primary and secondary diagnoses related to perforated peptic ulcers and related surgeries and interventions to select relevant patients.

Patients who underwent an emergency surgical repair of perforated gastric or duodenal ulcer from 1 January 2010 to 31 December 2017 at Fremantle Hospital Health Service (FHHS), Royal Perth Hospital (RPH), and Sir Charles Gairdner Hospital (SCGH) were included. All included patients were aged 18 years and above at the time of index operation. Hard copies and electronic files of all included patients were reviewed for data collection. Data on identification and treatment of causes and postoperative follow‐up were collected for each patient. Data pertaining to patient demographics, H. pylori testing, results, and eradication practices were collected, as well as other causes such as NSAID/Aspirin history and malignancy history. These details were captured from documented admission history taken, review of medication charts, and medication reconciliation charts. Descriptive analysis was used to assess the data.

Results

Three hundred and fifty‐nine patients were included in this study, of which 42% were female. The average age of included female patients was 64.1 years (range: 20–98) and male patients were younger at 52.8 years (range: 18–92). There were 175 (49%) patients with duodenal perforations, 182 (51%) patients with gastric perforations, and 2 (0.6%) patients who had gastric and duodenal perforations.

Causes of perforation were identified in 78% (279 of 359) of included patients with NSAID use as a main risk factor (Figure 1). Seventy‐two percent of included patients were tested for H. pylori as inpatients mostly through inpatient serology (Figure 2) and there was variability in the practice of providing H. pylori eradication therapy to inpatients prior to discharge (Figure 3).

Inpatient H. pylori Testing and Treatment

There was a 29.8% (107 of 359) H. pylori prevalence rate amongst included patients. Figure 3 shows the variability in targeted and empirical eradication therapy provided to included patients. The frequency and approach to intra‐operative biopsy varied among surgeons (Table 1), and the quality of some samples was inadequate, often lacking gastric mucosal cells. Of the patients who survived past 30 days after discharge, 55% attended a surgical clinic appointment, while some missed appointments or received follow‐up care elsewhere. The inpatient mortality rate was 8%, with a small number dying shortly after discharge. Only 29% of surviving patients underwent gastroscopy within 90 days, and a significant proportion were not tested for H. pylori during this procedure. There was a 9% failure rate for H. pylori eradication therapy among those who had received it as inpatients and underwent follow‐up gastroscopy.

Malignancy Causing Peptic Ulcer

Five patients (1.4% of included patients) were deemed to have a malignant cause of their gastric ulcer perforations. Two patients were diagnosed with diffuse large B cell lymphoma, and both had a history of Aspirin/NSAID use concurrently prior to presentation. One patient was diagnosed with adenocarcinoma of either gastric or pancreaticobiliary origin in the 2‐year post discharge period and passed away. Two patients presented with perforated peptic ulcer. One with a background of metastatic lung cancer and the other with non‐Hodgkin's lymphoma. Biopsies in these patients were not taken, and it was clinically presumed that their cause of perforation was their malignancy and/or their immunosuppressive treatments.

Discussion

Epidemiological changes in PUD over time are attributed to changes in socioeconomic development in developed countries, treatment of H. pylori , introduction of proton‐pump inhibitors [8] and H2 inhibitors, and more recently the increased uptake in NSAID use [4, 5]. There is a change in the ratio of infected men to women, previously 4–5:1 now becoming a 1:1 ratio with a similar change in the incidence of perforations between the genders. There has also been a change in the location of ulcers, which was previously predominantly found in the duodenum, but now there is an increase in the prevalence of gastric ulcers [8, 9].

In the United States, the annual rate of reduction of hospital discharges post peptic ulcer perforation was only 0.98% between 2005 to 2014, while the annual rate of reduction of hospital discharges following peptic ulcer bleeding was greater at 9.18% (95% CI: −15.77 to −2.07) between 2005 and 2007 and less at 2.08% (95% CI: −3.15 to −1.01) between 2007 and 2015 [10]. They attributed the slowing rate of reduction in PUD hospitalisation to the rise in non‐H. pylori‐related PUD such as infections, ischaemic causes, inflammatory as well as mechanical ulcerations [10]. They also noted a 57% increase in uptake in the regular use of aspirin between 2005 to 2010 as well as a 41% increase in non‐aspirin NSAID use [10]. Antibiotic resistance to H. pylori and migration from regions of high H. pylori prevalence were also felt to contribute to the slowing down in the annual rates of reduction in hospitalisations from complications of PUD [10].

The global epidemiology of peptic ulcer disease (PUD) is changing due to the eradication of Helicobacter pylori and increased prescription of NSAIDs, especially low‐dose aspirin for cardiovascular disease. In WA, this study found NSAID/Aspirin use may now be overtaking H. pylori as the main cause of peptic ulcer perforation, which could explain the greater number of gastric, rather than duodenal, perforations observed between 01/01/2010 and 31/12/2017. Quality and frequency of H. pylori testing and eradication were poor, and follow‐up practices inconsistent. Twenty‐eight percent of inpatients were not tested for H. pylori which may have clouded the true prevalence rate amongst this group of patients with perforation. Of the 72% of patients with a history of NSAID and/or Aspirin use, 66% (132 of 201) had documented evidence of NSAID cessation advice being given, in medical notes or discharge summary of index admission, clinic follow‐up or documented in the endoscopy report. However, this data is reliant on documentation by doctors and will therefore likely underestimate the true extent to which such advice was given to the patient at the time. While smoking and alcohol excess were also likely contributing factors to patients' presentations, preventative measures addressing these were not assessed in this study. This would also require the quality of general practitioner follow‐up and community‐based prevention strategies to be assessed which was beyond the scope of this study.

Malignant gastric perforation requiring surgery was rare in WA.

Management of perforated peptic ulcers centres on testing for and eradicating H. pylori or ceasing NSAIDs. However, clinical practice varied widely, particularly in H. pylori testing. Serology, the most common and low‐cost method (~$14), has 80%–95% sensitivity and specificity but cannot distinguish acute from past infection [11]. Despite this, it is often used as a proxy for current infection [11]. Breath tests offer higher sensitivity (95%) and specificity (98%) but are more expensive (~$75) [12]. Some surgeons used intra‐operative urease tests (~$1.50) [12], while others relied on histopathology (~$70) [12], although many samples were poor quality and inconclusive. This variability leads to inaccurate assessment of H. pylori prevalence and impedes treatment efficacy and prevention strategies.

Malignant gastric ulcer perforation was infrequent, reflecting Australia's low gastric cancer incidence (4.5 per 100 000) [13] and mortality (2.1 per 100 000) [13]. The national H. pylori prevalence is 24.6% [13], which may help explain these trends, although increased NSAID/Aspirin use [5] is likely contributing to more surgically managed gastric rather than duodenal perforations in WA. Globally, malignancy is found in 6%–14% [14] of perforated gastric ulcers, but intra‐operative biopsies add cost and time with low diagnostic yield if samples are suboptimal, which was often the case in WA. The study recommends selective biopsies only for suspicious lesions and suggests intra‐operative Clo tests as a low‐cost, effective alternative. Routine follow‐up gastroscopies with biopsy are recommended for presumed benign cases to rule out missed malignancies and to confirm ulcer healing.

Eradication therapy after surgical repair accelerates healing [15] and reduces recurrence rates [2]. Ulcer healing has been as high as 85.3% at 8 weeks on follow‐up endoscopy amongst patients receiving eradication therapy compared to 48.4% if treated with omeprazole only post operatively [16]. Another study reported no significant difference in ulcer healing at the 8 week endoscopy; however, it found a significantly lower recurrence rate on endoscopy after 1 year amongst those receiving eradication therapy of 4.8% compared to those receiving omeprazole alone (38.1%) [17]. However, empirical use of H. pylori eradication therapy raises concerns due to evolving resistance patterns [18] and changing disease aetiology. All patients with perforated peptic ulcer should be tested and treated for H. pylori as inpatients. Patients with NSAID/Aspirin‐related perforations should cease these drugs, if possible, with PPIs continued if cessation is not feasible [15, 19]. For those needing ongoing NSAID therapy, a selective COX‐2 inhibitor plus PPI is recommended [5, 15].

Post‐surgical care lacks robust evidence and consensus guidelines [15]. Only 28.5% of patients underwent follow‐up gastroscopy within 90 days of discharge, and 30% of these were not tested for H. pylori . Missed follow‐ups were due to non‐attendance, process errors, or scheduling delays, and some gastroscopies may have occurred outside the study timeframe or at other hospitals. Literature recommends follow‐up endoscopy 4–8 weeks post‐operatively, especially for gastric ulcer perforations, to rule out malignancy, test for H. pylori , and confirm healing [14, 20, 21, 22]. Long‐term recurrence rate averages 12.1% (95% CI: 6.3–17.8), rising to 24.6% over 5 years [9]. Risk factors for recurrence include NSAID/Aspirin use, H. pylori infection, older age, serious comorbidity, and immunosuppression [9]. The low risk of malignancy in duodenal ulcer perforations leads some surgeons to forego follow‐up endoscopy in these cases [8, 23]; however, with high recurrence rates in the context of changing risk profile and increased NSAID/Aspirin use, universal follow‐up is advisable for patients with any perforated peptic ulcer.

Surgical outpatient follow‐up was also suboptimal, with only 55% of surviving patients reviewed in clinic post‐discharge. There was wide variability in follow‐up organisation, with some patients directed to arrange care with GPs, increasing the risk of missed follow‐up. Incomplete follow‐up represents missed opportunities for recurrence prevention and maintaining care standards.

This retrospective audit was limited by the quality and completeness of clinical documentation. NSAID/Aspirin use was mainly recorded by admitting teams or pharmacist reconciliation, but details on type and duration were often unclear. The impact of lifestyle factors such as smoking or alcohol was not assessed. Additionally, patients treated surgically at peripheral hospitals were excluded, limiting generalisability.

Conclusion

NSAID/Aspirin use may now have overtaken H. pylori infection as the leading cause of PUD and perforation in WA, highlighting the need for targeted prevention strategies, patient education, and careful prescribing. All patients with perforated peptic ulcer—particularly those also taking NSAIDs or Aspirin—should be tested for H. pylori and treated if positive. These changes make it essential to confirm both eradication and ulcer healing, best achieved through universal follow‐up endoscopy 6–8 weeks postoperatively with better quality biopsies to exclude malignancy. Intra‐operative biopsies should be better reserved for ulcers that appear suspicious for malignancy.
