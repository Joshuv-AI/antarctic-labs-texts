The chloroplast genome of Taxus sumatrana, an endangered medicinal tree from Indonesia
Authors: Fitri Indriani, Muhammad Imam Surya, Vandra Kurniawan, Wanda Kuswanda, Haidir Haidir, Alya Sabila Fahrisa

Introduction

Taxus sumatrana (Miq.) de Laub. is a member of the family Taxaceae, widely recognized for its ecological and medicinal importance. This gymnosperm species is native to tropical and subtropical regions of Southeast Asia, including Indonesia, where it predominantly inhabits montane forests [1]. T. sumatrana in Indonesia is naturally distributed in the high-elevation montane forests of Sumatra and Sulawesi [2]. On Sumatra Island, the species is believed to occur along the largely unexplored Bukit Barisan Mountain range. Recent studies have documented the presence of T. sumatrana in several locations, including various mountains within Kerinci Seblat National Park, Mount Dempo Protected Forest (South Sumatra), Sibuatan Protected Forest (North Sumatra), and Mount Singgalang [3]. One of the most remarkable aspects of T. sumatrana is its medicinal significance. The tree contains taxol, compounds extracted from its bark and leaves, that are vital in producing chemotherapy drugs such as paclitaxel. These drugs are used to treat various cancers, including breast, ovarian, and lung cancer [4, 5]. Unfortunately, the high demand for taxol has led to overharvesting, putting wild populations under immense pressure. This evergreen tree is characterized by its needle-like leaves and red, berry-like structures called arils, which encase its seeds and serve as an essential food source for wildlife. Typically thriving in montane forests at elevations between 1,200 and 3,000 m, T. sumatrana belongs to a slow growth plant with low seed germination rates that prefers cool, humid climates and acidic, well-drained soils [1, 6]. However, T. sumatrana faces significant threats due to habitat loss, unsustainable harvesting, and its naturally slow growth and reproduction rates, exacerbating its endangered status [7].

Furthermore, conservation efforts are crucial to ensure its survival, yet these efforts require a thorough understanding of its genetic makeup. Chloroplasts, the organelles responsible for photosynthesis, play a central role in plant metabolism and energy production. They possess their genomes, which are typically circular and encode genes essential for photosynthetic processes and other metabolic pathways. Chloroplast (cp.) genomes have been widely utilized in plant systematics, phylogenetics, and population genetics due to their highly conserved structure, uniparental inheritance, and relatively low mutation rates [8, 9]. Despite its ecological and medicinal importance, the complete cp. genome of T. sumatrana remains largely unexplored.

Recent advances in sequencing technology have enabled the rapid assembly and annotation of complete cp. genomes, offering insights into plant genetic diversity and evolutionary dynamics. Comparative analysis of cp. genomes has been instrumental in understanding the adaptive mechanisms of plants to diverse ecological niches and environmental stressors [10, 11]. Additionally, the availability of complete cp. genome sequences supports the development of molecular markers for conservation biology and species identification. Moreover, this study aims to report the complete cp. genome sequence of T. sumatrana, providing a detailed analysis of its genomic structure, gene content, and phylogenetic implications. By decoding this genome, we contribute to the growing repository of genomic resources that facilitate evolutionary studies and conservation strategies, particularly for ecologically and economically significant species.

Main text

The complete cp. genome sequence of T. sumatrana

Fresh leaves of T. sumatrana were collected from Pesisir Bukit, Kerinci Regency, Jambi, Indonesia (-1 42’23.09”S, 101 23’2.2”E) at an elevation of 1,988 m above sea level. Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified CTAB (cetyltrimethylammonium bromide) protocol [12] and evaluated using agarose gel electrophoresis and the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). The extracted genomic DNA was used for library preparation, followed by sequencing on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform with a 300-cycle (150 bp paired-end) run. This generated a total of 48,613,755 paired-end reads with an average read length of 151 bp, amounting to approximately 14.5 Gbp of raw data. Quality control was performed using FASTQC version 0.11.8 [13]. Low-quality reads and adapter sequences were removed using Trimmomatic version 0.39 with the parameters: TruSeq3-PE.fa:2:30:10 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:28 LEADING:5 TRAILING:5 MINLEN:25. After filtering, a total of 9.5 Gbp of high-quality reads was retained. The clean reads were assembled using GetOrganelle version 1.7.7.1 [14] to reconstruct the cp. genome. Genome annotation was performed using CPGAVAS2 (http://47.96.249.172:16019/analyzer/annotate) [15] with reference to the Taxus mairei cp. genome (accession number KJ123824.1) and GeSeq (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/index.html) [16]. The annotation results were then manually verified using Unipro UGENE v45.1 [17] and NCBI Genomic Workbench v3.8.2 [18]. A circular map of the T. sumatrana cp. genome was generated using Organellar GenomeDRAW (OGDRAW) via the MPI-MP Chlorobox platform (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html) [19].

The complete cp. genome of T. sumatrana was assembled with a total length of 129,227 base pairs (bp) (Fig. 1), and exhibited a GC content of 35%. Similar to most Taxus genus, T. sumatrana lacks the typical inverted repeat (IR) regions that are commonly found in cp. genomes. The IR regions are known to play critical roles in genome stabilization, including replication initiation, structural maintenance, and gene conservation [20]. Their loss is linked to increased potential for genomic recombination [21]. Despite the IR loss, Taxus cp. genomes are relatively conserved, especially compared to other conifer families like Pinaceae, which show more rearrangement. The cp. genome of T. sumatrana encodes a total of 119 genes, comprising 81 protein-coding genes, 34 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, and four ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. Among these, 113 genes are present as single copies, while three genes (trnI-CAU, trnG-UCC and trnQ-UUG) are duplicated and present in two copies (Additional file 1: Table S1). Gene structure analysis revealed variation in intron and exon organization among the genes. A total of 19 genes were identified to contain introns, indicating post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms such as alternative splicing or intron-mediated enhancement of gene expression. Specifically, 13 genes were found to harbor a single intron, including petD, petB, rpI16, rpI2, ndhB, ndhA, trnK-UUU, atpF, rpoC1, trnL-UAA, trnV-UAC, accD and clpP. The rps12 and ycf3 genes are unique in possessing three exons. The rps12 gene contains only one intron, which is divided into two distantly located segments. The first segment includes a single exon, while the second contains two exons and one intron. During transcription, these segments are brought together through trans-splicing [22]. In contrast, ycf3 is a continuous sequence with three exons and two introns, undergoing cis-splicing during transcription (Additional files 1: Table S2). These findings underscore the functional complexity of the T. sumatrana cp. genome and provide a basis for further investigations into gene regulation, expression, and evolutionary adaptation in gymnosperm lineages.

Simple sequence repeats (SSR)

Microsatellite or SSR (Simple Sequence Repeat) identification was conducted using MISA v2 [23]. The parameters for SSR detection, based on unit size and minimum repeat number, were set as follows: 1–8, 2–5, 3–5, 4 − 3, 5 − 3, and 6 − 3. The maximum interruption allowed between two SSRs was set at 100 bases. Using these criteria, a total of 115 SSRs were identified in the cp. genome of T. sumatrana. Among them, mononucleotide repeats were the most abundant, accounting for 89 SSRs (77.4%) of the total SSRs detected (Additional files 2). Further analysis revealed a significant AT-rich bias among the mononucleotide repeats, with 96.63% composed of adenine (A) and thymine (T) nucleotides (Additional files 2). This high proportion of A/T-rich repeats is a common characteristic in plant cp. genomes and is thought to contribute to the overall AT-rich composition of the chloroplast DNA. These SSR markers could also serve as valuable tools in future studies focused on population genetics, phylogenetic analysis, and species identification within the Taxus genus.

Codon usage

The relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) was analyzed using Mega 11 [24] and visualization of codon frequencies utilized the “ggpubr” package in R version 4.2.3. T. sumatrana consist of 64 protein-coding genes region with 43,075 codons. The codon usage analysis revealed that the most frequently used codon was UUU, which encodes for phenylalanine (Phe), appearing 1,947 times. Conversely, the least frequently used codon was GCG, which encodes for alanine (Ala), and was found only 135 times. Among all amino acids, leucine (Leu) was the most abundant, reflecting its high occurrence in the chloroplast-encoded proteins, whereas tryptophan (Trp) was the least frequent. The RSCU values ranged from 0.34 (CGC) to 2.13 (AGA), suggesting a notable variation in codon preference across the genome (Fig. 2). Our findings revealed that most codons tended to end in A or U and had RSCU values ≥ 1, indicating that the observed codon usage bias may be linked to adaptive evolution in the cp. genome. This codon usage bias may reflect adaptive evolution of the cp. genome, potentially shaped by factors such as mutational pressure, natural selection, and translational efficiency [25].

Phylogenetic relationship among T. sumatrana and related species

A total of 24 cp. genome were used to determine the phylogenetic relationship of T. sumatrana with related species, using Sequoia sempervirens as the outgroup (Fig. 3). The analysis included several genera: Taxus, Cephalotaxus, Cupresus and Pseudotaxus. The cp. genomes were aligned using Mauve [26]. Of the 198,377 aligned nucleotide positions, 19,005 were identified as parsimony-informative, representing approximately 9.6% of the alignment. The best-fit nucleotide substitution model was determined using ModelTest-NG [27], with the aligned sequences as input. Based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), the general time reversible (GTR) with invariable site (I), and discrete Gamma (G) was selected. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using maximum likelihood (ML) in IQ-TREE v2.4.0 [28] with 100,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates, resulting in a consensus tree with a log-likelihood of − 484,023. Bayesian inference (BI) analysis was performed in MrBayes v3.2.7 [29] with 100,000 Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) generations 2 runs, 4 chains of each run, and sampling was conducted for each 1,000 generations. The resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized using TreeGraph2 [30].

Phylogenetic relationships of T. sumatrana and related species based on cp. genome data. Numbers above branches indicate Bayesian posterior probability values, while numbers below represented bootstrap support values based on the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method

T. sumatrana is currently considered a synonym of T. wallichiana according to the Plants of the World Online database (https://powo.science.kew.org/) [1]. However [31], recognized T. sumatrana as a distinct species from T. wallichiana based on morphological differences. In this study, phylogenetic analysis revealed that T. sumatrana is most closely related to T. mairei, forming a well-supported clade and highlighting their close evolutionary relationship within the Taxus genus. This finding is also consistent with the results of [32] and [33], who distinguished T. sumatrana from T. wallichiana based on molecular evidence. Molecular analyses based on plastid and nuclear markers indicate that T. sumatrana and T. wallichiana likely originated through allopatric speciation from a common ancestor [34, 35] —potentially with T. wallichiana expanding its range into Indonesia and giving rise to T. sumatrana. Alternatively, their current distribution and divergence could be the result of long-distance dispersal (e.g., via zoochory), followed by subsequent speciation.

In contrast, species from the genera Cephalotaxus and Pseudotaxus formed distinct clades, clearly separating them from the Taxus lineage, while Cupressus species grouped together, supporting the current taxonomic boundaries within the order. The overall topology of the tree supports the monophyly of Taxus and provides a molecular framework for future evolutionary and biogeographic studies of the group.

Limitations

In this study, only a single specimen of Taxus sumatrana was analyzed, which presents a limitation given the species’ potentially wide distribution and morphological variability across Sumatra. However, field observations revealed the presence of multiple individuals across various locations in Sumatra. To strengthen the robustness and reliability of the findings, it would be advantageous to perform WGS on a broader population of T. sumatrana from different regions of the island. Integrating these genomic data with morphological assessments and comparative analyses using existing Taxus genomic databases from other geographic regions would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the genetic diversity, population structure, and evolutionary history of T. sumatrana.
