Engineering polymeric RNA scaffolds as programmable combinatorial innate immune agonists
Authors: Yinying Yang, Andrew K Alvarez, Wenxuan Wang, Elias C D Issa, Emily Dorsey, Connie Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSentinel cells in the innate immune system decode combinations of pathogen-associated molecular patterns to shape immune responses, presenting an exploitable feature for vaccine adjuvants and cancer immunotherapies. Delivering innate immune agonists can direct responses toward patient-specific tumour antigens and enhance checkpoint blockade therapies. However, few adjuvants have been clinically approved, largely due to limited potencies or high toxicities. Key barriers remain in adjuvant design and translation: (i) reliance on single agonists, which poorly recapitulate complex pathogen sensing; (ii) poor understanding and control of synergistic effects among combined innate immune agonists; and (iii) a lack of scalable platforms for assembling diverse agonists. To address these issues, we developed polymeric RNAs (polyRNAs) as modular scaffolds that mimic pathogen structures and present multiple agonists. Synthesized by rolling circle transcription, polyRNAs achieve strong TLR3, TLR7, and RIG-I activation and can enable multivalent patterning of CpG-DNA for further engagement of TLR9 signalling. These polyRNA scaffolds are efficiently delivered with lipid nanoparticles and eliminate tumours in a syngeneic mouse model of colorectal cancer with comparable efficacies to clinical benchmarks. Our results establish polyRNAs as a programmable platform for combinatorial innate immune activation, with strong translational potential in cancer immunotherapy.

Introduction

Despite the remarkable success of cancer immunotherapies such as immune checkpoint blockade over the last decade, clinical benefit remains limited to a small fraction of patients [1–3]. Vaccines offer promising strategies for augmenting cancer immunotherapies and training the immune system to recognize and attack tumour cells. The development of effective vaccines is also essential for infectious diseases, which remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality among infants, children, and elderly individuals [4]. A key challenge in vaccine design is the generation of a robust and durable disease-specific immune response. Such effective immune responses require viral, bacterial, or tumour antigens to be internalized by antigen-presenting cells for priming cellular and humoral immune responses. Activation of the innate immune system plays a crucial role in shaping these adaptive immune responses [5]. The innate immune system has evolved to sense and respond to complex combinations of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as toll-like receptors (TLRs) and retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I). PRR activation triggers downstream signalling cascades leading to the nuclear translocation of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and interferon regulatory factors (IRFs), culminating in the production of proinflammatory cytokines and type I interferons that mediate the adaptive immune response.

The delivery of innate immune agonists has provided a powerful strategy to guide immune responses towards disease-specific antigens and to enhance the overall efficacies of immune checkpoint blockade therapies [6–9]. Towards this end, many synthetic innate immune agonists that activate specific PRRs have been developed as vaccine adjuvants for cancer and infectious diseases [8, 10]. However, only a handful of adjuvants to date have been clinically approved for use in vaccines or with cancer immunotherapies [11, 12]. The clinical translation of vaccine adjuvants remains limited by insufficient potencies and excessive toxicities, due to key knowledge and technological gaps: (i) while mounting evidence shows that combining PRR agonists [13, 14] can promote synergistic immune activation, the understanding and control of such combinatorial effects remain inadequate; (ii) simple mixtures of soluble immunostimulatory agents often produce mixed, poorly understood immune responses [15, 16]; (iii) there is a lack of scalable, modular platforms for assembling diverse classes of innate immune agonists to improve vaccine and immunotherapy efficacies.

Currently, many adjuvants are administered as a single agonist and can only activate a single or a few innate immune pathways, poorly mimicking naturally occurring immune responses to pathogens [8, 17]. Additionally, the specific immune responses of other traditional adjuvants, such as alum and emulsions, remain only partially understood [18]. These limitations highlight the importance of using more complex combinations of PAMPs with defined mechanisms of action to achieve robust and tailored immune responses [17]. The interactions among multiple agonists have been investigated through chemical linkers. For example, tri-agonists comprising three chemically linked small-molecule TLR agonists revealed that TLR9 agonists induced stronger TH1-biased immune signals when conjugated to TLR1/2, TLR4, and TLR7 agonists, while TLR2/6 agonists antagonized TLR9 activity and instead promoted a TH2-skewed immune response [14]. However, systematic studies examining the cumulative effects of diverse combinations of PRRs, along with the influence of their spatial organization, remain scarce. A major challenge is the efficient and functional co-delivery of multiple agonists to the same target cell, underscoring the need for a versatile molecular scaffold for the modular assembly of multiple innate immune agonists.

To enable the rational design of next-generation adjuvants, extensive efforts have focused on integrating multiple innate immune signalling pathways and characterizing their divergent downstream responses. Such studies have helped elucidate synergistic and antagonistic combinatorial cellular signalling circuits to guide the rational selection of PRR ligand combinations in multi-agonist design [10, 19, 20]. However, most mechanistic studies of the interactions between innate immune signalling pathways have relied on soluble ligands, which poorly resemble the spatial assembly and multivalency of PAMPs found on natural pathogens [21, 22]. Emerging evidence shows that multivalent PAMP presentation via nanoparticle conjugation or DNA origami presentation can enhance immune activation over free soluble agonists [15, 23–25]. Multivalent display of agonists enhances receptor clustering, mimicking natural pathogen structures to boost signalling and immune activation [26]. Importantly, controlling the spatial arrangement and density of such multivalent PAMP presentations can modulate the overall immune response profile. In one such example, the precise spatial assembly of CpG-DNA oligonucleotides (TLR9 agonists) on DNA origami scaffolds was shown to induce the strongest TH1 immune polarization at 3.5 nm spacing for optimal cell-based immunity against cancer, while the TH1/TH2 ratio was decreased with increasing distance between adjacent CpG-DNA motifs [15]. Multivalent PAMP presentation can further potentially reverse the antagonistic effects between certain pairs of PRRs to induce pro-inflammatory responses. For instance, costimulation of Dectin-2 with TLR4 using mannosylated lipid A was shown to enhance anti-inflammatory IL-10 production [27], while nano-silica capsule-presented multivalent mannan moieties that activate the same set of PRRs triggered a robust pro-inflammatory response [28]. Therefore, characterizing the immune response profiles of spatially defined arrays of PAMPs is essential for controlling and harnessing innate immune activation for vaccine and immunotherapy applications.

Here, we leverage the structural and sequence versatilities of RNA to develop a modular polymeric RNA (polyRNA)-based scaffold for combinatorial innate immune activation. Mammalian and other organisms employ a diverse repertoire of PRRs to recognize and respond to exogenous RNA in various infectious diseases. Among these PRRs, TLR7/8 is activated by single-stranded RNA (ssRNA); RIG-I is activated by short double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) with 5′-diphosphate/5′-triphosphate groups; melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 is activated by long dsRNAs (typically > 500 bp); and TLR3 is activated by dsRNAs in a sequence-independent manner [29]. These recognition capabilities create a versatile toolbox for the rational design of immunostimulatory RNAs to activate multiple PRRs. By encoding and enzymatically synthesizing repeat RNA sequences through rolling circle transcription (RCT), we engineer polyRNA structures comprising repeat motifs that serve as both innate immune agonists and molecular building blocks for patterning additional agonists in multivalent fashion. We have previously shown that polymerized short hairpin RNAs synthesized by RCT can promote NF-κB activation and cytotoxicity in cancer cells, with encoded small interfering RNA (siRNA) sequences enabling gene silencing [30, 31]. However, the innate immune activation mechanisms and adjuvant potential of such polyRNA macromolecules remain largely unexplored. We further expand the utility of polyRNA as a molecular scaffold for patterning additional innate immune agonists. We hypothesized that such an “all-in-one” system can not only enable potent combinatorial innate immune activation via the spatially controlled juxtaposition of over three agonist moieties in multivalent fashion, but also permit mechanistic explorations of how combinations and spatial densities of agonists mediate different immune responses.

To understand the immunostimulatory profile of polyRNA-based biomaterials, we first explore the linearly connected RNA repeats as PRR agonists on a polyRNA backbone. Through a panel of polyRNA structures with varying repeat motifs, we demonstrate that polyRNAs can activate multiple PRRs and induce comparable or greater overall innate immune activation versus poly(I:C), a synthetic long dsRNA TLR3 agonist. We then expand several polyRNA candidates as scaffolds for the multivalent assembly of CpG-DNA TLR9 agonists and characterize their immunostimulatory profiles in bone-marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). These polyRNA scaffolds can be successfully delivered via optimized lipid nanoparticle (LNP) formulations in a length-dependent manner. Finally, as a proof-of-concept, we show that polyRNA scaffolds can serve as an effective in situ cancer vaccine, eradicating established tumours in a syngeneic mouse model of colorectal cancer, with comparable efficacies to clinical benchmarks. Collectively, our results establish polyRNA-based biomaterials as a versatile platform for programmable combinatorial immune activation, with strong translational potential for cancer immunotherapies and modular utility for rational vaccine adjuvant design.

Materials and methods

General materials

DNA oligos were ordered from Integrated DNA Technologies, purified by RNase-free high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) or polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE).

T7 RNA polymerase was purchased from Biosearch Technologies. Nucleotide triphosphates (NTPs), T4 DNA ligase, and molecular biology buffers were purchased from New England Biolabs. Lipid components for LNP synthesis were ordered from MedChemExpress (MCE), Millipore Sigma, or Avanti Polar Lipids. 0.1 M, pH 4 Citrate buffer was purchased from Boston BioProducts. 3p-hpRNA - RIG-I Agonist (3p-hpRNA), R848 (Resiquimod), and Poly (I:C) High Molecular Weight [poly(I:C)] were purchased from InvivoGen. ODN 1826 was purchased from MCE.

Synthesis and characterization of polyRNA

The optimized conditions for the RCT reaction were determined through the systematic adjustment of concentrations of NTPs, circular DNA template, and T7 RNA polymerase [31]. Reactions were carried out in 25 μl systems for 24 h at 37°C, treated with DNase I, and quantified using QuantiFluor RNA System. All DNA template sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

The molecular weight distribution of the RCT products was analysed using agarose gel electrophoresis. The effects of reactant concentrations were analysed using stepwise least-squares linear regression. Optimized conditions were selected and used for the subsequent polyRNA synthesis as follows: for a typical 300 μl RCT reaction, 180 μl of the DNA ligation reaction was combined with 30 μl of 10 × RNAPol reaction buffer, 19.5 μl of 100 mM mixed NTPs, 37.5 μl of T7 RNA polymerase (50 000 U/ml) and 33 μl of RNase-free water. The reaction mixture was incubated for 24 h at 37°C. The product mixture was treated with DNase I to digest the DNA template at 37°C for 15 min. Then, 0.5 M ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution was added to a final concentration of 0.045 M to dissolve the magnesium pyrophosphate/RNA microsponge particles, releasing all polyRNA into the solution. After 15 min, the mixture was heated at 75°C for 10 min to inactivate DNase I before cooling on ice and equilibrating to room temperature. The polyRNA product was purified using RNA Clean & Concentrator-25 (Zymo Research). PolyRNA concentration and quality were determined by UV absorption (NanoDrop One) using the Oligo RNA mode. The size distribution of polyRNA was analysed by gel electrophoresis [1.5% agarose, 1 × TBE (45 mM boric acid, 2.5 mM EDTA, 0.13 M Tris, pH 7.6)]. The average lengths of polyRNAs were determined by ImageJ, estimated based on denatured samples. Denatured samples were heated at 70°C for 4 min and cooled on ice before loading.

RNA structure simulation

Explicit co-transcriptional folding simulations of full-length polyRNAs (∼1–9 kb) are computationally intractable with current kinetic RNA folding algorithms. Therefore, representative single repeat units and oligomeric RNAs were modeled using KineFold [32], which simulates nucleotide-by-nucleotide RNA folding during transcription and captures local, kinetically favored structures expected to dominate long repetitive RNAs. For oligomeric RNAs, structure predictions were performed with an upper length limit of 400 nt per simulation.

Regarding transcription initiation sites: (i) for templates containing a T7 promoter, transcription was initiated at the first cytosine downstream of the promoter; (ii) for dumbbell-shaped templates, the junction between the double-stranded region and the longer single-stranded region was used as the transcription starting site, according to prior reports [33].

Hybridization of polyRNA and complementary DNA combs

PolyRNA and DNA comb were mixed with 10 × hybridization buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, and 10 mM Tris, pH 7.5) and RNase-free water to a final nucleic acid concentration of 148.2 ng/μl. The molecular ratio of the polyRNA and DNA combs was calculated based on the mole number of repeat units in the polyRNA and the mole number of DNA combs. The mixture was heated at 75°C for 5 min to denature the nucleic acids and snap-cooled on ice. MgCl2 solution was added to a final concentration of 40 μM and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Hybrids were stored at −80°C until use.

All DNA comb sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Formulation of polyRNA-loaded LNPs (polyRNA-LNPs) and hybrid-loaded LNPs (hybrid-LNPs)

LNP formulations based on DLin-MC3-DMA (MC3) [34], OF-C4-Deg-Lin (C4) [35], L319 [36], C12-200 [37], FTT5 [38], and TT3 [39] were prepared as previously described with minor modifications. DLin-MC3-DMA (MC3), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC), cholesterol, and DMG-PEG2000 were solubilized in ethanol at a molar ratio of 50/10/38.5/1.5. OF-C4-Deg-Lin (C4), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), cholesterol, and DMG-PEG2000 were solubilized in ethanol at a molar ratio of 35/16/46.5/2.5. L319, DSPC, cholesterol, and DMG-PEG2000 were mixed at a molar ratio of 55/10/32.5/2.5. C12-200, DSPC, cholesterol, and PEG-DMG were dissolved in ethanol at a molar ratio of 50/10/38.5/1.5. FTT5, DOPE, cholesterol, and DMG-PEG2000 were mixed at a molar ratio of 20/30/40/0.75. TT3, DOPE, cholesterol, and DMG-PEG2000 were mixed at a molar ratio of 20/30/40/0.75.

The ethanol phase was mixed with citrate buffer (0.01 M, pH 4 for MC3, L319, C12-200, FTT5, and TT3-based formulations; 0.01 M, pH 3 for C4-based formulation) under vigorous pipetting to a final volume ratio of 1:3 ethanol to citrate buffer. For polyRNA-containing formulations, polyRNAs were dissolved in citrate buffer before addition to the organic phase at a concentration that resulted in a final nitrogen to phosphate (N/P) ratio of 10.92. For hybrid-containing formulations, hybrid solutions (pH 7.5) were mixed with an equal volume of citrate buffer to adjust the pH of the mixture to 4 or 3 and diluted with RNase-free water before addition to the organic phase at a concentration that resulted in a final N/P ratio of 10.92 for in vitro studies. For in vivo studies, an N/P ratio of 10.92 or 7.64 was used. The LNPs were dialyzed against 1 × phosphate buffered saline (PBS; 155 mM NaCl, 3 mM Na2HPO4, 1 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4) using a Pur-A-Lyzer Midi 3500 Dialysis Kit or Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Unit with 100 kDa molecular weight cutoff regenerated cellulose membrane (Millipore) for larger-scale preparation (two washes). The purified LNP solution was resuspended using 1 × PBS to a predetermined final concentration based on polyRNA concentration (5 ng/μl for in vitro studies or 200 ng/μl for in vivo studies). The particle size was determined using Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS following manufacturer-recommended settings [34]. The measurements for each sample were repeated three times independently. The encapsulation efficiency was determined using the QuantiFluor RNA System. Briefly, standard dsRNA dilution series, LNP samples, and LNP samples disrupted in 2% Triton X-100 in 1 × Tris-EDTA (TE) buffer were added to the working solution of QuantiFluor RNA dye and the fluorescence was measured using a plate reader. The quantities of free nucleic acid (mF) and total nucleic acid (mT) in each LNP formulation were calculated according to the dsRNA standard curve, and the encapsulation efficiency of each formulation was calculated by: (mT − mF/mT) × 100 (%).

Cell culture

CT26.WT cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) (Manassas, Virginia). RAW-Dual, HEK-Blue mTLR9, HEK-Blue hTLR3, HEK-Blue hTLR7, and HEK-Lucia RIG-I were purchased from InvivoGen. All cell lines were cultured according to the manufacturer’s specifications.

PolyRNA or hybrid complexation with transfection reagents

Complexation with Lipofectamine 3000: polyRNAs or hybrids were dissolved in Opti-MEM at a concentration of 10 or 20 ng/μl (calculated based on the polyRNA concentration in the mixture) and combined with 1.5 μl Lipofectamine 3000 reagent dissolved in an equal volume of Opti-MEM. After mixing, the sample was allowed to sit for 10 min at room temperature before use, giving a final polyRNA concentration of 5 or 10 ng/μl.

Complexation with Mirus TransIT-X2: 3 μl TransIT-X2 reagent was added to 97 μl solution containing polyRNAs or hybrids dissolved in Opti-MEM, giving a final concentration of 5 or 10 ng/μl (calculated based on the polyRNA concentration in the mixture). The solution was mixed thoroughly and allowed to sit at room temperature for 15 min before use.

Reporter gene and cell viability assays

RAW-Dual NF-κB and IRF assays: RAW-Dual cells were plated in 96-well plates (2 × 104 cells/well) in 80 or 90 µl of growth medium. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with the same volume of 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (HIFBS) in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM).def LNP samples or nucleic acid samples complexed with transfection reagents were added to each well (10 or 20 μl) to a final volume of 100 μl, giving a final dosage of polyRNAs of 0.5 μg/ml or 1.0 μg/ml. The treated cells were incubated for 24 h at 37°C.

For the NF-κB assay, 20 μl of supernatant was removed and combined with 180 µl of QUANTI-Blue working solution in a clear 96-well plate. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 1.5 h. The absorbance at 655 nm was measured by a BioTek plate reader, and the data were analysed by GraphPad Prism.

For the IRF assay, 20 μl of supernatant was removed and combined with 50 μl of QUANTI-Luc 4 working solution in a white opaque 96-well plate. The luminescence was measured by a BioTek plate reader, and the data were analysed by GraphPad Prism. To test the cell viability, 16 μl of MTS Assay working solution was added to each well. After one hour of incubation at 37°C, the absorbance at 490 nm was measured.

HEK-Blue human TLR3 (hTLR3), HEK-Blue human TLR7 (hTLR7), HEK-Blue Null1, HEK-Blue mouse TLR9 (mTLR9) NF-κB assays, and HEK-Lucia RIG-I IRF assay: cells were plated in 96-well plates (3 × 104 cells/well) in 80 or 90 µl of 10% HIFBS in DMEM. After 24 h, LNP samples or nucleic acid samples complexed with transfection reagents were added to each well (10 or 20 μl) to a final volume of 100 μl, giving the final desired dosages of polyRNAs. The treated cells were incubated for 24 h at 37°C.

For the NF-κB assay, 20 μl of supernatant was removed and combined with 180 µl of QUANTI-Blue working solution in a clear 96-well plate. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 1.5 h. The absorbance at 655 nm was measured by a BioTek plate reader, and the data were analysed by GraphPad Prism. For the IRF assay, 20 μl of supernatant was removed and combined with 50 µl of QUANTI-Luc 4 working solution in a white opaque 96-well plate. The luminescence was measured by a BioTek plate reader, and the data were analysed by GraphPad Prism. To test the cell viability, 18 μl of MTS Assay working solution was added to each well. After one hour of incubation at 37°C, the absorbance at 490 nm was measured.

BMDC activation assays

A total of 105 BMDCs isolated from C57BL/6 mice were seeded on a 96-well plate in complete BMDC media without antibiotics (Roswell Park Memorial Institute [RPMI] 1640, 10% HIFBS, 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol) overnight. BMDCs were treated with LNP-delivered polyRNA-based materials or respective controls. Twenty-four hours after treatment, supernatants were collected to determine secretion levels of cytokines using the Mouse IFN-beta and Mouse TNF-alpha DuoSet ELISA Kits (R&D Systems).

Animal experiments

All studies using animals were completed under an Animal Care Protocol approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Animal health assessments were completed using standard operating procedures approved by UM IACUC. For the subcutaneous single CT26.WT tumour model, CT26.WT (4 × 105) cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flank region of 6–7-week-old female BALB/c mice. Established CT26.WT (150–180 mm3) tumours were treated intratumorally with LNP-delivered polyRNAs or respective controls in 25 µl every three days for three total injections. Tumour volume was measured every three days via calliper measurements, and volumes were calculated using (Vtumour = L × W2 × 0.5, in which Vtumour is tumour volume, L is tumour length, and W is tumour width). Mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation when any diameter of the tumour reached 1.5 cm.

Statistical analysis

Results

Design of polyRNAs and activation of multiple PRRs

A panel of polyRNAs was rationally designed to incorporate structural motifs known to be recognized by distinct endosomal and cytosolic PRRs, enabling systematic interrogation of how polyRNAs as multivalent combinatorial agonists influence innate immune activation (Fig. 1A and B). For polyRNAs shown in Fig. 1, each repeat unit in this panel contained a 25 bp dsRNA segment with a common noncoding sequence to engage TLR3, whose recognition is largely dependent on RNA duplex length and structure in a sequence-independent manner [40]. All polyRNAs contained a 5′-triphosphate moiety, derived from in vitro transcription, permitting potential activation of the cytosolic sensor RIG-I, which preferentially recognizes 5′-triphosphate-containing dsRNAs [41]. To modulate activation of ssRNA-sensing endosomal PRRs, we varied the lengths of the ssRNA motifs within each repeat (6–20 nt). This parameter was selected based on the known length dependence of TLR7/8 activation [42] and also hypothesized to influence multivalent endosomal PRR engagement by altering the spatial separation of adjacent dsRNA motifs in polyRNA. In parallel, we varied the G/U content of the ssRNA motifs (33.3%–100%), as TLR7/8 can exhibit increased specificity for GU-rich ssRNA [43, 44]. The resulting polyRNAs, denoted as T3–T7, were transcribed from dumbbell-shaped circular DNA templates using T7 RNA polymerase and can fold into repeat hairpin or stem–bulge architectures [31, 45]. This design enables the simultaneous, multivalent presentation of dsRNA- and ssRNA-based PRR recognition elements within a single macromolecule, facilitating coordinated or synergistic engagement of TLR3, TLR7/8, and RIG-I. Structural simulation of each DNA template by Mfold [46] with labeled transcription initiation sites is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1.

To determine the expected co-transcriptionally folded secondary structures of the polyRNAs and their monomeric units, we used KineFold [32] for structural simulation. The monomeric units of T3–T7 polyRNAs folded as designed, forming a short double-stranded stem (25 nt) connected by two single-stranded loops (Fig. 1C). We then modeled oligomeric RNA (oligoRNA) structures with integral numbers of repeat units up to 400 nt in total length, to represent local folding in the polyRNAs. OligoRNAs of T4–T7 predominantly folded into concatenated hairpin-like structures connected by single-stranded spacers (Fig. 1D). While T3 oligoRNA also formed concatenated hairpins, it contained multiple pseudoknots in higher-order oligomers, indicating increased structural entanglement and reduced molecular extension.

The synthesized polyRNAs were first characterized by agarose gel electrophoresis, exhibiting average lengths of 500–1000 nt (Supplementary Fig. S2). The immune activation profile of each polyRNA was deconvoluted using specific PRR reporter cell lines, including HEK-Blue human TLR3, HEK-Blue human TLR7, and HEK-Lucia RIG-I cells, each of which co-expresses a specific PRR and an NF-κB- or IRF-inducible secreted downstream reporter. As we observed that activation levels by polyRNAs in different reporter cell lines are influenced by the transfection reagent, we used either the commercial lipid-based Lipofectamine 3000 or polymer-based Mirus TransIT-X2 transfection reagent, as optimized for each cell line. All polyRNAs in the panel induced activation of TLR3, TLR7, and RIG-I, confirming that the ssRNA, dsRNA, and 5′ triphosphate motifs assembled in the polyRNA macromolecules can be recognized by their respective PRRs (Fig. 1E and F). The polyRNAs induced much lower NF-κB activation in a parental HEK-Blue Null1 reporter cell line with weak baseline TLR expression, supporting the specificity of TLR3 and TLR7 activation.

In particular, T4, T5, and T6 polyRNAs outperformed poly(I:C), a synthetic TLR3 agonist based on a long dsRNA analogue, in TLR3 activation. These results indicate that the high dsRNA content of the polyRNAs, comprising linked 25-bp stems, can enable potent TLR3 activation. Interestingly, increasing the ssRNA spacer and loop lengths from 12 nt in T4 and T5 polyRNAs to 20 nt in T20 polyRNA greatly reduced TLR3 activation, suggesting that the spacing between dsRNA motifs in polyRNA can modulate TLR3 activation. Although canonical TLR3 activation requires continuous dsRNA segments of at least 40–50 bp [47, 48], our data indicate that multivalent presentation within higher-order assemblies can compensate for sub-optimal duplex lengths, likely through receptor clustering [48, 49]. Indeed, the corresponding monomeric and short oligomeric RNA units comprising only one or a few 25bp dsRNA units induced significantly lower TLR3 activation than the native higher-order polyRNA structures (Supplementary Fig. S3). This effect was particularly evident when comparing to native polyRNA structures purified by size-based filtration, while denatured polyRNAs exhibited reduced TLR3 activation. Together, these results suggest that polymerization enables efficient TLR3 signalling through multivalent spatial presentation, even when individual dsRNA motifs fall below the canonical length threshold.

Comparison of TLR7 activation levels showed that polyRNAs containing GU-rich ssRNA regions activated TLR7 more strongly, consistent with previous observations [29]. The polyRNAs further induced RIG-I activation to varying extents. Removal of the 5′ triphosphate by calf intestinal phosphatase digestion significantly attenuated RIG-I activation by both the benchmark RIG-I agonist, 3p-hpRNA (a commercial 89-nt 5′ triphosphate hairpin RNA), and most polyRNAs (Supplementary Fig. S4A and B), demonstrating that the 5′-terminal triphosphate is a critical determinant of RIG-I activation in these constructs. The polyRNAs exhibited lower RIG-I activation than the 3p-hpRNA benchmark agonist at equivalent total mass, which may be attributed to the much higher molecular weight and thus lower 5′ triphosphate mass content per molecule of polyRNA. This reduced potency may also be due to additional structural requirements for optimal RIG-I binding, including a 5′ blunt dsRNA terminus and the identity of the 5′ terminal base pair [41]. Based on transcription initiation constraints in our circular templates, the 5′ terminus in most polyRNAs is expected to reside in a single-stranded region rather than at a blunt dsRNA terminus. In addition, there may be some variation in the terminal base or base pair due to transcriptional heterogeneity and local folding. Despite these transcriptional constraints limiting canonical high-affinity RIG-I binding, RIG-I activation may arise from local dsRNA formation near the 5′ end due to RNA entanglement or pseudoknot formation (e.g. T3 polyRNA), or from rare transcription initiation events occurring within double-stranded regions.

To assess overall immune response via activation of the IRF pathway, we next tested the polyRNAs in a RAW 264.7 mouse macrophage reporter cell line (RAW-Dual). We observed much more potent IRF activation by polyRNAs over poly(I:C), likely due to the simultaneous activation of multiple PRRs, with even higher potency using GU-rich polyRNAs (Fig. 1G). Interestingly, we also observed potent RIG-I and IRF activation by a nonhairpin, predominantly single-stranded polyRNA (Supplementary Fig. S5), which may stem from extensive secondary structure formation in such a long RNA (Supplementary Figs S6 and S7). We further evaluated polyRNA-induced immune activation in human monocytes, confirming potent IRF activation in human THP1-Blue ISG reporter cells (Supplementary Fig. S8). The IRF activation trends among the polyRNAs closely mirrored those observed in the human RIG-I reporter cells, suggesting that polyRNA-induced IRF activation in THP1 monocytes is predominantly driven by RIG-I.

We additionally performed siRNA-mediated knockdown of TLR3, TLR7, and RIG-I in RAW-Dual cells, confirming that polyRNA-induced innate immune activation is receptor-dependent (Supplementary Figs S9 and S10 and Supplementary Discussion). Furthermore, removal of the 5′-triphosphate markedly reduced IRF activation, supporting a major contribution of RIG-I signalling to polyRNA-driven responses (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Altogether, these results demonstrate that polyRNAs can serve as combinatorial innate immune agonists, with tuneable PRR-specific activation via modulation of repeat component lengths and sequences.

LNP formulations can be optimized for specific polyRNA structures

We next evaluated the delivery of polyRNAs with LNPs, which have demonstrated efficacy in RNA delivery in both clinical and preclinical settings [50]. A panel of LNP formulations containing different ionizable lipids was screened for the delivery of polyRNAs in RAW 264.7 macrophage reporter cells (Fig. 2A and B). These polyRNAs spanned a broader parameter space and size distribution range, with simulated co-transcriptionally folded structures shown in Supplementary Figs S6 and S7. Hairpin-based polyRNAs were designed with a 25 bp dsRNA stem for TLR3 activation and 6–46 nt ssRNA loops or spacers with 26.7%–100% GU content for TLR7/8 activation. A nonhairpin, predominantly single-stranded polyRNA was also included.

We tested ionizable lipids with varied biodegradability, valency, and hydrophobic tail structures: the clinically approved DLin-MC3-DMA (MC3) [51] and other ionizable lipids that have been preclinically validated for mRNA or siRNA delivery, including OF-C4-Deg-Lin [35], L319 [36], C12-200 [37], TT3 [39], and FTT5 [38]. The polyRNAs were encapsulated into LNPs with varied diameters, encapsulation efficiencies, and polydispersities (Supplementary Table 3). The polyRNA LNPs with the highest IRF activation levels exhibited average diameters of 100–250 nm, polydispersities below 0.2, and encapsulation efficiencies of over 70%, consistent with many mRNA-loaded LNPs [34]. As shown in Fig. 2C–F, MC3-based LNPs delivered shorter polyRNAs much more efficiently than longer polyRNAs of over 4000 nt in average length, as determined by IRF and NF-κB activation in RAW 264.7 cells. This trend was maintained when activation levels were normalized to those of the corresponding Lipofectamine-delivered polyRNA or to cell viability (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. S11). Interestingly, MC3 LNPs yielded lower IRF activation compared to Lipofectamine but much higher NF-κB activation across most polyRNAs as well as poly(I:C), with several formulations exceeding the Lipofectamine-induced NF-κB response by over twofold (Fig. 2E and F). IRF activation was further improved for one of the most potent polyRNAs, T52, when using MC3 from different manufacturers (Supplementary Fig. S12).

The decreasing immune activation with increasing polyRNA length when delivered with MC3 LNPs may potentially be due to stronger interactions of longer polyRNA molecules, with the ionizable lipid decreasing intracellular RNA cargo release efficiency. Consistent with this hypothesis, LNPs formed with the biodegradable OF-C4-Deg-Lin (C4) ionizable lipid outperformed the nonbiodegradable MC3-based LNPs in IRF activation for longer polyRNAs, with comparable or slightly higher activation for shorter polyRNAs (Fig. 2C and D). Another biodegradable ionizable lipid, L319, yielded very weak IRF activation, which may be partially explained by poor encapsulation efficiencies (Supplementary Table 3). Other ionizable lipids (C12-200, FTT5, and TT3) also yielded low delivery efficiencies with various polyRNAs relative to Lipofectamine (Supplementary Fig. S13). Comparison of cellular uptake of short and long polyRNAs, delivered by various LNPs, showed little correlation with IRF activation, suggesting that differences in immune activation are influenced by downstream factors such as intracellular trafficking, RNA release kinetics, or presentation to PRRs (Supplementary Fig. S14). Together, these results suggest that polyRNA length is a key parameter in LNP delivery efficacy, and that delivery modality can influence not only the magnitude, but also the balance of innate immune pathway activation.

PolyRNA as a molecular scaffold for patterning complementary agonists

We next sought to harness the polyRNA as a molecular scaffold for patterning complementary or synergistic immunomodulatory functionalities in a spatially defined manner. Hybridizing nucleic acid moieties onto RNA macromolecules has shown promise in introducing immunostimulatory functionality without increasing formulation complexity [52]. Building upon such a strategy, we designed CpG-DNA “combs” comprising a CpG-rich DNA TLR9 agonist sequence, a hybridization sequence complementary to an ssRNA region within each polyRNA repeat, and a short linker sequence (Fig. 3A). The patterning of these combs along the polyRNA backbone can thus potentially enable multivalent presentation of TLR9 agonists. Successful hybridization was confirmed by gel electrophoresis, where a significant decrease in the free DNA comb band intensity was observed in the polyRNA/DNA hybrid sample compared to the DNA comb quantitative control (Fig. 3B). Under our optimized hybridization conditions, we achieved maximum comb incorporation at a DNA comb/polyRNA repeat molar ratio of 1:3. In contrast, a mismatch DNA comb control with a noncomplementary region showed no visible association with the polyRNA, confirming the sequence specificity of our hybridization-based patterning method (Supplementary Fig. S15).

To determine whether CpG-DNA-patterned polyRNA scaffolds enable activation of TLR9 in conjunction with the PRRs activated by the polyRNA backbone, we assessed the in vitro innate immune activation capabilities of polyRNA/CpG-DNA hybrids formed with hairpin structure-based (T52) and nonhairpin polyRNA (T19), using the HEK-Blue mTLR9 reporter cell line. For the nonhairpin polyRNA T19, when packaged with Lipofectamine, hybridization of complementary CpG-DNA combs elicited successful TLR9 activation, though less than that of the corresponding CpG-DNA comb quantitative control (Supplementary Fig. S16). This reduction in TLR9 activation may result from potential steric hindrance from the polyRNA scaffold, limiting CpG accessibility to TLR9. Further assessment of cumulative innate immune activation using RAW 264.7 mouse macrophage reporter cells showed that CpG-DNA-patterned T19 polyRNA retained most of the IRF activation induced by the polyRNA backbone (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, the CpG-DNA-patterned polyRNA triggered significantly higher NF-κB activation compared to polyRNA or CpG-DNA comb alone, indicating potential strong synergistic activation between TLR9 and other TLRs in such a patterned assembly (Fig. 3D).

To evaluate the combinatorial immune activation capabilities of CpG-DNA-patterned polyRNA delivered with LNPs, we next tested hybrids formed from the hairpin-structured polyRNA T52 and packaged with MC3-based LNPs. These LNPs exhibited similar sizes to those loaded with polyRNA alone, with 100–200 nm diameters and low polydispersities (Supplementary Fig. S17). Similar to the nonhairpin T19 polyRNA scaffold, IRF activation by the T52 polyRNA backbone was retained, although somewhat reduced upon CpG-DNA hybridization (Fig. 3E). Notably, assembly of CpG-DNA combs onto the T52 polyRNA scaffold triggered significantly higher TLR9 activation compared to the free CpG-DNA comb quantitative control, while no such effect was observed for mismatch CpG-DNA combs (Fig. 3F). These findings suggest that spatial patterning of PRR agonists on the polyRNA scaffold can enhance immune activation potency, likely by promoting stronger receptor oligomerization.

The differential effects of patterning CpG-DNA on distinct polyRNA scaffolds on TLR9 activation may be attributed to differences in the spacing between adjacent combs. Upon hybridization with either a DNA or RNA strand, RNA forms an A-form double helix (11 base pairs/helical turn, 2.6 Å axial rise/base pair, 23 Å helical diameter) [53]. ssRNAs are more flexible and have less defined structures than dsRNAs. However, if the ssRNA regions in polyRNA are assumed to have axial lengths per nucleotide similar to dsRNA, the spacing between adjacent CpG-DNA combs on T19 and T52 polyRNA hybrids can be estimated to be as low as 15.6 and 7.5 nm, respectively, based on hybridization of DNA combs to two adjacent RNA repeats. Thus, our data suggest that spatial assembly-enhanced activation is more likely to occur on a polyRNA scaffold with shorter spacing between adjacent annealing regions.

Because DNA:RNA hybrids can trigger the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)–stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway [54], we further assessed whether hybridizing CpG-DNA combs onto polyRNA activates this pathway. Comparison of IRF induction in wild-type versus cGAS-knockout RAW-Lucia ISG reporter cells revealed that T52:D52-CpG and T19:D19-CpG activation were largely cGAS-independent (Supplementary Fig. S18). Interestingly, CpG-DNA comb hybridization reduced TLR7 activation by the nonhairpin T19 but enhanced TLR7 activation for the hairpin-structured T52, suggesting that the net immune activation response reflects a scaffold-dependent balance between motif masking and increased nucleic acid density (Supplementary Fig. S19).

While we used magnesium ion-containing buffer to facilitate polyRNA hybridization with DNA combs, we additionally explored whether hybridization in the presence of manganese ions (Mn2+) can also promote efficient comb hybridization while enhancing immune activation. Mn2+ has been shown to amplify activation of the cGAS-STING pathway and synergize with immune checkpoint inhibitors [55, 56]. Compared to Mg2+, Mn2+ also effectively facilitated DNA comb hybridization to polyRNA scaffolds (Supplementary Fig. S20A). However, Mn2+-facilitated hybridization did not show additional benefit towards IRF activation and resulted in an antagonistic effect on TLR9 activation (Supplementary Fig. S20B and C).

We further investigated the tunability of the polyRNA scaffold immunostimulatory profile by incorporating pseudouridine (pseudoU) bases in the polyRNA backbone (Supplementary Fig. S21). Increasing substitution of uridines with pseudoU bases facilitated higher DNA comb hybridization efficiencies, which can be attributed to stronger base pair interactions (Supplementary Fig. S21A) [57, 58]. Consistent with the known ability of pseudoU incorporation to attenuate mRNA immunostimulation, IRF activation induced by polyRNAs and their CpG-DNA hybrids diminished with increasing pseudoU substitution (Supplementary Fig. S21B and C) [59]. However, unlike for unmodified T52 polyRNA, CpG-DNA assembly onto pseudoU-containing polyRNA did not enhance TLR9 activation compared to free CpG-DNA (Supplementary Fig. S21D). These observations provide a framework for understanding and tuning specific innate immune activation pathways in polyRNA scaffolding systems through modulation of backbone and agonist components. Altogether, our results demonstrate that polyRNAs provide a flexible platform for assembling DNA combs with extended PRR agonist motifs to elicit complementary or synergistic innate immune activation.

In vitro cytokine production induced by polyRNA scaffolds

Having verified the ability of polyRNA scaffolds to present a combinatorial array of functional innate immune agonists, we proceeded to examine the production of proinflammatory cytokines and type I interferons triggered by LNP-delivered polyRNA scaffolds in BMDCs. While all polyRNAs induced IFN-β and TNF-α secretion, longer polyRNAs (average length 5000 nt) elicited the lowest levels, consistent with observed MC3 LNP-delivered polyRNA trends in RAW264.7 reporter cells (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S22). This lack of increase in cytokine production may be partially due to an insufficient density of CpG-DNA combs assembled onto the polyRNA scaffold or steric hindrance of CpG-DNA presentation by the complex polyRNA tertiary structure.

Local administration of LNP-delivered polyRNA scaffolds eliminates established tumours

To assess the therapeutic potential of the polyRNA scaffolds, we explored their efficacies as in situ cancer vaccines in a CT26 syngeneic mouse model of colorectal cancer. As many others have shown, localized administration of immunotherapies such as PRR agonists and cytokines can achieve high local bioavailability while reducing systemic toxicity risks [60, 61]. We thus chose to employ intratumoral administration to evaluate the anti-tumour responses induced by the polyRNA scaffolds as a feasibility test.

In an initial in vivo pilot study, we tested three polyRNA scaffolds delivered with their respective optimal LNP formulations (N/P ratio = 10.92) from our in vitro results: (i) T52 polyRNA (hairpin repeat, average length 1000 nt) with MC3-based LNPs; (ii) T51 polyRNA (hairpin repeat, average length 9000 nt) with C4-based LNPs; and (iii) T19 polyRNA (nonhairpin repeat, average length 7000 nt) with C4-based LNPs. While the biodegradable C4 LNP-delivered T19 and T51 polyRNAs induced high IRF activation in vitro, these systems showed little therapeutic efficacies in CT26 tumour-bearing mice as single agents, for both base polyRNAs and their CpG-DNA-patterned hybrids (Supplementary Fig. S24). On the other hand, MC3 LNP-delivered T52 polyRNA and its CpG-DNA-patterned hybrid prolonged overall survival at a dose of 10 μg/mouse (Supplementary Fig. S24). T52 polyRNA scaffolds were thus selected for additional optimization and in vivo evaluation. We further confirmed specific PRR activation by T52, consistent with our initial polyRNA panel results (Supplementary Fig. S10A and B).

In an expanded in vivo study, CT26 tumour-bearing mice were treated with T52 polyRNA, its hybrids with CpG-DNA combs or mixture with mismatch CpG-DNA combs, or clinical benchmark agonists poly(I:C) and ODN1826, each delivered by MC3-based LNPs (MC3:DSPC:cholesterol:DMG-PEG molar ratio = 50/10/38.5/1.5, total lipids/RNA weight ratio = 20/1) (Fig. 5A). A lower dose of 5 μg/mouse was administered in this cohort to minimize potential toxicity. An MC3-based LNP formulation with an N/P ratio of 7.64 was used, as optimized from further in vitro innate immune activation experiments (Supplementary Fig. S25). While a transient body weight loss was observed in all treatment groups except the buffer and LNP vehicle-only control groups during treatment, the body weights decreased by 12% or less and returned to baseline levels quickly within 7 days after the last treatment, indicating good safety profiles (Supplementary Fig. S26A). Notably, polyRNA/CpG-DNA eradicated CT26 tumours in 37.5% (3/8) of mice, comparable to poly(I:C) (4/7) and ODN1826 (3/8) clinical benchmarks, while the base polyRNA only and polyRNA/mismatch CpG-DNA control eliminated tumours in 28.6% (2/7) of the mice, and the empty LNP vehicle showed no benefit (Fig. 5B and C). While we did not observe statistically significant differences in overall survival among the polyRNA-based and clinical benchmark treatment groups, polyRNA/CpG-DNA yielded the most significant improvement in overall survival compared to the buffer control group (Fig. 5B), exhibiting at least comparable therapeutic efficacy to poly(I:C) and ODN1826.

Furthermore, polyRNA/CpG-DNA demonstrated the most successful early-stage tumour suppression versus base polyRNA and polyRNA/mismatch CpG-DNA (Fig. 5D), with comparable tumour burden reduction versus the two clinical benchmarks (Supplementary Fig. S26B). Discussion

The challenge of developing rationally designed innate immune agonists to generate robust, durable disease-specific immune responses is compounded by a lack of tuneable molecular tools to interrogate the interactions between multiple different innate immune signalling pathways and to control spatial and combinatorial agonist presentation. Previous studies have investigated the combination-dependent activation of the innate immune system via chemically linked TLR tri-agonists [14] and nanoparticles presenting TLR agonists [23, 24]. However, chemical conjugation to a small molecule linker can potentially diminish the intrinsic activity of agonist motifs [14], while nanoparticle conjugation provides limited control of agonist spacing. Furthermore, the interactions between non-TLR PRRs signalling pathways remain underexplored. Thus, a highly modular platform is needed to assemble multiple PRR agonists with spatial control and to investigate different combinations of PRR signalling pathways in a high-throughput manner.

In this work, we developed polyRNAs as tuneable combinatorial immunostimulatory agents and as molecular scaffolds for patterning complementary innate immune agonists. In particular, we focused our efforts towards inducing strong combinatorial innate immune activation to drive tumour regression. We utilized a bottom-up approach to design polyRNAs that have linearly linked RIG-I, TLR3, and TLR7/8 agonist motifs along the backbone and can serve as building blocks for patterning TLR9 agonists via hybridization with modified DNA combs. Beyond RIG-I and TLR3/7/8, MDA5–LGP2–dependent sensing is also plausible given the known length and structural requirements for MDA5 activation [62, 63], although this was not directly assessed. This modular molecular platform, developed via RNA engineering, enables [1] the investigation of combinatorial innate immune responses induced by three or more PRR agonists, juxtaposed in the same molecule to ensure coactivation of innate immune pathways in the same cell; and [2] spatial modulation and multivalent presentation of agonists for tailoring immune activation potencies. In our in vitro mechanistic studies, we demonstrated that polyRNA-directed multivalent assembly of CpG-DNA TLR9 agonists can tune the innate immune response profile and induce synergistic activation. We further validated the in vivo therapeutic potential of these systems, showing that local administration of LNP-packaged polyRNA scaffolds as an in situ cancer vaccine yields comparable efficacy to clinical benchmarks in eliminating established tumours.

Our scaffold hybridization strategy is broadly applicable across a diverse array of PRR agonists, bypassing complicated chemical and bioconjugation processes, and can be expanded to additional functional biomolecules. The agonist activities of both the RNA scaffold and the branching combs are retained upon hybridization, allowing assessment of the combined and potentially synergistic immune activation. Importantly, this capacity for rational design can facilitate the decoupling of therapeutic efficacy from the systemic toxicity that has historically limited synthetic analogues, thus expanding therapeutic windows. Whereas the clinical translation of poly(I:C) has been hindered by adverse events stemming from indiscriminate broad-spectrum activation [64, 65], polyRNAs provide a modular framework for rationally tuning immune engagement. In particular, receptor-specific activation (e.g. RIG-I or TLR7/8) can be encoded at the sequence level, providing a strategy to reduce reliance on dominant TLR3 signalling, which has been associated with systemic inflammation [66]. In our studies, polyRNA formulations displayed manageable safety profiles in vivo. We anticipate that continued optimization of polyRNA immune activation profiles will enable further improvements in both potency and safety.

While the structural flexibility of the polyRNA scaffold can potentially promote optimal receptor engagement for immune activation, there remain limitations in the level of spatial control by our current polyRNA scaffolds. Our in vitro mechanistic studies suggest that decreased spacing between certain TLR agonist repeat motifs within or assembled onto the polyRNA backbone can enhance immune activation. In the case of CpG-DNA TLR9 agonists, it has been demonstrated that a TH1-polarized anti-tumour immune response is induced when CpG-DNA motifs are presented at a spacing between 2.5 and 7.0 nm. In the context of a DNA origami scaffold, a spacing of 3.5 nm, which approximates the distance between two CpG-DNA molecules binding to dimeric TLR9 in a crystal structure [67], exhibited the greatest potency as an in situ cancer vaccine, while a 7 nm spacing showed similar effects as random spacing [15]. A key limitation of the polyRNA scaffolds in this work is that they do not provide perfect spatial definition and may cause partial steric hindrance of CpG-DNA binding to TLR9 due to the three-dimensional entanglement of the polyRNA. We posit that the flexibility of the scaffold can partially mitigate the challenge presented by the polyRNA’s structural complexity, as the polyRNA backbone can undergo conformational changes to adjust the spacing between CpG-DNA molecules while the target cell reorganizes its cytoskeleton for PRR oligomerization to achieve flexibility-directed, adaptable optimal activation. Such a mechanism may also be possible for the TLR agonist repeat motifs along the polyRNA backbone and is not attainable for rigid scaffolds such as DNA origami. Indeed, we observed that T52 polyRNA/CpG-DNA, in which the spacing between CpG-DNA combs is estimated to be 7.5 nm for consecutive polyRNA repeats and even larger at the lower DNA comb:polyRNA repeat molar ratios used, led to greater tumour regression compared to the mismatch DNA comb hybrid control (random spatial arrangement).

Another limiting factor in the polyRNA scaffolds in this work is that the hybridization efficiency of DNA combs restricts the maximum density of patterned agonist molecules. Under our hybridization conditions, we achieved complete hybridization at a DNA comb/polyRNA repeat molar ratio of 1:3, as determined by agarose gel electrophoresis. This suboptimal density of the multivalently presented TLR9 agonists, together with the spatial imprecision posed by potential entanglement of the polyRNA structure, likely diminished the extent to which we could fully exploit multivalent agonist presentation for synergistic immune activation. Consistent with this limitation, we observed limited improvement in the therapeutic efficacy of the polyRNA/CpG-DNA over polyRNA only in our in vivo study. Future work will tackle these challenges by exploring additional methods to increase the density of patterned agonists via enzymatically catalysed hybridization [68] or covalent conjugation [69], as well as co-transcriptional incorporation of the patterned agonists during polyRNA synthesis. The polyRNA scaffold itself can also potentially be engineered to present more densely spaced comb hybridization sequences.

An additional consideration for future investigation is the heterogeneous size distribution of the polyRNA scaffolds. While this heterogeneity is intrinsic to the RCT process, and we observed high reproducibility in size and function between batches for each polyRNA, the polyRNA size distributions can potentially be tuned by RCT parameters for a given template. Size fractionation methods can also be utilized to achieve less polydisperse polyRNA scaffolds as well as higher-resolution mechanistic studies of the effects of polyRNA length on immune activation. These studies can be carried out in conjunction with expanded screening of LNP formulations or other delivery vehicles to determine polyRNA scaffold structure–function–carrier relationships. In addition to RCT, future studies will explore the assembly of pre-synthesized RNA strands to generate RNA scaffolds or RNA origami structures for multi-PRR agonist presentation with improved polydispersity and spatial control.

Concurrent with these structural refinements for polyRNA-based immune agonists, defining polyRNA structure-function-carrier relationships will be critical for therapeutic applications. While many clinically and preclinically validated LNPs have been optimized for mRNA delivery, the intracellular trafficking, release kinetics, and subcellular localization that maximize mRNA translation may not be optimal for polyRNAs, which engage PRRs across endosomal and cytosolic compartments [70]. Systematic screening of ionizable lipids and tuning of lipid ratios have been shown to strongly influence cellular trafficking and innate immune engagement [71, 72]. Applying combinatorial lipid libraries to polyRNA delivery may therefore enhance PRR activation and represents an important future optimization step [73, 74].

Beyond LNPs, polyRNA can alternatively be delivered with biodegradable microspheres, which have been extensively explored for immunostimulatory nucleic acid delivery to enable localized, depot-like release with prolonged endosomal or phagosomal exposure while reducing systemic distribution and cytokine-associated toxicity [64, 75]. These properties are particularly relevant for polyRNAs, whose TLR agonist activity depends on multivalent receptor engagement in the endosome rather than maximal cytosolic delivery. Furthermore, microsphere formulations are compatible with scalable manufacturing and have precedent in vaccine and adjuvant development, supporting their translational feasibility. Future work can explore microsphere-based platforms to achieve controlled, localized innate immune activation with improved safety profiles.

Finally, this work focused only on the use of polyRNA scaffolds as a single therapeutic agent for in situ cancer vaccination via local administration. While our in vivo data validated the feasibility of using polyRNA scaffolds as an immunotherapeutic agent, with comparable efficacy to the known TLR agonist benchmarks poly(I:C) and ODN1826, future studies will investigate combination therapies to evaluate the ability to augment immune checkpoint blockade, as well as systemic administration of the polyRNA scaffold. We expect that the programmable nature of the polyRNA scaffolds, together with the abovementioned design refinements, will enable customizable immune response profiles—something that is not readily achievable by current clinically used vaccine adjuvants—and high therapeutic efficacies with low toxicities. In conclusion, we envision that our polyRNA-based immune agonist platform can provide a versatile molecular tool for understanding and tuning innate immune responses, paving the way for rationally designed vaccine adjuvants.
