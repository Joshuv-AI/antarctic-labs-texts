Metformin as a Multifaceted Therapeutic Agent for Gastrointestinal Diseases: Mechanisms, Clinical Efficacy, and Future Directions
Authors: Sayedeh Azimeh Hosseini, Hassan Valadbeigi, Seyedeh Mahdieh Khoshnazar, Saeed Khoshnood, Shahab Falahi, Delsuz Rezaee, Ali Hematian, Mohammad Hossein Haddadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe high prevalence of gastrointestinal (GI) diseases and their significant impact on the quality of life require new therapeutic strategies. The development of novel therapeutic strategies should prioritize targeting the fundamental pathophysiological mechanisms underlying these diseases, including inflammation, cellular proliferation, and gut microbiota dysregulation. Metformin, a first‐line antidiabetic agent, exhibits pleiotropic pharmacological properties beyond its glucose‐lowering effects, such as anti‐inflammatory, antiproliferative, and microbiota‐modulating activities. These multifaceted mechanisms position metformin as a promising therapeutic candidate for a spectrum of disorders, from IBS to liver disorders. This review synthesizes preclinical and clinical evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of metformin across GI pathologies—such as Helicobacter pylori infection, inflammatory bowel disease, colorectal cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma—while elucidating its molecular mechanisms, such as AMPK/mTOR modulation, NF‐κB inhibition, and gut barrier stabilization. We critically evaluate combination therapies, ongoing clinical trials, and challenges, including lactic acidosis risk and GI intolerance to position metformin as a repurposed agent for GI disease management.Keywords: AMPK, cancer stem cells, combination therapy, gastrointestinal diseases, metformin, microbiota

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) diseases impose a profound global health burden, reflecting stark geographic and socioeconomic disparities. Developing countries endure high morbidity and mortality from severe infectious GI disorders. In contrast, developed regions exhibit a greater prevalence of inflammatory and neoplastic GI conditions, with the United States alone bearing annual costs exceeding $150 billion [1, 2, 3]. Rising urbanization accelerates inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) incidence, now exceeding 6.8 million globally [4]. GI cancers, which account for over a quarter of all cancer cases worldwide, are projected to increase sharply by 2040. Given the poor survival rates for many GI conditions and the rising global burden, the identification of novel therapeutic strategies to enhance treatment effectiveness and survival rates is urgently needed [5]. Research by Tseng demonstrates metformin's significant benefit in lowering risks for both intestinal diverticula and acute appendicitis in diabetic patients. These studies consistently show a clear dose–response relationship for this protective effect. The benefit is robust across different formulations of the medication [6, 7].

The pleiotropic effects of metformin benefits extend well beyond glycemic control, with robust cardiovascular protection evidenced in the landmark UKPDS trial, showing a 39% reduction in myocardial infarction and a 33% reduction in cardiovascular mortality, effects persisting after glycemic convergence. Metformin modestly reduces weight, improves lipid profiles, and enhances insulin sensitivity via AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation, also benefiting polycystic ovary syndrome. It exerts effects on vascular, metabolic, and reproductive health, demonstrating diverse therapeutic potential, as illustrated in Figure 1A [8].

The ability of metformin to modulate inflammation, oxidative stress, and cellular metabolism positions it as a multifaceted therapeutic agent for conditions ranging from IBD to liver disorders (Figure 1B) [9, 10]. The antibacterial effect of metformin against Helicobacter pylori ( H. pylori ), which causes gastric ulcers (GU) and gastric cancer (GC), is also known. In addition, the long‐term use of metformin may serve as a preventive measure against GC (Chin‐Hsiao [11]), esophageal [11], and colon cancer [12] in patients with T2D. Extensive studies have confirmed these findings [11, 13]. Recent studies have demonstrated that metformin can influence gut microbiota composition, strengthen intestinal barrier function, and reduce disease severity in various GI pathologies [14, 15].

Due to its predominant renal excretion, the safety of metformin in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) or acute kidney injury (AKI) requires more attention and evaluation [16]. Historically, the perceived risk of lactic acidosis led to contraindications in renal impairment. However, emerging evidence has challenged this paradigm, revealing ongoing debate regarding metformin's role in CKD management [17, 18]. This complex relationship between the beneficial of metformin and adverse GI effects highlights the need for a comprehensive understanding of its mechanisms and clinical applications in GI diseases [19]. In addition, the drug increases intestinal glucose uptake and lactate production, enhances glucagon‐like peptide‐1 concentrations, expands the bile acid pool within the intestine, and significantly alters the gut microbiome composition [20]. Given these multifaceted actions of metformin on intestinal physiology and metabolism, a systematic exploration of its broader therapeutic mechanisms in GI diseases is warranted.

This review aims to comprehensively evaluate the therapeutic potential of metformin in GI diseases by elucidating its molecular mechanisms, including AMPK/mTOR modulation, nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB) inhibition, microbiota regulation, and anti‐cancer pathways such as cancer stem cell targeting and ferroptosis induction. It synthesizes preclinical and clinical evidence across diverse GI conditions, from H. pylori infection and inflammatory bowel disease to colorectal and hepatocellular carcinoma. Furthermore, the review examines synergistic effects of metformin in combination therapies, addresses clinical limitations and outlines future directions.

Mechanisms of Metformin in GI Diseases

Metabolic Modulation

Metformin exerts its primary effects by inhibiting mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I, reducing ATP and elevating AMP levels, thereby activating AMPK, a central regulator of cellular energy homeostasis [21]. AMPK activation enhances autophagy and energy metabolism while suppressing oxidative phosphorylation, glucagon signaling, gluconeogenesis, lipogenesis, and protein synthesis [22]. It inhibits mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) by modulating TSC2 and phosphorylating Raptor, counteracting Akt‐driven mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) activation [23]. In addition to its effects on AMPK, metformin further suppresses mTOR signaling through Rag GTPase–dependent pathways [24]. This dual AMPK activation and mTOR inhibition modulate GI physiology, including Na+/H+ exchanger 3 (NHE3) downregulation via phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and Nedd4‐2–mediated retrieval and also reshape immune responses by shifting mTOR‐dependent T‐cell differentiation toward regulatory T (Treg) cells [25, 26].

The AMPK/mTOR signaling axis is central to the therapeutic actions of metformin in GI diseases, owing to its pivotal role in cellular energy homeostasis and metabolic regulation [27]. Metformin exerts therapeutic effects in obesity, metabolic syndrome, and pancreatic cancer by restoring critical metabolic signaling pathways disrupted in these conditions [28]. Metformin acts through AMPK‐dependent and independent intestinal pathways to regulate energy homeostasis, restore autophagy, and preserve barrier integrity via microbiota and immune modulation. Experimental, clinical, and epidemiological evidence links AMPK/mTOR modulation to reduced incidence, progression, and mortality of diverse gastrointestinal malignancies [21, 29, 30, 31]. In prospective cohorts, metformin combined with systemic chemotherapy increased AMPKα phosphorylation in 63% of patients, with 60% achieving disease control, correlating with enhanced AMPK signaling [21]. Beyond metabolic regulation, metformin inhibits the AMPK/Akt/PI3K/mTOR pathway, enhances CD8+ T‐cell responses, and modulates immune checkpoints, while in metabolic liver diseases it activates p38 MAPK, restores lipid homeostasis, reverses hepatocarcinogenesis, and normalizes macrophage polarization with T‐cell recovery [32, 33]. Through integrated regulation of autophagy and energy homeostasis, metformin improves hepatic steatosis in metabolic dysfunction‐associated fatty liver disease by elevating autophagy via AMPK activation and mTOR inhibition [30].

Anti‐Inflammatory Pathways

Metformin exerts its anti‐inflammatory effects in GI disorders primarily through inhibition of the NF‐κB signaling pathway, a central regulator of inflammatory responses [34]. By activating AMPK, metformin suppresses NF‐κB activity via the PI3K‐Akt pathway, leading to decreased synthesis of nitric oxide, prostaglandin E2, and pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α in macrophages [35]. Metformin mitigates TNF‐α‐driven NF‐κB activation, suppresses IL‐6 release, and reduces IL‐1β production in macrophages [15]. In GI tissues, these actions translate into reduced ICAM‐1/VCAM‐1 expression, diminished epithelial cytokine production, and protection against LPS‐induced intestinal barrier injury, consolidating NF‐κB inhibition as a core axis of its therapeutic benefit [15].

The immunomodulatory properties of metformin arise from its capacity to disrupt mTOR‐mediated signaling, by reprogramming T cell differentiation trajectories [26]. Metformin shifts immune responses from inflammatory Th1/Th2/Th17 polarization toward immunoregulatory T cells (Treg) dominance through AMPK activation [26]. Clinically, this manifests as elevated AMPK and Foxp3 with reduced Th17 cells in ulcerative colitis (UC) [36]. Metformin also modulates the STAT3/STAT5 axis, AMPK‐mediated STAT3 inhibition suppresses Th17 differentiation and IL‐17 production, while STAT5 activation sustains FOXP3 expression in Tregs. This dual action reduces TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐1β, and IL‐8, promotes anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophages, and induces apoptosis in pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophages [35]. Collectively, these mTOR/STAT3‐driven mechanisms underpin the efficacy of metformin across IBD, autoimmune disorders, and other Th17/Treg‐imbalanced conditions [37]. The immunomodulatory properties of metformin were extensively characterized by Ursini et al. [35].

Microbiota Modulation

Metformin modulates gut microbiota toward a short‐chain fatty acid (SCFA)‐enriched profile. It promotes the growth of SCFA‐producing taxa, notably Akkermansia muciniphila ( A. muciniphila ), Lactobacillus, and Bifidobacterium, enhancing butyrate, propionate, and acetate synthesis [14]. These metabolites possess notable therapeutic potential against GI inflammatory disorders and may serve as viable alternatives to conventional antibiotic growth promoters (AGPs) [38]. Enrichment of mucin‐degrading A. muciniphila supports mucus layer renewal and barrier integrity. SCFAs, via AMPK activation, upregulate tight junction proteins (occludin, ZO‐1, claudin‐1), expand goblet cell mass, and suppress LPS translocation, reducing endotoxemia and inflammation [39].

Metformin exerts significant GI therapeutic effects via modulation of bile acid metabolism and farnesoid X receptor (FXR) signaling, acting independently of its canonical AMPK activation. By reducing Bacteroides fragilis , a key bile salt hydrolase producer, metformin alters the bile acid pool [40, 41]. This occurs through suppression of intestinal bile acid reabsorption, increasing luminal bile acid exposure and beneficial metabolic signaling [42].

Anti‐Cancer Mechanisms

Metformin exerts its anti‐cancer potential in GI malignancies through a multifaceted network of AMPK‐dependent and ‐independent mechanisms. AMPK activation orchestrates key tumor‐suppressive processes, including mTOR inhibition, cell cycle arrest, suppression of oncogenes such as c‐MYC, activation of p53, and apoptosis induction via Stat3/Bcl‐2 and cytochrome C release [43, 44].

Metformin also modulates autophagy in a context‐dependent manner, either promoting or inhibiting it through AMPK regulation [38]. Independently of AMPK, metformin activates the PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 stress response, downregulates NF‐κB signaling, and blocks glutaminase‐mediated autophagy [45]. Furthermore, metformin induces pyroptosis via FOXO3–NLRP3 activation, collectively disrupting cancer cell survival and promoting diverse forms of regulated cell death [46].

Metformin promotes ferroptosis by directly downregulating SLC7A11 and GPX4, sensitizing tumor cells to iron‐dependent lipid peroxidation, while AMPK‐mediated mTOR inhibition enhances autophagy, particularly ferritinophagy, increasing intracellular iron and oxidative stress [47].

Cancer Stem Cell Inhibition

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are key drivers of tumor recurrence, metastasis, and treatment resistance, and metformin targets these populations through multiple, complementary mechanisms. In pancreatic cancer, metformin selectively inhibits CD133+ CSCs while sparing CD24+CD44+ESA+ populations, highlighting its specificity for CSC subsets most responsible for chemoresistance and relapse [48]. Mechanistically, metformin activates AMPK‐dependent inhibition of the sonic hedgehog signaling pathway, reversing epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) and impairing self‐renewal capacity [49]. These effects are further potentiated by induction of the unfolded protein response, immune system activation, and cell cycle arrest, collectively preventing recurrence and enhancing therapeutic response [50].

Emerging studies extend these findings to Colorectal cancer (CRC) and GC, demonstrating that metformin suppresses CSC activity by modulating oxidative phosphorylation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and Wnt signaling. Specifically, inhibition of the Wnt3a/β‐catenin pathway reduces stem cell formation and EMT in colorectal CSCs, though reactivation of this pathway can reverse the inhibitory effects of metformin [51]. Metformin selectively induces apoptosis in CSCs, while non‐CSC tumor cells primarily undergo cell cycle arrest without complete elimination, reflecting the heightened metabolic vulnerability of CSCs to glucose modulation [52, 53].

The efficacy of metformin is closely linked to its regulation of the AMPK‐mTOR signaling axis and glutamine metabolism. By impeding glutamine utilization, metformin enhances CSC suppression and may overcome potential resistance mechanisms [54]. Supporting this, metformin has been shown to reduce the expression of key CSC markers including CD44, CD133, and ALDH1, while modulating mTOR signaling [55]. In 3D culture systems, metformin significantly decreases tumor spheroid formation, downregulates CSC‐associated markers, and promotes differentiation, further illustrating its anti‐CSC potential [56].

Preclinical studies reinforce these mechanistic insights. In CRC models, metformin suppresses proliferation and induces apoptosis through upregulation of poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase and caspase‐3, demonstrating efficacy in both normal and 5‐fluorouracil (5‐FU)‐resistant cells [57]. In gastric CSCs, prolonged metformin exposure reduces proliferation and induces apoptosis, accompanied by downregulation of Akt expression, emphasizing the central role of Akt in CSC survival [58].

Available clinical evidence highlights the therapeutic efficacy of metformin across a spectrum of gastrointestinal diseases, demonstrating benefits in both metabolic regulation and oncological outcomes. Esophageal Cancer

Metformin has been shown to exert anti‐tumor effects through multiple mechanisms in esophageal cancer. It inhibits tumor cell proliferation and induces apoptosis by suppressing the activation of NF‐κB [59]. In esophageal adenocarcinoma cell lines, metformin reduces cell growth by downregulating cyclin D1 and cyclin‐dependent kinases 4 and 6, as well as by decreasing the phosphorylation of key growth factors [60]. Beyond its direct effects on tumor cells, metformin also modulates the tumor immune microenvironment of esophagus tumor. Administration of metformin shifts macrophage polarization from the M1/M2 phenotype toward a phospho‐Stat3‐positive profile, fostering an immune‐tolerant environment. Additionally, it suppresses the activity of cytotoxic T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, NK T cells, and Th17 T cells, thereby limiting immune evasion [61]. Observational studies, such as a large nationwide cohort from Taiwan, provide support for a preventive role of metformin in esophageal cancer among patients with type 2 diabetes. This study demonstrated a significantly reduced risk of esophageal cancer in metformin users compared to nonusers, with the protective effect becoming more pronounced with longer cumulative duration of medication use [11]. Clinically, the addition of metformin to concurrent chemoradiotherapy has been associated with a dose‐dependent enhancement of treatment response [62]. These findings highlight the potential of metformin as an adjunctive therapeutic agent, warranting further investigation to confirm its protective and anti‐cancer effects in esophageal cancer, as suggested by recent meta‐analyses [63, 64, 65].

Gastric Diseases

Helicobacter pylori Infection

Helicobacter pylori infection is a key driver of GU and GC [66]. Metformin has shown potential in mitigating these conditions, with evidence linking its use to a dose‐dependent reduction in H. pylori prevalence among individuals with T2D [67, 68]. Metformin demonstrates partial antimicrobial activity against H. pylori , inhibiting bacterial growth and viability in vitro and in vivo, and synergizes with amoxicillin to enhance eradication [69, 70]. Importantly, metformin exerts immunomodulatory effects in aspirin‐induced gastric ulcer models infected with H. pylori , promoting regulatory T cell responses and TGF‐β production. Histological observations indicate that metformin reduces ulcer severity while permitting bacterial colonization, suggesting that immune tolerance mechanisms may underlie its effects [70].

Gastric Ulcer

Insulin and metformin differentially modulate indomethacin‐induced gastric ulceration. In diabetic rats, both agents prevent ulcer formation, whereas in nondiabetic rats, gastroprotection is observed only with insulin, suggesting distinct mechanisms of action [71]. Metformin consistently reduces gastric acidity and ulcer index, mitigates inflammation, enhances mucin secretion, and promotes ulcer healing, while also protecting against ulcer development [70, 72]. Gastric Cancer

Gastric cancer is the fifth most common malignancy worldwide and the fifth leading cause of cancer‐related mortality [77]. Epidemiological studies have shown that the incidence of GC in diabetic patients treated with metformin is 36.4% lower than in 39.7% of patients not taking metformin [78]. Metformin use has been consistently associated with reduced GC risk, although meta‐analyses report heterogeneous outcomes, highlighting variability in study populations and methodologies [77, 79, 80, 81, 82, 83]. Nonetheless, a substantial body of evidence supports its potential as a therapeutic intervention for GC (Table 2). Interpretation of observational findings is limited by methodological challenges, including inadequate control for H. pylori infection, fluctuations in glycemic management, and time‐related biases, which may confound the observed associations.

Intestinal Diseases

Irritable Bowel Syndrome

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a chronic disorder that affects the large intestine and colon. The exact causes of IBS are multifactorial and involve a complex interplay of genetics, psychosocial elements, environmental factors, visceral hypersensitivity, inflammation, gut motility, dietary components, and the gut microbiota [86].

Metformin has been shown to be effective in reducing visceral hypersensitivity and immobility while improving sucrose absorption in rats with IBS‐like symptoms [87]. The potential of metformin to improve gut integrity is supported by the results showing a reduction in the urinary lactulose/mannitol ratio and sucralose excretion. These markers suggest that metformin may play a role in maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier, a critical factor in conditions such as IBS where the barrier may be compromised [88]. The inhibitory effect of metformin on mast cell activation and the suppression of pro‐inflammatory factors, including IL‐6, IL‐18, tryptase, PAR‐2, and ERK, indicate its potential clinical efficacy in the treatment of patients with IBS or IBS‐like symptoms [87].

Metformin reduces visceral hypersensitivity, improves intestinal permeability, and activates specific signaling pathways such as AMPK, nitric oxide, and central dopamine D2 [89]. Metformin attenuated the negative effects of TNF‐α on TEER and paracellular permeability of Caco‐2 cells, protected the integrity of tight junctions, and reduced TNF‐α‐induced MLCK production [90].

Given the positive results of metformin in the treatment of complications associated with IBS, scientists are currently investigating the development of novel derivatives of metformin (imeglimin) to improve their therapeutic potential in IBS. Through its mechanisms involving AMPK and opioid receptors, imeglimin has been found to effectively block lipopolysaccharide‐ or CRF‐induced visceral hypersensitivity and colonic hyperpermeability, improving visceral sensation and colonic barrier function in patients with IBS [91]. Although the therapeutic potential of metformin in IBS has not yet been fully explored, the pharmacological properties of metformin suggest that it may have a positive effect on the prevention and treatment of IBS.

Inflammatory Bowel Disease

Inflammatory bowel disease is a group of chronic inflammatory diseases of the GI tract, including UC and Crohn's disease (CD). The exact causes of IBD are still unclear but are thought to be influenced by a combination of genetic, environmental, microbial, and immunological factors [92].

In vitro studies demonstrate that metformin preserves intestinal barrier function by maintaining tight junction proteins such as claudin‐2 and claudin‐7 in organoid injury models, an effect mediated through AMPK phosphorylation [93]. Animal studies further support these findings, showing that metformin suppresses NF‐κB activation in intestinal epithelial cells, reduces oxidative stress, modulates microbiota composition, and ameliorates both colitis and colitis‐associated tumorigenesis [94]. Clinically, metformin use in patients with type 2 diabetes has been associated with a reduced risk of IBD, highlighting its translational potential as a therapeutic option [95].

In murine colitis models, metformin enhanced AMPK activation and alleviated disease severity, as reflected by reduced weight loss, preserved colon length, and lower disease activity indices. This improvement was accompanied by downregulation of proinflammatory mediators (IL‐18, IL‐1β, IL‐6, COX‐2, ICAM‐1, iNOS), underscoring potent anti‐inflammatory activity [96]. Mechanistically, metformin upregulated the Small Heterodimer Partner Interacting Leucine Zipper Protein (SMILE), an orphan bZIP nuclear receptor that suppresses NF‐κB–driven inflammation, while concurrently increasing AMPK and Foxp3 expression and reducing IL‐17, collectively promoting mucosal immune homeostasis in ulcerative colitis [36]. In line with these mechanistic and preclinical findings, recent clinical trials have evaluated the therapeutic potential of metformin in patients with UC. In one completed study involving 40 patients with mild to moderately active disease, metformin at 500 mg TID for 8 weeks significantly reduced colonic inflammation and improved disease activity, as reflected by NF‐κB suppression and Mayo score improvements (NCT05553704). Another ongoing trial is recruiting 46 patients with mild to moderate ulcerative colitis already on mesalamine therapy, testing the addition of metformin at 500 mg BID to mesalamine 1 g TID; its results are awaited to clarify whether combination therapy enhances quality of life and clinical response (NCT05574387). A further double‐blind investigation is assessing 40 patients with mild to moderately active disease receiving metformin 500 mg TID alongside standard therapy for 8 weeks, with outcomes focused on colonic NF‐κB expression (NCT04750135).

Emerging evidence highlights the pivotal role of gut microbiota in the pathogenesis of IBD. Interventions that modulate the gut microbiome, including metformin, have been shown to restore microbial balance and improve clinical outcomes in IBD [97]. Metformin treatment has been associated with restoration of microbial diversity reduced by inflammation, a decrease in pathogenic species such as Escherichia coli and Shigella, and enrichment of beneficial taxa including Lactobacillus and Akkermansia [14]. Similarly, in patients with UC, metformin administration led to favorable alterations in gut microbial composition, promoting the growth of Lactobacillus and Akkermansia muciniphila while reducing the abundance of Erysipelatoclostridium species [98]. This finding highlights the potential of metformin as a therapeutic agent for modulating the gut microbiota in patients with UC.

Colorectal Cancer

Colorectal cancer is a prevalent form of cancer, ranking as the third most frequently diagnosed malignancy worldwide [99]. In recent years, extensive scientific research has revealed a variety of metformin‐based treatment and prevention strategies for CRC. Preclinical and clinical studies have successfully characterized the genetic mechanisms underlying the efficacy of metformin in CRC models [100, 101].

Metformin exerts its anti‐cancer effect in CRC by inhibiting angiogenesis and cell proliferation through the activation of specific cellular signaling pathways that induce programmed cell death [102]. This mechanism is mediated by inhibition of mTORC1. Furthermore, metformin enhances AMPK activity, which further suppresses mTOR activity and acts as an effective agent against proliferation and angiogenesis in CRC [103]. In addition, activated AMPK inhibits the insulin/insulin‐like growth factor (IGF) signaling pathway as a conserved regulator of cell growth, proliferation, and survival in colon tumors [100]. Metformin elicits apoptosis and autophagy by affecting oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolic equilibrium via AMPK and mTOR signaling [104].

Metformin showed a dose‐dependent anticancer effect in a CRC model and reduced cancer cell proliferation. This was achieved through AMPK phosphorylation, which inhibits the activation of ribosomal protein S6 kinase (S6K) in HT29 [105]. Several studies have shown that metformin inhibits cell proliferation and enhances apoptosis and autophagy in different CRC cell lines such as SW‐480 and HT29, in a dose‐ and time‐dependent manner [106, 107]. The ability of metformin to reduce polyp recurrence is influenced by its effect on insulin resistance and the insulin/IGF‐related signaling pathway, as shown by Higurashi et al. [108]; insulin resistance may serve as a potential biomarker for predicting the anti‐tumor effects of metformin.

Evidence indicates that metformin use is associated with reduced CRC incidence, lower recurrence rates, and improved overall survival compared with nonusers [109]. A marked reduction in colorectal adenomas and advanced adenomas has also been reported among individuals prescribed metformin. Survival analyses demonstrate significant benefits, with users showing higher overall survival (HR 0.60, CI 0.53–0.67; p < 0.001) and CRC‐specific survival (HR 0.66, CI 0.59–0.74; p < 0.001), suggesting a therapeutic advantage in colorectal neoplasia [109]. A systematic review encompassing 66 studies and data from over 146 000 individuals with T2D further supported a protective role, noting not only reduced CRC risk but also enhanced chemosensitivity in preclinical models [110].

Beyond prevention, metformin may also enhance treatment responses. Patients with rectal cancer receiving metformin exhibited higher rates of pathologic complete response compared to both diabetic nonusers and nondiabetic counterparts, along with improvements in disease‐free and overall survival [111]. Nevertheless, inconsistencies across studies highlight the complexity of these associations and underscore the need for further investigation to clarify the role of metformin in CRC prevention and therapy.

GI‐Associated Organs

Liver Diseases

Metformin has emerged as a promising therapeutic candidate in hepatology, showing efficacy across a spectrum of liver disorders, including NAFLD, hepatic malignancies, and cholangiocarcinoma [112]. Evidence indicates that metformin maintains safe plasma concentrations of both the drug and lactate in patients with chronic liver disease (CLD) and T2D, with levels remaining below established safety thresholds. Comparable pharmacokinetics between these groups further suggest that impaired hepatic function does not significantly alter metformin metabolism [113].

Metformin appears to directly modulate fibrogenic pathways. Liver fibrosis, a precursor to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), is driven by complex interactions among hepatic stellate cells, Kupffer cells, and hepatocytes. Metformin has been shown to attenuate fibrogenesis through multi‐cellular mechanisms, thereby offering potential benefits in halting disease progression [114]. In parallel, both preclinical and clinical findings associate metformin use with a reduced incidence of HCC in diabetic patients [115]. Importantly, experimental evidence in nondiabetic HCC models suggests that metformin exerts immunomodulatory effects by regulating all‐trans retinoic acid (atRA) levels and enhancing CD8+ T cell activity through AMPK/STAT3/Gadd45β/JNK/c‐Jun signaling. Such mechanisms may broaden its therapeutic reach to nondiabetic populations [116].

In the context of biliary fibrosis, metformin treatment has been shown to inhibit Yes‐associated protein (YAP), a central mediator of fibrogenesis. This effect was demonstrated in animal models resembling primary sclerosing cholangitis, supporting its potential role in targeting cholangiopathies [117]. Furthermore, epidemiological data point to a 50%–60% reduction in biliary tract cancer incidence among long‐term users, with a clear dose–response relationship, although survival benefits remain uncertain [118]. Collectively, these findings position metformin as a multifaceted agent in liver disease, with implications extending from fibrosis attenuation to malignancy prevention.

Gallbladder Diseases

Metformin has been shown to reduce the risk of gallstone formation in individuals with polycystic ovary syndrome [119]. Supporting this observation, experimental studies in murine models of high‐fat diet–induced gallstones demonstrated a significant decrease in gallstone development following metformin treatment. However, these studies also reported an adverse effect, in which metformin induced mucosal calcification of the gallbladder, resulting in the formation of a porcelain gallbladder and associated complications [120].

Pancreatic Diseases

For pancreatic cancer patients with type 2 diabetes, metformin represents a therapeutic option to mitigate complications and mortality [121]. A meta‐analysis examining pancreatic cancer demonstrated that metformin exerts a beneficial effect on tumor stage and is associated with prolonged survival in patients with resected or locally advanced disease. Statistical analysis revealed a significant survival advantage in the metformin‐treated group compared to controls (HR = 0.86, 95% CI 0.76–0.97; p < 0.05) [122]. In patients who received metformin after surgery, survival was notably longer compared with those who did not. Transcriptomic profiling further showed that metformin suppressed genes linked to pro‐tumoral immune responses, especially those involved in monocyte polarization and M2 macrophage activation [123]. These findings highlight the potential of metformin as an adjunctive therapy in pancreatic disease.

Combination Therapy

Concomitant drugs play a crucial role in increasing the effectiveness of chemotherapeutic agents while minimizing side effects. The antineoplastic properties of metformin in combination with chemotherapeutic agents have shown potential for a synergistic response, allowing lower dosing of the latter and mitigating the side effects often associated with chemotherapy [124]. The combination of metformin with various drugs such as 5‐FU, dipeptidyl peptidase IV inhibitors, vitamin D3, aspirin, celecoxib, oxaliplatin, silibinin, cisplatin, arsenic trioxide, sorafenib, and rapamycin has shown promising results in the treatment of CRC, GC, and pancreatic cancer. These combinations have shown enhanced anticancer activity, including inhibition of tumor cell proliferation, suppression of metastasis, induction of apoptosis, and modulation of key signaling pathways involved in cancer growth and progression [13, 127, 130, 133, 134, 137, 138].

Clinical Challenges and Future Perspective

The translation of metformin's promising preclinical effects to clinical practice faces significant dosage‐related obstacles. The concentrations of metformin used in laboratory studies (5–20 mM) are 2000–10 000 times higher than those achieved with standard clinical dosing of 1000–2000 mg per day, which results in plasma concentrations of only 10–100 μM [141]. This substantial concentration gap may explain why clinical results are often less dramatic than preclinical studies suggest [141]. In addition, the critical, under‐investigated challenge in metformin therapy is its potential to trigger fatal lactic acidosis in T2DM patients with cardiovascular comorbidities, threatening to undermine its extensive pleiotropic benefits [142]. We critically examine the key clinical limitations associated with metformin administration, focusing on:

Lactic Acidosis Risk

One of the most serious yet rare complications of metformin therapy is lactic acidosis, a life‐threatening condition characterized by elevated lactate levels (> 5 mmol/L) and an increased anion gap metabolic acidosis. The incidence of metformin‐associated lactic acidosis (MALA) is estimated to be extremely low, approximately 3–10 cases per 100 000 patient‐years, and predominantly occurs in individuals with renal impairment, hepatic dysfunction, or conditions predisposing to hypoxia, such as sepsis and heart failure [143]. The primary mechanism involves mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to impaired lactate clearance and excessive lactate production [18]. Current clinical guidelines recommend discontinuation of metformin in patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 to minimize this risk [17].

Gastrointestinal Intolerance

Gastrointestinal side effects are the most frequently reported adverse reactions associated with metformin use, affecting up to 20%–30% of patients. These include nausea, diarrhea, bloating, and abdominal discomfort, primarily due to metformin's effect on intestinal glucose transporters and gut microbiota composition [144].

Vitamin B12 Deficiency

Long‐term metformin use has been associated with reduced serum vitamin B12 levels, with prevalence rates ranging from 10% to 30% among chronic users. The proposed mechanism involves interference with vitamin B12 absorption at the ileal level, likely due to alterations in calcium‐dependent membrane transport. This can lead to hematologic and neurologic complications, including anemia, peripheral neuropathy, and cognitive decline. Routine monitoring of vitamin B12 levels is recommended in patients receiving prolonged metformin therapy [145].

Patient‐specific considerations are essential for optimal therapeutic outcomes. The anticancer efficacy of metformin may differ between diabetic and nondiabetic patients, with questions remaining about whether anticancer effects can be demonstrated in patients who have never used glucose‐lowering drugs. Diabetic patients with longer metformin treatment cycles may experience different effects based on treatment duration and timing [26].

Future research directions emphasize the need for rigorous clinical trials to identify patients who may benefit from metformin treatment [146]. Studies should consider diabetic status, prognostic biomarkers, disease stage, and treatment regimens, as survival outcomes are influenced by factors including cancer type, differentiation, and staging [146]. Long‐term, randomized prospective studies are needed to confirm metformin's potential benefits, particularly given conflicting results from database analyses across different countries [147, 148]. Rigorous validation remains essential for clinical translation particularly as rigorous clinical studies are key to validating the safety and efficacy of combination therapies [147].

Conclusion

Metformin, long established as a cornerstone in T2D management, has emerged as a promising therapeutic candidate for a broad spectrum of gastrointestinal (GI) diseases. Its pleiotropic effects, encompassing anti‐inflammatory, antiproliferative, microbiota‐modulating, and metabolic regulatory actions, highlight its potential utility in conditions such as H. pylori infection, IBS, IBD, CRC, GC, and HCC. By modulating key pathways including AMPK/mTOR, NF‐κB, and gut barrier integrity, metformin offers benefits that extend well beyond glycemic control, positioning it as an attractive candidate for drug repurposing in gastroenterology. Nevertheless, important limitations must be acknowledged. The translational gap between preclinical studies, often employing supraphysiological metformin concentrations, and clinical settings, where only lower doses are feasible, remains a critical barrier. Variability in study design, patient demographics, and confounding factors, such as H. pylori status and diabetes duration, further complicates interpretation of current evidence. Safety considerations, including gastrointestinal intolerance and the risk of lactic acidosis in patients with renal impairment, underscore the importance of careful patient selection and monitoring. Future research should prioritize rigorously designed randomized controlled trials, particularly in nondiabetic populations, to establish the therapeutic efficacy of metformin and define optimal dosing regimens. Exploration of synergistic combinations with established therapies, including chemotherapeutics and immunomodulators, and the identification of predictive biomarkers will be essential to maximize clinical benefit. Advances in drug delivery approaches, such as extended‐release formulations or targeted delivery systems, may help mitigate adverse effects while enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Accordingly, metformin represents a versatile and mechanistically multifaceted agent with significant potential in GI disease management. While challenges remain, its repurposing warrants continued, high‐quality clinical investigation to fully delineate its therapeutic value in gastroenterology.
