Genome-wide identification and expression pattern analysis of the CPP transcription factor family in Medicago sativa L
Authors: Xinyue Ma, Xianyang Li, Li Zhao, Yuqi Zhang, Hao Liu, Fei He, Mingna Li, Xue Wang, Ruicai Long, Junmei Kang, Qingchuan Yang, Changhong Guo, Lin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Cysteine-rich Polycomb-like Protein (CPP) gene family is a small transcription factor family commonly found in animals and plants. It plays a crucial regulatory role in their reproductive development. To date, this gene family has been characterized in various species, such as Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago truncatula, Oryza sativa, and Glycine max. However, no research has been done on the CPP gene family in Medicago sativa L.ResultsThis study used bioinformatics to identify 31 CPP gene family members in Medicago sativa L., spread across 20 of its 32 chromosomes and named MsCPP1-MsCPP31 in chromosomal order. Phylogenetic analysis classified the 31 MsCPP proteins into five subgroups (Group I-V), each with similar conserved motifs and gene structures. Promoter cis-acting element analysis found that over two-thirds of these genes have MBS (drought-inducibility) responsive elements, indicating the MsCPP gene family may mainly respond to drought stress. Transcriptomic data analysis showed two genes have tissue-specific expression and six are expressed in all tested tissues. Moreover, transcriptomic profiling revealed that 18, 18, and 21 family members respond to drought, cold, and salt stresses, respectively, highlighting the significant role of MsCPP genes in abiotic stress responses. RT-qPCR validation confirmed the transcriptional responses of selected MsCPP genes to drought and salt stress. Our characterization of the MsCPP gene family enhances our understanding of Medicago sativa L.‘s molecular mechanisms for environmental adaptation and growth regulation.ConclusionThis study performed a comprehensive analysis of the CPP gene family in Medicago sativa L., covering gene structures, physicochemical characteristics, cis-acting elements, and expression patterns. The findings demonstrate that the MsCPP gene family is essential for the growth, development, hormone signaling, and stress response of Medicago sativa L.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07462-1.

Background

Transcriptional regulation of gene expression involves transcription factors recognizing promoter elements. Many transcription factors have been identified in plants in recent years [1]. The Cysteine-rich Polycomb-like Protein (CPP) gene family, a group of transcription factors present in both animals and plants but absent in lower eukaryotes like yeast, is of particular interest [2]. CPP transcription factors, also called Tesmin/TSO1 transcription factors, feature one or two cysteine-rich (CXC) domains and a short arginine-rich linker (R) connecting two CXC domains [3–5]. The CXC domain, also known as CRC, TCR, or CHC, is highly conserved and enables CPP transcription factors to bind DNA [2, 6–9]. Research has shown that CPP transcription factors can bind diverse DNA sequences and perform distinct functions [10].

The CPP gene family is essential for regulating plant growth and development across various species. In Arabidopsis, the TSO1 gene (AtCPP5) shows high expression in flowers, developing ovules, and microspores. Two classes of TSO1 mutants exist: Class I mutants, with missense mutations in the CXC domain, show floral organ defects, sterility, and meristem disorganization. Class II mutants (nonsense mutations/T-DNA insertions) retain normal floral morphology but have much lower fertility [11]. In soybean (Glycine max), GmCPP1 may regulate root development by modulating the leghemoglobin gene Gmlbc3 [12]. Similarly, under inositol stress conditions, overexpression of the wheat TaCPP5-1D gene results in higher germination rates and enhanced root and shoot activity compared to wild-type plants [3]. These findings collectively demonstrate that CPP genes are integral to the regulation of plant growth and development.

The CPP gene family exhibits multifaceted regulatory roles in plant stress responses. Current investigations employ diverse methodologies to elucidate their functions, some studies focus on characterizing individual CPP members, while others analyze expression patterns of the entire gene family under stress conditions. Functional studies of specific CPP genes include the following findings, In Solanum tuberosum (potato), StCPP3 interacts with type III secretion protein HrpB7 and negatively regulates plant resistance against Ralstonia solanacearum [13]. In Oryza sativa, OsCPP5 confers salt tolerance by upregulating the sodium transporter OsNHX1 in stress-adapted cultivars [14]. Transcriptomic analyses have revealed stress-responsive expression patterns across the entire gene family. For example, in maize (Zea mays), 73% of ZmCPP genes are upregulated under heat stress (42 °C for 12 h) [15]. In tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), 83% of SlCPP genes are rapidly activated (peaking at 6 h) under drought stress; following inoculation with the pathogen Cladosporium fulvum, SlCPP expression generally increases; whereas infection by Septoria lycopersici results in decreased expression of all SlCPP genes except SlCPP1 [16]. These findings collectively demonstrate that CPP genes play critical roles in mediating responses to both abiotic and biotic stresses.

Medicago sativa L. (alfalfa) is the most important forage legume crop globally. It ranks as the fourth most widely cultivated crop in the United States, following maize, wheat, and soybean [17]. Over the past century, intensive breeding efforts have focused on improving key agronomic traits in alfalfa, including tolerance to diverse stressors [18]. Recent advancements in multi-omics approaches—encompassing genomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analyses—have significantly enhanced our understanding of abiotic stress responses in M. sativa [19–28]. To date, CPP transcription factors have been identified in multiple plant species, including Arabidopsis thaliana (8 members) [29], Solanum lycopersicum (6 members) [16], Glycine max (20 members) [5], Zea mays (13 members) [15], Oryza sativa (11 members) [29], Cucumis sativus (5 members) [30], Medicago truncatula (9 members) [31]. However, no systematic characterization of CPP genes has been reported in M. sativa. This study identified 31 CPP gene family members in M. sativa and analyzed their gene structures, physicochemical properties, cis-acting elements, and expression patterns. These findings provide a foundational framework for further functional studies of the CPP gene family and its role in mediating abiotic stress responses in alfalfa.

Materials and methods

Identifying and chromosomal distribution of MsCPP gene family members

The genome data of alfalfa (Xinjiangdaye) were obtained from the Alfalfa Genome Project (https://fgshare.com/projects/whole_genome_sequencing_and_assembly_of_medicago_sativa/66380) [32]. The genome data of Arabidopsis thaliana and Medicago truncatula were retrieved from the Ensembl Plants database(https://plants.ensembl.org/index.html). To identify the CPP gene family members in alfalfa, the protein sequences of the CPP gene family from Arabidopsis thaliana were used as query sequences. A local BLAST program was employed to search for candidate CPP sequences in the protein sequences of Xinjiangdaye. The TCR domain profiles from the Pfam database (PF03638) were used to further eliminate redundancies [33]. Gene locus classification was determined by chromosomal physical position. Sequence variants with >98–99% nucleotide identity located at identical or adjacent genomic positions (≤ 10 kb apart) were classified as alleles of the same locus and assigned a shared gene ID (Locus ID). Conversely, sequences with significant similarity positioned on different chromosomes or >10 kb apart on the same chromosome were designated as distinct paralogous loci and received unique gene IDs [34]. The TBtools software was used to draw the chromosomal localization map of the MsCPP genes.

Physicochemical characterization and subcellular localization of the MsCPP gene family

The sequence length, molecular weights (MWs), and theoretical isoelectric points (pIs) of the MsCPP proteins were analyzed using the protein parameter function of TBtools [35]. Subcellular localization of MsCPP proteins was predicted using four dedicated tools: WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/), Cell-PLoc 2.0 (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/Cell-PLoc-2/), CELLO 2.5 (http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw/), and DeepLoc 2.1 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/DeepLoc-2.1/). Final localization assignments were determined by comprehensive consideration of predictions from all four tools.

Phylogenetic analysis, gene structure, conserved motifs and analysis of cis-acting elements of the MsCPP gene family

The CPP protein sequence of soybean was obtained from an online site (https://www.soybase.org/collections/). Phylogenetic trees were constructed using MEGA 11 software by the maximum likelihood method (ML), which was embellished using the Evolview online website (http://www.evolgenius.info/evolview/#/). Conserved structural domains of alfalfa were analyzed using MEME (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme), with the maximum number of motifs set to 10. Gene structure information was obtained from the alfalfa Gff annotation file. The Gene Structure View function of TBtools was used to visualize the above information. The promoter region (2000 bp upstream of the translation initiation codon) of the MsCPP genes was extracted and analyzed for promoter cis-acting elements using the PlantCARE online website (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/).

Gene duplication events and collinearity analysis of MsCPP the gene family

MsCPP genes duplication events [36] were analyzed using MCScanX software [37]. CPP gene collinearity information between Medicago sativa and Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago sativa and Medicago truncatula, and Medicago sativa and Glycine max were analyzed using MCScanX software. Finally, the results of the collinearity information were visualized using TBtools.

Acquisition and analysis of RNA-seq data

Transcriptome data for six tissues (nodules, roots, elongated stems, pre-elongated stems, leaves, flowers) of alfalfa were obtained from the NCBI public database (SRP055547). Transcriptome data under drought, cold and salt stress were obtained from NCBI public databases (SRR7091780 ~ SRR7091794 and SRR7160313 ~ SRR7160357) [38, 39]. Excel 2021 was used to collate transcriptomic data. The collected data was imported into TBtools to create the heatmaps.

RT-qPCR analysis of the MsCPP gene in response to drought and salt stress

Plant materials were derived from Zhongmu No.1 alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) provided by the Institute of Animal Science, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Two-week-old seedlings were subjected to abiotic stress treatments: salt stress (250mM NaCl) and osmotic stress (400mM mannitol, simulating drought). Root tip samples were collected at designated intervals post-treatment (salt:0,0.5,1,3,6,12,24 h; drought: 1,3,6,12,24 h). Untreated seedlings served as controls. Three biological replicates, each consisting of five seedlings, were analyzed per treatment.

Total RNA was isolated from leaf and root tip tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with the EasyScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TransGen Biotech, China). Gene-specific primers for MsCPP family members were designed using Primer 5.0 software. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) assays were conducted on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) with SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara, Japan). Each reaction included three technical replicates. The data were normalized relative to alfalfa actin gene expression levels. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCT method [40], using Actin 2 as the internal reference.

Results

Identifying and chromosomal distribution of MsCPP gene family members

In this study, we identified 31 MsCPP genes in the Xinjiangdaye alfalfa genome, which were labeled as MsCPP1 to MsCPP31(Table S1). These designations reflect their sequential arrangement and chromosomal positions. Distribution analysis revealed that among the 31 MsCPP genes, 29 are unevenly distributed across the 20 chromosomes, while MsCPP30 and MsCPP31 remain unassigned to any specific chromosomes(as shown in Fig. 1). The majority of the genes were concentrated on certain chromosomes, chr1.2, chr1.3, and chr1.4 each carrying three MsCPP genes, representing the highest number per chromosome.

Physicochemical characterization and subcellular localization of the MsCPP gene family

The MsCPP gene family is composed of relatively small proteins, with lengths ranging from 405 amino acids (MsCPP10) to 872 amino acids (MsCPP6). These proteins exhibit molecular weights ranging from 45.75 kDa to 95.45 kDa, reflecting the variation in their sizes. Notably, among the 31 MsCPP proteins in total, 14 are acidic and 17 are basic, demonstrating an approximately equal distribution between these two categories. The subcellular localization of MsCPP protein was predicted using four computational tools, including WoLF PSORT, Cell-PLoc 2.0, CELO 2.5 and DeepLoc 2.1 (Table S2). The combined results from all tools indicated that MsCPPs localize to the nucleus. The final prediction results are presented in Table 1.

Phylogenetic analysis of the MsCPP gene family

To investigate the evolutionary relationships within the CPP gene families, we constructed a phylogenetic tree using CPP protein sequences from alfalfa and three additional species: Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago truncatula, and Glycine max. These species possess 8, 9, and 20 CPP gene family members, respectively. The phylogenetic analysis grouped the 74 CPP protein sequences from these four species into five distinct subfamilies (I, II, III, IV, and V).

As illustrated in Figure. 2, the MsCPP proteins from alfalfa exhibited a close clustering pattern with those from Medicago truncatula (MtCPP), indicating a strong evolutionary relationship between the CPP gene families of these two legume species. In contrast, the CPP gene family of Arabidopsis thaliana, a member of the Brassicaceae family, showed a more distant relationship to the alfalfa CPP proteins. This observation aligns with the broader principles of plant evolution.

Analysis of the gene structure and conserved domains of MsCPP gene family members

To investigate the functions of the MsCPP gene family, we analyzed their gene structures and conserved motifs. The findings reveal that each subfamily exhibits similar gene structures and conserved motifs. Specifically, groups I and II each contain 7 introns, group III has 8–10 introns, and group V has 10 introns. In contrast, group IV shows significant variation, with intron numbers ranging from 3 to 12. Conserved motif analysis identified Motif 1, 9, and 10 as common elements in all MsCPP protein sequences. These motifs likely encompass the conserved TCR domain and can serve as a standard for identifying MsCPP family genes (Figs. 3 and 4).

Analysis of the cis-acting elements of the promoter in members of the MsCPP gene family

Through analysis of promoter regions in the MsCPP gene family, 10 cis-acting elements linked to growth and development, stress, and hormonal responses were selected. As shown in Fig. 5, it was noted that the MBS element (drought inducibility) was present in over two-thirds of genes, indicating that the MsCPP gene family primarily responds to drought stress. Notably, LTR elements associated with low-temperature responsiveness were only identified in MsCPP22 and MsCPP29, highlighting restricted cold stress tolerance mechanisms in this family.

Meanwhile, we have found that the promoter regions of some genes contain a relatively large number of specific elements. For example, the promoter region of MsCPP8 contains seven ARE elements, which are involved in anaerobic induction. The promoter region of MsCPP28 has six ABRE elements, which are associated with abscisic acid responsiveness. The promoter region of MsCPP23 includes five CGTCA-motifs and TGACG-motifs, which are related to MeJA responsiveness. These findings suggest a possible correlation: genes with more specific cis-acting elements in their promoter regions are more likely to be induced by the corresponding hormones or stresses.

Gene duplication events and collinearity analysis of the MsCPP gene family

By analyzing gene duplication in the MsCPP gene family, we identified 58 segmental duplication events involving 27 MsCPP genes (Fig. 6). For instance, MsCPP1 and MsCPP3 are located on chr1.1 and chr1.2, respectively (Table S3). The fact that no tandem duplications were found but segmental duplications were observed suggests that segmental duplication has played the leading role in the expansion of the MsCPP gene family.

To explore the evolutionary events of the CPP gene family across different crops, collinearity maps were constructed among Medicago sativa, Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago truncatula, and Glycine max. Comparative analysis revealed 23, 41, and 70 pairs of homologous genes between Medicago sativa and Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago truncatula, and Glycine max respectively. Additionally, 15, 24, and 22 collinear genes were identified between Medicago sativa and each of these species respectively (Fig. 7, Table S4/S5/S6). These findings confirm that the MsCPP gene family in Medicago sativa is more closely related to the CPP family in the leguminous plant Medicago truncatula than to that in the Brassicaceae plant Arabidopsis thaliana.

Analysis of the expression in different tissues of the MsCPP gene family members

By analyzing publicly available gene expression data for Medicago sativa, we investigated the expression patterns of MsCPP genes across six tissues (flower, leaf, pre-elongated stem, elongated stem, root, and nodule), revealing their tissue-specific characteristics (Table S7). As shown in Figure. 8, in tetraploid Medicago sativa, the majority of CPP genes exhibit predominant expression in the elongated stem, followed by root and nodule. Further analysis demonstrated that MsCPP14, MsCPP16, MsCPP17, MsCPP18, MsCPP22, and MsCPP26 are expressed in all six examined tissues, whereas MsCPP23 and MsCPP24 display expression restricted to root and elongated stem. Additionally, MsCPP6 shows the highest expression in flower. MsCPP1, MsCPP17, and MsCPP18 peak in root. MsCPP26 is most highly expressed in the pre-elongated stem. MsCPP22 reaches maximal expression in the elongated stem. These results indicate pronounced divergence in the expression patterns of MsCPP gene family members across plant tissues, likely linked to their tissue-specific functional roles.

Notably, under drought and salt stress treatments, the expression level of MsCPP1 was consistently higher than the control group. Under cold stress treatment, the expression level of MsCPP29 was consistently lower than the control group. RT-qPCR analysis revealed distinct stress response profiles, MsCPP1 showed significant upregulation under drought stress (peaking at 3 h with 4.12-fold increase) and salt stress (peak at 0.5 h, 4.33-fold). MsCPP16 exhibited late salt-specific induction (2.85-fold at 24 h), while MsCPP29 responded to both stressors—peaking at 3 h under drought (3.25-fold) and salt stress (3.15-fold at 3 h). Discussion

Distribution analysis shows that 29 of the 31 MsCPP genes are unevenly spread across 20 chromosomes, but MsCPP30 and MsCPP31 are still not anchored to any chromosome. Due to the absence of relevant annotation for the localization of the two genes in the Xinjiangdaye genome, we therefore inferred their physical locations. Primarily two possibilities exist: the incompleteness of the genome assembly and the biological characteristics of the genes themselves. On the one hand, they may reside on scaffolds that have not yet been anchored to chromosomal positions (e.g., telomeric/centromeric regions), or be difficult to locate due to highly repetitive sequences, complex structural variations, or insufficient sequencing depth. Alternatively, their sequences might span large assembly gaps (N-gaps), resulting in gene models that cross unconnected contigs, preventing precise localization. On the other hand, these genes may be situated within regions enriched with repetitive elements (such as transposons or tandem repeats), causing sequence alignments to yield multiple positional matches and thus preventing unique mapping. Furthermore, if located in dynamic chromosomal regions (e.g., rearrangement hotspots or near mobile elements), their positions may be inconsistent across different individuals or assembly versions [41]. However, we favor the incompleteness of the genome assembly as the primary cause. First, conserved domain analysis detected no transposase or reverse transcriptase domains, reducing the likelihood of their residence in repeat-enriched regions. Second, sequence analysis of both genes using the SSRIT tool (http://archive.gramene.org/db/markers/ssrtool) and RepeatMasker Web Server (http://www.repeatmasker.org/cgi-bin/WEBRepeatMasker) revealed that neither contains long repeat sequences or simple sequence repeats (SSRs) >50 bp. What is the impact of the missing localization of MsCPP30 and MsCPP31 on functional annotation? Due to the lack of localization information, we cannot obtain the precise physical positions of MsCPP30 and MsCPP31 on the genome or information about their flanking genes. This limits our analysis of their potential local gene cluster environments or adjacent regulatory elements. Such information holds potential value for gaining deeper insights into their transcriptional regulation and functional associations [42].What is the impact of the missing localization of MsCPP30 and MsCPP31 on evolutionary interpretation? First, the most significant impact lies in evolutionary analysis, specifically synteny analysis. The inability to map MsCPP30 and MsCPP31 to chromosomes prevents determination of syntenic relationships with other localized MsCPP genes. Furthermore, reliable syntenic block comparisons incorporating these genes cannot be performed between Medicago sativa (alfalfa) and its close relatives (e.g., Medicago truncatula or Glycine max). This limitation restricts our ability to comprehensively reconstruct the expansion history of this gene family within alfalfas. Second, while the phylogenetic positions of MsCPP30 and MsCPP31 provide sequence-based evolutionary insights, the absence of chromosomal localization precludes assessment of whether they belong to known tandem repeat clusters or represent products of segmental duplication events. This complicates accurate inference of their recent expansion mechanisms [43].

Our subcellular localization prediction shows that all MsCPP proteins are localized in the nucleus, a result consistent with localization predictions for CPP proteins in Solanum lycopersicum (tomato) [16], Phyllostachys edulis (mosaic bamboo), and Cucumis sativus (cucumber) [30, 44]. Furthermore, over 80% of CPP proteins in Zea mays (maize), Medicago truncatula, and Camellia sinensis (tea plant) were also predicted to localize to the nucleus [15, 31]. The subcellular localizations of CPP proteins discussed above are derived from predictive analyses. Given the inherent limitations of computational prediction, these results should be considered preliminary. Their definitive localization must be confirmed through subsequent experiments, such as GFP fusion assays.Notably, nuclear localization has been experimentally confirmed for CPP proteins such as TSO1 (AtCPP5) in Arabidopsis thaliana [7], as well as GmCPP1 and StCPP3 [12, 13]. Based on these shared characteristics across species, we propose that nuclear localization may represent a fundamental property of most CPP proteins.

We performed a phylogenetic analysis of CPP proteins from Medicago sativa, Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago truncatula, and Glycine max, classifying these sequences into five subgroups (I–V). Among these, several genes in Group III have been functionally validated to play critical roles in plant growth and development. For example, TSO1 and MYB3R1 form a regulatory module to coordinate cell proliferation with differentiation in shoot and root [45]. Additionally, AtTSO1 mediates floral development by controlling mitotic progression [12]. In soybean, GmCPP1, which binds to the Gmlbc3 promoter, and is involved in the regulation of leghemoglobin genes in symbiotic root nodules [7]. Proteins within the same subgroup typically exhibit conserved functional roles. Based on this functional coherence, we hypothesize that MsCPP20–24 genes in Group III may similarly participate in plant growth and developmental processes. However, the precise biological mechanisms of MsCPP20-24 require further experimental investigation.

Gene structure determines function, and thus structural differences may imply functional divergence. Within the MsCPP gene family, 28 out of 31 members possess dual TCR domains, while the remaining three members contain only a single TCR domain, all clustered in Group IV(Fig. 3, Table S10). We hypothesize that single-TCR-domain genes may lack certain functional capabilities compared to their dual-TCR-domain counterparts. Eukaryotic gene structure evolution involves intron gain and loss, with intron loss occurring more frequently [46]. Gene structure analysis revealed that Group IV exhibits the greatest intron number variation (ranging from 3 to 12), suggesting that some genes in this group may have undergone intron loss during evolution. Furthermore, all MsCPP genes share conserved Motif 1, Motif 9, and Motif 10, indicating these motifs are essential for maintaining the core functionality of the MsCPP family.

This study uncovered potential environmental response mechanisms (hormone and stress-responsive elements) through cis-acting element analysis of CPP gene promoters. However, the DNA-binding specificity of CPP transcription factors remains unresolved due to the absence of systematic motif data for this specific family in major public databases including PlantTFDB (http://planttfdb.gao-lab.org), JASPAR Plants (https://jaspar.genereg.net/category/plants/), and published DAP-seq datasets, precluding bioinformatic prediction of their downstream targets. To address this limitation, future work should first establish genome-wide DNA-binding profiles via DAP-seq or ChIP-seq to identify conserved motifs, followed by experimental validation of high-confidence targets using EMSA or dual-luciferase reporter assays, ultimately integrating binding data with phenomics to construct a complete regulatory framework from CPP TFs to downstream pathways. Such approaches will elucidate the molecular mechanisms governing CPP functions in plant development and environmental adaptation.

This study identified 31 MsCPP genes in the autotetraploid Medicago sativa (Xinjiangdaye). As a closely related diploid control, 9 MtCPP genes were identified in the Medicago truncatula genome. The significant expansion of MsCPP genes in Medicago sativa (31 vs. 9) is primarily attributed to its autotetraploid genome, which theoretically harbors gene copies derived from two ancestral subgenomes. However, the observed number of genes (31) substantially exceeds the expected count from simple tetraploidization (approximately 18, i.e., 9 × 2 = 18). This indicates that additional segmental duplication events occurred within the CPP gene family following tetraploidization, driving the expansion of this gene family.

Abscisic acid (ABA), a plant hormone, plays a critical role in drought response. Drought-responsive genes can be classified into two types based on ABA dependency: ABA-dependent genes and ABA-independent genes [47]. ABA-responsive genes typically contain a conserved cis-acting element, ABRE (PyACGTGG/TC), in their promoter regions, which is essential for ABA-mediated transcriptional regulation [48]. In the MsCPP gene family, cis-element analysis showed that 32.3% (10/31) of members (MsCPP1-3/6/8–9/17/23/25/28) harbor both ABREs and MBS elements, suggesting these genes may function as ABA-dependent drought-responsive genes. Conversely, 38.7% (12/31) of members (MsCPP11-15/22/24/26–27/29–31) retain only MBS elements, implying their potential role as ABA-independent drought-responsive genes. Experimental validation under ABA treatment and drought conditions is required to confirm these hypotheses.

Previous studies have clearly demonstrated that the CPP transcription factor family in multiple species responds to both biotic and abiotic stresses. For example, SlCPP6 exhibited a significant 4.37-fold upregulation compared to controls after 6 h of drought treatment [16], while TaCPP10-3 A showed an approximately 11-fold increase in expression 48 h post-infection with Fusarium graminearum [49]. In this study, transcriptomic data analysis and RT-qPCR experiments revealed substantial differential expression of the MsCPP gene family under abiotic stresses such as drought and salt. Specifically, the relative expression of MsCPP1 increased nearly fourfold relative to controls after 4 h of drought exposure, whereas MsCPP16 expression decreased to half of control levels under the same conditions. These data confirm that the MsCPP gene family responds to abiotic stresses. However, whether MsCPP genes are modulated by biotic stresses remains unresolved and requires further investigation.

In natural environments, plants face diverse stresses, including abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, and temperature extremes, as well as biotic stresses like pathogens and pests, all of which significantly impact their growth and development throughout their life cycle. While most current research independently investigates molecular mechanisms underlying tolerance to individual stress types, emerging evidence reveals signaling crosstalk between plant responses to biotic and abiotic stresses [50]. This raises the critical question of whether the CPP transcription factor family participates in mediating such cross-stress signaling interactions. To date, no studies have investigated this potential involvement. Therefore, clarifying the function of the CPP transcription factor family in the signal interaction between biotic and abiotic stresses can serve as a research subject for future studies.

Conclusions

This study successfully identified 31 CPP genes in the Xinjiangdaye genome. Phylogenetic analysis classified the MsCPP genes into five distinct subgroups, with members within each subgroup exhibiting conserved gene structures and motif compositions. The distribution of MBS (drought-inducible response elements) in promoter regions provided cis-regulatory evidence supporting functional diversification among MsCPP genes. Collinearity analysis demonstrated that segmental duplication was the primary driver of MsCPP family expansion. Transcriptomic profiling further revealed spatiotemporal expression patterns of MsCPP genes. RT-qPCR experiments confirmed the accuracy of the transcriptomic data. These findings establish a foundational framework for functional genomics studies of MsCPP genes and highlight potential candidates for improving stress resistance in Medicago sativa via molecular breeding.
