Association of magnesium deficiency, tobacco smoke exposure and hypertension in children and adolescents: evidence from the NHANES 2007–2018
Authors: Yongjian Gong, Cheng Zhou, Yu Wan, Haibin Yin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent studies suggested that the combination of tobacco smoke exposure (TSE) and dietary nutrients intake may be related to a higher or lower risk of hypertension. However, the relationship between dietary magnesium (Mg) intake, TSE and the odds of hypertension remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the association of TSE, dietary Mg intake and the odds of hypertension among children and adolescents.MethodData of this study were extracted from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys (NHANES) 2007–2018. Dietary Mg intake was calculated as the average of two days of dietary and supplementations intake. Serum cotinine concertation ≥ 0.05 µg/L or at least one-person smoking in the household was considered as exposing to the tobacco smoke. The weighted univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were utilized to explore the associations of dietary Mg intake, TSE and the odds of hypertension among children and adolescents with the evaluation index of odds ratio (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Subgroup analyses based on different age, gender and overweight were further assessed these associations.ResultsA total of 7,122 children and adolescents aged 8–17 years old and with the blood pressure measurement were included. Of which, 948 (13.31%) had hypertension. After adjusting all covariates, we observed children and adolescents exposed to tobacco smoke was related to higher odds of hypertension (OR = 1.34, 95%CI: 1.01–1.78); children and adolescents not reached the dietary Mg references intakes was related to higher odds of hypertension (OR = 1.48, (95%CI: 1.11–1.97); compared to children and adolescents non-TSE and reached the DRIs of Mg, those not reached the DRIs of Mg and exposed to tobacco smoke have the highest odds of hypertension (OR = 1.94, 95%CI: 1.30–2.89, P for trend = 0.002). These associations of dietary Mg intake, TSE and hypertension remain robust after the stratified analysis based on age, gender and overweight was conducted.ConclusionOur findings suggested there were a robust association between dietary Mg intake, TSE and hypertension in children and adolescents. Those children and adolescents with deficiency dietary Mg intake and exposed to tobacco smoke may have the high odds of hypertension. More restrictions on smoking as well as Mg supplementation in the prevention and treatment of hypertension among children and adolescents might be justified.Keywords: Dietary magnesium, Tobacco smoke exposure, Hypertension, Children and adolescents, NHANES database

Background

Hypertension is a well-recognized risk factor for cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) featured by an elevated blood pressure (BP) [1]. Worldwide, more than 1 billion adults suffering from hypertension, and the prevalence is still increasing [2]. Parallel to this rising global burden, the prevalence of hypertension in children and adolescents is constantly escalating [3]. Previous pathophysiologic and epidemiologic evidence has suggested that childhood hypertension is associated with adulthood hypertension and detrimental CVDs events [4–6]. Even worse, childhood hypertension can cause irreversible end-organ and vascular damage like adult hypertension [7]. Given the increasing prevalence of childhood and its important impacts on hypertension in adulthood hypertension, identification of modifiable risk factors aiming to prevent the development of hypertension in children and adolescents is imperative.

Environmental factors are one of the important factors for the occurrence and development of hypertension and tobacco smoke exposure (TSE) is a vital aspect of environmental pollution [8]. In the U.S, up to 24 million nonsmoking children and adolescents are exposed to tobacco smoke [9]. Children are especially exposed to tobacco smoke, not only are they potentially exposed to tobacco smoke in their home and social environments, but also normal physiological characteristics of childhood lung development make them susceptible to health effects of tobacco smoke [10]. Several studies shown an association between TSE and the risk of elevated BP among children and adolescents [11–13]. Exposure to parental smoking during childhood is related to decreased flow-mediated dilatation even 20 years later in adulthood [14].

In addition to environmental factors, diet is also an important risk factor for the occurrence of hypertension. Moore et al. [15, 16] two studies suggested that low levels of certain nutrients including dietary fiber, docesahexaenoic acid, vitamin E, and vitamin C and TSE may have a joint effect on the metabolic syndrome among children and this joint effect was greater than would be expected from the effects of individual exposures alone. Magnesium (Mg) is one of the essential micronutrients, and Mg deficiency is an important risk factor for oxidative stress and inflammation in vivo, which affects cardiovascular health [17]. Guerrero-Romero et al. [18] found low serum Mg level (< 1.8 mg/dL) is significantly associated with prehypertension and hypertension in apparently healthy children. Less is known, however, the association between dietary Mg intake, TSE and hypertension among children and adolescents.

Herein, using the data of National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys (NAHNES) database, we evaluated the association of dietary Mg intake, TSE and the odds of hypertension among children and adolescents, in addition, subgroup analysis based on age, gender and overweight was further performed to evaluate whether these associations were robust.

Methods

Study design and participants

Data of this cross-sectional study were extracted from the NHANES 2007–2018. The NHANES data is a representative sample of the non-institutionalized population of the United States. It is a two-year-cycle program conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) of America using a multistage, probability sampling methods [19]. NHANES examines a nationally representation sample of approximately 5,000 people from 15 different counties each year and aims to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States [20]. NHANES is a publicly available database and was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Broad, and all participants provided written informed consent. A detailed description of this database has been published. The requirement of ethical approval for this was waived by the Institutional Review Board of The Affiliated Changzhou No. 2 People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, because the data was accessed from NHANES (a publicly available database). The need for written informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board of The Affiliated Changzhou No. 2 People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University due to retrospective nature of the study. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

The included criteria were: (1) children and adolescents aged 8–17 years old; (2) with measurement of systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP); (3) with complete dietary Mg intake data; (4) exposed to tobacco smoke environment. The excluded criteria were: 1) children and adolescents missing height and weight data.

Dietary Mg intake assessment

Data of dietary Mg intake were estimated by 24-h dietary recall interviews. Diet recall interviews require respondents to report all food and beverages consumed in the past 24-h, the quantity of food reported, and a detailed description of the food. Food consumption data were converted to the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) standard reference codes, and food intake was linked to the USDA’s Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS) [21]. According to whether reaching the dietary reference intakes (DRIs) of dietary Mg, present study grouped dietary Mg intake into two categories: reaching the DRIs and without reaching the DRIs. The DRIs of dietary Mg was 240 mg/d and 410 mg/d for boys aged 7–13 years and aged 14–18 years, respectively. The DRIs of dietary Mg was 240 mg/d and 360 mg/d for girls aged 7–13 years and aged 14–18 years, respectively.

TSE assessment

Serum cotinine was a reliable nicotine biomarker to objectively measure TSE [22]. NHANES analyzed serum samples for cotinine using an isotope-dilution high-performance liquid chromatography/atmospheric pressure chemical ionization tandem mass spectrometric (ID HPLC-APCI MS/MS) method [23]. In present study, children and adolescents were considered exposed to tobacco smoke if they had at least one smoker in their household or if they had serum cotinine concentration ≥ 0.05 µg/ml. Question on reported exposure to tobacco smoke in subject’s households were administered to either an adult proxy respondent for children aged 6–11 years or self-reported by adolescents aged 12–18 years. Households-related questions included: (1) whether the child lived with a household tobacco smoker; and (2) whether the child lived with a household tobacco smoker who smoked indoors. These two covariates were used to derive a household TSE variable with three levels: (1) did not live with a household smoker; (2) lived with ≥ 1 household smoker who did not smoke indoors; and (3) lived with ≥ 1 household smoker who smoked indoors [24].

Definition of hypertension

BP was measured via a mercury sphygmomanometer at the NHANES mobile examination centers by professional personnel. Studied children and adolescents were allowed to sit for 5 min, and then BP was measured three times at an interval of 30 s. If the three measurements were the same, the SBP/DBP reading was recorded, but of more than one SBP/DBP reading was obtained, the average reading was recorded. We defined hypertension according to the 2017 American Academy of Pediatrics’ clinical practice guidelines [25]. For children aged 8–12 years old, we used SBP/DBP percentile tables to define hypertension based on gender, height, and age: (1) SBP and/or DBP ≥ 95th percentile, (2) or SBP ≥ 130 mmHg, and/or DBP ≥ 80 mmHg. For adolescents aged 13–17 years, hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 130 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 80 mmHg. Moreover, individuals who have elevated BP or had already been diagnosed with hypertension or who were taking antihypertensive medicines were also considered as hypertension [26].

Potential covariates

The potential covariates considered in this study included demographic and life characteristics [age, gender, race, poverty-to-income ratio (PIR) and maternal smoking during pregnancy], physical examination [height, weight and body mass index (BMI)] and dietary intake information (Mg and energy). Children and adolescent’s obesity was defined as a BMI at or above the sex-specific 95th percentile on the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) BMI-for-age growth charts and overweight was defined as a BMI at 85th to 95th on the CDC BMI-for-age growth charts [27]. Physical activity was grouped in yes or no according to the questionnaires of NHANES database. For children aged 8–12 years old, days physically active at least 60 min was defined as “yes”, otherwise “no”. For adolescents aged 13–17 years old, vigorous physical activity lasting at least 10 min was considered “yes”, otherwise “no”.

Statistics analysis

All statistical analyzes were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Using the proc surveyfreq in SAS software, the final sample size was weighted with SDMVPSU, SDMVSTRA and WTDR2D. SDMVPSU means that the masked variance unit pseudo-stratum is sdmvstra, and the masked variance unit pseudo-primary sampling unit (PSU) is sdmvpsu. SDMVSTRA refers to the confidence interval (CI) being applied to assess the reliability of an estimate. WTDR2D is the MEC exam weight (wtmec2 year) used for day 1 and day 2 dietary data weighting. Continuous data were expressed as mean and standard error (S.E.), and the weighted t-test was used for comparison between groups.

Categorical data were described by case number and percentage [N (%)], and comparisons between groups used the weighted χ2 test. Multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) was used to missing data imputation. Sensitivity analysis was performed before and after missing data imputation (Table S1). The weighted univariable and multivariate logistic regression models were used to explore the association of Mg deficiency and TSE and hypertension in children and adolescents, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model 1 was a crude model without adjusting covariates. Model 2 was first adjusted demographic information (age, gender, race and PIR). Model 3 was the fully adjusted model accounted for these variables as well as household food security category, overweigh BMI, maternal smoked when pregnant, physical activity and total energy intake. The association between Mg deficiency and TSE and hypertension among children and adolescents was further explored in different subgroup populations based on age, gender and overweight BMI. Two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered as statistical significance.

Results

Characteristics of children and adolescents

The flow chart of population screening was shown in Fig. 1. Totally, 10,169 children and adolescents were screened. Among them, 2,105 subjects missing dietary Mg intake information, 907 subjects missing complete TSE information, 34 subjects missing height data and 1 subject missing weight data were excluded. Finally, 7,122 eligible children and adolescents were included, with the mean age of 12.65 ± 0.06 years old. Of which, 948 (13.31%) children and adolescents had hypertension. The proportion of children and adolescents exposed to tobacco smoke was higher in the hypertension group than in the non-hypertension group (46.86% vs. 39.49%). Difference was found between gender, race, overweight BMI and total energy intake between two groups (all P < 0.05). Characteristics of included children and adolescents were shown in Table 1.

Association between single dietary mg intake or TSE and hypertension

Three weighted logistic regression models were utilized to investigate the association between single Mg intake or TSE and the odds of hypertension, as presented in Table 2. After adjusted age, gender, race, PIR, household food security category, overweight BMI, maternal smoking during pregnant, physical activity and total energy intake, we observed children and adolescents exposed to tobacco smoke were associated with high odds of hypertension compared to those not exposed to tobacco smoke (OR = 1.34, 95%CI: 1.01–1.78). Similar result was observed between Mg intake and hypertension odds. After adjusted all above covariates in Model 3, compared to children and adolescents reaching the DRI of Mg, those not reaching DRI of Mg were associated with the high odds of hypertension (OR = 1.48, 95%CI: 1.11–1.97).

Association of dietary mg intake, TSE and the odds of hypertension

As depicted in Table 3, children and adolescents were divided into four levels to explore whether dietary Mg intake and TSE had the association on the odds of hypertension (level 1: Non-TSE & Reached the DRIs of dietary Mg; level 2: TSE & Reached the DRIs of dietary Mg; level 3: Non-TSE & Non-reached the DRIs of dietary Mg; level 4: TSE & Non-reached the DRIs of dietary Mg). Across all three models, we observed compared to children and adolescents at level 1, those children and adolescents at level 4 were associated with the highest odds of hypertension. In model 3, which accounted for all covariates, the findings suggested hypertension risk increased 1.94-fold in children and adolescents at level 4 (OR = 1.94, 95%CI: 1.30–2.89, P for trend = 0.002). This finding suggested that if children and adolescents who were exposed to tobacco smoke also with the deficiency dietary Mg intake, they may associate with the higher odds of hypertension.

Discussion

In present study, we evaluated the association of dietary Mg intake, TSE and the odds of hypertension among children and adolescents. After adjusted all potential covariates, our findings were as follows: (1) children and adolescents exposed to tobacco smoke was related to the higher odds of hypertension; (2) children and adolescents unreached the DRI of Mg was related to the higher odds of hypertension; (3) compared to children and adolescents non-TSE and reached the DRI of Mg, those with TSE and unreached to DRI of Mg may have highest odds of hypertension; (4) the association of dietary Mg intake, TSE and hypertension remained significant after stratified by age, gender and overweight BMI, especially among boys or aged 12–17 years old. These results added to the epidemiologic evidence linking TSE and dietary Mg intake with hypertension.

To the best of our knowledge, this was a first study to evaluate the association of dietary Mg intake, TSE and hypertension among children and adolescents. Concerning U.S. prevalence of TSE, approximately 1-in-3 children were exposed to tobacco smoke [9]. The World Health Organization (WHO) stated several truths about TSE, namely that all types of tobacco use were harmful and there was no safe level of tobacco exposure [28]. The unique physiological characteristics of children and adolescents determine their susceptibility to environmental poisons was higher than adults, and tobacco smoke to environment was more serious. While the remarkable pulmonary consequences of TSE were clinically apparent in childhood, the cardiovascular effects of TSE were secret but substantial [10]. Sufficient clinical evidences shown that TSE was related to worse cardiovascular events in children and adolescents [11, 12, 29]. A study of cardiovascular risk in young Finns aged 3, 6, 9, 12, 15 or 18 years old suggested TSE in children and young was detrimental to their cardiovascular health and that consequences attributable to TSE may persist into adult life [29]. Liu et al. [11] focused the children and adolescents aged 8–17 years old in NHANES 2007–2012 suggested TSE was significantly related to DBP and SBP, which was consistent with the works of Levy et al. [12]. Moreover, several studies speculated that TSE may be equally or even more harmful to cardiovascular health than direct cigarette use due to increasing concentrations of some chemicals [30–32]. There was also several evidence suggested TSE has adverse cardiovascular consequences as early as the first decade of life including when the fetus was exposed to maternal smoking or maternal TSE in utero and in children who have no other atherosclerosis promoting risk factors [33, 34].

Although the mechanisms underlying TSE and hypertension have not been fully elucidated, impairment of endothelial function, arterial stiffness, inflammation as well as excessive oxidative stress level were smoking-related major determinates of initiation, and acceleration of the atherothrombotic process, thereby leading to cardiovascular events [35]. Diet was an important modifiable factor in the prevention and treatment of hypertension of hypertension. It was also possible that the joint effect of poor diet and TSE on childhood hypertension may be more than would be expected based on the individual effects. Mg was the second most abundant intracellular cation in human body and plays a critical role in numerous biological reactions [36]. Dietary therapies known to lower BP including increased Mg intake. A review of Banjanin et al. [37] summarized serum Mg was negatively related to the risk of hypertension. An average dietary Mg intake was below the DRIs and Mg supplementation in the prevention and treatment of hypertension might be justified. A cross-sectional from Tan et al. [38] observed Magnesium depletion score (MDS), an indicator to characterize Mg deficiency in human body, was positively related to the risk of developing hypertension among U.S. adults. The beneficial effect of high dietary Mg intake on decreasing and maintaining BP was also observed in a latest work cross-sectional study by Han et al. [39]. One of the mechanisms by which Mg lowers BP was via a natural calcium channel blocker. Mg competes with sodium for binding sites on vascular smooth muscle cells, increases prostaglandin E, binds to potassium in a cooperative manner, incudes endothelial-dependent vasodilation, improves endothelial dysfunction in hypertensive patients and reduces BP [40]. Except for BP, Mg regulated intracellular calcium, sodium, and pH and left ventricular mass, insulin sensitivity, and arterial compliance [41]. Moreover, Mg also suppresses circulating Na + K + ATPase inhibitory activity that reduces vascular tone [42].

Herein, we provided reference for the prevention of hypertension among children and adolescents based on the joint effect of TSE and dietary Mg intake on the hypertension. A representative and high-quality NHANES database was utilized, and the regression models were adjusted considering the covariates. These findings add new theoretical support for the potential risk of hypertension and reinforce the view that TSE and low Mg level are risk factors for hypertension. And these could help facilitate planning and guiding targeted strategies, which may include stringent restrictions on smoking, as well as dietary Mg supplements to prevent and treatment hypertension in children and adolescents globally. However, some limitations need caution in interpreting our findings. First, the cross-section study design of present study could not establish a causal relationship between TSE, dietary Mg intake and the risk of hypertension among children and adolescents. Second, due to limitations of NHANES database, several confounding covariates that may influenced outcomes, including parental hypertension and obesity status, were not included in the analysis. Finally, the findings from the U.S. children and adolescents may not generalizable to other population. More large-scale studies are needed to explore the relationship of TSE, dietary Mg intake and hypertension among children and adolescents, including clinical trials.

Conclusion

In summary, TSE and low dietary Mg intake level were related to higher odds of hypertension and there exist joint effects of TSE and dietary Mg intake on the odds of hypertension in American children and adolescents. Further prospective studies and mechanism research to further explore the burden and impact of TSE and Mg intake on the risk of hypertension among children and adolescents.
