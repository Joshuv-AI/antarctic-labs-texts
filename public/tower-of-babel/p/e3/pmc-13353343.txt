Gut microbiota biomarkers of chronic kidney disease progression identified by 16S rDNA sequencing and machine learning
Authors: Yanyan Zhang, Mingli Zhang, Shan Liu, Xingrui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a global health concern characterized by high prevalence and mortality rates, yet its underlying pathogenesis remains inadequately understood. This study aimed to investigate microbial biomarkers associated with CKD progression across various stages by employing 16S rDNA sequencing, complemented by machine learning techniques including Lasso regression, the Boruta algorithm, and K-fold cross-validation, alongside microbial network analysis. Fecal samples were collected from a cohort consisting of six patients with stage II CKD, five with stage III CKD, seven with stage IV CKD, and nine healthy controls. Following quality control of the sequencing data, we analyzed microbial composition, richness, and diversity, revealing significant differences among the groups. Ten differential microbial taxa were identified, with Actinobacteriota and Bifidobacterium showing the highest relative abundances. Machine learning methods highlighted six microbial biomarkers, including Eubacterium eligens and Lactococcus, all exhibiting AUC values exceeding 0.7, indicating their potential in distinguishing CKD patients from healthy individuals. Furthermore, species driving force analysis uncovered 26, 27, and 39 microbial interaction relationships between stages II, III, IV CKD, and controls, respectively. In conclusion, our integrative analysis elucidates stage-specific gut microbial biomarkers and functional pathways that are implicated in CKD progression, thereby supporting the mechanistic role of the gut-kidney axis and underscoring the potential for microbiota-based interventions and early diagnostic approaches in CKDKeywords: Chronic kidney disease, gut microbiota, 16S rDNA sequencing, microbial biomarkers, machine learning, gut–kidney axis

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a major global health burden, affecting hundreds of millions of people and contributing to significant morbidity and mortality [1]. It is defined by international guidelines as a glomerular filtration rate below 60 mL·min−1·1.73 m−2, with or without markers of renal injury, persisting for at least 3 months [2]. CKD represents a global health burden characterized by the progressive deterioration of renal function, which may eventually lead to end-stage renal disease [3]. Studies have shown that the pathogenesis of CKD is complex and involves multiple pathological processes, such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis [4]. Current clinical management strategies are mainly pharmacological or surgical, focusing on glucose and blood pressure control, nutritional supplementation, peritoneal dialysis, and kidney transplantation [5]. However, an effective cure remains unavailable; therefore, the principal therapeutic objective is to delay disease progression. Recent advances in microbiome research have provided growing evidence for a significant association between gut microbiota and CKD [6]. The theory of the “gut–kidney axis” describes the interplay between gut microecology and CKD [7]. In other words, nutritional and pharmacological interventions targeting intestinal flora may modulate metabolic disorders such as CKD [8,9]. However, specific changes in gut microbiota composition and their functional mechanisms across different pathological stages of CKD remain unclear, which limits precise intervention and treatment [9].

Gut microbes are microbial communities residing in the intestines of humans and animals, encompassing bacteria, fungi, viruses, and archaea. These microorganisms dominate the gut microbial community and play a key role in maintaining gut function and overall host health [6]. 16S rDNA sequencing is a high-throughput method widely used to analyze microbial community composition. The methodology involves three key steps [1]: sample processing, in which genomic DNA is extracted from the samples to obtain the genetic information of the microorganisms [2]; PCR amplification, using specific primers such as 27 F/1492R to amplify the 16S rDNA gene, often targeting highly variable regions (e.g., V3–V4 regions) that differ significantly among strains and provide strain-specific information [3]; sequencing and analysis, in which sequence data are generated using Sanger or high-throughput sequencing technologies. High-throughput sequencing typically employs sequencing-by-synthesis, where fluorescent reversible terminators are used to read the base information one by one, producing the complete sequence of amplified DNA fragments [10]. These sequences were then compared with those in public databases such as NCBI or EzBioCloud to assign taxonomic identities. Currently, 16S rDNA sequencing is widely used to study alterations in gut microbiota associated with CKD and their correlations with related inflammatory markers [6]. It has also been employed to study the effects of in vitro culture on human gut microbial composition using 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing combined with metabolomics approaches [11]. Alterations in the gut microbiota might impair the intestinal barrier, which in turn aggravated intestinal damage severity [12]. Nevertheless, it remained unknown whether CKD progression was linked to the systemic remodeling of the gut microbiota network.

This study aimed to explore the pattern of gut microbiota network reconstruction in CKD patients across different pathological stages (stage II, stage III, and stage IV) compared with healthy controls, to identify microbial biomarkers associated with CKD progression, and to analyze their functional characteristics using 16S rDNA gene sequencing combined with bioinformatics and machine learning approaches. This study encompassed analyses of microbial diversity, identification of differential microbial taxa, functional enrichment profiling, and screening and validation of microbial biomarkers. By elucidating dynamic alterations in gut microbiota across CKD stages, this study seeks to provide novel microbial targets for early diagnosis and intervention, while offering a more robust scientific basis for the theory of “gut–kidney axis.”

Materials and methods

Specimen collection and grouping

This academic inquiry obtained approval from Henan Integrative Medicine Hospital 2021-Ethics-006 Ethics Committee. All the samples were collected from Henan Integrative Medicine Hospital. Fecal samples were obtained from six patients with stage II CKD, five patients with stage III CKD, seven patients with stage IV CKD, and nine healthy control participants. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before sample collection.

Key factors known to influence gut microbiota composition were considered. First, regarding medication, all participants were required to have no history of antibiotic, probiotic, or prebiotic use within the three months prior to sample collection. Second, concerning diet, although detailed dietary records were not obtained, all participants were instructed to maintain their habitual diet and avoid major dietary changes before sampling. Third, for CKD etiology, We acknowledge that detailed information on long-term diet and medication history was not available for all participants, which represents a limitation of this study.

Following sample collection, 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing was performed on all samples. Genomic DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the OMEGA Stool DNA Kit (OMEGA Bio-tek, Beijing, China). PCR was performed to add sequencing universal adapters and sample-specific Barcode sequences to the amplicons of the target region. The PCR product libraries were quantified and pooled using the Promega QuantiFluor Fluorometric Quantitation System (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI). Finally, paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) was conducted on the Illumina sequencing platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA) in accordance with the standard operating procedures.

16S rDNA sequencing and data preprocessing

Following sequencing, high-quality clean reads were generated from raw sequencing data of the four sample groups through read merging and filtering. Quality control was conducted using the DADA2 package (v 1.32.0) [13], QIIME2 (v 2019.4) [14], and the phyloseq package (v 1.42.0) [15] to identify the amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) for each microbial taxon. To examine the common microbial species among the four groups, a Venn diagram was constructed using the ggvenn package (v 0.1.9) (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=ggvenn). Sufficiency of the sequencing data was assessed by generating rarefaction curves for each sample group using the EasyAmplicon package (v 3.19) [16].

Microbial diversity and abundance analysis

Alpha diversity describes the richness, diversity, and evenness of species within a locally homogeneous habitat [17]. In this study, the Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices were calculated to assess the richness and diversity of microbiome sequencing data using the cal_alphadiv function from the microeco package (v 1.9.1) [18]. The Wilcoxon test was then applied to compare these indices among the stage II CKD, stage III CKD, stage IV CKD, and control groups (p < 0.05). Beta diversity reflects differences in species composition – or species turnover – between communities across environmental gradients and is also termed between-habitat diversity [17]. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS; stress < 0.2) were performed using the microeco package (v 1.9.1) to evaluate intergroup compositional differences. To characterize microbial community composition across CKD stages and controls, the relative abundances of each taxon within each sample were determined. Bar plots were generated using the ggplot2 package (v 3.5.1) [19] to visualize the top 10 most abundant taxa at the phylum and genus levels.

Identification of differential microorganisms

Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) is a statistical method that combines nonparametric tests with linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to assess feature effect sizes, enabling the identification of taxa with differential abundances among groups [20]. In this study, LEfSe was applied to identify microbial taxa exhibiting significant differences in abundance across stage II CKD, stage III CKD, stage IV CKD, and healthy control groups. Taxa with p < 0.05 and an LDA score of >2 were considered significant and designated as candidate differential microorganisms. To further explore the common differential microorganisms across CKD progression, the Mfuzz package (v 2.67.0) [21] was used to conduct a trend analysis on the candidate taxa identified by LEfSe. Taxa exhibiting consistent patterns of change – either continuously increasing or decreasing from healthy controls through stages II, III, and IV CKD – were selected as differential microorganisms.

Functional enrichment and correlation analysis of differential microorganisms

Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2 (PICRUSt2; v 2.5.1) [22] was used to predict functional profiles of microbial communities. In this study, PICRUSt2 was used to perform Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes functional predictions for the previously identified differential microorganisms, with significance defined as p < 0.05. Based on these predictions, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to identify functional pathways with significant differences between the healthy control group and the stage II CK group, as well as among all four groups (p < 0.05). To evaluate correlations among differential microorganisms at the genus level, correlation analysis was conducted using the psych package (v 2.2.9) (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=psych), with significant correlations defined as |correlation coefficient| > 0.3 and p < 0.05.

Screening and assessment of microbial biomarkers

Two machine learning approaches were employed to identify key microorganisms among the differential taxa. Samples from patients with stage II, stage III, and stage IV CKD were combined into a disease group, and healthy control samples were used as the control group. First, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) analysis, implemented through the glmnet package (v 4.1-4) [23], was applied to the differential microorganisms to identify characteristic taxa using 5-fold cross-validation, based on the minimum error rate and lambda.min values. Similarly, the Boruta algorithm, implemented via the Boruta package (v 8.0.0) [24], was used to calculate the importance of each differential microorganism. Boruta is a random forest–based feature selection method that iteratively compares the importance of original features with that of randomly permuted shadow features to identify truly relevant variables. Microorganisms were then ranked by importance, and the most influential taxa were selected. The intersection of the characteristic microorganisms identified by the two algorithms was determined using the ggvenn package (v 0.1.9) to define the final set of microbial biomarkers. To evaluate the discriminatory power of these microbial biomarkers between CKD and control groups, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated using the pROC package (v 1.18.0) [25], with an area under the curve (AUC) threshold of >0.7 indicating strong discriminatory ability. Finally, correlations among microbial biomarkers at the genus level were assessed using the psych package (v 2.2.9), with significant correlations defined as |correlation coefficient| > 0.3 and p < 0.05.

Microbiome driver analysis

Microorganisms do not exist in isolation but interact through complex networks that respond dynamically to environmental changes. To identify taxa acting as potential drivers within the microbial community network, the NetShift algorithm was applied to genus-level microbial data to compare the control group with the stage II CKD group, the control group with the stage III CKD group, and the control group with the stage IV CKD group. The Neighbor Shift (NESH) score was calculated to quantify the changes in interactions among adjacent nodes within microbial community networks. Node drivability was subsequently evaluated by integrating NESH values with node connectivity metrics. Network properties, such as density, clustering coefficient, average path length, total number of nodes, total number of edges, and exclusive edges, were compared between the control group and the stage II CKD group.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (v 4.2.2). The Wilcoxon test was used to assess differences between the two groups. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sequencing depth and microbial diversity assessment

Rarefaction curves were used to evaluate the sequencing depth of the microbiome data. For the control group and the stage II, III, and IV CKD groups, the curves approached a plateau, indicating that further increases in sequencing depth would be unlikely to yield a substantial number of additional ASVs. This observation suggests that the current sequencing depth was sufficient to capture microbial diversity in the samples (Figures 1–4).

Identification of 10 differential microorganisms

LEfSe analysis identified 47 candidate differential microorganisms at the genus level across the control group and the stage II, stage III, and stage IV CKD group. Among these, 27 microorganisms showed significant differences in the control group, with Faecalibacterium exhibiting the highest LDA score. In the stage II CKD group, six microorganisms showed significant differences, among which unclassified Moraxellaceae had the highest LDA score. The stage III CKD group showed significant differences in three microorganisms, with Eisenbergiella exhibiting the highest LDA score. In the stage IV CKD group, 11 microorganisms exhibited significant differences, and Parabacteroides had the highest LDA score (Table 1).

The LASSO algorithm was applied to the 10 differential microorganisms, resulting in the identification of seven characteristic microorganisms (λ_min = 0.002178849). Meanwhile, the Boruta algorithm identified nine characteristic microorganisms. By intersecting the results of both algorithms, microbial biomarkers were determined. Eubacterium eligens group, NK4A214 group, unclassified Gastranaerophilales, Allisonella, Lactococcus, and unclassified Ktedonobacteria. Microbial biomarkers influenced by microbial communities across CKD stages

In the comparison between the stage II CKD and control groups, 26 driving relationships among microorganisms were predicted at the genus level. The microbial co-occurrence network at this stage exhibited a relatively sparse structure, with interactions mainly concentrated among Christensenellaceae, Dubosiella, and Eubacterium, indicating that the microbial interaction network remained in a relatively stable state during early-stage CKD progression (Supplementary Table S4).

Among the six network characteristics, only the clustering coefficient was higher in the stage II CKD group than in the control group, whereas the remaining indicators were generally more elevated in the control group than in the disease group. These results suggest that, although microbial composition had already changed in CKD II, the overall interaction network had not yet undergone substantial structural reorganization.

For the stage III CKD and control groups, 27 driving relationships among microorganisms were predicted at the genus level. Compared with CKD II, the number of driving relationships showed only a marginal increase, and the overall network topology remained largely comparable between the two stages. Frequent interactions were observed among Christensenellaceae, Coprococcus, Eubacterium, Methyloversatilis, and Dubosiella, indicating continuity of the dominant interaction patterns (Supplementary Table S5). Consistent with stage II, the cluster coefficient indicator was higher in the stage III CKD group than in the control group, while the expressions of the remaining indicators were generally higher in the control group. These findings suggest that from CKD II to CKD III, the microbial co-occurrence network changed in a relatively gradual manner and maintain32ed a stable topological organization.

In the comparison between the stage IV CKD and control groups, 39 driving relationships among microorganisms were predicted at the genus level, representing a marked increase compared with earlier stages. At this stage, the microbial interaction network became substantially denser, with frequent interactions observed among Acidibacter, Allisonella, CHKCI002, Georgfuchsia, Rikenella, and Rodentibacter, indicating a pronounced reorganization of microbial interactions (Supplementary Table S6). Consistently, the comparison of network topological properties revealed that, in CKD IV, most indicators – including network density, clustering coefficient, average path length, total edges, and exclusive edges – were higher in the disease group than in the control group. This simultaneous and substantial alteration across multiple network parameters suggests that the microbial co-occurrence network underwent a nonlinear and abrupt transition, rather than a gradual change, as CKD progressed to the advanced stage.

Collectively, these results indicate that while the microbial interaction network remained relatively stable during CKD II and CKD III, a pronounced shift in network topology and interaction patterns occurred at CKD IV, reflecting a transition of the microbial ecosystem to a new structural state.

Discussion

CKD represents a complex systems disorder, involving dysregulated crosstalk between host physiology and the gut microbial ecosystem. It is estimated that approximately 37 million adults in the United States have CKD, with more than one in seven affected by the disease [26]. In recent years, multiple studies have established that patients with CKD exhibit significant gut microbiota dysbiosis, characterized by reduced abundance of beneficial bacteria, overgrowth of opportunistic pathogens, and diminished microbial diversity. This dysbiotic state is closely associated with CKD stage, rate of renal function deterioration, and risk of complications. Recently published research has further deepened our understanding of this association: Miao H’s study reveals that gut dysbiosis in CKD patients can activate dysregulated metabolites via the gut-kidney axis, providing direct evidence for the molecular mechanism by which dysbiosis mediates renal injury [27]. Research has further revealed that patients with impaired renal function exhibit a significant reduction in the abundance of beneficial gut bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus species. Moreover, the degree of gut microbiota dysbiosis correlates positively with decreased levels of indole-3-pyruvate, indole-3-carboxaldehyde, and tryptophan in these patients. This confirms the potential value of gut microbiota structural stability in monitoring the progression of CKD [28]. In this study, using 16S rDNA sequencing data and bioinformatics analyses, we examined CKD progression, identified six microbial biomarkers – Eubacterium eligens group, NK4A214 group, unclassified Gastranaerophilales, Allisonella, Lactococcus, and unclassified Ktedonobacteria – and explored their relevance to the disease mechanisms. Our study reveals that the progression of CKD is associated with marked phase transitions in the architecture of the gut microbial ecological network. From stage II to stage IV, we observed not merely shifts in individual taxa abundances, but fundamental reorganizations in microbial interaction patterns, as evidenced by the increasing number and changing identity of driver relationships (from 26 to 39) and the shift in key topological properties. The six key microbial biomarkers we identified may serve as hub taxa or keystone species whose alteration could potentially trigger or signify these critical ecosystem-level transitions, thereby influencing host health via the gut-kidney axis. The panel of gut microbial biomarkers identified in this study could serve as promising candidates for the early diagnosis and risk stratification of CKD, while targeted regulatory strategies against key functional taxa are expected to provide novel clinical insights for the early intervention of CKD.

Eubacterium spp. are strictly anaerobic, gram-positive bacteria within the phylum Thick-walled Bacteria and family Eubacteriaceae. They play a role in host metabolic functions and contribute to immunomodulation and maintenance of intestinal homeostasis. It has been shown that the abundance of the Eubacterium eligens group in the gut is significantly reduced in patients with CKD, particularly in advanced stages [29], and this reduction is negatively correlated with renal function decline, as indicated by the decreased estimated glomerular filtration rate and increased serum creatinine [30]. Mechanistically, butyrate deficiency resulting from reduced Eubacterium eligens abundance can disrupt intestinal barrier function, promote endotoxin translocation into the bloodstream, trigger systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, and accelerate renal fibrosis [31]. In the microbial driver analysis, this taxon exhibited a crucial driving role in the microbial networks of both the CKD Stage II and Stage III groups, indicating that it may not only represent the earliest species to undergo alterations following initial ecosystem perturbation, but also consolidate and amplify the impacts of early perturbations, thereby driving large-scale remodeling of the network structure.

The NK4A214 group is a bacterial lineage within the family Ruminalococcaceae and is commonly found in the rumen of ruminants as well as in the intestines and other environments of humans and animals. This group plays a vital role in the digestive process, particularly in the degradation and fermentation of complex carbohydrates, converting polysaccharides into absorbable nutrients such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which provide energy for the host [32]. Additionally, NK4A214 may also interact with other microorganisms to maintain the balance of the gut microbial community. Although no definitive study has shown a relationship between NK4A214 and CKD, it is hypothesized that alterations in microbial communities, including the Ruminococcaceae NK4A214 group, may compromise intestinal barrier function [33]. Impaired intestinal barrier integrity could allow bacterial products in the gut, such as lipopolysaccharides, to translocate into the bloodstream, triggering systemic inflammation, a recognized contributor to CKD progression. Furthermore, changes in the gut microbial community may alter metabolite profiles, affecting host energy and lipid metabolism and indirectly influencing CKD progression. For instance, recent studies indicate that the potent renal metabolic modulator 1-methoxypyrene participates in microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) fibrosis via the AhR signaling pathway [34]. Research has also demonstrated that chronic kidney disease (CKD) leads to phospholipid metabolism disorders, with phospholipid dysregulation contributing to the pathology of CKD [35]. Separate studies have reported that the downregulation of probiotic levels associated with kidney disease correlates positively with decreased serum metabolite levels (indole-3-pyruvate, indole-3-carboxaldehyde, and tryptophan) [28]. Future studies should integrate metabolomics research to directly correlate the specific microbial biomarkers identified in this paper (such as the NK4A214 group) with alterations in these key metabolites, thereby elucidating their precise role in the progression of chronic kidney disease.

Gastranaerophilales (unclassified at lower taxonomic levels) have not yet been assigned to a specific family, genus, or species. Based on their classification at the order level, these microorganisms may share specific general characteristics. Members of the order Gastroanaerophilales are typically anaerobic or microaerobic bacteria adapted to relatively low-oxygen environments, such as specific regions of the human gastrointestinal tract. They may be involved in host digestive processes, contribute to nutrient metabolism, or interact with other microbial taxa; however, due to their unclassified status, detailed and definitive studies on their biological properties and metabolic functions remain limited [36,37]. Patients with CKD often experience gut dysbiosis, and Gastranaerophilales may play a role in this imbalance, potentially affecting intestinal homeostasis. As anaerobic bacteria, they may produce metabolites such as SCFAs, which may affect host metabolic and inflammatory states [38]. These microorganisms may play a role in modulating inflammatory responses and immune regulation in patients with CKD [39].

Allisonella is a group of anaerobic bacteria within the phylum Firmicutes and family Veillonellaceae. It is mainly found in the intestinal tracts of humans and animals and metabolizes lactic acid to produce SCFAs, including acetic acid and propionic acid. The metabolic activity of Allisonella is closely linked to host energy metabolism and intestinal health, and its lactic acid–metabolizing function may be compromised in patients with CKD, who are prone to metabolic acidosis because of impaired renal function [40]. By regulating lactate levels, Allisonella may indirectly affect the acid–base balance in patients with CKD [41]. Meanwhile, its metabolism produces SCFAs, such as acetic acid and propionic acid, which exhibit anti-inflammatory effects and support intestinal barrier function. Patients with CKD often experience gut dysbiosis, and alterations in the abundance of Allisonella may be associated with CKD progression [42]. Studies suggest that specific changes in gut flora may exacerbate uremic toxin accumulation and trigger inflammatory responses in patients with CKD. Although direct studies on the relationship between Allisonella and CKD are limited, its metabolic functions indicate that it may play an important role in the pathophysiology of the disease. Further research is required to clarify the specific role of Allisonella in CKD and to evaluate its potential therapeutic value [43]. Microbial driver analysis revealed that this taxon dominated the microbial networks in the late stage of CKD (Stage IV), which may indicate that the ecosystem had become “locked” into a novel, stable pathological state. These taxa are likely to be key contributors to the maintenance of such maladaptive stability, thus rendering the ecosystem difficult to reverse. The stage-specific driver succession we observed aligns with ecological theories of community dynamics. This hierarchical framework not only describes the observed succession but also suggests potential intervention points: targeting early Initiators might prevent network collapse, while modulating late-stage Lock-in taxa could be key to reversing established dysbiosis.

The genus Lactococcus belongs to the phylum Thick-walled bacteria and comprises gram-positive bacteria. The cells are typically spherical or ovoid and often form short chains or bilobed arrangements. These bacteria are parthenogenetic anaerobes and can grow under aerobic and anaerobic conditions. Their optimal growth temperature is approximately 30 °C, and many strains exhibit salt tolerance, enabling survival in environments with moderate salt concentrations. Lactococcus species are widely distributed in dairy products, plant materials, the oral cavity, the gastrointestinal tract, and other environments in humans and animals [44]. As a probiotic, Lactococcus may support intestinal health in patients with CKD by regulating intestinal flora balance, enhancing intestinal barrier function, and reducing inflammation. CKD is often associated with gut dysbiosis and systemic inflammation, and probiotic supplementation may help ameliorate these conditions [45]. Lactococcus may also reduce toxin burden in patients with CKD by maintaining gut health and reducing the production of uremic toxins [43], as well as by modulating chronic inflammation through the regulation of immune responses and limitation of proinflammatory factor release [46]. Although its precise mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated, Lactococcus represents a potential target for gut microbiota–based interventions in CKD [47].

Ktedonobacteria (unclassified) refers to unclassified microorganisms within the class Fibrobacteria. These microorganisms are typically unicellular. Although their specific biological functions remain poorly understood, they may indirectly influence CKD progression by affecting intestinal barrier function and modulating immune responses [48].

A central finding of this study is that the gut ecosystem in CKD undergoes significant ecological network rewiring despite maintaining overall taxonomic richness (alpha diversity). Our study challenges the simple paradigm of microbial ‘loss’ in disease. This suggests that CKD progression may be more closely linked to disruptions in microbial interactions and community stability than to mere changes in species abundance. The identified microbial biomarkers likely function as critical nodes within this rewired network, and their alteration could destabilize the entire ecosystem, contributing to disease pathophysiology.

However, this study still has several limitations. First, the sample size was limited and confounding factors were inadequately controlled. As a single-center exploratory study, it was constrained by a relatively small sample size, which restricted the extrapolation of the conclusions. Meanwhile, detailed records of long-term dietary habits and medication histories were unavailable for some participants, and these confounding factors (such as, gut microbiota, age, Diabetes,sex) might have interfered with the results of the microbiota analysis. Second, there were technical limitations in functional analysis. The functional predictions generated by PICRUSt2 rely on reference genome databases, which leads to deviations from the actual metabolic status of the microbiota. In addition, the STAMP software cannot display the specific values of confidence intervals, which reduces the rigor of the conclusions regarding the significance of differential pathways. Third, multi-omics integration and experimental validation were lacking. This study did not combine metabolomics and other technologies to clarify the correlations among microbiota, metabolites, and host phenotypes, thus limiting the mechanistic interpretation to the level of association analysis only. Based on the above shortcomings, future research can be advanced from three aspects: First, conduct multi-center, large-sample prospective cohort studies, improve the collection of clinical information such as diet and medication history, and reduce the interference of confounding factors through rigorous statistical methods to enhance the generalizability of the conclusions. Second, integrate multi-omics technologies with functional validation: combine metagenomics and metabolomics to characterize the functional activity of the microbiota, and verify the regulatory mechanisms of core driver taxa using animal or cell experiments. Third, focus on clinical translation applications: develop CKD risk assessment tools based on core microbial biomarkers, and explore microbiota-targeted intervention strategies such as probiotics and traditional Chinese medicine, so as to provide novel regimens for the treatment of CKD.
