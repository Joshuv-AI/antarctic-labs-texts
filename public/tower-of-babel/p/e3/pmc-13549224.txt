Effect of Incident Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension on Risk of Newly‐Diagnosed Mental Health Conditions
Authors: Anna Watzker, Oscar Patterson‐Lomba, Cameron Fowler, Yan Song, Alejandro Victores, Alen Dezia, Jiayang Li, Azeem Banatwala, Qingyi Cai, Lana Melendres‐Groves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a life‐limiting disease with psychosocial burden, although US population‐level estimates of incident mental health (MH) disorders remain limited. In this retrospective cohort study using Komodo Research Data (01/01/2016−03/31/2024), we evaluated the association between PAH diagnosis and risk of newly diagnosed MH conditions. Adults (≥ 18 years) with incident PAH were identified alongside a non‐PAH control group. Among patients with PAH (N = 4005), the risks of newly diagnosed MH conditions were compared before and after the PAH diagnosis date (index date). Between‐group comparisons of newly diagnosed MH conditions post‐index used inverse probability weighting to balance PAH and non‐PAH patients. Outcomes included 12‐month cumulative incidence and hazard ratios (HRs) from Cox models for any MH condition and prespecified categories (anxiety, mood, sleep‐wake, substance use). Post‐diagnosis, the 12‐month risk of any newly diagnosed MH condition nearly doubled compared with pre‐diagnosis (48.1% vs 24.9%; HR: 2.29; p < 0.001). Post‐diagnosis risks were also higher for sleep‐wake (27.1% vs 11.5%; 2.59), anxiety‐spectrum (11.4% vs 8.4%; 1.37), mood (9.7% vs 6.0%; 1.65), and opioid use disorders (1.5% vs 0.7%; 2.07; all p < 0.05). Compared with matched controls (N = 421,987), PAH patients had higher risks of any MH condition (39.5% vs 17.8%; HR: 2.57), anxiety (10.1% vs 6.5%; 1.58), mood (8.7% vs 5.3%; 1.68), and substance use disorders (5.2% vs 3.4%; 1.53; all p < 0.001). Adults with PAH experienced marked increases in newly diagnosed MH conditions after PAH onset and significantly higher risk than matched non‐PAH controls, supporting routine MH screening and integrated, proactive MH care in PAH management.

Introduction

Mental health (MH) conditions are highly prevalent among patients with chronic, progressive, and life‐limiting diseases such as advanced heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and cancer [1, 2, 3]. Depression, anxiety, and related disorders substantially affect patients' quality of life and add to the overall burden of disease [4].

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a rare, progressive, and life‐limiting disorder characterized by increased pulmonary vascular resistance that leads to right ventricular failure and premature death [5, 6]. In the United States (US), PAH is estimated to affect 15 to 50 adults per million [7], and recent registry data have provided 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year mortality rates of 8%, 16%, and 21%, respectively [8, 9]. Beyond the mortality risk, PAH manifests with symptoms including dyspnea, fatigue, chest pain, syncope, and edema, which markedly limit daily functioning [10, 11]. Further, the diagnostic journey of PAH is often prolonged and difficult, with frequent delays and misdiagnoses (e.g., asthma, COPD, or anxiety) that contribute to uncertainty, frustration, and elevated psychological strain [7, 12, 13]. Patients also face uncertain prognosis and the demands of lifelong treatment, further compounding the overall burden of disease [11, 14].

Together, these factors can impose a substantial psychological burden on patients with PAH, with depression and anxiety among the most frequent comorbidities. Meta‐analytic data across pulmonary hypertension (PH) cohorts estimated the pooled prevalence of depression at ~28% and anxiety at ~37%, with similar rates observed in PAH‐specific groups [15]. In smaller single‐center or multicenter cohorts, between one‐third and one‐half of patients screened positive for depressive or anxiety symptoms [16, 17]. In a cross‐sectional US study at an academic center (N = 108), nearly one‐third of patients with PH screened positive for a psychiatric disorder, including major depressive disorder and other disorders [18]. Similarly, clinical interviews in a German multicenter cohort (N = 217) found that more than one‐third of patients with PAH had a psychiatric disorder, most commonly adjustment disorder, major depressive disorder, and panic disorder [16]. These symptoms correlate strongly with worse health‐related quality of life, greater functional limitation, and higher hospitalization risk [19, 20, 21]. Disease severity measures, such as World Health Organization functional class, 6‐min walk distance, and hemodynamic parameters, are also predictors of psychiatric symptom burden [22, 23, 24].

While psychosocial distress has long been recognized in patients with PAH, longitudinal data characterizing the onset and evolution of MH disorders in this population remain scarce, particularly in the US. Most prior studies have been small (N < 300), cross‐sectional, and lacked pre‐diagnosis observation periods, limiting the ability to assess whether MH burden precedes, coincides with, or follows the diagnosis of PAH [16, 18, 25, 26, 27]. Moreover, few analyses have included matched comparison groups without PAH, making it difficult to determine whether elevated MH burden is specific to PAH or reflects general population trends [28, 29].

To address this evidence gap and advance our understanding of the impact of PAH onset on the risk of newly diagnosed MH conditions, we conducted a retrospective, longitudinal cohort study using a large, national US administrative claims database to estimate the incidence of newly diagnosed MH conditions before and after PAH diagnosis, and to evaluate whether the risk of MH disorders among patients with PAH exceeds that of matched control subjects without PAH.

Methods

Data Source

This retrospective, longitudinal cohort study utilized the Komodo Research Data (KRD + ), a comprehensive and representative US administrative claims database, spanning January 1, 2016, to March 31, 2024. KRD+ includes fully‐adjudicated claims data directly from 150+ payers (commercial, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, Managed Medicaid, and other payers) covering 130 million patients' lives in the US. The database captures information on settings of care (inpatient and non‐inpatient), medical claims (diagnoses, procedures), and pharmacy claims (dispensed medications). This study utilized only fully‐adjudicated and closed claims data to ensure completeness and accuracy in capturing diagnoses and outcomes.

The study was considered exempt research under 45 CFR § 46.104(d)(4) as it involved only the use of secondary data that were de‐identified in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, specifically, 45 CFR § 164.514.

Study Design

The analysis assessed the incidence of newly diagnosed MH conditions among patients with PAH, both before and after PAH diagnosis, and compared these outcomes with a matched control cohort without PAH. The overall study design, including observation periods, outcome assessment, and analyses are summarized in Figure 1. Study Cohorts

PAH Cohort

This study aimed to capture an incident, newly treated PAH population in KRD + . The PAH cohort consisted of adult patients newly diagnosed with PAH during the study period from January 1, 2016 to March 31, 2024. Patients were identified using a claims‐based operational definition: ≥ 1 inpatient claim or ≥ 2 outpatient claims (≥ 30 days apart) with a PAH diagnosis in any position, together with ≥ 1 prescription claim for a PAH‐indicated medication within 3 months after the first observed PAH diagnosis (index date) [9, 30]. Eligible patients were aged ≥ 18 years at the index date, had continuous health plan enrollment for ≥ 12 months before (baseline period) and ≥ 3 months after the index date. These continuous eligibility criteria resulted in a PAH cohort consisting of adults diagnosed with PAH between January 1, 2017 and December 31, 2023. Patients were excluded if they had any diagnosis for PH groups 2–5 (i.e., due to left heart disease, due to lung diseases or hypoxia, chronic thromboembolic PH, and PH with unclear and/or multifactorial mechanisms [31]) at any time during the study period or any evidence of a PAH diagnosis during the baseline period. Sample selection for the PAH cohort is illustrated in Supporting Figure 1. The diagnostic codes for identification of PAH and PH groups 2–5 are listed in Supporting Table 1.

Control Cohort

The control cohort consisted of adults without any PH diagnosis during the study period in KRD + , drawn from a random sample of 1 million individuals from the general insured population. This comparator group was constructed to estimate the background incidence of MH conditions in the general population, enabling assessment of the excess risk associated with diagnosis of PAH.

The index date for control subjects was randomly assigned from a uniform distribution across the study period, ensuring that index dates were evenly distributed over time. As with the PAH cohort, eligible control subjects were aged ≥ 18 years at the index date and had continuous health plan enrollment for ≥ 12 months before (baseline period), and ≥ 3 months after the index date. Patients were excluded if they had any diagnosis of PAH or of PH groups 2–5 at any time during the study period. Sample selection for the control cohort is illustrated in Supporting Figure 2.

Study Measures and Outcomes

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were assessed during the 12‐month baseline period. Incident (newly diagnosed) MH conditions, classified according to major diagnostic categories in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition, Text Revision, were identified from International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification diagnosis codes on medical claims [32]. MH conditions were assessed by the following main categories: anxiety spectrum disorders, mood disorders, substance use disorders, and other disorders (including neurocognitive, neurodevelopmental, somatic symptom‐related, obsessive compulsive‐related, feeding and eating, sleep‐wake, sexual dysfunctions, trauma‐ and stressor‐related disorders, and suicidal ideation or self‐harm). Any of the above MH conditions was also assessed as a composite endpoint.

To account for differences in follow‐up duration among eligible individuals and the incident nature of the outcomes of interest, MH diagnoses were evaluated as time‐to‐event outcomes, both pre‐ and post‐PAH diagnosis in the PAH cohort, and post‐index in the matched control cohort. A newly diagnosed MH comorbidity was defined as the first recorded diagnosis following a period of ≥ 12 months without the same diagnosis.

Because psychiatric conditions may be chronic, episodic, or underdiagnosed, a first observed claim following a 12‐month diagnosis‐free interval represents a newly documented diagnosis in claims rather than a confirmed incident case; it may reflect true disease onset, recurrence, or simply a change in coding or clinical recognition.

Statistical Analysis

Summary statistics were used to describe baseline demographic and clinical characteristics with means, standard deviations (SDs), medians, and interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables.

Time to incident MH condition was compared between the 12‐month baseline period and the follow‐up period using Cox proportional hazards models. Separate models were estimated for each MH category. Each model included a binary indicator for period (baseline vs. follow‐up) as the only covariate. To account for within‐person correlation, standard errors (SE) were calculated using a robust sandwich variance estimator clustered at the individual level. For each MH condition, hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), p values, and the estimated 12‐month cumulative incidence in the baseline and follow‐up were reported. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. p values were nominal, and no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied because the outcomes were prespecified clinically meaningful MH categories.

To compare MH risk between the PAH and control cohorts, we first applied inverse probability weighting (IPW) so that the patients in the control cohort were reweighted to resemble the PAH cohort, on average, thereby estimating the excess risk of MH conditions attributable to PAH (i.e., the risk PAH patients would have experienced in the absence of PAH). Propensity scores for cohort assignment (PAH vs. control) were estimated using logistic regression including the following baseline covariates: age, sex, region, index year, payer type, and the modified Quan‐Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [33]. The modification of the CCI involved excluding diagnosis codes used to define PAH from the chronic pulmonary disease component of the Quan‐CCI to avoid overlap between the comorbidity index and the cohort definition. After weighting, covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with values < 0.10 considered indicative of adequate balance.

Time to incident MH condition during the follow up period was compared between the PAH cohort and the matched control cohort using IP‐weighted Cox proportional hazards models, with robust SE to account for the weighting. Models were estimated separately for each MH category and included only a binary indicator for PAH versus control cohort, with no additional covariates. For each condition, HR with 95% CI, p values, and 12‐month cumulative incidence following the index date estimates were reported.

All analyses were conducted within the Komodo Sentinel platform using Snowflake version 9.25.1 (Snowflake Inc., Bozeman, MT) for data management and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R version 4.2.1 via RStudio (2022.07.0 Build 548, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) for statistical analyses.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Patients in PAH Cohort

A total of 4,005 adults with incident PAH met eligibility criteria and were included in the PAH cohort (Supporting Figure 1). Key characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Median follow‐up was 1.9 years. The mean age at diagnosis was 61.1 (SD: 14.4) years, 46.8% were female, and insurance coverage at index was predominantly Medicare (46.1%) or commercial (43.9%), followed by Medicaid (9.9%). The mean modified CCI was 2.4 (SD: 2.2) and the most common comorbidities (≥ 20%) were chronic pulmonary disease (38.7%), congestive heart failure (36.3%), peripheral vascular disease (23.4%), and renal disease (21.6%). Right heart catheterization within ±3 months of index occurred in 33.5% of individuals.

Incidence of MH Comorbidities: Pre‐ and Post‐PAH Diagnosis

Compared with the pre‐PAH diagnosis period, the post‐diagnosis period showed a significantly higher risk of newly diagnosed MH comorbidities (Figure 2). The 12‐month cumulative incidence of any newly diagnosed MH condition nearly doubled, from 24.9% pre‐diagnosis to 48.1% post‐diagnosis (HR: 2.29; p < 0.001). After PAH diagnosis, risk increased significantly for anxiety‐spectrum disorders (11.4% vs 8.4%; HR: 1.37; p < 0.01) and mood disorders (9.7% vs 6.0%; 1.65; p < 0.001). Among specific MH categories, the largest relative increase was observed for sleep‐wake disorders (27.1% vs 11.5%; HR: 2.59; p < 0.001), followed by other mood disorders (predominantly unspecified depression; 5.9% vs 2.7%; 2.21; p < 0.001) and opioid use disorder (1.5% vs 0.7%; 2.07; p < 0.05). No significant differences between pre‐ and post‐PAH diagnosis were observed for generalized anxiety disorder (HR: 1.07; p = 0.706), bipolar disorder (1.57; p = 0.102), trauma‐ and stressor‐related disorders (1.34; p = 0.058), and for other categories that were less common (each with < 1% 12‐month cumulative incidence).

To confirm that the observed increase in MH burden after diagnosis was not influenced by early mortality or differential follow‐up, a sensitivity analysis was conducted among patients who remained enrolled for ≥12 months after PAH diagnosis. Results from this analysis were broadly consistent with those from the overall cohort (Supporting Figure 3).

Matching of PAH and Control Cohorts

A total of 421,987 individuals without PAH met the eligibility criteria for inclusion in the control cohort (Supporting Figure 2). After weighting, baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were well balanced between the PAH and control cohorts, with all SMD ≤ 0.10 (Supporting Table 2).

Incidence of MH Comorbidities: PAH versus Non‐PAH Control Cohorts

Patients with PAH had more than a twofold higher 12‐month risk of any newly diagnosed MH condition compared with the matched control cohort (39.5% vs 17.8%; HR: 2.57; p < 0.001; Figure 3), a magnitude similar to that observed when comparing pre‐ and post‐diagnosis periods within the PAH cohort (Figure 2). Compared with the matched control cohort, elevated risks were observed in the PAH cohort for anxiety‐spectrum disorders (10.1% vs 6.5%; HR: 1.58), mood disorders (8.7% vs 5.3%; 1.68), and substance use disorders (5.2% vs 3.4%; 1.53; all p < 0.001). Among specific MH conditions, the largest relative risks for the PAH cohort compared with the control cohort were observed for sleep‐wake disorders (23.3% vs 8.0%; HR: 3.20), suicidal ideation or self‐harm (8.9% vs 3.8%; 2.44), opioid use disorder (1.3% vs 0.6%; 2.28), and somatic symptom disorders (0.7% vs 0.3%; 2.14; all p < 0.001). No significant differences between cohorts were observed for neurodevelopmental (HR: 1.23; p = 0.176), obsessive‐compulsive (0.99; p = 0.944), feeding/eating (1.63; p = 0.067), or sexual‐dysfunction disorders (1.38; p = 0.110).

Discussion

This study utilized a large, retrospective, longitudinal US claims cohort to assess the burden of MH conditions in over 4,000 patients with newly diagnosed PAH. By examining a broad range of MH comorbidities, the analysis provides a comprehensive assessment of the incremental MH burden attributable to PAH. Two complementary comparative approaches were used: (1) comparing the incidence of MH conditions before versus after PAH diagnosis within the PAH cohort, and (2) comparing post‐index MH incidence between patients with PAH and a matched control cohort without PAH. Together, these approaches offered within‐patient and between‐cohort perspectives on the MH burden associated with PAH.

The results demonstrate that the incidence of MH conditions doubled in the year following PAH diagnosis compared with the preceding year, underscoring a substantial increase in MH burden at disease onset. Consistently, patients with PAH had more than a twofold higher risk of MH conditions than matched control subjects without PAH, confirming that this excess burden is specific to PAH rather than general population trends. The largest relative risks were observed for MH conditions such as sleep‐wake, opioid use, and neurocognitive disorders, as well as suicidal intent, self‐harm, or suicidal ideation. Overall, the results of the pre‐ versus post‐PAH diagnosis and PAH versus control patient analyses were highly concordant in both direction and magnitude, reinforcing the robustness of the findings.

The observed rate of suicidal ideation or self‐harm warrants attention. Consistent with a prior study showing higher suicide probability, hopelessness, and depression scores among patients with PAH [29], our findings underscore the clinical importance of this issue. These results should be interpreted cautiously given limitations inherent to claims‐based data, including potential underdiagnosis or undercoding of MH conditions and increased documentation opportunities following PAH diagnosis. Nevertheless, these findings support the need for better coordinated PAH and MH care, including attention to psychological distress and suicidality.

This study builds upon and extends prior evidence on the MH burden associated with PAH by addressing key limitations of earlier work. Prior US and European studies based on questionnaires or structured clinical interviews have consistently reported high rates of depression, anxiety, and other mental disorders among patients with PAH. However, these studies were constrained by small sample sizes (N < 250) and single‐center or regional cohorts, reducing their generalizability to the broader PAH population [16, 18, 25]. Moreover, their cross‐sectional designs captured only prevalent MH conditions, making it difficult to determine when these conditions first developed and obscuring the temporal relationship between PAH onset and subsequent MH burden [26, 27]. Another critical limitation of prior work is the absence of appropriate comparative control groups without PAH. Without appropriate comparators, it was impossible to distinguish whether observed MH burdens were specific to PAH or reflected background population trends. Of the few studies incorporating comparator groups, the analyses relied on small, non‐US samples and lacked covariate balancing techniques [28, 29], increasing the risk of confounding and limiting the validity of causal interpretation.

The present findings underscore the importance of integrating routine MH screening and care into PAH management. The American College of Chest Physicians guidelines focus primarily on diagnostic and pharmacologic aspects of PAH management and do not specifically address mental health care. The European Society of Cardiology/European Respiratory Society guidelines acknowledge the psychosocial burden of PAH and recommend psychological assessment and referral for support as part of comprehensive care; however, they do not provide a structured or standardized framework for routine MH screening or management [31, 34]. Interventional trials of cognitive behavioral therapy and self‐help psychological programs have shown encouraging improvements in anxiety and depression among patients with PH [35, 36, 37, 38]. Additionally, experience from other chronic conditions, such as heart failure and COPD, suggests that integrated behavioral health programs are associated with improved patient outcomes [39, 40, 41]. It is therefore plausible that similar approaches could benefit patients with PAH, enhancing both psychological well‐being and overall disease management [42, 43].

Several limitations should be considered. First, symptoms of PAH often precede formal diagnosis, and the diagnostic journey can be prolonged. This may elevate the MH burden before diagnosis, potentially underestimating the true effect of PAH diagnosis on incident MH conditions when comparing the pre‐ and post‐diagnosis outcomes. Second, the median follow‐up of 1.9 years may be insufficient to fully capture the long‐term trajectory of MH conditions, particularly for conditions that develop gradually. Third, PAH was identified using a claims‐based algorithm requiring diagnosis codes and PAH‐indicated therapy. Prior chart‐validated study has shown that multidimensional algorithms incorporating PAH‐specific therapy achieve high sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value (PPV), with further gains in specificity and PPV when RHC is also required [44]. However, only 33.5% of patients had an observed RHC claim within ±3 months of index; requiring RHC would substantially reduce sample size and may falsely classify patients as not having PAH when the hemodynamic procedure is simply unobserved. Claims do not contain hemodynamic results, precluding definitive confirmation of Group 1 PAH. Some inclusion of patients with non‐Group 1 PH remains possible and could affect absolute MH incidence estimates; however, prior patient‐reported evidence has demonstrated substantial mental and emotional burden in non‐Group 1 PH [45], suggesting that these findings remain clinically relevant across pulmonary vascular disease populations. Fourth, although PAH is observed to be more prevalent in women [46], this study identified a higher proportion of men, likely reflecting the inclusion of off‐label use of PAH‐targeted therapies for non‐Group 1 PH conditions more common in men. However, because the analysis focused on comparative incidence of MH conditions rather than absolute PAH prevalence, this potential misclassification is unlikely to change the main findings. Fifth, increased healthcare contact after a PAH diagnosis may raise the likelihood of detecting MH conditions (i.e., surveillance bias). However, many patients also have substantial comorbidities (e.g., heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease) that result in frequent healthcare encounters even before PAH diagnosis, which may mitigate this concern. Future studies could further explore this issue through sensitivity analyses incorporating time‐varying measures of healthcare encounter intensity or by adjusting for visit frequency as a time‐varying covariate. Sixth, multiple MH outcomes were evaluated without formal multiplicity adjustment; reported P values are nominal. However, the consistency of findings across MH categories and across both analytic approaches (pre‐ vs. post‐diagnosis and PAH vs. matched controls) supports the robustness of the overall pattern. Seventh, because the study population was derived from insured individuals within the KRD+ database, findings may not generalize to uninsured populations or those outside the KRD+ database. Finally, as with all observational research, residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be fully ruled out, even after applying statistical adjustments.

Conclusions

This large US claims study demonstrates that the onset of PAH is associated with a substantial increase in MH burden, with post‐diagnosis risk nearly twice as high as both the pre‐diagnosis risk and that observed in matched non‐PAH control subjects. The marked rise in MH burden following PAH diagnosis suggests that the period immediately after diagnosis may represent a critical window for intervention. These findings highlight the importance of continuing to integrate routine MH screening and psychiatric care into PAH management to improve patient well‐being, enhance treatment adherence, and potentially improve overall clinical outcomes. Future research should evaluate whether integrating proactive behavioral health interventions can mitigate the elevated MH burden and improve long‐term outcomes for patients living with PAH.
