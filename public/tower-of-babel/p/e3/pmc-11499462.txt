Effectiveness of Lanzhou Lamb Rotavirus Vaccine and RotaTeq Against Hospitalized Rotavirus Infections Among Children During 2020-2023 in Guangdong Province, China: A Test-Negative Case-Control Study
Authors: Yao Yi, Jun Liu, Yingtao Zhang, Biao Zeng, Liling Lin, Caixia Li, Fen Yang, Hailong Zhang, Ruili Xie, Zhuhang Huang, Min Kang, Yawen Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe evidence regarding the effectiveness of Lanzhou Lamb Rotavirus Vaccine (LLR) and RotaTeq (RV5) against gastroenteritis (RVGE) caused by emerging genotypes in Chinese children remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a test-negative case–control study using gastroenteritis surveillance data from four cities (2020–2023) in Guangdong Province, China. Children aged 2 months to 5 years hospitalized with acute gastroenteritis were enrolled. Cases were rotavirus-positive; controls were rotavirus-negative. Vaccine effectiveness (VE) was estimated using multivariable logistic regressions.ResultsAmong 2650 children, 218 (8.2%) were rotavirus-positive, predominantly G8P[8]. Also, 1543 (58.23%) children were unvaccinated, while 632 (23.85%) and 475 (17.92%) received at least one dose of RV5 and LLR, respectively. Adjusted RV5 VE against any RVGE severity was 51.7% [95% confidence interval (CI) − 58.1–85.3%]) for one dose, 37.6% (95% CI − 58.5–75.4%) for two doses, and 64.1% (95% CI 38.0–79.2%) for three doses. For LLR, VE against any RVGE severity was 38.7% (95% CI 5.7–60.2%) for one dose, 74.6% (95% CI 35.3–90.0%) for two doses, and 58.8% (95% CI − 217.6–94.6%) for three doses. Against severe RVGE, RV5 VE was 67.2% (95% CI − 144.7–95.6%) for one dose, 74.0% (95% CI − 92.1–96.5%) for two doses, and 86.6% (95% CI 56.8–95.9%) for three doses. For LLR, VE against severe RVGE was 57.7% (95% CI 20.3–77.6%) for one dose, 73.4% (95% CI 11.9–92.0%) for two doses, and − 27.8% (95% CI − 949.7–84.4%) for three doses.ConclusionsBoth RV5 and LLR provided protection against RVGE, including the emerging G8P[8] genotype. Three doses of RV5 offered strong protection, while two doses of LLR also appeared to be an effective strategy against rotavirus infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-024-01040-y.

Introduction

Rotavirus is a leading cause of acute gastroenteritis (AGE) in young children globally, contributing significantly to severe diarrhea-related morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Rotavirus vaccines have been highly effective in reducing hospitalizations and deaths in high-income countries. In the absence of specific antiviral treatments, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends rotavirus vaccination as the most effective preventive strategy against rotavirus gastroenteritis (RVGE) [3].

Several rotavirus vaccines are currently available in different parts of the world, including the monovalent Rotarix, the monovalent Rotavac, the pentavalent Rotasiil, the pentavalent RotaTeq (RV5) [3], and the monovalent Lanzhou Lamb Rotavirus (LLR) vaccine [2]. Three rotavirus vaccines are currently licensed for use in China: the monovalent LLR developed by the Lanzhou Institute of Biological Products, the pentavalent human–bovine reassortant vaccine (RV5) produced by MSD, and a trivalent live human–lamb reassortant rotavirus vaccine (LLR3) also produced by the Lanzhou Institute [4]. Among these, LLR and RV5 were marketed in 2000 and 2018 in China, respectively, whereas LLR3 was not licensed until December 2023 [2, 4]. Despite their absence in the National Immunization Program, the uptake of RV5 and LLR has been increasing in recent years [2, 5].

Clinical trials have demonstrated the efficacy of RV5 in preventing rotavirus gastroenteritis caused by various genotypes. A pivotal trial reported a vaccine efficacy of 74% against any severity of RVGE and 98% against severe RVGE [6]. In China, a phase III trial showed that RV5 had an efficacy of 67.4% and 88.3% against any severity and severe RVGE caused by the predominant G9 genotype, respectively [7].

Post-marketing studies have further evaluated the real-world effectiveness of RV5, with estimates ranging from 58 to 94% against rotavirus-related hospitalizations and emergency department visits [8–10]. However, data on the post-marketing effectiveness of RV5 in China remain limited, with only a small study from Shanghai reporting a vaccine effectiveness (VE) of 85% against mild to moderate RVGE in children aged 14 weeks to 4 years [5].

For LLR, most post-marketing studies have reported a VE of 35–53% against any severity of RVGE and 52–88% against severe RVGE after at least one dose [11–13]. The VE against severe RVGE caused by the G3 and G9 genotypes was estimated to be 52% and 40%, respectively [11, 12].

However, the effectiveness of these vaccines in real-world settings can vary depending on factors such as vaccine type, vaccination schedule, population characteristics, and circulating rotavirus strains. Notably, the predominant rotavirus genotypes have been shifting in many regions, with G8P[8] emerging as a one of the most prevalent strains in recent years [14, 15]. In China, G8P[8] has been increasingly detected in the most populous cities such as Shanghai, Beijing, and Guangzhou [16–18]. RV5 consisted of genotypes G1, G2, G3, G4, and P1A[8] [19], whereas LLR consisted of genotype G10P[12] [20], neither of which was originally devised to prevent the G8 genotypes.

To date, no study has evaluated the real-world effectiveness of RV5 and LLR in the same population head-to-head, particularly against the G8P[8] genotype. Such data are crucial for informing vaccination policies and strategies in China. As new genotypes of rotavirus continue to pose a significant health burden on children in China, regional and national immunization programs are increasingly considering the inclusion of rotavirus vaccines [21]. However, the non-negligible cost differences across rotavirus vaccine types necessitate a careful analysis of their effectiveness before widespread implementation [22]. Furthermore, since the eligible ages of the vaccine types do not fully overlap, evidence of their effectiveness can help optimize coverage by broadening the reach of rotavirus protection. Even more, whereas the label of RV5 clearly indicates a three-dose immunization schedule for children aged 6–32 weeks, the label of LLR only states “one dose per year for children between 2 months and 3 years of age”, which renders the immunization schedule of the latter ambiguous [23]. It is typically perceived that the maximum possible number of doses is three, but the minimum number of doses to create protection is unknown [23].

To fill these knowledge gaps, the current test-negative case–control study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of RV5 and LLR in preventing RVGE of any severity and severe RVGE, including cases caused by the G8P[8] genotype among children in China. Given the access barrier to new vaccines due to relatively high costs, a lack of clear evidence on the effectiveness of existing vaccines could undermine the credibility of their use and exacerbate vaccine hesitancy among parents. By providing real-world evidence on the VE of RV5 and LLR against the emerging G8P[8] genotype, the current study may serve as a crucial piece of evidence to inform immunization planning decisions and optimize resource allocation. LLR3 was not investigated in the current study because it was not regulatorily approved in China until December 2023 such that the accrual of data remained insufficient for a reliable evaluation [23].

Methods

Study Design and Data

We conducted a retrospective test-negative case-control study to assess the effectiveness of RV5 and LLR against RVGE in Guangdong, a province in southern China with a population of over 126 million people. Our analysis utilized data from the national viral diarrhea surveillance network, specifically focusing on records of AGE in children. This data included information on children aged 2 months to 5 years who were hospitalized with AGE in seven hospitals across four cities (Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Zhongshan, and Shaoguan) between 2020 and 2023. All hospitals were sentinel sites within the national viral diarrhea surveillance network. These hospitals followed standardized and unified protocols for data collection and case identification, as outlined by the national surveillance guidelines. After obtaining parental consent, sentinel site healthcare providers collected demographic and clinical information through face-to-face interviews using case report forms. The inclusion criteria for the current analysis were: (1) hospitalization for AGE or development of AGE symptoms during hospitalization; (2) onset of diarrhea and/or vomiting within 14 days before hospital visit (for those hospitalization for AGE) or before enrollment (for those developing AGE symptoms during hospitalization); (3) age between 2 months and 5 years (eligible for RV5 and LLR vaccination); (4) availability of stool specimen collected within 48 h of hospitalization for rotavirus testing (only for those hospitalized for AGE); and (5) retrievable rotavirus vaccination history (including whether vaccinated, the number of doses, and the type of each dose). For the children who received rotavirus vaccines, those who received a rotavirus vaccine within 14 days before symptom onset were excluded to account for the time required for vaccine-induced immunity to develop. Children who received both types of vaccines were also excluded to avoid the ambiguous definition of their vaccination status. Rotavirus vaccination histories were retrieved from the Guangdong Provincial Immunization Information System.

Ethical Approval

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (No. 202406). All data collection was completed prior to the initiation of our study. While our research design is retrospective in nature, the original data collection process adhered to a prospective protocol established by the National Viral Gastroenteritis Surveillance Program. No identifiable data are included in this study.

Outcomes

The primary outcome in the analysis was laboratory-confirmed RVGE. Potential cases and controls in the current study were identified with symptoms of AGE, which was defined as ≥ 3 episodes of stools of a less formed character than usual within 24 h, with or without vomiting (≥ 2 episodes within 24 h). On top of that, cases were rotavirus-positive children, while controls were rotavirus-negative children. The secondary outcome of interest was severe RVGE, which necessitated the concurrent presence of positive laboratory test results for rotavirus alongside the manifestation of severe symptoms of AGE. Disease severity was assessed using the Modified Vesikari Clinical Severity Scoring System, with scores ranging from 0 to 20. Scores ≥ 11, 6–10, and 1–5 were classified as severe, moderate, and mild RVGE, respectively [24].

Laboratory Methods

Stool specimens were collected and processed for rotavirus detection and genotyping. Briefly, viral RNA was extracted from fecal suspensions using an automatic nucleic acid extraction instrument (Tianlong, China) and a matching extraction kit (Tianlong). Real-time quantitative PCR was performed to detect rotavirus, and other viruses that cause AGE (norovirus, sapovirus, astrovirus, and enteric adenovirus) using nucleic acid detection kits (Bojie, China). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, a result with Ct ≤ 35 was considered positive. Rotavirus-positive specimens underwent G/P genotyping by amplifying and sequencing the VP7 and VP4 genes using One Step RT-PCR Kit (Qiagen, China), followed by genotype determination using the Group A Rotavirus Online Genotyping tool (https://www.bv-brc.org).

Exposure and Covariates

The primary exposure of interest was rotavirus vaccination status, which was based on the type of vaccine received (RV5 or LLR) and the number of doses administered. Children were classified as unvaccinated if they had not received any rotavirus vaccine doses. Those who received at least one dose of RV5 or LLR were considered vaccinated with the respective vaccine type. For the primary analysis, vaccination status was categorized into the following groups: (1) unvaccinated (reference group); (2) 1 dose of RV5; (3) 2 doses of RV5; (4) 3 doses of RV5; (5) 1 dose of LLR; (6) 2 doses of LLR; and (7) 3 doses of LLR.

Several covariates were included in the multivariable logistic regression models to adjust for potential confounding factors. These covariates were: (1) sex (male vs. female); (2) age group at symptom onset (2–8 months, 9–23 months, or 24–60 months); (3) city indicators; and (4) calendar year of sampling (2020, 2021, 2022, and 2023). Age might be related to the cohort effect of vaccination willingness as well as the susceptibility to infection. City indicators were included to account for potential differences in rotavirus epidemiology and healthcare-seeking behavior across the study sites. Year of sampling was included to adjust for potential temporal changes in rotavirus epidemiology and vaccine uptake over the study period.

In addition to the analyses of VE defining infection based on all genotypes of rotavirus, subgroup analyses were performed to assess VE against rotavirus G genotypes G8 and G9, respectively, which are the most prevalent G genotypes of rotavirus in China [25, 26]. However, it was not possible to define the vaccination status by both type and number of doses due to small sample sizes for some type–dose categories in these subgroup analyses. As a result, we defined the vaccination status as either partially or fully vaccinated by vaccine type. Fully vaccinated was defined as receiving three doses for both types. Sensitivity analyses were also conducted by (1) excluding children with co-infections (rotavirus-positive cases with at least one of the four other viruses that cause AGE detected), and (2) using rotavirus-negative children with laboratory-confirmed infections of at least one of the four other viruses as the control group. Also, as a negative control (also known as a falsification test or placebo test) [27–29], the effectiveness of varicella vaccination against rotavirus infection was assessed to detect any residual confounding. Varicella vaccination status (Supplementary Table S1) was expected to have statistically insignificant VE against rotavirus infection. Otherwise, the results would suggest potential confounding in the VE estimates of RV5 and LLR.

Statistical Analysis

We compared the baseline characteristics of cases and controls using chi-square tests and t tests for categorial and continuous variables, respectively. Multivariable logistic regressions were conducted to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) and vaccine effectiveness (VE = 1 − OR × 100%) for different vaccine types (RV5 and LLR) and dosing schedules. All analyses were conducted using Stata 15.0, with two-sided p values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

During 2020–2023, we enrolled 2,839 children hospitalized with AGE, of whom 2650 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Among these, 218 (8.23%) tested positive for rotavirus. Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the cases and controls. The mean age was higher in cases (25.92 months) than in controls (18.94 months). No significant differences were observed across male and female participants. However, the percentage of individuals from the city of Shaoguan was higher (p = 0.010) in the controls (16.57%) than in the cases (8.72%). More, a higher proportion of cases (50.46%) had severe AGE compared to controls (21.30%). Most cases (78.44%) occurred between January and April of each year, while controls were more evenly distributed across months (Fig. 2). A higher proportion of cases (72.94%) were unvaccinated against rotavirus compared to controls (56.91%). The proportion vaccinated with RV5 was significantly lower in cases (11.01%) than in controls (25.00%), while the proportion vaccinated with LLR was similar between cases (16.06%) and controls (18.09%) (Table 1). In line with the recommended immunization schedules, the average age of RV5 recipients was younger than that of LLR for each of the dose regimens Table S2).

In the multivariable logistic regression (Table 3), the VE against any severity of RVGE was 59.2% [95% confidence interval (CI): 35.4–74.2%] for RV5 (at least one dose) and 49.0% (95% CI 23.9–65.9%) for LLR (at least one dose). When considering the number of doses received, the VE against any severity of RVGE was 51.7% (95% CI (− 58.1–85.3%) for one dose of RV5, 37.6% (95% CI − 58.5–75.4%) for two doses of RV5, and 64.1% (95% CI 38.0–79.2%) for three doses of RV5. For LLR, the VE was 38.7% (95% CI 5.7–60.2%) for one dose, 74.6% (95% CI 35.3–90.0%) for two doses, and 58.8% (95% CI − 217.6–94.6%) for three doses. Of note, the estimates for one and two doses of RV5 were not statistically significant, as was that for three doses of LLR. Discussion

In this test-negative case-control study, we evaluated the real-world effectiveness of RV5 and LLR against RVGE, including cases caused by the emerging G8P[8] genotype in Guangdong, China. Our findings demonstrate that both vaccines provided substantial protection against RVGE, with three-dose RV5 and two-dose LLR having VE of 64.1% and 74.6%, respectively, against any severity of RVGE, and 86.6% and 73.4%, respectively, against severe RVGE.

These results are consistent with previous post-marketing studies reporting high VE of RV5 against rotavirus-related hospitalizations and emergency department visits [8]. The VE estimates for LLR are also in line with existing literature, which generally shows VE ranging from 35 to 53% against any severity of RVGE and 52–88% against severe RVGE after at least one dose [11, 12]. Our study adds to this body of evidence by evaluating the two vaccines in the same population and during the same time period.

Notably, we found that both RV5 and LLR provided significant cross-protection against the G8P[8] genotype, which has been increasingly detected in China and other regions [14–16, 18]. The VE estimates against any severity of G8 RVGE were 76.2% for RV5 and 59.5% for LLR, while the VE against severe G8 RVGE was 92.1% for RV5 and 62.8% for LLR. These findings suggest that the vaccines, particularly RV5, may confer broad protection against diverse rotavirus genotypes. It is also important to note that the present study was neither designed nor adequately powered to conduct comparisons between different vaccine types. As such, the observed differences in VE estimates across vaccine types, which were relatively modest, should be interpreted with caution. These variations may be attributable to several factors, including but not limited to the distinct age intervals for which each vaccine is scheduled. The RV5 vaccine is typically administered in early infancy, while LLR is often given to older infants and young children, with the latter not fully matching the pattern of rotavirus disease burden since children are most vulnerable during infancy [30, 31]. This temporal difference in administration could potentially influence the observed VE, as age-related factors such as maternal antibody interference, immune system maturity, and exposure history may play roles in vaccine response.

The findings of our study have important implications both clinically and policy-wise. First, despite the shifts in the prevailing genotypes of rotavirus in major cities in China, both RV5 and LLR remain effective. This is particularly important, as the emergence of novel rotavirus strains, such as G8P[8], has raised concerns about the continued effectiveness of the currently available vaccines [16, 18]. Second, given the sizeable effect of both types of vaccines but different age groups for which they are indicated, the choice of vaccine type should consider a broad array of factors instead of VE alone. RV5 is indicated for infants aged 6–32 weeks, while LLR is indicated for infants aged 2 months—3 years [23]. Our results show that both vaccines are highly effective. However, the cost of the vaccines and the healthcare infrastructure for vaccine delivery should also be considered when making decisions about vaccine choice. Third, the evidence suggests that even two doses of LLR are helpful if the third dose is infeasible. This finding is particularly relevant for settings in which achieving high coverage with a three-dose vaccine schedule may be challenging due to factors such as limited healthcare access, parental hesitancy, delayed first-dose initiation, or competing health priorities. In the meantime, our ability to accurately estimate the VE for the three-dose LLR schedule was limited by the sample size of individuals receiving this regimen. This limitation highlights the need for future large-scale studies specifically designed to evaluate the effectiveness of the three-dose LLR schedule. Such studies should aim to recruit a larger cohort of children who have received the complete three-dose series of LLR, potentially through national immunization databases.

The emergence of G8P[8] as a predominant genotype in major urban centers of China, coupled with the continued prevalence of G9P[8] in other regions, highlight the complex and dynamic nature of rotavirus epidemiology. This genotypic shift underscores the need for continuous monitoring of circulating strains and regular assessment of VE against emerging genotypes. While our study's sample size was insufficient to reliably evaluate VE against G9P[8], this constraint does not undermine the overall value of our findings. Our results contribute valuable insights into the performance of RV5 and LLR vaccines against evolving strains, particularly G8P[8].

Several limitations should be noted when interpreting the current findings. First, the study participants were recruited solely from Guangdong Province, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions in China or globally. Future research should aim to include a more geographically diverse sample to enhance the representativeness of the results. Second, the study did not collect data on breastfeeding practices and previous rotavirus infections, both of which are known to confer some degree of protection against RVGE. As such, these factors could potentially confound the observed association between vaccination and disease risk. To further elucidate the influence of these variables on the VE of rotavirus vaccines, future studies should consider incorporating data on breastfeeding practices and prior rotavirus infections into their analyses. Third, the number of individuals receiving three doses of LLR was relatively modest, potentially leading to imprecise VE estimates for this regimen. To obtain more robust and accurate insights, future analyses on the effectiveness of the three-dose LLR strategy should leverage national data repositories to ensure a substantial sample size of individuals adhering to this regimen. More, while our sensitivity analysis excluded cases with co-infections from four other enteropathogenic viruses, resource constraints precluded screening for enteropathogenic bacteria and parasites, which, although consistent with many rotavirus VE studies, may not capture the full spectrum of potential enteric co-infections. Lastly, the recent introduction of LLR3, a new rotavirus vaccine with a dosing schedule similar to RV5, warrants future research. Given its novelty, comprehensive effectiveness studies on LLR3 are not yet feasible. Future investigations comparing LLR3 to RV5 and the original LLR will be crucial to inform immunization schedules and programs among children.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that both RV5 and LLR provided substantial protection against RVGE, including cases caused by the emerging G8P[8] genotype. The three-dose schedule of RV5 showed strong effectiveness, while two doses of LLR also appeared to be a viable schedule to protect children against rotavirus infection. These data support the use of either vaccine type when making clinical recommendations and designing immunization programs.
