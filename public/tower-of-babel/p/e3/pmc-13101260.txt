The predictive effect of Kellgren-Lawrence grade and joint space height on pain and functional outcome
Authors: Anni Rajamäki, Lari Lehtovirta, Aleksi Reito, Mika Niemeläinen, Antti Eskelinen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlain radiographs play a key role in the diagnosis of knee osteoarthritis (OA), with the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grading system being widely used to assess radiographic severity of the disease. Previous studies have suggested that radiographically less severe knee OA may be associated with inferior outcomes following total knee replacement (TKA). This study aimed to evaluate whether preoperative radiological findings are predictive of postoperative Oxford Knee Score (OKS) and residual knee pain after primary TKA.MethodsWe retrospectively included all patients who underwent TKA at our high-volume joint replacement center in 2018. The primary outcome was the OKS collected at one year postoperatively. The secondary outcome was patient-reported knee pain at one year. Preoperative plain radiographs were graded using the KL classification, and joint space height was measured. Additional covariates included age, gender, BMI, ASA score, and specific comorbidities. Multivariable analyses were performed, and model performance was assessed using R2 and variable importance metrics.ResultsA total of 1,401 patients were included. Patients with less severe osteoarthritis had lower postoperative OKS compared to those with more advanced disease. Similarly, a greater joint space height was associated with smaller improvements in OKS following TKA. In multivariable models, the R2 was 0.125 for postoperative OKS and 0.075 for residual pain. KL grade, joint space height, and mechanical axis were combined as an interaction term.ConclusionIn this large single-center cohort, comprehensive clinical and radiographic data were analyzed using advanced statistical methods. Our findings support previous evidence suggesting that comparatively less severe radiographic OA is associated with less favorable outcomes after TKA. However, the limited predictive capacity of the models highlights the multifactorial nature of postoperative outcomes and the challenges faced in forecasting patient-reported results following TKA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09725-w.Keywords: TKA, Total knee arthroplasty, Oxford knee score, Kellgren-Lawrence, Postoperative pain, Osteoarthritis

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a cost-effective treatment for patients who have end stage knee osteoarthritis (OA). Nevertheless, approximately 10 to 20% of patients who undergo TKA continue to experience pain and fail to achieve a satisfactory outcome from the surgery [1–3]. Many factors for the poor outcome have been proposed, including high BMI, back pain and chronic pain in other joints, unrealistic expectations, and psychological factors [2–4].

Plain radiographs have an essential role in the diagnostics of knee OA, providing an objective tool for assessing the severity of OA [5]. The Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grading system was developed to systematically assess the radiological stage of OA from plain radiographs [6, 7]. However, variability between physicians when performing KL-grading [8] has been reported. Another method to assess the severity of OA is to measure the joint space narrowing [9].

In some cases, symptoms do not always correspond with the radiological severity of knee OA, resulting in patients who have radiologically less severe disease ending up undergoing TKA surgery. There has been some discrepancy in the previous literature regarding how the radiological severity of knee OA affects pain and satisfaction after TKA. For example, some studies suggest that patients with less severe OA end up experiencing more pain and feeling more disatisfied after TKA [10–16], whereas at least two studies have found that the radiological stage of the OA had no impact on postoperative PROM results [17, 18]. However, all the above mentioned studies had limited numbers of patients and the scales and outcomes used varied. Also, there is no earlier studies combining the information of the KL class, joint space height and measured mechanical axis from the plain radiograph.

Identification of those patients who are unlikely to benefit from surgery and remain in pain afterwards would lead to the better targeting of preoperative patient counseling. Furthermore, avoiding unnecessary surgery reduces suffering and risk for complications.

We aimed to assess how preoperative radiological findings (KL-grading, joint space narrowing) predict (1) Oxford Knee Score, and (2) residual knee pain at one year after TKA.

Materials and methods

Patient selection and dataset

In 2018, 2,143 TKA surgeries were performed at our institution. 627 patients underwent TKA surgery for both knees at different times. For these patients with bilateral knee OA, we included only the first TKA surgery in the study. There were a total of 1401 patients who had complete OKS and radiographic information available and were included into the final study cohort.

The dataset was obtained from a data pool at our institution that contains all the data related to each performed TKA surgery. The data included basic patient demographics and all preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative follow-up information. The gathered information for this study were age, gender, BMI, ASA class, diagnosis of depression, medial and lateral collateral stability of the knee, and knee range of motion.

The Oxford knee score (OKS) is a commonly used patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) after TKA. The OKS is useful for assessing the patient’s ability to function in daily life and the symptoms, especially pain, experienced in different daily activities [19–21]. The results of OKS questionnaires from the preoperative stage and one year postoperatively were combined into the data. OKS questionnaires consist of 12 questions which evaluate the function of the patient’s knee during the previous four weeks. The maximum total score for OKS is 48 points. The first question of the OKS concerns knee pain on a scale from 0 to 4 (0 being severe and 4 being none). This information was included as a separate outcome measure in addition to total OKS score. The minimal clinically important change (MCID) for OKS is 9 points at group level analysis and 7 points when comparing the change at the individual level [22].

Radiographic assessment

Preoperative plain radiographs from all patients were analyzed, and the severity of OA was determined using the Kellgren-Lawrence classification. The KL classification has a 5-level ranking for analysing knee OA, with 0 being a normal knee, 1 being mild OA, and 4 being the most severe form of knee OA. The classification accounts for joint space narrowing, osteophytes, sclerosis, and deformity of bone ends [7]. The height of the femorotibial joint space was also measured separately to measure cartilage loss. The mechanical axis was evaluated from long-leg radiographs, and a calibration item was used to evaluate the margin of error in the femorotibial joint space.

Radiographs were analysed using the Sectra (Sectra Medical Systems AB, Linköping, Sweden) digital PACS radiograph archive. All measurements were performed by a trained medical researcher (MM). Information for Kellgren-Lawrence assessment as well as height of the joint space and mechanical alignment was obtained from plain radiographs of the knee. From each standing fixed flexion view (FFV) anteroposterior knee radiograph, we determined the degree of OA using the Kellgren-Lawrence classification. To assess cartilage loss, we measured the height of the femorotibial joint space in millimeters from the narrowest point. The mechanical axis of the lower leg was also recorded from long-leg radiographs. We used a calibration item in the radiographs, which was calculated from the images, to evaluate the margin of error in the femorotibial joint space. An additional 100 patients were also analyzed by another medical researcher (PK). The intraclass correlation coefficient for the KL grading was 0.406 and 0.697 for joint space height.

Outcome

The outcomes of this study were (1) total OKS and (2) residual knee pain at one year after TKA. The residual knee pain was measured with the first question of OKS.

Data analyses

Crude analyses were done with linear regression with outcome 1 (Total OKS after 1 year) and with ordinal regression with outcome 2 (residual knee pain after one year). Final analyses for outcome 1 were performed with a linear regression model using multiple imputation for missing variables. In outcome 2, the ordinal regression model was also used with multiple imputation, as the variables were in ordinal scales.

KL classification, joint space height, and preoperative mechanical alignment were combined as an interaction term in regression models because they have an interaction with each other. Only KL classes 3 and 4 were included here as there were only 6 patients in the group of KL2. Age, gender, BMI, ASA class, extension deficit, flexion range, diagnosis of depression, and collateral stability were used as other covariates. A diagnosis of depression was determined as a previously recorded diagnosis in patient records or as the use of antidepressant medication during the previous 12 months. Collateral stability was assessed separately for both lateral and medial sides. Stability was divided into stable, slightly loose, and loose. In our data, this was further divided to either stable or non-stable, with the term stable meaning stable on both sides. If either side of the knee was assessed to be slightly loose or loose, it would be counted as non-stable.

R2 was used to assess overall model fit, and information on the most important variables were gathered from multivariable regression models.

All the analyses were performed with R Foundation for Statistical Computing (Vienna, Austria), using the rms, ggplot2, hrbrthemes, dplyr, tidyr, and viridis packages (Suppl. 1).

Results

In 2018, 2,143 TKA surgeries were performed at our institution. Of these, a total of 1401 patients who had undergone TKA and had complete OKS and radiographic information available were included. Most of the knees were preoperatively graded as KL 4 (1,117), 278 were KL grade 3, and only 6 were KL grade 2. At one year after surgery, median OKS was 43 points (IQR 38–46, range, 1–48). Further, at one year after TKA, 47% of patients reported no pain, 30% had very mild pain, 14% had mild pain, 7% had moderate pain, and 0.9% had severe pain (see Table 1 demographics).

Joint space height was found to decrease in line with increased KL grade. For KL grade 2, the median joint space height was 2.9 mm (IQR 2.5–3.5) and for KL grade 3 it was 1.8 mm (1.2–2.4). Total OKS after one year

Patients with KL grade 4 had higher postoperative OKS (median 44, IQR 39–47) compared to patients with KL grade 3 whose median OKS was 41 (IQR 36–45) (Fig. 3). In univariate analysis, a higher KL grade predicted higher postoperative OKS. In the multivariable model, the R2 value was 0.125 (Suppl. 2.). The statistically significant variables for predicting higher OKS were higher preoperative OKS, varus mechanical axis determined from radiographs, lower BMI, and higher KL grade (Figs. 4 and 5a-b).

Residual knee pain after one year

At one year after TKA, patients with milder preoperative knee OA (KL grade 2 or 3) reported residual knee pain more often (34%) than patients with KL grade 4 OA (20%). The multivariable ordinal regression model had an R2 value of 0.075, and the statistically significant variables for prediction of residual pain were lower KL grade, increased varus in preoperative mechanical axis, lower preoperative OKS, diagnosis of depression, and higher BMI (Fig. 6, Suppl. 3.).

Discussion

Our aim was to assess how preoperative radiological findings predict postoperative OKS and experienced pain. We found that there is an association with increasing number of patients with less severe knee OA (KL grade 2–3) who report poorer functional outcome and more residual knee pain after undergoing primary TKA. Even though joint space narrowing correlated well with KL grading and postoperative OKS, it did not emerge as an important variable in the multivariable model. However, combining information on the mechanical axis was useful and improved the model. The most important variables explaining poorer postoperative OKS were lower KL grade, higher BMI, and lower preoperative OKS. Interestingly, when investigating postoperative residual knee pain alone, the most important variables explaining residual pain were increasing preoperative malalignment (defined by measurement of the mechanical axis of the knee) and a diagnosis of depression.

Surgical decision-making for total knee arthroplasty is based on patient-reported symptoms as well as radiographic severity. Consequently, some patients may proceed to TKA despite comparatively less severe osteoarthritic changes on imaging. The rationale for such decisions is multifactorial and may reflect persistent pain and functional limitation despite adequate conservative management, symptom–radiograph discordance, patient expectations, or other clinical factors. Conflicting results have been reported in the previous literature regarding preoperative imaging findings and their ability to predict postoperative outcomes. In a systematic review and meta-analysis by Shohat et al. [16], the authors concluded that less severe radiographic findings were associated with increased pain and dissatisfaction after TKA [16]. This meta-analysis combined multiple studies that had utilized different methods for assessing radiographic findings and PROMs. Only three studies, however, evaluated the association between preoperative KL grading and postoperative OKS. Of these, the study by Tilbury et al. [23] found no association between OA severity and any PROM outcome [23]. In their study, they had 271 participants and used multivariable methods, including confounders such as age, sex, preoperative scores, and Charnley score. The remaining two studies by Niemeläinen et al. [10] (n = 227) and Peck et al. (n = 1,708) found that patients with less severe radiological changes of OA reported poorer PROMs [10, 12]. However, neither of these studies used multivariable methods, and in the study by Niemeläinen et al. the patient cohort consisted of patients aged 65 years or younger.

A major strength of our study is the large cohort from a single high-volume academic joint replacement hospital. Further, information on KL grading and joint space height was available and combined into preoperative factors known to affect postoperative dissatisfaction. A limitation of the study is that the subgroup of patients with milder OA was rather small, with only 6 patients with KL grade 2 and no patients with KL grade 1. Multivariable analysis included joint space narrowing as a linear factor. There was a clear difference between the KL grade 3 and KL grade 4 groups, as patients with radiographically more severe OA had higher OKS and experienced less pain postoperatively. It should be noted, however, that the difference in OKS between the KL 3 and KL 4 groups was less than the MCID value previously defined for the OKS [22]. Also, the overall prediction capability of the models were low as R2 was 0.125 for total OKS and 0.075 for the residual pain. As a consequence, this result highlights the multifactorial nature of postoperative outcomes where the radiographic finding is only one factor among with many other clinical factors. The clinical decision-making regarding TKA candidacy should be based on also the symptoms when there is an evidence of the clear osteoarthrosis radiographically.

A further limitation of this study is that we were unable to eliminate possible interobserver and intraobserver bias, as the evaluation of the radiographs was performed by only one person. However, subsample of 100 radiographs were also assessed by another researcher (PK).

Conclusion

In this large single-center cohort, comprehensive clinical and radiographic data were analyzed using advanced statistical methods. Our findings support previous evidence suggesting that less severe radiographic OA (KL3 relative to KL4) is associated with less favorable outcomes after TKA, and thus serve to clarify the consensus around this topic. The limited predictive capacity of the models highlights the multifactorial nature of postoperative outcomes and the challenges faced in forecasting patient-reported results following TKA. When considering TKA for patients with less severe OA, the patients should be counseled on the realistic expectations of the ouctomes.
