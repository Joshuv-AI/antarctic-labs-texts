Glymphatic dysfunction is associated with cognitive impairment in white matter hyperintensities: A DTI-based study
Authors: Baogen Du, Weiqiang Li, Xin Wang, Shihao Ruan, Wenchen Sun, Yuanyuan Liu, Shanshan Cao, Ying Zhang, Jinying Yang, Gong-Jun Ji, Xingqi Wu, Panpan Hu, Jun Zhang, Chunhua Xi, Kai Wang, Qiang Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe underlying mechanisms of cognitive impairment in patients with white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) remain largely elusive. Glymphatic dysfunction, as measured by the diffusion tensor imaging along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index, has been associated with cognitive decline across various neurological and psychiatric disorders.ObjectiveThis study aims to examine the potential association between glymphatic dysfunction, as measured by the DTI-ALPS index, and cognitive impairment in patients with WMHs.MethodsA total of 88 patients with varying degrees of WMHs (Mild = 25, Moderate = 34, Severe = 29) and 32 matched healthy controls were recruited. Comprehensive neuropsychological assessments and MRI scans were performed on all participants. We compared demographic information, cognitive function, and DTI-ALPS indices between patients with WMHs and controls, and evaluated the correlation between cognitive impairment and the DTI-ALPS index.ResultsApart from hypertension, demographic characteristics were comparable across the four groups. Patients with WMHs showed significant impairments in emotional status, global function, language function, memory function, processing speed and cognitive flexibility. A reduction in Left_ALPS, Right_ALPS, and Mean_ALPS was observed in patients with WMHs, with these decreases significantly and negatively correlated with the WMHs volume (Left_ALPS, R = −0.381, PFDR < 0.001; Right_ALPS, R = −0.423, PFDR < 0.001; and Mean_ALPS, R = −0.476, PFDR < 0.001). After FDR correction, Mean_ALPS remained significantly correlated with language function (S-VFT: R = 0.338, PFDR = 0.030), while trends were observed between Mean_ALPS and emotional status, global function, memory function, and processing speed.ConclusionCognitive impairment in patients with WMHs is closely linked to decreased DTI-ALPS index, especially as white matter lesions worsen. Glymphatic dysfunction may play a critical role in this cognitive impairment, positioning the DTI-ALPS index may serve as a potential biomarker for cognitive impairment in patients with WMHs.

Introduction

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) encompasses a range of pathological changes affecting the brain's small blood vessels (Jacob et al., 2023), leading to parenchymal damage and representing a key contributor to cognitive impairment and dementia (Wardlaw et al., 2019, Luo et al., 2025). The neuroimaging features of CSVD on Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) include white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), cerebral microbleeds, cerebral infarction, recent subcortical small infarcts, and perivascular spaces (Vermeer et al., 2003), all of which are essential for the diagnosis of CSVD, with WMHs being the most common and prominent markers (Li et al., 2023). It must be mentioned that CSVD patients exhibit considerable clinical and imaging heterogeneity, with symptoms ranging from cognitive and motor dysfunction to emotional and sleep disturbances (Tian et al., 2023), all of which can severely impair the quality of life. Although there have been ongoing studies exploring the possible mechanisms of CSVD cognitive impairment, such as blood-brain barrier dysfunction, cerebral arteriosclerosis, reduced cerebral blood flow, and neuroinflammation (Wardlaw et al., 2019), there is still a lack of effective intervention methods to improve cognitive impairment (W. Zhang et al., 2021).

Recent studies have identified a waste clearance system in the central nervous system, known as the glymphatic system (Brown et al., 2018, colleagues from the Fondation Leducq Transatlantic Network of Excellence on the Role of the Perivascular Space in Cerebral Small Vessel Disease, 2020), which facilitates the transport and elimination of metabolites via cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and interstitial fluid through the perivascular space (Iliff et al., 2012). This process is regulated by various physiological factors such as arterial pulsation, respiration, and heart rate (Li et al., 2022).

Traditionally, assessing glymphatic function required the use of intrathecal tracer injections, limiting the evaluation of the glymphatic system in humans until the development of a non-invasive method (Klostranec et al., 2021). Taoka et al. proposed a non-invasive approach for evaluating glymphatic function based on diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), which calculate diffusion along the perivascular space, known as the DTI-ALPS index (Taoka et al., 2017; Y. Zhang et al., 2021). This method measures the water diffusion rate along the perivascular space (PVS) by utilizing the anatomical orientation of the medullary vein (x-axis), association fibers (y-axis), and projection fibers (z-axis) at the level of the lateral ventricular body. The DTI-ALPS index is based on the assumption that water molecules moving along the medullary vein (x-axis) generate a pseudo-diffusion effect, leading to a higher apparent diffusion rate along the x-axis, whereas this effect is absent in directions perpendicular to the medullary vein (y-axis and z-axis). Therefore, the difference in apparent diffusivity between the medullary vein direction (x-axis) and the directions perpendicular to the PVS (y-axis and z-axis) reflects the flow within the PVS, serving as an indirect indicator of glymphatic function (Zhang et al., 2024).

Abnormal glymphatic system function is increasingly recognized as a pathophysiological mechanism underlying the progression of CSVD (Brown et al., 2018, Zhou et al., 2024). Moreover, studies have frequently observed enlarged perivascular spaces (PVS) surrounding WMHs, further implicating glymphatic dysfunction in WMHs (Huang et al., 2021, Huo et al., 2023). Currently, relevant studies have shown that the reduced DTI-ALPS index is related to a series of neurological diseases, such as neurodegenerative diseases (Chen et al., 2021, Hsu et al., 2023, Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration Neuroimaging Initiative, 2023), stroke (Toh and Siow, 2021), headache (Zhang et al., 2023), etc. (Bae et al., 2023, Siow et al., 2022, Tu et al., 2023). While some studies have begun to examine the association between DTI-ALPS and CSVD (Hong et al., 2024, Li et al., 2024), current research mainly focuses on the relationship between DTI-ALPS and neuroimaging changes with CSVD (Lee et al., 2024; W. Zhang et al., 2021), with few studies have comprehensively investigated the link between glymphatic dysfunction and multidimensional cognitive impairment in patients with WMHs. Therefore, our study aims to investigate the relationship between glymphatic function and multidimensional cognitive impairment in patients with WMHs based on DTI-ALPS.

Materials and methods

Participants

A total of 88 patients with WMHs were recruited from the Neurology Outpatient Department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1). age between 40 and 80 years; 2). presence of high signal intensity in the white matter on brain imaging, with paraventricular and deep white matter lesions confirmed by experienced neurologists using T2FLAIR and T2WI sequences; 3). No obvious hand movement dysfunction, can cooperate to complete neuropsychological assessment and magnetic resonance data collection. The exclusion criteria were: 1). intracranial or extracranial vascular stenosis greater than 50%; 2). cardiogenic stroke classified by the TOAST criteria (Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment); 3). non-CSVD-related WMHs (e.g., multiple sclerosis); 4). other neurological, psychiatric, or severe systemic diseases, malignant tumors, epilepsy; 5). contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans (such as the presence of teeth or metal implants, claustrophobia, etc.) 6). Severe visual, auditory, and language impairments. Additionally, 32 healthy controls matched for age, gender, and education level were recruited based on the same exclusion criteria. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University, and all participants provided written informed consent. The demographic information, neuropsychological assessment, and MRI data of the participants were collected on the same day.

Neuropsychological assessments

All participants completed a comprehensive battery of neuropsychological assessments to evaluate emotional and cognitive function. Specifically, the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD7) and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ9) were used to assess emotional status. The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) was used to evaluate global cognition. Language function was assessed using the Verbal Fluency Test (VFT) and the Boston Naming Test (BNT). Memory function was evaluated using the Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT), which included immediate memory, delayed memory, and recognition components. Processing speed was assessed with the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT) and the Trail Making Test-A (TMT-A). Attention function was measured using the Digit Span-forward (DS-forward) test, while working memory was assessed using the Digit Span-backward (DS-backward) test. Cognitive flexibility was evaluated using the Trail Making Test-B (TMT-B). The neuropsychological assessments were administered by professionally trained neurology graduate students.

MRI acquisition

Both structural and diffusion MRI scans were obtained using a 3.0 T scanner (Discovery 750; GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI). During the scanning process, participants were asked to rest with their eyes closed and not fall asleep or think. Evaluation of CSVD marker

WMHs were qualitatively evaluated using the Fazekas scale, which is based on T2FLAIR images. This scale evaluates both periventricular and subcortical deep WMH, with a scoring range from 1 to 6 (Hope et al., 2013).For the quantitative extraction of WMHs volume, the UBO Detector was utilized (https://cheba.unsw.edu.au/research-groups/neuroimaging/quick-start-manual). The specific procedural steps involved in this process include co registration, T1 segmentation, registration to DARTEL, removal of non-brain tissue, FAST segmentation, and subsequently, the calculation of WMHs volume based on a k-nearest neighbor-based algorithm (Jiang et al., 2018).

Cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) were defined as small, round, or oval-shaped low signal events on ESWAN images (Gregoire et al., 2009), the total number of CMBs were recorded in four regions: subcortical, deep, infratentorial, and other brain areas.

Lacunes were defined as a cerebrospinal fluid-filled cavities measuring 3–15 millimeters in diameter, exhibiting cerebrospinal fluid signal on T2WI and a high signal edge on T2-FLAIR (Pasi et al., 2017), the total number of lacunes were recorded in four regions: subcortical, deep, infratentorial, and other brain areas.

PVS was defined as a linear, round, or oval regions of signal with signal intensity similar to that of cerebrospinal fluid across all sequences. Based on T2WI, the number of centro semiovale and basal ganglia regions were identified and classified into grades 1–4 (Doubal et al., 2010).

Processing of DTI-ALPS index

Structural and diffusion images were processed using the software WhiteMatterSF toolbox (https://github.com/jigongjun/Neuroimaging-and-Neuromodulation/blob/main/WMfun/WhiteMatter.m) (Ji et al., 2023), which includes SPM12 (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12), and FSL6.0.4(http://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl). The data preprocessing steps included head motion correction, bone flap removal, and segmentation, co-registering individual diffusion data and T1-weighted images, followed by eddy current correction and tensor fitting to generate the diffusion rate map.

At the level of lateral ventricles, the PVS runs horizontally from left to right, perpendicular to the lateral ventricular wall. In this position, the projection fibers of the cortex are oriented cranially, while the association fibers (superior longitudinal fasciculus) extend posteriorly. These three structures near the lateral ventricle are perpendicular to each other. Since the fiber bundles in this area are not aligned with the PVS, the diffusion coefficient along the PVS can be independently analyzed in this area. The DTI-ALPS is calculated using a previously published method (W. Zhang et al., 2021). The diffusion rate maps (Dxx, Dyy, Dzz) of each participant are obtained from the preprocessed DTI data, and registered to the standard space. Based on previous study, four regions of interest (ROIs), each with a 6 millimeters diameter, were placed in Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space with coordinate centers of (24,−12, 24), ((-28,−12, 24) (3(36,−12, 24)and (-4(-40,−12, 24)espectively. The average diffusion coefficients were calculated along the x-axis and y-axis, as well as the x-axis and z-axis directions on the projected and association fibers, respectively (donated as Dxxproj, Dxxassoc, Dyyproj, and Dzzassoc). The DTI-ALPS index for each side was calculated as [Dxxproj+Dxxassoc)/(Dyyproj+Dzzassoc)], and the mean DTI-ALPS (Mean_ALPS) was obtained by averaging the bilateral DTI-ALPS index. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 (v20.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, United States). For general demographic data, neuropsychological assessments, and DTI-ALPS index, appropriate tests were used depending on data distribution, including chi square test, kruskal-wallis test, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to analyze intergroup differences. Post hoc analyses were conducted for variables with significant differences to further clarify the group comparisons. In addition, Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses were applied based on data characteristics. To adjust for multiple comparisons, the False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to p-values, converting them to PFDR values. PFDR =p × n/rank, where “rank” represents the order of p-values from smallest to largest. A two-tailed p-value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, we used a battery of neuropsychological assessments to comprehensively evaluate the characteristics of cognitive impairment in WMHs patients and quantitatively assess the severity of white matter lesions in patients of different grades. Then, DTI-ALPS method was used to analyze the lymphatic system function of WMH patients, and further explored its correlation with the degree of white matter lesions and cognitive impairment. Our founding revealed that the DTI-ALPS index of patients with WMHs was significantly lower compared to the control group. Moreover, there was a strong correlation between the left, right, and mean DTI-ALPS index of patients with WMHs and the degree of white matter lesions. Additionally, we observed that impaired glymphatic transport, indicated by low mean DTI-ALPS index, was closely associated with cognitive impairment in patients with WMHs.

As a key component of vascular cognitive impairment, it is widely recognized that CSVD patients often experience concomitant cognitive impairment. However, the exact mechanisms underlying cognitive impairment in CSVD patients remain unclear. Further exploration of these mechanism is crucial for developing targeted treatments and improving patients' quality of life. In addition to well-known factors such as ischemia and hypoxia, inflammation, and blood-brain barrier disruption (Ip et al., 2024), growing attention has been given to the dysfunction of glymphatic function in CSVD patients. The brain's glymphatic system plays a vital role in promoting cerebrospinal fluid flow into the brain and clearing metabolic waste (Zeng et al., 2024). During this clearance process, cerebrospinal fluid flows into the brain interstitium through the vascular gaps around the arteries via aquaporin-4, completing the exchange between cerebrospinal fluid and interstitial fluid. It then drains through the perivascular spaces around the veins, removing metabolic waste (Rasmussen et al., 2018). When this system is impaired, the accumulation of metabolic waste and inflammatory cells may occur (Carotenuto et al., 2022), leading to neuronal swelling and death, which in turn affects brain structure and function (Hsu et al., 2023). Dysfunction of the glymphatic system is increasingly linked to various neurological diseases, such as AD (Taoka et al., 2017), PD (Si et al., 2022), cerebrovascular disease (Zeng et al., 2024), etc. (Butler et al., 2023). The perivascular space is the primary pathway for brain glymphatic drainage. The DTI-ALPS index provides an indirect assessment of glymphatic function by calculating the diffusion rate of water molecules within projection fibers, association fibers, and the perivascular space at the level of the lateral ventricle (Li et al., 2024). This method has shown strong concordance with glymphatic function assessed by contrast agent injection, as confirmed in previous studies (Liu et al. 2024; W. Zhang et al., 2021).

It is important to note that the DTI-ALPS index can be influenced by the imaging plane and the placement of ROI (Qiu et al., 2025). Manually placing ROIs may introduce variability between subjects, potentially affect the group consistency. Specifically, a study on the reproducibility of ALPS index suggests that changes in imaging plane and head position significantly reduce the reproducibility of the index, leading to a decrease in intraclass correlation coefficient to 0.19 and 0.22, respectively(Taoka et al., 2022). In this study, we used a published ROI based on a standard spatial template (Hong et al., 2024), which showed higher consistency compared to traditional manual methods for calculating the DTI-ALPS index (W. Zhang et al., 2021). Additionally, previous studies on cognitive impairment have reported inconsistent findings regarding DTI-ALPS indices between the left and right sides. Some studies have shown a reduction in DTI-ALPS only occurs on the left side (Taoka et al., 2017), while others have observed a significant decrease on the right side (Steward et al., 2021). These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in the study populations. To minimize the influence of lateral differences, we used the mean DTI-ALPS index in subsequent correlation analyses.

Previous studies have shown that the dysfunction of glymphatic system is associated with white matter lesion, likely due to the accumulation of toxic substances leading to elevated white matter signal intensity (Carotenuto et al., 2022). Our findings are consistent with this, as we also identified a correlation between the DTI-ALPS index and the severity of white matter lesion. Specifically, as the WMHs volume increased, the DTI-ALPS index decreased significantly. Additionally, prior research on CSVD has primarily focused on the effects of CSVD-related biomarkers on glymphatic system activity (W. Zhang et al., 2021) or exploring the temporal changes of DTI-ALPS (Li et al., 2024). Although some studies have investigated the relationship between glymphatic function and cognitive impairment, there are still relatively few, and most have employed limited cognitive assessment tools, resulting in an incomplete evaluation of cognitive function (Hong et al., 2024, Tian et al., 2023). In contrast, our study comprehensively assessed global cognition, various cognitive domains, and emotional function in patients with WMHs, exploring their correlation with the DTI-ALPS index.

An increasing number of studies highlight the relationship between DTI-ALPS index and cognitive impairment. A study of 133 CSVD patients found an independent correlation between DTI-ALPS and cognitive impairment (Tang et al., 2022). Similarly, a recent study has demonstrated that DTI-ALPS mediates episodic memory in patients with WMHs, suggesting the involvement of the glymphatic system in WMH-related cognitive impairment (Ke et al., 2022). These findings align with our own results, where we observed that even after FDR correction, a significant correlation between Mean_ALPS and language function remained, along with trends suggesting associations with global cognition, memory, executive function, and processing speed in patients with WMHs. The dysfunction of glymphatic function may indicate disruption of central nervous system homeostasis (Weller et al., 2008), and a lower DTI-ALPS index could reflect damage to neurons involved in cognitive function (Zhang et al., 2024), potentially explaining cognitive impairment in patients with WMHs. Therefore, our findings suggest that the DTI-ALPS index may serve as a potential biomarker for cognitive impairment in patients with WMHs.

It is important to acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, as a cross-sectional study with a relatively small sample size, the statistical power may be somewhat limited. Second, the DTI-ALPS index in this study was calculated based on four predefined ROIs, which may only reflect a portion of glymphatic function. Third, the investigation of demographic information is not detailed enough, such as a lack of detailed evaluation of exercise, BMI index, sleep quality, medication use, and undetected comorbidities. Fourth, although the participants in this study were able to cooperate in completing cognitive assessments, there was a lack of detailed evaluations of motor function, especially hand motor function, which are equally important for assessing cognitive function. Fifth, although DTI-ALPS has been widely used to evaluate glymphatic function, it must be acknowledged that it is not a direct measurement method. At the same time, it must be recognized that individual differences in DTI acquisition parameters, image processing techniques, or brain structure can affect the calculation of DTI-ALPS index, which may lead to misunderstandings. Sixth, all participants were scanned during the day, and circadian rhythms may influence glymphatic activity. Additionally, physiological factors such as sleep, respiration, and pulse, which can also affect glymphatic function, were not specifically examined in this study. Finally, the prevalence of WMHs and the severity of cognitive impairment may vary significantly among different age groups, ethnic groups, and populations with different risk factors, which may limit the generalizability of research findings. Therefore, it is planned to conduct multi-center, longitudinal studies in the future, strictly controlling these variables to validate the research results, and further validate their sensitivity and specificity through multimodal imaging such as DCE-MRI, IVIM, etc., in order to better explore the regulation of lymphatic function and cognitive impairment in patients with WMHs.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that a decrease in the DTI-ALPS index is closely linked to cognitive impairment in patients with WMHs, particularly as white matter lesions progress. These findings suggest that glymphatic dysfunction may contribute to cognitive decline, with the DTI-ALPS index potentially serving as a biomarker for cognitive impairment in this population. Future longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes, multi-center, and multi-ethnic cohorts, along with multimodal imaging, are needed to further validate these findings and explore the broader clinical applicability of DTI-ALPS.
