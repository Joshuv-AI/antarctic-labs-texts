Lactylation in cancer biology: Unlocking new avenues for research and therapy
Authors: Xiaoyu Hou, Zhenya Hong, Huimin Zeng, Changyi Zhang, Peng Zhang, Ding Ma, Zhiqiang Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLactylation, a newly identified post‐translational modification, plays a multifaceted role in cancer biology by integrating epigenetic and non‐epigenetic mechanisms. This review summarizes the latest research progress on lactylation, including its functions in epigenetic regulation and its broader impact on cellular processes. Lactate, as a metabolic byproduct, not only serves as an energy source for tumor cells but also acts as a signaling molecule driving various oncogenic processes. Lactylation facilitates cancer metabolic reprogramming, enabling tumor cells to adapt to hypoxic and nutrient‐deprived microenvironments. Moreover, lactylation mediates immune suppression in the tumor microenvironment, promoting immune evasion and therapy resistance. This review further explores the clinical potential of targeting lactylation, offering new avenues for innovation in cancer research and treatment. These findings highlight the pivotal role of lactylation in cancer progression and its significant value as a potential therapeutic target.Keywords: Lactylation, Post‐translational modification, Tumor microenvironment, Metabolic reprogramming

INTRODUCTION

The occurrence and development of tumors is a complex biological process involving multiple aspects such as gene mutations, changes in cell signaling pathways, and interactions within the cellular microenvironment. The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a key regulatory system in the growth and progression of tumors, which includes not only tumor cells but also immune cells, stromal cells, blood vessels, and various secreted factors and metabolic products [1]. Tumor cells adapt to this complex environment through metabolic reprogramming, one of the most notable features being the Warburg effect [2, 3], which refers to the preference for producing lactate through glycolysis under aerobic conditions rather than obtaining energy through mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). Lactate, as the main product of glycolysis, has long been considered a metabolic waste [4]. However, an increasing number of studies indicate that lactate has various biological functions, including serving as a primary fuel to provide energy in multiple scenarios [5, 6], and playing a crucial role as a signaling molecule in regulating tumor progression, immune evasion, inflammatory responses, and metabolic reprogramming [7, 8].

Although cancer is typically regarded as a genetic disease, epigenetic abnormalities play a profound and widespread role. First introduced by British embryologist Conrad Waddington in 1942, the term “epigenetics” refers to heritable changes in gene expression that occur without alterations to the primary DNA sequence [9]. Proper gene expression relies not only on DNA sequences but also on epigenetic information. Heritable changes in the epigenome can disrupt proper DNA modification patterns, alter nucleosome positioning, and induce histone modifications without changing the primary DNA base sequence, thereby influencing gene expression [10, 11]. Key epigenetic modifications include DNA methylation, histone post‐translational modifications, and chromatin remodeling [11]. Recent discoveries have identified lactylation as a novel form of epigenetic modification, in which lactate acts as an acyl donor to modify lysine residues on histones, impacting chromatin structure, gene expression, protein function, and stability [12]. Notably, lactylation has been shown to regulate gene expression and signaling pathways, such as activating platelet‐derived growth factor receptor β (PDGFRβ) transcription, playing a critical oncogenic role in promoting tumor progression and establishing positive feedback loops [13].

Metabolic reprogramming profoundly influences epigenetic regulatory mechanisms by altering metabolite concentrations and metabolic pathways. As a key metabolite, lactate impacts epigenetic marks, such as histone acetylation, deacetylation, and DNA methylation, through lactylation modifications [14, 15]. Tumor cells adapt to changes in the TME and sustain their proliferative and invasive capacities by modulating these epigenetic modifications. Moreover, lactylation interacts with other metabolic pathways in tumor cells, including redox balance, amino acid metabolism, and lipid metabolism, further driving metabolic reprogramming and ensuring survival under hypoxic and nutrient‐deprived conditions [16, 17, 18]. Beyond tumor cells, lactylation significantly affects other components of the TME. High lactate accumulation in the TME modulates the functions of immune and stromal cells through lactylation [19, 20]. For instance, elevated lactate concentrations suppress cytotoxic T cells and natural killer (NK) cells' activity, thereby weakening antitumor immune responses [21, 22]. Additionally, lactylation regulates tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) polarization, promoting their conversion to the tumor‐supportive M2 phenotype, which facilitates tumor growth and metastasis [12]. These findings highlight lactylation as more than a byproduct of energy metabolism, it serves as a signaling molecule and regulatory factor mediating the intricate interactions between tumors and their microenvironment.

The interaction between lactylation‐mediated epigenetic regulation and metabolic reprogramming has emerged as a critical frontier in tumor biology research. Lactylation not only influences gene expression and metabolic pathways within tumor cells but also shapes the complex metabolic and signaling networks of TME by regulating the functions of immune and stromal cells. These findings provide new insights into the interplay between tumor metabolism and epigenetics, offering a theoretical foundation for developing tumor therapies targeting metabolic regulation and epigenetic modifications. In this review, we systematically summarize recent advances in lactylation research, focusing on its regulatory mechanisms, roles in tumor and immune cells, and emerging therapeutic implications. This structured overview aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of lactylation's contribution to cancer progression and its potential as a clinical target.

THE ROLE OF LACTATE IN TUMOR CELLS

The production and transport of lactate

In the TME, lactate is not merely a metabolic byproduct but a multifunctional molecule with widespread effects on tumor cell survival, proliferation, immune evasion, and metastasis [23]. Beyond serving as an energy source through metabolic pathways, lactate also regulates tumor progression and reshapes the TME through various mechanisms (Figure 1).

Pyruvate can either enter the TCA cycle for further energy production or be converted to lactate by LDHA, while lactate can also be converted back to pyruvate via LDHB. Lactate is transported into cells via MCT‐1 and exported via MCT‐4. Once inside the cell, lactate can be converted into lactyl‐CoA by ACSS2 and GTPSCS, which serves as a donor for enzymatic lysine lactylation catalyzed by “writers” (e.g., p300/CBP). Alternatively, lactate can be directly incorporated into proteins via AARS1/2 without conversion to lactyl‐CoA. Lactylation occurs on both histone and non‐histone proteins. Lactylation can be reversed by “erasers,” such as HDACs and SIRTs, or be recognized by “readers,” such as DPF2 and Brg1. Additionally, lactate can activate GPR81 signaling, which promotes PI3K/AKT pathway activation and suppresses apoptosis in cancer cells. Moreover, MGO, a by‐product of glycolysis, is converted to LGSH by GLO1, and further metabolized to D‐lactate by GLO2. LGSH can mediate non‐enzymatic lactylation by directly transferring its lactyl group to lysine residues, resulting in Kla formation without enzyme involvement. The production of lactate and the Warburg effect

Lactate, an important organic acid with the chemical formula C3H6O3, is widely present in biological systems, particularly during metabolic processes. Due to the asymmetry of its carbon atoms, lactate exists as three isomers: L‐lactate, D‐lactate, and a racemic mixture (i.e., DL‐lactate) [24]. L‐lactate is the predominant form in mammals, whereas D‐lactate is considered an atypical mammalian metabolite [25]. Lactate is primarily generated through glycolysis, an anaerobic metabolic pathway. Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate is typically transported into the mitochondria, where it is oxidized into acetyl‐CoA and enters the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle for complete oxidation, producing more adenosine triphosphate (ATP). However, under hypoxic or anoxic conditions—or during high metabolic demand, such as intense exercise or septic shock—glucose undergoes glycolysis to produce two molecules of pyruvate [26, 27]. In these circumstances, the TCA cycle is inhibited, preventing the complete oxidation of pyruvate. Instead, pyruvate is reduced to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), generating two ATP molecules and one molecule of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) [28]. This process is crucial for maintaining energy balance under hypoxic conditions. Besides glycolysis, glutamine metabolism also contributes to lactate production [23]. Under cellular myelocytomatosis oncogene (c‐Myc) regulation, glutamine enters cells via specific amino acid transporters and undergoes enzymatic reactions to form α‐ketoglutarate, which feeds into the TCA cycle. The carbon backbone derived from glutamine is then converted to pyruvate and ultimately lactate, providing an alternative source of lactate for cancer cells.

In addition to endogenous production, certain commensal microorganisms, such as Lactobacillus species, can also generate large amounts of lactate through fermentation as a major metabolic end‐product [29]. These bacteria predominantly produce D‐lactate via stereospecific D‐lactate dehydrogenases [30], and are particularly active in mucosal environments such as the gut and female reproductive tract [31, 32]. Although D‐lactate is not a common mammalian metabolite, it can accumulate under pathological or dysbiotic conditions and exert physiological effects on the host. The stereoisomeric forms of lactate—namely, L‐ and D‐lactate—may differentially influence downstream pathways such as receptor interactions and epigenetic regulation. Notably, an increasing number of studies have demonstrated that D‐lactate and L‐lactate can lead to distinct patterns and functional outcomes of protein lactylation [33], although whether microbially derived D‐lactate participates in host protein lactylation remains unclear.

Under normal cellular respiration, glucose undergoes oxidation in the presence of oxygen, entering the TCA cycle to perform OXPHOS, which produces minimal lactate. However, Warburg discovered that tumor cells predominantly produce lactate through glycolysis, even under aerobic conditions—a phenomenon termed the “Warburg effect” [2]. The Warburg effect is characterized by rapid metabolism, consuming large amounts of glucose in the TME, synthesizing precursors necessary for tumor proliferation, and secreting lactate to create an immunosuppressive microenvironment conducive to tumor growth and metastasis [34]. Further research has shown that the metabolic reprogramming underlying the Warburg effect is driven by oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. Normally, hypoxia‐inducible factor 1‐alpha (HIF‐1α) is stabilized under hypoxic conditions, acting as a transcription factor to promote glycolysis and inhibit OXPHOS. Certain oncogenes, such as protein kinase B (AKT), phosphatidylinositol 3‐kinase (PI3K), rat sarcoma virus (RAS), and von Hippel‐Lindau (VHL), regulate HIF‐1α, allowing its expression even under normoxic conditions [35]. When HIF‐1α is present, it upregulates glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) to enhance glucose retention and increases the expression of hexokinase 2 (HK2), which catalyzes the first step of glycolysis [36]. In normoxic conditions, HIF‐1α is typically degraded as oxygen levels rise. However, in tumor cells, the PI3K/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway is frequently activated, further enhancing the expression of HIF‐1α and c‐Myc [37, 38, 39, 40]. This leads to the persistent transcription of enzymes involved in glycolysis and lactate production. Together, c‐Myc and HIF‐1α accelerate key rate‐limiting steps in glycolysis, inhibit pyruvate entry into the TCA cycle, and promote its conversion to lactate through LDHA. Concurrently, c‐Myc and HIF‐1α upregulate LDHA while suppressing lactate dehydrogenase B (LDHB), further driving lactate production [41]. Lactate, generated as a result of enhanced glycolysis driven by HIF‐1α and c‐Myc, can in turn activate c‐Myc, thereby promoting glutaminolysis and forming a positive feedback loop that exacerbates lactate accumulation [42]. In addition, the inactivation of p53 contributes to the Warburg effect. Reduced p53 expression increases the expression of GLUT and downregulates glycolysis and apoptosis regulator (TIGAR), redirecting glucose flux from the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) to glycolysis, thereby further enhancing glycolysis [35, 43].

Lactate shuttling via monocarboxylate transporter proteins

The transcellular transport of lactate primarily depends on the monocarboxylate transporter (MCT) protein family. MCTs belong to the solute carrier family 16 (SLC16) of transmembrane transport proteins, with 14 members identified to date [44]. These transporters play a crucial role in maintaining metabolic homeostasis both within tumor cells and in the TME [45]. They also significantly influence tumor survival, proliferation, and invasion by modulating lactate levels in the TME [45]. Among the MCT family members, monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT‐1) and monocarboxylate transporter 4 (MCT‐4) are the most critical in tumor metabolism. MCT‐1 primarily facilitates lactate uptake, particularly in tumor cells reliant on OXPHOS. These cells can import lactate from the TME via MCT‐1, convert it back to pyruvate through LDHB, and utilize it in the TCA cycle to generate energy [45, 46]. Lactate uptake is particularly important in tissues with high tumor heterogeneity [45]. Conversely, MCT‐4 predominantly exports the large quantities of lactate generated by glycolysis, a critical function in highly glycolytic tumor cells [45]. This prevents intracellular lactate accumulation and subsequent acidosis. MCT‐4 expression is typically regulated by HIF‐1α, reflecting metabolic adaptations to hypoxic environments [47, 48]. Through its export function, MCT‐4 contributes to lactate accumulation in the TME, leading to local acidification that promotes immune evasion and tumor invasiveness. Acidification of the TME activates matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which degrade the extracellular matrix, facilitating tumor invasion [49, 50, 51]. Additionally, the acidic microenvironment induces epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT), enhancing tumor cell motility and invasiveness, thereby increasing metastatic potential [52, 53]. Basigin (CD147), as an ancillary protein for MCT‐1 and MCT‐4, supports lactate shuttling by maintaining their expression and function, thereby promoting metabolic adaptation and malignant potential in tumor cells under the Warburg effect [54].

Tumor cells often exhibit a “metabolic symbiosis” model, wherein glycolysis‐active tumor cells expel lactate via MCT‐4, while neighboring OXPHOS‐active tumor cells uptake this lactate through MCT‐1 for further metabolism and ATP production [55]. This metabolic complementarity enables tumor cells to survive collectively in resource‐limited environments and drives tumor progression. Such metabolic flexibility provides tumors with a survival advantage in diverse microenvironments, facilitating proliferation and expansion. Overall, lactate shuttling via MCT proteins between tumor cells and the TME not only maintains metabolic homeostasis but also reshapes the acidic TME, influencing immune evasion, invasion, and metastasis.

Lactate as an energy source for tumor cells

In tumor cells, lactate serves not only as an end‐product of glycolysis but also as a metabolically valuable fuel, especially under nutrient‐deprived or hypoxic conditions. Through metabolic reprogramming, cancer cells utilize lactate to sustain proliferation and survival by channeling it into energy production and biosynthesis.

Tumor tissues are composed of various cell types, including tumor cells, cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and immune cells, which collectively support tumor growth, migration, invasion, and metastasis through intricate molecular mechanisms [56]. The TME features an aberrant vascular system, creating regions with differing oxygen availability. Oxygen‐rich regions are typically occupied by OXPHOS‐active normoxic tumor cells, whereas hypoxic regions are dominated by glycolysis‐active hypoxic tumor cells [57]. A metabolic symbiosis often exists between these two cell populations, wherein hypoxic tumor cells increase glucose uptake for energy production and secrete lactate as a byproduct, which is subsequently utilized by normoxic tumor cells as an energy source [55]. This metabolic symbiosis facilitates lactate sharing between tumor cells, linking anaerobic glycolysis and aerobic oxidation. Consequently, this cooperative strategy enhances the metabolic efficiency of tumor cells, enabling them to adapt and thrive in a dynamic environment.

Similarly, tumor cells adjacent to stromal cells can also undergo aerobic glycolysis [58]. The reverse Warburg effect demonstrates how tumor cells establish a metabolic symbiosis with CAFs [58]. CAFs are the primary stromal cell population in the TME and play a crucial role in providing nutrients to tumor cells [59]. In the reprogrammed TME, tumor cells induce CAFs to switch to an “aerobic glycolysis” metabolic mode, leading to lactate production. During this process, CAFs consume glucose at high rates, resulting in the production of excess lactate. This excess lactate is expelled from CAFs via MCT‐4 and subsequently taken up by normoxic tumor cells through MCT‐1. The lactate is converted into pyruvate with the help of LDHB, which then serves as fuel for tumor cell functions.

In summary, lactate enhances tumor metabolic adaptability by serving as both a fuel and a biosynthetic substrate. Its reutilization in the TME supports tumor progression and highlights the therapeutic relevance of disrupting lactate recycling in cancer.

Lactate signaling through G protein‐coupled receptors (GPCRs)

Beyond its metabolic role, lactate also functions as a signaling molecule in the tumor microenvironment. Through the activation of GPCRs such as G protein‐coupled receptor 81 (GPR81), lactate regulates both tumor cell behavior and stromal interactions, contributing to malignant progression [60].

The relationship between GPR81 and tumors was first reported in 2014, with high expression of GPR81 closely associated with pancreatic cancer growth and metastasis [60]. Silencing GPR81 significantly inhibited the proliferation of pancreatic cancer cells. Glycolytic tumor cells secrete large amounts of lactate into the TME, where lactate interacts with GPR81 on both tumor and non‐tumor cells, including dendritic cells (DCs), T cells, and macrophages [61, 62, 63]. The tumor‐promoting effects of GPR81 are linked to multiple signaling pathways: 1. Angiogenesis and apoptosis: Studies have shown that GPR81 is highly expressed in breast cancer [64, 65]. Inhibition of GPR81 signaling reduces the production of amphiregulin (AREG) via the PI3K/AKT/cAMP response element‐binding protein (CREB) signaling pathway, suppressing tumor‐associated angiogenesis and inducing cancer cell apoptosis [65]. 2. Immune evasion: Lactate induces programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) expression through GPR81, enhancing immune evasion in the lactate‐rich TME [66]. Silencing GPR81 reduces PD‐L1 levels and inhibits PD‐L1 promoter activity. The upregulation of PD‐L1 induced by lactate is dependent on LDHA and involves the activation of the transcriptional coactivator with PDZ‐binding motif (TAZ)‐TEA domain family member (TEAD) complex through the suppression of protein kinase A (PKA) activity. Lactate‐induced PD‐L1 activation further reduces interferon‐γ production and promotes T‐cell apoptosis, underscoring the critical role of lactate in tumor immune escape. 3. DNA repair and drug resistance: GPR81 promotes DNA repair activity and chemoresistance via the ATP‐binding cassette sub‐family B member 1 (ABCB1)‐mediated activation of the G protein alpha inhibitory subunit (Gαi)‐protein kinase C (PKC)‐extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK) pathway [67]. Additionally, GPR81 activation upregulates DNA repair proteins, including breast cancer type 1 susceptibility protein (BRCA1), nibrin, and DNA‐dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA‐PKcs), enhancing the cellular DNA repair response [68].

In addition to GPR81, lactate may regulate cellular behaviors in the TME through other GPCRs, such as GPR132. Studies in breast cancer and Lewis lung cancer models have shown that lactate signaling through GPR132 in TAMs promotes the pro‐tumorigenic M2 phenotype [69, 70]. Although the specific mechanisms of these GPCRs in lactate signaling remain to be fully elucidated, they are likely to play significant roles in tumor metabolic reprogramming, immune suppression, and tissue invasion.

In summary, lactate‐GPCR signaling plays a central role in coordinating tumor cell metabolism and shaping the immune and stromal microenvironment to support tumor progression. Therapeutically, disrupting this pathway offers potential to impair tumor‐promoting interactions and enhance immunotherapeutic efficacy.

LACTYLATION IN CANCER: A NOVEL POST‐TRANSLATIONAL MODIFICATION (PTM)

First reported in 2019, lactate accumulated during metabolism can directly participate in histone lysine lactylation (Kla), a PTM that regulates gene expression and immune microenvironment homeostasis [12]. This discovery expanded the understanding of lactate from a mere metabolic byproduct to a regulator of PTMs. A study has shown that 2,375 Kla sites across 1,014 proteins were identified in gastric cancer AGS cells. Kla levels were higher in tumors compared to adjacent tissues, with elevated Kla levels correlating with poor prognosis [71]. Similarly, analyses in hepatocellular carcinoma identified 9,275 and 2,045 Kla sites in cancerous and adjacent normal tissues, respectively [72, 73]. In oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), 1,011 Kla sites were identified in 532 proteins under normoxia, and 1,197 Kla sites in 608 proteins under hypoxia [74]. Zhang et al. [75] distinguished three isomers—lysine L‐lactylation (KL‐la), D‐lactyl‐lysine (KD‐la), and N‐ε‐(carboxyethyl)‐lysine (Kce)—using high‐performance liquid chromatography, confirming KL‐la as the predominant lactylation modification in cells and a major responder to glycolysis and the Warburg effect. Analyses of Kla sites on histones and non‐histone proteins suggest that Kla is a widespread PTM [71, 72, 73, 74]. Further research is required to elucidate its mechanisms and functional roles. Current evidence indicates that both histone and non‐histone lactylation promote cancer progression through diverse regulatory mechanisms (Table 1 and Figure 2).

Histone Kla

Histones are positively charged basic proteins that are widely present in the nuclei of eukaryotic cells, where they tightly bind to DNA. They play essential roles in gene expression regulation, chromatin structure maintenance, and DNA replication. The major types of histones include H1, H2A, H2B, H3, and H4 [112]. These proteins interact with DNA to form the fundamental structural unit of chromatin, the nucleosome. Each nucleosome consists of an octamer comprising two molecules each of H2A, H2B, H3, and H4, around which approximately 147 base pairs of DNA are wrapped [112]. Histone H1 binds between nucleosomes, facilitating further DNA compaction [113]. PTMs of histones represent a crucial epigenetic mechanism, where various acyl groups attach to the N‐terminal tails of histones, influencing multiple biological processes involved in tumor progression [114].

Since the discovery of histone lactylation in 2019 [12], its significance in tumor biology has rapidly emerged. A pioneering study in ocular melanoma demonstrated that histone H3 lysine 18 lactylation (H3K18la) enhances the expression of YTH domain family protein 2 (YTHDF2), which recognizes N6‐methyladenosine (m6A)‐modified period circadian regulator 1 (PER1) and TP53 mRNAs, promoting their degradation and contributing to tumor progression [15]. In the same context, H3K18la upregulates alpha‐ketoglutarate‐dependent dioxygenase (ALKBH3) and disrupts tumor‐suppressive promyelocytic leukemia protein (PML) condensates via N1‐methyladenosine (m1A) demethylation of speckled protein 100A (SP100A), further driving malignancy [107] (Figure 2A).

In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), lactylation in hepatic stellate cells promotes fibrosis through the upregulation of hexokinase 2 (HK2), laying an epigenetic foundation for tumor development [115]. Modifications at H3K9, H3K14, H3K18, and H3K56 have been implicated in HCC progression [94, 95, 96]. Knockdown of pyrroline‐5‐carboxylate reductase 1 (PYCR1) reduces H3K18la and insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS1) expression by suppressing glycolysis and downstream PI3K/AKT/mTOR and mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK)/ERK pathways, thus inhibiting tumor growth [95]. Additionally, H3K18la upregulates YTH domain‐containing protein 1 (YTHDC1), stabilizing m6A‐modified nuclear enriched abundant transcript 1 (NEAT1) and activating lipid metabolism via stearoyl‐CoA desaturase (SCD), which promotes tumor progression [97] (Figure 2B).

In gastric cancer, H3K18la contributes to tumor development via multiple mechanisms. H3K18la activates vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM1) transcription, stimulating the AKT/mTOR pathway to promote tumor cell proliferation, metastasis, and immune evasion [90]. Moreover, it increases methyltransferase 14 (METTL14) expression, which facilitates the m6A‐mediated degradation of the activating transcription factor 5 (ATF5) mRNA and inhibits tumor stemness by suppressing the WDR74/β‐catenin axis [89]. Upregulation of GLUT3 enhances LDHA activity, increasing lactate production and lactylation at H3K9, H3K18, and H3K56, thereby facilitating tumor progression [91] (Figure 2C).

In colorectal cancer (CRC), H3K18la plays a pivotal role in multiple tumor‐related pathways. Under hypoxic conditions, H3K18la enhances rubicon‐like autophagy enhancer (RUBCNL) transcription, which recruits the class III PI3K complex via beclin 1 (BECN1), driving proliferation, drug resistance, and metastasis [81]. In liver metastasis of CRC, high GPR37 expression enhances glycolysis and induces C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 1/5 (CXCL1/5) via H3K18la, recruiting neutrophils to facilitate metastasis [83]. Moreover, H3K18la upregulates the m5C methyltransferase NOP2/Sun RNA methyltransferase family member 2 (NSUN2), which promotes glycolytic reprogramming via m5C modification of enolase 1 (ENO1) mRNA, with Y‐box binding protein 1 (YBX1) acting as the reader. The resulting lactate accumulation further enhances H3K18la formation and induces NSUN2 K356 lactylation, which is crucial for capturing target RNAs, thereby forming a positive feedback loop that accelerates CRC progression [84]. In addition to H3K18la, other histone lactylation marks are also implicated in CRC progression. Dormant CRC cells exhibit elevated H4K8la, H4K12la, and H3K14la alongside increased glycolysis; notably, H4K12la correlates with ATP‐binding cassette (ABC) transporter expression and chemoresistance [82] (Figure 2D).

In clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), histone Kla establishes a carcinogenic loop. Inactivation of VHL triggers H3K18la, which upregulates PDGFRβ and subsequently enhances glycolysis, thereby reinforcing H3K18la in a positive feedback loop that promotes tumor progression [13] (Figure 2E). In bladder cancer, suppression of the circular RNA derived from XRN2 (circXRN2)/hippo pathway promotes H3K18la and lipocalin‐2 (LCN2) expression, contributing to cancer progression [77] (Figure 2F). Similarly, in anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, the BRAFV600E mutation enhances glycolysis, leading to elevated H4K12la, transcriptional dysregulation, and accelerated tumor cell cycling [76] (Figure 2G).

In prostate and lung cancers, histone Kla facilitates neuroendocrine differentiation and metabolic reprogramming via mitochondrial dysfunction and the Numb/parkin pathway, contributing to therapy resistance [116]. In prostate cancer (PCa), the DNA topoisomerase 2‐alpha (TOP2A)/LDHA/lactylation axis forms a positive feedback loop: TOP2A enhances glycolysis, which increases H3K18la and further upregulates TOP2A [108] (Figure 2H). In lung cancer, basic leucine zipper and W2 domains 2 (BZW2) regulates isocitrate dehydrogenase 3 gamma (IDH3G) expression via H3K18la [103], while fargesin (FGS) suppresses glycolysis and Kla signaling by targeting pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) [104]. Liver kinase B1 (LKB1) also inhibits H4K8la and H4K16la, altering specificity protein 1 (Sp1)‐mediated gene transcription [106]. (Figure 2I)

In breast cancer, H3K18la upregulates c‐Myc, which induces serine and arginine rich splicing factor 10 (SRSF10) to regulate the alternative splicing of murine double minute 4 (MDM4) and BCL2‐like 1 (Bcl‐x), promoting tumor growth. Inhibition of glycolysis weakens the c‐Myc/SRSF10 axis, suppressing tumor cell proliferation [78] (Figure 2J). In endometrial cancer, H3K18la promotes ubiquitin specific peptidase 39 (USP39) expression, stabilizes phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1), and activates the PI3K/AKT/HIF‐1α pathway, thereby enhancing glycolysis and tumor progression [88] (Figure 2K).

Histone Kla not only regulates tumor cells but also modulates non‐tumor cells in the TME, acting as a crucial bridge between tumor metabolism, epigenetics, and transcriptional processes. Several studies have revealed that histone Kla regulates immune cell function in the TME through various mechanisms. For instance, histone Kla can induce TAM to polarize towards the pro‐tumor M2 phenotype [12]. Exogenous lactate entering macrophages via MCT‐1 can induce lactylation at the H3K18 site, exacerbating this polarization [12]. Furthermore, lactylation at the H3K18 site in tumor‐infiltrating myeloid cells is significantly associated with increased methyltransferase 3 (METTL3) expression, activating the expression of pro‐tumor factors, such as interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), via the Janus kinase 1 (Jak1)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling pathway [117]. In natural killer T (NKT)‐like cells in high‐lactate malignant pleural effusion (MPE), lactylation at the forkhead box P3 (FOXP3) promoter region significantly increases, contributing to immune suppression [118]. Regarding T cells, histone Kla increases PD‐L1 expression in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cells, influencing the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibition [119]. In PCa and glioma, it induces CD8+ T cell exhaustion through the expression of CD39 and CD73 [120, 121].

In summary, increasing evidence supports the critical role of histone Kla across different cancer types. Histone Kla promotes tumor progression, metabolic reprogramming, and immune evasion by regulating glycolysis and related gene expression. It activates tumor‐associated genes and signaling pathways, while forming positive feedback loops in specific cancers, further enhancing tumor cell growth, invasiveness, and drug resistance. Due to its multifaceted influence on metabolism and gene regulation, histone Kla represents a promising target in cancer therapy. As more research on lactylation emerges, its mechanisms may become clearer.

Non‐histone Kla

As previously mentioned, several studies utilizing liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS)‐based proteomics have detected proteins and Kla in various tumor cells, identifying numerous diverse Kla sites, most of which are located on non‐histone proteins. This greatly expands the functional diversity of proteins. The function and enzymatic activity of non‐histone Kla are related to its subcellular localization and interactions with other proteins. These characteristics depend on the properties of the Kla‐modified proteins and may regulate the function of target proteins in different ways. Therefore, non‐histone lactylation, which should not be overlooked, has been widely studied, particularly in key processes related to tumor metabolism, proliferation, and invasion in gastric cancer, HCC, CRC, and PCa (Table 1).

In gastric cancer, copper stress increases lactylation at the K229 site of methyltransferase 16 (METTL16), thereby enhancing its m6A modification of ferredoxin 1 (FDX1) mRNA and improving the efficacy of the copper ionophore, elesclomol. Combined use of elesclomol and a sirtuin 2 (SIRT2) inhibitor effectively induces cuproptosis, highlighting the potential of METTL16 lactylation as a therapeutic target in copper‐rich, high‐lactate gastric cancer [92]. Lactate‐mediated lactylation of NSUN2 at K508 enhances its activity, stabilizing glutamate‐cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC) mRNA via m5C modification, which increases glutathione (GSH) levels and confers resistance to ferroptosis in gastric cancer [93] (Figure 2C).

In HCC, a global lactylation proteomics analysis identified 9,275 Kla sites in tumor and adjacent liver tissues, with the majority found on non‐histone proteins. Further studies showed that lactylation at the K28 site inhibited the function of adenosine kinase 2 (AK2), promoting proliferation and metastasis in HCC cells [72]. ATP binding cassette subfamily F member 1 (ABCF1)‐K430la promotes HCC progression by activating the lysine demethylase 3A (KDM3A)/H3K9me2/HIF‐1α axis, highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target [98]. Kla also plays a crucial role in HCC progression and drug resistance. Increased glycolysis leads to lactate accumulation and lactylation of key proteins such as centromere protein A (CENPA), insulin‐like growth factor 2 mRNA‐binding protein 3 (IGF2BP3), and cyclin E2 (CCNE2), regulating the expression of genes like cyclin D1 (CCND1), phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase 2 (PCK2), and nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (NRF2), thus enhancing the metabolic adaptation, antioxidant defense, and proliferation of tumor cells. Lactylation of CENPA enhances its synergistic interaction with Yin Yang 1 (YY1), promoting the expression of CCND1 and neuropilin‐2 (NRP2), thereby driving tumor progression [100]. Lactylation of IGF2BP3 forms a feedback loop with the m6A methylation of PCK2 and NRF2, maintaining lenvatinib resistance in HCC [99]. As a de‐lactylase, SIRT3 inhibits the lactylation of CCNE2, effectively reducing its pro‐growth activity and exerting a tumor‐suppressive effect in HCC [101]. Furthermore, enhanced lactylation markers such as H3K56la and aldolase A (ALDOA) K230/322la observed in liver cancer stem cells (LCSCs) help maintain stemness and tumor‐forming ability [102] (Figure 2B).

In CRC, lactylation of key glycolytic enzymes such as phosphofructokinase platelet (PFKP) and ALDOA regulates their activity, potentially forming a negative feedback mechanism in glycolysis [122]. Additionally, lactylation at K408 of eukaryotic translation elongation factor 1 alpha 2 (eEF1A2), regulated by the acetyltransferase lysine acetyltransferase (KAT) 8, promotes protein synthesis and CRC cell proliferation, with KAT8 knockout significantly inhibiting tumor growth [85]. Under hypoxic conditions, lactylation of β‐catenin in CRC cells increases its stability, promoting malignant behavior [86]. A serine/glycine‐deficient diet (‐SG diet) inhibits CRC growth and enhances anti‐tumor immunity by activating cytotoxic T cells, but it also increases lactylation of PD‐L1, leading to immune evasion by tumor cells. Blocking the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1)/PD‐L1 signaling pathway can reverse immune evasion caused by PD‐L1 lactylation and restore CD8+ T cell function, demonstrating the potential of this strategy in treating solid tumors [87]. These studies highlight the significant role of lactylation in CRC metabolism, protein synthesis, and epigenetic regulation, providing potential therapeutic targets (Figure 2D).

In PCa, multiple studies have identified mechanisms of tumor progression and resistance, revealing potential therapeutic targets. A study on enzalutamide (Enz) resistance showed that SLC4A4 drives p53 lactylation through the nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB)/STAT3 pathway, promoting Enz resistance. Knockout of SLC4A4 can reverse this resistance, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target in advanced PCa [109]. Another study found that Gambogic acid (GBA) induces pyroptosis by directly acting on the canopy FGF signaling regulator (CNPY3), a process that recruits SIRT1 to remove lactylation on CNPY3, thereby triggering pyroptosis in PCa cells [110]. Finally, a study on angiogenesis in PCa revealed that lactate enters tumor cells via MCT‐1 and induces HIF‐1α lactylation, thereby stabilizing HIF‐1α, enhancing its transcriptional activity, and promoting cell migration‐inducing protein (CEMIP, also known as KIAA1199) expression, which in turn increases the secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) and promotes angiogenesis and vasculogenic mimicry [111]. These findings suggest that targeting lactylation, metabolism, and angiogenesis pathways could help overcome treatment resistance and inhibit tumor progression in PCa (Figure 2H).

In lung cancer, hypoxia induces the lactylation of SRY‐box transcription factor 9 (SOX9) to promote stemness, migration, and invasion via promoting glycolysis [105].

In cervical cancer, two studies have highlighted the crucial role of lactylation in tumor progression. One study found that lactate‐induced lactylation of Discoidin, CUB and LCCL domain‐containing protein 1 (DCBLD1) stabilizes its expression and activates the pentose PPP, promoting cell proliferation and metastasis. Lactylation of DCBLD1 at the K172 site inhibits the autophagic degradation of glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), ensuring sustained activation of PPP [80]. The second study showed that high‐risk human papillomavirus type 16 (HPV16) E6 promotes G6PD dimerization by inhibiting lactylation at the K45 site, enhancing G6PD enzyme activity, and activating PPP. This regulation lowers reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, promoting cervical cancer cell proliferation [79] (Figure 2K).

Overall, while research in this area is still emerging, existing findings highlight the enormous potential of non‐histone lactylation in cancer metabolism, tumor progression, and treatment resistance, laying the foundation for further exploration of this novel modification mechanism in cancer therapy.

Key enzymes of Kla

Since the discovery of lactylation, researchers' interest in this novel modification has steadily grown. However, our understanding remains limited, particularly regarding substrate specificity, the roles of specific enzymes, and reaction kinetics. To comprehensively understand lactylation, it is essential to gain a deep insight into the enzymes that play critical roles in this process (Figure 1). These include “writers,” responsible for adding lactyl groups to lysine residues on proteins; “erasers,” which remove these modifications; and “readers,” which specifically recognize and bind to lactylation marks. These enzymes form the regulatory network of lactylation, driving intricate intracellular signaling pathways and functional regulation (Table 2).

Writers of Kla

The currently known mechanisms of lactylation include enzymatic and non‐enzymatic pathways [139]. Enzymatic lactylation is facilitated by “writers” of lactylation. Acetyl‐CoA Synthetase 2 (ACSS2) and guanosine triphosphate (GTP)‐specific SCS (GTPSCS), as the first identified lactyl‐CoA synthetases, convert lactate into lactyl‐CoA and cooperate with lactylation “writers” (such as KAT2A and p300) to catalyze the specific modification of lactate groups, thereby completing the lactylation process [137, 138]. Non‐enzymatic lactylation, on the other hand, is mediated by lactoylglutathione (LGSH), which is derived from methylglyoxal (MGO), a byproduct of glycolysis (Figure 1). In this process, MGO is first converted to LGSH through the action of glyoxalase 1 (GLO1), and LGSH is subsequently hydrolyzed by glyoxalase 2 (GLO2) to produce D‐lactate. During this process, the lactate moiety from LGSH can be non‐enzymatically transferred to lysine residues, forming the “LactoylLys” modification on proteins [140]. This enzyme‐independent mechanism provides an additional layer of metabolic regulation, linking glycolytic flux to protein function through spontaneous post‐translational modification

Among the enzymatic mechanisms, lysine acetyltransferase p300 was identified as one of the earliest lactylation “writers.” In 2019, Zhang et al. [12] demonstrated that p300 catalyzes lysine lactylation, with its knockdown or overexpression in HEK293T cells and mouse bone marrow‐derived macrophages significantly altering histone lactylation levels. Subsequently, Cui et al. [123] reported that silencing p300 in macrophages resulted in a marked decrease in Kla levels. Additionally, CREB‐binding protein (CBP), a homolog of p300, collaborates with p300 to mediate the lactylation of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and other substrates, a process effectively inhibited by the p300/CBP inhibitor C646 [124]. Other KATs, such as KAT2A, also known as general control non‐derepressible 5 (GCN5) [125]; KAT5, also known as Tat‐interacting protein 60 kDa (TIP60) [126]; KAT7, also known as histone acetyltransferase binding to ORC1 (HBO1) [127]; and KAT8 also known as males absent on the first (MOF) [85], have also been implicated in lactylation, potentially acting synergistically with p300/CBP. Notably, TIP60 has been shown to regulate the lactylation of vacuolar protein sorting 34 (Vps34) [126].

Despite these findings, the intracellular concentration of lactyl‐CoA in mammalian cells is typically at least 1,000‐fold lower than that of acetyl‐CoA, which may substantially limit the lactyltransferase activity of p300 [139]. Recent studies have revealed that alanyl‐tRNA synthetases 1 and 2 (AARS1 and AARS2) can catalyze the formation of lactyl‐AMP from lactate and ATP, transferring lactyl groups to lysine residues, potentially compensating for the low levels of lactyl‐CoA in tumor cells [128, 129, 130, 131]. Moreover, elevated lactylation in certain cancers is associated with poor prognosis. For instance, KAT8 enhances protein translation efficiency by promoting eEF1A2 lactylation, driving tumor progression [85]. Nonetheless, the reaction efficiency and functional diversity of lactylation‐related enzymes remain incompletely understood. Future research may identify additional lactylation “writers” and elucidate their pivotal roles in epigenetics and tumor metabolic reprogramming.

Erasers of Kla

Erasers of lactylation are enzymes or proteins capable of reversing lactylation by catalyzing the removal of lactyl groups, including histone deacetylases (HDACs) and sirtuins [132, 141]. Zu et al. [134] reported that SIRT2 is an effective histone Kla eraser in vitro. Among the HDAC family, HDAC1‐3 exhibit high delactylation activity, with HDAC3 demonstrating the strongest capability—thousands of times more active than SIRT2 [132, 142]. Within the sirtuin family, SIRT1 and SIRT3 also play crucial roles in lactylation regulation. For instance, SIRT1 significantly reduces global lactylation levels in cardiomyocytes [133], while SIRT3 regulates the lactylation status of CCNE2 in hepatocytes, influencing cell proliferation [101]. Additionally, HDAC1‐3 can remove not only acetylation and lactylation but also other lysine modifications, such as crotonylation, indicating broad substrate specificity [143].

Although lactylation and acetylation share some common transferases and erasers, their dynamic regulation and biological timeframes differ, suggesting that lactylation may possess unique regulatory mechanisms and functional significance [12]. Importantly, the function of lactylation erasers varies significantly across tissues and cell types. For example, HDAC11 maintains cancer stemness in hepatocytes by regulating glycolysis, potentially indirectly influencing lactate levels and lactylation [144]. Despite being in its infancy, research on lactylation erasers underscores their critical role in the dynamic balance of this modification. Further investigation into their substrate specificity, functional preferences, and roles in various physiological and pathological contexts will help elucidate lactylation's central role in cellular metabolism, epigenetic regulation, and disease progression, paving the way for novel therapeutic strategies targeting lactylation regulation.

Readers of Kla

In biology, “readers” are proteins or domains capable of recognizing and interacting with lactylation groups. To date, reported lactylation reader proteins include double PHD fingers 2 (DPF2) and Brahma‐related gene 1 (Brg1), which play critical roles in distinct biological contexts. DPF2 has been identified as a specific reader of H3K14la, co‐localizing with H3K14la at oncogene promoters in cervical cancer to promote gene expression and tumor cell survival [135]. Disrupting this interaction suppresses oncogene expression and impairs cell viability. Brg1, as a reader of H3K18la, facilitates pluripotency and epithelial adhesion gene expression by binding to H3K18la during induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) reprogramming [136]. These findings demonstrate that lactylation regulates gene expression and cell fate through its reader proteins, offering new insights into its roles in tumorigenesis and reprogramming.

Interactions between lactylation and other PTMs and epigenetic modifications

Lactylation, as a novel PTM, not only enriches the layers of epigenetic regulation but also reveals the intricate interplay between metabolism and gene expression through its interactions with other classical modifications such as acetylation, phosphorylation, and methylation. These interrelationships play critical roles in various biological processes, offering new insights into the mechanisms underlying disease development and progression.

KATs and HDACs, the classical writers and erasers of acetylation, play critical roles in regulating lactylation. Both modifications preferentially target lysine residues and share enzymes such as p300, which may result in competition at specific sites, thereby affecting the function of target proteins. For instance, H3K18 can undergo both lactylation (H3K18la) and acetylation (H3K18ac). In hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), exogenous lactate promotes lactylation while inhibiting acetylation at H3K18, whereas class I HDAC inhibitors enhance acetylation and suppress lactylation at this site [115]. These opposing effects have been linked to site‐selective competition, in which lactylation and acetylation act as mutually exclusive modifications under specific metabolic conditions. A notable example of this interplay is the “lactate clock” observed in macrophages. Upon bacterial stimulation, macrophages initially exhibit high levels of acetylation, which gradually decline as lactylation increases. This temporal shift from acetylation to lactylation facilitates the transition from a pro‐inflammatory M1 state to an anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby promoting immune resolution and homeostasis [12]. Beyond histones, non‐histone proteins may also exhibit competition between lactylation and acetylation. For example, hyperacetylation of poly(ADP‐ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) suppresses its intrinsic ADP‐ribosylation activity, whereas hyperlactylation enhances it. Since both modifications occur on the same residues, it has been hypothesized that lactylation may compete with acetylation, thereby blocking its regulatory effects and restoring PARP1's ribosylation activity, which is crucial for DNA damage repair [145]. Importantly, the relative abundance and dynamics of these modifications also differ significantly. The intracellular concentration of lactyl‐CoA is estimated to be approximately 1/1,000 that of acetyl‐CoA, suggesting that lactylation may be more limited under physiological conditions [139]. Moreover, isotope tracing studies have revealed that lactylation reaches a steady state within 24 hours, whereas acetylation does so in about 6 hours, indicating distinct temporal kinetics [12]. Moreover, lactylation and acetylation may exhibit cooperative interactions. During iPSC reprogramming, H3K18la and H3K27ac synergistically regulate the expression of pluripotency genes such as octamer‐binding transcription factor 4 (OCT4) and SOX2, providing essential support for stem cell functionality [146].

Beyond its functional interplay with acetylation, lactylation exhibits unique characteristics compared to other emerging lysine acylation types, such as crotonylation, 2‐hydroxyisobutyrylation, 2‐hydroxybutyrylation, and succinylation [147]. Lactylation originates from lactate, the end‐product of glycolysis, establishing a direct metabolic‐epigenetic link, whereas many other acylations are derived from fatty acid oxidation or amino acid catabolism. Crotonylation, for instance, is distinguished from other acylations by its unique C–C–π planar structure and rigidity, and tends to mark active chromatin with stronger transcriptional activation than acetylation [148]. Histone crotonylation and lactylation exhibit distinct genomic distributions and regulate different gene sets, particularly in the brain, where they contribute to neurodevelopment and transcriptomic remodeling. Both modifications are catalyzed by p300 and may compete for the same lysine residues, with elevated intracellular lactate shown to reduce crotonylation levels, suggesting substrate‐based antagonism [149]. Likewise, 2‐hydroxyisobutyrylation originates from branched‐chain amino acid metabolism and utilizes 2‐hydroxyisobutyryl‐CoA as its acyl donor. Catalyzed by p300, this modification introduces a bulky, hydrophilic group that activates glycolytic enzymes such as ENO1, supporting metabolic adaptation in proliferative cells [150]. In contrast, lysine 2‐hydroxybutyrylation arises from 2‐hydroxybutyrate, a ketone body produced during fasting or ketogenic states [151]. It acts as an epigenetic signal to activate transcription in metabolic genes, especially during energy stress [147]. These modifications reflect distinct metabolic environments compared to lactate‐driven lactylation. Lysine succinylation, characterized by the addition of a negatively charged succinyl group, results in a +100.02 Da mass shift and significantly alters protein structure and surface charge [152]. While lactylation is mostly linked to glycolytic output and nuclear events, succinylation is more prominent in mitochondrial and cytoplasmic contexts, modulating metabolic enzyme activity.

Lactylation and phosphorylation establish a complex regulatory relationship through positive feedback loops. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), LDHA phosphorylation is closely associated with lactylation. TTK protein kinase (TTK) phosphorylates LDHA at tyrosine 239 (Y239), activating LDHA and increasing lactate production, which in turn promotes H3K18la. This lactylation modification enhances the transcription of TTK and BUB1 mitotic checkpoint serine/threonine kinase B (BUB1B) genes, forming a positive feedback loop: glycolysis‐derived lactate induces histone Kla, activating TTK and BUB1B expression, while their overexpression further promotes glycolysis, driving PDAC progression [153]. Additionally, studies have shown that UNC‐51‐like kinase 1 (ULK1) enhances LDHA activity through phosphorylation, thereby increasing lactate production. Lactate, as a signaling molecule, regulates autophagy by Vps34 lactylation (at lysine‐356 and lysine‐781), modulating its lipid kinase activity [126]. These findings suggest that phosphorylation and lactylation exhibit reciprocal promotion and regulation in tumors, jointly contributing to metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic control. This positive feedback mechanism underpins tumor progression and highlights its critical roles in disease development.

Lactylation is closely interconnected with RNA modifications (e.g., m6A and m1A) and histone methylation, directly influencing the dynamics of these modifications and reshaping cellular functions and tumor behavior through metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic networks. Recent studies [15, 92, 154, 155] have highlighted the intricate interplay between m6A RNA modification and lactylation, underscoring their regulatory roles in biological processes such as cancer progression and immune responses. As a pivotal RNA epigenetic modification, m6A is regulated by methyltransferases like METTL3 and METTL16, affecting mRNA stability and translation efficiency [154]. In tumors, lactylation links metabolic reprogramming to this epigenetic regulation, profoundly impacting cellular behavior in the tumor microenvironment. For instance, the lactylation of demethylase AlkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5) enhances its demethylation of m6A‐modified interferon‐β (IFN‐β) mRNA, promoting antiviral immune responses [155]. Similarly, under copper stress in gastric cancer, METTL16 lactylation drives m6A modification on FDX1 mRNA, inducing cuproptosis [92]. In tumor‐infiltrating myeloid cells, lactate‐induced METTL3 lactylation increases its RNA‐binding efficiency, activating immunosuppressive signaling pathways [117]. Histone Kla also promotes YTHDF2 expression, regulating m6A‐modified mRNA and driving tumor progression [15].

m1A, a dynamic RNA modification, primarily regulates RNA transcription efficiency and translation activity. In high‐risk ocular melanoma, histone Kla enhances ALKBH3 expression and activity, reducing m1A levels and inhibiting the formation of tumor‐suppressive promyelocytic PML condensates, thereby driving tumor malignancy [107]. Furthermore, lactylation serves as a “bridge” with methylation to co‐regulate chromatin states and downstream gene expression. For example, methyl‐CpG‐binding protein 2 (MeCP2) K271 lactylation interacts with H3K36me3 to increase chromatin accessibility, repressing runt‐related transcription factor 1 (RUNX1) transcription and promoting M2 macrophage polarization [156].

In summary, the intricate crosstalk between lactylation and other PTMs, as well as epigenetic modifications, underscores the multilayered nature of cellular regulatory networks. These interactions are pivotal in tumor metabolic reprogramming and microenvironment shaping, offering valuable insights for identifying potential therapeutic targets. However, the precise mechanisms underlying these relationships, particularly their roles in distinct physiological and pathological contexts, warrant further investigation.

LACTYLATION AND CANCER METABOLIC REPROGRAMMING

Metabolic reprogramming in cancer

The “Warburg effect” refers to the preference of cancer cells for glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation, even under oxygen‐sufficient conditions [2]. Although glycolysis is less efficient, generating only 2 ATP molecules per glucose molecule, cancer cells compensate by upregulating glucose transporters (e.g., Glut1, Glut3, Glut4) and glycolytic enzymes (e.g., HK2, LDHA) [157, 158, 159, 160]. Transcription factors c‐Myc and HIF‐1α synergistically activate glycolytic gene expression, while the tumor suppressor p53 inhibits glycolysis by repressing Glut expression and promoting TIGAR expression [160, 161, 162]. Pyruvate, the end product of glycolysis, is preferentially converted to lactate by LDHA, regenerating NAD+ to sustain glycolytic flux [163]. Lactate is exported via MCT‐4, leading to tumor microenvironment acidification, which facilitates matrix degradation and promotes tumor invasion and metastasis [45]. Furthermore, lactate can be utilized by neighboring tumor cells, establishing a “metabolic symbiosis” [55]. Beyond energy production, glycolysis provides key precursors for nucleotide, lipid, and amino acid biosynthesis, supporting rapid cell division and proliferation [164]. Additionally, glycolytic enzymes contribute to gene regulation, chromatin remodeling, and cancer invasion [165]. These multifunctional roles underscore the potential of targeting glycolytic enzymes as promising anticancer strategies.

In addition to relying on glycolysis, many tumors depend on glutaminolysis to meet their bioenergetic demands and metabolic reprogramming [166]. Glutaminolysis metabolizes glutamine into glutamate and α‐ketoglutarate, fueling the TCA cycle. Intermediates of the TCA cycle are critical for synthesizing lipids, cholesterol, and amino acids. Furthermore, NADH and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FADH2) generated by the TCA cycle supply electrons to the mitochondrial electron transport chain, producing ATP. This process provides energy and essential precursors to support sustained biosynthesis and rapid proliferation in tumor cells. The upregulation of glutaminolysis is primarily mediated by c‐Myc, which enhances the expression of the key enzyme glutaminase 1 (GLS1) by suppressing miR‐23a/b, thereby promoting glutamine uptake and metabolism [167, 168]. This metabolic reprogramming renders tumor cells dependent on glutaminolysis, highlighting a potential therapeutic strategy to target tumor glutaminolysis and c‐Myc‐mediated glutamine dependence.

Lipid metabolism is a hallmark of cancer metabolism, providing essential components for membrane synthesis, energy storage, and signaling. Cancer cells often upregulate de novo fatty acid synthesis, a process driven by enzymes such as ATP citrate lyase (ACLY), acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (ACC), fatty acid synthase (FASN), and SCD [169, 170, 171]. This upregulation plays a critical role in tumor growth and progression. Cholesterol synthesis through the mevalonate pathway further supports tumorigenesis by regulating membrane dynamics and Ras‐Raf signaling [172]. Sterol regulatory element‐binding proteins (SREBPs), specifically SREBP1 for fatty acids and SREBP2 for cholesterol, coordinate lipid synthesis [173]. These transcription factors are frequently overexpressed in cancers and are influenced by tumor suppressors [e.g., AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK)] and oncogenes (e.g., mutant TP53) [174, 175]. Systemic lipid dysregulation, such as obesity, promotes cancer development by increasing insulin resistance, inflammation, and growth factor secretion [169, 176]. Although dietary restriction may inhibit tumors in certain contexts, its efficacy may be diminished in cancers with hyperactivated PI3K/AKT signaling, as this pathway can compensate for reduced insulin signaling [177].

Enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis is another hallmark of cancer cell metabolism. Mitochondria not only serve as the primary source of energy but also participate in various critical metabolic pathways, providing essential molecules for biosynthesis, growth, and proliferation. They also regulate redox balance and calcium homeostasis, maintaining cellular stability [178]. Impaired mitochondrial function or insufficient biogenesis significantly inhibits tumor initiation and progression. Compared to normal differentiated cells, cancer cells often adapt mitochondrial function to balance energy demands, biosynthesis, and rapid proliferation, underscoring mitochondria's critical role in tumor development [179]. c‐Myc is a potent promoter of mitochondrial biogenesis, driving mitochondrial transcription and DNA replication by activating mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) [180]. TFAM facilitates the formation of transcription and replication complexes, optimizing mitochondrial gene expression and DNA replication [181]. Loss of c‐Myc results in a substantial reduction in mitochondrial biogenesis and number, ultimately impairing cancer cell metabolic pathways and tumorigenesis [182].

Lactylation and metabolic reprogramming

The connection between metabolic reprogramming and lactylation represents a key link between tumor metabolism and epigenetic regulation in the TME (Figure 3). First, enhanced glycolysis driven by metabolic reprogramming leads to increased lactate production, which directly fuels histone lactylation (Figure 3A). Next, lactylation interacts with metabolic pathways to reinforce glycolytic flux, forming a positive feedback loop that maintains the metabolic advantage of tumor cells (Figure 3B). Additionally, lactylation directly regulates the expression of metabolic genes, further contributing to metabolic reprogramming in the tumor microenvironment (Figure 3C). Together, this bidirectional crosstalk ultimately promotes tumor progression, highlighting lactylation as a critical integrator of metabolic and epigenetic remodeling in cancer (Figure 3D).

Regulation of lactylation by metabolic reprogramming

Metabolic reprogramming enhances glycolysis activity by upregulating key enzymes, thereby increasing lactate production (Figure 3A). The upregulation of LDHA expression not only promotes lactate generation but also stimulates the lactylation of histones and non‐histone proteins, which in turn regulate gene transcription and cellular functions. In brain metastases of lung cancer, Aldo‐keto reductase family 1 B10 (AKR1B10) enhanced glycolysis by upregulating LDHA expression, resulting in increased lactate levels that activated CCNB1 transcription via H4K12la, thereby accelerating the cell cycle and contributing to chemotherapy resistance [183]. Additionally, under nutrient deprivation, LDHA is activated by phosphorylation by ULK1, leading to lactate production. This lactate, through the lactylation of Vps34, regulates autophagy, linking glycolysis to cellular autophagy regulation [126].

Metabolic reprogramming not only upregulates LDHA but also regulates key enzymes, such as MCT, HIF‐1α, and c‐Myc, indirectly increasing lactate production and providing precursors for lactylation. In the context of tumor metabolic reprogramming, lactate transport mediated by MCT is tightly linked to the regulation of HIF‐1α and c‐Myc. For example, in PCa, lactate enters the cell via MCT‐1, where it stabilizes HIF‐1α through lactylation, promoting the transcription of KIAA1199 and enhancing glycolysis, angiogenesis, and vessel mimicry, thereby driving tumor progression [111]. In gliomas, hypoxia‐induced metabolic reprogramming leads to lactate accumulation in tumor cells, which is transported into macrophages via MCT‐1. This lactate uptake triggers H3K18la modification, thereby upregulating tumor necrosis factor superfamily member 9 (TNFSF9) expression and promoting malignant progression [184]. Furthermore, metabolic reprogramming upregulates c‐Myc to enhance glycolysis. In pancreatic cancer, for instance, Ras homolog family member F (RHOF) increases c‐Myc expression, promoting PKM2 transcription and enhancing glycolysis. Lactate‐induced lactylation and nuclear translocation of snail family transcriptional repressor 1 (Snail1) accelerate EMT [185]. In HCC, glypican‐3 (GPC3) mediates c‐Myc lactylation, enhancing its protein stability, which further increases stemness and glycolytic activity [186]. PKM2 also plays a crucial role in this process by promoting lactate production and lactylation‐mediated modifications. In glioblastoma (GBM), the interaction between PKM2 and aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family member A3 (ALDH1A3) enhances their tetramerization, leading to lactate accumulation, which drives X‐ray repair cross‐complementing 1 (XRCC1) lactylation, thereby improving DNA repair and increasing resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [187].

The impact of lactylation on metabolic reprogramming

A study utilizing the Public Human Meltome Atlas revealed that lactylation is commonly found on glycolytic enzymes [188]. Experimental evidence shows that lactylation at the K147 site of fructose‐bisphosphate ALDOA inhibits its enzymatic activity, suggesting that lactate may directly regulate glycolysis through lactylation. Additionally, lactylation at the K321 site of dehydrogenase/reductase 7 (DHRS7) may alter its thermal stability, indicating its potential functional significance [188]. Lactylation primarily affects glycolysis by modulating the transcriptional levels and structural functions of key glycolytic factors.

Lactylation plays a crucial role in tumor metabolic reprogramming by regulating the function and expression of key glycolytic enzymes (Figure 3C). Histone lactylation and activation of the NF‐κB signaling pathway promote the expression of c‐Myc and enhance c‐Myc mRNA stability through METTL3‐mediated m6A modification, thereby promoting tumor cell proliferation [189]. Lactylation primarily targets the K62 site of PKM2, inhibiting its conversion from a tetramer to a dimer, thereby enhancing pyruvate kinase activity while suppressing its nuclear function and regulating macrophage metabolism and phenotype switching [190]. Additionally, lactylation enhances glycolytic metabolism in endometrial cancer by activating USP39, which stabilizes PGK1 to activate the PI3K/AKT/HIF‐1α signaling pathways, thereby accelerating tumor cell proliferation [88]. In AK2, lactylation at the K28 site inhibits its function, promoting the proliferation and metastasis of HCC [72]. Moreover, histone Kla regulates the expression of HK‐1 and PKM in non‐small cell lung cancer, decreasing glycolytic metabolism while optimizing mitochondrial function to maintain metabolic homeostasis [191]. These studies highlight the multifaceted role of lactylation in regulating key glycolytic enzymes and their associated metabolic processes.

Crosstalk between lactylation and metabolic reprogramming

Metabolic reprogramming significantly increases lactate production by enhancing glycolysis, and lactate, in turn, drives lactylation modifications (Figure 3B). These modifications regulate the function or gene expression of key molecules, promoting metabolic adaptation and tumor progression, thereby creating a robust positive feedback loop.

In glioma stem cells (GSCs), enhanced glycolysis leads to lactate accumulation, driving the lactylation of polypyrimidine tract‐binding protein 1 (PTBP1)‐K436. This lactylation stabilizes PTBP1 and enhances its RNA‐binding capacity, promoting the expression of glycolysis‐related genes, such as 6‐phosphofructo‐2‐kinase/fructose‐2,6‐bisphosphatase 4 (PFKFB4), further amplifying glycolysis and lactate production. This establishes a positive feedback loop that exacerbates tumor progression [192]. In HCC, SRSF10 interacts with MYB proto‐oncogene (MYB) to enhance MYB RNA stability, thereby upregulating the expression of glycolysis‐related enzymes, such as GLUT1, HK1, and LDHA. The enhanced glycolysis pathway promotes H3K18 lactylation, which in turn further increases SRSF10 expression. Lactylation strengthens glycolysis and lactate production through a positive feedback loop, ultimately contributing to immune evasion mechanisms [193]. Additionally, drug‐resistant models show that increased glycolysis leads to lactate accumulation. Lactylation modifications enhance IGF2BP3's ability to capture PCK2 and NRF2 mRNAs, reprogramming serine metabolism and strengthening antioxidant defenses, thus maintaining drug resistance. This cycle can be effectively inhibited by blocking lactylation or metabolic pathways [99]. In PCa, TOP2A promotes H3K18la by upregulating glycolysis and LDHA. H3K18la regulates TOP2A expression, forming a positive feedback loop between TOP2A and glycolysis/LDHA that promotes lactate production and cancer progression [108]. In CRC, NSUN2 regulates ENO1 expression through m5C modification, reprogramming glucose metabolism, and increasing lactate production. The accumulation of lactate, in turn, activates NSUN2 transcription via H3K18la and promotes NSUN2 lactylation at the K356 residue, enhancing its ability to bind target RNAs. This positive feedback loop, involving the NSUN2/YBX1/m5C‐ENO1 axis, further drives glycolysis and lactate production, exacerbating tumor metabolic reprogramming and progression [84]. In PDAC, H3K18la activates mitotic checkpoint genes TTK and BUB1B, which in turn activate glycolysis‐related enzymes, such as LDHA, promoting lactate production and maintaining the lactylation and glycolysis cycle, thereby accelerating disease progression [153]. In Alzheimer's disease (AD), metabolic reprogramming in microglia is driven by glycolysis, with H4K12la upregulating the expression of glycolysis‐related genes, PKM2. This forms a positive feedback loop of glycolysis/lactylation/PKM2, exacerbating microglial dysfunction and worsening disease pathology [194].

Overall, these studies highlight the critical role of lactylation in bridging metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation. By forming positive feedback mechanisms, lactylation serves as a key driver of metabolic adaptation and disease progression (Figure 3D). These findings provide novel insights into the potential of targeting lactylation and metabolic pathways in combination for therapeutic interventions.

LACTYLATION MEDIATES IMMUNE SUPPRESSION IN THE TME

Lactate and lactylation regulate immune cell function in the TME through multiple mechanisms, promoting tumor immune evasion. In effector T cells and NK cells, lactate suppresses proliferation and functional activity by lowering the pH of the microenvironment [195, 196]. Simultaneously, lactate enhances the immunosuppressive function of regulatory T (Treg) cells, further weakening antitumor immune responses [197, 198]. In macrophages, lactate drives polarization toward the M2 phenotype, enhancing its pro‐tumor functions [199]. In myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), lactate increases their number and enhances their immunosuppressive effects [200]. Furthermore, lactate regulates the expression of various immune‐related genes through lactylation, such as enhancing PD‐L1 expression via histone lactylation (e.g., H3K18la), which inhibits T cell function and strengthens the immune evasion capacity of tumor cells [21]. These findings suggest that lactate and lactylation are key mediators of tumor immune regulation, providing potential therapeutic targets for cancer treatment. Lactylation and T cells

Lactylation significantly influences the function of Treg cells (Figure 4A and Table 3). In HCC, lactylation of moesin at Lys72 enhances its interaction with the transforming growth factor β (TGF‐β) receptor 1, activating the SMAD family member 3 (SMAD3) signaling pathway, thereby promoting the immunosuppressive function of Treg cells. Reducing lactate levels or inhibiting LDH can decrease Treg cell induction, enhance antitumor immune responses, and inhibit tumor growth [202]. Moreover, in colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CRLM), lactylation of retinoic acid‐inducible gene I (RIG‐I) inhibits its DNA‐binding activity with NF‐κB in macrophages, leading to the downregulation of NOD‐like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (Nlrp3) transcription and subsequently affecting the immunosuppressive role of Treg cells and the anti‐tumor function of CD8+ T cells [203]. In GBM, intracellular lactate accumulation promotes H3K18la, which upregulates chemokine (C‐C motif) receptor 8 (CCR8) expression in Treg cells and induces CCL1 and CCL18 production in macrophages, thereby disturbing the Treg/Th17 balance, enhancing chimeric antigen receptor regulatory T (CAR‐Treg) cell infiltration, and impairing chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR‐T) cell function [120] (Figure 4A‐B).

Meanwhile, lactylation also modulates the activity of effector T cells, especially CD8⁺ T cells (Figure 4C and Table 3). Studies on CD8+ T cells have shown that specific histone lactylation sites, such as H3K9la and H3K18la, are enriched in these cells and initiate the transcription of key functional genes [21, 201, 209]. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), elevated levels of H3K9la are associated with poor responses to immunotherapy. The downstream gene IL‐11, regulated by H3K9la through the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, suppresses the effector functions of CD8+ T cells [201]. In non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), H3K18la promotes immune evasion by activating the POM121 transmembrane nucleoporin (POM121)/MYC proto‐oncogene (MYC)/PD‐L1 pathway, while inhibiting H3K18la enhances the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells within the tumor [21]. Targeting these histone lactylation mechanisms, such as by blocking metabolic pathways or targeting the associated epigenetic regulation, can restore CD8+ T cell functions and significantly improve immunotherapy outcomes.

In summary, both histone and non‐histone lactylation contribute to reshaping the immune landscape within tumors. By impairing cytotoxic cell function and reinforcing immunosuppressive pathways, lactylation plays a pivotal role in tumor immune evasion. Targeting lactylation‐related mechanisms may complement existing immunotherapies and open new avenues for cancer treatment.

Lactylation and macrophages

Lactylation's regulation of macrophage polarization and its role in tumor immune evasion have garnered significant attention. Macrophages are crucial immune cells in the TME, and their phenotypes can be classified into pro‐inflammatory M1 and anti‐inflammatory M2 types (Figure 4B and Table 3). In 2019, it was first proposed that histone lactylation could regulate the transition of macrophages from the M1 to M2 phenotype. In the late phase of M1 macrophage polarization, increased histone lactylation induces the expression of Arginase 1 (Arg1), promoting the shift towards the M2 phenotype [12]. Moreover, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) regulates tumor growth and M2 macrophage polarization through modulation of lactate levels and protein lactylation, promoting the establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor environment [204]. Tumor‐resident bacteria in CRLM enhance lactate production via RIG‐I lactylation, promoting M2 macrophage polarization and inhibiting the NF‐κB signaling pathway, thereby weakening the antitumor immune response [203]. Similarly, H3K18la inhibits retinoic acid receptor γ (RARγ) expression, boosting IL‐6 production in macrophages, and targeting this axis may help reprogram macrophage function [205]. The role of lactylation in GBM is also significant; in glioma cells, the MCT‐1/H3K18la/TNFSF9 axis facilitates M2 macrophage polarization, further promoting tumor progression [184]. Additionally, glycolytic monocyte‐derived macrophages (MDMs) with high GLUT1 expression suppress T cell activity by upregulating IL‐10 expression through lactate‐driven histone lactylation [206]. In HCC, the SRSF10/glycolysis/H3K18la feedback loop enhances M2 macrophage polarization and immune checkpoint resistance, and targeting this loop may improve the efficacy of anti‐PD‐1 therapies [193]. In PCa, lactate reduction using a PI3K inhibitor diminishes histone lactylation and restores macrophage phagocytic activity [19]. Combining PI3K inhibitors with immune checkpoint inhibitors or targeted therapies enhances anticancer effects and reverses lactylation‐induced immune suppression [19, 207].

These findings suggest that lactylation plays a crucial role in regulating macrophage function and the TME, revealing new therapeutic opportunities in cancer treatment.

Lactylation and other cellular components in the TME

Lactylation in the TME not only regulates the functions of T cells and macrophages through metabolic and epigenetic mechanisms but also profoundly influences the phenotypes and functions of other immune and stromal cell types. In gastric cancer, CAFs secrete lysyl oxidase (LOX), which activates the TGF‐β signaling pathway, promoting glycolysis and lactate accumulation. The resulting lactate induces H3K18la at the PD‐L1 promoter, thereby enhancing PD‐L1 expression and reducing the efficacy of PD‐1/PD‐L1 immune checkpoint blockade therapy. These findings reveal a mechanism by which CAFs regulate PD‐L1 expression through lactylation, offering insights into improving immunotherapy strategies [208] (Figure 4D and Table 3).

In malignant pleural effusion (MPE), FOXP3+ NKT‐like cells utilize lactate via high expression of MCT‐1 and LDHB to maintain their immunosuppressive properties and high H3K18la levels, accompanied by elevated IL‐4, IL‐10, and TGF‐β expression (Figure 4E and Table 3) [118]. Application of the MCT‐1 inhibitor 7‐(N‐benzyl‐N‐methylamino)‐2‐oxo‐2H‐chromene‐3‐carboxylic acid (7ACC2) significantly reduced FOXP3 expression and histone lactylation, suggesting the critical role of lactylation in promoting the immunosuppressive functions of NKT‐like cells [118].

Lactylation also shapes the phenotype and function of tumor‐infiltrating myeloid cells (TIMs), enhancing tumor immune evasion. In CRC, lactate promotes H3K18la in TIMs, which induces METTL3 expression. This enhances m6A modification of JAK1/STAT3 pathway components, thereby increasing IL‐6 and IL‐10 expression and reinforcing the immunosuppressive function of TIMs (Figure 4F and Table 3). Notably, lactylation sites within the zinc finger domain of METTL3 are essential for its RNA‐binding ability, further underscoring the role of lactylation in TIMs regulation [117].

Despite significant progress in understanding the role of lactylation in various cell types within the TME, research on other components remains limited. Future studies should further investigate the relationship between lactylation and the diversity and complexity of the TME, providing a more comprehensive theoretical basis and potential targets for anticancer therapies.

THE POTENTIAL CLINICAL VALUE OF LACTYLATION

In the earlier sections of this review, we outlined the key substrates and enzymes involved in lactylation. The primary substrates for lactylation are lactate, a byproduct of glycolysis, and lactyl‐CoA, with KATs and HDACs serving as critical enzymes. Consequently, factors regulating lactate levels, such as the activity of glycolytic enzymes and glucose availability, as well as interventions targeting lactylation and delactylation enzymes, may significantly influence lactylation levels and tumor progression. Lactate‐associated pathways as potential therapeutic targets

LDHA is a key enzyme in tumor metabolic reprogramming, converting pyruvate to lactate to support tumor growth and metabolism. In recent years, LDHA inhibitors have gained attention as potential anti‐cancer therapies [237]. The LDHA inhibitor oxamate inhibits the LDHA‐induced conversion of pyruvate to lactate, thereby disrupting glycolysis, inhibiting proliferation, inducing apoptosis, arresting tumor cells in the G0/G1 phase, and stimulating ROS production [21, 88, 120, 189, 210, 211, 212, 213, 214, 215]. In acute T‐cell lymphoblastic leukemia, where LDHA activity is significantly elevated, oxamate effectively inhibits the proliferation of Jurkat and DU528 cells and induces apoptosis [210]. Similarly, oxamate has shown anti‐proliferative effects in esophageal cancer, high‐risk neuroblastoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and gastric cancer [211, 212, 213, 214]. In addition, oxamate has been widely used in studies exploring lactylation's role in tumor progression, where it effectively reduces lactate production and lactylation levels, leading to changes in tumor metabolism, immune response, and gene expression [21, 88, 120, 189, 215]. Another small‐molecule inhibitor, FX11, interferes with glycolysis by inhibiting LDHA activity and thereby reducing lactate production through competitive inhibition of the LDHA active site, which disrupts both lactate production and NAD⁺ regeneration [216]. This not only directly restricts tumor growth but also enhances anti‐tumor immunity, demonstrating strong therapeutic potential. In osteosarcoma and lymphoma models, FX11 effectively reduced lactate accumulation and inhibited tumor growth [217, 218]. Beyond oxamate and FX11, other LDHA inhibitors, such as galloflavin, GNE‐140, and 1‐(phenylseleno)‐4‐(trifluoromethyl) benzene (PSTMB), have also been shown to target glycolysis, induce apoptosis, and suppress cancer cell proliferation [219, 220, 221, 222].Galloflavin and PSTMB inhibit the enzymatic activity of LDHA by occupying the NADH binding site, while GNE‐140 binds to the active site of LDHA, blocking the interaction between pyruvate and NADH. Functionally, these inhibitors reduce tumor cell proliferation, promote apoptosis, and in the case of PSTMB, also induce mitochondrial ROS production.

Beyond LDHA, LDHB has also attracted attention as a context‐dependent therapeutic target due to its role in lactate oxidation and metabolic flexibility. In oxidative tumors, LDHB supports mitochondrial metabolism and contributes to treatment resistance [238]. Accordingly, LDHB inhibition—via siRNA, shRNA, or gene editing—has been shown to sensitize tumor cells to radiotherapy in several models. For instance, LDHB silencing enhances the effect of radiation by promoting mitochondrial lipid peroxidation and thereby reducing tumor growth [239]. In tamoxifen‐resistant breast cancer and metastatic lung cancer, suppression of LDHB, either alone or in combination with MCT‐1 inhibition, disrupts lactate recycling and reduces cell viability [240, 241]. Conversely, LDHB is epigenetically silenced in various tumors, including breast cancer, prostate cancer, and NSCLC [242, 243, 244]. This silencing, often due to promoter hypermethylation, shifts metabolism toward glycolysis, promoting proliferation, invasion, and migration under hypoxia [245]. Clinically, reduced or absent LDHB expression has been correlated with poor prognosis, supporting its potential tumor‐suppressive role [244, 246]. While current studies rarely focus on directly restoring LDHB expression, we proposed that restoring LDHB expression through gene overexpression or epigenetic agents like 5‐azacytidine may redirect glucose metabolism toward oxidative phosphorylation, reduce lactate accumulation, and inhibit tumor progression. Although further studies are needed, early LDHB downregulation may represent a critical event in metabolic reprogramming. Taken together, LDHB plays dual and context‐dependent roles in cancer. Its inhibition or activation may offer therapeutic benefits depending on the tumor's metabolic state.

MCTs, particularly MCT‐1 and MCT‐4, play critical roles in tumor metabolism by mediating lactate transport to sustain glycolytic activity in cancer cells. Inhibiting MCT activity disrupts glycolysis, significantly restricting tumor cell growth, proliferation, and migration, while also enhancing sensitivity to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [226]. Multiple MCT inhibitors have demonstrated notable anti‐tumor effects, including quercetin, simvastatin, α‐cyano‐4‐hydroxycinnamic acid (CHC), 4,4'‐Diisothiocyanatostilbene‐2,2'‐disulfonic acid (DIDS), AR‐C122982, AR‐C155858, and 7ACC2 [223, 224, 226, 247]. These inhibitors block MCT‐mediated lactate transport, thereby disrupting the glycolytic phenotype of tumor cells. For instance, quercetin, a flavonoidal MCT‐1 inhibitor, reduces the apparent rate of pyruvate‐to‐lactate conversion, induces cell cycle arrest, inhibits cell proliferation, and promotes apoptosis in breast and colorectal cancer cells [223, 224, 225]. CHC, acting as an inhibitor of both MCT‐1 and MCT‐4, has been shown to disrupt glycolysis, thereby decreasing cell viability, inhibiting proliferation, and enhancing cell death [224, 226]. DIDS, another MCT‐1 inhibitor, exhibits similar glycolysis‐disrupting effects [224]. Additionally, simvastatin, targeting MCT‐4, contributes together with CHC to the reversal of radiation resistance in tumor cells [226]. AR‐C122982 and AR‐C155858, two distinct MCT‐1 inhibitors, similarly help overcome radiation resistance [226]. In lactylation‐related studies, 7ACC2, a small‐molecule inhibitor of MCT‐1, and CHC have been shown to regulate lactate metabolism. Specifically, 7ACC2 reduces FOXP3 expression and H3K18la levels in NKT‐like cells to modulate tumor immune suppression [118], while CHC has been implicated in myocardial fibrosis [118, 248], underscoring their therapeutic potential. Additionally, in osteosarcoma, CHC or MCT‐1 shRNA knockdown delayed tumor growth and enhanced sensitivity to doxorubicin [227]. AZD3965, a selective MCT‐1 inhibitor with demonstrated cytostatic and cytotoxic effects, has completed a Phase I clinical trial, highlighting its potential to suppress tumor growth by blocking lactate metabolism [228]. Future research may focus on combining AZD3965 with other cancer therapies, such as chemotherapy or immunotherapy, particularly for metabolically dependent cancer subtypes like lymphoma and certain solid tumors, opening new avenues for metabolic‐targeted treatments [229].

In summary, targeting lactate metabolism offers a promising strategy to disrupt the metabolic reprogramming that supports tumor growth, progression, and immune evasion. LDHA inhibition reduces lactate production and glycolytic flux, impairing cancer cell proliferation and enhancing sensitivity to conventional therapies. LDHB, a more context‐dependent target, plays dual roles in cancer metabolism—either supporting oxidative metabolism and therapy resistance, or acting as a tumor suppressor when epigenetically silenced. Modulating the LDHA/LDHB balance, rather than targeting one isoform alone, may allow more flexible and tumor‐specific interventions, depending on the tumor's metabolic phenotype. MCTs, particularly MCT‐1 and MCT‐4, mediate lactate transport across the plasma membrane and are essential for maintaining the glycolytic phenotype of cancer cells; their inhibition disrupts lactate shuttling, induces metabolic stress, and improves response to chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Collectively, LDHA, LDHB, and MCTs constitute interrelated and targetable nodes within the lactate metabolic network. Their coordinated modulation—guided by the specific metabolic context of each tumor—may enhance the efficacy of metabolic‐targeted therapies and provide a foundation for more personalized and adaptive cancer treatment strategies.

Lactylation‐based antitumor therapeutic strategies

P300, the first KAT identified in lactylation, catalyzes the transfer of lactyl‐CoA‐mediated lactylation modifications to lysine residues, while HDACs remove these modifications [249]. Targeting lactylation‐related enzymes has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. As a significant tumor promoter and prognostic biomarker, p300 is considered a key therapeutic target in various malignancies, attracting considerable attention in the development of its inhibitors [249]. A study has shown that p300 inhibitors regulate glycolysis by suppressing p300/CBP‐mediated histone acetylation [150]. In HCC, p300/CBP promotes metabolic reprogramming by increasing H3K18ac and H3K27ac levels, thereby activating the expression of glycolysis‐related metabolic enzyme genes, such as phosphoserine phosphatase (PSPH) and deoxythymidylate kinase (DTYMK) [230]. The novel p300 inhibitor B029‐2 significantly suppresses glycolysis and nucleotide synthesis, effectively inhibiting HCC cell growth [230]. This suggests that glycolysis inhibition may reduce lactate and lactylation levels, ultimately delaying tumor progression. Recent studies have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of p300 inhibitors through the inhibition of p300‐mediated lactylation in various disease models [124, 231, 232]. In sepsis, the p300 inhibitor C646 blocks p300‐mediated HMGB1 lactylation, reducing endothelial permeability and improving survival [124]. In ischemic stroke, the p300 inhibitor A‐485 mitigates neuronal death and glial activation by lowering lactylation levels, thus reducing brain injury and promoting functional recovery [232]. In autoimmune uveitis, A‐485 inhibits p300‐mediated YY1 lactylation, thereby suppressing inflammatory gene expression, attenuating microglial activation and migration, and reducing FGF2 expression to inhibit retinal angiogenesis [231]. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting lactylation‐related p300 pathways in metabolic and inflammatory disorders. Notably, the p300 inhibitor CCS1477 is currently undergoing a Phase I/II clinical trial in patients with metastatic castration‐resistant PCa [250].

Targeting delactylation‐related enzymes also represents a promising therapeutic strategy. Given the pro‐tumorigenic role of lactylation in cancer progression, exploring the reduction of lactylation levels via sirtuin‐activating compounds (STACs) is of particular interest. STACs include natural SIRT activators (e.g., Honokiol, Resveratrol) and synthetic small molecules (e.g., SRT1720, SRT1460, ADTL‐SA1215, MDL‐800) [251]. A recent study demonstrated that Honokiol activates SIRT3 to remove CCNE2 K348la modification in HCC, thereby regulating lactylation levels, inducing apoptosis, and suppressing tumor progression [101]. Although research on synthetic SIRT activators remains limited, future studies are expected to deepen our understanding. The effects of HDAC inhibitors on lactylation appear complex. For example, in MCF‐7 cells, histone lysine acetylation (Kac) reaches equilibrium within 6 hours, whereas Kla continues to increase over 24 hours [12]. This suggests distinct metabolic dynamics for Kac and Kla. As both modifications share the same “writers” and “erasers,” potential competition may exist between them. Additionally, inhibitors targeting HDAC1‐3 significantly elevate histone Kla levels [132].

Strategies for targeting lactate‐lactylation in combination with immunotherapy

Immunotherapy has revolutionized cancer treatment by activating the patient's immune system, particularly T cells, to specifically target tumor cells, demonstrating remarkable clinical efficacy [252]. However, its therapeutic outcomes often fall short of expectations due to limited efficacy, a narrow antitumor spectrum, and severe adverse effects. Studies have shown that immunosuppressive factors within the TME are major barriers to the success of immunotherapy, with lactate accumulation and lactylation modifications playing key roles in immune suppression [253]. Lactate weakens the effectiveness of immunotherapy by inhibiting T cell function and enhancing the activity of immunosuppressive cells. Meanwhile, lactylation further regulates immune cell function and gene expression, exacerbating tumor immune tolerance [253]. Combining anti‐lactate and anti‐lactylation therapies with existing immunotherapeutic strategies represents a promising solution. By targeting lactate metabolism or lactylation, this approach not only alleviates immunosuppression in the TME but also enhances the antitumor efficacy of immunotherapy and prolongs its therapeutic benefits.

Targeting lactate metabolism and lactylation modifications offers a novel approach to overcoming barriers in cancer immunotherapy. In LDHA‐deficient B16‐F10 murine melanoma tumors, CD8⁺ T cells treated with anti‐PD‐1 exhibit enhanced mitochondrial activity and elevated ROS levels, thereby improving antitumor immune responses and boosting the efficacy of anti‐PD‐1 therapies [233]. Similarly, as a key lactate transporter overexpressed in various tumors, MCT‐4 can be inhibited genetically or pharmacologically (e.g., using the MCT‐4 inhibitor VB124). This alleviates the acidic tumor microenvironment, enhances chemokine secretion, promotes T cell infiltration and cytotoxic activity, significantly delays tumor progression, and improves anti‐PD‐1 therapy outcomes [234, 254].

KATs and HDACs regulate histone epigenetic modifications that influence CD274 transcription, thereby modulating PD‐L1 expression [235]. Targeting these enzymes in combination with PD‐L1 inhibitors may help overcome immune resistance and improve antitumor efficacy. It has been demonstrated that tumor cells secrete PD‐L1 through exosomes or splice variants, contributing to immune checkpoint blockade therapy resistance [235]. Interrupting PD‐L1 secretion or expression may enhance therapeutic outcomes. p300/CBP inhibitors have been demonstrated to significantly improve the efficacy of PD‐L1 blockade therapy by suppressing CD274 gene transcription, thereby preventing PD‐L1 secretion via exosomes. This, combined with anti‐PD‐L1 antibodies, enhances the antitumor activity of T cells [235]. Similarly, tribbles homolog 3 (TRIB3) interacts with p300 to inhibit CD8⁺ T cell tumor infiltration, impairing immune responses. Targeting TRIB3 with p300 inhibitors accelerates its degradation, promoting T cell recruitment and enhancing the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade therapy [255]. Moreover, the combination of radiotherapy and immunotherapy has shown efficacy in lung cancer treatment, though it may not fully activate antitumor immune responses. In mouse models, HDAC inhibitors such as Entinostat have been found to enhance the effects of radiotherapy and anti‐PD‐1 therapy by upregulating type II interferon signaling and the MHC‐II pathway, leading to increased CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and reduced Treg cells, thereby improving the antitumor effects of radiotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors [236].

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVE

Conclusions

Lactylation, a novel post‐translational modification, has emerged as a critical player in the TME, influencing both the epigenetic landscape and metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells. The Warburg effect, a hallmark of cancer metabolism, is intimately connected with lactate production, which not only serves as a metabolic substrate but also as a signaling molecule. The transport and shuttling of lactate through MCT further enhance its role as a pivotal metabolic and signaling mediator within the TME. Lactylation, as a key epigenetic modification, regulates both histone and non‐histone proteins, modulating gene expression and cellular behavior in response to the altered metabolic states of tumor cells.

The regulatory enzymes that govern lactylation, including lactyltransferases (writers), lactylase enzymes (erasers), and lactylation‐binding proteins (readers), provide new targets for therapeutic intervention. Moreover, the intricate crosstalk between lactylation and other epigenetic modifications, such as acetylation and methylation, underscores the complexity of this modification in orchestrating tumor progression. Additionally, lactylation mediates immune suppression in the TME by altering the function of immune cells such as T cells and macrophages, suggesting a potential avenue for immune modulation therapies.

In the context of metabolic reprogramming, lactylation regulates and is regulated by shifts in the metabolic pathways that cancer cells utilize to thrive in hypoxic and nutrient‐deprived conditions. These insights point to lactylation as a central player in the interplay between metabolism and epigenetic regulation, with broad implications for cancer cell survival, immune evasion, and therapy resistance.

Future perspectives

As our understanding of lactylation expands, several exciting future directions for research and therapeutic applications emerge. 1. Targeting lactylation in cancer therapy: Given its central role in both epigenetic regulation and metabolic reprogramming, targeting lactylation‐related enzymes (writers, erasers, and readers) presents a promising therapeutic strategy. Developing small molecules or biologics that modulate lactylation could potentially disrupt tumor growth and metastasis. 2. Lactate‐dependent immune modulation: The immunosuppressive effects of lactylation within the TME are a promising target for immunotherapy. By understanding how lactylation influences immune cell function, we could design strategies to counteract lactate‐driven immune suppression, thereby enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapies, such as checkpoint inhibitors or adoptive T cell therapies. 3. Lactylation as a biomarker: The dynamic changes in lactylation patterns during cancer progression may serve as biomarkers for disease prognosis or therapeutic response. The identification of lactylation‐specific signatures could aid in patient stratification for personalized treatment approaches. 4. Crosstalk with other epigenetic modifications: Further exploration of the crosstalk between lactylation and other epigenetic modifications, such as acetylation, phosphorylation, and methylation, is crucial for understanding the broader regulatory networks governing cancer progression. The development of integrative models could help uncover the complex relationships between these modifications and their combined impact on tumor biology. 5. Combination therapies: Since lactylation is deeply involved in both metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming, combining therapies that target lactate metabolism and epigenetic regulation (e.g., p300 inhibitors) could provide synergistic effects. Additionally, integrating these approaches with immunotherapy may improve the overall therapeutic outcomes by reprogramming both cancer cells and immune cells. 6. Investigating the TME in metastasis: While much of the research on lactylation has focused on primary tumors, its role in metastatic spread remains underexplored. Understanding how lactylation influences metastasis, particularly in distant organs, could provide new insights into how lactate‐driven epigenetic modifications contribute to tumor dissemination and resistance to conventional therapies. 7. Expanding the scope of non‐Histone lactylation: While histone lactylation has been well‐characterized, the functional impact of lactylation on non‐histone proteins remains largely unexplored. Investigating how lactylation modulates metabolic enzymes, transcription factors, and RNA‐binding proteins could uncover novel mechanisms of tumor progression and reveal new targets for therapy. 8. Deciphering spatiotemporal dynamics of lactylation: The distribution and functional roles of lactylation may vary across tumor regions or disease stages. Emerging tools such as single‐cell epigenomics and spatial omics offer powerful means to resolve lactylation dynamics in situ, enabling a more precise understanding of its roles in tumor heterogeneity and therapy response.

In summary, lactylation represents a promising, yet underexplored, modification in cancer biology. Future research will need to focus on clarifying its molecular mechanisms, its role in immune modulation, and how it can be targeted therapeutically to improve cancer treatment strategies. By addressing these challenges, lactylation could become an integral component of precision oncology, enabling the development of more effective, personalized therapies for cancer patients.
