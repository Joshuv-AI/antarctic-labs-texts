Photovoltaic arrays reshape multitrophic biodiversity through spatial heterogeneity in Qinghai Tibet Plateau alpine meadows
Authors: Yanjun Guo, Wenqian Sun, Feiyang Xue, Xidong Zhu, Hang Yang, Limin Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySolar photovoltaic (PV) arrays are rapidly expanding in grasslands, yet their ecological effects in high-altitude alpine meadows remain poorly understood. On the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, the distinctive climate and high ecological sensitivity of alpine meadows suggest that PV-induced microhabitat heterogeneity may generate complex multi-trophic responses. We surveyed vascular plants, plateau pikas and zokors, and soil bacterial communities across grazing lands, fenced enclosures, and three PV microhabitats: beneath panels, panel edges, and inter-panel gaps. PV deployment was associated with cooler, moister, less wind-exposed near-surface conditions during summer sampling, together with shifts in plant, small-mammal, and bacterial richness and diversity. Piecewise SEM indicated that microclimatic variation was the most consistent upstream pathway, with soil and vegetation acting as intermediate links. Beneath panels had higher vegetation cover, biomass, and small-mammal activity, whereas inter-panel gaps supported higher bacterial diversity and abundance, highlighting within-site spatial heterogeneity in shaping biodiversity responses to PV infrastructure.

Introduction

Meeting global climate and biodiversity targets requires a rapid transition away from fossil fuels toward low-carbon energy systems.1 Solar photovoltaic (PV) power is among the fastest-growing renewable technologies worldwide, and large ground-mounted PV arrays are increasingly being deployed in open landscapes, including natural and semi-natural grasslands.2 While the climate benefits of PV deployment are widely recognized, the land-use change associated with utility-scale PV installations inevitably alters surface energy balance, microclimate, and habitat structure.3,4 There is growing concern that, without careful planning, the expansion of solar infrastructure could undermine the ecological integrity of already fragile ecosystems, particularly in regions where grasslands provide critical ecosystem services and support high biodiversity.5,6

PV arrays do not simply replace vegetation with infrastructure; they create a fine-scale mosaic of shaded and unshaded patches that can strongly modify near-surface environments.7 By intercepting solar radiation, panels typically reduce incoming light, air and soil temperature, wind speed, and evapotranspiration beneath and around their footprint, while increasing relative humidity and soil moisture.8 These physical changes can, in turn, affect soil development, plant growth, and the distribution and behavior of animals and microorganisms.9 Recent studies have begun to frame solar parks as “novel ecosystems” or “ecological filters” that can either exacerbate land degradation or, in some cases, facilitate vegetation recovery and biodiversity enhancement, depending on local climate, land-use history, and management.10 Although multi-taxon and ecosystem-level assessments and integrative studies are becoming increasingly common, most still focus on only a handful of taxa in a piecemeal manner, and the study regions remain largely concentrated in low-elevation temperate, Mediterranean, or arid landscapes.11,12,13 This hampers our ability to generalize how PV infrastructure reshapes ecological processes across multiple trophic levels.

High-altitude alpine meadows on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (QTP) are an important frontier for both climate mitigation and biodiversity conservation.14 The QTP harbors some of China’s richest solar resources and has become a priority region for large-scale PV development.15 At the same time, it functions as a key ecological security barrier and the “Asian Water Tower,” sustaining downstream water resources and supporting unique cold-adapted biota.16,17 Alpine meadows in this region are characterized by strong solar radiation, low temperatures, short growing seasons, and shallow, often nutrient-poor soils.18 These systems are highly sensitive to land-use change, overgrazing, and climate warming, with documented shifts in plant functional composition, soil nutrient status, and wildlife populations.19,20 Yet, despite the rapid expansion of PV facilities on the QTP, their ecological consequences for alpine meadow structure and function remain poorly quantified, particularly relative to traditional land-use regimes such as grazing and fenced exclosures.21,22,23,24

Understanding PV impacts in alpine meadows requires a multi-trophic perspective. Vascular plants form the primary producers that regulate productivity, soil protection, and habitat structure.25,26 Native small mammals such as plateau pikas (Ochotona curzoniae) and plateau zokors (Eospalax baileyi) are key herbivores and ecosystem engineers: Through foraging, burrowing, and mound building, they reshape vegetation patterns, soil physical properties, and nutrient cycling, with cascading effects on other biota.27,28 Soil bacterial communities underlie decomposition, mineralization, and greenhouse gas regulation, and respond sensitively to microclimatic and edaphic changes.29 These three biological groups—plants, small mammals, and soil bacteria—are tightly coupled via bottom-up and top-down interactions, meaning that any PV-induced modification of microclimate and soil conditions could propagate through the entire meadow food web.30,31 However, integrated assessments that simultaneously consider these taxa across distinct PV-created microhabitats remain critically scarce on the QTP and in other alpine or high-elevation grasslands.32

Another important but underappreciated aspect of PV ecology is within-site spatial heterogeneity.33 Panel rows, panel edges (PEs), and PB generate contrasting microhabitats that differ in shade, soil moisture, and disturbance intensity.34 At the same time, PV arrays are commonly embedded in landscapes with ongoing or historical grazing and, in some cases, adjacent fenced areas established for vegetation restoration. This creates a hierarchical structure of land-use and microhabitat types—from grazed grasslands to fenced enclosures, and further to PV arrays and their internal sub-zones—which may support distinct plant assemblages, small mammal populations, and microbial communities.35 Disentangling the relative contributions of land-use change (grazing vs. fencing vs. PV) and fine-scale PV-induced heterogeneity is essential for predicting whether solar development will ultimately erode or enhance multi-trophic biodiversity.36 Here, we adopt a space-for-time substitution approach, using spatial contrasts among nearby grazed, fenced, and PV areas to infer PV-associated patterns while acknowledging the absence of before-construction baselines.

In this study, we used a multi-taxon field survey in a representative PV facility on the QTP to evaluate how PV-induced spatial heterogeneity influences alpine meadow biodiversity. We compared traditional grazing areas, fenced enclosures, and PV arrays, and, within the PV arrays, distinguished among three microhabitat sub-zones: PB, PEs, and beneath-panel centers (PCs) (Figure 1). Across these zones, we quantified near-surface microclimate, soil physicochemical properties, plant community structure and diversity, relative activity indices of plateau pikas and plateau zokors, and soil bacterial community composition and predicted functional potential. We hypothesized that: (1) PV installations would significantly modify microclimate and soil conditions relative to grazed and fenced grasslands, thereby altering biodiversity across multiple trophic levels; (2) the direction and magnitude of biodiversity responses would differ among the distinct microhabitats created within PV arrays, reflecting fine-scale spatial heterogeneity; and (3) PV-induced environmental changes—particularly shifts in microclimate and soil nutrients—would mediate these multi-trophic responses, with plant community structure acting as a key intermediary linking abiotic factors to small mammals and microbial communities. By integrating environmental measurements with multi-trophic biodiversity data, this study aims to clarify the mechanisms by which PV infrastructure reshapes alpine meadow ecosystems and to provide a scientific basis for biodiversity-friendly solar energy planning in high-altitude grasslands.

Overview of the study area

This study was conducted in Maqu County, Gannan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, Gansu Province, northwestern China—a region dominated by typical alpine meadow grasslands of the QTP. The PV facility examined in this study has been in operation for over five years, providing suitable conditions for evaluating long-term ecological effects. The research site was located in the Nima Town Photovoltaic Station (33°40′13″N, 102°9′6″E; 3,449 m a.s.l.), which has an installed capacity of 40 MW and covers approximately 69.33 ha. The park is enclosed by perimeter fencing and equipped with PV panels mounted at 1.8 m height with a 3.5 m row spacing. Panels rotate automatically along the north-south axis by up to 180° in response to solar irradiance. The region receives 550–680 mm of mean annual precipitation, has a mean annual temperature of 1.2 °C, and an average relative humidity of 53%. Dominant plant species include Elymus dahuricus, Stipa purpurea, Poa annua, Kobresia humilis, Potentilla anserina, and Taraxacum maurocarpum.

Plot design

Field surveys were conducted in late July 2023. Local grazing (primarily sheep) occurs from May to October each year. Three land-use types were established within and around the PV facility:(1) a non-grazed PV area (PV),(2) a fenced enclosure without PV infrastructure (E), and (3) a traditionally grazed area without PV infrastructure (G). Within the PV installation, three microhabitat subzones were identified according to panel position: PB, PEs, and beneath-PCs. For each zone and subzone, three replicate plots (22 m × 10.5 m) were randomly established. To minimize confounding factors as much as possible, we selected comparison areas within the same regional landscape context and conducted sampling during the same period.

Collection of microclimatic data

Microclimatic variables—including air temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed—were measured using a Kestrel 5400 portable meteorological instrument. Photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) was measured using an ECA-GG01 PAR meter, and evaporation was quantified using evaporation pans. Measurements were taken every 2 h from 10:00 to 16:00 over three consecutive days at 20 cm above ground. For each sampling point, three replicate measurements were obtained and averaged. Ten evaporation pans (150 mm diameter) filled with water were placed at each sampling point for three consecutive days, and water loss was measured over 24-h intervals.37 The microclimate measurements above represent only short-term conditions during the sampling period; future work could use continuous sensor monitoring across seasons to test whether these relative differences remain consistent over longer time scales.

Soil sampling and analysis

From each plot, soil samples were collected after removing aboveground vegetation using a 7 cm soil auger at depths of 0–10 cm, 10–20 cm, and 20–30 cm. The 0–10 cm samples were split into two subsamples: one stored at −80 °C for full-length 16S rDNA gene sequencing, and the other air-dried for physicochemical analysis. All deeper-layer samples were air-dried for soil chemical measurements. Soil pH was determined using a glass electrode. Soil organic carbon was measured via potassium dichromate oxidation with external heating. Total nitrogen, nitrate-N, ammonium-N, and alkali-hydrolysable nitrogen were analyzed using a continuous flow analyzer. Total phosphorus was measured by NaOH fusion and molybdenum-antimony colorimetry, and available phosphorus was extracted with NaHCO3 and quantified using the same method. Total and available potassium were determined via flame photometry.38

Plant sampling and survey

Total vegetation cover was estimated using the step-point method. Within 0.25 m2 quadrats, species-level cover was quantified using the point-intercept technique. Species frequency was determined from 30 circular quadrats per plot. For each species, natural plant height was measured with 10 replicate observations. All vegetation within each quadrat was clipped at ground level, sorted into functional groups, oven-dried at 75°C–80°C, and weighed to determine total and functional group-specific aboveground biomass.

Survey of small mammal population density

Relative population density of Tibetan pikas was estimated using the burrow-blocking and reopening method, which quantifies the number of active burrows. The relative density of plateau zokors was estimated by counting the number of newly formed zokor mounds per unit area.

Soil microbial analysis

Soil microbial DNA was extracted using the Power Soil DNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen, USA). DNA concentration and purity were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and UV spectrophotometry. The full-length bacterial 16S rDNA gene was amplified using the KAPA HiFi Hot Start Ready Mix PCR Kit and sequenced on the PacBio SMRT platform.

High-quality reads were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity. Taxonomic assignments were made using the SILVA database. Downstream analyses included α-diversity indices, β-diversity comparisons, community composition analyses, and RDA.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Raw data were organized and processed in Microsoft Excel. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted in SPSS 19.0, and figures were prepared in Origin 2024. To characterize the integrated biodiversity response across trophic levels, we quantified multi-trophic diversity using a multidiversity (MTD) index.39 This index was used to summarize the overall biodiversity pattern across the major trophic groups examined in this study, rather than relying solely on separate taxon-specific results for plants, soil bacteria, and small mammals. For each trophic group, one diversity-related metric was selected and standardized prior to aggregation, and the MTD value for each plot was then calculated as the mean of the standardized components. Higher MTD values therefore indicate a higher overall level of biodiversity across the trophic groups included in this study. Plant community diversity was quantified using α-diversity metrics.40 Predicted bacterial functional profiles were inferred from 16S rRNA-based taxonomic assignments using FAPROTAX. Pearson’s correlations were visualized using correlation plots. RDA was applied to identify environmental variables associated with variation in plant, rodent, and bacterial diversity. Environmental variables highlighted by the RDA were subsequently incorporated into piecewise SEMs to evaluate potential pathways linking PV-associated environmental heterogeneity with biological responses. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used for dimensionality reduction of plant and soil bacterial community data. Model fit was assessed using Shipley’s test, with Fisher’s C values of p > 0.05 considered to indicate acceptable model fit.

Results

Spatial heterogeneity in microenvironmental conditions under photovoltaic installations

PV installations generated pronounced microclimatic changes with clear spatial structuring in the alpine meadow, reflected in both microclimatic and soil conditions. Compared with the grazed and fenced areas, the PV sites were characterized overall by lower air and soil temperatures, higher air humidity, reduced wind speed, and lower evaporation, indicating that the panels substantially reconfigured the near-surface hydrothermal environment (Figure S1). Marked spatial heterogeneity also emerged within the PV field itself: The inter-panel spaces were relatively warmer, brighter, more wind-exposed, and subject to stronger evaporative demand; the beneath-panel zones were cooler and more sheltered; while the PE zones consistently maintained the highest soil moisture across all soil layers. By contrast, the response of soil nutrients was more strongly dependent on soil depth and exhibited greater spatial variability. Although soil pH and total nitrogen differed little among areas, phosphorus and potassium fractions showed clear microhabitat- and soil-layer-specific variation, with available nutrients displaying stronger spatial heterogeneity than total nutrient pools (Figures S2–S4). Taken together, these results indicate that the PV plots maintained looser and moister soils overall. Distinct microhabitats were formed within the installation: The PEs retained more moisture, whereas the panel-center beneath-panel zones (PC) exhibited lower nutrient availability than the other PV microhabitats.

Impacts of photovoltaic power plants on plant community structure and diversity

These microclimatic and edaphic shifts were accompanied by marked vegetation responses. Biomass was highest at PE sites, and PB supported taller and denser vegetation, whereas grazing strongly reduced plant height, biomass, and cover (Figures 2A–2C). Plant diversity increased under PV structures. Species richness and diversity were higher in PV plots than in grazed sites, with PB microhabitats exhibiting the greatest richness across treatments (Figures 2D–2G). Toxic species were less common in PV areas than in E plots (Figure S6) and are reported here as observed differences among habitats. Together, these patterns indicate that PV arrays were associated with higher plant diversity in some sampled PV microhabitats relative to grazing sites (Figure S7). Because these comparisons are based on observational spatial contrasts (space-for-time substitution), the above patterns should be interpreted as statistical associations consistent with PV-associated microhabitat heterogeneity rather than definitive causal effects.

Regression analyses showed that variation in plant community structure and diversity was closely associated with microclimatic gradients during the sampling period (Figure S8). In general, microhabitats with lower radiation loads, cooler conditions, reduced wind exposure, lower evaporative demand, and higher humidity supported more developed vegetation and greater diversity. Microclimatic variation emerged as the most consistent correlate of vegetation differentiation among treatments. RDA revealed strong associations between lower wind speed and soil temperature, reduced evapotranspiration, elevated humidity and soil moisture, and increases in plant height, cover, biomass, and richness (Figures S9 and S11).

Influence of photovoltaic power plants on the population densities of plateau pikas and plateau zokors

Significant spatial variation in the relative activity of plateau pikas and plateau zokors was observed across the study zones (Figure 3). Plateau pikas showed the lowest numbers of total and active burrow entrances in fenced areas, whereas PB contained fewer entrances than grazed sites, PEs, and beneath-PCs. Plateau zokors showed a different distribution pattern. Grazed areas had significantly fewer total and newly formed mounds than all other zones, while PE plots showed the highest number of new mounds (p < 0.05). As mound counts similarly represent digging activity rather than absolute density, these results suggest that both grazing exclusion and PV infrastructure create soil conditions more suitable for zokor excavation.

RDA (Figure S12) showed that spatial variation in rodent activity was associated with gradients in plant community attributes, soil conditions, and microclimatic variables. However, several sources of uncertainty remain. Microhabitat characteristics covary with both vegetation and microclimate, and the lack of pre-construction baseline data limits inference about absolute changes in rodent abundance. Spatial autocorrelation among plots may also influence effect sizes. Even so, the consistent associations between rodent activity patterns and environmental gradients indicate that PV installations and grazing exclusion jointly reshape surface and subsurface conditions, creating habitat mosaics that structure rodent distributions across the landscape.

Impacts of photovoltaic power plants on soil bacterial community structure and diversity

PV installations were associated with pronounced spatial structuring of soil bacterial communities across the landscape. As important decomposers in soil systems, bacteria contribute to nutrient turnover and may represent one mechanistic link between environmental variation and responses at higher trophic levels. At the phylum level (Figure 4), Proteobacteria dominated all zones but reached their highest relative abundance at PE sites, whereas fenced plots (E) exhibited lower proportions. Bacteroidota declined at PE, while Verrucomicrobiota were consistently enriched beneath panels (PC), between panel gaps (PB), and at PE relative to grazed (G) and fenced sites. Planctomycetota peaked in grazed soils, consistent with their sensitivity to disturbance and surface exposure. Because phylum-level patterns integrate multiple ecological strategies, these differences likely reflect combined responses to moisture, temperature, shading, and resource availability rather than single-factor effects. Genus-level patterns (Figure S10) showed similar microhabitat structuring. Candidatus Udaeobacter, RB41, and Terrimonas were the dominant taxa and exhibited clear spatial differentiation across PV environments. Candidatus Udaeobacter was significantly enriched beneath panels and was lowest in grazed soils. RB41 abundance was elevated in PC and PB relative to other zones, again with grazed areas showing the lowest values. Terrimonas was significantly more abundant in PC than in PE, E, or G. These genera are typically associated with oligotrophic or moisture-sensitive niches, and their distribution patterns are consistent with the cooler, wetter, and more shaded microhabitats generated by PV structures.Figure 4Soil bacterial community composition at the phylum level in different regions

Predicted microbial functions (Figure 5) indicated shifts in biogeochemical potential across PV-modified soils. Within the PV sites, predicted bacterial functional profiles suggested a greater representation of taxa associated with nitrogen fixation and denitrification, whereas functional categories associated with organic-matter decomposition were reduced. RDA (Figure S13) showed that variation in bacterial communities was significantly associated with soil nutrient availability and microclimatic factors, with the first two axes explaining a substantial portion of the constrained variance. Moisture and near-surface temperature were the strongest correlates, and permutation tests confirmed the significance of the overall model and individual predictors. Plant community attributes and rodent activity also showed measurable correlations with community structure. Environmental variables exhibited partial collinearity, and a considerable fraction of variation remained unexplained by the measured factors, indicating that multiple biotic and abiotic gradients jointly contributed to the observed multivariate patterns.

SEM (Figure 9) quantified the relationships among PV installations, microclimatic variation, soil conditions, vegetation structure, microbial communities, and rodent activity. Across all four models, PV showed a consistently significant negative effect on microclimate (standardized path coefficient = −0.802, p < 0.001), indicating that microclimate was the environmental module most sensitive and most consistently responsive to PV disturbance. In the plateau zokor model (Figure 9D), model fit was good (AIC = 239.207, Fisher’s C = 2.156, p = 0.707). PV had a significant positive effect on the plant community (0.626, p < 0.001), and soil also showed a significant positive effect on the plant community (0.308, p < 0.05). Plateau zokors were influenced primarily by a significant positive effect of the plant community (0.653, p < 0.001) and a significant negative effect of microclimate (−0.257, p < 0.05). The model explained 64.3%, 41.9%, 6.0%, and 55.9% of the variance in microclimate, soil, plant community, and plateau zokors, respectively. In the plant community model (Figure 9A), the overall fit indicated a degree of misfit (AIC = 175.194, Fisher’s C = 8.139, p = 0.017). PV influenced the plant community primarily through the microclimatic pathway, as reflected by the significant negative effect of PV on microclimate and the significant negative effect of microclimate on the plant community (−0.413, p < 0.01), whereas all soil-related pathways failed to reach significance. The model explained 64.3%, 6.0%, and 18.9% of the variance in microclimate, soil, and plant community, respectively. In the soil bacterial model (Figure 9B), model fit was good (AIC = 186.643, Fisher’s C = 0.167, p = 0.920), but the direct effects of microclimate and soil on the bacterial community were both weak and non-significant, with path coefficients of 0.041 and 0.198, respectively. The model explained 64.3%, 6.0%, and 4.2% of the variance in microclimate, soil, and the bacterial community, respectively, indicating that variation in the bacterial community was only weakly captured by the measured pathways. In the plateau pika model (Figure 9C), the overall fit likewise indicated a degree of misfit (AIC = 259.53, Fisher’s C = 11.303, p = 0.023). PV had a significant positive effect on the plant community (0.626, p < 0.001), and soil also had a significant positive effect on the plant community (0.308, p < 0.05). Plateau pikas were subject to significant negative effects from both microclimate (−0.802, p < 0.001) and the plant community (−0.658, p < 0.001), whereas the direct effect of soil was non-significant. The model explained 64.3%, 41.9%, 6.0%, and 55.9% of the variance in microclimate, soil, plant community, and plateau pikas, respectively. Discussion

Our study suggests that PV installations can act as important sources of ecological reorganization in alpine meadows, with their influence originating in microclimatic alteration and cascading through soils and vegetation to shape microbial communities and small mammal populations.41 By markedly modifying near-surface temperature, humidity, wind exposure and moisture regimes, PV arrays restructure soil conditions, plant assemblages and patterns of habitat use by fauna.42,43 These changes broadly align with our hypothesized pathways, although their magnitude and direction differ across taxonomic groups and fine-scale microhabitats.44 Collectively, our findings highlight the strongly context-dependent nature of PV impacts in high-elevation grasslands, shaped jointly by direct microclimatic shifts and indirect pathways mediated through soil processes and biotic interactions.

Microclimate and soil as primary mediators of biological communities

PV installations act as strong microclimatic regulators in alpine meadows, creating cooler and moister near-surface conditions that reshape patterns of energy and water exchange.45 These shifts collectively alleviate surface desiccation, improve moisture retention, and alter the soil energy balance.46 Notably, the most pronounced cooling and moisture-enhancing effects occurred directly beneath the panels, where the facilitative influence on grassland recovery often exceeded that of fencing alone. Comparable buffering effects have been documented in solar facilities worldwide, supporting the view that PV structures function as physical “microclimate modifiers” capable of reshaping local hydrological and thermal regimes.47,48

Microclimate emerged as the principal pathway through which PV infrastructure influenced ecological processes.49 By altering temperature, humidity, and wind exposure, PV systems reorganized soil nutrient dynamics and promoted vegetation growth.50 Soil appeared to function as an important intermediary linking microclimatic variation to biodiversity responses by influencing vegetation structure and soil bacterial community composition.51 Elevated soil moisture and moderated temperatures within PV zones enhanced the accumulation and bioavailability of carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus, consistent with findings that reduced wind stress and lower surface temperatures limit nutrient loss.52 SEM, RDA, and regression collectively indicated that microclimatic improvements bolster plant development both directly and through their influence on soil.53 Although certain microclimatic shifts were associated with changes in bacterial assemblages, the ecological implications of these shifts for nutrient turnover remain inferential because bacterial functional profiles were predicted rather than directly measured.54 We observed pronounced differentiation in bacterial community composition across the study zones.55 Previous work has shown that the “water-temperature-air/heat” conditions that develop among habitats can impose strong environmental filtering on decomposer communities.56 Because microbes function as decomposers that regulate nutrient supply within ecosystems, reorganization of bacterial community structure is likely to be linked with multiple trophic levels.57 Such restructuring may influence plant community structure by altering soil conditions, thereby modifying the habitat and foraging environment available to small mammals. In addition, soil bacteria can directly affect plant growth and reproduction through plant growth-promoting effects.58

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that PV infrastructure influences biodiversity through an integrated chain of microclimatic, edaphic, and biotic interactions, with the magnitude and direction of responses varying among taxa and across fine-scale microhabitats.59

Divergent responses across taxa

PV-induced environmental changes can be transmitted across multiple trophic levels through cascading effects that propagate through food webs.60 Soil microbial communities respond sensitively to shifts in moisture and nutrient availability,61 and the PV zones exhibited higher bacterial diversity and enhanced predicted functional potential—most notably for nitrogen cycling. Such predicted functional differences may be relevant to plant productivity, but direct links to ecosystem processes remain to be tested.62 However, bacterial responses within PV areas were not spatially uniform; community composition and structure were also shaped by within-array heterogeneity.63 At both the phylum and genus levels, dominant taxa were largely concentrated within the PV zone, likely because PV-driven shading, cooler and moister conditions, and substrate-supply gradients impose persistent environmental filtering on bacterial assemblages.64 Differences in soil bacterial community composition can directly alter organic-matter decomposition and nutrient release, thereby reshaping the soil resources that underpin plant community growth.65

Small mammal communities displayed similarly pronounced yet ecologically distinct responses to PV-driven habitat modification.66 Increased vegetation cover beneath and around the panels diversified foraging opportunities, while the cooler, moister, and more friable soils facilitated burrowing. These shifts may alter habitat suitability for predators and other higher trophic consumers, but their broader implications for trophic stability remain speculative and were not directly tested in this study.67,68 However, it is important to note that excessive increases in small-mammal activity may also entail trade-offs.69 High levels of foraging and burrowing can intensify localized herbivory and soil disturbance, alter microscale nutrient redistribution processes, and, under certain conditions, increase the extent of bare-ground patches and the risk of erosion.70 Such processes are more likely to become evident over longer time scales. Therefore, assessing the ecological roles of small mammals requires a more balanced, multi-faceted perspective.71 Microclimate and soil act as environmental drivers, while soil microbial communities serve as a crucial intermediary trophic component that links these abiotic factors to biotic responses across multiple trophic levels.72 Plants translate soil nutrient availability into aboveground community resources, and small mammals, in turn, modulate these dynamics through foraging and bioturbation.73 Together, these interactions shape the integrated, microhabitat-dependent ecological outcomes observed within PV arrays.74

Critically, the direction and magnitude of these responses differed across taxonomic groups, underscoring that the ecological impacts of PV infrastructure cannot be inferred from single-species or single-trophic-level studies.75,76 Rather, they emerge from a suite of interconnected pathways operating simultaneously throughout the food web.77

PV-induced spatial heterogeneity as an important driver of multi-trophic diversity

Fine-scale heterogeneity within PV arrays arises from the asymmetric redistribution of light, heat, and moisture imposed by panel geometry, creating a mosaic of contrasting microhabitats at meter scales.78,79 Beneath-panel zones receive substantially reduced irradiance, maintain cooler and wetter soils, and experience lower evaporative demand, whereas PE and inter-panel zones are relatively more exposed to solar radiation and subject to stronger fluctuations in temperature and humidity.80,81 These microhabitat contrasts are ecologically important not simply because they shift the mean state of local environmental conditions, but because they increase the spatial variability of key abiotic filters that govern species establishment, persistence, and interaction. In this sense, PV arrays restructure the alpine meadow environment into a heterogeneous template of ecological opportunities and constraints, allowing different taxa to occupy distinct microhabitats according to their environmental tolerances and resource requirements.82

This mechanism helps explain why PV-induced changes in biodiversity were spatially differentiated rather than uniform across trophic levels. In our study, beneath-panel habitats, with their cooler, moister, and less evaporative conditions, were more conducive to vegetation recovery and soil softening, thereby improving habitat suitability for burrowing small mammals and altering the belowground environment experienced by microbial communities. By contrast, the warmer and more exposed PE and inter-panel habitats likely favored plant species and soil bacterial taxa better adapted to higher radiation load, greater evaporative stress, and stronger temporal variability.81,83 The resulting pattern was therefore not a simple increase or decrease in biodiversity across the PV system as a whole, but a redistribution of diversity across microhabitats, with different trophic groups responding to different components of the same heterogeneous environmental template.

Importantly, the ecological significance of this heterogeneity extends beyond parallel, taxon-specific responses. Because plants, soil bacteria, and small mammals are linked through resource supply, habitat structure, and trophic interactions, spatially differentiated responses at one trophic level can propagate to others. Variation in moisture, temperature, and light availability can first modify plant cover, productivity, and community composition, which in turn influences food resources and shelter for small mammals, while also regulating litter inputs, rhizosphere conditions, and nutrient cycling pathways that shape soil bacterial assemblages.84,85 Soil microorganisms, as decomposers, may further feedback on plant performance through nutrient turnover, reinforcing the coupling between aboveground and belowground responses. Thus, PV-induced heterogeneity likely operates through both direct environmental filtering and indirect biotic pathways, generating cascading effects across trophic levels rather than isolated responses within any single group.86,87

Our SEM and regression results are consistent with this interpretation by identifying microhabitat-scale variation in water, temperature, and light conditions as the most consistent correlates of trophic-level diversity patterns. These findings suggest that the primary ecological effect of PV development in alpine meadows is not merely the alteration of background abiotic conditions, but the creation of pronounced small-scale spatial heterogeneity that reorganizes ecological niches. Importantly, multidiversity in this context does not imply a uniform increase across all trophic groups; instead, it reflects the integrated consequence of spatially differentiated responses among plants, soil bacteria, and small mammals across PV-created microhabitats. Thus, elevated multidiversity arises from the coexistence of distinct trophic assemblages across heterogeneous habitats, rather than from parallel increases in every component of the community. MTD therefore emerges as an integrated property of heterogeneous environmental restructuring.88 Recognizing this intrinsic heterogeneity is essential for understanding, and ultimately managing, the net ecological consequences of utility-scale PV installations in grassland ecosystems.

Taken together, by integrating the responses of plants, small mammals, and soil microbial communities with coupled microclimatic and edaphic processes, this study proposes an integrative framework for evaluating the ecological impacts of PV infrastructure in alpine meadows. Overall, our results suggest that PV arrays create pronounced small-scale environmental heterogeneity, and that this heterogeneity is associated with differentiated biodiversity responses across trophic groups. At the same time, implementing regular mowing or controlled grazing may help regulate vegetation structure, maintain desired ecosystem states, and accommodate grassland use; however, the net effects and potential trade-offs remain to be tested through small-scale pilot experiments and long-term monitoring.

In summary, PV-associated microclimatic modification and within-site spatial heterogeneity were linked to changes in plant community structure, small-mammal activity, and soil bacterial diversity across habitats. Taken together, these trophic-level responses suggest that the ecological consequences of PV development in alpine meadows depend not only on land-use conversion itself, but also on the fine-scale habitat mosaic created within PV facilities. Looking ahead, future research should establish comprehensive baseline datasets prior to PV construction, broaden biodiversity monitoring to encompass additional taxa such as insects and birds, and adopt long-term, multi-scale and cross-regional study designs. Such approaches are essential for rigorously evaluating the cumulative ecological impacts of solar energy on grassland ecosystems and for informing win-win strategies that simultaneously advance climate-change mitigation and biodiversity conservation.

Limitations of the study

Although this study highlights the potential impacts of PV installations on plants, small mammals, soil microbial communities, microclimate, soil properties, and multitrophic biodiversity in alpine meadows of the QTP, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study employs a space-for-time substitution design and lacks pre-construction baseline data; therefore, the observed differences should be interpreted as patterns associated with PV infrastructure rather than as direct causal effects. Second, the data were collected over a short period during the growing season and do not capture seasonal dynamics, limiting the reliability of extrapolations to year-round ecological effects. Third, belowground analyses were confined to bacterial communities, excluding other key microbial groups such as fungi. Finally, the study focused primarily on plants, small mammals, and soil bacteria, omitting insects, birds, and other important taxa, which constrains a comprehensive understanding of ecosystem-wide responses. Future research should incorporate long-term, multi-season, and multi-taxon monitoring, alongside the establishment of pre-construction baseline data, to more fully assess the cumulative ecological consequences of PV development in grassland ecosystems.

Experimental model and study participant details

Field study system

This study was conducted in alpine meadow grasslands on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau. The biological materials and study organisms examined in this field study included vascular plant communities, soil bacterial communities, and field activity signs of two native small mammals, plateau pikas (Ochotona curzoniae) and plateau zokors (Eospalax baileyi).

Plants

Vascular plant communities were surveyed in field plots across grazed grasslands, fenced exclosures, and photovoltaic microhabitats. Plant species were recorded at the community level. Individual plants were not maintained, propagated, or experimentally manipulated under laboratory conditions. Genotype, age, developmental stage, and sex were not determined because the study focused on community-level vegetation attributes rather than individual-level plant traits.

Small mammals

Plateau pika and plateau zokor activity was assessed indirectly using field signs. Plateau pika activity was estimated from active burrow entrances, and plateau zokor activity was estimated from newly formed mounds. No animals were captured, handled, housed, marked, or experimentally manipulated. Therefore, age, developmental stage, and sex were not determined. Because the study used field activity indices rather than individual-level animal measurements, the influence of sex on the results could not be assessed.

Soil bacterial communities

Soil bacterial communities were characterized from field-collected soil samples using full-length 16S rDNA sequencing. No bacterial strains were isolated, cultured, maintained, or experimentally manipulated. Therefore, strain-level maintenance, authentication, and mycoplasma testing were not applicable.

Human participants, cell lines, and primary cell cultures

This study did not involve human participants, human samples, cell lines, or primary cell cultures.

Method details

Overview of the study area

This study was conducted in Maqu County, Gannan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, Gansu Province, northwestern China—a region dominated by typical alpine meadow grasslands of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau. The photovoltaic (PV) facility examined in this study has been in operation for over five years, providing suitable conditions for evaluating long-term ecological effects. The research site was located in the Nima Town Photovoltaic Station (33°40′13″N, 102°9′6″E; 3,449 m a.s.l.), which has an installed capacity of 40 MW and covers approximately 69.33 ha. The park is enclosed by perimeter fencing and equipped with PV panels mounted at 1.8 m height with a 3.5 m row spacing. Panels rotate automatically along the north–south axis by up to 180° in response to solar irradiance.

The region receives 550–680 mm of mean annual precipitation, has a mean annual temperature of 1.2 °C, and an average relative humidity of 53%. Dominant plant species include Elymus dahuricus, Stipa purpurea, Poa annua, Kobresia humilis, Potentilla anserina, and Taraxacum maurocarpum.

Plot design

Field surveys were conducted in late July 2023. Local grazing (primarily sheep) occurs from May to October each year. Three land-use types were established within and around the PV facility:(1) a non-grazed PV area (PV),(2) a fenced exclosure without PV infrastructure (E), and(3) a traditionally grazed area without PV infrastructure (G).Within the PV installation, three microhabitat subzones were identified according to panel position: inter-panel gaps (PB), panel edges (PE), and beneath-panel centers (PC). For each zone and subzone, three replicate plots (22 m × 10.5 m) were randomly established. To minimize confounding factors as much as possible, we selected comparison areas within the same regional landscape context and conducted sampling during the same period.

Collection of microclimatic data

Microclimatic variables—including air temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed—were measured using a Kestrel 5400 portable meteorological instrument. Photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) was measured using an ECA-GG01 PAR meter, and evaporation was quantified using evaporation pans.

Measurements were taken every 2 h from 10:00 to 16:00 over three consecutive days at 20 cm above ground. For each sampling point, three replicate measurements were obtained and averaged. Ten evaporation pans (150 mm diameter) filled with water were placed at each sampling point for three consecutive days, and water loss was measured over 24-h intervals.

The microclimate measurements above represent only short-term conditions during the sampling period; future work could use continuous sensor monitoring across seasons to test whether these relative differences remain consistent over longer time scales.

Soil sampling and analysis

From each plot, soil samples were collected after removing aboveground vegetation using a 7 cm soil auger at depths of 0–10 cm, 10–20 cm, and 20–30 cm. The 0–10 cm samples were split into two subsamples: one stored at −80 °C for full-length 16S rDNA gene sequencing, and the other air-dried for physicochemical analysis. All deeper-layer samples were air-dried for soil chemical measurements.

Soil pH was determined using a glass electrode. Soil organic carbon was measured via potassium dichromate oxidation with external heating. Total nitrogen, nitrate-N, ammonium-N, and alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen were analyzed using a continuous flow analyzer. Total phosphorus was measured by NaOH fusion and molybdenum-antimony colorimetry, and available phosphorus was extracted with NaHCO3 and quantified using the same method. Total and available potassium were determined via flame photometry.

Plant sampling and survey

Total vegetation cover was estimated using the step-point method. Within 0.25 m2 quadrats, species-level cover was quantified using the point-intercept technique. Species frequency was determined from 30 circular quadrats per plot. For each species, natural plant height was measured with 10 replicate observations.

All vegetation within each quadrat was clipped at ground level, sorted into functional groups, oven-dried at 75°C–80°C, and weighed to determine total and functional group–specific aboveground biomass.

Survey of small mammal population density

Relative population density of Tibetan pikas was estimated using the burrow-blocking and reopening method, which quantifies the number of active burrows.The relative density of plateau zokors was estimated by counting the number of newly formed zokor mounds per unit area.

Soil microbial analysis

Soil microbial DNA was extracted using the PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen, USA). DNA concentration and purity were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and UV spectrophotometry. The full-length bacterial 16S rDNA gene was amplified using the KAPA HiFi HotStart Ready Mix PCR Kit and sequenced on the PacBio SMRT platform.

High-quality reads were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity. Taxonomic assignments were made using the SILVA database. Downstream analyses included α-diversity indices, β-diversity comparisons, community composition analyses, and redundancy analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Raw data were processed in Excel. One-way ANOVA was performed using SPSS 19.0, and figures were generated in Origin 2024. Multi-trophic diversity was quantified using the multidiversity index. Plant community diversity was quantified using α-diversity metrics. Pearson correlations were visualized using correlation plots. Microbial functional profiles were predicted using FAPROTAX. Redundancy analysis (RDA) was used to identify key environmental drivers of plant, rodent, and microbial diversity. Significant environmental variables identified by RDA were incorporated into piecewise structural equation models (SEM) to evaluate causal pathways. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used for dimensionality reduction of plant and soil bacterial community data. Model fit was assessed using Shipley’s test, with Fisher’s C (p > 0.05) indicating satisfactory model performance.
