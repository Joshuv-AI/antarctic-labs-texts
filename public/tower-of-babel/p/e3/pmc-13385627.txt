Research on tissue injury of low-temperature plasma ablation with different saline temperatures
Authors: Tao Li, Xiang Li, Ning Luan, XiaoDong Xu, Yan Yan, Luo Ba, Ze Zhao, Ke Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate tissue damage and recovery after low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation in saline at 0 °C,4 °C, and 25 °C, and to determine optimal temperature parameters for plasma radiofrequency ablation. A total of 72 sides of oral and buccal mucosa from 36 New Zealand white rabbits were randomly divided into a long-term observation group and an immediate execution group. Subsequently, they were assigned to three temperature groups: 0 °C, 4 °C and 25 °C. In the long-term observation group, after ablation of the buccal mucosa on both sides of the rabbits, the damage area and the number of days required for healing at each postoperative time point were analyzed. Another group collected samples after the same treatment and performed pathological analysis to evaluate the depth of tissue damage, the extent of inflammatory cell infiltration and the proportion of collagen fibers at different temperatures. The complete healing time of the 0 °C group was shorter than that of the other two groups. On the third day after surgery, both the direct damage and the overall damage areas of the 0 °C group were larger than those of the 4 °C group and the 25 °C group. From the first to the third day after surgery, the recovery rate, whether for direct damage or overall damage, was better in the 4 °C and 25 °C groups compared to the 0 °C group. Additionally, from the seventh to the ninth day after surgery, the direct damage recovery rate in the 4 °C group was better than that in the 0 °C group. The postoperative tissue damage depth in the 0 °C group was shallower than in the 4 °C group. There were no significant differences among the three groups in inflammatory cell infiltration or the proportion of collagen fibers in the injured tissue. The initial injury area in the 0 °C group was larger, and the recovery rate was slower at certain time points during the recovery period. However, it achieved complete healing earlier than the 4 °C group and the 25 °C group. These results indicate that the temperature of the saline solution not only affects the extent of early injury but also influences the subsequent speed of tissue repair.Level of evidence: II.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44361-3.

Introduction

Low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation utilizes the energy of low-temperature plasma radiofrequency to induce vigorous molecular motion, resulting in a distinct endogenous thermal effect. This process converts the electrolyte, typically physiological saline, between the radiofrequency ablation electrode and the tissue into a plasma layer. The accelerated particle layer rapidly dissociates molecular bonds with the target tissue, decomposing them into carbohydrates and oxides. This effectively ablates or coagulates the tissue, facilitating tissue necrosis. At a lower temperature (40–70 °C), tissue protein is liquefied and ablated, achieving a cutting and ablation effect while minimizing damage to normal tissues. This allows for efficient removal of diseased tissue, accompanied by hemostasis and suction within a short timeframe, thereby reducing patient discomfort and shortening the recovery period.

Currently, low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation has been widely used in otolaryngology head and neck surgery. Procedures include surgical resection of tonsil hypertrophy, adenoid hypertrophy, epiglottis cyst, obstructive sleep apnea-hypopnea syndrome, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and laryngeal carcinoma1–3. Various clinical surgical resection methods exist, including cold instrument stripping, CO2 laser resection, and electric knife resection, each with drawbacks such as residual tissue, recurrence, substantial intraoperative blood loss, and postoperative pain. Low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation offers advantages such as reduced bleeding, minimized tissue damage, improved visualization, and enhanced intraoperative efficiency4.

Despite operating at lower temperatures (40–70 °C) compared to traditional radiofrequency ablation, which enhances treatment efficiency and reduces operation time, intraoperative and postoperative blood loss, and complications and recurrence rate, the thermal damage caused by plasma radiofrequency ablation remains notable. No relevant research has been reported so far.

Therefore, we hypothesize that different medium temperatures may lead to variations in tissue damage and healing. To verify this hypothesis, we conducted a study using normal saline at three temperatures (two low temperatures and one normal temperature as a control) as the medium for low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation surgery. We evaluated tissue damage in terms of area, depth, and inflammatory response. This study aims to investigate the impact of plasma radiofrequency ablation on tissue damage at different medium temperatures and provide insights for its clinical application.

Materials and methods

Research overview

Animals were individually housed in home cages and provided with standard food and water before and during the experiments. The temperature of the feeding room was maintained at 20 ± 2 °C. All the animals were acclimated under standard laboratory conditions and had free access to standard water and food. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guiding Principles in the Care and Use of Animals (China) and were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Beijing YongXinKangTai Technology Development Co., Ltd (Refinement No. YXKT2023L002). Low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation was performed on 72 sides of buccal mucosa in 36 New Zealand white rabbits. Using a random number table, the experimental animals were randomly assigned to three temperature groups: the 0 °C group (n = 12), the 4 °C group (n = 12), and the 25 °C group (n = 12).

After using the plasma radiofrequency ablation system and matching plasma knife to ablate the buccal mucosa on both sides of New Zealand white rabbits, normal saline at different temperatures was used as the medium. The long-term observation group recorded and intelligently analyzed the direct damage and total damage area of the operation area on days 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, and 17. We also noted the number of days required for basic healing and complete healing of the operation area. In the immediate observation group, experimental animals were euthanized immediately, and materials were collected for pathological analysis. The depth of tissue damage, degree of infiltration of inflammatory cells (macrophages and neutrophils), and the proportion of collagen fibers were evaluated and compared at the three temperatures. Euthanasia was performed by injecting air into the ear vein. While euthanizing an animal, the animal was unconscious. And the animal corpses were disposed of in a pollution-free manner by a biomedical waste disposal company (Beijing Runtai Environmental Protection Technology Co., Ltd.). Direct injury refers to damage sustained in the area directly contacted by the plasma knife tip. Overall injury represents the sum of direct injury and thermal conduction damage to surrounding tissues (pale mucosal areas). Basic healing indicates a smooth surface in the injured area, though the mucosa remains discolored compared to surrounding normal tissue. Complete healing signifies no discernible difference between the injured area and surrounding normal mucosa. All assessments were conducted by two senior otolaryngology specialists using a blinded evaluation method.

Low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation method

The low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation system used in this study was the Coblator II plasma surgery system, manufactured by Smith & Nephew Medical Products Co., Ltd. along with supporting plasma electrosurgical unit (model: EIC5870-01). The cutting energy level was set to level 7, which is commonly used in clinical practice. The plasma cutter head was connected to 0.9% physiological sodium chloride solution.

The setup procedure included connecting the power cord, pedal wire, host end of the multi-function cable, flow control end of the multi-function cable, and the plasma cutter head. After turning on the power, the infusion tube was connected to the flow controller, and the controller’s function was adjusted. To test the plasma cutter head, it was immersed completely in the saline solution, and the ablation button was pressed. The presence of an orange-yellow light around the plasma cutter head or changes in water lines indicated its readiness for use. The key to plasma generation is saline. Once the infusion tube was connected to the flow controller, the “AUTO” switch was pressed, and the infusion set was activated to drip saline synchronously at the adjusted speed.

Histological assessment

The New Zealand white rabbits underwent ablation and were subsequently sacrificed for specimen collection. The specimens were fixed in formalin, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, sliced into 4 μm thin sections, baked, and stained with both Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) and Masson’s trichrome stain. HE staining was employed to assess tissue depth of injury, where a senior pathologist utilized K-Viewer 1.5.2.5 (Ningbo Jiangfeng Biological) pathological image analysis software to draw a vertical line from the damaged tissue edge to normal tissue. The longest line segment boundary with normal tissue, representing the deepest damage depth, served as the evaluation index. For evaluating inflammatory cell infiltration, such as macrophages and neutrophils, the line segment with the deepest damage was taken as the focal point, and the total count of inflammatory cells was recorded at 20× magnification.

Following Masson staining, collagen fibers appeared blue, while cytoplasm and nuclei appeared pink and brown, respectively. Proportion analysis of collagen fibers was conducted using a fully automatic digital pathological slide scanner KF-PRO-120 (Ningbo Jiangfeng Bio-Information Technology Co., Ltd.). The entire slide was scanned, and the Area Quantification module of HALO software was utilized to analyze the Masson-stained images. The blue color of collagen fiber staining was selected as a standardized positive criterion. The image was then analyzed to calculate the percentage of blue collagen fibers in the tissue.

Statistical analysis

Data processing was performed using the statistical software SPSS 26.0. Measurement data are expressed as mean ± SD. Healing time, injury area, recovery rate, and injury depth were analyzed using two-way mixed ANOVA with Tukey correction. Infiltration comparisons were conducted using the Pearson χ2 test. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, the recovery rate data were standardized: (X − Y)/X. For example, the recovery rate from postoperative day 1 to postoperative day 3 is calculated as (damage area on postoperative day 1 − damage area on postoperative day 3) / damage area on postoperative day 1.

Immediate euthanasia group

The postoperative tissue injury depth (µm) in the 0 °C group (216.10 ± 22.65 μm) was significantly shallower than that in the 4 °C group (265.56 ± 73.11 μm) and the 25 °C group (256.68 ± 51.84 μm), with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). However, there was no significant difference in tissue injury depth between the 4 °C group and the 25 °C group. For the depth of damage, an ordinary two-way ANOVA was used (row factor with 12 levels; temperature as the column factor with 3 levels; α = 0.05; total N = 36). The row factor showed no significant difference (F(11,22) = 1.9925, p = 0.0813), while the main effect of temperature was significant (F(2,22) = 3.8995, p = 0.0355). Subsequently, Tukey’s multiple comparison adjustment was applied to the main effect of temperature (n = 12 per group): the difference between 0 °C and 4 °C was significant (p_adj = 0.0400), while the differences between 0 °C and 25 °C (p_adj = 0.1030) and between 4 °C and 25 °C (p_adj = 0.8860) were not significant. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality of ANOVA residuals. Additionally, there were no significant differences in the degree of inflammatory cell infiltration in the injured area and the proportion of collagen fibers among the three groups (P > 0.05) (Fig. 5). The comparison of the proportion of no/with infiltration among the three groups was performed using Pearson’s chi-squared test (χ2 = 2.67, df = 2, p = 0.264). For inflammatory cell infiltration, a large amount means more than 60 inflammatory cells in a high-power field, a medium amount means more than 20 inflammatory cells but less than 60 cells, and a small amount means no more than 20 inflammatory cells in one high-power field.

Discussion

The primary principle of low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation technology involves generating a 100 μm plasma thin layer using a 100 kHz plasma radiofrequency electric field. This plasma layer, formed between the radiofrequency plasma cutter head and the body tissue submerged in electrolyte (usually 0.9% sodium chloride solution), is converted into low-temperature plasma. Within this plasma, charged particles are accelerated rapidly, gaining sufficient kinetic energy to break molecular bonds in the target tissue, leading to its decomposition into carbohydrates and oxides. This process facilitates tissue ablation or coagulation, ultimately promoting tissue necrosis6,7. The technique offers several advantages over traditional methods such as lasers and electric knives, which operate at higher temperatures (around 400 °C). Low-temperature plasma minimizes thermal damage to surrounding tissues, controls bleeding effectively, and facilitates rapid clotting. Additionally, normal saline can dissipate local heat during the procedure. Low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation integrates cutting, ablation, coagulation, saline flushing, and suction, enhancing surgical precision and reducing operating time. Its clearer surgical field of vision enables local and precise tissue manipulation, resulting in reduced surgical risks, shorter recovery times, and decreased patient discomfort. As a result, low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation has become widely utilized in otolaryngology due to its numerous benefits.

The key research direction lies in determining whether there is significant damage to the normal tissue surrounding the lesion following low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation, and its consequential impact on patient prognosis. Additionally, there is a paucity of studies addressing the temperature of normal saline irrigation in existing literature. Research has shown that ablation in a low-temperature saline irrigation environment may mitigate thermal damage compared to normal-temperature saline8. Therefore, our study delves deeper into tissue damage and inflammatory cell infiltration, building upon previous long-term naked-eye observations of mucosal recovery. Through pathological analysis, we aim to assess the extent of damage while incorporating an intermediate temperature of 4 °C between 0 °C and 25 °C, informed by existing literature. Our research focuses on exploring the effects of both 0 °C and 4 °C temperatures as low temperatures, utilizing them as flushing temperatures for normal saline during low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation. This investigation aims to determine which temperature induces less tissue damage, thereby providing a clinical basis for subsequent research on plasma radiofrequency ablation systems.

In this study, there was no significant difference in the mean number of days to primary healing among the three groups (P > 0.05). However, when comparing the mean number of days to complete healing across the three groups, the 0 °C group exhibited a shorter complete healing period than both the 4 °C and 25 °C groups, with statistically significant differences observed (P < 0.05). There was no significant difference between the 4 °C and 25 °C groups. Thus, our research indicates that the 0 °C group demonstrates distinct advantages in the complete healing of thermal injury tissue following plasma radiofrequency ablation.

Studies showed that after the mucosa was stimulated, inflammatory cells produce a certain degree of infiltration and spread9,10, to investigate the thermal damage to the tissue resulting from heat generated by the plasma radiofrequency ablation system and the subsequent damage to surrounding tissue via heat conduction, we defined direct damage and overall damage separately. Following low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation, the direct damage and overall damage areas of the three temperature groups all exhibited a trend of initially increasing and then diminishing. In our study, the total damage area of the 0 °C group peaked on the third day, with a significantly larger area compared to the 25 °C group, indicating a more pronounced reaction. Furthermore, the immediate postoperative injury area of the 0 °C group was notably larger than that of the 25 °C group. This disparity may be attributed to the initial 25 °C normal saline being closer to the normal body temperature of rabbits. In contrast, the 0 °C normal saline induced a transient reaction due to the sudden temperature shift from a lower temperature to normal body temperature, resulting in a larger immediate postoperative injury area in the 0 °C group.

In our study, from postoperative day 1 to day 3, the recovery rate, whether for direct injury or overall injury, was better in the 4 °C group and the 25 °C group compared to the 0 °C group. In addition, from postoperative day 7 to day 9, the recovery rate of direct injury in the 4 °C group was better than that in the 0 °C group. Due to the insufficient sample size in this study, further research by subsequent researchers is needed to better understand these mechanisms. It should be noted that in two-dimensional photos, the size of a wound could vary significantly depending on the shooting angle. Although we kept the camera’s height, distance, and angle fixed during the shooting process and tried to maintain the rabbit’s posture and mouth opening consistently, there were still some errors in recording the wound area and the results of recovery.

The study results indicate that there was no significant difference in the degree of inflammatory cell infiltration in the injured area among the three temperature groups (P > 0.05). This lack of difference may be attributed to the immediate execution, which did not allow enough time for an inflammatory response to occur in the body. However, the depth of postoperative tissue injury in the 0 °C group was significantly shallower than that in the 4 °C group and the 25 °C group (P < 0.05). Conversely, there was no significant difference in tissue injury depth between the 4 °C group and the 25 °C group (P > 0.05). Although there was no difference in the inflammatory cells produced by the three groups after tissue stimulation by the external environment, the depth of tissue damage caused by 0 °C normal saline as the medium was shallower. This indicates that tissue damage in the 0 °C group was less severe than that in the other two groups. Fibroblasts secrete type Ⅰ collagen, which then forms collagen fibers through a series of processes. The main pathophysiological change in scar tissue formation is the gradual increase of collagen fibers. However, there was no significant difference in the proportion of collagen fibers in the postoperative tissues of the 0 °C, 4 °C, and 25 °C groups. It is considered that the scar formation process after injury, such as the formation of collagen fibers, requires a certain amount of time. This limitation is also acknowledged in this study.

In summary, following low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation, there was no significant difference in the degree of inflammatory cell infiltration among the three temperature groups in a specific area. However, the depth of injury in the 0 °C group was significantly lower compared to the other two groups. From the perspective of long-term recovery, the 0 °C group required significantly less time to achieve complete restoration of mucosal tissue compared to the 4 °C and 25 °C groups, indicating that lower temperatures are more favorable for mucosal prognosis. This finding underscores the potential for optimizing the application of plasma radiofrequency ablation technology, showcasing significant innovation and advancement. Moreover, this study affirms that using 0 °C normal saline as a medium for plasma radiofrequency ablation outperforms using 4 °C normal saline. Nonetheless, further exploration by subsequent researchers is warranted to identify the optimal saline temperature for low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation, potentially uncovering a temperature range between 0 and 4 °C that offers the most favorable outcomes11,12.

Conclusion

The temperature of the saline solution not only affects the extent of early injury but also influences the subsequent speed of tissue repair. Lower temperatures appear to offer a promising avenue for minimizing collateral damage and enhancing overall tissue recovery post-ablation.
