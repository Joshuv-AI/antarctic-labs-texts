Immunometabolic reprogramming in lung cancer: interplay between immune and stem-like cells in immune checkpoint inhibitor resistance
Authors: Ji-Yong Sung, Eui Tae Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide. Despite the success of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly those targeting PD-1/PD-L1 pathways, a significant number of patients exhibit either primary resistance or acquire resistance over time. While genomic and epigenetic mechanisms contribute to this resistance, increasing evidence points to the pivotal role of immunometabolism—the interface of cellular metabolism and immune regulation. This review focuses on how altered metabolic states in both immune cells and cancer stem-like cells (CSCs) within the lung tumor microenvironment contribute to ICI resistance. We discuss key metabolic pathways involved in immune suppression, the metabolic plasticity of CSCs, and how these factors interact to shape a metabolically hostile tumor niche for immune effector cells. Finally, we explore emerging therapeutic strategies targeting immunometabolism to enhance immunotherapy efficacy in lung cancer.Keywords: Immunometabolism, Cancer stem-like cells (CSCs), Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) resistance, Tumor microenvironment (TME), Metabolic reprogramming, Metabolic plasticity, Immunosuppressive niche

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases and continues to be associated with a high mortality rate, despite recent advances in therapy. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have dramatically improved survival in a subset of patients by reactivating exhausted T cells [1]. However, the majority of patients fail to benefit from ICIs due to mechanisms of immune evasion rooted not only in tumor genomics but also in tumor-host metabolic interactions [2]. The tumor microenvironment (TME) of lung cancer is characterized by a complex and evolving metabolic ecosystem shaped by cancer cells, immune infiltrates, stromal components, and hypoxia [3]. Within this niche, competition for nutrients and the accumulation of metabolic byproducts profoundly influence immune cell functionality [4]. Moreover, a subpopulation of tumor cells termed cancer stem-like cells (CSCs) exhibit high metabolic plasticity and are increasingly recognized as architects of immune evasion and therapeutic resistance [5]. The emerging field of immunometabolism provides a conceptual framework to understand how energy metabolism shapes immune responses in cancer [6]. In lung cancer, elucidating the immunometabolic interactions between immune cells and cancer stem cells may provide novel strategies to overcome resistance to ICIs and improve the long-term effectiveness of therapies [7, 8] (Fig. 1).

Basic concepts of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are a class of immunotherapies designed to block inhibitory pathways that normally limit T cell activation, thereby restoring anti-tumor immunity. The most well-studied ICIs in lung cancer target the programmed death-1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis and cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA-4). By preventing PD-1 or CTLA-4 signaling, these agents reinvigorate exhausted T cells and enhance cytotoxic responses against tumor cells. Despite their clinical success, many patients experience either primary resistance—where tumors fail to respond initially—or acquired resistance after an initial period of benefit. Mechanisms underlying resistance include tumor-intrinsic factors (e.g., loss of antigen presentation, oncogenic signaling), as well as tumor-extrinsic factors such as metabolic constraints and immunosuppressive cell populations within the tumor microenvironment. Currently approved ICIs for lung cancer include nivolumab and pembrolizumab (anti–PD-1), atezolizumab and durvalumab (anti–PD-L1), and ipilimumab (anti–CTLA-4). These agents have demonstrated significant improvements in progression-free survival and overall survival in subsets of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients; however, resistance remains a major clinical challenge. Therefore, a deeper understanding of both the mechanisms of action and resistance profiles of ICIs is essential to optimize treatment strategies and develop effective combination therapies.

While this review primarily focuses on non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which represents the majority of available clinical and translational evidence, many of the immunometabolic mechanisms discussed may also extend to other histological subtypes such as small-cell lung cancer (SCLC). However, further studies are required to validate the applicability of these findings across diverse lung cancer subtypes.

Metabolic reprogramming in immune cells in the lung tumor microenvironment

The functionality of immune cells within the lung TME is tightly regulated by their metabolic states. Tumor cells, immune cells, and stromal components dynamically compete for essential nutrients such as glucose, glutamine, fatty acids, and oxygen. This competition, combined with tumor-derived metabolic byproducts and hypoxia, creates an immunosuppressive environment that impairs effective anti-tumor responses. Understanding how immune cell metabolism is altered within this niche is critical to overcoming ICI resistance [8, 9].

CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs)

CD8+ T cells are the primary effectors of anti-tumor immunity. Upon activation, they undergo a metabolic switch from oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) in quiescent states to aerobic glycolysis, a process essential for supporting proliferation, cytotoxic granule production (e.g., perforin, granzymes), and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) secretion [10]. However, this glycolytic reprogramming is impaired in the TME for several reasons.Glucose depletion occurs because highly glycolytic lung tumor cells consume the majority of extracellular glucose, leaving CD8+ T cells metabolically starved. [11]. This limits glycolysis-dependent activation signaling and inhibits mTORC1, a master regulator of T cell effector functions [12]. Excess tumor-derived lactate leads to lactic acid accumulation, which impairs CD8+ T cell effector functions through intracellular acidification and inhibition of glycolytic enzymes such as GAPDH. [13]. Lactic acid also suppresses cytokine production and promotes mitochondrial depolarization [14]. Low oxygen tensions in the lung TME create hypoxia, activating HIF-1α, which can paradoxically support T cell glycolysis but also induce exhaustion markers (e.g., PD-1, LAG-3) and compromise long-term memory formation. [15, 16].

Moreover, impaired mitochondrial function is commonly associated with exhausted T cells. Exhausted CD8+ T cells exhibit fragmented mitochondria, reduced spare respiratory capacity (SRC), and impaired fatty acid oxidation (FAO) [17, 18]. Notably, PD-1 signaling actively represses PGC-1α-mediated mitochondrial biogenesis, directly linking immune checkpoint pathways to bioenergetic failure. Further details on the metabolic constraints shaping immune dysfunction are described in Section “Metabolic constraints in the lung tumor microenvironment”.

Overall, metabolic insufficiency in CD8+ T cells limits their cytotoxic potential and contributes directly to primary resistance to ICIs.

CD4+ T cells: helper T (th) cells, effector T cells (teff), and regulatory T cells (tregs)

CD4+ helper T cells (Th cells) support CD8+ T cell priming, B cell function, and cytokine coordination. Effector T cells (Teff), which include activated CD4+ and CD8+ subsets, mediate direct anti-tumor activity through cytokine secretion and cytotoxic function. Like CD8+ T cells, Th1 and Th17 subsets rely heavily on glycolysis for proliferation and IFN-γ or IL-17 secretion [19, 20]. However, in the glucose-deprived TME, Th cell function is compromised, skewing differentiation away from inflammatory phenotypes. In contrast, Tregs, which play a pivotal role in maintaining immune tolerance, are metabolically suited to thrive in the hostile lung TME. Tregs depend on FAO, utilizing lipid oxidation and OXPHOS for energy, which enables them to function in low-glucose conditions. [19, 20]. Tregs rely on lactate utilization, upregulating MCT1 transporters to import and use lactic acid as a carbon source, which supports Foxp3 stability and suppressive capacity. [20, 21]. Tregs undergo AMPK activation under metabolic stress, which enhances autophagy and sustains mitochondrial health. [19, 20, 22]. Tregs also secrete immunosuppressive cytokines (TGF-β, IL-10) and express high levels of immune checkpoints (CTLA-4, PD-1). Their metabolic adaptability allows them to dominate the TME and actively suppress effector T cell responses [20, 21, 23–25]. Through ectonucleotidases CD39 and CD73, Tregs convert extracellular ATP into adenosine, which suppresses Teff cells via the A2A receptor [24, 26]. Through promotion of kynurenine accumulation, Tregs induce indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) expression in dendritic cells, which results in tryptophan depletion and immunosuppression, as kynurenine enhances Treg proliferation and suppresses Teff cells [25, 27].

The balance between effector and regulatory CD4+ T cells is tightly regulated by metabolism, with nutrient competition and lactate accumulation favoring Treg stability over Teff responses.

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs)

A key feature of MDSC metabolism is arginine catabolism, where arginase 1 (ARG1) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) deplete local arginine, an amino acid essential for T cell receptor (TCR) signaling and T cell proliferation [28, 29]. Through elevated expression of CD36 and other fatty acid transporters, MDSCs enhance fatty acid uptake, thereby fueling FAO and promoting immunosuppressive function. [30, 31]. MDSCs generate high levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) through NOX2 and mitochondrial pathways, which leads to T cell receptor nitration and dysfunction. [32, 33]. These metabolic pathways not only maintain MDSC viability but also actively shape a microenvironment where effector T cells (Teffs) are rendered anergic.

MDSCs exploit arginine and fatty acid metabolism to suppress T cell activity, thereby reinforcing an immunosuppressive microenvironment that undermines ICI efficacy.

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)

TAMs exist along a functional continuum from M1-like (pro-inflammatory) to M2-like (immunosuppressive). In lung cancer, the TME favors M2 polarization, characterized by enhanced OXPHOS, FAO, and upregulation of ARG1 and IL-10.

One key metabolic feature of M2 TAMs is lipid metabolism, whereby TAMs enhance lipid uptake and storage through scavenger receptors (CD36, MARCO), fueling mitochondrial respiration and supporting M2 gene expression [34, 35]. The hypoxia response involves HIF-1α activation, which promotes VEGF secretion and angiogenesis, thereby facilitating tumor growth and immune exclusion. [36, 37]. M2 macrophages regulate iron metabolism by modulating iron homeostasis through ferritin and ferroportin, which contributes to cancer stem-like cells niche support and oxidative stress regulation. [38–40]. Importantly, M2 TAMs secrete chemokines such as CCL22 and CCL18, which recruit Tregs and further reinforce an immunosuppressive loop [41].

Lipid-driven polarization of TAMs toward an M2-like state sustains immune suppression and promotes ICI resistance in the TME.

Dendritic cells (DCs)

DCs bridge innate and adaptive immunity by presenting antigens to T cells. Effective antigen presentation requires high mitochondrial activity and metabolic flexibility. Tumor-induced metabolic stress disrupts DC function by driving abnormal lipid uptake in DCs, which causes ER stress and defective antigen processing. [42, 43]. When glucose is restricted, glycolysis is limited, which is a critical pathway required for DC activation and cytokine production (e.g., IL-12). [44, 45]. Lactic acid impairs MHC class II expression and the upregulation of costimulatory molecules. [46]. The net effect is dysfunctional DCs that fail to prime T cells, contributing to an ineffective immune response.

Impaired glycolysis and abnormal lipid accumulation in dendritic cells compromise antigen presentation and T cell priming, weakening ICI responses.

Natural killer (NK) cells

Natural killer (NK) cells play a key role in detecting and destroying tumor cells without relying on antigen presentation. Activation of NK cells requires robust glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism. In the lung TME, nutrient competition reduces glucose and glutamine availability, impairing NK cell cytotoxicity [47, 48]. Metabolic reprogramming that restores NK cell activity, such as IL-15-mediated enhancement of mitochondrial fitness, is being explored in combination with ICIs [49].

Metabolic constraints in the lung tumor microenvironment

The lung TME presents a uniquely hostile and metabolically restrictive landscape for infiltrating immune cells. This harsh environment is shaped by the intrinsic metabolic activities of tumor cells, stromal remodeling, inflammatory signaling, and the physical characteristics of the lung tissue itself, such as high oxygen demand and exposure to inhaled toxins. Various immune cell subsets and cancer stem-like cells (CSCs) undergo distinct metabolic reprogramming that critically shapes their functional phenotypes in the tumor microenvironment. Several key features define the metabolic adversity of the lung TME.

Collectively, the metabolic constraints of the lung TME—driven by nutrient depletion, lactic acidosis, hypoxia, and oxidative stress—synergize to suppress antitumor immunity and reinforce resistance to ICIs.

Nutrient depletion: a tug-of-war between tumor and immune cells

Cancer cells in the lung TME exhibit a high basal metabolic rate and aggressive nutrient uptake, primarily through aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect) and glutaminolysis [50]. This leads to a competitive depletion of key nutrients essential for immune cell function, as T cells—especially activated CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes—require high rates of glycolysis for their effector functions.However, glucose is heavily consumed by tumor cells via overexpression of GLUT1 and activation of PI3K-Akt-mTOR signaling, leaving insufficient resources for T cells [48]. Glucose restriction leads to impaired mTORC1 activity, decreased cytokine production (e.g., IFN-γ), and impaired proliferation. As an essential nutrient for T cells and myeloid cell activation, glutamine is rapidly consumed by lung tumor cells through glutaminase (GLS1), fueling anabolic pathways such as nucleotide biosynthesis and redox homeostasis. Glutamine depletion not only inhibits T cell growth but can also enhance regulatory T cell (Treg) suppressive capacity via alternative metabolic reprogramming [51, 52]. Arginine is a critical amino acid for T cell proliferation and survival, but it is often depleted by myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and M2 macrophages through arginase-1 (ARG1) activity. [53]. Depletion of arginine results in reduced expression of CD3ζ, an essential part of the T cell receptor (TCR) complex, ultimately impairing T cell activation [54, 55]. In tumor-associated cells, tryptophan is catabolized by indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), leading to its depletion and the accumulation of kynurenine, which promotes T cell anergy, enhances Treg generation, and directly suppresses effector T cell responses through aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling. [25, 27]. These patterns of nutrient depletion act synergistically to suppress anti-tumor immunity while enhancing the function of immunosuppressive cells within the TME.

Nutrient depletion in the lung TME deprives effector T cells of glucose, glutamine, and arginine, while simultaneously enhancing Treg and MDSC suppressive activity, thereby weakening ICI efficacy.

Lactic acidosis: a double-edged sword of tumor metabolism

Lung tumors, especially NSCLC, are characterized by high glycolytic rates regardless of oxygen availability, resulting in excessive lactate production [56]. Lactate, transported via monocarboxylate transporters (MCT1/4) [47], accumulates in the TME and leads to acidification (pH < 6.5), exerting widespread immunosuppressive effects. In CD8+ T cells, high lactate levels inhibit TCR signaling, reduce IFN-γ production, and suppress proliferation. [47]. Lactate also impairs mitochondrial function and contributes to T cell exhaustion via epigenetic remodeling [57–60]. Acidosis in dendritic cells (DCs) inhibits maturation, antigen processing, and presentation capacity, which results in reduced priming of naïve T cells [61–63]. In natural killer (NK) cells, lactate hinders the release of cytotoxic granules and reduces the production of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) [61, 64]. Lactate acts as a fuel source in Tregs and M2 macrophages, enhancing the suppressive function of Tregs [65]. M2 macrophages also thrive under acidic conditions and increase secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β, further dampening immune activation [66]. Recent evidence suggests that lactic acidosis stabilizes HIF-1α and upregulates PD-L1 expression on both tumor and immune cells, directly contributing to ICI resistance [67].

Excessive lactate accumulation acidifies the TME, impairing T and NK cell function while promoting immunosuppressive phenotypes such as M2 macrophages, ultimately fueling ICI resistance.

Hypoxia: an architect of immune dysfunction

The lung TME is often hypoxic due to rapid tumor growth outpacing angiogenesis, compounded by abnormal and leaky vasculature [68]. Low oxygen conditions (hypoxia) facilitate immune evasion via various pathways. Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIF-1α and HIF-2α) mediate immune suppression by upregulating VEGF, PD-L1, CD73, and adenosine receptors, which collectively blunt T cell activation and promote immune exclusion [69–72]. Hypoxia affects T cell migration and retention by downregulating chemokines (e.g., CXCL9/10) necessary for T cell recruitment and upregulating CXCL12, which retains T cells in the stroma, away from tumor nests [73, 74]. Antigen presentation is impaired under oxygen scarcity, which reduces MHC class I and II expression and affects the antigen processing machinery in both tumor cells and antigen-presenting cells (APCs) [75]. Hypoxia has a differential impact: CD8+ and Th1 cells are highly sensitive to hypoxic stress, while Tregs and M2 TAMs adapt by enhancing mitochondrial respiration and shifting to FAO. Thus, hypoxia not only limits effective immune infiltration and function but also reshapes the immune composition in favor of regulatory and tumor-supportive phenotypes.

Oxidative stress and ROS

Elevated levels of ROS in the lung TME originate from both tumor metabolism (e.g., mitochondrial dysfunction, NADPH oxidase activity) and inflammatory myeloid cells. Although ROS at low concentrations support T cell signaling, their accumulation beyond a certain threshold can lead to cellular damage. In effector T cells, high ROS damages DNA, lipids, and proteins, leading to apoptosis or functional paralysis [76]. Checkpoint upregulation occurs as ROS enhance the expression of inhibitory receptors including PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3, thereby contributing to T cell exhaustion [77]. ROS promote stemness by activating NF-κB and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways, which enhance the self-renewal and therapy resistance of cancer stem-like cells [78]. cancer stem-like cells are known to thrive in ROS-rich environments and contribute to immune evasion through low MHC expression and high PD-L1 expression [79, 80]. Interestingly, while tumor cells develop antioxidant defenses (e.g., NRF2, glutathione), T cells often lack sufficient detoxification capacity, making them more vulnerable to oxidative stress [81].

Oxidative stress and excess ROS reshape immune cell metabolism by impairing antigen presentation and cytotoxic activity, while tumor cells exploit antioxidant defenses to maintain survival and foster ICI resistance.

Cancer stem-like cells: metabolic architects of immune resistance

Cancer stem-like cells (CSCs) are a rare but highly influential subpopulation within lung tumors that possess the hallmarks of stemness—namely self-renewal, pluripotency, therapy resistance, and a unique ability to initiate metastasis. In recent years, cancer stem-like cells have been increasingly recognized not only as drivers of tumor recurrence but also as key orchestrators of immune evasion through metabolic rewiring [82, 83].

Cancer stem-like cells act as metabolic architects of immune resistance by leveraging plasticity in glycolysis, OXPHOS, and metabolite secretion to suppress effector immune cells and sustain ICI resistance.

Characteristics of cancer stem-like cells in lung cancer

In lung cancer, cancer stem-like cells are typically identified by surface markers such as CD133, ALDH1, CD44, and EpCAM [84]. These cells are enriched following conventional therapies like chemotherapy and radiotherapy, which eliminate rapidly dividing cells but spare slow-cycling, quiescent cancer stem-like cells. The persistence of these cells is tightly associated with disease relapse, metastasis, and poor overall survival. Importantly, lung cancer stem-like cells reside in specialized niches characterized by hypoxia, acidic pH, and immunosuppressive cell populations such as M2-like TAMs, which further sustain cancer stem-like cells maintenance.

cancer stem-like cells in NSCLC also exhibit epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) features, expressing genes like ZEB1, TWIST1, and SNAI2, which are metabolically regulated and contribute to immune escape. These EMT- cancer stem-like cells show increased PD-L1 expression and reduced MHC class I levels, shielding them from T cell-mediated lysis [85–87].

In lung cancer, CSCs are defined by their self-renewal capacity, metabolic adaptability, and ability to shape an immunosuppressive niche, thereby laying the foundation for therapeutic resistance and tumor relapse.

Metabolic plasticity of cancer stem-like cells

Cancer stem-like cells are metabolically flexible and capable of switching between glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) depending on microenvironmental cues. This plasticity allows cancer stem-like cells to survive metabolic stress and evade immune surveillance, as many of them favor OXPHOS for energy production, unlike bulk tumor cells that rely predominantly on glycolysis. This preference supports a higher ATP yield, sustains mitochondrial membrane potential, and limits excessive ROS generation. Lung cancer stem-like cells upregulate key regulators of mitochondrial respiration, including PGC-1α, SIRT1, and TFAM, which promote mitochondrial integrity and biogenesis [88]. Lipid metabolism and FAO represent another hallmark of cancer stem-like cells metabolism, as upregulation of CPT1A, ACADVL, and PPARα enables cancer stem-like cells to use fatty acids as an alternative energy source [89, 90]. FAO not only supports energy production under hypoxia but also generates NADPH for redox buffering. This is critical in oxidative TME conditions, particularly in the lung where exposure to environmental toxins exacerbates oxidative stress. To maintain redox homeostasis, cancer stem-like cells tightly regulate intracellular ROS levels, and enhanced glutathione (GSH) synthesis together with NRF2 activation allow them to detoxify ROS and evade immune detection [91, 92]. Low ROS helps prevent immunogenic cell death (e.g., ferroptosis) and maintains the stemness phenotype [93]. Through mitochondrial biogenesis, cancer stem-like cells develop robust networks that are often larger and more complex than those of differentiated tumor cells, while fission and fusion dynamics mediated by MFN2 and DRP1 are regulated by nutrient availability and stress signals [94]. These mechanisms influence how cells determine their fate and respond to therapeutic agents. Together, these metabolic traits endow cancer stem-like cells with exceptional adaptability to environmental stressors, including those imposed by immune cells and metabolic competition.

The remarkable metabolic plasticity of CSCs, including their ability to switch between glycolysis and OXPHOS, enables survival under hostile conditions and reinforces immune evasion, ultimately sustaining ICI resistance.

Epigenetic–metabolic interplay in cancer stem-like cells

One of the most intriguing aspects of cancer stem-like cells biology is the tight coupling between metabolism and epigenetic regulation. Metabolic intermediates serve as substrates or cofactors for chromatin-modifying enzymes, enabling cancer stem-like cells to epigenetically reinforce their stem-like identity and immune resistance. For example, acetyl-CoA, generated via glycolysis or β-oxidation, fuels histone acetylation through histone acetyltransferases (HATs) [95]. Increased histone acetylation at stemness loci (e.g., OCT4, SOX2, NANOG) enhances transcription and preserves pluripotency [96]. S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), derived from methionine metabolism, supports DNA and histone methylation [97]. Methylation of immune-related genes (e.g., MHC class I) can result in silencing, diminishing immune recognition [98]. As a cofactor, α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) supports the activity of TET DNA demethylases and JmjC histone demethylases [99]. In cancer stem-like cells, α-KG levels affect epigenetic landscapes that regulate differentiation and immune checkpoint expression. For example, high α-KG availability promotes open chromatin and stemness-related gene expression, while also influencing PD-L1 and CD47 expression in a context-dependent manner [100–102]. These metabolic-epigenetic circuits are not static but dynamically adjust based on nutrient availability, immune pressure, and therapeutic stress, allowing cancer stem-like cells to persist and evade immune destruction.

Epigenetic–metabolic crosstalk in CSCs sustains stemness and immune evasion, where metabolites act as epigenetic regulators and epigenetic programs in turn reinforce metabolic reprogramming, ultimately driving ICI resistance.

Cancer stem-like cells-mediated immune suppression

Cancer stem-like cells actively contribute to shaping an immunosuppressive niche, both by direct immune evasion and indirect immune modulation, and one mechanism involves PD-L1 overexpression. Cancer stem-like cells often display elevated PD-L1 expression, which is transcriptionally regulated by mitochondrial stress responses and post-translationally modified via SIRT1-mediated deacetylation. This dampens cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) activity and facilitates ICI resistance [103]. Lung cancer stem-like cells activate the adenosine pathway by upregulating CD39 and CD73, which convert ATP to adenosine in the extracellular space [104]. Adenosine binds to A2A receptors on T cells and NK cells, suppressing their cytotoxic function and promoting Treg recruitment [105]. Cancer stem-like cells may favor OXPHOS, but they retain glycolytic flexibility, allowing them to secrete lactate through MCT4 under hypoxic conditions [106]. Lactate acidifies the TME, which suppresses effector T cell function and promotes M2 macrophage polarization [107]. Cancer stem-like cells release extracellular vesicles (EVs) that carry immunomodulatory contents such as miR-21, miR-155, and enzymes like IDO1 or ARG1 [108]. These EVs reprogram dendritic cells and macrophages into tolerogenic phenotypes, reinforcing immune evasion. “Don’t Eat Me” signals go beyond PD-L1, as cancer stem-like cells upregulate CD47 and adopt β2-microglobulin-low phenotypes to inhibit phagocytosis by macrophages and escape immune-mediated clearance [109, 110]. Collectively, these mechanisms render cancer stem-like cells highly resistant to immunotherapy. Not only do they evade direct killing by T cells and NK cells, but they also modulate the immune microenvironment to their advantage, thereby creating a niche that is hostile to immune effector cells and permissive to tumor progression.

Beyond these immune evasion mechanisms, cancer stem-like cells–immune cell metabolic interplay represents a unique dimension of therapeutic resistance. Cancer stem-like cells undergo profound metabolic rewiring, including enhanced oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), fatty acid oxidation (FAO), and redox buffering, which enables their persistence in hostile tumor microenvironments. These programs actively suppress immune surveillance by competing with effector lymphocytes for glucose and amino acids, secreting lactate to acidify the TME, and generating adenosine via CD39/CD73 to blunt T cell and NK cell activity. At the same time, immune cells impose reciprocal metabolic constraints on cancer stem-like cells through oxidative stress, nutrient restriction, and inflammatory metabolites, which drive cancer stem-like cells plasticity, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-like programs, and epigenetic remodeling. This bidirectional metabolic crosstalk forms a self-reinforcing circuit in which cancer stem-like cells shape an immunosuppressive niche through metabolic dominance, while immune-derived stress fosters cancer stem-like cell heterogeneity and survival. Together, these dynamics explain why cancer stem-like cells-enriched tumors often display profound resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors and conventional therapies, underscoring the importance of targeting cancer stem-like cells–immune cell metabolic interactions as a novel therapeutic strategy.

Immunometabolic alterations in both immune cells and CSCs can be systematically understood through a stepwise framework: metabolic rewiring → functional immune/CSC consequence → mode of ICI resistance → restoration of responsiveness by metabolic intervention. For instance, CSC-derived lactate secretion acidifies the TME, leading to impaired CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity and PD-1 upregulation, which contributes to primary resistance to anti–PD-1/PD-L1 therapy. Targeting lactate transporters (MCT1/4 inhibitors) has been shown to restore T cell glycolysis and reinvigorate ICI responsiveness. Similarly, CD39/CD73-mediated adenosine production by CSCs and Tregs generates high extracellular adenosine, suppressing Teff and NK cell function, thereby driving acquired resistance. Clinical and preclinical data indicate that CD73 blockade combined with ICIs can enhance immune infiltration and overcome adenosine-driven suppression. Furthermore, lipid metabolic reprogramming in M2-like TAMs promotes immunosuppressive cytokine release and Treg recruitment, which are tightly linked to non-response to ICIs. Pharmacological targeting of TAM lipid metabolism or macrophage reprogramming has been reported to synergize with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. By explicitly mapping these relationships, the review underscores how discrete metabolic pathways in CSCs and immune subsets converge to shape ICI resistance, and how metabolic interventions offer rational strategies to restore therapeutic efficacy.

CSCs mediate immune suppression by secreting lactate and adenosine and by remodeling the cytokine milieu, thereby paralyzing effector immune cells and reinforcing resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Therapeutic targeting of immunometabolism in lung cancer

Recent advances in understanding the metabolic interplay between tumor cells, cancer stem-like cells, and immune cells have unveiled promising therapeutic avenues in lung cancer. Targeting immunometabolism aims not only to restore effector immune cell function but also to disrupt tumor and cancer stem-like cells metabolic programs that contribute to immune evasion and therapeutic resistance. The complexity of metabolic networks demands multipronged strategies that combine metabolic modulators with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) or other conventional therapies.

Therapeutic strategies that target immunometabolic pathways—such as lactate blockade, adenosine signaling inhibition, and macrophage reprogramming—offer promising opportunities to overcome ICI resistance and improve clinical outcomes in lung cancer.

Restoring immune cell metabolism

Teffs require robust bioenergetic and biosynthetic support to proliferate, secrete cytokines, and mediate cytotoxicity [111]. However, the lung TME imposes metabolic constraints that impair Teff function. Therapeutic interventions aim to rejuvenate Teff metabolism and reinvigorate anti-tumor immunity, with mitochondrial rejuvenation addressing the mitochondrial dysfunction that is a hallmark of exhausted T cells within the TME. Compounds that activate PGC-1α—an essential regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism—have demonstrated promise in recovering mitochondrial function and increasing mitochondrial content [112]. Agents such as bezafibrate and small molecules that enhance NAD+ availability—nicotinamide riboside (NR) or nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN)—boost mitochondrial respiration, ATP production, and redox balance [113, 114]. Preclinical models of NSCLC demonstrate that PGC-1α activation reverses T cell exhaustion and improves response to anti-PD-1 therapy. Checkpoint-mitochondria coupling occurs as PD-1 signaling directly impairs mitochondrial dynamics by reducing mitochondrial fusion and biogenesis, which leads to decreased OXPHOS and increased T cell dysfunction. [115]. Combining ICIs with metabolic activators that target mitochondrial pathways synergistically enhances T cell fitness and tumor clearance. For example, co-administration of anti-PD-1 antibodies with mitochondrial-targeted antioxidants or SIRT1 activators has yielded improved outcomes in murine lung cancer models [115–118]. mTOR activation involves the mTORC1 pathway, which regulates glycolytic metabolism critical for Teff proliferation and effector function. While chronic hyperactivation of mTOR can lead to exhaustion, carefully controlled reactivation of mTORC1 signaling—using agents like rapamycin analogs in low doses or intermittent dosing schedules—can restore glycolytic flux, cytokine production, and cytotoxic activity [119]. This fine-tuning of mTOR signaling is an emerging therapeutic approach currently under clinical evaluation.

Restoring immune cell metabolism through approaches such as nutrient supplementation, mitochondrial rejuvenation, and checkpoint blockade synergy can reinvigorate T cell function and enhance responsiveness to ICIs.

Targeting tumor and cancer stem-like cells metabolism

Simultaneously targeting tumors and cancer stem-like cells metabolic dependencies can reduce immune suppression and sensitize tumors to immune attack, with OXPHOS inhibitors such as IACS-010759, a complex I inhibitor, and phenformin disrupting mitochondrial respiration and preferentially impairing cancer stem-like cells that rely on OXPHOS [120]. In preclinical lung cancer models, these inhibitors decrease cancer stem-like cells frequency, reduce tumorigenicity, and increase susceptibility to CTL-mediated killing [103]. However, systemic toxicity and metabolic compensation remain challenges. Cancer stem-like cells utilize FAO to support their energy production and maintain redox balance, and inhibitors like etomoxir (a CPT1A inhibitor) and perhexiline have demonstrated efficacy in preclinical lung cancer stem-like cells models by blocking FAO, increasing ROS, and inducing apoptosis [121, 122]. Importantly, FAO inhibition enhances immune-mediated clearance by increasing antigen presentation and reducing immunosuppressive metabolites [123]. CD73 inhibitors target the ectonucleotidase CD73, which catalyzes the conversion of AMP to immunosuppressive adenosine in the TME.Monoclonal antibodies such as oleclumab have entered clinical trials in NSCLC, aiming to restore T cell and NK cell cytotoxicity by blocking adenosine signaling [124]. Early results show improved immune infiltration and synergy with anti-PD-1 agents. Inhibiting lactate transporters MCT1 and MCT4 with compounds like AZD3965 reduces lactate export from tumors and cancer stem-like cells, thereby mitigating lactic acidosis [125]. This restores Teff glycolytic capacity, improves TCR signaling, and decreases M2 macrophage polarization [126]. Dual inhibition of MCTs is under active investigation for its ability to reshape the lung TME metabolically.

Targeting the metabolic dependencies of tumor cells and CSCs—such as glycolysis, OXPHOS, and adenosine production—offers a rational strategy to weaken tumor adaptability, disrupt immune suppression, and overcome ICI resistance.

Combination strategies

Given the metabolic complexity and plasticity of lung tumors, combination therapies integrating metabolic modulators with immunotherapy show the greatest promise, as exemplified by metformin combined with anti-PD-1 therapy, where metformin—a widely used AMPK activator and complex I inhibitor—modulates tumor metabolism and T cell function [127]. Clinical studies in NSCLC patients suggest that metformin co-administration enhances ICI efficacy, potentially by improving Teff mitochondrial fitness and reducing tumor glycolysis [128]. Ongoing randomized trials aim to confirm these findings. IDO1 inhibitors block IDO1 to restore tryptophan availability and reduce kynurenine-mediated suppression, showing initial promise, but phase II/III trials ultimately yielded disappointing results [129]. However, combined with other metabolic or immune modulators, IDO1 inhibitors may synergize to overcome resistance mechanisms. Dietary interventions provide a non-pharmacological means to influence tumor and immune metabolism, and ketogenic diets, by limiting glucose availability and increasing ketone bodies, may impair tumor glycolysis while supporting Teff oxidative metabolism [130, 131]. Similarly, arginine-rich diets or supplementation can counteract arginine depletion by MDSCs, enhancing T cell proliferation and function [132]. Clinical evidence remains preliminary, but ongoing studies are exploring these approaches as adjuvants.

As summarized in Table 2, several classes of metabolic agents are currently under clinical investigation in NSCLC and other solid tumors. However, the clinical translation of these strategies remains challenging. Trials of OXPHOS inhibitors such as IACS-010759 demonstrate that cancer stem-like cells are vulnerable to mitochondrial blockade, but dose-limiting toxicities and systemic metabolic compensation restrict their applicability. FAO inhibitors including etomoxir and perhexiline show potential to impair cancer stem-like cells survival and enhance immune clearance, yet their narrow therapeutic index and off-target effects raise concerns for long-term use. Similarly, metabolic checkpoint modulators such as CD73 or IDO1 inhibitors initially appeared promising for restoring anti-tumor immunity, but later-phase results have been mixed, largely due to patient heterogeneity and the absence of robust predictive biomarkers. Taken together, these experiences highlight the need for improved patient selection, incorporation of metabolic biomarkers into trial design, and rational combination regimens with immune checkpoint inhibitors. While targeting immunometabolism remains compelling, its clinical success will ultimately depend on overcoming these translational barriers.

Future directions and conclusions

The intricate immunometabolic landscape of lung cancer represents both a formidable challenge and a promising frontier in oncology. ICI resistance, which limits the efficacy of current immunotherapies, is increasingly recognized to arise from complex metabolic interactions between cancer stem-like cells, immune effector cells, and the TME. These interactions form a highly competitive and dynamic metabolic network where nutrient availability, mitochondrial function, and metabolite-mediated signaling pathways collectively orchestrate immune surveillance and tumor progression. While preclinical murine models have provided critical insights into immunometabolic reprogramming, their translational relevance to human NSCLC must be interpreted with caution. Species-specific differences in metabolic pathways, immune cell composition, and tumor microenvironmental dynamics limit the extent to which findings in mice can be directly extrapolated to patients. Moreover, growing evidence from metabolomic and transcriptomic profiling highlights profound interpatient heterogeneity in NSCLC, with distinct metabolic signatures shaping therapeutic responses and resistance mechanisms. These observations underscore the importance of validating preclinical discoveries in patient-derived models and incorporating biomarker-driven stratification into clinical trial designs to ensure that therapeutic strategies are both effective and broadly applicable.

Advancing beyond the limitations of current therapies will require future studies to focus on a comprehensive, system-level analysis of metabolic interactions. Emerging technologies such as single-cell metabolomics allow unprecedented resolution in dissecting metabolic heterogeneity within both tumor and immune cell populations. Analyzing metabolic profiles in real time at the single-cell level allows researchers to uncover how distinct cancer stem-like cells subpopulations flexibly rewire their metabolism to avoid immune surveillance and resist treatment. Simultaneously, advances in spatial transcriptomics and metabolite imaging enable mapping of metabolic gradients and cell–cell interactions within the native tumor architecture [133–135]. These approaches will illuminate how spatial proximity between metabolically distinct cancer stem-like cells and immunosuppressive myeloid cells fosters local immune exclusion, and how metabolic niches evolve during treatment. Moreover, metabolic flux analysis—using isotope tracing and computational modeling—can quantify substrate utilization and biosynthetic pathway engagement, providing mechanistic insights into metabolic bottlenecks that limit immune cell fitness or support cancer stem-like cells stemness. Leveraging these multi-omics datasets will facilitate the identification of robust metabolic biomarkers predictive of ICI responsiveness and resistance. For instance, profiling mitochondrial activity, fatty acid oxidation levels, or extracellular adenosine signatures in patient biopsies may stratify patients likely to benefit from metabolic-targeted combination therapies. This personalized metabolic profiling represents a crucial step towards precision immunometabolism, wherein therapeutic regimens are tailored to the unique metabolic landscape of each tumor. On the therapeutic front, the rational design of combination strategies that simultaneously target cancer stem-like cells metabolic dependencies and restore immune cell bioenergetics promises to disrupt the metabolic “arms race” in the TME. Agents that inhibit oxidative phosphorylation or fatty acid oxidation in cancer stem-like cells, combined with mitochondrial rejuvenators or glycolysis enhancers for effector T cells, could synergistically reverse immune evasion. Integrating such metabolic modulators with ICIs, chemotherapy, or radiation may also prevent the emergence of resistant cancer stem-like cells clones by depriving them of metabolic plasticity. Importantly, the field must also address key challenges such as the potential systemic toxicity of metabolic inhibitors, compensatory metabolic rewiring, and interpatient variability in metabolic phenotypes. The development of selective delivery systems, including nanoparticle-based carriers or tumor-targeted prodrugs, alongside adaptive clinical trial designs incorporating metabolic biomarkers, will be pivotal in overcoming these hurdles.

In conclusion, the convergence of immunology, metabolism, and cancer stem cell biology heralds a new era in lung cancer treatment. A deeper mechanistic understanding of how cancer stem-like cells metabolism reshapes the immune landscape will not only inform novel therapeutic interventions but also drive the discovery of predictive biomarkers essential for patient stratification. As metabolic profiling technologies mature and integrate into clinical practice [133–135], the vision of metabolism-informed immunotherapy—customized to the metabolic vulnerabilities of both tumor and immune cells—becomes increasingly attainable. Beyond their capacity to dissect metabolic heterogeneity, emerging single-cell metabolomics, imaging mass spectrometry, and spatial transcriptomics platforms hold significant promise for biomarker discovery and patient stratification in NSCLC. By resolving metabolic signatures at cellular and spatial resolution, these technologies can identify predictive biomarkers of ICI responsiveness and resistance that are invisible to bulk profiling. Integrating these datasets with machine learning and systems biology approaches will enable the construction of predictive models that classify patients according to metabolic vulnerabilities, thereby guiding rational trial design and therapeutic personalization. Ultimately, embedding such tools into clinical pipelines could transform immunometabolism from a conceptual framework into a practical cornerstone of precision oncology. Such advances have the potential to transform therapeutic outcomes, ultimately improving survival and quality of life for lung cancer patients worldwide.

Future progress in immunometabolism research will depend on integrating biomarker-driven clinical trials and precision targeting of metabolic vulnerabilities, paving the way for more effective and durable immunotherapy in lung cancer.
