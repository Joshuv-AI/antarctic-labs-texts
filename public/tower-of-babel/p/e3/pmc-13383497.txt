Development and validation of an Online Comprehensive Geriatric Screening (O-CGS) Tool
Authors: Supakorn Chansaengpetch, Ornicha Thititagul, Somboon Intalapaporn, Narongpon Dumavibhat, Angkana Jongsawadipatana, Orawan Supapueng, Arunotai Siriussawakul, Piyapat Dajpratham, Varalak Srinonprasert, Prapat Suriyaphol, Suwannee Suraseranivongse, Visit Vamvanij, Weerasak Muangpaisan

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe healthcare landscape is undergoing a significant transformation through digitalization. Embracing digital technology presents an opportunity to enhance the health and well-being of older adults. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and evaluate the validity and usability of an online comprehensive geriatric screening (O-CGS) tool for intrinsic capacity declines.MethodsBetween September 2023 and September 2024, 381 community-dwelling Thai adults aged 55 and above were recruited at the health check-up clinic of a university hospital in Bangkok, Thailand, using convenience sampling. To evaluate the validity, all participants had completed an initial online self-administered assessment followed by face-to-face reference standard assessments. Sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio, negative likelihood ratio, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and accuracy were reported.ResultsAmong 381 participants, the median age was 64.8 years, with 85% being female. The prevalence of geriatric syndromes ranged from 2.4% (frailty) to 50% (cognitive impairment). The sensitivity of the screening tool varied from 48% (for sarcopenia) to 100% (for depression), while specificity ranged from 54% (for fall risk) to 98% (for sarcopenia). Notably, the newly developed online cognitive screening tool demonstrated acceptable performance in detecting mild cognitive impairment and severe cognitive impairment, with a sensitivity of 61% and specificity of 63%. On average, participants spent 37 min completing the O-CGS. Additionally, participants reported high satisfaction with the tool, with a mean score of 4.4 out of 5.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated the satisfactory performance and feasibility of a self-administered O-CGS tool, aligning with the healthy aging concept.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27855-7.Keywords: Healthy aging, Screening, Geriatric syndromes, Intrinsic capacity, Digital technology, Delivery of health care, Community health care

Background

The world’s population is aging rapidly. By 2050, the proportion of older adults will double from 12% to 22% [1]. Thailand is already facing this shift, with over 20% of the population aged 60 or older, making it the second most-aged ASEAN country [2, 3]. By 2031, Thailand is expected to become a super-aged society, with over 28% of its population aged 60 and above [4]. This demographic transition brings significant challenges, including a rising old-age dependency ratio and increasing public healthcare costs [3].

Older age often coincides with multimorbidity and functional decline. By 2050, Thailand’s population of individuals aged 80 and above is projected to increase tenfold, with one-quarter of them requiring assistance with daily activities [3]. Without adequate support, long-term care (LTC) dependence may rise, placing a substantial financial burden on the healthcare system. To address these challenges, it is crucial to prioritize preventive health approaches, as reflected in Thailand’s 20-year National Strategic Plan (2017–2036) and the UN’s Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030) [5, 6]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has introduced the Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) framework to optimize functional ability by identifying declines in intrinsic capacity. These guidelines are designed for the primary care management of older adults in the community, aiming to detect declines across six key domains—locomotion, vitality, cognition, psychological well-being, vision, and hearing—through face-to-face assessments and an online application for screening by healthcare professionals. However, self-screening using the online tool and its accuracy compared to face-to-face assessments have not yet been reported in current studies [7]. In Thailand, the Ministry of Public Health (MoPH) has been developing a screening and assessment manual for older adults since 2014, which was updated in 2021. Additionally, a community-based older adult screening system was developed, with over 1.05 million village health volunteers (VHVs) providing services as first-line healthcare providers alongside public health officers. This initiative aims to screen older adults in communities nationwide across nine key health domains, including cognition, locomotion, nutrition, vision, hearing, depression and suicide risk, urinary incontinence, activities of daily living (ADLs), and oral health. However, this screening process relies on trained VHVs, which requires a significant workload and is not a self-screening tool for older adults. Additionally, further data is needed to assess the accuracy of the screening compared to standard clinical assessments. Moreover, the Thai MoPH is promoting geriatric clinics across all healthcare levels, focusing on comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA), health promotion, and multidisciplinary care.

CGA is vital in managing older adults’ health and addressing physical, mental, functional, and social well-being leading to a personalized care plan [8]. However, studies have reported mixed outcomes, with systematic reviews showing no significant impact on mortality, functional status, or hospital stay, while meta-analyses suggest it can reduce caregiver burden and improve quality of life, particularly for those over 80 and in short-term follow-ups [9, 10]. These discrepancies may be due to variations in CGA characteristics, patient profiles, and healthcare settings. Essentially, identifying those most likely to benefit from CGA is essential, with frail and multimorbid individuals being suitable candidates [8]. While CGA is detailed and typically used in specialized settings for high-risk older adults, geriatric screening focuses on early detection and prevention, allowing for proactive management before significant decline occurs. Both approaches are complementary, with screening serving as an entry point to more comprehensive assessments when needed.

Thailand has developed a comprehensive geriatric screening (CGS) tool covering multiple health domains, including fall risks, cognition, psychological well-being, nutritional status, hearing, vision, frailty status, sarcopenia status, and oral health. However, implementing CGS within primary care remains time-intensive, often requiring up to 45 min per visit, which poses a burden on healthcare providers [11]. To enhance efficiency, it is necessary to improve healthcare delivery by optimizing patient experiences and supporting the healthcare workforce.

Digital transformation is reshaping healthcare, offering opportunities to promote preventive care and improve service delivery for older adults [12]. The growing availability of digital geriatric assessment tools presents a promising solution to streamline pre-visit screenings and reduce consultation time. Studies from various countries have demonstrated the feasibility of self-administered online screening tools for geriatric syndromes among community-dwelling older adults [13, 14]. However, many of these tools require further validation to ensure their effectiveness in screening for geriatric conditions. Therefore, this study aims to develop and validate an online geriatric health screening tool tailored for pre-visit assessments, as well as to assess its usability.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study was conducted with a cross-sectional diagnostic study design at the check-up and outpatient clinics of the Department of Preventive and Social Medicine, Siriraj Hospital, which is a tertiary hospital in Bangkok, Thailand. Health maintenance refers to routine preventive healthcare practices, including regular health check-ups, screening, vaccination, and individualized lifestyle recommendations based on health evaluations to preserve health and prevent disease; therefore, the patients visiting our clinics primarily comprised individuals seeking these services, resulting in a predominantly healthy cohort. Community-dwelling adults aged 55 years and over were recruited by convenience sampling from September 2023 to September 2024.

Study population

The study population consisted of community-dwelling mature and older adults aged 55 years and over who were able to use digital technology, including the Internet and tablets. Individuals were excluded if they had unstable psychiatric disorders, advanced or severe medical conditions, acute illnesses such as acute cardiovascular disease or infection, or if they were unable to communicate in Thai, stand, walk, or hold a grip. Participants with implanted cardiac devices or metal implants were also excluded. Withdrawal criteria included participants with incomplete data necessary for assessing the diagnostic performance of the screening tools.

Development of the online screening tool

The online geriatric health screening tool was developed through conference sessions with experienced geriatricians. The cognitive screening test used in our study was newly developed by our research team through a structured, multi-step process. A blind content validity index (CVI) assessment of the novel online cognitive assessment was conducted. This involved a panel of geriatric experts from relevant fields, including a geriatrician, neurologist, psychiatrist, and senior nurse. The CVI threshold set for this assessment was 0.8 or higher, indicating acceptable content validity of the screening tool [15]. Our novel online cognitive screening tool demonstrated a favorable result, showing a CVI of 1.0. Subsequently, the established online tool for the study was created using SurveyMonkey® (www.surveymonkey.com). We piloted the study among 50 participants aged 60 and over to demonstrate the feasibility of a self-administered online geriatric health screening tool. Participants’ completion time was 26 min on average. Nearly all participants expressed satisfaction, with 60% reporting being very satisfied and 38% satisfied.

Assessment process

The assessment process comprised two steps, which were an initial online self-assessment health screening (Step 1) followed by a reference standard evaluation (Step 2). All participants consecutively completed Step 1 and Step 2 assessments at the same visit or no longer than four weeks apart, regardless of the results of Step 1, to validate the online screening tools. Step 1 was the online screening. All validated health domains included cognition, mood, nutritional status, locomotor function, frailty status, and sarcopenia status. In addition, there were also questionnaires on eye, Ear Nose Throat (ENT), and oral health conditions. Step 2 comprised in-person reference standard assessments for cognition, mood, nutritional status, locomotor function, frailty status, and sarcopenia status.

Covariates regarding demographics, lifestyle factors, physical activity levels, co-morbidities, activities of daily living (ADLs), the medication used, and health maintenance were collected through the online tool. Measurements of anthropometric data and other assessments that were parts of the reference standard tests (i.e., body composition analysis [BIA], hand grip strength measurement, and the Short Performance Physical Battery [SPPB]) [16] were carried out during a face-to-face assessment. All measurements were performed by trained staff in accordance with the measurement protocols. Notably, the staff were blinded to the results of the Step 1 assessment to prevent the potential bias influenced by the screening outcomes.

Measurements

Physical activity

Physical activity levels were evaluated using the validated Thai version of the short format International Physical Activity Questionnaire (Thai short IPAQ) [17, 18]. The IPAQ-SF estimates physical activity by calculating total MET-minutes per week, obtained by multiplying the minutes and days spent in walking, moderate, and vigorous activities by their respective standard MET values (3.3, 4.0, and 8.0) and summing them. The total score is then used to classify individuals into low, moderate (≥ 600 MET-min/week), or high (≥ 3000 MET-min/week) physical activity levels, or to provide a continuous MET-minutes/week score, based on activities performed during the last 7 days.

Functional status

The assessed functions included both basic and instrumental ADLs, evaluated using the Barthel index and the Lawton IADL (L-IADL) scale [19–22], respectively. The Barthel index and L-ADL scores have a possible range of 0 to 100 and 0 to 8, respectively. Higher scores indicate a greater level of independence in participants’ ADLs. However, some individuals may not have performed activities such as housekeeping, food preparation, and laundry. To account for this, the option “not available” for these specific tasks was provided. Consequently, the corrected IADL (C-IADL) score was calculated by the following formula: , where a is the number of activities never performed in one’s life [23].

Anthropometric measures

Anthropometric measurements included body weight in kilograms (kg), height in centimeters (cm), body mass index (BMI), calf circumference (CC) in cm, and mid-arm circumference (MAC) in cm. Body weight and height were assessed using the calibrated digital measuring instrument Seca 787 (Seca GmbH & Co. kg in Hamburg, Germany). All followed the standard recommendation [24]. For example, to measure the CC, the participant could either sit with their legs hanging loosely or stand with their weight evenly distributed on both feet. The tape measure was wrapped around the calf at its widest part, and the CC was then recorded. Additional measurements above and below the initial point were taken to ensure that the largest measurement was accurately obtained. Self-reported current height and body weight, and body weight in the past year were also obtained from the online screening.

Handgrip strength and skeletal muscle mass

Handgrip strength was assessed using a handheld digital dynamometer (Takei 5401) with a minimum measurement of 0.1 kg. The measurements were taken three times on the dominant hand, and the maximum value of the three dynamometer values (in kg) was used for the analysis. The BIA was conducted using Tanita MC-780 MA. The total appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) in kg was collected.

Step 1 assessment (online screening)

Cognition

The cognitive domain was evaluated through a combination of three brief but essential tasks designed to assess key cognitive functions affected early in mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia: the three-picture recall task, self-reported memory and orientation problems, and the clock reading test.

These components were selected to collectively cover distinct yet complementary cognitive domains—memory, orientation, and visuospatial function—while ensuring feasibility for self-administered, online delivery. This multimodal design enhances both diagnostic coverage and practical usability, particularly in community-based, older adult populations.

First, the three-picture recall task was developed to evaluate an individual’s capacity to remember and recognize visual information, primarily targeting delayed visual memory, a domain often impaired in amnestic MCI and early Alzheimer’s disease. Prior research shows that visual memory recall is comparable in diagnostic utility to verbal memory in predicting progression to dementia [23]. Participants were shown three real-life images simultaneously—a dog, a car, and an eye —and instructed to memorize them. Following a brief distraction task (i.e., the other two components), participants were asked to select the correct images from a grid of nine pictures categorized into animals (dog, cat, rooster), facial features (eye, ear, lips), and vehicles (car, bus, motorcycle). This design is based on the evidence that visual recall is a sensitive indicator for early cognitive decline and has comparable predictive validity to verbal memory tests.

Second, self-reported memory and orientation problems were assessed using a question adapted from the WHO ICOPE Handbook App [25]: “Do you have problems with memory or orientation, such as not knowing where one is or what day it is?”. This question captures subjective cognitive complaints, which, while limited in sensitivity, are important early indicators of perceived cognitive change and align with internationally endorsed screening frameworks for intrinsic capacity.

Third, the clock reading test was designed to evaluate visuospatial and executive function, domains commonly affected in various dementias. Participants were shown three analog clock faces set to 11:10, 2:40, and 5:50 (Fig. 1) and were instructed to type in the corresponding times (e.g., “11.10” or “23.10” for the first clock). This task builds on evidence from prior studies validating its accuracy in identifying cognitive impairment and its correlation with established assessments such as the clock drawing test and the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [26, 27].

Both the picture recall and clock reading tasks were scored out of three points each, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 6. A screening result was considered positive if the participant scored ≤ 5/6 on the combined recall and clock tasks or responded “yes” to the self-reported memory/orientation question. This scoring threshold was determined based on pilot study data, which showed acceptable sensitivity and specificity in detecting MCI.

Taken together, these three components offer a brief yet multidimensional cognitive screen optimized for online self-administration in older adults. They were selected for their diagnostic relevance, user accessibility, and alignment with validated cognitive constructs.

Mood

This domain focuses on screening for depression using the Two Questions Plus (“2Q Plus”), which has been recommended by the Department of Mental Health, the Ministry of Public Health of Thailand [28, 29]. It consisted of three questions with a binary response: (1) “In the past two weeks, have you felt down, depressed, or hopeless?” (2) “In the past two weeks, have you experienced little interest or pleasure in doing things?” and (3) “In the past month, have you felt overwhelmingly sad and lacked the motivation to continue with life?“. The former two questions were known as “2Q”. A result was considered positive when the participant responded “yes” to any question.

Nutritional status

Malnutrition screening was conducted using the two questions with BMI criteria. The following questions required a yes/no response: (1) “Have you been experiencing a loss of appetite or eating less?” (2) “In the past three months, have you unintentionally lost weight of more than 3 kg?“. In addition to these questions, the self-reported BMI was used to identify those at risk for malnutrition. A BMI of less than 18.5 kg/m2 was considered an indicator of potential malnutrition. This questionnaire was based on the Malnutrition Screening Tool (MST), showing a sensitivity of 73% and a specificity of 84% [30]. The result was considered positive when the participant responded “yes” to either question or had a BMI less than 18.5 kg/m2.

Locomotor function (Fall risk)

This domain aims to assess fall risk using a questionnaire based on the Three Key Questions (3KQ), considered a part of the CDC’s Stopping Elderly Accidents, Deaths, & Injuries (STEADI) recommendation for fall prevention in older adults [31]. The questions comprised: (1) “Do you feel unsteady when standing or walking?” (2) “Do you worry about falling?” and (3) “How many times have you fallen in the past year?“. The first two questions had a yes/no response format, while the third question allowed for multiple response options, including none, 1–3 falls, and 4 or more falls. Moreover, the definition of a fall was provided with the questionnaire, which was an unintended landing on the floor, ground, or a lower level without involvement of a major intrinsic event (such as a heart attack, stroke, or seizure) or an overwhelming external hazard (such as being hit by a vehicle) [32]. A result was considered positive when the participant responded “yes” to any question.

Frailty status

The screening for frailty utilized the Thai version of the FRAIL scale (T-FRAIL), which has been validated [33, 34]. The FRAIL scale is a 5-item questionnaire assessing characteristics related to fatigue, resistance (ability to climb one flight of stairs), ambulation (ability to walk 300–400 m in the distance), the number of comorbidities (≥ 5 illnesses), and loss of weight (≥ 5% in the past year). Each item required a self-report response, scored as either 0 or 1 point based on the presence of the characteristic. The percentage change in body weight between the participant’s self-reported current and body weight from the past year was calculated for the assessment. The total score ranged from 0 to 5, with a higher score indicating greater frailty characteristics. A conventional cut-off score of the FRAIL scale is three or greater. Meanwhile, according to the T-FRAIL [34], a cut-off score of one or greater demonstrated the highest sensitivity (93%) with a specificity of 61%.

Sarcopenia status

Two questionnaires were used for sarcopenia screening. First, the Thai version of the MSRA-5 questionnaire was utilized [35], assessing aspects regarding age (70 years or older), hospitalization in the past year, ability to walk for 1 km, having three meals a day, and experiencing weight loss of more than 2 kg in the past year. This 5-item questionnaire has also been modified from the original MSRA-5 [36] to improve its sensitivity in detecting sarcopenia. According to the modified version, the screening was considered positive if the participant scored 30 or lower. Additionally, the Thai version of the SARC-F questionnaire [35] was also used to assess sarcopenia status. This questionnaire consisted of five domains that evaluate difficulties related to strength (lifting 10 pounds [4.5 kg] of weight), assistance in walking (walking across a room), transferring from a chair or bed, climbing one flight of stairs, and a history of falls in the past year. The maximum score was 10, and a score of 4 or more indicated a positive result for at-risk sarcopenia [37]. SARC-CalF is also evaluated [38]. It combines the SARC-F questionnaire with calf circumference (CC) measurement. The SARC-F consists of five items (strength, walking assistance, rising from a chair, stair climbing, and falls), each scored 0–2, giving a total score of 0–10; an additional 10 points are added if CC is below the specified cutoff (e.g., < 33 cm in women, < 34–36 cm in men). The total score ranges from 0 to 20, and a score ≥ 11 indicates a positive screen for sarcopenia.

Eye, ENT, and oral health conditions

The part determined the participant’s need for eye and ENT specialist consultation or appointment using a questionnaire. These questions included: (1) “Have you undergone an eye examination by an ophthalmologist in the past year?” (2) “Have you been experiencing any of the following (episodes of dizziness or vertigo, ringing in the ears, hearing loss, and difficulties with swallowing, aspiration, or chewing? )”.

For the oral health domain, this screening aimed to address any potential dental and oral health issues and evaluated the need for dental department consultation or appointment accordingly. Participants were asked whether any symptoms related to their dental and oral health were present. These included toothache or oral pain, bleeding or pus oozing when toothbrushing, the need for wearing a denture, having a tooth hole, and no dental visit for 6–12 months. However, we did not validate the questionnaires for screening these conditions.

Step 2 assessment (in-person reference standard)

Cognition

Cognitive assessment was conducted using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) with permission [39]. Certified psychologists performed the cognitive assessment using the validated Thai version of the MoCA (MoCA-Thai) [40]. In this study, participants whose score was 24 or less on the MoCA-Thai without cognitively related ADL impairments were classified as having MCI. Those with cognitively related ADL impairment were classified as having dementia, while participants whose score was 25 or greater were considered cognitively normal.

Additionally, the MOCA scoring also uses cut-off ranges based on educational levels to increase the accuracy of classification, which are divided into three groups: EDU 1, Primary education (6 years or less of education) or no formal education; EDU 2, Secondary education (junior high and high school); EDU 3, Higher education (university level or equivalent, bachelor’s degree or higher). The cutoff points for each cognitive status are as follows: Normal cognitive function: EDU 1: ≥ 20, EDU 2: ≥ 23, EDU 3: ≥ 25; MCI: EDU 1: 16–19, EDU 2: 19–22, EDU 3: 22–24; Significant cognitive impairment or dementia: EDU 1: ≤ 15, EDU 2: ≤ 18, EDU 3: ≤ 21.

Mood

Depression was assessed using the Thai version of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [41]. According to a meta-analysis, higher specificity was observed for older participants across various cutoff values, compared to semi-structured interviews as a standard evaluation, with a median increase of 5% [42]. Participants with a PHQ-9 score of 7 or above were classified as having depression in this study.

Nutritional status

The Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) criteria was used as the reference standard for defining malnutrition [43]. The criteria consider three phenotypic characteristics and two etiologic criteria. Malnutrition was fulfilled when at least one phenotypic criterion and one etiologic criterion were present. We concurrently used the Thai-translated Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) to evaluate the participant’s nutritional status [44, 45]. The MNA includes a questionnaire and an anthropometric assessment considering the BMI, CC, and MAC. The MNA classified individuals into three categories: a score of 24 to 30 indicated normal nutritional status, a score of 17 to 23.5 indicated being at risk of malnutrition, and a score below 17 indicated malnourishment.

Locomotor function (Fall risk)

The assessment of locomotor function was conducted using the SPPB test [16], evaluating balance, gait speed, and lower extremity strength. It has been shown to be a useful tool for predicting future falls in community-dwelling older adults [46]. A cutoff score of 9 or lower was used as a reference standard to identify individuals at risk of falls.

Frailty status

This study used the Fried frailty phenotype as a reference standard for frailty assessment [47]. We followed a previous Thai study on frailty [48],4 which modified some criteria to comply with the local context as follows: (1) Unintentional weight loss: a loss of more than 4.5 kg or 5% of body weight in the past year, (2) Self-reported exhaustion: presence if having at least three days in the past week in response to the question, “Last week, how often did you feel any tension or fatigue?“, (3) Weakness: handgrip strength values (in kg) divided by body weight (in kg) of < 0.26 and < 0.35 in females and males, respectively (based on the data of older Thai aged between 60 and 69), (4) Slow walking speed: a 4-meter gait speed test of 0.65 m per second (m/s) for men with a height of 173 cm and women with a height of 159 cm or; 0.76 m/s for men with a height of >173 cm and women with a height of >159 cm, (5) Low physical activity: total MET scores (MET-minutes per week) 600 for male and 368 for female [49]. Based on the presence of these characteristics, participants were categorized as either non-frail, pre-frail, or frail, for none, 1–2, 3, or more characteristics, respectively.

Sarcopenia status

This study adopted the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 criteria as the reference standard for diagnosing sarcopenia [50]. The criteria are as follows: (1) Low muscle mass: the ASM divided by height squared (ASM/ht2) value of 7 kg/m2 for men and 5.7 kg/m2 for women, (2) Low muscle strength: Handgrip strength values < 28 kg for men and < 18 kg for women, (3) Low physical performance: the SPPB score of 9 or the duration to complete the 5-time chair rise test of 12 s. To diagnose sarcopenia, a participant met the criteria for low muscle mass along with either low muscle strength or low physical performance. Those who fulfilled all three criteria were considered to have severe sarcopenia.

The researchers received permission to use the instruments in this study (MoCA, MSRA-5, SARC-F, Barthel ADL, Lawton IADL, T-FRAIL, and the FRAIL scale).

Usability and satisfaction

Upon completing the online screening process, participants were asked to provide feedback on their experience and satisfaction with the use of online geriatric health screening. This feedback was collected through a questionnaire. The first five questions will use a Likert scale response format (i.e., strongly disagree, disagree, neutral, agree, and strongly agree), corresponding to a score of 1–5. These questions assessed the ease of usage, font size, clarity of instructions and questions, and the acceptability of the overall time taken. Participants were also asked to rate their overall satisfaction with this online screening tool using a Likert scale response (i.e., very dissatisfied, dissatisfied, neutral, satisfied, and very satisfied).

Furthermore, participants were asked, “How likely are you to recommend this online tool to a friend or others once it is implemented?“. They could provide a score from 0 to 10, with 0 indicating “not at all likely” and 10 indicating “extremely likely.” Based on the scores provided, participants were classified into three groups, namely promoters (scored 9–10), passives (7–8), and detractors (0–6). Subsequently, the Net Promoter Score (NPS) was calculated by subtracting the percentage of detractors from the percentage of promoters [51]. Thus, the possible NPS score ranged from − 100 to + 100, with higher scores indicating higher satisfaction.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data were presented using counts and percentages for categorical data. Continuous data was displayed as either mean (standard deviation, SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), depending on the data distribution. For assessing the validity of the online geriatric health screening tool, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive likelihood ratio (LR+), negative likelihood ratio (LR-), and accuracy were calculated for each screening tool. These measures were compared to the reference standard to determine the performance of the online screening tool in identifying each specific health condition. We also employed Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to provide the corresponding Area Under the Curve (AUC) and the optimal cutoff value. Spearman’s correlation coefficient test was utilized to estimate the correlation. A p-value less than 0.05 will be considered statistically significant. The statistical analysis was carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc, version 23.1.6 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium).

Results

Demographics and characteristics

During the study period, a total of 400 eligible participants were enrolled. Of these, 381 were included in the analysis, while 19 were withdrawn due to incomplete necessary data (Fig. 2). The age of participants ranges from 55 to 88 years, with a mean age (SD) of 64.8 (6.4) years, and a majority were female (85%). Functional status assessments showed that participants had high scores on IADL, with a mean C-IADL (SD) of 7.8 (0.6), whereas the mean Barthel Index score (SD) was 98.7 (3.4). 62% of the participants had a bachelor’s degree or higher, while 9.5% had completed primary education or less (six years of schooling or below). Prevalence of geriatric syndromes

In Step 1, the percentage of positive screening results ranged from 1.6% for sarcopenia screening (SARC-F) to 49% for fall-risk screening (Table 3). Notably, the most prevalent ENT symptoms reported by participants included dizziness and vertigo, affecting up to 25% of individuals, whereas 16% reported hearing loss. 72% of the participants had visited an ophthalmologist within the past year. For depression screening, the performance of 2Q and 2Q Plus both achieved a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 82%. Additionally, 3.9% of participants reported a positive response to question 3 (felt overwhelmingly sad and lacked the motivation to continue with life). For fall risk screening, the three Key Questions demonstrated a sensitivity of 55% and a specificity of 54%.

The diagnostic performance of malnutrition screening using only two questions on appetite loss and unintentional weight loss demonstrated a specificity of 89% and a sensitivity of 30% in identifying individuals at risk of malnutrition and malnourishment (by MNA). Meanwhile, relying solely on self-reported BMI (< 18.5 kg/m²) resulted in a sensitivity of 38% and a specificity of 96%. However, combining all criteria improved diagnostic performance, yielding a sensitivity of 62% and a specificity of 85%. Similarly, such improvement was also observed when compared with the GLIM criteria for malnutrition, with a sensitivity of 54% and a specificity of 80%.

For detecting frailty, the FRAIL scale demonstrated a sensitivity of 78% and 22% and a specificity of 56% and 98% at cutoffs of ≥ 1/5 and ≥ 3/5, respectively. However, for detecting pre-frail and frail individuals, these cutoffs yielded a sensitivity of 66% and 7% and a specificity of 67% and 100%, respectively.

Regarding sarcopenia screening, the optimal cutoff scores for both the modified and original MSRA-5 remained unchanged (30 and 45, respectively). The modified MSRA-5 (cutoff ≤ 30/34) demonstrated a sensitivity of 48% and a specificity of 62% (AUC = 0.572, 95%CI: 0.488–0.656, p = 0.101), whereas the original MSRA-5 (cutoff ≤ 45/60) yielded a sensitivity of 52% and a specificity of 55% (AUC = 0.569, 95%CI: 0.484–0.654, p = 0.117).

Since the diagnostic performance of the questionnaire for sarcopenia screening was suboptimal, we explored using CC as a screening tool (Table 5). First, using a recommended CC cutoff at ≤ 33 cm for females and ≤ 34 cm for males, we found a sensitivity of 62% and a specificity of 67%. Nonetheless, the optimal CC cutoff for detecting sarcopenia in this study was ≤ 36.9 cm for males and ≤ 33.6 cm for females, which yielded a sensitivity of 86% and 79%, and a specificity of 57% and 59%, respectively. Based on this, we thereby estimated the sensitivity and specificity of CC using a cutoff at ≤ 33 cm for females and ≤ 36 cm for males, resulting in a sensitivity of 66% and a specificity of 63%. Second, we applied the SARC-CalF tool [38], which incorporates these CC cutoffs as an additional 10 points to the SARC-F score. Using a cutoff at ≤ 33 cm for females and ≤ 36 cm for males and a threshold of ≥ 11/20, this approach yielded a sensitivity of 48% and a specificity of 85%.

Discussion

Overall, our study demonstrated satisfactory diagnostic performance of the self-administered online geriatric health screening tool in detecting geriatric syndromes. This comprehensive tool screened for cognitive impairment, depression, malnutrition, fall risk, frailty, and sarcopenia using various questionnaires and criteria. Additionally, high usability and satisfaction ratings support its practicality for Thai community-dwelling adults aged 55 and older. While screening tools typically prioritize sensitivity, some components in this study demonstrated higher specificity to reduce false-positive results and improve feasibility in clinical practice, particularly in pre-visit screening contexts where excessive referrals may burden healthcare systems.

The novel cognitive screening tool showed acceptable validity, with a sensitivity and specificity of over 60%. However, the sensitivity improved to 86% for dementia screening. To our knowledge, no studies have yet validated the ICOPE app’s memory complaint question. Our findings suggest that relying solely on this question has limited diagnostic performance. The drawback is its subjective nature, which can introduce bias, especially in self-administered assessments. In the meantime, the combined scores from two cognitive assessments (i.e., the three-picture recall task and the clock reading test) performed well in distinguishing between those with severe cognitive impairment from normal control (86% sensitivity and 67% specificity). An optimal cutoff score of 5 out of 6 was identified, meaning that failing any item in either task was considered a positive result.

The three-picture recall task primarily evaluates delayed visual memory, a cognitive function often impaired in individuals with amnestic MCI and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [52]. A community-based cohort analysis found that visual amnestic MCI could predict AD comparably to verbal amnestic MCI [53]. A recent Thai study on an application-based cognitive screening test incorporating a visual memory task demonstrated exceptional performance [54]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis found that delayed visual recall had a sensitivity of 86% and a specificity of 88% in distinguishing AD from normal cognitive control, though performance was lower for MCI, which is consistent with our findings [55]. The clock reading test assesses the visuospatial cognitive function that is frequently affected in dementia, including AD [26]. Notably, a previous study found that a score on the clock reading test correlated to that of the more recognized clock drawing test [27]. Plus, prior research demonstrated its ability to differentiate AD cases from controls, with a sensitivity of 82% and a specificity of 70% [26]. Therefore, picture recall and clock reading tests appear to be useful screening tools for cognitive impairment, particularly dementia. However, future research with larger, more diverse samples, including individuals with lower education levels and varying degrees of cognitive impairment, could further improve their validity.

In the present study, we utilized the “2Q Plus” to screen for depression, which combines the two conventional questions “2Q” with an additional question regarding suicidal ideation. This tool maintained a remarkable sensitivity (100%) and specificity (82%) despite the low depression prevalence. Moreover, an additional question is advantageous in that we can simply identify individuals at risk of suicide. A study conducted in Thailand reported similar results, showing that the 2Q had a sensitivity of 97% and a specificity of 85% [56]. A study conducted in community centers using the 2Q also revealed comparable findings, with a sensitivity of 98% and specificity of 74% [57]. However, their study used a lower PHQ-9 score cutoff of 5 as a reference standard that could lead to a higher prevalence. On the other hand, some studies have reported lower accuracy of the 2Q, with a sensitivity ranging from 47% to 62% [58, 59]. These can be attributed to differences in the prevalence of depression among the study populations, resulting in lower precision, as well as variations in the reference assessments used. For example, the 2Q itself is also a component of the PHQ-9, which may thereby inflate its accuracy when using the PHQ-9 as a reference standard. However, it is important to note that the current gold standard for depression diagnosis remains the DSM-5 criteria [60].

The prevalence of malnutrition in this study was relatively low, which may be due to the generally better health status of the study population. Malnutrition, as determined by the GLIM criteria, was observed in 6.8% of the participants. However, when using the MNA, 16% were identified as being at risk of malnutrition, whereas fewer than 1% met the criteria for malnourishment. Such disproportion between these two assessments was also found in a previous outpatient-setting study [61]. The screening tool for malnutrition included the two questions recommended by the ICOPE, assessing appetite loss and unintentional weight loss. These questions exhibited limited sensitivity in this study, making them less suitable for standalone screening. Previous studies have also reported suboptimal sensitivities ranging from 46% to 58% among community-dwelling older adults [58, 59]. Crucially, our findings suggest that adding a BMI criterion (< 18.5 kg/m²) to the ICOPE question improved its diagnostic performance, resulting in a sensitivity of 62% and a specificity of 85% in identifying individuals at risk of malnutrition and malnourishment. Moreover, a study on application-based screening employing the same criteria also demonstrated greater performance [62]. However, that study reported superior performance compared to ours, with both sensitivity and specificity reaching 90%. As a result, the use of our screening approach could be justified by such evidence.

The fall risk screening based on the CDC’s 3KQ [31] demonstrated limited diagnostic performance, with a sensitivity of 55% and a specificity of 54%, which were lower than those reported in prior studies [62, 63]. For example, a clinician-administered 3KQ, validated against 12-month prospective falls in community-dwelling Thai older adults, achieved a high AUC of 0.845, with a sensitivity of 94% and a specificity of 75% [63]. Essentially, our study used the SPPB as the reference standard, which is a surrogate marker for falls rather than actual fall occurrences. This may explain the lower diagnostic performance observed in the present study. Given its robust performance shown by the prior research, its diagnostic performance may be underestimated in this circumstance.

Frailty is a multidimensional geriatric syndrome characterized by cumulative decline across multiple body systems, increasing mortality risk [64]. In this study, the FRAIL scale demonstrated favorable diagnostic performance at a cutoff of 1/5, with a sensitivity of 78% and specificity of 56% for detecting frailty. A prior study in older Thai adults also reported the highest sensitivity (93%) at this cutoff [34]. In contrast, the original FRAIL scale at a cutoff of 3/5 lacks sufficient sensitivity. Necessarily, screening for frailty helps providers identify underlying causes or other geriatric syndromes associated with frailty, thereby implementing timely interventions. However, both the FRAIL scale and Fried criteria primarily assess physical frailty. Thus, it is worth noting that these assessments may not fully capture the broader attributes of frailty.

Muscle health plays a vital role in healthy aging. Despite its importance, awareness and screening for sarcopenia remain low, emphasizing the need for early detection and prompt intervention [65]. We used the modified Thai version of MSRA-5 and the SARC-F questionnaire for sarcopenia screening, which demonstrated suboptimal diagnostic performance. In this study, the modified MSRA-5 showed a sensitivity of 48% and a specificity of 62%, in contrast to another study conducted in general medicine clinics, which reported higher sensitivity (83%) but lower specificity (43%) [35]. When scored using the original MSRA-5, its diagnostic performance remained comparable to the modified version, with both maintaining the originally recommended optimal cutoff (≤ 30/34 for the modified version and ≤ 45/60 for the original). However, since the modified version provided a slightly higher AUC and has been validated in Thailand [35], we thereby recommend using it in local settings at present if opting for the MSRA-5 for sarcopenia screening. Meanwhile, the SARC-F questionnaire failed to identify any sarcopenic participants, indicating a complete lack of sensitivity in this study. This aligns with previous studies reporting sensitivities as low as 21–22% [35, 66]. As a result, SARC-F may not be suitable for sarcopenia screening in the Thai population. In addition, the self-administered nature of these tools may also affect their validity compared to face-to-face assessments by healthcare professionals.

Notably, we found that using CC as a screening method provided better accuracy, with both sensitivity and specificity exceeding 60%, outperforming MSRA-5. The optimal CC cutoff was ≤ 33 cm for women and ≤ 36 cm for men, while the recommended AWGS 2019 cutoff was ≤ 33 cm for women and ≤ 34 cm for men [50]. Furthermore, incorporating CC into SARC-F (SARC-CalF) did not improve diagnostic performance compared to CC alone or MSRA-5. Given its simplicity and quick administration, CC measurement during routine visits may serve as a practical alternative for sarcopenia screening.

The diagnostic performance of the online screening tool reflects deliberate trade-offs between sensitivity and specificity across different health domains. For conditions like depression, where prevalence is low but clinical significance is high, the tool emphasizes high sensitivity to ensure no cases are missed, while maintaining good specificity to avoid over-referral. In contrast, domains such as fall risk and cognitive impairment apply more balanced thresholds, resulting in moderate sensitivity and specificity to capture a broader range of cases without overwhelming follow-up services. For frailty and sarcopenia, the screening strategy varies by severity: early-stage detection emphasizes sensitivity to identify at-risk individuals, whereas advanced-stage detection favors specificity to ensure efficient use of limited healthcare resources. These patterns reflect the influence of domain prevalence, resource implications, and ROC-derived cut-points in shaping screening.

The online geriatric health screening tool received positive feedback on usability and satisfaction, with most participants finding the assessment time acceptable. These favorable findings align with an NPS of + 58%, indicating net positive perceptions. The average duration of 37 min may feel lengthy for some individuals, though not all components included in this validation study will be part of the final screening. For example, while this study assessed sarcopenia using two methods, only one may be used in clinical practice. A previous study on traditional geriatric screening reported a total service time of up to 104 min [67]. Accordingly, we believe that implementing a pre-visit online assessment could enhance healthcare service efficiency.

This study has several strengths. First, its rigorous validation process is particularly valuable in the context of online self-administered assessments, where outcomes may differ from traditional face-to-face methods. Additionally, most screening and reference standard assessments were founded on well-established questionnaires. Second, this study provides a comprehensive evaluation, covering the ICOPE’s intrinsic capacity domains as well as frailty and sarcopenia. In contrast, most validation studies focus on only one or a few domains. Third, including a usability assessment reflects an emphasis on patient-centered care, ensuring the screening service aligns with user needs and preferences.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be considered. First, its single-center design may limit the generalizability of findings. However, as this study focused on validating an online screening tool, the results remain relevant to similar healthcare settings. Second, recruitment through convenience sampling may introduce selection bias, as participants were generally healthier and more educated than the broader Thai population. Third, reliance on technology-literate individuals may exclude those less familiar with digital tools or with limited access to digital technology. Involving caregivers in the assessment process and implementing strategies to narrow the digital divide could enhance inclusivity. Fourth, gold-standard diagnostic tests were not used in certain geriatric domains. For example, the DSM-5, the current gold standard for diagnosing cognitive impairment and depression, was not utilized. While this may affect diagnostic performance, the screening tools in this study were validated against widely accepted clinical assessments. For the online cognitive screening, this study did not include formal assessments of construct validity or psychometric reliability, such as test-retest or inter-rater reliability. However, we evaluated content validity through expert review using the Content Validity Index (CVI) and assessed convergent validity by comparing the tool with a composite outcome of MoCA and ADL scores. Additionally, we measured user-centered aspects such as simplicity, practicality, and acceptability via participant rating scores, providing important insights into the tool’s feasibility and usability in real-world settings. While this is a limitation, the focus on clinical relevance and user perception aligns with the early-phase evaluation goals. Future studies should include comprehensive psychometric validation across broader settings.

Conclusions

This study validates the self-administered comprehensive online geriatric health screening tool for Thai adults aged 55 and over, aligning with healthy aging principles. The tool demonstrated satisfactory diagnostic performance in detecting cognitive impairment, depression, malnutrition risk, and frailty; however, its performance in fall risk and sarcopenia screening was suboptimal. Additionally, usability and satisfaction levels were high, with strong participant endorsement. The tool could also alleviate healthcare burdens by streamlining pre-visit screenings. While these findings support its practical use, further refinements are needed to improve performance in certain domains.
