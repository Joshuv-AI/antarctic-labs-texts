Pediatric cardiopulmonary resuscitation quality in crib versus standard hospital bed model at a single center
Authors: Ava R Wilstead, Ken Tegtmeyer, Kelly Collins, Andrea R Meisman, Kyesha James, Bin Zhang, Rashmi D Sahay, Matthew W Zackoff, Daniel A Loeb, Maya L Dewan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Bed occupancy, Workload, Situation awareness, Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, Intensive care units, Pediatric

Introduction

Despite decades of research on pediatric in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) and cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), gaps remain in understanding how ergonomic factors influence CPR quality, defined by chest compression (CC) depth, rate, release, and fraction.1 Suboptimal bed ergonomics may impair CPR performance. Simulation studies suggest bed height and surface rigidity affect compression depth and consistency, including during resuscitation on infant warmers,2, 3, 4 although others report no significant effect of bed height on quality metrics.5 Failure to deliver high-quality CPR can negatively impact critical clinical outcomes, including return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), survival to pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) discharge, and survival to hospital discharge.6

While previous simulation research has evaluated the effect of bed height on CPR quality,2, 3, 4, 5 the ergonomic features of specialized hospital cribs used in infant and toddler care have not been examined in clinical care. At Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center (CCHMC), clinicians have raised concerns during cardiac arrest hot debriefings about whether these cribs adequately support high-quality CPR; this issue was documented in approximately 40% of debriefs involving patients resuscitated in these cribs over the past two years. Although a standard fixed height stool is available across the institution for compressors, clinicians frequently report challenges when performing CPR in these cribs, particularly limited height adjustability and side rail designs that may restrict optimal rescuer positioning. This study evaluated the impact of crib ergonomics on CPR quality metrics. We hypothesized that CPR performed in specialized hospital cribs would not differ significantly in quality measures, including compression rate, depth, and compression fraction, compared with CPR performed on standard resuscitation surfaces.

Methods

Study design and setting

A retrospective observational cohort study was performed to evaluate IHCA events requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation within the CCHMC PICU from January 1, 2017 to May 31, 2025, involving patients younger than three years of age. The CCHMC PICU is a large, freestanding academic medical center with 48 medical-surgical beds. There is a separate neonatal and cardiac ICU. Within the PICU, all CPR events are managed by a trained internal code team composed of PICU staff with preassigned roles, including a dedicated CPR coach. A CPR coach is a bedside clinical team member designated during or prior to a cardiac arrest to provide real-time feedback on CPR rate, depth, compressor rotations, and defibrillation to support high-quality CPR.7 The resuscitation team uses ZOLL R-series monitor/defibrillator (ZOLL Corporation, Chelmsford, MA) during CPR events to capture real-time CPR quality metrics, which are routinely downloaded and stored as device reports. The selection criterion for children under three was based on the manufacturer’s recommended age limit for ICU crib usage (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA). Researchers collected de-identified data from electronic health records and ZOLL reports, including demographics, arrest details, outcomes, and bed surface type, and securely stored it per institutional confidentiality policies.

Study population

All PICU IHCAs with >1 min of CPR involving patients under three years of age during the study period were included in the analysis. Cases where CPR was initiated outside of the PICU or where essential data regarding bed surface type or CPR quality were unavailable were excluded from the study. This study was approved by the CCHMC IRB (#2025-0456).

Data collection

All CPR events in the PICU are tracked. Data on demographics, arrest characteristics, crib model usage, and outcomes were extracted from the electronic medical record. CPR quality metrics, including CC rate, depth, and fraction, were obtained from case review software (ZOLL Medical Corporation, Chelmsford, MA).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was CPR quality metrics as defined by the American Heart Association (AHA), including mean and median CC depth, rate, and fraction for the event.8 The AHA defines high-quality CPR as a mean chest compression rate of 100–120 compressions per minute and a mean depth of >4 cm for infants (<1 year) and >5 cm for children (1–8 years). Internally, we target a chest compression fraction of >80%, recognizing that this metric is not specifically defined within AHA guidelines. We prespecified subgroup analyses by age, including infants <1 year, given differences in recommended compression depth and patient size.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized patient demographics. Categorical variables were presented as frequency count and percentage. High-quality CPR metrics (CC rate, depth, fraction) were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared between specialized hospital crib and standard resuscitation surfaces using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. SAS version 9.4 was used, and all tests were conducted as two-sided tests with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Among children younger than three years with IHCA in the PICU, CPR quality did not differ between specialized hospital cribs and standard resuscitation surfaces for compression rate or depth. Median CC rate was 113/min (IQR 109–117) in the specialized crib versus 113/min (IQR 111–118) on standard surfaces (p = 0.6), and median CC depth was 3.3 cm (IQR 2.7–4.0) versus 3.2 cm (IQR 2.9–3.7), respectively (p = 0.9). Median CC fraction was higher in the specialized crib group (0.94 [IQR 0.89–0.98]) compared with standard surfaces (0.85 [IQR 0.76–0.95]; p = 0.01), with both groups exceeding the local target of 80%. CPR duration did not differ between groups (8 min [IQR 4–24] in specialized crib group vs. 15 min [IQR 10–38] in standard surfaces group; p = 0.06) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of pediatric IHCA events among infants and toddlers, CPR performed in specialized hospital cribs was associated with similar quality to that delivered on standard surfaces. Among children under three years, CC depth and rate were similar, with a significantly higher compression fraction observed in the specialized crib group. In infants younger than one year, CC depth and fraction did not differ, while CC rate was modestly lower and statistically significant but remained within recommended targets. These findings suggest that specialized hospital cribs can support high-quality CPR during real-world PICU resuscitations.

Compared with standard hospital beds, Stryker specialized hospital cribs have a higher minimum and narrower adjustable height range, which may contribute to differences in CPR performance. Stryker cribs typically adjust between 33 and 40 in,9 whereas standard hospital beds can be lowered to approximately 20 in.10 Reduced adjustability may alter provider biomechanics without preventing achievement of CPR quality targets, providing clinical context to the role of ergonomics in resuscitation performance.

These results extend prior simulation-based studies2, 3, 4, 5 examining bed height and surface rigidity by evaluating CPR quality during actual clinical events. Prior work has yielded mixed findings, with some studies demonstrating that bed height and surface characteristics influence compression depth and rescuer fatigue, while others report no effect on CPR quality metrics. Importantly, prior literature on bed height has focused on simulated environments and not actual IHCA events.1 Our study addresses this gap by evaluating specialized cribs with restricted height adjustment during real pediatric IHCA, a population and setting not previously evaluated.

The higher CC fraction observed in specialized hospital cribs among children under three years of age may reflect reduced interruptions related to patient repositioning or bed transitions or may be a marker of shorter CPR events. Although both groups exceeded institutional targets for compression fraction, even small improvements in minimizing pauses may be clinically meaningful given the established association between compression fraction and outcomes in pediatric cardiac arrest.6 However, it is important to note that for both children less than 3 years old and those less than 1 year old, we failed to reliably meet CC depth targets of >4 cm for children 0–1 years old and >5 cm for children 1–3 years old. Inability to achieve recommended depth targets for children in these age groups is well described and not a unique challenge for this study.11, 12, 13

These findings contrast with clinician concerns raised during post-arrest debriefings regarding limited height adjustability and side-rail design of specialized cribs. While such ergonomic features may affect rescuer comfort, positioning, or perceived effort, they did not translate into measurable deficits in CPR quality in this study. It is possible that these concerns reflect increased rescuer fatigue14 or other practical challenges that were mitigated through our team-based resuscitation practices, including the use of a dedicated CPR coach to optimize technique and timing.7 It is also possible that these factors buffer short-term performance but may be less durable during more prolonged resuscitation events. Together, these findings underscore the importance of integrating frontline experiential concerns with objective performance data when considering equipment modifications or ergonomic interventions, particularly in the context of longer-duration CPR.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design limits causal inference and generalizability and introduces the potential for confounding bias between groups. Differences in patient characteristics, illness severity, arrest circumstances, and team composition may have influenced both the exposure (bed surface) and CPR quality metrics. The sample size was small, limiting power to detect clinically meaningful differences in CPR quality and precluding robust subgroup analyses. In addition, we were unable to examine associations between CPR quality and clinical outcomes, and outcomes such as ROSC and survival were not powered for comparison and should be interpreted cautiously. We could not assess rescuer fatigue, body mechanics, or subjective ease of CPR, which may influence provider experience despite preserved metrics. We were also unable to account for potentially important unmeasured confounders, including variability in CPR coaching. The presence, experience, and effectiveness of a dedicated CPR coach may significantly influence compression quality and team performance, and differences in coaching across events could have attenuated or obscured true differences between groups. CPR duration, although not statistically different between groups, may also have influenced results. Because this analysis was limited to cardiac arrest events with available CPR quality data, the findings may not generalize to all cardiac arrest events. In particular, very brief events or events in which defibrillator pads were not applied early enough to capture CPR quality data may be underrepresented. Additionally, because CPR quality metrics were derived from a feedback device, these measures, particularly compression depth, may be subject to inherent device-related measurement variability and should be interpreted with this limitation in mind.

Despite these limitations, this study provides preliminary, clinically relevant evidence that specialized hospital cribs may not compromise CPR quality in infants and toddlers during pediatric IHCA. These findings support continued use of ICU cribs during resuscitation in similar settings while underscoring the need for multicenter studies to better characterize rescuer ergonomics, fatigue, and workflow. Incorporating structured ergonomic assessment into post-arrest debriefings and equipment evaluation may further optimize provider experience and resuscitation performance.

Conclusion

In this cohort of pediatric IHCA events, CPR quality metrics were similar in specialized hospital cribs and standard surfaces, with performance remaining similar despite clinician ergonomic concerns. These findings suggest that specialized cribs can be used for infant and toddler resuscitation without apparent loss of CPR quality and highlight the value of pairing frontline feedback with objective performance data to guide quality improvement.
