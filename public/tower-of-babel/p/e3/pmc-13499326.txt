Stakeholder perspectives on implementing ED time series forecasting: a qualitative interview study
Authors: Luka Petravić, Kaja Gril Rogina, Janez Žibert, Tit Albreht, Andreja Kukec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmergency departments (EDs) around the globe are experiencing increasing patient attendance and staffing challenges, making it difficult for management to balance supply and demand. Time series forecasting (TSF) is a statistical technique that uses historical data and external variables to predict phenomena over time. Given the growing interest in TSF applications in emergency care, the aim of this study was to explore how these technologies could be aligned with everyday clinical demands to close the translational science gap.MethodsThis was a prospective qualitative interview study. Through interviews with European emergency department directors, we collected and analysed perspectives on the practical applicability of time series forecasting in everyday emergency care. The study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).ResultsWe interviewed 26 ED directors in 19 countries. Over half had prior knowledge of time series forecasting technology. Only three countries had TSF implemented in daily practice. Most ED directors reported that their management decisions are informed by data and indicated openness to the adoption of additional decision-support tools, such as TSF. Novel, previously undescribed external variables were identified, including local primary care availability, fruit harvesting, and neighbouring hospital conditions (e.g. ED closures).ConclusionsWe recommend that future TSF modelling studies in ED implement, at a minimum, both short- and long-term forecast horizons; conduct in-depth interviews with local senior staff to align models with operational needs; and validate models using real-world data from deployment sites. Through semi-structured interviews, we identified key directions for further TSF model development to enhance usability and support translation from academia into clinical practice.Study registrationThis study was preregistered via Open Science Framework site (10.17605/OSF.IO/SGZH7).Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-026-01671-z.

Background

Emergency departments (EDs) provide care for both acute and chronic patients and represent a main entry point for individuals seeking medical attention [1, 2]. EDs worldwide are experiencing overcrowding and staff shortages. Most EDs are currently not well equipped with decision support systems to aid in managing patient flow logistics and human resource allocation. This lack of support negatively affects patient care and safety, as well as staff working conditions [3].

Time series (TS) data consist of sequential observations collected over time to describe a phenomenon [4]. Time series forecasting (TSF) aims to predict future values of an observed phenomenon based on its historical patterns and additional explanatory variables, across short- to long-term horizons [4]. TSF is also increasingly applied to predicting ED attendance, demonstrating feasibility and promising accuracy of the predictions [2, 5–7].

In our previous work, we identified a translational gap between laboratory-based research and clinical implementation of TSF in EDs [5]. Currently, there are no guidelines on how to approach TSF in ED settings in a way that makes theoretical work applicable to real-world environments. To date, no study has systematically examined how TSF parameters should be defined to ensure clinical usefulness. No study has inquired of potential users on how to move models to everyday use, making the technological advances a reality [8]. Furthermore, little is known about whether such technologies already exist in routine practice or whether existing institutional systems are capable of acting on TSF outputs.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to uncover what would make TSF useful for ED leadership, how such systems could be implemented, and which real-world operational considerations should not be overlooked in development.

Methods

Study design

We undertook a pragmatic qualitative descriptive interview study. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis with a hybrid inductive–deductive coding strategy to identify ED directors’ perspectives regarding the practical usefulness of time series forecasting for their everyday use. The study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [9]. The UKC Maribor IRB deemed that the study does not need ethics approval (UKC-MB-KME-5/25). Study participants were informed of their rights before taking part in the study. This study was preregistered [10].

The study was carried out in two phases. First, we recruited only Slovenian ED directors and refined the interview guide based on initial feedback [11]. The pilot phase led to minor wording and sequencing changes to improve clarity; no new domains or substantive research questions were introduced. As the interview content remained closely aligned with the final guide, pilot interviews were included in the final dataset. To assess the potential influence of pilot participation, we conducted a sensitivity analysis comparing themes identified in pilot and non-pilot interviews. Second, we conducted interviews with ED directors from abroad.

Setting/sample

LP is a PhD student and a medical doctor currently in his residency of internal medicine. He regularly works in ED, knows the workflows and has conducted other research in this field – pooling studies, cardiac arrest registry and more. The topic of health IT is one he has been doing for over 8 years. The TSF research spans back 3 years, before his PhD studies. During his PhD study he has conducted a systematic review of the topic. He received formal training in qualitative research methodology as part of his PhD programme studies. Data were obtained by doing video, live or written interviews with the target population which were the ED directors from European countries. Data saturation was not predefined as a stopping criterion. Recruitment was guided pragmatically by the objective of obtaining approximately two ED directors per EU country where feasible. Data collection continued until the planned recruitment period ended. After all interviews were completed, thematic saturation was assessed retrospectively by examining the emergence of new themes across successive interviews. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. No relationship was established between the interviewer and participants prior to study commencement; basic information about the researcher and study aims was provided in the study invitation (Supplement 1). The interviews were conducted between February 2025 and January 2026.

We used convenience sampling. Based on publicly available contact information, we emailed ED directors from differentcountries in Europe. In addition, ED directors were approached for face-to-face interviews at the European Resuscitation Council 2025 conference. None of the individuals who responded declined participation. Data were collected via Zoom interviews, in-person recorded interviews or e-mail correspondence. No individuals other than the participants and researchers were present. A semi-structured interview guide was developed as part of the study protocol (Supplement 1) and pilot-tested during the first study phase [11]. Repeat interviews were not conducted both in pilot and main part of the study, as the questions were modified after the first interview and then no new questions were added. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed using Soniox (model stt-async-v3). No field notes were taken. The planned interview duration was 10 min. After completing data collection, we retrospectively assessed thematic saturation by reviewing the order in which new themes emerged across interviews. Core themes appeared repeatedly by interview 12, and no substantial new themes were identified after interview 16. Thus, saturation represents a retrospective analytic assessment rather than a criterion used to terminate recruitment. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment or correction; transcription quality was manually verified using two randomly selected transcript segments per interview. Coding and thematic synthesis were conducted with substantial reliance on a large language model. Specifically, Claude Opus 4.5 (January 2026 release) performed two analytical steps, both governed by the prompts in Supplement 2: (i) structured extraction of pre-specified fields from each de-identified transcript into a JSON template covering participant profile, perceived utility, information needs, forecast horizons, actionability, influencing variables, and recommendations; and (ii) cross-case aggregation of those extractions into frequency tables, consensus and divergence mappings, and a candidate thematic framework. The deductive scaffolding (the extraction template, the prior categories drawn from our previous systematic review, and the comparative dimensions for aggregation) was specified by the research team; the inductive identification of codes and themes within and across that scaffolding was performed by the LLM in the first instance.

Human involvement took the form of verification rather than independent parallel coding. LP checked AI-generated extractions against the corresponding source transcripts and corrected misattributions, fabricated content, and over-generalisations. Quantitative tallies reported in Tables 2, 3, 4 and 5 and Supplement 3 were re-counted from the verified extractions rather than taken directly from LLM aggregation. Co-authors KGR, AK, TA and JŽ then reviewed the consolidated thematic framework against source material, with attention to whether dissenting and outlier views had been preserved. Where LLM output and human reading diverged, the human reading was retained, and disagreements among co-authors were resolved by discussion until consensus was reached.

We did not conduct fully independent parallel human coding of transcripts, did not pre-specify a sampling fraction for co-author verification, and did not formally quantify inter-rater agreement between LLM and human readings or the proportion of final codes that required correction. We report this transparently as a limitation of the protocol rather than as established methodological rigour.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria included [i] Employment in a European ED, [ii] Role as ED director, [iii] English language proficiency and [iv] Person agreeing to being recorded. No exclusion criteria.

Data extraction, data analysis, and storage procedures

Data from the interviews were extracted using Soniox, after which the transcripts were stored offline and further analysed. Claude Opus 4.5 (used in January 2026) was employed to assist with transcript summarisation, and all outputs were manually checked to ensure the accuracy of both transcription and summarisation (see prompt in Supplement 2). Claude Opus 4.5 was also used to support data analysis (see prompt in Supplement 2), with results independently reviewed to ensure fidelity to the source data.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 115 invitations sent, 26 participants completed interviews, yielding a 23% response rate. Participants’ countries of origin are shown in Fig. 1. Median interview length was 16 min (IQR 10 min). Among the participants, 25 were medical doctors and one was a nurse. The sample included 7 females (27%) and 19 males (73%). Most participants worked in metropolitan EDs (n = 22, 85%) and 18 (69%) were employed in tertiary-level hospitals.

Regarding professional roles, 18 participants held combined clinical-academic-administrative positions, 7 held clinical-administrative roles, and one held an administrative-only role. Most participants (58%, n = 15) had basic prior knowledge of TSF technology, while the remainder (42%, n = 11) had no prior knowledge. Three (12%, n = 3) were already using TSF technology in practice. Finally, most participants (92%, n = 24) reported making management decisions about their EDs based on quantitative data. Sensitivity analysis showed that the main themes identified among the four pilot participants were also present in the remaining sample. Excluding pilot interviews did not materially change the thematic framework, key recommendations, or interpretation of findings. Therefore, pilot participants were included in the final analysis.

Key targets for time series forecasting

Most participants believe TSF would be useful in their departments (very useful 62%, somewhat useful 15%, conditionally useful 8%), while 12% are sceptical about its usefulness due to the constraints of ED systems in their organization. One person believed that it would not be useful at all.

Participants identified several variables that TSF models should prioritise for prediction (Table 2). Among the options provided, total daily patient volume (96%), peak hour of attendance (92%) and triage category predictions (77%) were deemed most important. Notably, admission predictions emerged as a high priority need that was not initially anticipated and was mentioned spontaneously by participants from AT, NL, BE, IT as potentially more valuable than arrival forecasting.

Thematic analysis

Perceived barriers to TSF implementation

Participants identified eight distinct barriers to TSF utility (Supplement 3). Staffing constraints emerged as the dominant concern (69%), with one participant noting “It is hard to set optimisations if you have trouble providing basics.” Rigid scheduling regulations (62%) posed a structural barrier, with legal requirements ranging from one to seven weeks’ advance notice depending on jurisdiction. Over half of participants (54%) raised concerns about limited actionability—the gap between prediction and response capacity. As one participant observed, “You can decide something, but it doesn’t happen.”

Less frequently cited barriers included data quality issues (31%), existing pattern knowledge (27%), work culture constraints (23%), hospital boarding bottlenecks (23%), and lack of standby capacity (19%). These latter concerns reflected the view that ED crowding often stems from admission flow rather than arrival volume, limiting the value of ED-specific forecasting.

Perceived benefits of TSF

Despite barriers, 85% of participants endorsed TSF for optimising staffing allocation (Supplement 3). Nearly half (46%) valued forecasting as evidence for advocacy, using data to justify resource requests to leadership. As one participant explained, “If you have solid data, you can go to your chief.”

Other perceived benefits included anticipating surges (42%), coordinating admissions and beds (38%), long-term planning (35%), objective measurement (23%), and event preparation (19%). The emphasis on admission coordination, particularly among Dutch, Austrian, and Belgian participants, reinforced the view that downstream bottlenecks rather than arrival volume represent the critical constraint.

Recommendations for TSF research

Participants offered practical guidance for researchers (Supplement 3). Local validation was considered essential (n = 14), with models needing to be “hospital-specific and local.” Twelve participants recommended focusing on admission rather than arrival predictions, arguing this holds greater operational value. Ten emphasised aligning forecast horizons with existing scheduling cycles to ensure actionability.

Additional recommendations included integrating weather data (n = 8), visualising forecasts on real-time dashboards (n = 6), and accounting for local events and religious calendars (n = 6). Research-oriented participants suggested creating benchmarking datasets and open-source models to facilitate validation and adoption.

Consensus and divergence

Strong consensus (≥ 75%) emerged around TSF’s potential utility in principle (85%), the value of total volume and peak hour predictions (96% and 92%), one-month horizons as actionable (77%), and the importance of data quality and local validation (100% and 88%) (Supplement 3).

Moderate consensus (50–74%) characterised views on triage category predictions (77% positive), short-term prediction utility (62% seeing limited value), and scheduling software adoption (54% supportive).

Notable divergence (< 50% agreement) appeared on optimal forecast horizons, with preferences split between short-term (23%) and long-term (38%) depending on local scheduling flexibility. Same-day prediction utility divided participants almost evenly (46% vs. 54%), determined largely by staffing model flexibility. Whether TSF adds value beyond experiential knowledge also varied (62% vs. 38%), reflecting differences in analytical sophistication and years of site-specific experience.

Cross-cutting patterns and participant typology

Preferred forecast horizons clustered by regulatory context. An EU directive requires reasonable notice, which can vary from country to country [12, 13]. Nordic and Germanic countries favoured one- to three-month horizons, reflecting legal scheduling requirements, whereas Southern and Eastern European sites showed greater variation (Supplement 3). Current analytics use correlated positively with perceived TSF value, although sites with very mature infrastructure sometimes perceived less incremental benefit.

Four participant types emerged from the analysis. Advanced Adopters (n = 3; Netherlands, Denmark, Finland) were already using TSF supported by sophisticated infrastructure, mentioning good accuracy of their system “…8-hour prediction with ± 1 patient accuracy.” Aspiring Enthusiasts (n = 12) expressed strong interest –“ It’ll definitely help with resource allocation,” but faced resource constraints, viewing TSF as a potential solution. Pragmatic Adopters (n = 6) recognised conditional value, dependent on actionability and appropriate forecast horizons –“ Long-term would be useful because I can actually do something.” Constrained Sceptics (n = 5) questioned the practical utility of TSF given fundamental barriers, with one characterising the discussion as “discussing non-alcoholic strawberry beer without tasting it.”

Discussion

To date, this is the first study to explore how TSF can be translated to the ED clinical context and adapted for everyday use. Prior studies have been done to assess how to improve methodological aspects of TSF in ED space [14, 15], but there is a lack of translational science studies. Overall, participants perceived TSF as a potentially valuable tool for improving patient flow management and preparing for periods of increased patient influx.

In large, profit-driven systems, management trends increasingly rely on data insights to better understand process flows and improve outcomes [16]. This approach is now extending to healthcare and, notably, to EDs [17]. By leveraging routinely collected metadata from patient care processes, management can improve operational efficiency and patient outcomes [18].

Denmark (commercial software), the Netherlands (research), and Finland (commercial software and research) already use some form of TSF software in ED operations [19–21], and similar solutions are offered by companies in the United States [22, 23]. Participants using these systems reported positive experiences and described TSF as integral to their operations, although none were able to provide measurable quantitative evidence of its impact. Most participants reported already using data-driven approaches, such as staffing based on yearly averages or adjusting staff composition to better match patient needs.

Overall, ED management staff supported the use of TSF technology, recognising its potential to leverage large volumes of data generated through routine care [17]. However, many participants had not yet seen such technology implemented in practice. As one participant noted, discussing TSF without firsthand experience was like “discussing non-alcoholic strawberry beer without tasting it,” highlighting the difficulty of assessing value in the absence of tangible implementation.

Participants identified total daily patient volume and peak hour of attendance as the most important values to predict. This preference reflects the relative stability of triage category distributions over time, in contrast to the variability of patient volume and peak attendance. Importantly, admission predictions emerged as a high-priority need that had not been identified during the study planning phase. ED patient flow consists of arrivals followed by admission or discharge; delays in admission to inpatient wards substantially increase ED length of stay, including for patients ultimately discharged [24, 25]. Given this complexity, TSF applications in EDs should incorporate both patient influx and efflux to adequately support operational decision-making.

Most EDs in our sample did not use dedicated software for staff scheduling. Participants broadly agreed that a one-month forecast horizon is most useful for staff scheduling, while shorter horizons support day-to-day operational monitoring and longer horizons inform policy-level decisions. With ED attendances increasing globally year on year, aligning forecasting horizons with operational planning cycles is increasingly important [24, 25].

In previous work, we identified the value of eliciting expert opinion from senior clinicians regarding factors influencing ED attendances [5]. In the present study, participants identified patterns consistent with prior systematic reviews, including day of the week, time of day, and influenza seasonality [2, 5–7]. Additionally, participants highlighted highly localised drivers not previously described in systematic reviews, such as agricultural harvest periods (e.g. olive and cherry picking), primary care availability within ED catchment areas, general practitioner referral patterns, and capacity at nearby hospitals [2, 5–7]. Limited primary care accessibility has been strongly linked to increased ED utilisation [26, 27]. Same-day GP appointments can reduce avoidable ED attendances [27], yet achieving this remains challenging given wide international variation in primary care access [28] and documented deterioration in some EU countries [29]. These findings underscore the importance of interpreting ED demand patterns within their regional healthcare context.

Thematic analysis

The thematic analysis revealed a fundamental tension between predictive capability and operational response capacity. Although 85% of participants endorsed TSF in principle, more than half (54%) identified limited actionability as a major barrier, captured by the observation that “you can decide something, but it doesn’t happen.” This implementation gap aligns with the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research, which identifies structural constraints and implementation climate as key determinants of whether evidence-based interventions translate into practice [30]. In our sample, rigid scheduling regulations requiring one to seven weeks’ advance notice, chronic staffing shortages, and hospital boarding bottlenecks exemplified these constraints. The emphasis on admission coordination among Dutch, Austrian, and Belgian participants is consistent with evidence that ED crowding is driven primarily by output factors rather than input volume [31].

Four distinct participant profiles emerged, each suggesting different implementation strategies. This typology aligns with Rogers’ diffusion of innovations theory, which categorises adopters based on their innovativeness and readiness to embrace new technologies [32], and with Greenhalgh and colleagues’ adaptation of diffusion theory to healthcare service organisations [33]. Advanced Adopters in the Netherlands, Denmark, and Finland already operated TSF systems integrated with sophisticated analytics infrastructure, prioritising refinement rather than adoption as the priority. Aspiring Enthusiasts were the largest group and they expressed strong interest but faced resource constraints that TSF alone cannot resolve, reflecting what Weiner terms insufficient “change efficacy” despite adequate “change commitment” [34]. Pragmatic Adopters recognised conditional value contingent on appropriate forecast horizons and demonstrated actionability. Constrained Sceptics questioned whether TSF offers meaningful benefit given fundamental barriers, with one characterising the discussion as “discussing non-alcoholic strawberry beer without tasting it.” This typology suggests that implementation strategies should be tailored: optimisation and benchmarking for Advanced Adopters, concurrent investment in staffing and scheduling flexibility for Aspiring Enthusiasts, and demonstrable actionability for Pragmatic Adopters.

Strong consensus emerged on several parameters guiding future TSF development. Participants universally emphasised the need for local validation, favouring hospital-specific models over generalised solutions. This concern is supported by evidence showing substantial performance degradation when prediction models are applied outside their development context [35]. A one-month horizon achieved 77% endorsement as the minimum actionable timeframe for staff scheduling, though optimal horizons clustered by regulatory context. Nordic and Germanic countries favoured one- to three-month windows reflecting legal requirements, while Southern and Eastern European sites showed greater flexibility. This finding operationalises the “CDS Five Rights” framework, which emphasises delivering the right information at the right time within clinical workflows [36]. Recent work has proposed actionability as a distinct metric for evaluating predictive models, beyond traditional performance measures [37]. Our findings suggest that forecast utility depends less on predictive precision than on temporal alignment with decision-making processes and the capacity to act on predictions. Notably, 46% of participants recommended shifting research focus from arrival to admission predictions, challenging prevailing research paradigms that prioritise short-term arrival forecasting accuracy [2, 5–7].

Future research should prioritise qualitative research into user experience with TSF systems already deployed in real-world settings, similar to prior work conducted in Australia [38]. We believe that qualitative research has a role in complementing technological development, ensuring that new models meaningfully augment existing processes rather than introducing solutions that fail to address real-world constraints. Such studies should ideally also respond to some of the specific needs that professionals working in EDs have. Another important aspect of future research is identifying subgroups and analysing differences between systems (e.g. Nordic and Germanic vs. Southern and Eastern European regulatory context, different participant typologies, different education, differnet logistical realities, etc.).

Limitations and strengths

There are some notable limitations to this study. First, qualitative research cannot be generalised to all practice settings or capture all potential perspectives. For example, we limited the sample to Europe-based ED directors. We used convenience sampling, which can lead to selection bias. Due to low response rate, it is possible that we interviewed participants who were already more inclined to using new technology and our pool consisted of early adopters. This bias can hinder the transferability of our conclusions into real world settings. We acknowledge that due to low response rate there are possible patterns that our study did not uncover and could be specific to certain regions or subregions of Europe not covered by our sample. Second, although we undertook efforts to minimise social desirability bias, participants may have provided responses they believed interviewers would view favourably. Third, interpretation of transcripts using reflexive thematic analysis involves inherent subjectivity in discerning meaning from the data. In addition, a substantive limitation concerns our use of a large language model for initial coding and thematic aggregation. Although every AI-generated extraction was checked against source transcripts and the thematic framework was reviewed by four co-authors, our verification protocol fell short of independent parallel coding: humans audited LLM output rather than coding the data separately and then reconciling. We did not pre-specify a sampling fraction for the co-author review, did not formally quantify rates of correction or disagreement, and did not assess inter-rater agreement. The risks flagged by Ashwin and colleagues [39]-thematic homogenisation, smoothing of dissenting views, and confident misattribution; are therefore not fully excluded by our procedure. We attempted to mitigate these specifically by retaining and reporting divergent cases, outlier quotations, country-level disaggregation, and the four-type participant typology, all of which would be expected to suffer first if homogenisation had occurred. Research shows that AI-driven thematic analysis is feasible and accurate [40]. We also note that at the time of analysis COREQ-LLM had been announced but not finalised, and we have aimed to report the procedure with the level of transparency we anticipate the finalised guidance will require [41]. Four Slovenian participants were recruited during the pilot phase and provided feedback used to refine the interview guide. Their inclusion may have introduced instrumentation bias, as they were exposed to earlier versions of the interview process. However, modifications after the pilot were limited to changes in wording and sequencing, and a sensitivity analysis indicated that excluding pilot interviews would not materially affect the major themes or conclusions. Nevertheless, some risk of bias remains and should be considered when interpreting the findings. The Slovenian participants cover a broad range of EDs from large metropolitan to small rural, which we believe outweighs the potential bias they introduce. Due to the low response rate we also had to deviate from our study protocol and have not covered the whole of Europe as planned and have not achieved the planned 2 participants per EU country goal. Due to the saturation of our data set occurring at around 15 participants, we believe that this shouldn’t have drastically affected our outcomes. The data saturation was not planned before study commencement and was done retrospectively. We have not carried it out after each interview, rather we completed the whole sample of interviews and carried it out after study period ending. We also acknowledge that we have pragmatically analysed the data as a homogenous group, making it possible that regional, heritage, social, clinical and organisational differences hinder the applicability of our findings. Aggregating diverse settings may mask important subgroup differences. Our findings may not fully transfer across all subgroups and careful evaluation before implementation of such systems must be done. Finally, health system variations, including the access and behavioural patterns that vary across the different countries, may have also contributed to some of the observations.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine how TSF can be translated into the ED clinical context and adapted for routine practice. A key strength of this work lies in its focus on real-world applicability, bridging the gap between theory and everyday clinical workflows.

Conclusions

To date, this is the first qualitative study examining the use of TSF in ED environments. We explore the European context to identify how TSF can be made operationally useful and to help bridge the translational gap between research and clinical practice. TSF in ED settings remains a novel technology, used in only three countries within our sample. We recommend that future TSF modelling studies incorporate, at a minimum, both short- and long-term forecast horizons, conduct in-depth interviews with local senior staff to ensure alignment with operational needs, and validate models using real-world data from deployment sites.
