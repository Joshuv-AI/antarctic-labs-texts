A 79-year-old man from a rural setting in eastern Ontario presented to a community hospital in July with 2 days of fatigue, chills, and generalized weakness that led to a fall. He had a history of ulcerative colitis, type 2 diabetes, and dyslipidemia, and was taking azathioprine, atorvastatin, and dexlansoprazole.

On presentation, the patient was febrile (39.8°C), with otherwise stable vital signs. Neurologic examination was normal without focal deficits. Cardiac and respiratory examinations revealed no murmurs or crackles. His abdomen was soft and nontender, with no evidence of synovitis or rash. Laboratory investigations showed lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury, and elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and C-reactive protein levels (Table 1). His chest radiograph was normal, and electrocardiography showed normal sinus rhythm, without atrioventricular block. The admitting physicians suspected an unidentified infection in an immunocompromised host. Blood and urine samples were drawn for culture, and the patient was started on empiric ceftriaxone (1 g/d) for common enteric and urinary organisms.

On the second day, the patient remained febrile and developed a new altered sensorium and mild shortness of breath. Laboratory investigations showed anemia, leucopenia, worsened thrombocytopenia, and transaminitis. Computed tomography (CT) of his head, chest, abdomen, and pelvis was unremarkable. Given the lack of response to ceftriaxone and the patient’s residence in an endemic region, tick-borne disease was suspected. On further history, he did not recall any tick bites or prior Lyme disease, but he did work frequently in the woodlands, so real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tests for Anaplasmosis phagocytophilum and Babesia and blood smears were ordered to investigate for anaplasmosis and babesiosis. Lyme serological testing (pooled immunoglobulin [Ig] M and IgG) by the Public Health Ontario Laboratory was deferred as it might indicate prior exposure and the team felt it would result in low specificity for active infection. Further, the team felt Lyme disease did not account for the severe cytopenia and failure to respond to ceftriaxone within 48 hours. Antibiotic treatment was broadened to include piperacillin–tazobactam, vancomycin, and doxycycline to cover anaplasmosis or another unidentified bacterial infection before transfer to tertiary care hospital.

During transfer, the patient’s blood smear showed intracytoplasmic morulae within neutrophils, which suggested a diagnosis of anaplasmosis. He reported having headaches, which were associated with fluctuating disorientation, and he developed hypoxia requiring 2 L/min of supplemental oxygen. He did not have meningismus or focal neurologic deficits, but he had developed jugular venous distension with bibasilar crackles on chest auscultation. Laboratory investigations showed elevated B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) and troponin levels. After negative blood and urine cultures and a PCR result positive for anaplasmosis, we confirmed the diagnosis of anaplasmosis and narrowed his antibiotic coverage to oral doxycycline monotherapy (100 mg twice daily). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed a newly reduced ejection fraction of 43% with left ventricular global hypokinesis, compared with prior imaging. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed focal myocardial edema with enhancement in the basal inferolateral wall, consistent with acute focal myocarditis (Figure 1).

Although we were concerned about encephalitis, we were unable to perform a lumbar puncture because of the patient’s thrombocytopenia. His brain MRI showed no abnormal parenchymal or leptomeningeal enhancement but did reveal nonspecific enhancement of the clivus and hypoglossal canal (Figure 2). His fluctuating confusion seemed more consistent with delirium than encephalitis. Given the cardiac complications caused by anaplasmosis, we planned a 14-day course of oral doxycycline, which would also treat any possible coinfection with Lyme disease. The patient’s cognition normalized within 24 to 48 hours, and his blood counts, renal function, and liver nzymes gradually improved for the remainder of his hospitalization. He was discharged in stable condition after 8 days, and we prescribed bisoprolol for managing his reduced left ventricular ejection fraction. At 4-month follow-up, he was asymptomatic, and repeat TTE showed normalized ventricular function.

Discussion

We describe a case of anaplasmosis associated with acute myocarditis. As the incidence of tick-borne diseases rises rapidly across Canada — with 40 cases in Ontario and a noted increase in Quebec and Manitoba — considering anaplasmosis as a possible pathogen is increasingly important for front-line clinicians.1–3 Anaplasmosis, also known as human granulocytic anaplasmosis, is an infection caused by Anaplasma phagocytophilum. The most common mode of transmission is via a bite from the blacklegged tick, Ixodes scapularis. First described in 1994 in the northeastern United States,4 recent surveillance data from Ontario show an increase in the proportion of ticks infected by A. phagocytophilum.5,6 Anaplasmosis is now provincially reportable.3

The clinical presentation of anaplasmosis is often nonspecific with fever, malaise, headache, and gastrointestinal symptoms.3 Anaplasmosis infections vary from self-limited to severe with end-organ complications requiring hospitalization. Prevalence data for complications are limited, with respiratory symptoms, central nervous system (CNS) symptoms, and rash reported in fewer than 10% of identified cases.3 Risk factors for complications include advanced age, immunosuppression, and chronic comorbidities such as diabetes.4,7 Delayed diagnosis of infection contributes to risk of complications.7 Acute respiratory distress syndrome, acute renal failure, and meningoencephalitis are other reported complications of anaplasmosis; myopericarditis is rare.7,8 End-organ complications causing death from anaplasmosis are rare, with case fatality rate of less than 1%.7 Complications of anaplasmosis differ from early disseminated Lyme disease, the most commonly encountered tick-borne infection in Canada (Table 2).9,10 Other less common febrile tick-borne infections expected to rise in incidence include babesiosis — which presents with cytopenias, hemolysis, or acute kidney injury — and Powassan viral infection, which presents with meningoencephalitis.11

Anaplasmosis is a clinical diagnosis with common laboratory findings, including thrombocytopenia, lymphopenia, elevated ALT levels, and elevated creatinine levels. Anaplasmosis has no typical imaging findings.10,12 Blood smear (if available), PCR,3 and paired serology are used for diagnosis of acute anaplasmosis. Gold-standard diagnostic testing is bacterial culture, but this is available only at research and reference laboratories. Peripheral blood smear showing intracytoplasmic morulae within neutrophils supports a rapid diagnosis, with a variable sensitivity (21% to 73%) depending on the reader, but may not be available in all laboratories.10,12,14 Polymerase chain reaction offers higher sensitivity (70% to 100%) and specificity of 100% during the early febrile phase of the first week after symptom onset.12,15 Serology requires a fourfold rise in IgG titers between acute (within 2 wk) and convalescent (up to 12 wk) samples to retrospectively confirm diagnosis, with sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 65% to 100%.10,12,15

In patients presenting with acute febrile illness of unclear origin and suggestive laboratory abnormalities, with possible tick exposure in an endemic area, physicians should have high suspicion for anaplasmosis and other tick-borne infections. Further testing for tick-borne coinfections may include Lyme serology for those without the classic erythema migrans rash but other symptoms suggestive of disseminated disease (Table 2),16 babesia blood smear and PCR in the setting of hemolysis, and Powassan virus serology in patients with meningoencephalitis.11 Given the end-organ manifestations, clinicians should have a low threshold for testing these coinfections. Empiric treatment for anaplasmosis should be initiated while awaiting confirmatory testing, and persistent fever despite 48 hours of doxycycline should suggest an alternative diagnosis.

The current recommendation for treatment of anaplasmosis is oral doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 10 to 14 days, which also treats coinfection with Lyme disease.10,17 Patients generally improve within 24 to 48 hours of doxycycline initiation, and lack of response should lead to consideration of alternative diagnoses. For pregnant patients or patients with life-threatening allergies to doxycycline, alternative antibiotics should be considered in consultation with an infectious disease specialist.17 Limited data support second-line agents, but reports suggest positive outcomes with rifampin.10

This case highlights the importance of considering tick-borne diseases as part of the infectious workup for patients in endemic regions. Patients should be asked about tick exposures, proximity to deer or pets, use of preventive measures (N,N-diethyl-meta-toluamide [DEET], protective clothing, and skin inspections), and prior Lyme disease.9 However, tick-borne diseases are challenging as 50% to 75% of patients with early Lyme disease do not recall tick bites.9 The Public Health Agency of Canada has outlined risk areas for Lyme disease, searchable by postal code, which are likely an effective proxy for risk areas for anaplasmosis.18 Clinicians should also consult provincial public health reports for locally relevant maps of anaplasmosis incidence and risk.19 As endemic regions of tick-related illnesses have expanded rapidly over the past decade, testing for tickborne diseases should be considered in nonendemic areas, if clinicians have a high index of suspicion.

In our patient, laboratory abnormalities, including thrombocytopenia and elevated transaminases, helped raise suspicion of anaplasmosis. He had risk factors for complications, including advanced age, immunocompromise from azathioprine therapy, and type 2 diabetes. His condition progressed rapidly after presentation to multiorgan dysfunction including pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, hepatitis, and myocarditis. Myocarditis is a focal or diffuse inflammatory disease of the myocardium resulting from a range of infectious or noninfectious causes. Clinical manifestations range from asymptomatic presentations to chest pain, arrhythmias, cardiogenic shock, or sudden cardiac death. A definitive diagnosis of myocarditis is established by endomyocardial biopsy; however, in most cases, the diagnosis is made through a combination of clinical history, echocardiography, and characteristic cardiac MRI findings. We suspected myocarditis in our patient, given his clinical signs of acute heart failure, which we confirmed with cardiac MRI. Management of myocarditis involves treating both the underlying cause and its cardiac complications. 18 Continuous cardiac monitoring is recommended because conduction abnormalities and arrythmias may emerge during the evolution of myocarditis,20 particularly when Lyme carditis is a consideration.9 At our institution (the University of Ottawa Heart Institute), patients with symptomatic myocarditis are typically monitored on telemetry for approximately 72 hours, with duration individualized according to clinical status and troponin trend. Similar to the complete resolution with normalized left ventricular function observed in our patient, previously reported myopericarditis caused by anaplasmosis has been shown to completely resolve with doxycycline.8

Our patient’s confusion and headache presented a diagnostic challenge, particularly since a lumbar puncture was not possible because of his thrombocytopenia. Although CNS infection was our major concern, he did not have meningismus or cranial nerve palsy, and his MRI showed no parenchymal or leptomeningeal enhancement. We ultimately attributed his fluctuating symptoms to delirium, secondary to his systemic illness. The rapid recovery within 48 hours after initiating doxycycline, along with positive PCR testing, supported anaplasmosis as the primary infection. Lyme disease causing CNS infection would have been expected to respond much more slowly, and his course of progression beyond 24 hours on ceftriaxone made a posttreatment inflammatory response (Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction to treatment of spirochetal infections) less likely as the sole explanation of his symptoms and laboratory abnormalities.9 This trajectory differs from a previous anaplasmosis case with positive Lyme serology, which was treated with 21 days of ceftriaxone to cover potential neuroborreliosis.21 Conversely, it aligns with another described case of anaplasmosis associated with meningoencephalitis that achieved full recovery with 14 days of doxycycline, despite negative results from cerebrospinal fluid analysis.22

Anaplasmosis is increasing in prevalence, and its carrier is expanding in geographic regions across Canada. Although the clinical presentation is variable, this case highlights anaplasmosis with associated myocarditis. In cases of infection of unknown origin in endemic regions, particularly in patients who are deteriorating despite broad-spectrum antibiotics, tick-related diseases should be investigated even in the absence of known tick bites. Empiric antimicrobial therapy with doxycycline should not be delayed in a patient with a suggestive clinical presentation. Initiation of effective treatment in anaplasmosis is associated with favourable outcomes, even in patients with complications.
