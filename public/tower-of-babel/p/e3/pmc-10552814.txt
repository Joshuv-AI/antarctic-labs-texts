Assessment of explicit and implicit memories during remimazolam anaesthesia using the process dissociation procedure
Authors: Kyung Mi Kim, Ji-Yeon Bang, Byung-Moon Choi, Gyu-Jeong Noh
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDMemory formation during remimazolam anaesthesia, where a bispectral index (BIS) is sometimes not maintained at less than 60 despite the maximal dose, is worthy of evaluation.OBJECTIVEInvestigate the formation of explicit and implicit memories using the process dissociation procedure during remimazolam anaesthesia at a BIS of 60 to 80.DESIGNA prospective cohort study.SETTINGA tertiary medical centre in Seoul, South Korea, between March 2022 and July 2022.PATIENTSOne hundred patients undergoing general anaesthesia using remimazolam.INTERVENTIONSThe BIS was maintained at 60 to 80 during anaesthesia induction with remimazolam. Words were spoken to patients via headphones for 15 min.MAIN OUTCOME MEASUREThe primary outcome was the probability of explicit or implicit memory formation as calculated using the original and extended models, within 24 h after word presentation. Conscious recall memory was assessed using a short-structured interview within 1 and 24 h after surgery. Memory formation was inferred to be absent if 0 was included in the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the probability.RESULTSThe main results showed no evidence of explicit or implicit memory. The 95% CI of the probability of explicit memory formation included 0 for both models, –0.01 (–0.04 to 0.02) and –0.04 (–0.10 to 0.01), respectively. The 95% CI of the probability of implicit memory formation did not include 0 when evaluated using the original model, 0.08 (0.06 to 0.10), but included 0 when evaluated using the extended model, 0.00 (–0.03 to 0.03). The modified Brice interview revealed no evidence of awareness.CONCLUSIONSThere was no evidence of explicit or implicit memory formation during remimazolam anaesthesia (BIS 60 to 80). Further research is warranted to establish whether explicit and implicit memories are still absent even in the presence of surgical stimulation.CLINICAL TRIAL REGISTRATIONKCT0006752 (http://cris.nih.go.kr)

Introduction

Remimazolam is an ultra-short-acting benzodiazepine with advantages, such as rapid onset and offset, availability of reversal agent and a high safety profile for haemodynamic stability. Several recent studies suggest that remimazolam is well tolerated and effective for procedural sedation and induction and maintenance of general anaesthesia.1–3 Clinical investigations have demonstrated the noninferiority of remimazolam to propofol under general anaesthesia.4,5 However, the bispectral index (BISTM, Medtronic, Dublin, Ireland) of some patients administered remimazolam for general anaesthesia cannot be maintained at less than 60, typically recommended for general anaesthesia, even if they have been given the maximum dose, 12 mg kg−1 h−1 for induction and 2 mg kg−1 h−1 for maintenance.6–8 Postoperative interviews suggested that most patients had no memory formation during remimazolam anaesthesia at the level associated with a BIS of more than 60.7,8 Nevertheless, we cannot be certain that memory formation did not occur solely based on the current evidence. This is because there is no established method to evaluate memory formation during remimazolam anaesthesia, and most available observations are anecdotal and have been obtained within the scope of clinical practice. Therefore, it is still a crucial practical issue to evaluate whether memory formation would occur during remimazolam anaesthesia before it can be safely used as a general anaesthetic.

Memories that may form during general anaesthesia can be divided into explicit or implicit memory depending on whether they can be consciously recalled.9 Explicit memory refers to the clear remembering of events through conscious control processing, corresponding to recollection in the process of memory formation; anaesthesia awareness is directly related to explicit memory.9 In contrast, implicit memory through involuntary automatic processing cannot be clearly remembered; it can cause behavioural changes after anaesthesia.9,10

Whether explicit memory occurs in a patient under general anaesthesia can be confirmed through a postoperative survey.11 Unlike the formation of explicit memory, that of implicit memory cannot be determined only by the responses to the survey. In addition, a process dissociation procedure (PDP), which can mathematically separate unconscious automatic processing and conscious control processing of memory, has been introduced as an alternative method for the investigation of memory formation.10 The PDP uses a word stem completion test as a practical method for separating and evaluating the occurrence of explicit and implicit memories.10 Several studies have used the PDP with the word stem completion test to determine the formation of explicit and implicit memories during surgery with different types of anaesthetics.12–16

We hypothesised that the PDP in combination with the word stem completion test could help determine memory formation during remimazolam anaesthesia by separating and evaluating the occurrence of explicit and implicit memories. To test this hypothesis, we aimed to investigate explicit and implicit memories using the PDP, while the BIS was maintained at 60 to 80 in patients under remimazolam anaesthesia.

Materials and methods

Ethics and patients

Ethics approval for this prospective study was provided by the Ethics Committee of Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Centre, Seoul, South Korea on 5 November 2021 (#2021-1571) The study was registered in the International Clinical Trial Registry (http://cris.nih.go.kr, KCT0006752, date of registration: 12 November 2021) before patient enrolment.

This study included patients who underwent elective surgery with remimazolam as general anaesthesia at a single tertiary hospital in South Korea between March 2022 and July 2022. After obtaining written informed consent, 130 patients aged 20 to 70 years with an American Society of Anesthesiologists’ physical classification of 1 or 2 who were scheduled to undergo elective breast or thyroid surgery were included. All patients spoke Korean as their primary language and had a final educational background of high school graduation or higher. Patients were excluded if they had received any drug affecting the central nervous system within two weeks before surgery or if they had a neurological or psychiatric disorder, hearing deficit or a previous allergic reaction to benzodiazepines and opioids.

Anaesthesia methods and study protocol

The patients fasted from midnight on the day of surgery and received no premedication (such as midazolam) owing to the risk of impairment of implicit and explicit memories. On arrival in the operating room, an electrocardiogram, a noninvasive blood pressure device and pulse oximeter (Datex-Ohmeda S/5; Planar Systems, Inc., USA) were attached, and a BIS monitor (BISTM, Medtronic, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) was applied to the forehead. All data were recorded continuously until the end of anaesthesia. The study drug, remimazolam, (Byfavo Inj, Hana Pharm Co., Ltd, Korea) was infused at 1 or 2 mg kg−1 h−1 based on the discretion of the attending anaesthesiologists, and within the range of the approved maintenance dose,17 using a syringe pump (Perfusor, Space; B. Braun Melsungen AG, Germany). Remimazolam was adjusted between 0.2 and 2 mg kg−1 h−1 to maintain the target BIS range (60 to 80). The simulated concentrations of remimazolam and the modified observer assessment of alertness/sedation (MOAA/S) scores (Table 1) were measured during surgery. Ventilation was assisted using a face mask and 100% oxygen.

Modified observer assessment of alertness/sedation scores

After the BIS values had stabilised to within the target range for more than 1 min and the MOAA/S scores had reached 1 or less, a list of 24 Korean words was delivered to the patients via a headphone (SRH440, Shure Inc., Illinois, USA). The word list was recorded as an MP3 file in the voice of a woman in her early 20 s, and it included four smaller lists of six words each, as described previously (Supplementary Table S1).18 Each word on the list consisted of two-syllable nouns that are used at a frequency of more than 400 times per million words in the modern Korean vocabulary according to the Language Information Institute at Yonsei University.18,19 The 24 words were assigned randomly to four lists, two of which were presented during remimazolam anaesthesia (12 target words). Each word was repeated 12 consecutive times with a 2 s delay between repetitions, resulting in an approximately 15 min duration.13,19 In addition, during word presentation, the cumulative dose and simulated effect-site concentration (Ce) were calculated using pharmacokinetic data and also the blood-brain equilibration rate constant (ke0) proposed by Schüttler et al.,20 as described previously.3

Immediately after completion of the word presentation, induction and maintenance of general anaesthesia with remimazolam were performed according to the standard operating procedure for general surgery in all patients.3 At the end of the surgery, remimazolam and remifentanil were discontinued. The neuromuscular blockade was antagonised using sugammadex or a combination of neostigmine and glycopyrrolate according to the discretion of the attending anaesthesiologist. During emergence, recovery from anaesthesia was defined as the patient's ability to open their eyes in response to verbal commands. When a patient met the extubation criteria (BIS ≥80 and train-of-four ratio >0.9 for >1 min), they were extubated and transferred to a postanaesthesia care unit (PACU).

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint of this study was to assess memory formation using a PDP with the word stem completion test. The word stem completion test was conducted in the general ward within 24 h after the end of surgery. Although only two lists of words (the target words) were presented during anaesthesia, the postoperative word stem completion test consisted of all four lists (including target and distractor words). During the word stem completion test, two sets of word lists were used for the inclusion and exclusion conditions: one list of target words and one list of distractor words for the inclusion condition, and one list of target words and one list of distractor words for the exclusion condition (Supplementary Table 2). For example, if a patient was randomly assigned to group 1 by the computer, they would be presented with lists 1 and 3 as target words during anaesthesia. During the postoperative word stem completion test, they would be audibly presented with the first syllable of every word in lists 1 to 4 inclusive. When lists 1 and 2 were played, the patient was asked to complete the word stem on the test sheet using the word they had heard during word presentation in the operating room. If the patient could not remember, they were instructed to write down the first word that came to mind. When lists 3 and 4 were played, the patient was instructed to complete the word stem on the test sheet using other words (while avoiding the words presented during anaesthesia). If they could not remember it, they were asked to complete the word stem with the first nonrecalled word that came to mind.

The probability of the occurrence of explicit and implicit memories was obtained for each individual, and the average values and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated for the entire group. Memory formation was interpreted as not having occurred if 0 was included in the 95% CI and was interpreted to have occurred when 0 was not included in the 95% CI.19,22

The secondary study outcome was conscious recall memory, as assessed by the modified Brice interview.11 This was conducted by a trained research nurse blinded to the study conditions and scores of the questionnaire items in the recovery room after the restoration of consciousness, and in the ward within 24 h after the end of surgery. The answers to the interviews were evaluated based on the Michigan Awakening Classification System, which has excellent inter-observer agreement (Supplementary Table S3).23

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SigmaStat for Windows (version 3.5, Systat Software, Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Values were considered statistically significant at a P value less than 0.05. The normal distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are summarised as the mean ± SD [range], and nonnormally distributed continuous variables are presented as median [IQR]. Categorical data were presented as the number of patients.

Sample size estimation

As this was an exploratory study, sample size calculation was not performed. We aimed for 100 participants because this was considered a feasible number for enrolment that would provide sufficient information for the confirmatory trial.

Of the remaining 130, 30 were excluded for the following reasons: deviation of BIS values outside the target range for more than 10% of the word presentation time of 15 min (24), refusal to perform the word stem completion test (3) and violation of study protocol (3) (Fig. 1). Discussion

Using a PDP in combination with the word stem completion test, we found that no evidence of explicit or implicit memory when BIS was maintained at 60 to 80 during remimazolam anaesthesia. The short-structured postanaesthesia interview showed that patients had no conscious recall during remimazolam anaesthesia, while the BIS remained between 60 and 80.

The PDP was introduced by Jacoby as a tool for mathematically isolating the effects of conscious (explicit memory) versus unconscious (implicit memory) control of memory.10 To assess the relative contributions of explicit and implicit memories to the memory test, it is generally combined with a WS test, which is a practical method of repeating certain words during anaesthesia, and evaluating whether they can be recalled consciously or unconsciously after the anaesthesia wears off.14,18,19,22 One of the critical characteristics of PDP is that instead of performing separate tests, it requires subjects to attempt a single test under two different conditions.10 In the inclusion condition, subjects are typically instructed to complete the word stem completion test using recalled words (if possible) or with the first words that come to mind. In the exclusion condition, subjects are forbidden to use the recalled words and are instructed to respond with words that have not been presented previously. As a result, explicit memories help performance under the inclusion condition but hinder performance under the exclusion condition, whereas implicit memories always help performance (both inclusion and exclusion conditions) by increasing the likelihood of subjects responding with previously learned words.

Some researchers have argued that the possibility of accidental word-matching should be considered when calculating the probability of explicit and implicit memories. This led to the establishment of an extended model with a calculation process different from that of Jacoby's original model.21 Using this extended model, Hadzidiakos et al.22 re-analysed data from previous studies13,24,25 to re-evaluate the occurrence of explicit and implicit memories. Although these studies had reported evidence of implicit memory function during surgery using the original model, the re-analysis using the extended model showed no such evidence. Our study, which investigated memory formation during remimazolam anaesthesia using both original and extended models, supports these findings. We found that implicit memory formation occurred only when word stem completion data were analysed using the original model, while there was no evidence of explicit or implicit memory function when the data were evaluated using the extended model. These results suggest that the extended model effectively blocks aspects that can be mistaken for implicit memory by accounting for the possibility of coincidental guessing. Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of selecting an appropriate model when using the PDP to assess memory formation.19,22

Maintaining an appropriate hypnotic depth during general anaesthesia is essential to prevent memory formation. In modern anaesthesia, processed electroencephalogram (EEG)-based hypnotic depth indicators, such as BIS and the patient state index, have been widely established as standard techniques for monitoring during anaesthesia to assess the hypnotic effect of anaesthetic drugs.26 There is no evidence of memory formation for auditory information presented at BIS levels of 40 to 60 during general anaesthesia.15,27 However, BIS values during general anaesthesia with remimazolam are higher than those during general anaesthesia with propofol.28 In addition, intra-operative BIS values were reported to increase to more than 60 even when patients were confirmed to reach sufficient hypnotic levels for general anaesthesia based on other hypnotic depth indicators, such as spectral edge frequency and pupil diameter.7

Moreover, BIS value took approximately twice as long (135 s) to fall to less than 60 as the time taken to achieve an MOAA/S score of 1 or less (63 s),8 and it did not correlate well with loss of consciousness in remimazolam anaesthesia.2,8,29 Notably, in our study, BIS values during word presentation for 15 min were maintained at more than 60 (60 to 80) for more than 13.5 min despite a MOAA/S score of 2 or less. These findings may be because the BIS index has not been validated for monitoring the depth of anaesthesia with remimazolam.7,8,17 The BIS algorithm was originally optimised to yield an approximately linear response to increasing doses of propofol, and the databases used to develop the BIS index did not include EEG data from patients under remimazolam anaesthesia. It is also possible that EEG changes during remimazolam anaesthesia were different from those during anaesthesia using other intravenous anaesthetics. EEG changes following an intravenous bolus of midazolam, a benzodiazepine similar to remimazolam, predominantly showed evidence of beta activation, these resulted in BIS values remaining around 60.30 Similarly, a phase I study of 20 healthy volunteers reported that EEG changes during remimazolam infusion were characterised by an initial transient power increase in the beta frequency band.20 A prominent increment in beta waves (13 to 25 Hz) was observed during remimazolam anaesthesia in surgical patients with intra-operative BIS values of more than 60, even though the remimazolam dose was increased to the maximum (2 mg kg−1 h−1).7 Further studies on precise EEG analysis are needed to determine whether this activation of the beta wave is associated with such a high BIS value more than 60 and to determine the appropriate range of BIS values during anaesthesia.

This study has several limitations. First, the patients were not exposed to noxious surgical stimuli. Thus, our observations could not preclude the possibility that particular surgical characteristics other than hypnotic state fluctuations contribute to residual memory function during seemingly adequate degrees of hypnosis.15 Further research is required to establish whether implicit and explicit memory functions are absent even in the presence of surgical stimulation during remimazolam anaesthesia with BIS 60 to 80. Second, our patients received remimazolam infusion not only during word presentation but also throughout the entire operation. Thus, it is possible that remimazolam administered during surgery affected the word stem completion test results. However, remimazolam is known to cause anterograde amnesia, not retrograde amnesia,7 and the infusion during surgery would not have a significant effect on the results of the word stem completion test. Third, 24 patients were excluded because the BIS values deviated outside the target range for more than 10% (mean 25.8% [14.4 to 72.6%]) of the word presentation time of 15 min. Out of a total of 4318 BIS data points from 24 patients, 16.7% of the BIS values were less than 60, and 14.1% were greater than 80. Although no intra-operative arousal or recall was reported in these patients, the word stem completion test was not conducted. Therefore, based solely on our findings, it is difficult to conclude whether there was no explicit or implicit memory formation in patients who failed to maintain BIS values within the target range for more than 90% of the word presentation time. Fourth, memory was tested on the next day at an average of 16.2 h ± 4.5 [8.4 to 23.2] after word presentation. It is difficult to completely rule out the possibility that the timing of these memory tests has affected the test results, potentially leading to a lower probability of memory formation. Finally, this study was performed on Korean patients who spoke Korean as their primary language. This necessitates caution when generalising our observations to other populations, and further studies are needed in groups with other first languages.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that, in the absence of any noxious stimulus, explicit or implicit memory formation does not occur in patients under remimazolam anaesthesia at BIS levels between 60 and 80. Further research should aim to establish whether explicit and implicit memories are still absent even in the presence of surgical stimulation during remimazolam anaesthesia at BIS levels of more than 60.
