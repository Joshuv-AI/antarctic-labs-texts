Transarterial Embolization for Medically Refractory Kasabach–Merritt Syndrome: A Case Report
Authors: Taiga Matsumoto, Hiro Kiyosue, Yasuyuki Kaku, Hidetaka Hayashi, Yoshitaka Tamura, Akitake Mukasa, Toshinori Hirai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveKasabach–Merritt syndrome (KMS) is a life-threatening disorder characterized by severe thrombocytopenia and consumptive coagulopathy associated with vascular tumors, typically Kaposiform hemangioendothelioma (KHE) or tufted angioma. Drug therapy is the first-line treatment, but some cases are refractory, and no established salvage therapy exists. This report describes a case of KMS resistant to multiple drug therapies in which transarterial embolization (TAE) was successfully performed.Case PresentationA 2-month-old male infant presented with a left posterior neck mass. Examination revealed severe thrombocytopenia (5000/μL) and coagulation abnormalities. Imaging studies diagnosed KMS with KHE. Platelet counts did not improve despite sequential treatment with corticosteroids, beta-blockers, and sirolimus. TAE was indicated due to the high risk of fatal bleeding. Angiography identified feeding vessels from the posterior muscular branch of the vertebral artery and the ascending cervical artery. TAE was performed using 20% n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA) for the vertebral artery branch and gelatin sponge particles for the ascending cervical artery branch. Post-embolization, platelet counts normalized, and the tumor shrank.ConclusionIn a case of drug-resistant KMS, TAE effectively restored platelet counts and prevented fatal complications. When performed with appropriate embolic selection and careful complication management, arterial embolization can be a useful adjunctive treatment.

Introduction

Kasabach–Merritt syndrome (KMS) is a relatively rare pathology characterized by thrombocytopenia and abnormalities in the coagulation–fibrinolysis system, associated with underlying diseases such as Kaposiform hemangioendothelioma (KHE) or tufted angioma.1,2) It frequently causes massive hemorrhage or multiple organ failure, with a reported mortality rate of 10%–37%.3) Medical therapies such as steroids, interferon, vincristine, and propranolol are typically administered, but cases of treatment resistance are observed. Recently, mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase (mTOR) inhibitors have been reported as effective, yet no established treatment protocol exists.4) Furthermore, mTOR inhibitors take 1–4 weeks to increase platelet counts. This might be too slow in an emergency case. We report a case of medically refractory KMS treated by transarterial embolization (TAE) with a literature review.

Case Presentation

A 2-month-old male infant with a large neck mass was referred to our hospital. The patient was born at 39 weeks and 1 day of gestation with a birth weight of 3400 g via spontaneous vaginal delivery. His family history was unremarkable. Physical examination revealed a large, firm, and elastic mass in the left posterior neck. Laboratory investigations on admission showed mild anemia (hemoglobin: 8.3 g/dL), marked thrombocytopenia (platelet count: 5000/μL), and coagulopathy (fibrinogen: 123 mg/dL; fibrinogen degradation product: 32.9 μg/mL; D-dimer: 28.1 μg/mL). Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a large soft-tissue mass with marked enhancement extending from the left retropharyngeal space to the posterior neck. The mass compressed the pharynx anteriorly, extended into the spinal canal, and compressed the cervical spinal cord (Fig. 1). It revealed isointensity to muscle on T1-weighted MR images and mixed low and high intensity on T2-weighted MR images (Fig. 2A and 2B). 3D time-of-flight MRA revealed dilated feeding arteries from the left vertebral artery and the ascending cervical artery to the mass (Fig. 2C). Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with KHE complicated by KMS.

The patient required platelet transfusions (90 mL: 15 mL/kg) every 2 to 7 days. Initial medical management commenced with combined beta-blocker and corticosteroid therapy. Propranolol was initiated at 1 mg/kg/day, escalated to 2 mg/kg/day after 2 days, and further increased to 3 mg/kg/day 3 days later. Corticosteroids were started intravenously and subsequently switched to oral prednisolone (PSL) at 2 mg/kg/day. Although this regimen was maintained for 4 weeks, the platelet count did not improve sufficiently. Consequently, sirolimus was introduced, and a tapering schedule for PSL was planned.

Sirolimus was started at 0.04 mg/kg/day (0.25 mg/day) targeting a trough level of 5–15 ng/mL; however, the serum concentration was subtherapeutic at 3.4 ng/mL. Two weeks later, the dose was increased to 0.05 mg/kg/day (0.33 mg/day), achieving a therapeutic level of 5.8 ng/mL.

The PSL dose was tapered, and then the regimen was switched to hydrocortisone (2.4 mg/kg/day). Following the introduction of sirolimus, the patient was weaned off blood transfusions. Chronologically, however, the platelet count failed to normalize despite the sustained therapeutic concentration of sirolimus. Five weeks after the initiation of sirolimus (3 weeks after reaching the therapeutic range), the platelet count was 75000/μL, which then decreased to 65000/μL 2 days later. Based on these findings, the medical therapy was deemed partially refractory as the platelet recovery plateaued at suboptimal levels and began to show a concerning downward trend. Furthermore, the tumor continued to exert a critical mass effect, invading the spinal canal and compressing the spinal cord.

Although the patient had not yet exhibited overt neurological symptoms, the incomplete resolution of the consumptive coagulopathy and the unacceptably high risk of sudden hemorrhage made waiting further for the maximum effect of sirolimus unsafe. Therefore, we decided to perform TAE as a prompt, adjunctive intervention to physically occlude the tumor feeding vessels, halt the platelet trapping within the tumor, and definitively eliminate both the bleeding risk and the impending neurological compromise.

At the time of TAE, the patient was 4 months old. Under general anesthesia, a 3-Fr sheath was placed into the right femoral artery, and then a 3-Fr catheter was introduced into the left vertebral artery. Vertebral angiography revealed a large contrast blush representing the neck mass supplied by multiple feeders originating from the segmental arteries at the C1 to C5 levels (Fig. 3A). A 1.7-Fr microcatheter was navigated into feeders from the posterior branches of the C3 and C4 segmental arteries, and those feeders were embolized with a 20% n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA)–Lipiodol mixture (Fig. 3B and 3C). Preoperative contrast-enhanced CT suggested that the vertebral artery supplied the intraspinal component of the lesion. Although intraoperative 3D rotational DSA could have provided detailed microvascular anatomy, it was intentionally omitted to strictly minimize radiation exposure and contrast medium overload in this infant. Consequently, without 3D spatial confirmation, we relied on 2D angiographic findings and anatomical principles. Branches directing anteromedially from the vertebral artery toward the lesion were judged to carry a prohibitively high risk of sharing or giving off radiculomedullary supply to the spinal canal. To absolutely preclude the catastrophic complication of spinal cord infarction, we deliberately preserved these anteromedial feeders. Left subclavian angiography immediately after embolization showed marked regression of tumor stain from the vertebral artery branches, while supply from the ascending cervical artery became apparent (Fig. 3D and 3E). The vertebral and ascending cervical arteries frequently anastomose, allowing for a compensatory hemodynamic shift following the reduction in vertebral artery perfusion. Subsequently, the microcatheter was navigated into the feeders from the ascending cervical artery. Before performing embolization of the ACA feeders, we used 2D angiography to confirm the absence of any overt dangerous anastomoses or spinal branches toward the spinal cord. To prevent the migration of embolic material into small, invisible dangerous branches, gelatin sponge particles cut into 1-mm cubes were utilized for cautious embolization (Fig. 3F). Final angiography showed a marked reduction of the blood supply to the entire tumor (Fig. 3G). No complications were observed during and after the procedure.

At 18 months post-embolization, the patient remains free of coagulation abnormalities under continued oral sirolimus therapy.

Discussion

KHE is a locally aggressive vascular tumor.2,3) While it rarely metastasizes, it poses significant management challenges due to its infiltrative growth into surrounding tissues. The most critical complication, KMS, characterized by severe thrombocytopenia and consumptive coagulopathy, occurs in approximately 40%–70% of cases and carries a high mortality rate due to hemorrhage.2,3)

The primary treatment goals for KMS are to control thrombocytopenia and prevent life-threatening complications. Pharmacotherapy is the standard first-line treatment; however, conventional agents such as corticosteroids and beta-blockers (e.g., propranolol) often prove ineffective, particularly for KHE-associated KMS.5,6) Recently, sirolimus, mTOR inhibitor, has emerged as a promising agent.1,4) Although recent reports suggest that lower target trough levels (e.g., 5–10 ng/mL) can achieve efficacy with reduced adverse events, response can be slow or incomplete in aggressive cases.1) In our case, despite sirolimus administration, platelet recovery was insufficient, prompting the decision to intervene with TAE. Table 1 summarizes the indications and timing of TAE in previously reported cases.7–10) In clinical scenarios involving high-severity disease or pre-existing hemorrhagic signs, TAE is often performed as a “bridge therapy” to prevent life-threatening bleeding during the latency period required for sirolimus to take effect. In the present case, although the patient exhibited persistent thrombocytopenia and a critical mass effect, there were no overt signs of active hemorrhage. This clinical stability allowed us to wait for an adequate period after initiating sirolimus, ensuring the procedure was performed at the most appropriate timing after confirming a plateaued response to medical therapy.

Reports indicate that embolization can rapidly improve platelet counts, often within 48 h to 28 days, by reducing platelet trapping within the tumor.10–12) However, TAE in the head and neck region in infants carries risks of serious complications, including arterial injury at the puncture site, cerebral infarction or cranial nerve deficits due to non-target embolization. A careful and gentle procedure is essential to avoid such complications. A 3-Fr catheter system was employed to minimize puncture site complications, and careful catheter/microcatheter navigation was performed to avoid vascular injury.

Selection of embolic materials and careful injection may be important to avoid non-target embolization. Because the use of small-diameter microspheres in the head and neck region carries a significant risk of unexpected ischemic complications—such as skin necrosis or cranial nerve palsy—previous reports on embolization for KHE frequently recommend using larger microspheres (300–500 μm).10,13) While particles are easier to handle, they carry inherent limitations. Particles themselves are radiolucent, invisible on X-ray fluoroscopy or DSA.14) Furthermore, particles can aggregate proximally in the feeder and may flow back into the parent artery such as the vertebral artery. For this reason, we utilized NBCA for the feeders from the vertebral artery. Unlike particles, NBCA mixed with Lipiodol is radiopaque, allowing for real-time, precise visualization of the embolic cast.

Furthermore, particle clusters may aggregate near the proximal nutrient branches and potentially reflux into parent arteries such as the vertebral artery.

Other embolization materials include metal coils, bleomycin, ethanol, NBCA, and Onyx.3,4,9) Coils provide proximal occlusion but limited distal penetration, while bleomycin and ethanol induce endothelial damage and thrombosis, with potential severe complications.4,15) NBCA and Onyx are liquid embolic agents with different mechanisms: NBCA induces rapid polymerization and thrombosis, whereas Onyx primarily provides mechanical occlusion.9,16,17) Activation of the mTOR pathway and induction of non-specific inflammatory processes are also implicated, with reports indicating lesion reduction; however, serious complications such as pulmonary fibrosis are known. Ethanol has potent embolizing power but also causes significant inflammatory spread to surrounding areas.15) Reports using Onyx also exist, showing favorable outcomes.9)

There is a potential risk of migration of liquid embolic materials (or small particles) into the branches supplying the spinal cord or nerve root. In this case, the tumor invaded into the spinal canal, supplied by feeders from anterior branches of the segmental artery, and we therefore avoided embolizing those feeders with NBCA. Large gelatin sponge particles were used for embolization of feeders from the ascending cervical artery.

Although the tumor was hypervascular, our primary goal was to prevent fatal hemorrhage rather by reducing the tumor vascular bed than by complete devascularization of the tumor. A dual-agent embolization strategy demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes in the present case.

Furthermore, continuing sirolimus after embolization acts as a complementary maintenance therapy. Embolization provides immediate “de-bulking” of the vascular bed and rapid hematological improvement, while sirolimus suppresses neovascularization and tumor regrowth over the long term.4,10) This combination strategy is likely to enhance sustained therapeutic efficacy.

We acknowledge that, as a single case report, our findings regarding the optimal selection of embolic materials cannot be generalized to all KHE patients. Additionally, given the relatively short follow-up period, the long-term durability of embolization and the potential for late recurrence remain to be determined. Further accumulation of cases is therefore warranted to establish a standardized protocol for combining sirolimus and TAE.

Conclusion

In cases of drug-resistant KMS with local infiltration, TAE is a valuable treatment option that can improve platelet counts and prevent fatal complications. When performed with meticulous attention to safety and appropriate embolization techniques, it serves as a highly effective adjunctive therapy.
