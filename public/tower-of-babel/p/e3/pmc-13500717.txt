Dynamic catalytic interface driven three-step synergistic mechanism for boosting ammonia and hydroxylamine synthesis
Authors: Yuxiang Li, Junliang Xie, Tingyi Weng, Zhenjie Lu, Xing Yan, Yanhui Liu, Shuaishuai Lu, Shengli Zhang, Huan Chen, Fang Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the short-term industrial landscape remaining unchanged, green synthesis technologies for ammonia (NH3) and hydroxylamine (NH2OH) are critical for carbon neutrality. Alternatively, electrocatalytic nitrate reduction (NIRR) can be integrated with membrane separation technology via a modular design, simultaneously achieving pollution control and resource recovery. However, the microscopic mechanism of NIRR remains ambiguous given the complex dynamic catalytic interface, hindering advanced catalyst development. Here, we propose a three-step synergistic mechanism at dynamic catalytic interface, which integrates interfacial microenvironment regulation, OH species cycle, and reverse hydrogen spillover for efficient NH3 and NH2OH synthesis across different scenarios. Notably, dual-site heterostructure catalyst exhibits almost 100% NH3-Faradaic Efficiency (FENH3) across a wide nitrate concentration range, reaching a maximum NH3 yield of 10.27 mmol h−1 cm−2. The simultaneous NH3 synthesis-recovery system maintains almost 100% FENH3 and NH3 recovery efficiency over 120 h, accompanied by long-term durability and negligible performance degradation. Additionally, cyclopentanone-mediated NIRR process delivers a satisfactory NH2OH-Faradaic Efficiency (83.48%), and the assembled zinc-nitrate battery achieves a high peak power density (57.6 mW cm−2).Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

Annual carbon dioxide emissions from the production of nitrogen-based chemicals reach approximately 400 million tons, creating conflict with carbon neutrality and sustainable development objectives1–3. Ammonia (NH3) and hydroxylamine (NH2OH) serve as important chemical feedstocks with extensive applications in the agriculture, medicine, and chemical industry4,5. To date, the industrial synthesis of NH3 is dominated by the energy-intensive Haber-Bosch process, which accounts for approximately 2% of global energy consumption accompanied by substantial CO2 emission6. Unfortunately, conventional industrial methods for NH₂OH synthesis (e.g., the Raschig process and nitric oxide reduction) also rely on ammonia as a primary feedstock7. Additionally, nitrate (NO3−) is widely distributed at various concentrations within photovoltaic and nuclear wastewater, and its denitrification process contributes to the restoration of nitrogen cycle imbalance8. To address growing energy and environmental challenges, electrochemical nitrate reduction reaction (NIRR) technology has been developed to simultaneously remove NO3− and synthesize NH3 under mild conditions. It is noteworthy that NH2OH, a valuable intermediate in the electrochemical nitrate-to-ammonia process, has been demonstrated to be applicable to oxime synthesis. In general, the NIRR process involves three key stages: (1) the transport and activation of interfacial water (H2O); (2) the generation of active hydrogen (*H) during H2O dissociation; (3) the deoxygenation and hydrogenation of adsorbed intermediates (*NOx) for NH3 synthesis. However, the presence of competitive hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and various by-products (NO2−, N2, N2H4, etc.) lead to the challenges in achieving high NH3 selectivity9. To meet the requirement of industrial-scale production, the designed catalyst must achieve high Faradaic Efficiency (FE) at ampere-level current density while maintaining long-term operational stability.

Hence, recent studies have increasingly focused on the rational design of dual-site catalysts and their tandem catalysis mechanism to facilitate efficient intermediate conversion, enabling the selective product distribution dominated by NH310–12. Owing to the favorable alignment of the Cu d-orbital energy levels with the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital π* of nitrate, Cu-based catalysts confer a distinct advantage in the initial 2e− process (NO3−→NO2−)13,14. In contrast, noble metals (e.g., Ru, Pd, Pt) and non-noble transition metal compounds (e.g., CoP, Ni hydroxides, Fe2O3), possessing favorable water-activation properties, critically govern the subsequent 6e− process (NO2−→NH3)15–17. Based on this, our previous work confirmed that the dual-site catalyst consisting between CoP and Cu3P sites could modulate the synergistic catalysis effect of *H and *NOx, facilitating the simultaneous enhancement of NIRR activity and NH3 selectivity18. Furthermore, recent studies have focused on optimizing *H transfer and utilization between dual sites to enhance the tandem catalytic mechanisms. For instance, the oxygen-vacancy-rich WO3-x was designed as Lewis acid sites for *H generation and storage, while the strong electronic interaction between intercalated *H and adjacent Ru nanoclusters facilitated the deprotonation process of the Brønsted acid sites (HxWO3). The hydrogen intercalation and spillover processes between dual sites accelerated the hydrogenation kinetics from NO3− to NH319. Despite the fact that the dual-site functional mechanism has been associated with *H kinetic behavior, the microscopic mechanism focusing on dynamic catalytic interface still requires further investigation20.

Here, we systematically elucidated the three-step synergistic mechanism involving interfacial microenvironment regulation, OH species cycle, and reverse hydrogen spillover at dynamic catalytic interface, which confirmed the interaction among three key stages of NIRR process. In-situ investigation combined with ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations revealed that the weak hydrogen-bond network facilitated the transport and activation of interfacial H2O, thereby ensuring the timely delivery of feedstock during Volmer step. The differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) combined with deuterium labeling experiments indicated that the fast OH species cycle mediated by dynamic reconstruction enhanced the interaction between active sites and electrolyte composition, promoting the *H generation and supply in the Volmer step. The kinetic characterization combined with density functional theory (DFT) calculation confirmed that the reverse hydrogen spillover between dual sites accelerated the *H transfer and utilization, thus efficiently driving *NOx hydrogenation while inhibiting HER process. Based on this, we constructed dual-site heterostructure and dual-site doping catalysts to investigate the differences in their microscopic synergistic catalysis mechanisms. Experimental results demonstrate that the higher NIRR activity of dual-site heterostructure catalyst originates from the exposed high-index edge facets, stable Cu-O-Co bridging motifs, and well-matched interfacial synergy, which together enhance this integrated three-step synergistic mechanism.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of electrocatalysts

Owing to its intrinsically high conductivity, Cu foam (CF) was employed as the current collector and self-supporting electrode for electrolysis. The derived Cu(OH)2 nanowires (NW) provided a template for the epitaxial growth of cobalt carbonate hydroxide (CoCH). Subsequently, rCo(OH)2/CuNW heterostructure was obtained by pre-reduction and electrochemical activation of CoCH/Cu2O, while Cu-doped Co(OH)2 was prepared by direct electrochemical activation without pre-reduction (Fig. 1a). During the direct electrochemical activation of CoCH/Cu2O, the reconstruction from CoCH to Co(OH)₂ species was accompanied by the decomposition of Cu2O, resulting in a Cu doping effect in Cu-Co(OH)2. In contrast, the additional pre-reduction process allowed the Cu2O-derived CuNW species to be remained in rCo(OH)2/CuNW during the reconstruction process. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of both rCo(OH)2/CuNW and Cu-Co(OH)2 displayed a series of peaks corresponding to Cu phase (JCPDS 85–1326) and β-Co(OH)2 phase (JCPDS 30-0443) (Suppl. Fig. S1)21. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed the nanoflower-like structure of Cu-Co(OH)2, while rCo(OH)2/CuNW heterostructure consisted of hexagonal Co(OH)2 nanoplates supported on CuNW. This is because the additional pre-reduction treatment played a key role in forming crystal-optimized hexagonal Co(OH)2 nanoplates, as further confirmed in rCo(OH)2@CC (Suppl. Figs. S2–6). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) and corresponding Fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern of rCo(OH)2/CuNW identified a dihedral angle of 56.5° between the (0002) and (101¯1) facets of the β-Co(OH)2 phase, accompanied by a series of exposed high-index edge facets along the [12¯10] zone axis (Fig. 1b)22. Meanwhile, the crystal-optimized Co(OH)2 nanoplates formed during reconstruction process were tightly embedded with the CuNW to construct the rCo(OH)2/CuNW heterostructure, which facilitated efficient axial charge transport between the active species and the CF substrate (Fig. 1c). Furthermore, the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern along [0001] zone axis confirmed the perfect single-crystal structure of Co(OH)2 nanoplates, with (0001) facets as the top/bottom surface and six {101¯0} edge facets (Fig. 1d and Suppl. Fig. S7). Due to the Cu2+ doping effect (t2g6eg3), the local structural asymmetry induced by Jahn-Teller distortion aggravates the thermodynamic instability of high-index crystal facets, thus making Cu-Co(OH)2 preferentially expose low-index (0001) facets with lower energy and higher thermodynamic stability (Suppl. Fig. S8)23. To eliminate interference from CF substrate, Grazing incidence X-ray diffractometry (GIXRD) was employed to investigate the shallow surface of catalysts by adjusting the incident angle to 1°. There was a negative shift of Co(OH)2 diffraction peak in Cu-Co(OH)2 powder, further confirming the doping effect of Cu (with a larger atomic radius) within the Co(OH)2 crystal24. The resulting increase in lattice constant was consistent with the HRTEM observations. Unlike the Cu doping effect in Cu-Co(OH)2, rCo(OH)2/CuNW powder displayed a stronger diffraction peak assigned to CuNW on the catalyst surface (Fig. 1e). The energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) data indicated that the Cu doping percentage in Cu-Co(OH)2 nanoflowers was 3.6%, roughly one-third of CuNW content in rCo(OH)2/CuNW (Suppl. Fig. S9 and Suppl. Table S1). The difference in Co(OH)2 edge facets and Cu-O-Co bridging motifs between the two significantly influenced the NIRR reactivity.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was executed to investigate the electronic structure of surface elements (Suppl. Fig. S10). The peaks of Co 2p spectra in Cu-Co(OH)2 displayed a positive shift (0.2 eV) compared to rCo(OH)2/CuNW, suggesting the electron donating around the Co sites caused by Cu doping effect25. Meanwhile, all the Cu 2p spectra exhibited similar Cu(II)-type binding energy peaks under the action of hydroxyl radical. However, the binding energy center of Cu-Co(OH)2 in Cu LMM Auger spectra skewed towards low-valence-state Cu, which was attributed to the electron accepting around Cu sites18. X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) was employed to probe the atomic-scale coordination information of the catalysts. In the Co K-edge X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra, both rCo(OH)2/CuNW and Cu-Co(OH)2 displayed characteristic peaks similar to the Co(OH)2 reference (Fig. 1f). Notably, the lower valence state of rCo(OH)2/CuNW was reflected in the pre-edge peak similar to Co foil and the lower absorption edge energy than that of Cu-Co(OH)2, as confirmed by plotting main absorption edges as a function of oxidation states (Fig. 1g)22. Although Fourier-transform extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) illustrated two main peaks assigned to Co-OH (1.6 Å) and Co-O-Co (2.8 Å) scattering paths, rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited an additional Co-Co scattering path (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Table S2)26. The wavelet transform of EXAFS further confirmed the difference in Co-Co bonds between the two, as evidenced by the lower coordination numbers of Co-OH and Co-O-Co bonds in rCo(OH)2/CuNW compared to Cu-Co(OH)2 (Fig. 1i–j and Suppl. Fig. S11, 12)27,28. As a result, the additional pre-reduction treatment before electrochemical activation facilitated the formation of low-valence-state Co/Co(OH)2 species.

Electrocatalytic NIRR performance

The NIRR performance of catalysts was evaluated through various electrochemical experiments using a three-electrode system in H-type cell. Due to the positive correlation between current density and electrocatalytic activity, linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed in 1.0 M KOH with and without 0.1 M KNO3. Specifically, rCo(OH)2/CuNW displayed the highest current density of 1.4 A cm−2 at −0.80 V vs. RHE among all catalysts, demonstrating its enhanced NIRR activity (Fig. 2a). Subsequently, ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectra and calibration curves were used to quantify the electrolysis products (Suppl. Fig. S13, 14). In the NIRR process, the excessive accumulation of NO2− intermediate will cause the low selectivity towards NH3. Although rCuNW exhibited a high current response during the NIRR process, its excessively high NO2− yield indicated the poor deoxygenation and hydrogenation capabilities compared to rCo(OH)2@CC (Suppl. Figs. S15–19)29. Owing to the synergistic advantages of dual sites, rCo(OH)2/CuNW displayed higher current density and lower FENO2− in the potential range from −0.1 to −0.8 V vs. RHE (Fig. 2b). Hence, rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited a higher FENH3 (97.41%) at −0.4 V vs. RHE compared to Cu-Co(OH)2 (89.48%) (Fig. 2c). Meanwhile, rCo(OH)2/CuNW achieved a high NH3 yield of 6.06 mmol h−1 cm−2 with an NH3 partial current density (jNH3) of 1.3 A cm−2 at −0.8 V vs. RHE (Suppl. Fig. S20, 21). Experimentally, electrochemical performance might be affected by electrode area. A maximum NH3 yield of 10.27 mmol h−1 cm−2 was obtained at an electrode area of 0.25 cm2, corresponding to a nearly 100% FENH3 and a high jNH3 (2.2 A cm−2), which seems competitive with those reported for high-performance Cu-based electrocatalysts in previous works (Suppl. Fig. S22 and Suppl. Table S3). Notably, the FENH3 still remained close to 100% despite a 16-fold increase in the electrode area, demonstrating the promising industrial application potential of rCo(OH)2/CuNW. To decouple geometric effects from intrinsic activity, turnover frequency (TOF) values were calculated under the assumption that all Co and Cu sites participate in NIRR process. Specifically, rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited higher TOF values (Co sites: 0.29 s−1; Cu sites: 0.37 s−1) compared to Cu-Co(OH)2 (Co sites: 0.17 s−1; Cu sites: 0.34 s−1), implying its intrinsically high activity for NH3 synthesis (Suppl. Fig. S23). As for possible by-products, rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited negligible hydrazine (N2H4) yield and low FEH2, demonstrating its high product selectivity towards NH3 during the NIRR process (Suppl. Figs. S24, 25). Despite the competitive NIRR performance of rCo(OH)2/CuNW, control experiments combined with different quantification methods were employed to exclude contamination and minimize error (Suppl. Fig. S26). Specifically, there was negligible NH3 detected in electrolyte after electrolysis both at open circuit potential and in 1.0 M KOH. Furthermore, the NH3 yield and FENH3 calculated by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) were comparable to those obtained by UV-vis method (Suppl. Fig. S27).

As an essential criterion for evaluating catalysts, the stability of electrocatalytic performance and active species should be investigated under different application conditions. Specifically, the electrolysis cycle test demonstrated the slight fluctuation in current density, NH3 yield, and FENH3 over twenty consecutive cycles, confirming the stable NIRR performance of rCo(OH)2/CuNW (Suppl. Fig. S28). After cycle test, the XRD, SEM, XPS, XAFS results of rCo(OH)2/CuNW revealed the steady-state morphology, crystal phase, and electronic structure of Co(OH)2 and Cu species (Suppl. Figs. S29–32). Notably, the O 1 s spectra exhibited a slight increase in Co-O/Co-OH ratio after cycle test (Suppl. Fig. S33). ICP-MS results indicated that the leaching concentrations of Co and Cu in rCo(OH)2/CuNW were both below 0.01 mg L−1, which further verified its structural stability (Supplementary Table S4). To simulate various industrial wastewater scenarios, experiments involving different pH conditions and nitrate concentrations were performed to investigate the NIRR performance of rCo(OH)2/CuNW. Due to the faster mass transfer, both current density and NH3 yield increased with the nitrate concentration (Suppl. Fig. S34). Under simulated alkaline low-level nuclear wastewater conditions, rCo(OH)2/CuNW achieved the FENH3 of no less than 93.81% in the nitrate concentration range from 20 mM to 1000 mM (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, the flow cell and membrane electrode assembly (MEA) were respectively coupled with a gas-permeable polypropylene fiber membrane to integrate the simultaneous ammonia synthesis-recovery system, thus enabling continuous production and in-situ separation of NH3 (Suppl. Fig. S35-36). Compared to the flow cell, MEA-based alkaline anion-exchange membrane (AEM) electrolyzer adopts a zero-gap design and compact configuration, aiming to minimize the voltage drop at industrial high current densities. In the continuous 120 h electrolysis process, rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited almost 100% FENH3 and NH3 recovery efficiency, accompanied by an NH3 recovery rate of ~1.8 mmol h−1 (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Table S5).

Even in neutral reverse osmosis wastewater, the nitrate concentration can still reach thousands of ppm. For simulated neutral wastewater, rCo(OH)2/CuNW still maintained almost 100% FENH3 across a wide range of nitrate concentrations, achieving a maximum NH3 yield of 4.96 mmol h−1 cm−2 (Fig. 2d). Inspired by protective groups in organic synthesis and previous studies on organic solvent-mediated electrosynthesis, active *NH2OH, serving as an important intermediate during the NIRR process, could be captured to form oxime in the presence of ketone30. To broaden the applicability of the three-step synergistic mechanism under different operating conditions, a decoupled two-step strategy was adopted for NH2OH synthesis and subsequent oximation in a neutral medium. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) and 1H NMR spectra confirmed the successful conversion of cyclopentanone to cyclopentanone oxime during the cyclopentanone-mediated NIRR process (Suppl. Fig. S37 and Fig. 2f). By optimizing the hydrolysis of cyclopentanone oxime for efficient NH2OH release, rCo(OH)2/CuNW achieved a maximum NH2OH yield of 29.38 mg h−1 cm−2 (Suppl. Figs. S38, 39). A satisfactory FENH2OH of 83.48% was obtained at −0.3 V vs. RHE, a value comparable to those of most recently reported electrocatalysts (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Table S6). Meanwhile, the consecutive electrolysis cycle test further confirmed the stable NH2OH yield and FENH2OH, as determined by various quantitative methods (Fig. 2h and Suppl. Fig. S40). Furthermore, the NIRR-coupled cascade oximation strategy can also be extended to the synthesis of various oximes from the corresponding ketones, demonstrating its broad generality and promising commercial prospects (Suppl. Fig. S41).

Interfacial microenvironment and reaction kinetics

The origin of NIRR activity was further demonstrated by comprehensive analysis of interfacial microenvironment and reaction kinetics. The required *H in NO3− deoxygenation and hydrogenation processes comes from the adsorbed H2O on catalyst surface, where interfacial transport and activation are regulated by the hydrogen-bond network. Specifically, the Raman spectra displayed the O-H stretching vibration of interfacial H2O, which could be deconvoluted into three fitting peaks assigned to 4-coordinated hydrogen-bond water (4HB-H2O), 2-coordinated hydrogen-bond water (2HB-H2O), and alkali metal-ionized water (K+-H2O)31. The proportion and position of three peaks were highly dependent on the applied potential, revealing that the detected signal originated from a few water layers close to catalyst surface (Fig. 3a). The potential-dependent Stark shift in the position of the O-H stretching vibration reflects the sensitivity of the interfacial H2O structure to the interfacial electric field. Accordingly, the Stark effect is defined as the O-H stretching vibration position as a function of the applied potential. Under an external electric field, the donated electrons fill the antibonding orbital of O-H bond, thus weakening the bond energy and causing a red shift in the three fitting peaks (Suppl. Fig. S42)32. The proportion of 4HB-H2O and 2HB-H2O gradually decreased with the negative shift of potential, while that of K+-H2O increased (Fig. 3b, c). This evolution, combined with the observed red shift, indicated the transport from bulk water (4HB-H2O and 2HB-H2O) to interfacial water (K+-H2O) within the flexible hydrogen-bond network. Notably, rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited a more significant K+-H2O Stark effect (76.1 cm−1/ V) compared to Cu-Co(OH)2 (56.2 cm−1/ V), implying a closer distance between its Helmholtz plane and H atoms of interfacial H2O33. Previous studies confirmed that K+-H2O demonstrated lower activation energy for H2O dissociation compared to 2HB-H2O and 4HB-H2O34. As the potential shifted negatively, the proportion of K+-H2O on rCo(OH)2/CuNW increased at a progressively faster rate than that on Cu-Co(OH)2, thereby facilitating *H generation and subsequent NO3− hydrogenation. Furthermore, rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited a larger double-layer capacitance (Cdl, 32.92 mF cm−2) than Cu-Co(OH)2 (19.64 mF cm−2), indicating its higher intrinsic activity (Suppl. Fig. S43-44). The electrochemical surface area (ECSA) and ECSA-normalized NH3 yield of rCo(OH)2/CuNW indicated that the favorable NIRR performance lies not only in the number of active sites, but also in the activity of each site (Suppl. Fig. S45).

As an indicator for assessing reaction kinetics, the Tafel slope close to 120 mV dec−1 suggested that the first electron transfer might be the rate-determining step (RDS) in H2O dissociation and NIRR process (Suppl. Fig. S46)35. The lower Tafel slope of rCo(OH)2/CuNW confirmed its kinetic advantages in the Volmer step and NO3− deoxygenation step36. Taking into account the competitive HER process, the Bode phase plots were employed to probe the kinetic behavior of *H during the NIRR process. The low and middle frequency ranges of phase angle peak corresponded to the Volmer and Heyrovsky steps of HER process, and the intensity of phase angle peak revealed the charge transfer rate at electrode-electrolyte interface37. As the potential shifted negatively, the phase angle peak gradually weakened and shifted towards middle frequency range, indicating a transition in the dominant process from *H generation (Volmer step) to *H consumption (*NOx hydrogenation step) (Fig. 3d and Suppl. Fig. S47). Meanwhile, rCo(OH)2/CuNW displayed a phase angle peak with lower frequency and weaker intensity at −0.20 V vs. RHE compared to Cu-Co(OH)2, indicating its stronger suppression of Heyrovsky step and faster NIRR reaction kinetics (Fig. 3e)38. Furthermore, the water dissociation rate was evaluated using H/D kinetic isotope effect (KIE), which was obtained from the ratio of NH3 yield in H2O and D2O-based electrolytes. Although FENH3 maintained its stability in deuterated electrolyte, there was a decrease in current density and NH3 yield due to the slower kinetics of D2O dissociation (Fig. 3f, g). All catalysts displayed KIE values exceeding 1, implying that the proton-coupled transfer step was involved in the RDS of NIRR process39. A smaller KIE value signifies faster H2O dissociation kinetics and proton transfer rate. Specifically, rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited a lower KIE value (1.25) than that of Cu-Co(OH)2 (1.48), indicating that its accelerated Volmer step and *H transfer alleviated the kinetic barrier associated with the RDS (Suppl. Fig. S48).

In-situ attenuated total reflectance-surface enhanced infrared absorption spectra (ATR-SEIRAS) combined with DEMS were employed to ascertain the reaction pathway and related intermediates of the NIRR process. In the potential range from +0.2 to −0.8 V vs. RHE, rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited the emergence and subsequent intensification of seven absorption bands. Specifically, the downward absorption band at 1253 cm−1 was assigned to the consumption of NO2−, while the upward absorption bands at 1600~1700 cm−1 and 3450 cm−1 corresponded to *NO, *H2O bending vibration, and *H2O stretching vibration24,31. The subsequent hydrogenation process was confirmed by the N–O stretching vibration in NH2OH (1158 cm−1) and *NH2 wagging mode (1378 cm−1)40. Notably, the absorption bands of NH4+ (1476 cm−1) and *NH2OH (2875 cm−1) gradually intensified with the negative shift of potential, confirming the generation of ammonia with high selectivity during the NIRR process (Fig. 3h)41,42. Furthermore, in-situ DEMS results exhibited six distinct m/z signals (16, 17, 30, 31, 33, and 46) during the LSV measurement cycles, which corresponded to intermediate products of NH2, NH3, NO, HNO, NH2OH, and NO2 (Fig. 3i)17.

OH species cycle and reverse hydrogen spillover

To elucidate the interaction between active sites and electrolyte composition during the NIRR process, the OH species cycle mechanism mediated through dynamic reconstruction was proposed and verified. Due to the redox reaction (Eq. 1), the restructured β-Co(OH)2 phase and generated NO2− were detected when rCo/CuNW was immersed in the electrolyte containing NO3− (Suppl. Fig. S49). Subsequently, the dehydroxylation process from Co-OH to [CoO6] units and further to Co was confirmed by in-situ Raman spectra in 1.0 M KOH (Fig. 4a), which corresponded to the Volmer step (Eq. 2) and electroreduction process (Eq. 3)43. With the addition of small amounts of NO3− and application of negative potential, the characteristic Raman peaks of Co(OH)2 appeared and disappeared intermittently, indicating the cyclic and dynamic characteristics of the above three reconstruction reactions (Suppl. Fig. S50). Thus, the OH species cycle during the NIRR process consisted of the following steps: (1) the involvement of H2O in the redox reaction from Co to Co(OH)2; (2) the generation of *H in the Volmer step accompanied by the transformation from Co(OH)2 to CoOx; (3) the completed dehydroxylation process of adsorbed hydroxyl species (OHad) in the electroreduction process from CoOx to Co. Furthermore, the proton exchangeability and OH species cycle mechanism during the dynamic reconstruction process were positively and inversely verified by H/D isotope labeling experiments. Specifically, rCo(OD)2/CuNW was deuterated via the pre-activation and reconstruction in D2O-based electrolyte, and then used for NIRR tests in H2O-based electrolyte. Due to the dehydroxylation process of rCo(OD)2/CuNW, the D2O and H2O Raman peaks located at 2525.7 cm−1 and 3417.2 cm−1 were detected simultaneously, demonstrating the OH species evolution from lattice OD to D2O (Suppl. Fig. S51)20. Meanwhile, in-situ DEMS displayed the mass-to-charge ratio signals (m/z) of 19 and 20 assigned to NHxD(3−x) and HxD(2−x)O in rCo(OD)2/CuNW, indicating that the lattice OD of catalyst was eventually used for ammonia synthesis through the OH species cycle (Suppl. Fig. S52). The 1H NMR spectra of rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited the characteristic peaks of ND4+ and NH4+ after NIRR test in D2O-based electrolyte, which further provided positive verification for the involvement of OH species cycle during the NIRR process (Suppl. Fig. S53)44.

To further investigate the contribution of OH species cycle to *H supply (Eq. 2) during the NIRR process, a Co(OH)2-based Volmer step model was constructed for mechanism studies (Suppl. Fig. S54). Initially, interfacial H2O is adsorbed on the surface by dangling one hydrogen atom toward catalyst surface under the action of hydrogen-bond interaction. Subsequently, protons in OHad are removed by the OH− generated through H2O dissociation, accompanied by the transformation from Co(OH)2 to CoOx. Meanwhile, the generated Co sites (not pure metallic Co) during the dehydroxylation process obtain the *H from the dangling hydrogen atom in interfacial H2O15. As a crucial transition state, CoOx is closely associated with reaction kinetics of Volmer step. Specifically, rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited a higher Co-O/Co-OH ratio in O 1 s spectra, demonstrating the faster *H generation rate during the Volmer step (Suppl. Fig. S55). As evidenced by the lower valence state and applied potential required for the dehydroxylation process, rCo(OH)2/CuNW possessed more activated dynamic sites and faster OH species cycle compared to Cu-Co(OH)2 (Fig. 1g and Fig. 4a). The accelerated OH species cycle enabled the regeneration from CoOx sites to Co and then to Co(OH)2 sites, ensuring more available sites for H2O activation and *H adsorption during the Volmer step. The electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra exhibited the strongest DMPO-H signal for rCo(OH)2/CuNW in the absence of NO3−, implying that the accelerated OH species cycle positively influenced *H generation rate (Fig. 4b). With the addition of NO3−, rCo(OH)2/CuNW also displayed the strongest DMPO-OH signal among all catalysts containing Co(OH)2 phase, which was attributed to the faster dehydroxylation process within OH species cycle (Fig. 4c). In the electroreductive hydrogenation system, the formation of DMPO-OH might be explained by the Forrester-Hepburn mechanism, which involved the nucleophilic attack by active H2O on DMPO, coupled with the dehydroxylation process (Co2+ → Co0) (Suppl. Fig. S56). Although the accelerated OH species cycle and Volmer step produced abundant *H, rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited a negligible DMPO-H signal. This is because the produced *H at Co(OH)2 sites was rapidly transferred to Cu sites for *NOx hydrogenation, thus avoiding the dimerization of over-covered *H18.

Given that the above tandem catalysis was closely correlated with *H generation, transfer, and adsorption/desorption behaviors, the reverse hydrogen spillover mechanism was proposed and verified by various experiments19,45. In-situ shell-isolated nanoparticle enhanced Raman spectra (SHINERS) investigated the evolution of active species and adsorbed intermediates on catalyst surface, providing the direct evidence for reverse hydrogen spillover and tandem catalysis between dual sites. As the potential shifted negatively from open circuit potential (OCP) to −0.8 V vs. RHE, the A2u (~512 cm−1) and Eg modes (560~645 cm−1) of Co-OH gradually weakened and transformed into the A1g mode (~660 cm−1) of [CoO6] units (Fig. 4d)21,37. In addition to the evolution from Co-OH to [CoO6] units, the attenuation of [CoO6] units at more negative potentials further confirmed the dehydroxylation process within OH species cycle. Notably, rCo(OH)2/CuNW displayed a stronger peak assigned to the Cu-H bond (~2120 cm−1) at lower potentials compared to Cu-Co(OH)2, demonstrating its enhanced reverse hydrogen spillover effect from Co(OH)2 sites to Cu sites (Fig. 4e)16,46. Due to more available Cu sites and stronger anchoring towards *NOx in rCo(OH)2/CuNW, the peak of Cu-N bond (~555 cm−1) emerged at −0.3 V vs. RHE and intensified with the negative shift of potential, ensuring effective utilization of transferred *H in the deoxygenation and hydrogenation steps of *NOx47. With the consumption of NO3−, there was the attenuation and intensification of peaks at ~732, 1045, and 1380 cm−1, corresponding to the N–O bending mode, ν1, and ν3 modes of NO3−, respectively48. As the potential shifted negatively to −0.2 V vs. RHE, the H-O-H bending mode (~1619 cm−1) appeared, further confirming the tandem catalysis between H2O dissociation and NO3− reduction18. Meanwhile, the adsorbed intermediates including *NO2 (~1216 cm−1), *HNO (~1548 cm−1), *NH2O (1325 cm−1), and *NH2 (~1686 cm−1) successively emerged at more negative potentials, confirming the deoxygenation and hydrogenation steps of the NIRR process47. Based on the combined results of in-situ ATR-SEIRAS, DEMS, and SHINERS, the NIRR process of rCo(OH)2/CuNW was speculated to follow the pathway of *NO3→*NO2→*NO → *HNO → *NH2O→*NH2OH→*NH2→*NH3.

To investigate *H adsorption behavior during the reverse hydrogen spillover process, the Nyquist plots and fitting parameters were obtained using a double-parallel equivalent circuit model (Suppl. Fig. S57 and Suppl. Table S7). As the second parallel component, Cφ and R2 represented *H adsorption capacitance and *H adsorption resistance, respectively49. Specifically, the *H adsorption charge (QH), calculated by integrating Cφ at different applied potentials, was employed to quantify the adsorbed *H amount on catalyst surface50. The QH of rCo(OH)2/CuNW reached up to 457.8 μC, much higher than that of Cu-Co(OH)2 (148.6 μC), suggesting its enhanced *H transfer from Co(OH)2 sites to Cu sites of Cu-O-Co bridging motifs under the action of reverse hydrogen spillover (Fig. 4f). To assess the kinetic differences in *H adsorption behavior, the Tafel slope was calculated by plotting logR2 as a function of applied potential51. The Tafel slope of rCo(OH)2/CuNW (75.0 mV dec−1) was smaller than that of Cu-Co(OH)2 (102.8 mV dec−1), further confirming that the faster OH species cycle contributed to *H adsorption kinetics (Fig. 4g). Furthermore, the *H desorption kinetics was explored by plotting the hydrogen desorption peak position in cyclic voltammograms (CV) curves as a function of scan rates (Fig. 4h and Suppl. Fig. S58)52. The rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited a lower slope compared to Cu-Co(OH)2, indicating its faster *H desorption kinetics for the promotion of reverse hydrogen spillover process (Fig. 4i).

AIMD simulations and DFT calculations

To gain atomic-scale insight into the three-step synergistic mechanism, the AIMD and DFT were performed to investigate the kinetic and thermodynamic properties of three key stages (interfacial H2O transport, *H generation, and *NOx hydrogenation) during the NIRR process. Unlike the Cu doping effect in Cu-Co(OH)2, the dominant facet of Cu nanowires was stepped Cu(110) in rCo(OH)2/CuNW, as confirmed by electrochemical OH− adsorption measurements (Suppl. Fig. S59). Given this, two theoretical models were constructed and simplified as Cu-Co(OH)2 and Co(OH)2/Cu, corresponding to a Cu-doped hexagonal Co(OH)2 model and a heterostructure model consisting of Co(OH)2 supported on Cu(110) (Suppl. Fig. S60 and Supplementary Data 1). According to the previous analysis, the dual‑site catalysts present Cu and Co(OH)2 sites, accompanied by the exposure of unsaturated Co sites during the Co(OH)2‑based Volmer step. The distinct functions and thermodynamic behaviors of these three types of active sites underlie the interactions among interfacial microenvironment regulation, OH species cycle, and reverse hydrogen spillover (Suppl. Fig. S61-62). Specifically, Co(OH)2 sites are responsible for hydrogen-bond network reshaping, interfacial H2O transport and activation. Subsequently, the unsaturated Co sites generated during dynamic reconstruction serve as a bridge linking the Volmer step and reverse hydrogen spillover process, accepting *H produced from H2O activation at Co(OH)2 sites and transferring it to Cu sites of Cu-O-Co bridging motifs. Ultimately, Cu sites can effectively anchor NOx intermediates and undergo deoxygenation and hydrogenation reactions with the migrated *H for the synthesis of NH3.

The role of interfacial microenvironment within the Helmholtz plane in regulating interfacial H2O transport remains ambiguous during the NIRR process. In general, H2O molecules can serve as up to two hydrogen acceptors and two hydrogen donors, thus coupling to form the hydrogen-bond network. The smaller average number of hydrogen bonds per interfacial H₂O molecule implies more disordered hydrogen-bond network, thereby facilitating H2O transport from bulk to interface53. The representative snapshots and hydrogen-bond distribution indicated that the average number of hydrogen bonds for two models followed the order of Co(OH)2/Cu (2.1) < Cu-Co(OH)2 (3.9), with corresponding numbers of H2O molecules being 9 and 12 (Fig. 5a–c). Owing to the more exposed Co sites, the radial distribution functions of H and O atoms for Co(OH)2/Cu model indicated that polarized H2O molecules in O-down configuration preferred to dangle hydrogen atoms towards catalyst surface, which was conducive to the proton transport and Volmer step kinetics (Fig. 5a, b)54. These findings confirmed that abundant dehydroxylation sites combined with stepped Cu(110) nanowires in rCo(OH)2/CuNW could regulate the interfacial microenvironment by reshaping the hydrogen-bond network, thereby ensuring the timely delivery of interfacial H2O for Volmer step.

To verify the functional mechanism of OH species cycle and reverse hydrogen spillover, we calculated the related parameters for the steps of H2O adsorption, *H generation, and *H transfer. The alkaline HER process was simulated according to the previously established Co(OH)2-based Volmer step. The *H2O adsorption energy (∆G*H2O) of two models followed the order of Co(OH)2/Cu (−0.29 eV) < Cu-Co(OH)2 (−0.11 eV), further confirming that rCo(OH)2/CuNW enhanced the accessibility of interfacial H2O layer to active sites (Suppl. Fig. S63). As the potential-determining step (PDS) of H2O dissociation, the lower energy barrier (∆G*H+*OH, 0.12 eV) observed in Co(OH)2/Cu model indicated that interfacial H2O could be efficiently dissociated upon reaching the active sites, implying the adequate *H supply (Suppl. Fig. S64). Given that the excessive accumulation of *H tends to dimerize to form H₂, the reverse hydrogen spillover channel from Co(OH)2 sites to Cu sites should be constructed and optimized to improve the proton-utilization efficiency for *NOₓ hydrogenation49. That is because stepped Cu(110) nanowires exhibited advantages in anchoring *NOx, as confirmed through in-situ identification of Cu-N bond in rCo(OH)2/CuNW and favorable *NO3 adsorption energy (∆G*NO3, −1.53 eV) of stepped Cu(110) among different facets (Suppl. Fig. S65).

In the process of reverse hydrogen spillover on Co(OH)2/Cu model, one H2O molecule reacts with *H on outer Co site (site 1) and dissociates, thereby delivering one dangling hydrogen atom to inner Co site (site 2). After repeating the above steps once again with another H2O molecule, *H is finally transferred from inner Co site (site 2) to Cu site (site 3) of Cu-O-Co bridging motif. Following the overcoming of energy barrier (∆G*H+*OH, site 1, 0.12 eV) in the first Volmer step, the resulting *H can be immediately transferred to Cu sites for *NOₓ hydrogenation via two barrier-free H2O dissociation processes, effectively suppressing *H dimerization (Fig. 5d). For Cu-Co(OH)2 model, after overcoming the energy barrier of the first Volmer step (ΔG*H+*OH, site 1, 0.38 eV), the generated *H must overcome a higher thermodynamic barrier (ΔG*H+*OH, site 2, 0.47 eV) to enable the second H2O dissociation, which is a prerequisite for smooth *H migration to Cu site of the Cu-O-Co bridging motifs (Fig. 5e). Hence, the reverse hydrogen spillover process on Cu-Co(OH)2 model was thermodynamically unfavorable, while its unstable Cu-O-Co bridging motifs at dynamic interface serve as an additional detrimental factor that further suppresses the reverse hydrogen spillover efficiency.

Benefiting from the highly exposed high-index edge facets in rCo(OH)2/CuNW, *H is able to successfully cross single-crystal Co(OH)2 lateral faces and ultimately migrate to Cu nanowire substrate. Furthermore, the *H adsorption energy (ΔG*H) of Co(OH)2/Cu model (−0.17 eV) was nearly thermoneutral, implying the favorable *H adsorption/desorption balance for the reverse hydrogen spillover from Co sites to Cu sites (Fig. 5f). Thermodynamically, the spontaneous *H transfer can effectively match the *H generation rate of enhanced Volmer step, thereby establishing a robust proton reservoir to drive the subsequent *NOx hydrogenation process.

To reveal the advantages of three-step synergistic mechanism, we calculated the free energy diagrams and optimized structural models based on the inferred NIRR pathway. Initially, the Co(OH)2/Cu model exhibited stronger NO3− adsorption compared to Cu-Co(OH)2 model, indicating its stronger d–p orbital hybridization between Cu sites and reactant. Despite the mostly exothermic steps in the entire proton-coupled electron transfer process, the thermodynamic barriers in *NO hydrogenation and *NH3 desorption still need to be overcome. Compared to the higher energy barrier of PDS (i.e., from *NO to *NOH) on Cu-Co(OH)2 model (0.24 eV), the significantly reduced barrier for this step observed in Co(OH)2/Cu model (0.09 eV) was comparable to that of PDS (∆G*H+*OH, site 1, 0.12 eV) in the reverse hydrogen spillover process, implying matched thermodynamic driving force and well-matched interfacial synergy between *H supply and *H utilization (Fig. 5g). In addition, the overall reaction pathway on Co(OH)2/Cu model was smoother than that on Cu-Co(OH)2 model, which explains why the enhanced NIRR activity of rCo(OH)2/CuNW originates from exposed high-index edge facets, stable Cu-O-Co bridging motifs, and well-matched interfacial synergy (Suppl. Fig. S66). These findings further confirmed that this integrated three-step synergistic mechanism enhanced the interaction among interfacial H2O transport, *H generation, and *NOx hydrogenation. Notably, these DFT calculations and AIMD simulations adopted simplified ideal models without considering complex interfacial interactions and solvent effects, thereby providing only qualitative theoretical insights.

Zinc-nitrate battery performance

As an environmentally-friendly energy device, zinc-nitrate (Zn-NO3−) battery, consisting of CF-derived catalysts as the cathode and Zn plate as the anode, was designed to simultaneously produce electricity and convert nitrate from wastewater into value-added ammonia (Fig. 6a)55–57. The open circuit voltage (OCV) of Zn-NO3− batteries based on rCo(OH)2/CuNW and Cu-Co(OH)2 reached 1.16 V and 1.12 V respectively, close to the calculated theoretical voltage (Fig. 6b). In the process of discharge of Zn-NO3− battery, electrons are released from anode and transferred to cathode through the external circuit, accompanied by electricity generation and NO3− reduction58,59. Notably, the Zn-NO3− battery based on rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited a peak power density of 57.6 mW cm−2, which is competitive with those of previously reported Zn-NO3− batteries (Fig. 6c and Suppl. Table S8). Operating under galvanostatic discharge at 240 mA cm−2, rCo(OH)2/CuNW-assembled Zn-NO3− battery achieved a maximum NH3 yield of 17.91 mg h−1 cm−2 with FENH3 of 94.14% (Fig. 6d and Suppl. Fig. S67). The Zn-NO3− battery based on rCo(OH)2/CuNW exhibited a smaller voltage drop across a wide range of current densities, indicating its high rate capability (Fig. 6e)60. Furthermore, the charge and discharge curves revealed the feasibility of rechargeable Zn-NO3− battery (Fig. 6f). The galvanostatic discharge–charge curve at 2 mA cm−2 demonstrated the stable voltage output and low discharge-charge voltage gap, further confirming the competitive energy storage and conversion capacity of Zn-NO3− battery based on rCo(OH)2/CuNW (Fig. 6g). To prove the practicability, two series-connected Zn-NO3− batteries successfully powered a LED display with scrolling marquee (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Video 1).

Discussion

In summary, we designed rCo(OH)2/CuNW heterostructure as a model catalyst to investigate the microenvironment regulation, structural evolution, and *H transfer behavior at dynamic catalytic interface during the NIRR process. In-situ characterization and theoretical calculation systematically elucidated the interaction among three key stages of NIRR process: (1) the weak hydrogen-bond network facilitated H2O transport from bulk to interface; (2) the OH species cycle mediated by dynamic reconstruction accelerated *H generation and supply in the Volmer step; (3) the reverse hydrogen spillover between dual sites enhanced the *H transfer kinetics, thereby promoting *NOx hydrogenation while inhibiting the HER process. Benefiting from this integrated three-step synergistic mechanism, rCo(OH)2/CuNW achieved almost 100% FENH3 in alkaline and neutral electrolyte, and the cyclopentanone-mediated NIRR process exhibited a satisfactory FENH2OH (83.48%) with a maximum NH2OH yield of 29.38 mg h−1 cm−2. Notably, the simultaneous NH3 synthesis-recovery system demonstrated well-maintained stability with almost 100% FENH3 and NH3 recovery efficiency, accompanied by an NH3 recovery rate of ~1.8 mmol h−1. Additionally, the assembled zinc-nitrate battery exhibited a high peak power density (57.6 mW cm−2) and NH3 yield (17.91 mg h−1 cm−2), accomplishing three goals with one action for nitrate removal, ammonia synthesis, and electricity supply. Given the exploration of dynamic catalytic interface, we believe that this synergistic mechanism study will provide guidance for the rational design of advanced catalysts, thus facilitating the technological development aimed at green synthesis for NH3 and NH2OH.

Methods

Chemicals

Cobaltous nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O, 99.99%), urea (CH4N2O, 99.95%), cyclopentanone (C5H8O, 99%), potassium nitrite (KNO2, 99%), hydroxylamine sulfate (H6N2O2·H2SO4, 99%), and zinc acetate (C4H6O4Zn, 99.99%) were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Ammonium persulfate ((NH4)2S2O8, 99.99%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 98%), potassium hydroxide (KOH), potassium nitrate (KNO3, 99%), and ammonium chloride (NH4Cl, 99.95%) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 38 wt%), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98 wt%), acetone (C3H6O, 99.5%), and ethanol (EtOH, 99.5%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Preparation of Cu(OH)2NW

Initially, Cu foam (CF) was pre-treated by sequential ultrasonic cleaning in dilute hydrochloric acid, acetone, and ethanol, each for several minutes. In a typical procedure, 296 mg of (NH4)2S2O8 was dissolved in 2.5 M aqueous NaOH solution, into which a piece of pre-treated CF (1 cm²) was then immersed for 10 min. Finally, the resulting blue sample was cleaned three times using deionized water and then placed in a vacuum oven overnight to obtain Cu(OH)2NW.

Preparation of CoCH/Cu2O

3 mmol Co(NO3)2·6H2O and 45 mmol CH4N2O were dissolved in 30 mL deionized water and stirred under room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) for 30 min. The above solution was transferred into Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave with Cu(OH)2NW, and placed in the oven at 90 °C for 4 h. After natural cooling, the light purple sample was cleaned three times using deionized water and then placed in vacuum oven overnight to obtain CoCH/Cu2O.

Preparation of rCo(OH)2/CuNW

The CoCH/Cu2O in crucible was placed in the center of quartz tube and carried out at 450 °C for 2 h with a heating rate of 5 °C /min under H2/Ar (10%) atmosphere. After naturally cooling to room temperature (25 ± 2 °C), the black sample was taken out and marked as rCo/CuNW. The rCo(OH)2/CuNW was obtained by the reconstruction of rCo/CuNW as working electrode using cyclic voltammetry (CV) and chronoamperometry (CA) for 30 min, respectively. The mass loading of the catalyst was approximately 1 mg cm−2.

Preparation of Cu-Co(OH)2

The Cu-Co(OH)2 was obtained by the direct reconstruction of CoCH/Cu2O.

Preparation of rCuNW

The Cu(OH)2NW was carried out the same thermal reduction under H2/Ar (10%) atmosphere and subsequently reconstructed to obtain rCuNW.

Preparation of rCo(OH)2@CC

Initially, the carbon cloth (CC) was substituted for Cu(OH)2NW to carry out the same hydrothermal experiment as CoCH/Cu2O. Subsequently, the above precursor was executed the same thermal reduction under H2/Ar (10%) atmosphere and reconstructed to obtain rCo(OH)2@CC.

Characterization

The crystal structures of all catalysts were analyzed using a Rigaku MINIFLEX600 with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. GIXRD patterns were recorded on a Bruker D8 Discover diffractometer by adjusting the incident angle to 1°. The microstructures of all catalysts were characterized using SEM (JSM − 7800 F PRIME) and HAADF − STEM (FEI Tecnai G2 F20). The chemical compositions and valence states of all catalysts were analyzed by XPS (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha). XAFS measurements were performed on a Table XAFS-500 (Specreation Instruments Co., Ltd., China). NMR spectra were recorded on an Avance Ⅲ 500 MHz NMR spectrometer. UV-vis spectra were collected on a T700B UV-vis spectrophotometer (Persee/PG Scientific). GC‒MS analysis was conducted on an Agilent 5977 C mass spectrometer equipped with an Agilent Technologies 8860 GC system.

In-situ experiments

In-situ EPR measurements were performed on an EMXplus-9/12 spectrometer with DMPO serving as the spin trap for *H and *OH. The catholyte was bubbled with high-purity Ar gas (> 99.999%) for 30 min and added in DMPO before electrolysis in a three-electrode system. After 5 min electrolysis at −0.4 V vs. RHE, the catholyte was taken out for EPR detection.

In-situ ATR-SEIRAS was recorded on a Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer equipped with an MCT detector cooled by liquid nitrogen. The powder catalyst was exfoliated from the self-supporting electrode and mixed with Nafion solution to form an electrocatalyst ink. The as-prepared ink was then dropped onto the Au-deposited Si prism, which functioned as the working electrode after drying. Meanwhile, Hg/HgO electrode and Pt wire served as the reference electrode and counter electrode, respectively. The electrolysis was conducted across a potential window ranging from + 0.2 to −0.8 V vs. RHE.

In-situ SHINERS was recorded on a LabRAM HR Evolution Raman spectrometer (HORIBA Jobin Yvon) with an excitation wavelength of 532 nm. The working electrode was fabricated by modifying a self-supporting electrode with shell-isolated nanoparticles (50 nm Au core; 5–10 nm SiO2 shell). Meanwhile, Hg/HgO electrode and Pt wire served as the reference electrode and counter electrode, respectively. Electrochemical measurements and Raman spectral acquisition were performed simultaneously to monitor the reaction process in real time. The electrolysis was conducted across a potential window ranging from + 0.2 to −0.8 V vs. RHE.

In-situ DEMS tests were performed to detect reaction intermediates on a QAS100 (Shanghai LingLu Co., Ltd). In an electrochemical cell (50 mL), CF-derived catalysts, Hg/HgO electrode, and Pt wire served as the working electrode, reference electrode, and counter electrode, respectively (Suppl. Fig. S52). The rCo(OD)2/CuNW was deuterated via the pre-activation and reconstruction in D2O-based electrolyte (Energy Chemical, 99.8% D), and then used for NIRR tests in H2O-based electrolyte (1.0 M KOH with 0.1 M KNO3), which was bubbled with high-purity Ar gas (> 99.999%) for 30 min before the DEMS measurement. The volatile products were transported through a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane via a vacuum pump to the mass spectrometer. The L-DP01 PTFE membrane (pore size: 0.02 μm; prosity: 50%) was purchased from LingLu instrument. The six cycles of LSV test were conducted at 5 mV s−1 after the baseline of mass signals became smooth and steady, and the mass-to-charge (m/z) signals were collected for analysis.

Electrochemical OH− adsorption measurements

Before electrochemical OH− adsorption measurement, all electrodes were reduced for 5 min at −0.6 V vs. RHE. Subsequently, the CV was performed in Ar-saturated 1.0 M KOH at a scan rate of 10 mV s−1. The potential ranged from −0.025 to +0.475 V vs. RHE for CV measurement. The facet proportion of Cu was calculated by normalizing the OH− adsorption charge of each facet.

Electrochemical nitrate reduction measurements

Electrochemical nitrate reduction reaction (NIRR) to ammonia experiments were performed under ambient conditions (room temperature: 25 ± 2 °C) in an H-type cell (80 mL in volume), with 40 mL of electrolyte (1.0 M KOH with 0.1 M KNO3) in both the cathode and anode compartments. Specifically, a standard three-electrode system, with the compartments separated by a Nafion 117 proton exchange membrane (thickness: 183 μm), was connected to an electrochemical workstation (CHI 1140D) via the working electrode, Hg/HgO reference electrode (1.0 M KOH), and platinum (Pt) counter electrode. The Nafion 117 membrane was pretreated by heating in a 5 wt% H2O2 aqueous solution, 0.5 M H2SO4, and DI at 80 °C for 1 h, respectively. All electrolyte solutions were freshly prepared prior to use and stored at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) in the dark. More importantly, the electrolyte was purged with high-purity Ar gas (>99.999%) for at least 30 min before electrolysis. The prepared working electrode was pre-activated in Ar-saturated electrolyte (1.0 M KOH with 0.1 M KNO3) by CV and CA measurements, respectively. The linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1 from + 0.2 to −0.8 V vs. RHE, with or without 50% iR compensation. To determine the NH3 yield and FENH3, the NIRR test was carried out using CA at various potentials for 0.5 h with a stirring rate of 200 rpm. All current densities were normalized to the geometric surface area of the working electrode, unless otherwise noted. All potentials reported versus the Hg/HgO reference electrode were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale according to the following Nernst equation: E (vs. RHE) = E (vs. Hg/HgO) + 0.059 × pH + 0.098. When pH =14, the difference is 0.924 V. The actual pH of as-prepared electrolyte was determined to be 13.9 ± 0.1 (close to 14) via three independent measurements using a calibrated pH meter. Before each test, the reference electrode was calibrated using fresh Hg/HgO as the counter electrode. The difference was confirmed to be lower than 10 mV.

The cyclopentanone-mediated NIRR process was executed in H-type cell, containing 45 mL electrolyte (0.5 M K2SO4 with 0.1 M KNO3) at cathode and anode, respectively. The two-step oximation strategy was realized by adding ketone to the electrolyte after galvanostatic electrolysis. To evaluate the performance of NH2OH synthesis, 1 mL of electrolyte after oximation was taken, and pH was adjusted by adding 10 M H2SO4 overnight to hydrolyze the oxime and form hydroxylamine sulfate.

Simultaneous NH3 synthesis-recovery experiment

The simultaneous ammonia synthesis-recovery system integrated an MEA electrolyzer and an NH3 recovery reactor. Specifically, 1 L of simulated wastewater (1.0 M KOH with 0.1 M KNO3.) was circulated through cathode and anode of MEA electrolyzer respectively via a peristaltic pump at a flow rate of 60 mL min–1. The MEA-based alkaline anion exchange membrane (AEM) electrolyzer consisted of an anode (NiFeOx, 1.0 cm2), a cathode (rCo(OH)2/CuNW, 1.0 cm2), and an AEM (Versogen Piperlon-A60-HCO3). The NH3 recovery reactor was assembled using multiple bundles of hollow gas-permeable polypropylene fibers within a sealed chamber, with both ends secured using epoxy resin adhesive. Meanwhile, the circulating 1.2 M H2SO4 (500 mL, 60 mL min–1) in the chamber served as an acid trap, enabling the NH3 diffusion separation from catholyte and subsequent enrichment in the acid trap when catholyte passes through the polypropylene fiber micropores. During the electrolysis process, the catholyte was refreshed with an interval of every 36 h.

Quantification of ammonia

The indophenol blue colorimetric method was employed to determine the concentration of NH3 produced after electrolysis. Specifically, 2 mL of diluted catholyte was taken and sequentially mixed with 2 mL of 1.0 M NaOH solution (containing 5 wt% salicylic acid and 5 wt% sodium citrate), 1 mL of 0.05 M NaClO solution, and 0.2 mL of 1 wt% sodium nitroprusside solution. After thorough mixing, the resulting solution was incubated in the dark environment for 2 h, and its absorbance was measured at 655 nm on a UV−vis spectrophotometer. A calibration curve was established using a series of NH3 standard solutions with gradient concentrations, which was adopted to calculate the NH3 concentration in the electrolyte.

Quantification of hydroxylamine

Two different colorimetric methods were employed to determine the concentration of NH2OH produced after electrolysis.

1,10-phenanthroline colorimetric method: The catholyte was taken and treated with various volumes of 10 M H2SO4 solution to determine the optimal hydrolysis conditions. Subsequently, 3 mL of diluted catholyte after hydrolysis treatment was sequentially mixed with 100 μL of acetate buffer, 100 μL of 4 mM ammonium ferric sulfate aqueous solution, and 100 μL of 10 mM 1,10-phenanthroline ethanolic solution. After thorough mixing, the resulting solution was incubated in the dark environment for 1 h, and its absorbance was measured at 510 nm on a UV-vis spectrophotometer. A calibration curve was established using a series of NH2OH standard solutions with gradient concentrations, which was adopted to calculate the NH2OH concentration in the electrolyte.

8-hydroxyquinoline colorimetric method: The catholyte was taken and treated with various volumes of 10 M H2SO4 solution to determine the optimal hydrolysis conditions. Subsequently, 1 mL of diluted catholyte after hydrolysis treatment was sequentially mixed with 1 mL of 0.05 M phosphate buffer (pH = 6.8), 0.8 mL deionized water, 0.2 mL of 12 wt% trichloroacetic acid solution, 1 mL of 1 wt% 8-hydroxyquinoline ethanolic solution, and 1 mL of 1 M sodium carbonate solution. After thorough mixing, the resulting solution was heated for 1 min at 100 °C in a water bath under dark conditions, and its absorbance was measured at 705 nm on a UV-vis spectrophotometer. A calibration curve was established using a series of NH2OH standard solutions with gradient concentrations, which was adopted to calculate the NH2OH concentration in the electrolyte.

Quantification of nitrite

The Griess colorimetric method was employed to determine the concentration of NO2− produced after electrolysis. The chromogenic reagent consisted of N-(1-naphthyl)ethyl-enediamine dihydrochloride (0.2 g), sulfanilamide (4 g), H3PO4 (10 mL), and H2O (50 mL). Specifically, 2 mL of diluted catholyte was taken and mixed with 0.1 mL chromogenic reagent. After thorough mixing, the resulting solution was incubated in the dark environment for 20 min, and its absorbance was measured at 540 nm on a UV-vis spectrophotometer. A calibration curve was established using a series of NO2− standard solutions with gradient concentrations, which was adopted to calculate the NO2− concentration in the electrolyte.

Quantification of hydrazine

The Watt and Chrisp colorimetric method was employed to determine the concentration of N2H4 produced after electrolysis. The chromogenic reagent consisted of p-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde (5.99 g), HCl (30 mL), and ethanol (300 mL). Specifically, 2 mL of diluted catholyte was taken and mixed with 2 mL chromogenic reagent. After thorough mixing, the resulting solution was incubated in the dark environment for 20 min, and its absorbance was measured at 458 nm on a UV-vis spectrophotometer. A calibration curve was established using a series of N2H4 standard solutions with gradient concentrations, which was adopted to calculate the N2H4 concentration in the electrolyte.

1H NMR quantitative experiment

The 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) was executed to analyze ammonia yield. Specifically, the catholyte after CA test was taken out and adjusted to acidity with 4 M H2SO4 for further quantification by 1H NMR (500 MHz) with internal standard of maleic acid. The NH3 calibration curve was obtained as follows. Initially, a series of NH3 standard solutions with gradient concentrations was prepared as calibration standards. Subsequently, 5 mL of each standard solution and test sample were mixed with 1 mL of 4 M H2SO4 and 2.5 mg of maleic acid. Then, 50 μL of DMSO-d6 was added to 0.5 mL of the above mixture for NMR detection. Finally, the calibration curve was established based on the peak area ratio of NH4+ to maleic acid, as a positive correlation existed between the NH3 concentration and this area ratio.

Computational details

The Density functional theory (DFT) calculations and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) code. The projector augmented wave (PAW) method and Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional were employed to describe the ionic potential and the exchange correlation interactions. Van der Waals interaction (DFT-D3 method with Becke-Jonson damping) was incorporated. The plane-wave kinetic energy cutoff was set to 500 eV. A 3 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh was used for the Brillouin zone sampling in all calculations.

For the Co(OH)2/Cu slab models, the Cu substrate was cleaved along the (110) plane with a thickness of four atomic layers, and the supported Co(OH)2 was constructed as a nine-atomic-layer slab terminated by the (001) facet. For the Cu-Co(OH)2 slab models, a portion of Co atoms in the pristine Co(OH)2 (001) slab were randomly substituted with Cu atoms, resulting in an atomic ratio of Co to Cu close to 5:1. A vacuum region of 30 Å was added between adjacent slabs to eliminate interactions between periodic images. A dipole correction was applied along the z-direction perpendicular to the surface to eliminate the artificial polarization caused by the asymmetric slab structure. For solvation effects, the implicit solvation model implemented in VASPsol was employed to account for the solvent that was not explicitly included in the quantum chemical calculations. To simulate an aqueous solution environment, water was chosen as the implicit solvent with a dielectric constant ε = 78.4. For structure relaxation, the energy convergence criterion for electronic relaxation was set to 1 × 10−5 eV, and the ionic relaxation was performed until all forces were smaller than 0.01 eV Å−1.

The ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were employed to investigate the interfacial solution environment and the hydrogen bond distribution. A significant amount of lattice-matched water was introduced as the initial water structure above the Co(OH)2/Cu and Cu-Co(OH)2 surfaces to simulate the interfacial water environment. The initial interfacial system consisted of either a Co(OH)₂/Cu slab or a Cu-Co(OH)₂ slab, along with 120 water molecules at a density of ρ = 1.0 g cm‒3. The AIMD simulations were conducted in the canonical (NVT) ensemble, with the system temperature maintained at 300 K using a Nosé -Hoover thermostat. A time step of 0.5 fs was used to ensure an accurate description of hydrogen atom dynamics. The total simulation time was 10 ps, with the first 4 ps allocated for system equilibration and the subsequent 6 ps used for production sampling and data analysis. All hydrogen bonding data were statistically analyzed using custom Python scripts. Additionally, ten K+ counterions were added to compensate for the negative charge on the electrode surface.

The assembly of Zn-NO3− battery

An aqueous Zn-NO3– battery was assembled in an H-type cell containing 40 mL of catholyte (1.0 M KOH with 0.1 M KNO3) and 40 mL of anolyte (1.0 M KOH with 0.02 M (CH3COO)2Zn). Furthermore, CF-derived catalysts and Zn plate, serving as the cathode and anode of the Zn-NO3– battery, respectively, were separated by a Nafion 117 proton exchange membrane (thickness: 183 μm).
