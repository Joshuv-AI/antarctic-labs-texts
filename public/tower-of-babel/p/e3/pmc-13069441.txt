Incidence and costs of cervical spondylosis in urban China from 2013 to 2017: a nationwide population-based study
Authors: Lin Zhuo, Shuyang Li, Feifei Zhou, Shengfeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundCervical spondylosis (CS) is a degenerative disease with an increasing burden due to aging populations and lifestyle changes. Large-scale epidemiological studies are lacking in China, and early-stage cases are often overlooked. This study aimed to assess the incidence and cost of clinically recognized CS among adult people in China.MethodsThis population-based cohort study was conducted by using national-wide data from Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance and Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance between 2013 and 2017, covering approximately 300 million residents in 23 provincial regions in China. CS were identified by the primary diagnosis. The incidence and cost of these clinically recognized CS in urban China were calculated.FindingsA total of 2,928,103 patients with clinically diagnosed CS were identified in this study (60.58% females and 39.42% males). CS was most common in people aged 50–59 years old (25.1%). The overall crude incidence of CS in urban China in 2017 was 405.65 per 100,000 person-years. The crude incidence among females was higher than males. Incidence was highest in East China and lowest in North China. During the five-year period from 2013 to 2017, the overall annual costs of patients with CS increased from 145.17 million US dollars to 1064.17 million US dollars, and the estimated annual cost per-patient increased from 597.32 US dollars to 970.67 US dollars.InterpretationAs the population ages, China will face an ever-increasing burden from CS. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Prior to this study, cervical spondylosis (CS) was widely recognized as a common, age-related degenerative disorder contributing significantly to neck pain, radiculopathy, and myelopathy globally. Existing epidemiological evidence in China primarily stemmed from single-center, cross-sectional studies or surveys with limited regional scope and sample sizes. These studies suggested a high prevalence but could not provide robust, nationwide incidence data, particularly for clinically recognized cases within the healthcare system. Furthermore, while the economic burden of musculoskeletal disorders was acknowledged, comprehensive, large-scale analyses quantifying the direct medical costs attributable to CS management in China were scarce. The lack of population-based, longitudinal data from representative samples hindered a clear understanding of the disease's incidence trends, demographic disparities, and associated healthcare expenditures at a national level.

Added value of this study

This study provides the first large-scale, population-based epidemiological and economic assessment of clinically recognized cervical spondylosis in urban China, utilizing nationwide medical insurance claims data covering approximately 300 million residents across 23 provinces from 2013 to 2017. It offers robust, longitudinal evidence on the incidence of CS requiring medical attention, revealing an overall crude incidence of 405.65 per 100,000 person-years in 2017, with a higher burden among females and a peak in the 50–59 age group. Crucially, the study quantifies the substantial and rapidly growing economic burden, demonstrating a more than seven-fold increase in total annual costs (from $145.17 million to $1064.17 million) and a significant rise in per-patient costs (from $597.32 to $970.67) over the five-year period. The geographical analysis identifying East China as the highest incidence region adds further nuance to understanding the national landscape of CS burden.

Implications of all the available evidence

The collective evidence, including the new findings from this study, underscores cervical spondylosis as a major and escalating public health challenge in China, driven by an aging population and likely influenced by lifestyle factors. The steep rise in both the number of cases and associated healthcare costs signals a pressing need for the healthcare system to anticipate and manage this growing demand. The demographic and geographical patterns highlighted in this study suggest that prevention and management strategies should be prioritized for middle-aged and older adults, particularly women, and tailored for high-incidence regions. Future policies and clinical guidelines should emphasize cost-effective, early intervention and standardized management pathways to mitigate the clinical and economic impact of CS. Further research should investigate modifiable risk factors and evaluate the long-term outcomes and cost-effectiveness of different treatment modalities within the Chinese healthcare context.

Introduction

Cervical spondylosis (CS) is an umbrella term encompassing a spectrum of chronic, progressive degenerative conditions affecting the cervical spine and the surrounding accessory structures, including intervertebral discs, facet joints, and surrounding connective tissues.1 Based on the needs of researchers in different fields, CS has different classification methods. Each classification presents with varying clinical manifestations and disease progression, but most methods have ignored patients with early stages of CS, which is often asymptomatic or presents with mild and nonspecific symptoms, despite observable changes on imaging.2 As the condition advances, nerve root and spinal cord compression can lead to neurological deficits, neck stiffness, and pain, significantly impairing physical function, reducing quality of life, and even resulting in disability, which brings a substantial disease and economic burden.

With shifts in lifestyle and an aging population, CS has become increasingly prevalent, establishing CS as a major global public health concern. In the United Kingdom alone, the economic burden of CS amounts to approximately £681.6 million.3 Despite its rising significance, previous studies have focused on individual subtypes of CS, but comprehensive epidemiological data on CS remain scarce. Moreover, inconsistencies in classification criteria, varied definitions, and diverse etiological factors create substantial diagnostic ambiguity, leading to delays in intervention.4 For example, neck pain, a hallmark symptom of CS, affects an estimated 203 million people globally.5 However, due to the often mild symptoms and nonspecific nature of CS physical signs, the vast majority of affected individuals remain undiagnosed, or couldn't receive standard management. This discrepancy highlights the “tip of the iceberg” phenomenon, where only a fraction of individuals with cervical spine disorders seeking medical attention diagnosed and treated with a certain subtype—represents only a fraction of the true burden. The vast majority, hidden beneath the surface, remain undiagnosed and untreated, potentially leading to a significant and underrecognized disease burden that could have far-reaching consequences for public health.6

In China, CS has become an increasingly prominent health issue, exacerbated by rapid urbanization, demographic shifts, and evolving lifestyle patterns. Factors such as prolonged screen exposure and the growing prevalence of sedentary, desk-based occupations have further amplified the disease burden.7 However, epidemiological research on CS in China remains limited. To date, the only community-based cross-sectional study, conducted in 2010, reported a prevalence of 13.76%.8 The absence of large-scale, population-based studies impedes the development of evidence-based prevention strategies and the efficient allocation of healthcare resources. The Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI) and Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URBMI) databases—two major national administrative healthcare databases—offer a unique opportunity to bridge this knowledge gap. This study aims to estimate the incidence and associated costs of CS across diverse populations and regions in China by leveraging data from 2013 to 2017, providing full picture of CS burden in Chinese population. By providing robust epidemiological evidence, this study seeks to inform healthcare resource allocation and policy planning, offering critical insights into disease burden and guiding strategies for prevention and management in China.

Methods

Data source

This nationwide population-based study comprehensively analyzed data from the National Insurance Claims for Epidemiological Research (NICER) spanning from January 1, 2013 to December 31, 2017. Detailed population, data sources, and methodology have been described in previous studies.9 Briefly, this population-based study was performed using the Urban Basic Medical Insurance (UBMI) database. The database includes Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI) for working and retired employees in cities (i.e., employers and employees from government agencies and institutions, state-owned enterprises, private businesses, social organizations, and other private entities), and Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URBMI) covering citizens without employment in cities, which are the two main medical insurance programs for the Chinese urban population. By 2016, UBMI covered more than 95% of the entire Chinese urban population.10 UEBMI and URBMI are updated monthly in all cities. All medical records of the insured population were recorded in the database of their registered provincial regions, regardless of the place of receiving medical services or the proportion of reimbursement of medical services. The observation period (2013–2017) was chosen because it represents a policy-stable phase of the national basic medical insurance system, and because nationwide NICER data beyond 2017 were not available to the authors under the data use agreement at the time of study design.

This study was approved by the ethical review committee of the Peking University Health Science Center (IRB. No.: IRB00001052-18012), and the requirement for informed consent was waived because of the use of deidentified administrative databases. All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. This study adhered to the Reporting of Studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) statements for reporting.

Study population

Approximately 300 million urban-insured people from 23 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions were included (Table S2). To be specific, the original databases included data from 31 provincial regions. Eight provincial regions were excluded from this analysis because of the absence or abnormality of crucial data.

We extracted the provincial level variables from UEBMI and URBMI: demographic characteristics (sex, date of birth, and registered provincial region), diagnostic information (visiting dates, diagnostic texts, diagnostic codes, type of medical service (emergency, outpatient, or inpatient service), and medical expenditure.

Individuals with the following conditions were excluded: (1) without a valid national insurance ID or (2) had conflicting information recorded, i.e., records of medical treatment reimbursement time earlier than the individual's first enrollment time.

All claim records were anonymized to protect patients’ privacy.

Case identification

According to ICD-9 codes (i.e., 722.0 and 722.4) and ICD-10 codes (i.e., M43 and M50), and medical terms in Chinese (i.e., “Cervical disc herniation” and “Neck pain”), potential patients with CS were identified first (Table S1). To avoid missing patients when using the medical terms in Chinese, a fuzzy string-matching algorithm was constructed to extract potential CS patients from the database. This claims-based case identification approach was further validated through an independent review of the diagnoses by two orthopedic surgeons (SYL and FFZ), who confirmed the definitive CS cases. The detailed validation procedures are described in Method S1 and illustrated in Figure S1.

All patients who met the above case definition were included in the CS study cohort. We set up a 4-year washout period (2013–2016) to reduce the influence of prevalent CS cases11 and patients were classified as incident cases for 2017 if they received their first recorded CS diagnosis in 2017. Patients were therefore not counted as new incident cases if (1) CS was identified during the 4-year washout period (2013–2016), or (2) the diagnosis text was clearly described as “old CS” by doctors.

Statistical analysis

We calculated the incidence in 2017 among adults aged ≥18 years after excluding cases identified in the 4-year washout period (2013–2016). Thus, incident CS cases were those receiving their first diagnosis in 2017. Incidence were stratified by sex and age (18–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, and ≥80 years) and calculated by a two-step approach (Method S2).12

First, we calculated the age- and sex-specific incidence in each province. The calculation was based on the total person-years of the population enrolled in the Urban Basic Medical Insurance (UBMI) for each year in each provincial region. The numerator included patients diagnosed with CS, estimated from the denominator population in each provincial region, while accounting for missing data. The population included in each province was divided into three groups: individuals without claim records (N1), individuals with complete diagnostic information records (N2), and individuals with claim records but lacking diagnostic information (N3). The observed patients with CS(M1) were from N2, and the number of patients (M2) in N3 was estimated using Poisson regression of 10-fold multiple imputations. Then, we calculated the national average estimates using a random-effects meta-analysis to pool the province-specific estimates. The Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was used to examine the stability of the variance of province-specific estimates.13 Subgroup analysis was calculated by sex and age (i.e., 18–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, ≥80), and the geographical region was also analyzed. Rates were expressed as per 100,000 person-years at risk and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated assuming a Poisson distribution. All of the results given by different imputation sets were combined according to Rubin's Rule.14 The age-standardized incidence of CS in 2017 was calculated by direct standardization using the 2010 and 2020 Chinese national census populations as domestic standard populations, and the 2016 Canadian, 2010 US, and 2017 Australian populations as alternative external standard populations to facilitate international comparison and to assess the robustness of our estimates under different age structures. Post hoc ecological analyses were conducted to explore geographical variation in CS incidence. Association analyses across six geographical regions were first performed using linear regression, followed by province-level multivariate meta-regression to adjust for potential confounders including education, urbanization, economic level, diet, environment, and healthcare-related factors. A complementary city-level analysis assessed the association between observed CS incidence and urban disposable income using negative binomial regression (Method S3).

The costs of CS were also evaluated. Two types of costs were evaluated: 1) total costs for CS and 2) drug costs for CS. Additionally, the per patient costs of the above types were evaluated. The costs from 2013 to 2016 were also discounted by the consumer price index (CPI) to costs in 2017.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the results: (1) including only observed CS patients to get the lower bounds of the rates, and (2) excluding the top 10% of provinces with missing diagnostic information. The Student t-test for continuous variables and the χ2 Tests for categorical variables were used to compare characteristics between sexes. All statistical analyses were conducted with R version 4.4.1 and a two-sided p < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Incidence of cervical spondylosis

The overall crude incidence of CS in urban China in 2017 was 405.65 per 100,000 person-years (95% confidence interval [CI], 262.79–548.52). The crude incidence among females was 492.81 (95% CI, 317.37–668.25), which was higher than that among males (323.19; 95% CI, 209.14–437.25), corresponding to an incidence rate ratio of 1.53 (95% CI, 0.92–2.52; p < .05). Incidence was highest in East China (588.94; 95% CI, 252.59–925.29) and lowest in North China (125.88; 95% CI, 21.60–230.16) (Table 2). Regarding the age trend in incidence, the incidence was peaked in 50–59 years old (649.90; 95% CI, 438.85–860.96) in the whole population, while the highest incidence was at 50–59 years old (816.65; 95% CI, 560.80–1072.50) in female and 60–69 years old in male (539.25; 95% CI, 381.74–696.77), respectively (Table 2, Fig. 1). Discussion

This study is the first to estimate the incidence and economic burden of CS using a nationwide database in China, revealing the above key findings. First, the crude incidence of clinically recognized CS was 405.65 per 100,000 person-years in 2017. The per-patient total cost for CS patients increased 1.63-fold (62.5%) over the study period, while drug costs remained relatively stable. This pattern suggests that the rising economic burden may be partly related to broader healthcare use for underlying diseases and comorbidities in this population. Second, the incidence was consistently higher in women than in men, peaking at 50–59 and 60–69, respectively. Third, significant regional variations were observed, with East China exhibiting the highest incidence.

High incidence rate and cost

In our study, the incidence of CS was 405.65 per 100,000 person-years, markedly higher than previously reported rates that focused solely on specific subtypes. For instance, prior studies reported the incidence of cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) as 41 per 1,000,000 population in North America15 and 4.04 per 100,000 person-years in Taiwan, China.16 Similarly, the reported incidence of cervical radiculopathy varied significantly, ranging from 0.832 to 1.79 per 1000 population.17 These figures illustrate a crucial limitation: current clinical practice typically identifies only a fraction of CS patients—those with clearly defined symptomatic subtypes—representing merely the “tip of the iceberg”. In reality, a vast majority of affected individuals remain unrecognized, reflecting a substantial hidden disease burden beneath the clinical detection threshold. Given China's existing medical resource constraints, particularly the significant shortage of orthopedic specialists,18 addressing CS through tertiary prevention becomes not only practical but economically advantageous. Preventative strategies initiated at early or asymptomatic stages could significantly alleviate the progression of cervical degeneration, thereby reducing the eventual demand for advanced medical interventions and surgery. Analogously, guidelines emphasizing tertiary prevention have already been established for conditions such as osteoporotic fractures, underscoring the necessity and effectiveness of such strategies.19 However, no comprehensive guidelines or consensus currently exist for CS as a whole; existing recommendations typically focus narrowly on specific clinical subtypes and fail to sufficiently emphasize the potential benefits of preventive lifestyle modifications.

The notably high incidence rate observed in this study validates the “iceberg” analogy and underscores a critical public health implication: effective management of CS requires a shift from reactive clinical treatment primarily focuses on surgical intervention, toward proactive population-level prevention.20 By implementing structured preventive measures, healthcare systems could achieve substantial reductions in medical expenditures, alleviate the burden on specialized orthopedic services, and facilitate a more rational allocation of limited healthcare resources. Recognizing and addressing this previously overlooked population is essential for accurately estimating disease burden and informing public health policies.21 Ultimately, our findings advocate for a broader, prevention-oriented approach to CS management. Prioritizing early-stage interventions and educational initiatives can significantly reduce individual, family, and societal burdens, highlighting an urgent need for comprehensive, evidence-based guidelines targeting the prevention and management of CS across diverse populations.

Sex-based differences in cervical spondylosis incidence

Our study found that the incidence of CS was consistently 1.53-fold higher in women than in men. The peak incidence notably occurred at 50–59 years in women, compared to 60–69 years in men. This gender disparity is consistent with international reports and highlights distinct epidemiological features of CS in females. Studies in Poland and Italy similarly found peak incidence in the 51–60 and 50–59 age groups, respectively,14 while Fejer et al. reported women experience neck pain 33% more frequently than men.22 These differences emphasize the importance of examining sex variations in epidemiological patterns.

The observed sex disparity in CS arises from physiological, psychological, and socio-environmental factors. The female incidence peak corresponds with menopause, where estrogen deficiency reduces anti-inflammatory effects and accelerates cervical degeneration, increasing vulnerability to inflammation-driven symptoms (particularly pain) and structural decline. Initially, nucleus pulposus inflammation predominates in middle age, exacerbating symptoms. With aging, chronic degeneration induces nuclear atrophy and reduced intradiscal pressure, diminishing annulus damage risk and symptom manifestation after age 60.23,24

Additionally, estrogen plays a crucial role in maintaining bone density and muscle integrity, further increasing women's vulnerability to CS by accelerating degeneration.3

Psychological and environmental factors exacerbate CS disparities in women. Menopausal psychological stressors intensify symptom perception, while heightened pain sensitivity and health literacy.25 Chinese women also exhibit pronounced protein-calcium deficiencies (below male intake and global recommendations), accelerating muscle loss and elevating CS risk.26 Occupational exposures like prolonged sedentary work significantly elevate CS incidence. Approximately 320 million Chinese women (43.2% of the total workforce) face such high-risk conditions, representing globally comparable workforce proportions based on China government and World Bank public data.

Additionally, traditional cultural expectations impose substantial caregiving duties on Chinese women, elevating musculoskeletal strain via intensive household labor.27 This sex-based disparity in family and household responsibilities has persisted across generations despite increased male engagement in domestic roles,28 with similar patterns across East Asia.

Finally, the influence of anatomical structure can't be ignored, as women generally possess narrower cervical spinal canals and foramina, and limited variability in female posture, movement patterns, and muscle activation, increasing their susceptibility to nerve compression and associated symptoms.29

Impact of economic development on cervical spondylosis incidence

The regional distribution of CS in China reveals a striking pattern: incidence rates are highest in the eastern provinces, followed by the south-central and northwestern regions. It cannot be ruled out that demographic characteristics and economic development influenced the geographic gradient of CS incidence. CS, once considered a disease of aging and manual labor, is increasingly recognized as a modern “disease of affluence.” In wealthier regions, sedentary lifestyles, long hours at desks, and heavy reliance on digital devices—hallmarks of urban professional life—are key contributors to the rising burden of cervical spine disorders. China's eastern provinces, including Jiangsu, Shandong, Zhejiang, and Guangdong, consistently lead the nation in GDP rankings and urbanization based on government public data. Enhanced healthcare engagement in developed regions—reflecting greater health awareness, improved medical access, and care-seeking willingness—artificially elevates reported incidence through timely diagnosis. This surveillance bias may indicate superior system responsiveness rather than solely true prevalence, confounding burden comparisons. Internal migration further influences regional disparities. Working-age adults (30–59 years)—a high-risk cohort—relocate from economically disadvantaged regions to developed coastal areas for employment.30 This concentrates the disease burden in destination areas where heightened occupational exposures and superior diagnostic capacity coexist. Importantly, the diagnosis of CS depends not only on clinical symptoms but also on specialized imaging and professional expertise.4 Top-tier spinal care centers are disproportionately located in the east and south-central China, reinforcing regional diagnostic disparities. However, these interpretations are based on ecological comparisons. As such, the association between economic development and CS incidence should be interpreted with caution.

Yet, these patterns are not fixed. China is undergoing a period of extraordinary transformation. As national strategies increasingly prioritize balanced regional development, the currently less developed western regions are likely to evolve toward the socioeconomic profile that now typifies the eastern provinces. In this sense, China stands not only as a rising global power but as a prototype for emerging economies. Therefore, understanding the dynamics of CS in affluent areas offers a valuable blueprint for anticipating and mitigating its rise in the developing regions of tomorrow—regions that are already on their path to prosperity.

Strengths and limitations

This study utilized a large, nationally representative dataset of the urban Chinese population, providing reliable estimates of CS incidence and associated costs. The large sample size, longitudinal study design, and diagnostic data from medical records allowed for robust and generalizable findings.

However, several limitations merit consideration. First, the database primarily covered UBMI-insured urban residents and did not include rural populations, who differ in insurance coverage and healthcare access; Nevertheless, by standardization using census population, we enhanced the comparability of incidence estimates, however the burden of CS in rural areas may differ and warrants further investigation. Second, case ascertainment relied on insurance claims data without access to imaging or laboratory information, which may have introduced diagnostic misclassification. Third, although sensitivity analyses were performed, the washout period may not have fully excluded prevalent cases.31 In addition, the absence of individual-level socioeconomic data limited subgroup analyses, and post hoc ecological analyses of economic indicators were therefore exploratory. Finally, our analysis was based on claims data from 2013 to 2017, as more recent data were unavailable. Although cervical spondylosis is a chronic degenerative condition with relatively slowly evolving risk factors, the restricted study period and washout window limited our ability to assess longer-term secular trends in incidence and to evaluate the effects of subsequent insurance reforms and centralized procurement policies on costs and inpatient utilization. Future studies using more recent nationwide claims data are needed to validate these findings and to evaluate changes in incidence, utilization, and costs.

Conclusion

In this population-based study, the incidence and cost of clinically recognized CS increased in China. As the population ages, China will face an ever-increasing burden from CS, so more attention should be given to the management of elder patients and high risk groups.

These findings highlights strategic public health adaptations. China's trajectory—where CS burden rise in an aging, economically robust developing society mirrors developed nations—offers transferable insights for incidence modeling and care innovation. Calculating the incidence of CS in China and exploring its practical management mode can enlighten other developing countries in their health-related policies. Additionally, for all countries facing the challenge of aging, it is necessary to prepare in advance to cope with the age-related medical burden that has come or is about to come.
