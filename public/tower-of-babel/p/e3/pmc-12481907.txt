Reliability of Provocative Maneuvers for Hand Pathologies: Concordance Between Nonspecialist and Specialist Assessments in a General Clinic Setting
Authors: Clay B Thames, Evan Bowen, Greg Vance, Bradley Hathaway, Kacy Benedict, Mark Dodson, Marc Walker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeProvocative maneuvers are frequently employed by hand surgeons to evaluate common hand pathologies. Although prior studies have evaluated the efficacy of individual maneuvers independently, to date, no studies have been performed evaluating the concordance between nonsurgeon-administered Tinel, Eichhoff, Finkelstein, and carpometacarpal Grind test and attending hand surgeon diagnoses in a general hand clinic population.MethodsA prospective cohort study was performed on new patients presenting to the hand clinic. All four provocative maneuvers were performed on each patient. Positive versus negative results were recorded by a student and compared with the final clinical diagnoses by fellowship-trained, board-certified hand surgeons retrospectively. Descriptive statistics and χ2 analysis were performed.ResultsA total of 93 patients were enrolled in the study. The concordance between the nonsurgeon examination and the hand surgeon examination was analyzed. Analyses showed that Tinel test, Grind test, and Eichhoff test were meaningfully associated with their respective hand pathologies, whereas Finkelstein test was not. Among the tests evaluated, the Grind test showed the greatest concordance, and the Tinel test offered high concordance while also limiting false-positive examinations, even between a nonsurgeon and a hand specialist.ConclusionsThis prospective study revealed varied concordance among maneuvers. The Grind test, Tinel test, and Eichhoff test demonstrated efficacy in identifying carpometacarpal arthritis, carpal tunnel syndrome, and de Quervain tenosynovitis, respectively. Although these tests were not designed for screening, these maneuvers may support early hypothesis generation when evaluating undifferentiated upper-extremity complaints in the clinic.Type of study/level of evidenceDiagnostic IIb.Key words: Carpal tunnel, Eichhoff test, Tinel test

Accurate and timely diagnosis of hand disorders remains a critical challenge in musculoskeletal medicine. Many common hand conditions, including carpometacarpal (CMC) arthritis, carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS), and De Quervain tenosynovitis (DQT), rely on clinical evaluation and provocative maneuvers to aid in diagnosis. Despite the widespread use of tests, such as Tinel test, the Grind test, Eichhoff test, and Finkelstein test, there is a lack of comprehensive studies evaluating their collective diagnostic utility in a broad patient population.1, 2, 3, 4 Oftentimes, nonspecialist evaluators, such as general practitioners or trainees, may be the first to evaluate a patient and may use these tests during the encounter. Most prior studies have assessed these tests in cohorts of symptomatic individuals, but none have assessed how these tests, when performed by nonspecialists, align with the clinical impression of an attending hand surgeon.5,6

Many innovations in clinical hand diagnosis have been made over the last century.7 Phalen test was analyzed by De Smet et al8 and was deemed to have minimal clinical utility when used alone to diagnose CTS. Similar findings were reported in Wiesman et al.9 The WHAT test, pioneered by Goubau et al,10 and the radial synergy test, developed by Lee et al,11 have provided clinicians with other ways to clinically assess for DQT, illustrating the continued evolution of provocative maneuvers. These novel studies provide evidence that clinicians continually seek more accurate clinical tests to help diagnose hand-related pathology. Similarly, the Grind test for CMC arthritis has been shown to have a more limited clinical utility than the pressure-shear test.12 Recently, however, Billig and Sears13 suggested that a considerable portion of surgeons are not aware of current guidelines for diagnosing CTS and may overuse electrodiagnostic studies testing, emphasizing the utility of the physical examination.

The most common consensus in modern-day management of CTS, CMC arthritis, and DQT is that provocative maneuvers provide some utility but are best used in combination with other tests such as patient history, imaging, and nerve conduction studies.14

The goal of this study was to systematically evaluate the diagnostic value of provocative maneuvers performed by a nonspecialist in all patients presenting to a hand clinic, regardless of their chief complaint, when compared with specialist attending physician diagnoses. By comparing the results of the tests with the diagnoses, we examine whether these tests accurately identify pathology in individual patients. We are thus able to evaluate the concordance of these tests’ results with actual diagnoses when used by nonspecialists. This approach provides a novel perspective on the broader utility of these maneuvers beyond traditional targeted testing. We hypothesize that through statistical analysis of the previously mentioned provocative maneuvers, we will demonstrate that these tests do provide clinical utility even if administered by nonsurgeons. Our findings have the potential to refine current diagnostic pathways, enabling earlier detection and intervention for hand disorders. Late detection is associated with worse clinical symptoms and outcomes.15,16 This study not only contributes to the existing body of literature but also offers practical insights for clinicians aiming to optimize patient evaluation strategies in hand clinics.

Materials and Methods

After obtaining approval from the institutional review board, a 17-question data collection tool was designed in the Research Electronic Data Capture portal, where patient data were also securely stored (Appendix S1, available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org). A cross-sectional study was performed by enrolling all patients who presented to either the plastic surgery or orthopedic surgery clinic, regardless of the patient’s chief complaint, until 100 patients were recruited. However, only data for 93 patients were analyzed because of missing data from seven of the evaluated patients. Informed consent was obtained, and patients were allowed to opt into or out of the study. Demographic data were then collected, including patients’ employment status, self-described ethnic background, hand dominance, prior history of hand surgery, and their chief complaint for presentation to the clinic. Subsequently, the four provocative maneuvers (Tinel test, Grind test, Eichhoff test, and Finkelstein test) were performed on the extremity with the chief complaint by a researcher trained in the provocative maneuvers but not board-certified in a surgical specialty, and the results of the test were recorded.

Based on the results of the provocative maneuver tests alone, patients were given a “diagnosis” of DQT, CTS, or CMC arthritis and then correlated this result with the diagnosis made by the attending hand surgeon during the patient’s clinic visit. Although physical examination and history gathered by an attending physician are not the gold standard diagnostic tools for CTS, DQT, or CMC arthritis, the study seeks to analyze the potential concordance of clinic findings between trained and untrained examiners. Inclusion criteria included all adult patients presenting to the hand and upper-extremity clinic without consideration of chief complaint or reason for presentation.

A descriptive analysis was used to describe the comparison between the differential and actual diagnoses using percentage values for each selection. χ2 tests were used to compare attending diagnosed hand pathology with the results of the nonsurgeon-administered provocative maneuvers and stratified with demographic variables. Statistical analyses were performed using PSPP version 2.0.

Results

Ninety-three patients presenting to hand and upper-extremity clinics over a 7-month period were enrolled in the study. Demographic data are displayed in the Table 1, and 47 of the 93 patients tested received a diagnosis of hand-related pathology following their clinic visit. Criteria for inclusion in the hand-related pathology group were any diagnoses related to the hand and upper extremity that were documented in the patient’s medical record. If no hand-related diagnosis was present after the patient’s visit, this was considered negative for hand pathology. Hand pathologies were not only limited to the diagnoses of DQT, CTS, and CMC arthritis but also included ballistic traumas, mucoid cysts, trigger fingers, and others. Tinel test

Tinel test was performed on all 93 participants, with 37 (39.8%) testing positive and 56 (60.2%) testing negative. There was a positive concordance between the nonspecialist examination and the attending physician examination, with 89.1% of the patients diagnosed with CTS by the attending physician exhibiting a positive Tinel test during untrained examination. χ2 analysis showed a considerable association between a positive Tinel test and hand pathology (P < .001), suggesting that the test is a reliable indicator of underlying conditions (Table 2).

Grind test

Among the participants, 17 (18.3%) tested positive for the Grind test, whereas 76 (81.7%) tested negative. Ultimately, three patients were diagnosed with CMC arthritis, and of these, two had positive Grind tests on the untrained examination. Many patients, however, had a positive Grind test on untrained examination, showing the limited utility of this test as a diagnostic tool because of high false-positive rates. Statistical analysis confirmed a notable relationship between Grind test results and all hand pathology (P < .001). Grind test should be interpreted cautiously in a clinical setting, especially if performed by a nonsurgeon (Table 3).

Eichhoff test

Eichhoff test was positive in 47 (50.5%) participants and negative in 46 (49.5%). For the two patients diagnosed with DQT, both had a positive Eichhoff test on an untrained examination, but many patients without DQT also had a positive test without a diagnosis of DQT. This shows the limited ability of this test to differentiate between hand pathologies on the nonspecialist examination. A χ2 test indicated a notable correlation between Eichhoff test and pathology (P < .001). However, studies suggest that Eichhoff test should be used in conjunction with other diagnostic maneuvers to confirm DQT (Table 4).

Finkelstein test

Finkelstein’s test was performed on all participants, with 19 (20.4%) testing positive and 74 (79.6%) testing negative. Of the two patients diagnosed with DQT, only one had a positive Finkelstein test on the untrained examination. χ2 analysis indicated a notable association between a positive Finkelstein’s test and hand pathology (P = .001). These findings suggest that it has a limited ability to distinguish DQT from other hand pathology if used by a nonspecialist and that it should be used alongside other clinical evaluations for diagnosing De Quervain tenosynovitis (Table 5).

Tests related to their intended pathologies

Among patients who demonstrated a positive Tinel sign yet were not diagnosed with CTS, a range of alternative pathologies was identified. Notably, these included three cases of Dupuytren contracture, two trapezium fractures, and one trigger finger, along with various other conditions. Similarly, among those who exhibited a positive Eichhoff test without confirmation of DQT, alternative diagnoses were observed. Many of these patients (n = 14) were found to have CTS, whereas additional findings included a single case of Dupuytren contracture, two trapezium fractures, and several other pathologies. In patients with a positive Finkelstein test who did not meet diagnostic criteria for DQT, the most prevalent alternative diagnosis was again CTS (n = 8), followed by one case each of Dupuytren contracture, a ganglion involving the thumb CMC joint, and a trapezium fracture, among other conditions. Finally, in individuals with a positive Grind test but no evidence of CMC arthritis, alternative diagnoses included seven cases of CTS, one case of osteoarthritis affecting the small finger proximal interphalangeal joint, one ganglion at the thumb CMC joint, one trigger finger, and an assortment of other pathologies.

Our study recruited 23 patients with diagnosed CTS, two patients with diagnosed DQT, and three patients with diagnosed CMC arthritis. Of the patients with DQT, one tested positive for every provocative maneuver, whereas the other tested positive only for Eichhoff test. Of the patients with CMC arthritis, one had no reactivity to any of the tests; one had a positive reaction to Tinel, Eichhoff, and Grind; and the final patient was positive for Tinel and Grind. For the patients with CTS, 20 tested positive for Tinel test, whereas three tested negative. Six tested positive for the Grind test, and all of these came from the positive Tinel group. Fourteen tested positive for Eichhoff test, and seven tested positive for Finkelstein test.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to understand the role that provocative maneuvers play in clinical medicine today. Although each one of these tests was developed to identify a particular pathology in the clinical setting, their reliability, efficacy, and utility have been called into question. Our study aimed to elucidate the concordance between the results of the tests performed by nonsurgeons and the diagnoses made by attending physicians. Our results indicate that the provocative maneuvers themselves are good at eliciting positive results in patients with hand pathology, but the study of their utility as tests for a particular clinical diagnosis was limited by our patient cohort. Our study expands upon current literature, as the tests were administered to a wide variety of patients with vague chief complaints related to the upper extremity rather than a targeted patient population. We found that regardless of underlying pathology, hand-related issues cause pain that can be elicited by most of the described maneuvers.

Association between hand pathology and positive test results

Our analysis revealed a positive association between hand pathology and positive test results. Although clinical and anatomical research regarding each individual test’s ability to diagnose a specific condition has been conducted previously, a study that incorporates our approach of broad testing by nonspecialists irrespective of chief complaint, with a larger sample size, and compared with gold standard diagnostic techniques, might yield different results.17,18 An increase in sample size would also increase the number of false positives, as was shown by the amount of false positives elicited by nonsurgeon examinations in our limited cohort, furthering the point that these tests are not screening tests. Interestingly, Eichhoff test approached the concordance of Tinel test for diagnosing CTS, with 14 of the patients with CTS testing positive for Eichhoff test. We also had eight patients who tested positive for every test. These patients’ actual diagnoses were four CTS, one DQT, one ganglion, one trapezium fracture, and one flexor tendon contracture.

Positive tests with incongruent diagnoses

Many of the patients in the study did not have the diagnoses for which our tests were intended. This led to many patients testing positive for a maneuver when the root cause of their pain was something different. Importantly, this study does not advocate for the use of these provocative maneuvers as screening tools in the general population. However, this instead explores whether the maneuvers can help guide early clinical reasoning and undifferentiated patients, especially when the initial examination is performed by a provider without specialty training. This study is particularly important in the context of primary care, urgent care, or emergency settings where diagnostic certainty is often rare and providers lack access to advanced testing. A powerful tool of a physician is a patient’s history, and because of the nature of the study, this was an element we avoided assessing to blind the researcher performing the testing maneuvers. However, based on our results, we would not recommend blindly administering these tests as screening tests to each patient who presents to the clinic, as their use is best suited for a patient who presents with specific symptoms.

Major limitations

Of our patients diagnosed with CMC arthritis, two also had CTS. It has also been well documented that other hand pathologies skew the results of the tests themselves, further limiting their utility in a clinical setting.17, 18, 19 This study was also limited by sample size, as only 93 patients were studied, and of those 93, only 47 had a diagnosed hand pathology. However, over half of the patients with a diagnosed hand pathology in the study had one of the diagnoses of CMC arthritis, CTS, or DQT. Another limitation of this study was the lack of patients presenting with DQT and CMC arthritis. However, many prior studies have evaluated the sensitivity and specificity of these diagnostic tests in patients with these diagnoses. The final limitation was the lack of comparison of test results with a gold standard diagnostic tool. These tests are difficult to obtain and have the potential not to be completed at later visits. Hence, the study designers opted to work within the confines of one clinic visit to collect all relevant data for the study to limit dropout rates and loss to follow-up.
