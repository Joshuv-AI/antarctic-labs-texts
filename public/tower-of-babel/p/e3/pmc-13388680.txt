Diverse livestock management strategies sustained the emerging chalcolithic town of Tepe Farukhabad, Iran
Authors: Anna Luurtsema, Kara Larson, James Strawn, Henry T Wright, Alicia Ventresca-Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPastoral economies were critical to the development of cities in Late Chalcolithic southwest Asia; yet, pastoral strategies in small settlements, particularly outside of the Mesopotamian heartland, remain understudied. This study uses carbon (δ13C) and oxygen (δ18O) isotope analyses of caprine molars to investigate herd management in the Early Uruk to Late Uruk occupational phases at Tepe Farukhabad, in lowland southwest Iran. In both phases, a pronounced separation in carbon values indicates that herds accessed two discrete environments. One set of animals was likely foddered over the winter months and herded to pastures at higher altitudes in the summer, indicated by δ18O maxima corresponding with δ13C minima. Other animals were likely pastured at lower elevations, possibly near Tepe Farukhabad, as their lower carbon isotope values suggest greater access to C3 plants. These findings indicate that herd management strategies remained diverse and local amid the cultural and climactic transformations of the Uruk period.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55245-x.Subject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The rise of cities, writing systems, and interregional trade networks were key developments in Mesopotamia and its borderlands during the Uruk period (c. 4000−3100 BCE). Coinciding with these changes, herd management strategies were modified to support the demands of emerging urban centers1–4. Reliance on animal dairy and fibers increased, evident in Late Uruk cuneiform signs depicting weaving and regional increases in the number of loom weights and spindle whorls identified5–6. Culling patterns derived from zooarchaeological data also indicate greater retention of animals into adulthood, suggesting they were managed for secondary products rather than meat alone7–8. In contrast, management strategies focused on meat production typically cull animals once maturity is reached at 1–3 years old9–11. The intensification in dairy and fiber production witnessed in the Chalcolithic is synthesized under the secondary products revolution model12,13; although the ‘revolution’ varied regionally in its timing and scale, it still marked an inflection point in the trajectory of human–animal relationships and Mesopotamian society as a whole6,14,15. In several Mesopotamian Uruk sites, the importance of caprines’ contributions to the economy and food supply were particularly apparent, as sheep and goat remains comprised over 90% of the faunal assemblages16,17. More than producers of primary and secondary goods, caprines also served as instrumental actors in the state-making projects of early cities as a mobile form of capital18,19.

An intensification in the exploitation of secondary products is thought to coincide with the development of a dedicated mobile pastoral sector, which would have been critical for provisioning densely inhabited urban centers4,20. This specialization is speculated to have culminated in the bifurcation of pastoral and agricultural lifeways, wherein animals were herded by pastoralists on the peripheries of sedentary farming communities21,22. Others argue that a wide array of subsistence strategies existed in the past, and that agricultural and pastoral economies were likely overlapping and co-productive23,24. For instance, recent isotopic evidence from Early Bronze Age northwestern Iran suggests the majority of herders kept their animals within a day’s journey of Köhne Shahar25. As agricultural production intensified in Chalcolithic Southwest Asia26–28, it became more feasible to graze caprines on agricultural fields than distant pastures29,30. Indeed, the notion that ancient herders were inherently nomadic or marginal to agricultural society has been challenged as an inaccurate extrapolation of ethnographic studies and limited archaeological data [31: 116–127]. One question that remains unanswered from these debates is how pastoral strategies varied outside of the Mesopotamian heartland, where settled communities were smaller and management of livestock is understudied.

Located on the fringes of Mesopotamia at the foothills of the Zagros Mountains, the site of Tepe Farukhabad (DL-32) (Fig. 1) was one of around 40 pre- and protohistoric sites identified in the Deh Luran Plain over several seasons of survey between 1960 and 196932–34. The site is uniquely situated to examine how peripheral towns negotiated pastoral strategies amid the significant cultural and environmental shifts of the Uruk period. Between the Early Uruk phase (EU) (c. 4000−3800 BCE) and Middle Uruk phase (MU) (c. 3800−3300 BCE), Farukhabad was one of several sites inhabited on the plain34:173–176. By the Late Uruk phase (c. 3300−3100 BCE), Farukhabad emerged as the largest of seven sites34:176–178.

Location of Tepe Farukhabad in southwestern Iran. The map was generated in ArcGIS Online and labeled in Adobe Illustrator 2025.

Changes in the zooarchaeological and ceramic assemblages accompany this demographic shift. The production of local pottery styles continued at Tepe Farukhabad during the first half of the Uruk Period; however, by the LU phase, ceramics overwhelmingly resemble the Late Uruk wares produced in Uruk and Susa34–36:186–187,37.

Per Richard Redding’s initial zooarchaeological analysis of the assemblage, caprines are the primary form of subsistence at Tepe Farukhabad throughout the Uruk occupational periods38. A key limitation, however, is that most of the assemblage is grouped broadly as Ovis-Capra (often including possible gazelle, as Redding notes), which restricts species-level resolution. In terms of domesticate representation, in the Early Uruk phase, caprines are dominant in the assemblage, but are almost entirely represented by aggregated Ovis-Capra remains, with only a few identifiable goats (Capra hircus) and no securely identified sheep (Ovis aries). Cattle (Bos taurus) are present in low but steady proportions, while equids appear in relatively higher frequencies than later phases38: 258–260. In the Middle Uruk, caprines continue to be well-represented, still largely restricted in the Ovis-Capra categorization. Identifiable elements suggest a mild goat-leading ratio over sheep, though this reflects only a small portion of the assemblage, and cattle remain present but secondary. By the Late Uruk, caprines still remain overwhelmingly dominant with a decrease in wild fauna presence, and there is a subtle shift towards increased presence of sheep, from what was able to be identified38. Cattle continue to be present at low levels, maintaining a secondary role in the subsistence system. Across the assemblage, caprine mortality profiles indicate that most caprines survived beyond their first year, with a relatively low juvenile culling and a substantial proportion living past three years. This pattern points to herd management strategies extending beyond immediate meat production, likely incorporating secondary products, such as milk or wool9–11. The Uruk sequence reflects a stable reliance of caprines, with a gradual shift towards increased emphasis on sheep management by the Late Uruk38,39. Compounding factors in the Late Uruk may have been responsible for the changes observed in Tepe Farukhabad’s faunal, ceramic, and settlement patterns. These include a period of increased aridity, which would have lowered agricultural productivity40, alongside regional political destabilization associated with the movement of populations41.

To better understand the transition from early to later periods, our study examines caprine livestock management strategies at Tepe Farukhabad. The aim is to understand how caprines shaped lifeways within this community and how herding strategies varied over time. Specifically, did herd management practices change accordingly with the sociocultural and environmental shifts between the Middle and Late Uruk phases? Did herding practices change during the Late Uruk amid increasing aridity, population movement in the Deh Luran plain, and an influx of Uruk material culture? By measuring stable carbon and oxygen isotopes from sequentially sampled tooth enamel from sheep and goats, we investigate seasonal changes in diet and mobility over a portion of caprine lives. In doing so, we expand the limited isotopic data available from this period and region, while examining how pastoral strategies were practiced at a town on the edge of the Uruk expansion.

Geography and ecology of the Deh Luran Plain

Tepe Farukhabad is located in southwestern Iran in the province of Khuzestan on a semi-arid steppe known as the Deh Luran Plain (Fig. 2). Situated in a transitional piedmont zone (100–300 m asl) between the Zagros Mountains to the north and east and the lowlands to the west and south, the Deh Luran Plain afforded several environmental benefits for inhabitants of early villages and urban centers33. As in the mountains, the region received rainfall during the winter, and the waters of the Mehmeh and Dawairij Rivers provided water for irrigation to support agriculture in the summer. For these reasons, the Deh Luran plain was home to many early villages and urban sites, including Tepe Farukhabad33,34. Tepe Farukhabad was established at an elevation of 140 m asl on the banks of the Mehmeh River, which provided water for irrigation. The intensive irrigation built to support agriculture at these sites may have contributed to high soil salinity levels on the plain36. Particularly in high-altitude, arid regions, naturally saline areas would have provided essential nutrients for livestock, especially goats42.

Ecological zones of the Deh Luran plain. The map was modified with permission from Wright 1981 using Adobe Illustrator 2025.

Today, this region is characterized by low rainfall, high evaporation rates, and a stark contrast in temperature and precipitation between the summer and winter. Rainfall in Khuzestan averages 300 mm/year in the lowlands to the south and up to 750 mm/year in the mountainous north43 (Fig. 3). Most rainfall occurs between October and March, peaking in November with 72 mm on average. Extreme precipitation events are more common in southwestern Iran than other regions, as moisture from the Mediterranean and Red Sea systems converges around the Zagros Mountains44–46. Evaporation rates are extremely high (> 2000 ml/year), which results in high soil salinity and drought, necessitating irrigation to support crops in antiquity36,47. Dry winds from the northwest during the summer months contribute to average daily highs of 110 ℉ (43 ℃). In the winter, temperatures in the lowlands range between 53 and 78 ℉ (12 to 26 ℃), though snowfall is common at higher altitudes in the Zagros Mountains.

Vegetation in the Deh Luran Plain is a mix of C3 and C4 plants. A comprehensive review of plant species in the Middle East estimated 36% of species in Iran follow the more arid-adapted C4 photosynthetic pathway48. The main C4 plant families in Iran are Chenopodiaceae (goosefoot), Poaceae (grasses), and Cyperaceae (sedges), while the most abundant C3 plant families include Papilionaceae (legumes), Linaceae (flax), and Rubiaceae (bedstraw). C4 plants in Iran are generally more abundant in warmer, drier environments with hypersaline soils that are common in deserts and salty marshes49. In comparison, C3 plants prefer more temperate climates with greater rainfall; in Iran, such environments coincide with areas of higher elevation, such as the Zagros Mountains49. In Iran, there are greater proportions of C4 plants in lowland areas with less rainfall, while high altitude locales or areas with higher average annual rainfall. In the Uruk period, a seasonal marsh, fed from mountain snow melt and river flooding, may have provided a more fertile environment for C3 plants to grow36; alternatively, saline depressions may have provided good places for C4 plants to grow in the height of summer. Broadly, the carbon isotope composition of plants is influenced by altitude, water availability, salinity, and aridity. Thus, in mountainous areas, the δ13C values of C3 plants increase along altitudinal gradients, meaning that they increase as one goes up in altitude50–53. The observed correlation is quite modest, with only a 1.5‰ increase in the Alps over an altitudinal transect from 400 to 2500 m54. The proportion of C4 taxa is lower at higher altitudes and greater at lower altitudes55–58. These changes in plant proportions may have a greater effect on the isotopic composition of herbivore tissues as they move from low to high elevations, or vice versa59.

Stable carbon and oxygen isotope analysis to reconstruct herd management

Sequential stable isotope analyses are a useful tool for the analysis of faunal remains and the reconstruction of ancient herding practices25,59–64. Stable carbon and oxygen isotope values can reveal the strategies utilized by herders to maximize the productivity of their herds through the close examination of animal diet and landscape use. The measurement of isotope values of sequential enamel samples from sheep and goat molars demonstrate the water and plants consumed over the course of enamel development, making it possible to gauge seasonal changes in diet61,65–67.

For instance, a pattern of 13C enrichment could suggest that the animals were consistently fed with C4 plant-based fodder, indicating deliberate long-term management of livestock over several seasons. Additionally, in arid regions such as the Middle East, herders developed specific strategies to manage livestock in response to drier environments68,69. For example, transhumance, which involves moving animals seasonally between different grazing areas, is a common practice in arid regions to optimize limited food and water sources70. Vertical transhumance, the movement of livestock from lowland areas in the winter, to high elevation sites in the summer can also be a viable option for herding70,71. This can result in animals grazing on different types of plants or in different geographic locations throughout the year, leading to variability in their isotopic values62,64,72. In arid regions such as southwestern Iran, C4 plants such as grasses and shrubs, which have greater water use efficiency, are found in greater abundance than in other areas of the world48,49. The abundance of δ13C within livestock tooth enamel can vary depending on the type of plants that were ingested during the life of the animal. As a result, animals grazing or consuming fodder in arid regions may exhibit isotopic values that are enriched in 13C relative to wetter or colder areas, due to the predominance of C4 plants in their diet. Sequential sampling can elucidate diet and landscape use by providing data on a monthly or sub-monthly basis, allowing for the examination of seasonal patterning. Combined with stable oxygen isotope analysis, this can further reveal the use of mobility or foddering to mitigate the effects of aridity.

Stable oxygen isotope ratios from enamel bioapatite reflect the δ18O composition of drinking water and food73,74. The oxygen composition of water is impacted by varied water sources, including rivers, lakes, and groundwater, and environmental factors, such as temperature, elevation, and rainfall75. In arid or semi-arid regions of the Middle East, including the Deh Luran Plain, water sources at different altitudes can exhibit varying δ18O values due to differences in precipitation patterns, evaporation rates, and proximity to bodies of freshwater74,76. In semi-arid or arid areas, herders tend to move their livestock to higher altitudes during the dry season to access better grazing lands, where water is more abundant and C3 vegetation predominates62,71. Livestock consume water from different sources (surface water, plants), with lower δ18O values compared to those at lower altitudes, resulting in corresponding changes in their own tissue δ18O values77,78. Stable oxygen isotope analysis of tooth enamel bioapatite provides a temporal record of the animals’ intake of water and vegetation, which might indicate different management practices. For example, animals grazing at higher altitudes with limited water availability may consume vegetation with lower δ18O values, while animals at lower altitudes with more access to water sources may consume vegetation with higher δ18O values79.

Another practice that may result in changes to oxygen isotope ratios is the manipulation of birthing seasons59,80–82. In the Middle East, access to water and food resources can be limited, thus manipulating the birth season of livestock can be a viable strategy to optimize milk availability over longer periods of the year83,84. This could involve intentionally delaying or advancing the timing of animal births to coincide with periods of abundant resources, such as seasonal rainfall or the availability of fertile grazing land. As a result, the oxygen isotopic composition of animal tissues may exhibit δ18O values that reflect the isotopic composition of the water and vegetation available at different times of the year80,82. Variation in season of birth is evident when we compare sequential δ18O values, specifically where the maxima and minima values fall along the enamel root junction85.

Incremental δ13C and δ18O series of individuals with > 3 data points

In most environments, sequential stable carbon and oxygen isotope values of herbivores are expected to follow a corresponding sinusoidal curve in which the δ18O maxima aligns with the δ13C maxima. In concert, the δ18O minima aligns with the δ13C minima. This results from herbivores ingesting plants over multiple seasons, essentially, in the summer months, water becomes more δ18O-enriched with increasing temperatures; C4 plants are more likely to thrive in dry, hot environments than C3 plants, causing δ13C values to increase during the summer. During the cooler months, water becomes δ18O-depleted, and a mix of C3 and C4 plants growing in the landscape result in a depletion of δ13C.

Among individuals with more than five data points, a naturally occurring pattern was observed for two sheep: OC9 and OC11. Sheep OC9 follows a strong sinusoidal curve, with δ18O-maxima (representing summer months) that aligns with the δ13C-maxima, which is similar to Sheep OC11, an individual whose incremental samples are only weakly sinusoidal in nature (potentially due to small sample sizes or tooth length).

Conversely, three individuals (OC7, 16, and 18) have δ18O-maxima that correspond to δ13C-minima, which is inconsistent with the expected seasonal patterning (as discussed above). One of these individuals (OC7) is a sheep whose incremental samples for δ18O results in a very weak sinusoidal curve. Comparatively, two goats (OC16, 18) have incremental values for δ18O resulting in strong sinusoidal curves, yet the δ18O-maxima align with the δ13C-minima. Graphs visualizing carbon and oxygen results by individual are available in Supplemental Figs. 2 through 7.

Birth seasonality

Using a custom R script to estimate birth timing with a cosine-fitting approach86(see Methods, Supplemental Tables 2, and Supplemental R Code), we modeled a total of seven second molars, all of which exhibited three or more oxygen isotope values. While we modeled each in terms of birth seasonality (see Supplemental Table 2), only two molars returned R-squared values above 0.7, which is what we chose as the lowest value for goodness of fit. These include a Middle Uruk goat (OC16) which had an R2 = 0.82, which placed its birth in February (Fig. 8; Supplemental Fig. 9). The second, a Late Uruk sheep (OC14) had an R2 = 0.74 and the model placed their birth in April (Fig. 8; Supplemental Fig. 8). OC14 was between 4 and 6 years old at its death, and OC16 was between 1 and 2 years of age11. Overall, this demonstrates that the births of sheep and goats were taking place in at least two seasons, winter and spring.

Discussion

Located in a transitional piedmont zone in the foothills of the Zagros Mountains, Tepe Farukhabad offered convenient access to varied environmental zones, including mountains, foothills, and lowlands that included brackish wetlands. There are two distinct herding practices observed during the Middle and Late Uruk phases, evident from a pronounced separation in carbon isotope values in this study. This indicates that herders utilized distinct environments across different phases of the Uruk period.

Among sheep and goats from the Middle Uruk with three or more data points, stable carbon values exhibit a split at − 7.3‰, with three individuals in Middle Uruk Group 1 (MU1) (OC8, 16, 19) having carbon values above − 7.3‰ and in Middle Uruk Group 2 (MU2) (OC3, 5, 9, 17, 18) having values below − 7.3‰. One sheep (OC7) falls between these groups with values from − 5 to − 8.9‰. The absence of an overlap in carbon values between MU1 and MU2 suggests that these two groups of animals were managed in environments with differing proportions of C3 and C4 vegetation. Individuals in group MU2 with carbon values below − 7.3‰ consumed a diet that was considerably less enriched in 13C, suggesting these individuals had access to primarily C3 plants. Conversely, individuals in group MU1 with carbon values above − 7.3‰ consumed a diet that was more enriched in 13C, indicating they were managed in an area with a greater proportion of C4 plants, or were foddered over the winter with plants cut during dry or hot months.

Although Late Uruk sheep do not display an absolute divide in carbon values, as the Middle Uruk animals did, there is a pronounced difference observed in the carbon isotope values for the six sheep with three or more data points. In Late Uruk Group 1 (LU1) (OC14 and OC15) animals had higher carbon values (ranging from − 4.7 to − 8.2‰) than Late Uruk Group 2 (LU2) (OC10 and 11; range from − 7.9 to − 10.2‰), thus the break between these groups fell closer to − 7.9‰. As in the earlier phase, livestock from the Late Uruk were also managed in pastures with different proportions of C3/C4 plants or were being foddered.

The stable oxygen isotope values from Middle Uruk livestock further indicate the utilization of two environments. While there was some overlap in oxygen isotope values, the MU1 group (OC8, 16, and 19) had a lower range of values (from 2.2 to − 3.5‰) than the MU2 group (OC3, 5, 9, 17, and 18) who had a range of δ18O from 6.9 to 1.2‰, with a single outlying value of − 4.0‰ for OC18. In terms of oxygen isotope values, there was a good amount of overlap, thus OC7 could have easily fit into either group MU1 or MU2. As δ18O values of precipitation generally decrease with increasing elevation, the higher values exhibited by MU2 (OC3, 5, 9, 17, and 18) suggest that they were living in drier conditions, possibly ingesting water from shallow ponds or brackish water sources, such as a marshy area33,34,36. Comparatively, the lower values exhibited by MU1 (OC8,16,19) could suggest that they were located in a slightly wetter location or accessing deeper bodies of water, for example from a larger river, a well, or a spring. Variation in δ18O between MU1 and MU2 indicates that they had access to different water sources. Similarly, Late Uruk livestock had a similar split in δ18O values, with slightly more overlap. Group LU1 (OC14, 15) had values ranging from 1.9 to − 2.3‰ and group LU2 (OC10, 11) had slightly higher values ranging from (0.5 to 4.0‰). Similarly, this suggests that LU1 and LU2 accessed different water sources.

The amplitudes in δ13C and δ18O are lower than we expected for this arid region. Specifically, our expectation was that sheep and goats would have δ18O amplitudes approaching 5 or 6‰, given the high variation in modern precipitation in the region that ranges from ~ 1 to − 16‰87. Further, in other areas of the world with high proportions of C4 plants there tends to be greater variation in δ13C values over a full year; for example, in a sample set from Greece carbon isotope values ranged from 2.3 to 7.679. However, the amplitudes for δ13C and δ18O (of sheep and goats) at these sites ranged from 0.9 to 3.8‰ (average of 1.8‰) and 0.5 to 9.5‰ (average of 3.2‰), respectively. Thus, it seems that there was a dampening of these values, likely due to foddering of livestock with dry chaff.

Group MU1 (OC8, 16, 19) had δ18O maxima that corresponded with δ13C minima. Thus, these caprines were not ingesting naturally occurring vegetation year-round. Instead, their δ13C values suggest that they were ingesting a greater proportion of C4 vegetation. This suggests that either they were being herded at low elevations during the winter months or that they were being foddered over the winter months with plant matter that was enriched in 13C (possibly grass cut at the end of the summer when there was a greater proportion of C4 vegetation). During the summer months they were likely ingesting locally available plants, possibly at higher altitudes. If pastures were at lower elevations in summer, where there was a greater abundance of C4 vegetation available, then they might have also been foddered with small amounts of C3 plant chaff that led to the dampening of carbon isotope variation. Their water source seems quite stable over time with a narrow range of amplitude (from 1.4 to 4.2‰), suggesting that they were herded in a slightly wetter or colder locale than those in groups MU2 and LU2. Group LU1 (OC14, 15) was quite similar to MU1, with δ18O maxima that corresponded with δ13C minima, suggesting a similar management strategy.

Comparatively, the MU2 group (OC3, 5, 9, 17, 18) and LU2 group (OC10, 11) caprines were likely pastured at lower elevations closer to Tepe Farukhabad, as their comparatively lower carbon isotope values indicate greater access to C3 plants. It may be that these caprines were being foddered with the dry leftover chaff from domesticated C3 grains, as several of them have δ18O maxima that corresponded with δ13C minima (OC5, 9, 10, 17, 18). This is clear for OC18, which has lower carbon values over the summer months when there should be a greater proportion of C4 vegetation on the landscape. The lack of intense seasonal variation in δ18O, especially for the LU2 group, suggests that they had access to a regular source of water, but one that was in a drier locale than where the MU1 and LU1 caprines were located.

These distinct herding practices observed in the Middle and Late Uruk include a divide between caprine management strategies that stayed relatively stable over time. One set of caprines (MU2/LU2) were pastured at lower elevations in dry landscapes near flowing water and foddered over the hottest part of the summer with chaff from C3 domesticates. A second set (MU1/LU1) was ingesting a greater proportion of 13C enriched vegetation over the summer, which then increased over the winter months. Thus, they were ingesting a greater proportion of C4 plants over the winter, either at lower elevations or potentially foddered with grass cut at the end of the summer. During the summer months they were likely ingesting locally available plants, maybe at higher altitudes, and possibly small amounts of C3 plant chaff as well. Although Late Uruk individuals do not display a complete divide in carbon values as the Middle Uruk animals did, there is a pronounced difference observed in the carbon isotope values for the caprines.

The presence of both caprine management strategies across the Uruk period suggests that an array of strategies was maintained amid the cultural and environmental instability that characterized the shift from the Middle to Late Uruk. Herders did not widely adhere to a single method of animal management, even as cultural influences arrived from Uruk and the population of the Deh Luran Plain aggregated at Tepe Farukhabad during the Late Uruk36. No single model of animal management prevailed as the most productive or efficient. Rather, the sustained practice of foddering throughout the Middle and Late Uruk suggests that some caprine management strategies (MU2 and LU2) were based on an agropastoral model, wherein animals and crops were managed concurrently, thus limiting the need for movement of the herd. Meanwhile, other caprines were likely more mobile (MU1 and LU1), indicated by slightly lower δ18O from accessing a wetter locale, suggesting that they were not necessarily tied to agricultural activities at Tepe Farukhabad.

Due to the preservation of dental enamel, our ability to drill sequential samples was minimized. Thus, when attempting to model birth seasonality, very few individuals were available for analysis. There were two individuals with reliable birth seasonality models, OC14 and OC16, which were born in the winter (February) and early spring (April), respectively. This suggests that the season of lambing was extended over several months. This might not be all that unusual, as the locale often has seasonal temperatures reaching 60 ℉ (16 ℃) at this time of year. However, it does indicate that milk availability was extended over a longer period than typical for animals born in the spring59,88.

The age-at-death distribution of sheep and goats in this study further elucidates the pastoral strategies utilized in the Uruk period. After sheep and goats reach their maximum adult weight between one to two years old, it becomes inefficient to continue investing resources in their care if they are being raised to produce meat. Therefore, animals cared for past the age of three were likely raised for their secondary products, namely dairy and wool89,90. In the early phases (EU, MU), only three out of thirteen animals were culled between the ages of one and two, indicating they were raised solely for their meat. The other ten were culled between the ages of three and six, suggesting they produced secondary products. By the later phase (LU), animals were kept well past maturity to produce secondary products, as all were culled between the ages of four and six. The predominance of older animals across all three phases aligns with the growing regional emphasis on secondary products that occurred in the fourth millennium BCE12,14.

In all, this study expanded limited isotopic data available from Late Chalcolithic Iran and revisited the faunal remains from a legacy collection excavated in 1968. Despite having small datasets due to poor sample preservation, we presented new insights into ancient pastoral strategies and human-animal relationships at a pivotal point in the history of southwest Asia. The Uruk period brought considerable regional sociopolitical and climatic changes, including the rise of cities, centralized administration, writing, and aridification. Locally, the residents of Tepe Farukhabad also navigated their incorporation into the Uruk sphere of influence, shifting settlement patterns in the Deh Luran Plain, and an increased demand to export raw materials like bitumen36. Additionally, a regional intensification in caprine pastoralism constituted a core part of the secondary products revolution12,14.

The heterogeneity observed in stable carbon and oxygen isotope values indicates that herders engaged in a variety of herding strategies throughout the Uruk period. No single strategy dominated; some animals (MU1 and LU1) were foddered and herded to different altitudes (vertical transhumance) to access seasonally available pastures, a strategy reminiscent of the mobile pastoral model proposed to have developed during this period to provision cities4,21. These individuals may have served as seasonal purveyors of goods and communication between the settlements in the Deh Luran Plain3. However, others (MU2 and LU2) were less mobile and were managed at lower altitudes, allowing herders to manage crops alongside their herds. This arrangement is consistent with agropastoral models which emphasize agricultural and pastoral activities as mutually constitutive, as the animals’ manure fertilized the fields they would later be foddered from23,29–31,91. Further, evidence of local animal management from Tepe Farukhabad contradicts models which position herders as the nomadic counterparts to settled agriculturalists22. Although these results would be benefitted by additional data from contemporary settlements in southwest Iran, the persistence of several animal management strategies from the Middle to Late Uruk suggests that herders were vital members of settled agricultural communities and did not conform to one model of herd management amid the many transitions of the Uruk period.

Methods

Fauna sampled for analysis

Faunal remains from this study were sourced from the legacy collection from the 1968 excavations at Tepe Farukhabad. Nineteen caprine mandibular molars were selected from the archaeological layers dating to the Early, Middle, and Late Uruk occupational phases (Supplemental Table 1). Of the nineteen, fifteen were identified to be from the genus Ovis and four were from the genus Capra92. Second and third molars were preferred over first molars as they erupt later in life, thus the carbon and oxygen isotope values from enamel will not be impacted by suckling and weaning93. Only mandibular molars were selected as they grow together more tightly than maxillary molars, thus preserving the development chronology and allowing for the reconstruction of herd management strategies during their lifetimes94. As enamel develops over the lifespan, the teeth were sampled sequentially to investigate changes in environment and diet over the period of a year95. To estimate the age of the animal at death, tooth wear was analyzed for enamel erosion, following the protocol outlined by Payne (1973)11.

Sampling procedure

Sample preparation was conducted at the Ancient Protein and Isotope Laboratory at the University of Michigan. Prior to sampling, the surfaces of the teeth were cleaned with a hand drill to remove surface contaminants. Tooth enamel carbonate samples were then prepared following the sequential sampling protocol outlined in Ventresca Miller et al.96. Using a Dremel drill equipped with a diamond-coated drill bit, a series of 1–2 mm wide horizontal bands was drilled incrementally along the buccal surface of the tooth from the root to the crown. The number of bands per tooth varied depending on tooth size and fragility but ranged from n = 2 to n = 11. The resulting powdered enamel was collected on weighing paper, transferred to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube, and weighed, ideally falling between 5 and 8 mg. Sampling surfaces were cleaned with acetic acid between samples, and the drill tip was rinsed with acetic acid and deionized water between samples.

All enamel samples were then washed with 0.1 M acetic acid and agitated using an electronic agitator (vortex) until a cloudy consistency was achieved. After ten minutes, the samples were spun in a centrifuge until the bioapatite separated from the acid and settled at the bottom of the tube. The acetic acid was then removed and replaced with deionized (Milli-Q) water to rinse the sample. Samples were agitated (vortexed) and then spun in the centrifuge before replacing the water; this process was repeated four additional times. After the final rinse, samples were sealed with punctured parafilm, frozen overnight, and the next day placed in a freeze dryer to remove any remaining liquid. Samples were weighed again to determine the amount of bioapatite remaining. Samples were run on a Delta C Plus mass spectrometer with a Kiel IV in the Paleoclimate and Common Era Laboratory at the University of Michigan.

Modeling of oxygen isotopes

Birth seasonality was modeled using a cosine-fitting approach following the method developed by Chazin et al.86 for reconstructing livestock birth timing from sequential δ18O values measured along the tooth enamel growth axis. We developed custom R scripts to fit cosine functions to intra-tooth δ18O profiles using non-linear least squares regression (see Supplemental Table 2; Supplemental R Code). The model estimates the amplitude, phase shift, and periodicity of the isotopic signal, allowing the timing of birth to be inferred relative to the annual cycle. δ18O series with three or more data points were modeled utilizing a cosine function to estimate birth season. Only second molars were modeled as they form predictably over one year, while third molars exhibit more inter-individual variability and form over a period longer than a year, thereby recording more than one seasonal cycle85,93,97.

Model performance was evaluated using goodness-of-fit metrics (R2), where values closer to 1 indicate a stronger fit between the modeled curve and the observed isotopic data (R2 = 1 representing a perfect fit), and only statistically robust fits were retained for interpretation. As seasonal reconstructions of birth seasons are less reliable when profiles contain fewer than twelve samples, we assessed the quality of each cosine fit model and report the coefficient of determination (R2) for each individual. The R2 value, quantifies how well the fitted cosine curve explains the observed variation in δ18O. For example, higher R2 values (closer to 1) indicate a stronger model fit and thus greater confidence in the seasonal interpretation, while lower values suggest caution in interpreting modeled birth timing due to poor fit or limited data resolution. Estimated birth timing was then standardized to a calendar framework to facilitate comparison across individual animals (Fig. 8; see also Supplemental Figs. 8, 9).

The condition of many teeth was not ideal for sequential sampling which would allow for a high-resolution study of seasonality; in several cases, once drilling began, the internal structure of the tooth began to deteriorate, likely due to prolonged exposure to arid environmental conditions. This prevented a higher number of samples from being collected. Of the 96 samples sent for analysis on the mass spectrometer, 76 returned viable results, averaging four samples per individual. Our results include sheep and goats from three occupational phases, the Early (n = 1), Middle (n = 12), and Late (n = 6) Uruk. Four animals produced only one sample; these values were included in summary statistics and graphs but omitted from broader discussion, as the limited sample size prevents interpretations of seasonality and movement. Due to the limited number of samples from the Early Uruk, the results from the Early and Middle Uruk will be presented together unless noted otherwise.
