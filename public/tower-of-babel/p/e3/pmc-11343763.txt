Inverse design of bistable composite laminates with switching tunneling method for global optimization
Authors: Katherine S. Riley, Mark H. Jhon, Hortense Le Ferrand, Dan Wang, Andres F. Arrieta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Bistability enables adaptive designs with tunable deflections for applications including morphing wings, robotic grippers, and consumer products. Composite laminates may be designed to exhibit bistability due to pre-strains that develop during the processing of the polymer matrix, enabling fast reconfiguration between two stable shapes. Unfortunately, designing bistable laminates is challenging because of their highly nonlinear behavior. Here, we propose the Switching Tunneling Method to address this challenge by alternating between gradient-based local minimization and tunneling search phases, with the enhancement of objective expression switching to improve numerical conditioning. Results demonstrate high effectiveness compared to existing optimizers; the Switching Tunneling Method achieves a 99% success rate in finding all energy minima across general composite layups. Additionally, our method facilitates the inverse design of variable pre-strain fields, enabling bioinspired, positive Gaussian curvatures, which are not possible with conventional pre-strain laminates. Validations through both finite element analysis and 3D printed samples confirm the optimal designs.

Introduction

While most structures are monostable with a single stable configuration, multistable structures can reversibly morph between multiple stable configurations and maintain each configuration without externally applied force. This enables efficient adaptation to changing operating conditions with simplified actuation and sensing systems, making multistable structures of interest across myriad applications, ranging from airfoils that change shape in different aerodynamic conditions1,2 to grippers that hold objects without continuous actuation3–6, and self-deploying stents7. However, the highly nonlinear behavior that enables this shape change functionality also makes multistable structures very difficult to design. The lack of an efficient inverse design methodology for multistable structures to have specific deflections or curvatures in their stable states is a major obstacle to their widespread, practical implementation.

Bistable structures are an important subset of multistable structures with two stable states. Bistability in thin, initially flat composite laminates arises from mismatched pre-strains8–10, typically using unidirectional fiber reinforced composites in an antisymmetric layup, such as [0°/90°] or [±45°] (Fig. 1a). Upon curing and cooling, orthotropic material properties create contrasting residual pre-strain fields in different layers, leading to bifurcation of the equilibrium path into two branches representing two co-existing stable states (Fig. 1b). The strain energy landscape exhibits two minima, each corresponding to a stable state, and an unstable equilibrium at the peak of the energy barrier (Fig. 1c). In this type of bistable laminate, conventional unidirectional fiber layers lead to cylindrical stable shapes with constant and nearly zero Gaussian curvatures, meaning one principal curvature ≈ 0 (Fig. 1a)8,9,11. Non-cylindrical shapes have only been demonstrated by imposing an initial doubly curved geometry via the laminate processing12,13, or by using a specialized machine to mechanically apply a distributed pre-stress field to certain layers of a laminate during the curing process14,15. This is in contrast to biological examples that achieve complex curvatures through a bilayer of spatially varying pre-strain fields. For example, Venus flytrap leaves16 can snap to a closed shape with positive Gaussian curvature to trap prey. However, previous attempts to imitate the Venus flytrap have been limited to conventional pre-strain and failed to demonstrate the positive Gaussian curvature of the plant5,17–20.

Recent advances in manufacturing methods allow for more complex reinforcement schemes and resultant pre-strain fields. For example, variable stiffness composites with spatially varying fiber orientations (Fig. 1d) are commonly used to adjust load paths and alleviate stress concentrations21–24 and may be fabricated by piecewise layup arrangement25–29, advanced tow placement30,31, or composite 3D printing32,33. Cast composites with reinforcing platelets aligned along magnetic field lines34–36 and 3D printing with material shape memory37–39 enable curvilinear pre-strain patterns and allow for more complex macroscale geometries. These advancements in manufacturing greatly expand the design possibilities for bistable structures, indicating the potential for synthetic bistable laminates to mimic biological stable shapes.

While the accessible design space for pre-strained bistable structures has vastly increased, the design process remains slow and iterative, impeding progress40. Previous optimization efforts for bistable laminates focused on bending stiffness41, load-carrying capacity42, and aerodynamic performance1,43. Optimization studies on variable stiffness laminates (Fig. 1d) have focused on maximizing bending stiffness and buckling strength44–50, as well as reliability51,52. However, little research has focused on the component shape, which is critical for biomedical and robotic applications. Currently, bistable structures are largely designed either by choosing from a narrow set of simple, well-known unidirectional layups or through a forward, iterative design process using finite element analysis (FEA), which typically requires a priori knowledge of the expected stable states11. The inverse problem for designing the required pre-strain distribution to match desired stable shapes is highly complex due to its intricate nonlinear energy landscapes with multiple minima. Moreover, variable pre-strain designs pose additional challenges due to the added design variables and manufacturing constraints compared to conventional pre-strain problems49,53.

Global optimization algorithms—such as evolutionary54,55, swarm intelligence56–58, and multistart46,59 algorithms—are effective for solving multiple minima problems, and have been used in previous bistable laminate optimization work42,60–62. However, these methods are computationally expensive for large design spaces and rely on a high number of initial start points63. The tunneling algorithm63–65 is a gradient-based global optimizer alternating between local minimization and tunneling for as-good solutions to identify all the global optima. Versions of tunneling phase constructions include classical64,66, tabu67, and exponential65. The exponential tunneling algorithm has successfully addressed classical mathematical global optimization problems, such as the 2D Shubert function with up to 760 local minima and 18 global minima65, and practical problems, such as phase stability analysis68 and Gibbs free energy minimization69. Its blend of gradient-based minimization and effective handling of multiple minima makes it promising for bistable laminate design.

In this paper, we propose the switching tunneling method (STM) for the inverse design problem of finding the optimal bilayer pre-strain fields to match targeted deflections and bioinspired curvatures in two stable states. The STM is described in the “Methods” section together with analytical strain energy derivation and optimization model formulations. Results and discussion of implementing the STM to both find the stable states of a given layup and optimize to match target stable shapes are then presented with FEA and 3D printing experimental validation, followed by the conclusions. The established optimization framework is readily extendable to general inverse design problems described by nonlinear functions.

Optimization problems

Two optimization problems are presented: solving for the stable states of a given layup, and finding the layup to match target stable shapes. Solving for the stable states is a difficult, highly nonlinear optimization problem. A bistable design may have stable states at unequal energy levels, and monostable, symmetric laminates have much lower internal energy than bistable, unsymmetric laminates. Switching tunneling method for global optimization

The shape matching problem is highly nonlinear and has many local minima at the stable states of feasible layups. Furthermore, due to the g1(b) = ∇2Π term, the objective function F(b) has both very high function values away from minima and very narrow minima valleys. To tackle this challenge, we propose the Switching Tunneling Method (STM) which alternates between local minimization and tunneling search phases and strategically switches between primary and secondary functions. This approach enhances performance and precision based on proximity to a minimum, utilizing different switching strategies for the minimization and tunneling phases.

Tunneling optimization is a strategy to find all global minima when there may be many local minima, as well as many global minima with equivalent objective function values65,71 and has previously been used to find the stable energy states of chemical systems68,69. The STM implements switching in both the minimization and tunneling phases with distinct strategies. Local minimization uses the default primary function Fp (switch OFF) until convergence. Once convergence is reached with Fp, the routine switches to the secondary function Fs (switch ON) to drive the minimization to the exact minimum. During tunneling, the algorithm defaults to the primary function to calculate T(x) ← Tp(x) (switch OFF) when determining the search direction, d, and acceptable step size, α, as it searches for a zero. If the secondary function, Fs, is not too flat, as evaluated by ∣∣Fs(xk)∣∣ ≥ ξ where ξ ≥ 0, this indicates that the search is likely near an equivalent or better minimum than the current F*. The algorithm then switches to T(x) ← Ts(x) (switch ON), which is more useful close to a minimum. Implementation

We code the STM in Python, with the layout loosely based on the code from67,73. For the local minimizer, we use the gradient-based scipy package sequential least squares programming (SLSQP) function74. The tunneling root finding algorithm is coded following that in ref. 65, with the step size acceptance conditions changed to the standard Wolfe criteria75. All optimizations are run on the Purdue University Bell server76, see Supplementary Methods.

Results and discussion

The proposed STM’s effectiveness and efficiency are compared with existing optimizers using the exercise of finding all the stable states of a bilayer laminate with constant pre-strain. Next, stable shape matching is performed for targeted deflections and Gaussian curvatures, with experimental validation using Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) 3D printing37. We consider a 1.968 × 1.968 × 0.0552 in3 (50 × 50 × 1.4 mm3) laminate printed with polylactic acid (PLA) and activated in a water bath at 149 °F. Imperial units are chosen for convenient scaling (see Supplementary Methods). The material properties are as follows: longitudinal, transverse and shear moduli of E1 = 2291.596 psi, E2 = 2291.596 psi and G12 = 881.383 psi; Poisson’s ratio of ν12 = 0.3; and the coefficients of thermal expansion along longitudinal, transverse and shear directions of α11 = −0.00054/°F, α22 = 0 and α12 = 0. Detailed print settings are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Shape matching optimization set 2: targeting positive Gaussian curvatures in both states

Designing bistable structures with two positive Gaussian curvatures of κG = κ1κ2 > 0, is highly challenging. This condition implies that both principal curvatures face the same direction, like a bowl or sphere. While κG > 0 is found in biological pre-strained structures, it is not possible with conventional pre-strained designs, as noted above. By Gauss’s Theorema Egregium, transforming a flat (κG = 0) surface to a surface with nonzero Gaussian curvature (κG ≠ 0) requires stretching (extensibility) in addition to bending. For a thin structure, bending is significantly more energetically favorable than stretching, thus resulting in the preferred deflection mode for this class of structures78. Prior to the equilibrium bifurcation point (Fig. 1b), the conventional pre-strain laminate is monostable in a shallow saddle shape (κG < 0). However, after the bifurcation point, the bistable conventional shell has two cylindrical stable states with one principal curvature ≈ 0 away from the edges of the shell, and κG ≈ 079.

Previous attempts to imitate biological structures, such as the fast snapping leaves of the Venus flytrap, have used bistable composites to achieve fast, reversible morphing but suffered from the limitations of conventional pre-strain. These designs fail to achieve the positive Gaussian curvature that allows the natural flytrap’s leaves to fully enclose prey5,17–20. Natural Venus flytrap leaves grow in a bilayer with contrasting, spatially varying pre-strain fields16,80,81. Here, we demonstrate how the STM allows us to achieve positive Gaussian curvatures in a square, variable pre-strain laminate, sharing the same dimensions as the previous example. Conclusion

We present the switching tunneling method (STM) as an efficient optimization approach to solve both the stable states of a given laminate and the design problem for bistable, pre-strained laminates with bioinspired, nonzero Gaussian curvatures. Bistable laminates are typically designed using a priori knowledge-based iterative forward design loops with FEA, or costly heuristic optimization algorithms. Instead, the STM combines the gradient-based global optimization two-phase tunneling approach with objective expression switching to handle the highly nonlinear problem with multiple minima. Compared to existing optimizers, the STM shows promising results, successfully solving for all equilibria in 99% of cases. Moreover, we implement the STM to achieve inverse design of variable pre-strain laminates targeting stable states with two positive Gaussian curvatures. This development enables the design of bistable laminates with stable shapes featuring targeted curvatures, unlocking an important new design space. Positive Gaussian curvature, unattainable with constant pre-strain over the domain, is crucial for bioinspired designs but has been challenging due to the inextensibility of structural materials favoring cylindrical shapes. The STM established here could also be applied to laminates with more complex pre-strain patterns, geometries, and boundary conditions with available analytical models, including those that implement alternative analytical formulation strategies27,28,50. Implementing the STM with a higher-order model would be critical when the effects of the natural boundary conditions at the edges of the laminate significantly impact the solution. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the selected analytical model loses accuracy when the deflected shape at the edges is markedly different from that in the center due to the lower-order polynomial shape functions selected. This is a direct consequence of employing the Rayleigh-Ritz method to approximate the mid-plane displacements, which requires shape functions (Eq. (2)) that only need to satisfy geometric boundary conditions. As extensively done for modeling bistable laminates8–10,27,28,41,42, we utilize the Rayleigh-Ritz method as reasonable accuracy displacement approximations are obtained with low computational costs. More advanced formulations presenting a good compromise between accuracy and computational efficiency that better capture the effects of the geometric and natural boundary conditions may be used to further improve the attainable results82. While implementing such high accuracy models into our formulation could yield improved results, this is left to future work.

It is important to note that any optimizer can only solve the problem it is given. Therefore, there are several scenarios in which the STM may fail. If the analytical formula or numerical solver cannot adequately approximate the original system, including the boundary conditions, the optimal solution may not match experimental or high-fidelity numerical solutions. Additionally, if a target shape is not available in the chosen design space, the STM only converges to the solution closest to the target. However, the proposed STM is a very general design strategy that applies to a variety of complex systems with multiple local/global optima, such as liquid crystal elastomers83, inflatable tubes84, chemical systems68,69, and neural networks with tunable hyperparameters85. To be extended to one of these systems, the STM only requires a suitable analytical or numerical model—with available gradients and acceptable efficiency—to represent a significantly rich design space.
