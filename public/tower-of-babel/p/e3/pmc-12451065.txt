Geographic Proximity of Family Members and the Provision of Unpaid and Paid Care for Older Adults Not Living With a Spouse: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Joanne Spetz, Lei Chen, Leslie Ross, Donovan T. Maust
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThis study examines the association of family geographic proximity with probabilities of older adults who do not live with a spouse receiving family, unpaid non‐family, and paid care.MethodsRestricted geographic sample person and other person files from Round 5 of the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS). We calculated descriptive statistics and estimated multivariate logistic regressions with the primary outcome of whether a respondent who did not live with a spouse received help from a family member and secondary outcomes of receiving help from an unpaid non‐family member or paid worker (n = 2084). Geographic proximity of family members was defined by family living in the same urban area or, for those not in urban areas, the same county.Results86.4% of the study sample had family in the same geographic area. Those with a family helper were more likely to have family living in the same geographic area compared to those without a family helper (88.7% vs. 73.3%, p < 0.001). In adjusted models, respondents with at least one family member in the same geographic area had significantly higher odds of receiving help from family (OR = 2.03, p = 0.006) but no difference in receipt of unpaid non‐family help (OR = 1.12, p = 0.682) or paid help (OR = 0.73, p = 0.257).ConclusionWhen family members live nearby, older adults who do not live with a spouse have a higher probability of receiving support from family members; however, if they do not have family nearby, there is no greater likelihood that they will have unpaid non‐family or paid support. Older adults who do not live with a spouse need more program and policy support, especially if they do not have family nearby.

Introduction

By 2050, the number of older adults will exceed the number of adolescents and young people globally [1]. This demographic shift will challenge most nations and, accordingly, the World Health Organization has recommended that nations establish and strengthen sustainable long‐term care systems, including both health care and social care [2]. Growth in the population of older adults will contribute to rising demand for assistance with activities of daily living (ADLs; e.g., eating, bathing, dressing, toileting, and transferring) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs; i.e., higher‐order activities like medication management and food preparation that allow a person to live independently) [3]. In the United States, for the 70% of individuals 65 years or older who develop significant functional needs before they die [4], some of this care may be provided by public programs or private insurance, but nearly three‐quarters is “informally” provided by family, friends, and neighbors [5].

Spouses provide many hours of care for those needing functional support and, thus, older adults who do not live with a spouse are at particular risk of not receiving sufficient support for their ADL and IADL needs [6, 7]. They are at greater risk for emergency admissions, high‐cost hospitalizations, and mortality than are older adults living with others [8], although high levels of neighborhood cohesion have a protective effect against these risks [9]. Digital technologies also have the potential to mitigate risks associated with living alone, but research to date has been inconclusive about the protective effects of digital technology access among older adults living alone [10]. Prior research of people living with dementia—including both those living with a spouse and those not—has associated closer proximity of family members with a lower likelihood of using formal paid care and of moving to a nursing home [11, 12]. This study adds to the literature by focusing on the provision of both unpaid and paid care for older adults with ADL and IADL needs who do not live with a spouse. This study uses geographic information in nationally representative survey data to estimate the effect of geographic proximity on the probabilities of receiving unpaid and paid care among unmarried and widowed people, and married people who do not live with their spouse, to add to our understanding of complex decisions surrounding ADL and IADL needs.

Methods

Data Source

Data were from the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS), which follows a nationally representative cohort of Medicare‐enrolled persons in the United States aged 65 and older. Each survey round captures an Other Person (OP) file of individuals identified by the NHATS respondent in the course of survey completion [13], such spouses/partners, household members, children/stepchildren, and helpers with respondent self‐care or other needs. The OP file includes information about the “other person”, such as their role relative to the respondent and hours of care provided.

This analysis uses Round 5, which was fielded in 2015. We accessed the restricted data files that include the location of the NHATS respondent as well as those individuals represented in the OP file. Geographic data include the city, state, county, and census tract. Because all data are secondary, the Institutional Review Board determined the study was exempt under Category 4 and certified that it approved the Restricted Data Protection Plan provided for our study.

Sample

We began with 7070 community‐dwelling respondents who completed the Round 5 survey. This sample was then limited to those who did not have a spouse living with them, who needed assistance with ADLs (eating, bathing, or toileting) or IADLs (laundry, shopping, meal preparation, paying bills, or tracking prescription medication), and identified at least one OP. Where the NHATS respondent was married but did not live with the spouse or partner identified in the OP, we assumed that these non‐cohabitating partners would not help with care, and thus excluded them. The final analytic data file consisted of 2084 unique respondents (Figure S1).

Variables

Outcomes. We merged data about OPs to their respective respondents, with separate indicators for three categories of OPs: family members (grouped as daughters, sons, and other relatives), unpaid non‐family members, and paid helpers. The primary outcome variable was whether the SP had a family member providing help, with the secondary outcomes of help from either an unpaid non‐family member or a paid person.

Primary Predictor. The explanatory variable of interest was whether the respondent had any family residing in the same Core‐Based Statistical Area (CBSA) or in the same county if they did not live in a CBSA. A CBSA consists of one or more counties anchored by an urban center of at least 10,000 people plus adjacent counties that are socioeconomically tied to the urban center by commuting. A CBSA is an appropriate measure of geographic proximity (in the absence of specific location data) because it combines counties that are contiguous and for which there are inter‐county economic and commuting linkages. We determined whether the respondent had family members in the same geographic region by matching their geographic information to that of individuals in the OP; we created an indicator variable for whether the respondent had one or more family members in the same geographic area.

Covariates. Other variables included respondent gender (male/female), age (65‐74 years, 75‐84 years, 85 years and older), race (White non‐Hispanic, Black non‐Hispanic, Hispanic, other), living arrangement (alone, with others), rurality (metropolitan, non‐metropolitan), and Medicaid enrollment. We used the combination of ADL and IADL impairment to reflect 3 levels of functional need, from least to most: (1) no ADL needs and one or two IADL needs; (2) no ADL needs but three or more IADL needs; and (3) any of four ADL (eating, bathing, toileting, and dressing) needs [7, 14]. We followed NHATS guidance to create a variable reflecting dementia classification (no impairment, possible, or probable) [15]. Finally, we included a well‐being score calculated from 11 items that measure well‐being for older adults living in the community [16].

Data Analysis

We calculated descriptive statistics and compared the characteristics of those receiving help from family to those not receiving family help. We computed weighted statistics using weighting procedures recommended by NHATS [17]. We then estimated three separate weighted multivariate logistic regression models, in which the dependent variables were whether the SP received family help, whether the SP received non‐family help, and whether the SP received paid help. The explanatory variable was whether a family member lived in the same CBSA/county; models controlled for the other covariates. Statistical tests were two‐sided. All analyses were conducted using SAS® version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Bivariate Results

Table 1 shows the weighted analysis for characteristics for the full sample (unweighted N = 2084), respondents with a family helper (unweighted N = 1717), and respondents without a family helper (unweighted N = 367). Most people who did not live with a spouse and needed support had family in the same geographic area (86.4%), and those with a family helper were more likely to have family living in the same geographic area compared to those without a family helper (88.7% vs. 73.3%, p < 0.001). Compared to those without a family helper, a higher percentage of respondents receiving family support lived with others (59.2% vs. 26.6%, p < 0.001) and had higher levels of functional need (e.g., the prevalence of any ADL impairment was 26.5% vs. 13.7% [p < 0.001 overall] for those with vs. without family support). More of those with a family helper had possible or probable dementia than those who did not have a family helper (13.8% vs. 12% and 16.2% vs. 6.0%, respectively; p < 0.001 overall).

Multivariate Logistic Regression Results

Table 2 presents the results of multivariate logistic regression models of whether respondents living without a spouse received help from family, unpaid non‐family, and paid individuals, and Figure 1 presents the odds ratios of the association between the presence of geographically proximate family members and support from family, unpaid non‐family, and paid providers. Respondents who had one or more family members in the same geographic area had significantly higher odds of receiving help from family (OR = 2.0, p = 0.006) but no difference in the likelihood of having unpaid non‐family help (OR = 1.1, p = 0.68) or paid help (OR = 0.7, p = 0.26). Several other respondent characteristics were significant predictors of the odds of receiving family help, non‐family help, or help from a paid provider. Respondents living with others had higher odds of having a family helper (OR = 2.7, p < 0.001) but lower odds of having a non‐family helper (OR = 0.4, p < 0.001) or paid provider (OR = 0.2, p < 0.001). The severity of functional impairment was positively, but not significantly, associated with whether the respondent received family help. In contrast, the level of functional impairment was associated with higher odds of unpaid non‐family (OR = 2.3, p < 0.001; OR = 2.8, p < 0.001) and paid (OR = 7.3 and 42.3, p < 0.001 for both) help.

Discussion

Based on the weighted analyses for the sample of 2084 participants of the Round 5 NHATS survey, we estimated that, of more than 8.4 million older Americans who did not live with a spouse and had functional impairment, nearly 1.2 million (13.6%) did not have a family member that lived nearby. Geographic proximity of family members was a significant predictor of receiving family support among these individuals, which is not surprising. At the same time, the proximity of family—or lack thereof—was not associated with receiving paid support or unpaid help from non‐family. This suggests that when family members do not live nearby, non‐family support does not compensate for the lack of proximate family support, a worrisome finding given the growing need for long‐term support among community‐dwelling adults who live alone [18].

Unsurprisingly, living arrangements were a significant predictor of the type of help received, with older adults living with others more likely to receive family help and less likely to receive unpaid non‐family or paid help. Similarly, previous research shows that high‐intensity caregiving was more prevalent among care providers who lived with their care recipient [19]. Although cohabitation between adult children and older parents has traditionally been more common in Asian countries, it is becoming increasingly common in the United States as older adults live with their children to receive necessary care and support due to declining health and independence [20]. Many adult children also believe that taking care of older parents is one of their responsibilities [21].

It was unexpected that neither the presence of possible or probable dementia nor the severity of functional impairment was associated with whether an individual received family help. In contrast, higher ADL and IADL needs were associated with higher odds of receiving both unpaid non‐family help and paid help. And, while dementia was not associated with receiving paid help, it was associated with lower odds of unpaid non‐family help. Other research has reported that the use of paid help is greater among people with dementia versus those without dementia [20], and that older adults with dementia have larger caregiving networks than those without dementia [21]. The differences in these results may be related to the sample differences: our study focused on community‐dwelling older adults who did not live with a spouse and who had functional limitations, while the other studies included adults who lived with spouses or were limited to Medicare‐Medicaid dual enrollees [22, 23]. While older adults with family nearby may receive help regardless of their actual objective need, older adults who do not live with a spouse may have particular difficulty engaging non‐family social networks to identify support [24].

Finally, we found that Medicaid enrollment was not associated with support from paid caregivers. This is consistent with recent research that reported that the generosity of state Medicaid home‐ and community‐based services did not fill the gap in unmet needs between older adults living alone and those living with others [24]. Access to Medicaid services shows promise in reducing unmet care needs [25], but this is not sufficient to ensure that people who do not live with a spouse and do not have family nearby receive the support they need.

Strengths of this study include the robust NHATS methodology and the use of geographic data to identify the proximity of family members. However, a limitation is the potential of nearby family members to live just across a county line and thus not being identified as geographically proximate. In addition, this analysis did not capture whether family help was from adult children versus other family members, nor did it examine the degree to which older adults might have specific unmet care needs. Moreover, due to the limitations of using a secondary data set, we were not able to include other variables related to paid and unpaid care, such as access to and availability of other aging services, and we were not able to address causal inference due to the cross‐sectional nature of the data set. These are avenues for future research, along with whether the geographic proximity of family and support from non‐family caregivers changed related to the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Conclusion

There is a considerable proportion of individuals needing support with ADLs and IADLs, particularly those living without a spouse, who live in home and community settings. A network of family, unpaid non‐family, and paid support can help ensure care needs are met and delay transitions of these older adults to institutions, while reducing stress on family members. While the geographic proximity of family is (unsurprisingly) associated with receiving support from family, the lack of geographically proximate family is not associated with greater use of unpaid support. The challenge of supporting the care needs of family members with ADL and IADL limitations may generate even greater strain when family members do not live nearby. The fact that those without family nearby were no more likely to report receipt of non‐family or paid caregiving suggests that these older adults may benefit from additional services and supports to ensure their ability to safely age in place [22].

This suggests that clinicians and aging care leaders need to improve supportive services for people with functional needs who do not live with a spouse and do not have family nearby.
