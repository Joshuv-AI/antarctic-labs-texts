Evaluation of the extent of bone healing in mandibular distraction osteogenesis using pre-operative and post-operative imaging pixel value ratio: a cohort study
Authors: Yuhe Shen, Liangliang Kong, Weimin Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe pixel value ratio (PVR) is a metric measured on X-ray images that has been used in many studies to assess the time of final external fixator removal. The aim of this study was to investigate the value and potential influencing factors of PVR in the early phase of mandible healing by distraction osteogenesis (DO) in Pierre Robin sequence.MethodsWe performed a retrospective analysis on 342 Pierre Robin sequence patients with bilateral mandibular lengthening surgery, measured PVRs in the osteotomy position of children before surgery, and monitored PVRs in the early stage of bone regeneration at the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd month respectively after surgery. The potential effects of gender, weight, and age of fixation period were also analyzed.ResultsPVRs before osteotomy and at the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd month afterward were 0.96±0.04 (R) and 0.97±0.05 (L); 0.4±0.1 (R) and 0.42±0.1 (L); 0.68±0.11 (R) and 0.71±0.11 (L); and 0.97±0.04 (R) and 0.98±0.04 (L), respectively, which had significant differences (P<0.001). We found no significant difference in PVRs by gender. In terms of age, there was a significant difference in PVRs between children aged less than 28 days and older than 120 days before surgery. There were also significant differences between those aged less than 28 days and older than 120 days, and children aged 8–28 days and 29–60 days at the 3rd month after surgery. In terms of weight, the PVRs of children with a birth weight less than 4 kg and those with an admission weight less than 2.5 kg were significantly different.ConclusionsDuring the infancy period, the healing process of the mandibular DO focuses on the early healing phase (1–3 months). Our results showed that PVRs gradually increased during the first 3 months after mandibular osteotomy and during the process of DO. Gender had no effect on PVRs, whereas age, birth weight, and admission weight had a greater effect. The results of this study suggest that PVRs can be used as an indicator for the assessment of early lengthening bone growth in patients with mandibular lengthening and for determining whether to remove the distraction osteogenic apparatus.Keywords: Imaging pixel value ratio, distraction osteogenesis (DO), degree of mandible healing, Pierre Robin sequence

Introduction

Distraction osteogenesis (DO) is a technique for the regeneration of bone. It stimulates bone tissue regeneration and reconstruction by giving the bone tissue a tension of stretching. The pixel value ratio (PVR) is a metric measured via X-ray images that has been used in many studies to assess the time of final external fixator removal (1,2). However, all of these studies have only described its influence after long bone lengthening (3). McCarthy successfully adopted this method for craniomaxillofacial bone regeneration in 1992 (4). He performed osteotomy of the mandibular horizontal ramus, and just as in long bones, fixed extension rods at the front and back on both sides of the osteotomy line. Similar to the distraction of long bones, after a fixed period, daily distraction began, stimulating the regeneration of bone tissue and the surrounding tissues. After the regeneration was completed, another fixed period ensued. The reconstruction of the mandible tissue was completed. However, the assessment of mandible growth has always been the focus of research. The effect and potential influencing factors of PVR in the early stage of mandibular healing by DO have been rarely discussed.

Currently, there are various evaluation methods to monitor bone lengthening and bone growth during long bone DO, including standard radiography (X-ray), three-dimensional (3D) computed tomography (CT) scan, ultrasound and biomechanical assessment, and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), among others (5,6). Despite being more expensive and radiation intensive, 3D CT scan is widely used (7,8). Ultrasonic backscatter techniques have been applied in the assessment of osteoporosis (9). During the distraction phase, the cortex is incomplete; hence, ultrasound can be considered. X-ray can be used as a common tool to assess bone growth because it is inexpensive and convenient. However, all the assessments are subjective and rely closely on the experience of the clinician and an objective quantitative evaluation index of X-ray is needed.

Pixel values can be used as an evaluation metric for long bone density. Liu et al. evaluated bone mineral content (BMC), bone mineral density (BMD), and hardness in 26 patients, and concluded that pixel values of X-ray images could be used as an evaluation metric (10). In recent years, the assessment of bone scabs at different stages and during bone healing with PVR has displayed good reliability. With bone healing, the density of regenerated bone and its PVR are gradually increased, which provide new and important information for future assessment of bone healing during DO of long bone (1). Then, in the case of long bone DO, the pixel values can be used to assess the healing condition of the bone tissue. We investigated whether the same method be applied to evaluate the DO and regeneration of bone in the mandible, and concluded that it can be used to assess the healing condition of the regenerated bone in DO of the mandible. Although there is overlap between the anterior part of the mandible and the cervical vertebrae, the comparison conditions remain unchanged and the PVR is constant. Therefore, PVR is a quantifiable, simple, and reliable metric for the evaluation of tissue healing in infants and children with bone injuries. Meanwhile, we can also compare PVRs of the regenerated bone with its adjacent bone to determine whether the density of the mandible is consistent with the pre-operative normal mandible and to decide whether the extender can be removed. In our study, we used PVR to assess the maturity of the late healing tissue of the mandibles, which was used in conjunction with 3D CT to determine the time to remove the extender and reduce the retraction rate of mandibular lengthening. PVR can also be used to evaluate early bone healing in the mandibles without DO and during the initiation of DO. The early PVR assessment is helpful in understanding the degree of bone tissue healing of the mandible.

The purpose of this study was to investigate the early stage of PVR of mandible tissue during mandible lengthening and its potential influencing factors on it. This requires no additional cost to the patient, and it is a promising method for objectively measuring the healing status of mandible regeneration. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0314/rc).

Methods

Experiment design and patients

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (No. 202008080-1), and informed consent was provided by the parents or legal guardians of all individual participants. We reviewed the image data of children undergoing mandibular distraction from January 2015 to January 2022 at the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. All radiographic examinations were part of routine clinical follow-up. Low-dose pediatric imaging protocols were applied to minimize radiation exposure. As a retrospective study, all eligible patients during the study period were included, and no prior sample size calculation was performed. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) cases all had bilateral mandibular lengthening; (II) cases had their brackets removed during the 3rd month after surgery; (III) cases underwent successful mandibular distraction; and (IV) parents voluntarily agreed to their child undergoing multiple X-ray examinations. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) patients with osseous nonunion or delayed bone healing; (II) patients for whom removal of the brace was delayed due to infection; (III) missing pre-operative and post-operative data; and (IV) parents expressing reluctance for their child to undergo multiple X-ray examinations.

Finally, our study recruited a total of 342 cases. Pre-operative CT was performed. To understand the changes in bone healing after DO, we measured the pixel values at 1, 2, and 3 months post-operatively, calculated the PVR and conducted an analysis. Age and weight groups were stratified according to clinically relevant neonatal categories and previous pediatric studies to reflect developmental differences. The cases were divided into several subgroups based on factors such as gender, age, birth weight, and weight.

Surgery method and post-operative management

All children were operated on by a single pediatric plastic surgeon. DO devices were placed in the bilateral mandibles, as shown in Figure 1. Currently, there are two types of DO devices: the internal distraction device and the external distraction device (11). All of our cases were fitted with an inbuilt distraction device. Distraction was started on the 5th day after osteotomy with a traction rate of 0.6 mm each time, twice daily (1.2 mm/day total). The lengthening ended within 14–21 days in all 342 children, and the fixation period was 3 months. X-ray examinations were performed before surgery and at the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd month after surgery. The conditions for removal of the lengthening device were as follows: (I) absence of osseous nonunion and infection; (II) duration of fixation was 3 months; and (III) CT reconstruction of the mandible was complete.

Calculation of PVR in X-ray image

We first determined the osteotomy line in pre-operative X-ray image and measured the pixel values before and after the osteotomy line. All radiographs were obtained using the same imaging system and standardized exposure parameters. PVR, as a ratio, minimizes variability caused by imaging conditions. Measurements were made using Neusoft PACS/RIS version 5.5 software (Neusoft Corp., Shenyang, China) and the target area is shown in Figure 2A. First, we located the measurement area and used the midpoint between the two screws as a marker for measuring the regenerated bone, as shown in Figure 2B. Second, we archived the images and adopted the X-ray image measurement tool to measure pixel values on the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd month post-operative X-ray images, with a measurement area of 5 mm2. We took the average pixel value in the measurement area for calculation. The regenerative bone zone and its distal and proximal normal bone zones were identified by screw position, as shown in Figure 2C. Two independent observers performed measurements, and intra-/inter-observer reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients.

Pre-operative and post-operative mandibular distraction osteogenesis X-ray image. (A) An example of pre-operative mandibular distraction osteogenesis X-ray image. The red-circled areas represent measurement area which is 5 mm2. (B) An example of post-operative mandibular distraction osteogenesis X-ray image. The red-circled markers indicate the regenerated bone between two screws. (C) An example of post-operative mandibular distraction osteogenesis X-ray image. The top and bottom red-circles represent distal and proximal normal bone adjacent to regenerative bone (middle circle).

To improve the accuracy of the pixel values, the metal part of the distraction was strictly avoided to enter the measured area. First, we measured the pre-operative pixel values. We divided the pixel values at the 2 osteotomy line positions by the sum of the pixel values 1 cm before and after the osteotomy line. This way, we could obtain the pre-operative PVR. The post-operative PVR could be measured at the middle position of the extended regenerated bone, multiplied by 2, and then divided by the ratio of the regenerated bone to its distal and proximal normal bone. This yielded the post-operative PVR (the calculation area of PV is shown in Figure 3). The calculation formulas for pre-operative PVR and post-operative PVR are as follows:

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using the software SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). PVRs and its standard deviations (SD) were statistically analyzed to find out whether there was a significant difference between PVR and gender, weight, and each month in extension. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using independent t-tests, whereas multiple group comparisons were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni post hoc correction. A P value <0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference.

Results

Extension-month based subgroup analysis of PVR

We recruited a total of 342 pediatric cases for this analysis. During the first, second, and third months after the osteotomy surgery, the PVR continued to increase. One month after osteotomy lengthening, the PVRs were 0.4±0.1 on the right side and 0.42±0.1 on the left side. When extended to 3 months, the PVR values in the bone lengthening group were 0.97±0.04 on the right side and 0.98±0.04 on the left side and approached normal (≈1). These findings suggest that bone regeneration approaches normal density at approximately 3 months; however, device removal decisions should be based on combined radiographic and clinical assessment rather than PVR alone. Discussion

It is important to assess the duration of bone regeneration after DO, including maturation and stabilization into normal bone through alteration. It determines whether the extended stent can be removed after the bone reconstruction has matured; early removal can cause retraction of the bone and late removal is inconvenient for the patient. The process of maturation and reconstruction of long bones after trauma has been investigated using PVR. Elsheikh considered PVR a valuable tool to quantify the maturity of long bone regeneration and to provide an objective decision for removing intramedullary nails (12). Khan et al. conducted quantitative assessment of DO regeneration on digital radiographs using pixel density ratio (13). Bafor et al. conducted a statistical study of PVR in 42 patients who underwent lower limb lengthening and concluded that PVR is a rapid and reliable evaluation metric to objectively assess the healing status of regenerated bone and bone maturity during distraction (14). Balakumar et al. found that PVR may be a reliable indicator of bone graft fusion to aid clinical decision-making by measuring and analyzing PVR of bone grafts in 30 patients (15). Although some studies have shown gender differences in adults, Liu et al. studied the genders of long bone lengthening patients and found no significant difference, which is consistent with our study, and there was no difference in PVR between the genders of children (1). For the effect of obesity or weight on BMD, Garvey et al. concluded that there is an effect in adults, with a significant negative correlation between obesity and BMD (β=−0.003) (16). Yanet et al. asserted that PVR demonstrates significant potential as an objective tool for assessing docking site healing in bone transport procedures (17). Ma et al. studied the effect of obesity on BMD at different sites, and the results showed a negative correlation between the three sites selected (femur, femoral neck, and spine) (18). However, children seem to show an opposite response to that of adults; although obesity in adults may lead to low bone density and lower PVRs, children who have low weight display a lower bone density. Our results suggested that there was a difference between birth weight below 4 kg and above this weight, which means that the heavier the weight is, the closer the bone density is to normal. Among 342 children with micrognathia, those who weighed above 2.5 kg and had a good nutritional status had a better bone density.

Our results showed that the PVRs of early stage of mandibular DO are much smaller than the normal bone. The PVRs gradually increased in the following 3 months. It almost normalized in the 3 months after osteotomy and eventually neared 1, similar to the PVR before surgery. This indicates that the distraction device can be removed after 3 months, providing a theoretical basis for us to remove the distraction device after 3 months. Clinicians can conduct X-ray examinations at 3 months to measure PVR. If it reaches or comes close to 1, the DO device can be removed. Therefore, PVR can be a necessary examination before removal of the brace, and the retraction rate should not or rarely occur after the distraction is removed. PVR can be primarily used to assess the maturity of extension bone in the late stages of regeneration to determine the time of bone healing and the time to remove the external fixator.

The advantages of our study were as follows: First, this is the first study using PVR to assess and monitor the early healing process of the mandible and its potential influencing factors (gender, age, and lengthening site). Second, our study has the largest sample size ever for mandible lengthening in a PVR analysis. Third, our results may provide a potential reference for using PVR to assess complete healing of mandible at the early stage and may compensate for the lack of CT. Fourth, we analyzed the relationship between birth weight and mandible density for the first time. Fifth, we determined the effect of the children’s weight before surgery on mandible DO.

However, there are several limitations to our study. Firstly, as a retrospective multi-factor analysis, the study design could not be fully comprehensive, and certain potentially relevant variables—such as the relationship between blood biochemical indicators and PVR—were not included. Secondly, due to unavoidable anatomical overlap, the cervical vertebrae may interfere with mandibular X-ray imaging, leading to higher pixel values in the anterior mandible. Since both pre-operative and post-operative images are affected under the same conditions, the impact on the PVR is minimal, and PVR itself helps to mitigate this limitation. Thirdly, PVR measurements may be influenced by the presence of titanium nails; to address this, we excluded a 2 mm area surrounding the titanium hardware, thereby reducing its impact on evaluation. Finally, this study focused on a specific pediatric age group, and it remains unclear whether the findings can be generalized to older children or adolescents. Overall, although certain unavoidable factors may influence the results, their effects can be significantly minimized through our standardized measurement approach.

Conclusions

This study represents one of the first large-scale investigations focusing on early-stage mandibular healing (1–3 months) using PVR in infants undergoing DO. Our results showed that PVRs gradually increased during the first three months after mandibular osteotomy and during the process of DO. Gender had no effect on PVRs, whereas age, birth weight, and admission weight had a greater effect. The results of this study suggest that PVRs can be used as an indicator for the assessment of early lengthening bone growth in patients with mandibular lengthening and for determining whether to remove the DO apparatus. Although a definitive cut-off value has not been established, our findings suggest that a PVR approaching 0.9–1.0 may indicate advanced bone consolidation (Figure 8). However, this should be interpreted in conjunction with CT findings and clinical assessment.
