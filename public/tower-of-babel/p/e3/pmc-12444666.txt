Covalently Oriented AuNP–Antibody Bioconjugates Enable Early and Sensitive Detection of Opisthorchis viverrini Urinary Antigen
Authors: Sawinee Ngernpimai, Oranee Srichaiyapol, Patsara Thongmee, Phattharaphon Wongphutorn, Chanika Worasith, Anchalee Techasen, Apiwat Chompoosor, Jureerut Daduang, Paiboon Sithithaworn, Patcharaporn Tippayawat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Opisthorchis viverrini (OV) infection remains a major public health concern in Southeast Asia due to its strong association with cholangiocarcinoma. Early and accurate detection of OV infection is crucial for timely intervention and reduction of cancer risk. While enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)-based urine antigen detection methods have shown high sensitivity, their dependence on laboratory infrastructure limits their field utility. This study aims to develop and compare two gold nanoparticle (AuNP)-based lateral flow immunoassay (LFIA) formats utilizing distinct antibody conjugation strategies for rapid, noninvasive detection of OV excretory–secretory (OV–ES) antigens in urine. Two types of AuNP–antibody bioconjugates were prepared: (i) physical adsorption of monoclonal anti-OV antibodies on citrate-capped AuNPs (AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV) and (ii) covalent conjugation with orientation control on amino-terminated AuNPs (AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV). These were incorporated as signal reporters in LFIAs, which were evaluated for sensitivity, specificity, detection limit, cross-reactivity, and diagnostic performance against urinary ELISA results by using clinical urine samples. LFIA based on covalently oriented AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV (LFIA-TEG-NH-mAb-OV) demonstrated significantly lower limits of detection (1.54 vs 5.94 ng/mL), higher sensitivity (98.81 vs 96.83%), and improved specificity (81.94 vs 62.37%) compared to the LFIA based on passive adsorption format (LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV). Moreover, LFIA-TEG-NH-mAb-OV showed better agreement with the ELISA reference (κ = 0.805) and lower cross-reactivity with other helminth infections. This is the first study to report the use of covalently oriented antibody-AuNP conjugates for early urinary detection of OV, offering a field-deployable, accurate, and scalable solution for the diagnosis and control of opisthorchiasis in endemic regions.

Introduction

Liver and intrahepatic bile duct cancer ranks among the most common cancers worldwide. The absolute number of new cases of liver and intrahepatic bile ducts that occurred in 2022 was globally 866,136 cases, and 87.60% (758,725 cases) of deaths were reported. The highest rates around 70% of liver cancer cases lived in Asia (607,361 cases), especially East and Southeast Asia. Notably, more than 85% mortality rates (530,928 cases) were found. Most primary liver cancers are hepatocellular carcinomas. Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is rare in most parts of the world, except in Thailand and other parts of Southeast Asia, where rates are high. , The primary and most crucial risk factor for CCA is the infection caused by liver flukes, known as Opisthorchis Viverrini (OV). When raw or undercooked fish that contains metacercariae is consumed, humans can become infected. The risk of developing CCA is influenced by the intensity of the infection, previous or current exposure to the parasite, and genetic polymorphisms of the host. Long-term infection of OV can ultimately cause cholangiocarcinogenesis by a variety of processes. The occurrence of these conditions may therefore be prevented by the early diagnosis and treatment of OV infections.

To date, the diagnosis of OV infection traditionally relies on the detection of parasite eggs in feces. However, this parasitological method is fraught with limitations such as false positives due to confusion with minute intestinal flukes’ eggs and false negatives in cases of light infections or biliary duct obstruction, where eggs might not be present in feces. However, the microscopic examination method used for OV diagnosis struggles with differentiating OV eggs from those of other similar parasites and requires skilled parasitologists and laboratory technicians, making it impractical for use in remote regions. Although immunodiagnostic methods detecting antibodies in serum are highly sensitive and specific, they often involve invasive sample collection and cannot reliably indicate current infection. , Thus, a novel diagnostic OV excretory–secretory (ES) antigens in urine (urine OV-ES assay) detection approach was developed to provide higher accuracy than fecal immunochromatographic test (FIT), such as copro-antigen detection using capture enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and the gold nanoparticles (AuNPs)-LISA assay, utilizing gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) for signal enhancement in ELISA. , The urine OV-ES assay based on the ELISA method demonstrates effectiveness with a cutoff value not more than 32.9 ng/mL. However, its requirement for significant processing time and well-equipped laboratories makes it unsuitable for field use or resource-limited areas. To make urine OV-ES detection for opisthorchiasis more practical, it is essential to simplify the assay and shorten the test time. Lateral flow immunoassay (LFIA) has therefore been developed to address this limitation.

Since the sensitivity of an LFIA is crucial for early diagnosis, the labeling probe required in fabricating a lateral flow test strip has been taken into account to achieve a reproducible and reliable assay. It is thought that the process of loading antibodies to the AuNP surface is more important. The coupling methods for preparing antibody to AuNPs probes include several approaches, such as physical adsorption, one-step Ab covalent immobilization, linker-mediated and linker-free two-step Ab immobilization, and covalent coupling. , Due to its straightforward operation and high coupling efficiency, physical adsorption is the most commonly used approach. A new rapid diagnostic test (RDT), based on immunochromatographic lateral flow methodology, was developed for detecting OV-ES antigens in urine samples. The test uses a specific monoclonal antibody (mAb) to the OV-ES antigen. The reaction is signaled by AuNP labeling probes-antimouse IgG conjugate. The RDT yielded high diagnostic performance. One such method involves covalent coupling that takes place as one of the strategies for oriented immobilizing Ab on the AuNPs surface to get better immunoassay efficiency. Compared with the conventional passive adsorption, this coupling approach can effectively improve AuNP probe robustness. When Ab is oriented properly, this can enhance its bioactivity and completely expose the Fab domain, resulting in effective target recognition. Some researchers reported good results in the orientation of antibodies with this approach. , Also, this is in agreement with our previous work; the covalently fixed “end-on” orientation of mAb for Listeria monocytogenes detection was fabricated on an in-house LFIA. Our evaluated LFIA exhibited a unique characteristic, such as an accessible Fab region of the Ab for antigen binding, excellent stability, and nonspecific protein adsorption. Also, this advanced LFIA offered practical performance across different types of samples, including human blood, milk, mushroom samples, and clinical blood samples. Moreover, the reproducibility of the POCT strips is highly practical.

Despite advancements in ELISA for urinary detection of OV-ES antigens, such methods remain impractical for large-scale screening in endemic areas due to their reliance on laboratory infrastructure. LFIAs offer a promising alternative for point-of-care diagnosis; however, their sensitivity and specificity are often compromised by suboptimal antibody conjugation techniques. In this study, we present a novel approach that strategically compares two conjugation methods: conventional passive adsorption and covalent, orientation-controlled attachment of monoclonal antibodies to gold nanoparticles for the development of enhanced LFIA devices. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report applying site-directed covalent antibody conjugation to AuNPs for urinary antigen detection in opisthorchiasis. Our results demonstrate that the covalent conjugation approach not only significantly improves assay sensitivity and specificity but also minimizes cross-reactivity with other helminth infections. This innovation bridges the diagnostic gap between lab-based methods and field-deployable platforms, offering a robust, noninvasive, and highly specific tool for early detection and control of opisthorchiasis in endemic settings.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Instruments

Details of chemicals, materials, and instruments are presented in the Supporting Information.

Synthesis of AuNP-Citr

AuNP-Citr was synthesized using a sodium citrate reduction method. Additional details regarding the preparation of AuNP-Citr are described in the Supporting Information.

Preparation of AuNPs-TEG-NH2

AuNPs-TEG-NH2 was prepared according to our previous report. A colloidal solution of cysteamine-AuNPs was synthesized by reducing HAuCl4 with NaBH4 in the presence of cysteamine, which acted as a stabilizing agent. Subsequently, the cysteamine-AuNPs were ligand-exchanged with the HS-TEG-NH2 chain using a standard place-exchange method. Additional details regarding the preparation of AuNPs-TEG-NH2 are described in the Supporting Information.

Conjugation of mAb-OV3 with AuNP

The conjugation of mAb-OV3 to AuNP-Citr was prepared using a passive method with slight modifications based on a previous study. Initially, the AuNP-Citr colloidal solution was obtained at an optical density (OD) of approximately 1, which was then adjusted to a pH of 8.5 by using 0.1 M K2CO3. Subsequently, 50 μL of a 0.2 mg/mL mAb-OV3 solution in 2 mM borate buffer, pH 9.0, was added to the adjusted AuNP-Citr colloidal solution. The resulting mixture was gently shaken for 10 min at room temperature. After completing the mixing process, a sufficient volume of a 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution was added to achieve a final concentration of 1% (v/v) BSA. The resulting mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The resulting pellets were washed three times with 2 mM borate buffer, pH 9.0, and then redispersed in a solution containing 2 mM borate buffer, pH 9.0, 1% BSA, and 20% sucrose to obtain a final optical density of ∼5 before use.

The conjugation of the mAb-OV3 to AuNP-TEG-NH2 was performed using the covalent orientation method, as described in our previous report. This process involved a coupling reaction utilizing 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC)/N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), as detailed in the Supporting Information.

LFIA Preparation

Detailed procedures for this LFS preparation are provided in the Supporting Information.

Ethical Statement

All procedures were performed according to the protocol approved by the Khon Kaen University Ethics Committee (Project number HE67401). The need for informed consent was waived due to the secondary use of leftover samples for research purposes by the Khon Kaen University Ethics Committee for Human Research (KKUEC).

Clinical Sample Collection and Preparation

The sample populations in this study were divided into two groups. Group 1 was designated for the evaluation of the performance of LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV. This group consisted of well-defined urinary antigen ELISA-positive and -negative individuals for OV (n = 156) from endemic areas in Northeastern Thailand, with samples sourced from the Department of Parasitology Biobank at Khon Kaen University. Group 2 was selectively recruited to assess the cross-reactivity of LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV with other helminthiases identified through fecal examination (n = 95), also from endemic areas in northeastern Thailand, and these samples were obtained from the same biobank.

Urine samples were centrifuged at 500 g for 15 min at 4 °C to separate supernatants and then stored at −20 °C for analysis by urinary in-house ELISA, LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV.

Analytical Procedure

To detect the OV-ES antigen in a urine sample, 100 μL of the sample was applied to the sample pad of an LFIA. After 15 min, the intensity at the test line (TL) of the LFIA was analyzed, and images were captured using a rapid lateral flow strip reader. The performance of LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV was evaluated.

Limit of Detection (LOD) Studies

Crude somatic OV-ES antigens were added to negative urine specimens in concentrations ranging from 0.2 to 5000 ng/mL to evaluate the limit of detection (LOD) for LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV.

Cross-Reactivity Studies

To identify potential sources of false-positive results in LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV, urine samples were analyzed from individuals who tested negative for OV infection but were infected with other helminths. The helminth infections included in this study were Strongyloides stercoralis (Ss) (n = 4), Echinostoma sp. (n = 4), minute intestinal flukes (MIFs) (n = 3), and Taenia sp. (n = 1). A false-positive diagnosis was recorded when the TL intensity from the urine of an individual infected with one of these helminths exceeded the cutoff value established from sample group 1.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22 (International Business Machines, USA). The McNemar χ2 test was employed to assess the significance of differences in prevalence proportions based on diagnostic testing. The Kruskal–Wallis tests were utilized to evaluate the correlation between antigen concentration and the grading scores for LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV. The sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values of LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV were calculated using urinary antigen ELISA detection as the reference method. Diagnostic agreement between the tests was measured using Cohen’s κ coefficient (κ-value), interpreted according to the following guidelines: κ < 0 indicated no agreement; 0–0.2 indicated poor agreement; 0.21–0.4 indicated fair agreement; 0.41–0.6 indicated moderate agreement; 0.61–0.8 indicated good agreement; and 0.81–1.0 indicated excellent agreement. Statistical significance was reached when P values were >0.05.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of AuNPs and Their Conjugation with mAb-OV3

The labels utilized in the conjugates play a vital role in improving the analytical performance of LFIA. Currently, AuNPs have been extensively used in LFIA due to their excellent light absorption capacity, which can be easy to visually observe. , In this study, we compared two methods of antibody-AuNP conjugation to determine their enhanced ability to amplify the LFIA as a signal reporter for detecting OV-ES antigen in a urine sample. Two different strategies were studied for the conjugation of the mAb-OV3 to the AuNP surface (Scheme ). These strategies included physical adsorption (passive absorption) and covalent conjugation with control over the antibody orientation. The first strategies of antibody conjugation, the AuNPs with anionic citrate capping, known as AuNP-Citr (Figure a), were utilized to attach the mAb-OV3 through a physical adsorption method. This method is based on hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, as well as binding between positively charged amino acids, such as those present in the N-terminal of antibodies, and negatively charged citrate-coated AuNPs. Physical adsorption conjugation simply occurs by mixing the two components of AuNPs and antibodies at optimal pH and ion concentration. This method allows for nonoriented but strong binding of antibodies to the surface of AuNPs without the need for modification of either the antibodies or noble AuNPs. , In the second strategy, covalent conjugation for antibody arrangement was demonstrated using AuNP-TEG-NH2 (Figure b), which was modified by attaching a short tetraethylene glycol with an amino headgroup ligand (HS-TEG-NH2) to cysteamine-capped AuNPs (Figure b). The process of covalently oriented immobilization of antibodies on AuNP-TEG-NH2 is a similar approach as the one used in our previous report. , This immobilization process involved activating the antibodies using EDC/NHS cross-linkage in a pH 5–6 solution, resulting in antibodies with a net positive charge. Also, the Fab fragment of the antibody contains a greater number of positively charged residues compared to the Fc fragment. Therefore, antibody molecules are more likely to bind to the NH2-TEG-AuNP surface by utilizing the bottom Fc part and forming covalent bonds. This strategy can provide the appropriate orientation for immobilizing antibodies, with the Fab portion, which contains the antigen-binding sites, directed toward the solution. This makes them more accessible for interacting with the antigen. ,

Two Strategies for Conjugating AuNPs and mAb-OV3 as Signal Reporters in LFIA for the Detection of OV-ES Antigen in Urine

Initially, the size and morphology of AuNP-Citr and AuNP-TEG-NH2 were estimated by TEM. The TEM images revealed that both AuNPs displayed a spherical morphology and were well dispersed (Figure c,d). The average diameters of AuNP-Citr and AuNP-TEG-NH2 were analyzed by randomly measuring the particle diameters in the TEM images. The core size of AuNPs-Citr was determined to be 22.5 ± 2.7 nm, with diameters ranging from 17.3 to 30.2 nm (Figure c, right). Meanwhile, AuNP-TEG-NH2 showed an average core size of 26.1 ± 2.4 nm, with diameters ranging from 20.2 to 32.3 nm (Figure d, right). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and average size analysis reveal that the AuNP-TEG-NH2 nanoparticles are about 4 nm in size, which is slightly larger than the AuNPs-Citr. This difference can be attributed to the capping agents, such as citrate and cysteamine, which have an impact on the growth of the AuNP cores. However, the varying sizes of AuNPs, with normalized standard deviations not exceeding 2–4%, do not significantly influence their sensitivity and signal amplification. Additionally, the synthesized AuNP-Citr and AuNP-TEG-NH2 demonstrated exceptional quality, exhibiting well-controlled nanoparticle sizes and maintaining excellent colloidal stability in solution. Furthermore, their sizes and shapes are suitable for antibody conjugation in the LFIA applications.

The AuNP-Citr was conjugated with mAb-OV3 using a physical adsorption method, whereas the AuNP-TEG-NH2 was conjugated with mAb-OV3 through a covalent orientation method. The optimization of antibody conjugation conditions on AuNPs in this study was based on previously published reports and our own recent investigations. For passive adsorption, borate buffer at pH 9.0 was selected in alignment with established protocols reported in the literature, ,, which indicate that this buffer condition maximizes antibody adsorption onto AuNP-Citr while minimizing aggregation and nonspecific binding. For covalent conjugation, we employed acetate buffer at pH 5.5, following our earlier findings , which demonstrated that EDC/NHS-mediated coupling under these conditions leads to optimal “end-on” orientation of antibodies on the AuNP-TEG-NH2 surface, thereby enhancing antigen accessibility and resulting in improved assay sensitivity and specificity. The adoption of these optimized conditions, as validated in our previous studies and supported by the literature, is directly responsible for the improvements in sensitivity and specificity observed in the present LFIA platform.

To verify the successful conjugation, two types of labeling detection probes, AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV, were characterized for their localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), hydrodynamic diameter (DH), and ζ potential. The results are present in Figure . After conjugation with mAb-OV3, the LSPR of AuNP-Citr exhibited a red shift from 522 to 528 nm, while the LSPR of AuNP-TEG-NH2 shifted from 524 to 525 nm (Figure a). The largest red shift of 6 nm was observed through the physical adsorption of mAb-OV3 on the AuNP-Citr surface, while a 1 nm shift of the LSPR peak was noted with the covalently oriented conjugation of mAb-OV3 on AuNP-TEG-NH2. The physical adsorption of antibodies on the AuNP-Citr surface significantly impacted the position of the LSPR peak. In contrast, when covalent linking methods were utilized, no significant changes were observed in the LSPR peak. Consistent with previous reports, there were no significant alterations in the LSPR spectra before and after antibodies were conjugated to the AuNPs through covalent bonding. ,, The LSPR depends on the dielectric constant in the immediate layer to the AuNP core. Changes at the interface due to adsorption or chemical binding are reflected in the shift of the LSPR band. The change of LSPR suggested that the dielectric constant of the surrounding of the AuNP surface was changed, which was in agreement with the attachment of the mAb-OV3 molecules on both AuNP surfaces. Furthermore, both AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV spectra exhibited narrow LSPR peaks, confirming that the conjugated AuNPs were stable and not aggregated after mAb-OV3 conjugation. The ζ potential of AuNP-Citr and AuNP-TEG-NH2 before and after their conjugation with mAb-OV3 is illustrated in Figure b. The AuNP-Citr exhibited a ζ potential of −28.9 ± 2.1 mV, attributed to the citrate adlayer formed during synthesis. This value increased to −11.7 ± 0.8 mV following the physical adsorption of mAb-OV3 onto its surface. In contrast, the AuNP-TEG-NH2 demonstrated a ζ potential of +28.7 ± 1.5 mV due to its surface modification with HS-TEG-NH2. Following the covalent immobilization of mAb-OV3, the ζ potential rose to +46.8 ± 1.4 mV. The increase in ζ potential confirms the successful surface modification of both AuNP-Citr and AuNP-TEG-NH2, indicating the formation of bioconjugates with mAb-OV3. Interestingly, the AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV led to a more positive ζ potential, considering the mAb-OV3 on the AuNP-TEG-NH2 surface was exposed to the part of more positively charged than mAb-OV3 on the AuNP-Citr surface. Thus, we attributed this unanticipated result to a greater arrangement of dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis that confirmed both physical adsorption and covalent bonding of mAb-OV3 to the surfaces of AuNP-Citr and AuNP-TEG-NH2, respectively. The mean DH of AuNP-Citr and AuNP-TEG-NH2 was measured at 24.9 ± 0.5 and 36.8 ± 0.2 nm, respectively. Upon conjugation with mAb-OV3, the DH increased by 52.6 ± 1.8 nm for physical adsorption and 74.7 ± 0.8 nm for covalently oriented immobilization (Figure c). The increase in DH resulting from the physical adsorption and covalent bonding of mAb-OV3 indicates that a monolayer has formed on the surface of the AuNP, based on the size of an IgG molecule. , Notably, the thicker layer observed for the covalently conjugated particles suggests a distinct antibody orientation, likely reflecting a more “end-on” configuration that improves Fab region accessibility and enhances antigen-binding activity, compared to the more random orientation from passive adsorption. While these DLS results provide important indirect evidence for differences in antibody arrangement, further direct confirmation using high-magnification TEM or binding kinetics via SPR was not carried out in this study but will be considered in future work to more fully elucidate the relationship among conjugation method, antibody orientation, and assay performance.

To confirm the Fab region of the antibodies in the immobilization, the orientation of the antibodies bound to the AuNP surface was evaluated in both conjugation methods tested here. The number of accessible Fab antigen-binding sites on the resulting AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV was evaluated using an aggregation-based assay. The DH of AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV before (D1) and after (D2) aggregation generated by antimouse IgG Fab-specific antibodies were determined using DLS. The ratio of D2/D1 was calculated as the test score. A higher test score suggests more aggregation due to a greater number of accessible Fab molecules on the conjugated particles. The test score results are shown in Figure d. The AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV probe showed a higher test score of 1.7 ± 0.01 compared to the test scores of AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV, which were 1.33 ± 0.02. This suggests that the AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV probe effectively aligns the antibody’s orientation better than AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV, resulting in a significantly higher accessibility of Fab for interacting with OV-ES antigen.

Limit of Detection of LFIA Based on AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV as a Signal Reporter

To assess the performance of the two conjugate types as signal reporters, AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV were evaluated for their ability to detect the OV-ES antigen in urine samples using an LFIA platform. The AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV solutions were individually sprayed onto each conjugate pad and subsequently assembled onto a backing card for integration into the LFIA, resulting in LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV. The sensitivity of both LFIA tests was evaluated by introducing crude somatic OV-ES antigens into negative urine specimens at concentrations ranging from 0 to 5000 ng/mL. After applying 100 μL of the urine sample and allowing the test to complete within 15 min, the LFIA was analyzed using a rapid strip reader to evaluate the color intensities of the control line (CL) and test line (TL). Figure a,b illustrates that all strips exhibited red bands on the CL areas, indicating that both types of signal reporters, LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV, flowed through the test strip without any interruption caused by the pH effect of the urine sample. The intensity of the red bands on the TL of LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV diminished as the concentration of OV-ES antigens decreased from 5000 to 2.4 ng/mL. No visible bands were detected by the naked eye in the TL areas for OV-ES antigens in urine at concentrations ranging from 1.2 to 0 ng/mL (Figure a). On the other hand, when using LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV, a decrease in the pink-red bands on the TL was observed as the concentration of OV-ES antigens decreased from 5000 to 0.6 ng/mL. Additionally, red bands were not visible to the naked eye in the TL areas for OV-ES antigens at concentrations ranging from 0.3 to 0 ng/mL (Figure b). These results indicate that the LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV exhibited a more pronounced red color response on the TL compared to LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV when evaluated with low concentrations of OV-ES antigens. It is noted that the covalent and site-specific immobilization of mAb-OV3 on AuNP-TEG-NH2 leads to more effective antigen-binding activity in the LFIA platform. , The relative quantitative intensities of LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV were assessed by using a RapidScan strip reader. As shown in Figure c, the TL intensities for both LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV decreased as the concentration of OV-ES antigens decreased from 5000 to 0 ng/mL. Significant TL intensities were observed between LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV at concentrations of OV-ES antigens ranging from 1250 to 5000 ng/mL, as well as at 156.3 to 312.5 ng/mL, 9.8 ng/mL, 0.6 ng/mL, and in urine samples with 0 ng/mL of OV-ES antigens. The significantly higher level of nonspecific binding in the LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV compared to the LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV suggests that the specific binding site of the mAb-OV3 is not optimally positioned for interaction with the OV-ES antigen in AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV. This indicates that the orientation of the antibody on the AuNP surface is either random or less controlled when using the passive conjugation approach, which is consistent with previous reports. , Figure c presents the sigmoidal calibration curves, revealing a correlation coefficient of 0.9089 for the LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and 0.9863 for the AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV. Using sigmoidal calibration curves, the LOD was determined using a method outlined in previous literature. The LOD for the OV-ES antigen in urine with AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV was found to be 18.24 ng/mL, which is approximately two times lower than the LOD of 27 ng/mL for LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV. This finding suggests that the advantages gained from covalent coupling with a fixed orientation, achieved by controlling the pH for the activation of mAb-OV3, can enhance the efficient and proper orientation of this specific antibody. Consequently, this results in the development of a highly sensitive signal reporter in the LFIA.

The specificity of both LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV was evaluated by testing OV, crude OV-ES, S. stercoralis (Ss), and Taenia (T) antigens spiked into negative urine samples. The LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV produced a strong TL with the OV antigen and a moderate response with the crude OV-ES antigen. Minimal or no signals were observed in the presence of Ss and T antigens, indicating limited cross-reactivity (Figure S1a). In contrast, the LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV produced significantly stronger signal intensities for both OV and OV-ES antigens (Figure S1b), while still showing a minimal response to Ss and T antigens. Quantitative analysis using bar graphs (Figure S1c) confirms that LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV (red bars) generates significantly higher TL intensities for both OV and OV-ES compared to LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV (black bars). The increased TL intensity indicates improved detection sensitivity. These findings demonstrate that while both LFIA systems exhibit high specificity for OV antigens, this specificity is especially notable for the OV-ES antigen. Minimal cross-reactivity with Ss and T antigens further underscores this selectivity, confirming that mAb-OV3 maintains its specific recognition in both conjugate formats, an essential characteristic for reliable diagnosis. Moreover, the superior performance of LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV may be attributed to improved mAb-OV3 orientation and reduced steric hindrance on the AuNP surface due to the SH-TEG-NH2 modification of the AuNPs.

Diagnostic Performance of LFIA Based on AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV as a Signal Reporter

Based on the performance results of spiking crude somatic OV-ES antigens in urine samples, the LFIA incorporating AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV probes exhibited comparable abilities in measuring OV-ES antigens in negative urine samples at levels that corresponded to the reference interval for healthy adults. , In addition, urine samples from various subjects were used to evaluate the accuracy of the AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV probes in the LIFA platform. A total of 156 adult subjects underwent urinary ELISA testing to measure OV-ES antigen levels, resulting in 60 negative and 96 positive results. Subsequently, all urine samples were specifically tested with both LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV to obtain the results. The interpretation of the LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV results was based on the intensity of the TL as recorded by a RapidScan strip reader. The cutoff value for color intensity was calculated according to the previous report (mean TL intensity of negative urine +3SD of TL intensity of negative urine). This resulted in a cutoff value of 520.23 arbitrary units (au) for LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and 310.31 au for LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV. These values will be used to indicate the presence or absence of OV-ES antigen in urine for future analyses. The sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values of LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV were determined by comparing them to those of urinary ELISA, which served as the reference method. The LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV demonstrated a sensitivity of 96.83% and a specificity of 62.37%. The positive predictive value for this assay was 63.54%, while the negative predictive value was 96.67%, resulting in an overall accuracy of 76.28% for detecting OV-ES antigen in urine samples. In contrast, LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV showed a sensitivity of 98.81% and a specificity of 81.94%. The positive predictive value for this method was 86.46%, and the negative predictive value was 98.33%, achieving a total accuracy of 91.03% for detecting OV-ES antigen in urine samples (Table ). Furthermore, the higher incidence of false-positive results in LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV compared to LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV may be attributed to the very early stages of OV infection, during which OV-ES antigen secretion could be too low for detection in LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV. Additionally, the increased occurrence of false-negative results in LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV relative to LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV may be attributed to the inherent lack of specificity linked to physical and ionic interactions. During passive conjugation, random antibody orientations are more likely to occur. These antibodies have multiple binding sites on the surface of AuNPs, which can lead to nonspecific binding. ,

Next, the correlation between the urinary ELISA assay and both LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV for detecting the OV-ES antigen in urine samples was conducted. Among the paired urine samples tested with urinary ELISA (n = 156), in cases negative for OV infection by ELISA (n = 60), only 2 cases (3.3%) tested positive for antigen by LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV. In the group of urine positive samples (n = 96), 61 cases (63.5%) tested positive for LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV (Figure a). The same samples were tested with LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV. In cases negative for OV infection by ELISA (n = 60), only 1 case (1.67%) tested positive for antigen by LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV. In the urine positive group (n = 96), 83 cases (86.5%) were positive results by LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV (Figure b). Higher percentages of OV-ES antigen positivity were observed with LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV (62.5–100%) compared to LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV (56.3–100%) across various antigen concentrations (33.0–2000.0 ng/mL) (Figure ). Furthermore, a significant relationship was found between OV-ES antigen concentrations in urine and the positive rates in both LFIA-AuNP-Citr-OV-3 (χ2 = 90.3, P < 0.001) and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV (χ2 = 114.8, P < 0.001). In addition, we conducted a study comparing the diagnostic agreement in both LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV with a urinary ELISA assay. The κ tests revealed moderate agreement (κ = 0.546) between the urinary ELISA and LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV. However, the agreement between the ELISA assay and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV was excellent (κ = 0.805) (Table ). The diagnostic performance of LFIA-AuNP-Citr-OV-3 exhibited a lower correlation with OV-ES antigen concentration and a lower diagnostic agreement with the ELISA assay compared to LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV. These findings suggest that covalent coupling of the antibody with a fixed orientation in the signal reporter can improve the reliability of LFIA for detecting urinary OV-ES antigens. However, the limitation of our present study is that the validation of the LFIA was performed within a single center setting and involved a relatively limited and homogeneous patient sample set. Consequently, the generalizability of our findings to wider and more diverse populations requires cautious interpretation. To establish the true clinical applicability and robustness of the LFIA platform, future studies will need to involve larger-scale enrollments across multiple institutions, encompassing patients of diverse demographics, ethnicities, and clinical backgrounds. Such comprehensive multicenter studies will enable a more accurate evaluation of the assay’s performance in varied real-world contexts, address potential sources of variability, and provide stronger evidence for its widespread clinical adoption. ,, The promising diagnostic performance observed in comparison to our in-house ELISA suggests substantial potential for practical use, particularly in resource-limited or community screening contexts. Nevertheless, future studies incorporating a parallel, head-to-head evaluation of our LFIA with commercially available kits will be needed to comprehensively demonstrate specificity, sensitivity, and operational advantages.

Cross-Reactivity Test of LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV

Urine samples from 95 participants were used to evaluate the specificity of LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV. Out of these cases, 83 were confirmed to be infected with OV, while 12 were confirmed to have other helminth infections using the FECT technique. Figure a displays the cross-reactivity performance of LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV. Among the 83 subjects who tested positive for eggs, urinary OV-ES antigens were detected in only 61 of these individuals, and 22 subjects were found to be negative. Additionally, of the 12 subjects with other helminth infections, 7 (58.33%) cases were found to be positive. In Figure b, LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV successfully detected urinary OV-ES antigens in 79 out of 83 cases, leaving only 4 subjects with negative results. Among the 12 subjects with other helminth infections, 5 (41.67%) were found to be positive. The results demonstrated that the diagnostic performance regarding the cross-reactivity of LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV is lower than that of LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV, primarily due to the differences in the antibody conjugation strategies employed. In addition, a low-intensity OV infection in subjects may contribute to false-negative results for both LFIA-AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV and LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV. The concentration of urinary OV-ES antigens can be influenced by several factors, including the duration and severity of biliary fibrosis, as well as the formation of circulating immune complexes that are difficult to detect. , The absence of detailed clinical stratification in our cohort represents a limitation and may contribute to the observed variability in LFIA results, including false negatives among microscopy-confirmed cases. Future prospective studies should incorporate standardized fibrosis staging (such as ultrasound grading or liver elastography), infection duration assessment, and measurement of immune complex levels to better elucidate these effects. Such analyses will support a more nuanced understanding of antigen detection biomarkers and optimize the interpretation of the LFIA results in clinical practice. Furthermore, these cases may indicate the presence of mixed infections involving other helminths, alongside a low-intensity OV infection. This suggests that OV could be a recent infection occurring during the prepatent period when eggs are undetectable. ,, Despite these encouraging results, a wider evaluation of cross-reactivity is warranted. Endemic populations are often exposed to a diversity of pathogens, including parasites, bacteria, and viruses, which may contribute to nonspecific immunological responses or potential assay interference. Comprehensive assessment with a larger and more diverse panel of pathogens, at both the antigen and clinical sample level, will help confirm the robustness and diagnostic reliability of the LFIA in real-world settings.

Conclusions

This study compares two AuNP–antibody conjugation strategies, physical adsorption (AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV) and covalent fix-oriented antibody conjugation (AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV), for the development of an enhanced LFIA for OV-ES antigen detection in urine. The LFIA based on AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV as a signal reporter exhibited superior analytical performance, demonstrating significantly enhanced sensitivity, specificity, and lower detection limits compared with the LFIA based on AuNP-Citr-mAb-OV. These improvements are attributed to the optimized orientation and accessibility of the antibody Fab region. These enhancements were confirmed through comprehensive physicochemical characterizations and performance assessments in clinical samples. LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV achieved a diagnostic accuracy of over 91%, with excellent agreement with the standard urinary ELISA. Additionally, it demonstrated reduced cross-reactivity with other helminth infections when compared to LFIA-AuNP-TEG-NH-mAb-OV. These findings underscore the value of antibody orientation in LFIA design and its critical role in enhancing diagnostic efficiency. Our results advocate for the application of oriented antibody immobilization strategies in point-of-care diagnostics, especially for parasitic infections like opisthorchiasis, where early, accurate, and noninvasive detection is essential. This LFIA platform, incorporating covalently oriented antibodies, holds significant promise for broader deployment in endemic and resource-limited settings to support mass screening and timely treatment interventions.
