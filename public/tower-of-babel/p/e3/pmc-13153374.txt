A performance-optimized V2V task offloading framework for real-time vehicular communication
Authors: Tariq Qayyum, Asadullah Tariq, Ikbal Taleb, Mohamed Adel Serhani, Zouheir Trabelsi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs vehicular applications become increasingly complex, their computational demands often exceed the capabilities of individual vehicles. Vehicular Edge Computing (VEC) alleviates this limitation by enabling task delegation to nearby edge resources; however, high mobility, dynamic topology, and fluctuating vehicle density make real-time offloading decisions challenging. To address these issues, we propose a performance-optimized Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) task offloading framework for dense and dynamic Vehicular Ad-hoc Networks (VANETs). The framework follows a two-stage design: (i) context-aware edge-node selection based on live topology capture via periodic beaconing, and (ii) cumulative score-based dynamic priority queuing at the selected edge node. The priority score jointly considers relative speed, distance, task size, and task priority, and its weights can be tuned to match application requirements and network conditions. Using OMNeT++/Veins/SUMO simulations, we evaluate dissemination and system delay, packet delivery ratio, task completion/success rate, and task processing failure rate. Results show improvements of up to 27% in system delay, 18% in packet delivery ratio, and 24% in task completion ratio compared with representative baselines, demonstrating robust performance under high density and mobility.Keywords: Task offloading, VANETs, IoT, Vehicular communication, Edge computing, V2VSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The rise of the Internet of Things (IoT) has significantly impacted various fields, including the evolution of traditional vehicular ad-hoc networks (VANETs) into advanced vehicular networks. These networks are crucial for creating dynamic vehicle communication systems that enhance intelligent transport systems (ITS) through efficient information exchange. Vehicular networks enable new applications in road safety, entertainment, and traffic management that were previously challenging to implement. This transformation allows vehicles to share data and information with other vehicles and roadside units (RSUs), enhancing the stability and functionality of VANETs. Consequently, vehicular networks facilitate smoother travel, increase driver awareness of traffic conditions, help avoid road accidents, identify less congested routes, and contribute to reducing fuel consumption and air pollution1.

According to reports, 121 billion dollars are wasted in time and fuel yearly due to traffic congestion, and 1.3 million people die in road accidents2,3. Therefore, rapid advancement in technology is utilized to support various intelligent applications integrated into transportation, which provide features such as traffic prediction, route planning, object detection, and safety-related information, to enhance the driving experience4. These applications generate significant data, often surpassing the vehicle’s computational resources. As a result, one feasible solution lies in cloud computing. However, most vehicular network applications necessitate minimal latency to make well-informed decisions ahead of time. Vehicular networks operate through two primary data communication models: Vehicles to Roadside Units (V2R) and Vehicle to Vehicle (V2V). These models facilitate a diverse array of applications within vehicular networks, including road safety, congestion management, emergency message dissemination, infotainment, internet access, and path planning5–7. Problem statement – Computational resources vary from one vehicle to another, yet intelligent applications demand significant computational power. Because vehicles have limited computing capabilities, some tasks frequently miss deadlines due to insufficient resources to meet their demands. To tackle this issue, the vehicle requests a neighboring edge vehicle or edge server to execute its task8. Time-sensitive applications, like driver behavior prediction to prevent accidents, traffic flow management, and driver assistance, demand low latency. However, in highly loaded scenarios, when the load on a specific edge node exceeds its processing power, it leads to long queues, increased packet drops to manage limited buffer sizes, and degraded performance for latency-sensitive applications9. In such scenarios, when an edge node faces excessive load, it can delegate tasks to the next available edge node within its range10. This strategy aids in enhancing response time by distributing the computational load across multiple nodes, thereby alleviating the burden on individual nodes and ensuring smoother task execution11,12.

Objective – To address the limitations of traditional cloud computing, a more distributed approach is necessary to improve system performance. By bringing computational and storage resources closer to users, performance can be enhanced, particularly in scenarios with varying demands and high mobility13–15. Efficient task handling is crucial in both urban and highway environments, where factors like vehicle density and mobility can significantly affect network traffic and system load. An effective offloading strategy is essential to maintain performance and avoid bottlenecks under such dynamic conditions.

In scenarios where vehicles possess abundant computational resources, they serve as edge nodes, offering computational capabilities to other nodes experiencing resource scarcity. However, in high-mobility scenarios, the computational support provided by fixed-position roadside units can hinder task execution, as their immobility may not align with dynamic computational demands16. Latency-sensitive tasks deployed to RSUs may either expire or fail to transmit results to requesting vehicles due to the vehicles moving out of range rapidly in high-mobility scenarios17,18. The selection of an edge vehicle by the requesting vehicle relies on various parameters such as speed, direction, task size, distance, and queue size. This selection process aims to identify nodes that maintain a prolonged communication range and can fulfill the resource requirements effectively19–21.

The absence of an adaptive scheduling algorithm in traditional systems can lead to inefficient data transmission and increased latency, as these systems cannot dynamically adjust to the varying network conditions and vehicle densities. This limitation often results in suboptimal communication performance and reduced reliability in vehicular networks.

In this paper, we introduce an edge-based task offloading scheme for V2V vehicular environments. Delay-sensitive tasks are routed to the most suitable edge node based on factors such as connection time and resource availability. Vehicles with ample computational resources broadcast their availability to nearby vehicles. When vehicles require additional computational power, they connect with these advertised vehicles to utilize their resources. Subsequently, the tasks received by the advertised vehicle are executed based on the sensitivity to delay.

Positioning and novelty – While many VEC/V2V offloading schemes use weighted decision rules or priority scheduling, the novelty of our work is in the end-to-end integration of (i) dynamic topology capture using a time-varying neighbor/edge table updated via beaconing, (ii) contextual edge-node selection that favors more stable V2V contacts under high mobility, and (iii) dynamic priority queuing at the selected edge node using a normalized multi-factor cumulative score. This two-stage design (select-then-schedule) explicitly targets dense and rapidly changing VANETs, where single-criterion schemes (e.g., nearest-first, strongest-compute-first, or stay-time-only) can become unstable or overload specific nodes.

Literature review

The advent of the Internet of Things (IoT) has significantly impacted the connectivity between data, people, and various devices, particularly in the context of modern vehicular systems. These systems not only provide computing facilities to drivers and passengers while on the move but also highlight the need for scalable, secure, and robust information exchange among vehicles as their numbers increase22–24.

One of the critical challenges in vehicular networks is the limited range of Edge servers, which are not always able to accommodate the mobility of vehicles equipped with computing facilities. Vehicles outside the range of an Edge server must enter its range to offload computing tasks, which is not suitable for latency-sensitive applications25. To address this26, proposed a multi-hop task offloading scheme allowing vehicles outside the communication range of an edge server to offload tasks through a multi-hop transfer. However, this method struggles with network congestion and increased waiting times in dense environments.

Alternatively27, introduced a Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) offloading scheme within VANETs, where vehicles offload tasks to others within their communication range through direct connections. This method selects the longest communication path for offloading but does not consider the high mobility-induced topological changes, potentially leading to disconnections28. Moreover29, proposed a technique utilizing idle resources for V2V offloading, employing particle swarm optimization to manage the distribution of tasks. However, this technique is limited to nodes within one hop, which diminishes its effectiveness in dynamic networks.

Furthermore30, suggested an offloading scheme based on knowledge-defined networks that identifies multiple offloading paths and their associated costs. This approach proposes alternate paths in case of disconnections during offloading but does not consider node availability, which might reduce system performance. On the other hand31, implemented a federated learning-based technique to exploit underutilized computing resources on vehicles. This technique divides the computational task into several parts, allocating them across different nodes and the edge server, though it may suffer inefficiencies in multi-hop environments due to suboptimal next-hop selections based on a greedy algorithm.

Recently32, leveraged parked vehicles as a resource pool, proposing a deep learning-based model to determine task offloading based on vehicles’ available time and energy levels. This approach aims to enhance response times for delay-sensitive applications. In contrast33, and34 introduced advanced frameworks and methodologies to optimize resource allocation and task processing, albeit facing challenges related to the inherent instability and dynamic nature of vehicular communications.

Despite its thorough approach, it confronts challenges in real-time adaptability and efficiency in rapidly changing conditions, which may limit its scalability and practical implementation.

Dynamic topology capture

Vehicular connectivity is inherently time-varying due to mobility. We model the VANET as a time-varying graph G(t)=V(t),E(t), where V(t) represents vehicles present at time t, and an edge (i,j)∈E(t) exists if (i) the inter-vehicle distance satisfies dij(t)≤cr(t) and (ii) the communication quality satisfies Qcom(i,j)≥Qmin. Topology information is captured dynamically through periodic beaconing and edge advertisements. Each vehicle maintains a neighbor/edge table refreshed every beacon interval Δb; entries not refreshed within a timeout Tout are removed, reflecting link breakage and topology changes. The proposed offloading framework uses instantaneous context extracted from this table (distance dij(t), relative speed sij(t), and direction θij(t)) as stability proxies during edge-node selection and task prioritization. Proposed framework

In the domain of vehicular communications and intelligent transportation systems, the concept of Vehicular Edge Computing (VEC) emerges as a transformative approach to handle the computational demands of modern vehicles. Leveraging the distributed computing power of vehicles and roadside units, VEC facilitates enhanced processing capabilities directly at the network’s edge. This approach is particularly crucial in dynamic vehicular environments, where traditional cloud computing faces challenges due to latency and network congestion issues.

Our proposed scheme introduces a robust framework for managing computational tasks through an efficient task-offloading mechanism. By capitalizing on the inherent computational resources of certain vehicles, designated as edge nodes EN, and the cooperative engagement of user nodes UN, our system significantly reduces the task processing delays and optimizes resource allocation. The edge nodes, equipped with superior computational power, broadcast their availability and capabilities, thereby facilitating a distributed computing environment where tasks can be offloaded seamlessly from user nodes lacking sufficient resources. This decentralized architecture enhances the system’s overall efficiency while also ensuring reliability and speed in task processing. The proposed algorithms encompasses task prioritization and resource management that further refines the system’s performance, making it adept at handling the high mobility and variable connectivity inherent in vehicular networks. Through this innovative approach, our scheme addresses the pressing need for rapid processing requirment in vehicles, paving the way for more sophisticated applications in vehicular networks.

Edge nodes for computation offloading

The node with excessive computational resources broadcasts advertisement messages within its communication range. The advertisement message contains information like current speed, direction, workload, and available computational capacity. Free nodes FN, which are not currently attached to any EN, establish a connection with EN and periodically exchange stay-alive beacons with EN. When UN needs assistance in task computation, UN sends the task along with parameters such as relative speed, direction, required computation (defined in Millions of Instruction per Second - MIPS), and distance). The task request received at EN is executed based on the overall task’s cumulative score. The algorithm 1 shows the behavior of an EN within a vehicular network, focusing on the handling of task requests from user nodes UN. The process begins with initializing TaskReqflag to zero, used to monitor the arrival of new task requests. The algorithm continuously loops through as long as no task requests are detected (i.e., while TaskReqflag remains zero). The algorithm constantly checks for incoming task requests. When a task request is identified (i.e. TaskReq becomes true), the TaskReqflag is set to one, indicating that a task is now being processed. After recognizing a task request, the edge node evaluates its current resource availability. If there are sufficient resources available to handle the task (Resource_available become true), the task is then added to a queue for processing. Here, we can prioritize tasks based on certain criteria such as urgency or computational intensity, distance, and speed of the vehicle. After queuing the task, the process transitions to another algorithm 2, which manages the actual execution of the queued tasks. Concurrently, the edge node updates the requesting UN about the task’s acceptance and potential commencement time. However, in case the resources are not available to process the task, the algorithm redirects back to the start of the loop, continuing to monitor for either the availability of resources or the arrival of new task requests.

The proposed algorithms ensures that the edge node manages its resources efficiently and communicates consistently with user nodes about the status of their requests. It is designed to be robust and adaptable, capable of handling dynamic changes in network conditions and resource availability, which are common in high-mobility vehicular environments.

User node - requesting vehicles

The UN periodically exchanges the latest information through periodic beacons with EN in its communication range and EN also provides updates to UN. Whenever UN needs computational assistance it simply offloads the task to EN within its communication range along with (speed, direction, and task size). The Algorithm 3 specifies the operations of a UN within a vehicular network, which involves handling tasks based on the availability of resources from an edge node EN.

The process begins when the user node receives an advertisement indicating available computational resources from an edge node. Upon receipt, the user node evaluates the advertised resources to determine if they match the requirements necessary for its pending tasks (checkResources). If the resources meet the user node’s criteria desiredResources equals true), the algorithm proceeds to offload the task to the edge node (offload Task to EN). This offloading includes sending the task data to the edge node, which will handle the task execution. Concurrently, the user node marks the task as pending in its local status registry (add pending against status task), awaiting completion confirmation or result delivery from the edge node. However, in case the available resources do not meet the requirements (desiredResources equals false), the algorithm skips the offloading process and moves directly to the end of the routine, indicating no action is taken (go to end). Performance comparison and discussion

The proposed methodology is evaluated against several existing task assignment algorithms, including Random, Distance First (DF), Strongest Computing Power First (SCF), and Shortest Stay-time First (SSF). In the Random algorithm, tasks are assigned arbitrarily to any nearby vehicle47. In the DF approach, vehicles within the communication range of the requesting vehicle are sorted by proximity, and tasks are assigned to the nearest vehicles first48. The SCF algorithm ranks nearby vehicles based on their computing power, prioritizing tasks for vehicles with the highest computing capabilities49. Finally, the SSF algorithm allocates tasks to vehicles expected to depart soonest, favoring those with the shortest available time to complete the task49. By comparing the performance of the proposed methodology against these benchmark algorithms, its efficacy and efficiency in task assignment can be thoroughly assessed.

Conclusion

Our study introduced a performance-optimized VEC framework that enhances task offloading in V2V vehicular environments characterized by high density and mobility. The proposed approach adopts a two-stage design: context-aware edge-node selection driven by dynamic topology capture through periodic beaconing, followed by cumulative score-based dynamic priority queuing at the selected edge node. By jointly accounting for relative speed, distance, task size, and task priority, the framework improves resource utilization and reduces queue build-up, thereby lowering end-to-end task latency. Comprehensive evaluation in OMNeT++/Veins/SUMO demonstrated consistent gains over baseline strategies across multiple metrics, including dissemination/system delay, packet delivery ratio, and task completion success rate. In addition, the newly reported task processing failure rate analysis confirms that the proposed mechanism sustains higher reliability under dense traffic and high mobility, where frequent link disruptions and queue overflow can degrade conventional methods. While these results validate the framework’s effectiveness, incorporating explicit security protections and incentive mechanisms for cooperative vehicles remains important future work. A detailed analysis of signalling overhead and energy consumption is also a valuable extension, particularly for power-limited scenarios and larger-scale deployments.
