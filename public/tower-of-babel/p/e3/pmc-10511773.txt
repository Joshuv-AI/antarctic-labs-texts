Does the RAPIDO trial suggest a benefit of post-operative chemotherapy after preoperative chemoradiation in rectal cancer? No, it does not.
Authors: J Socha, W Michalski, K Bujko
Published: 2023

Recently, Dijkstra et al.1 presented a post hoc analysis of the value of post-operative chemotherapy (pCT) in patients who received preoperative chemoradiation within the standard-of-care arm of the landmark RAPIDO trial. Depending on the hospital’s policy, pCT was either administered or not in this group. Three analyses were conducted to compare patients treated in hospitals with a policy to provide pCT (pCT+ group) with those treated in hospitals without this policy (pCT− group): (i) intention-to-treat analysis, including all patients who underwent curative resection; (ii) per-protocol analysis, including pCT+ patients who actually started pCT; and (iii) analysis including pCT+ patients who received at least 75% of the prescribed pCT cycles (Figure 1). Propensity score stratification was used to adjust for imbalances in confounders in analyses 2 and 3. Stratified Cox regression analysis was carried out for the cumulative probability of disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS).

Simplified Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) diagram from Dijkstra et al.1

All three analyses did not show statistically significant differences between the pCT+ and pCT− groups for DFS and OS. Analysis 1 revealed hazard ratios of 1.08 for DFS and 1.03 for OS, indicating a direction towards worse outcomes in the pCT+ group compared with the pCT− group. Analysis 2 showed hazard ratios of 0.78 for DFS and 0.82 for OS, while analysis 3 revealed ratios of 0.63 and 0.74, respectively, indicating a direction towards better outcomes in the pCT+ group compared with the pCT− group. Based on these findings, the authors concluded that their ‘data suggest a benefit of pCT’.

We disagree with this interpretation. The intention-to-treat analysis (analysis 1), which is the most appropriate method for comparing treatment efficacy, did not provide any indication of a benefit of pCT. Notably, the same conclusion was also drawn from the main RAPIDO article, which stated that the hospital’s policy on pCT did not affect primary and secondary outcomes.2 By contrast, analyses 2 and 3 are biased by exclusions. Figure 1 shows that a much higher proportion of patients were excluded from the pCT+ group compared with the pCT− group. The patients excluded from the pCT+ group did not receive pCT or complete all the cycles due to reasons such as being unfit or experiencing post-operative complications, as mentioned by the authors. Consequently, it is likely that the patients excluded from the pCT+ group generally had a poorer prognosis compared with those receiving pCT or those in the comparator group (pCT−). In other words, the comparator group is likely inflated by non-excluded patients with a poor prognosis, introducing a substantial risk of bias in analyses 2 and 3. Propensity score stratification cannot fully compensate for this bias, as it cannot adjust for unmeasurable confounders or factors not included in the model, such as performance score after surgery. Despite this, the authors based their conclusion on analyses 2 and 3 and not on analysis 1. In our opinion, the RAPIDO trial does not provide reliable evidence suggesting a benefit of pCT.
