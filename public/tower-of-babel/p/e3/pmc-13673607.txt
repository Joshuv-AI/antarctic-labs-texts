Information-seeking experiences during genetic testing and counseling: a qualitative study of patients at high risk for hereditary breast cancer
Authors: Sun-Young Park, Yeon-Joo Kim, Heejung Chae, Yoon-Jung Choi, Kum Hei Ryu, Eun-Gyeong Lee, Mijin An, Sun-Young Kong, So-Youn Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenetic testing and counseling have become increasingly prevalent in breast cancer treatment with the advancement of precision oncology. Understanding patients’ information-seeking experiences is essential for providing better genetic cancer services. However, these experiences are insufficiently understood. This qualitative study explored the information-seeking experiences during genetic testing and counseling of patients at high risk for hereditary breast cancer.MethodsFour focus group interviews were conducted with 17 breast cancer patients who had undergone genetic testing and counseling. Participants were purposively recruited from the National Cancer Center’s outpatient clinic in South Korea. Data were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed inductively using thematic analysis, complemented by a deductive approach informed by Lambert and Loiselle’s Health Information-Seeking Behavior (HISB) framework.ResultsThree main themes, derived from 24 codes and seven subthemes, captured the characteristics of HISB: (1) type of information sought—stage-specific information needs, (2) amount of information sought—varied preferences for information amount based on coping with information load and uncertainty, and (3) preferred methods of information delivery—desire for clear, reliable, and supportive communication from providers.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the need for personalized information delivery at each stage: core decision content before testing, and results-contingent plans after testing—all tailored to individual preferences and coping capacity for information load and uncertainty. This study underscores the critical role of empathic and clear communication from HCPs supported by supplementary materials in facilitating informed decision-making and improving outcomes for patients at high risk for hereditary breast cancer.Keywords: Genetic testing, Genetic counseling, Hereditary breast cancer, Information seeking-behavior, Patient-centered communication, Information needs

Background

Advancements in precision oncology have resulted in increased use of genetic testing and counseling in breast cancer care [1, 2]. For patients at high risk of hereditary breast cancer, early detection of pathogenic variants (PVs) through genetic testing is crucial for guiding cancer treatment decisions and implementing strategies such as risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy (RRSO) and contralateral prophylactic mastectomy (CPM) [3]. Approximately 5–10% of breast cancers are hereditary [1], most of which are associated with PVs in high-penetrance genes (BRCA1, BRCA2, TP53, CDH1, PTEN, PALB2, and STK11) or moderate-to-low-penetrance genes (ATM, CHEK2, BRIP1, RAD51C, and RAD51D) [3]. Genetic testing also enables cascade family testing, thereby improving outcomes for patients and their families [3].

Despite these clinical benefits, patients undergoing genetic testing often experience difficulties in both obtaining and comprehending relevant information [4, 5], as well as interpreting complex test results [4–6]. Insufficient information and misunderstanding of the information hinder patients’ adequate knowledge acquisition, compromise informed decision-making and coping related to disease management, and ultimately reduce their quality of life [7].

Patients undergoing genetic testing and counseling seek comprehensive yet practical information on treatment planning, cancer risk management, and family communication [8, 9]. However, the type and amount of information they seek can vary [7] depending on their gender, age, education level, and family cancer history [6, 9] and evolve across pre-, peri-, and post-testing stages [6]. Patients generally prefer accurate, relevant, comprehensible, and easily accessible information through multiple channels, such as online platforms, printed educational materials, and healthcare professionals (HCPs) [7, 10], among which patients primarily regard HCPs as the most trustworthy information sources [7].

Understanding patients’ information-seeking experiences is essential for developing tailored counseling strategies that support informed decision-making [11]. However, research on this topic remains limited, particularly in Asian countries, where patients’ unique informational needs and preferences are not well understood [7, 9]. Such experiences are influenced by healthcare systems and cultural norms, which vary significantly between Asian and Western contexts [12]. Therefore, addressing this gap requires examining these experiences in diverse cultural settings.

In South Korea, the national health insurance has covered genetic testing for individuals at risk of hereditary breast and ovarian cancer since 2013, significantly reducing the financial barriers to genetic testing [13]. However, genetic counseling is neither reimbursed nor officially recognized, and no national certification for genetic counselors is currently available [14]. Consequently, doctors, advanced practice nurses, or trained nurses often provide genetic counseling depending on institutional resources and staffing [14, 15]. Although national health insurance coverage has improved access to genetic testing, the lack of coverage for counseling may result in patients’ information-seeking needs and preferences being unmet, creating a distinct pattern of information-seeking experiences in the Korean context.

This qualitative study aimed to explore the information-seeking experiences of patients at high risk for hereditary breast cancer during genetic testing and counseling in the Korean healthcare system. Specifically, the study addressed the following research question: What types and amounts of information do these patients seek, and what are their preferred methods of information delivery?

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study adopted a naturalistic paradigm to clarify patients’ experiences in real-world settings by generating context-dependent knowledge [16, 17]. This naturalistic paradigm acknowledges that reality differs across individuals, is socially constructed, and is contextually interpreted. Based on this paradigm, this study observed patients’ information-seeking experiences as they naturally occurred, interpreted them while reflecting patients’ meanings and perspectives, and assumed that contextual features of the Korean healthcare system and patient–HCP interactions shape these experiences [17].

This study was informed by the health information-seeking behavior (HISB) framework proposed by Lambert and Loiselle [7], which conceptualizes information-seeking experiences as the use of specific actions and strategies to acquire information and integrates the type, depth, and seeking methods of information, which can vary across contexts and over time. Based on this framework, we explored patients’ information-seeking experiences across two dimensions: (1) the information dimension, focusing on the type (content and range) and amount (level of detail) of information sought, and (2) the methods dimension, which encompasses preferred delivery methods shaped by the accessibility, credibility, and clarity of the information provided.

This study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines [18]. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the National Cancer Center (Reference Number: NCC2023-0274). All participants provided written informed consent before the focus group interviews (FGIs).

Recruitment and data collection

We recruited eligible participants from the outpatient clinic at the National Cancer Center in Korea by sending text message advertisements containing study information. A research nurse (M.J.A.) assessed eligibility and explained the study’s purpose, methods, and interview questions to those who expressed interest in participating.

Eligible participants were breast cancer patients aged 20 years or older who had undergone genetic testing and counseling owing to a risk of hereditary cancer. Patients were excluded if they were simultaneously receiving anti-cancer treatment, such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery, that required hospitalization or had metastatic cancer, because their information-seeking experience might differ from that of the target population.

We used purposive sampling to ensure that participants with diverse genetic testing options (Next-Generation Sequencing [NGS] and Sanger sequencing of BRCA1/2 [BRCA1/2 testing]) and test results (PV, not detected [negative], or variant of uncertain significance [VUS]) were included for a comprehensive exploration of the patient experience.

FGIs were conducted using a semi-structured guide developed based on the HISB framework [7] (Table 1). Probing and emergent questions were used to clarify participants’ responses and explore and compare their diverse experiences. A trained female researcher (S.Y.P.) with seven years of experience conducting qualitative studies on hereditary cancer patients and no prior relationship with the participants conducted all face-to-face FGIs. The FGIs were held at the National Cancer Center or a nearby café, audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and supplemented with field notes to capture contextual details. We anonymized all data by assigning pseudonyms for names of people, locations, and hospitals to ensure confidentiality.

Data analysis

Data analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s [19, 20] thematic analysis, utilizing both inductive and deductive approaches. Two researchers (S.Y.P., and M.J.A.) independently analyzed the interview data through the following steps: (1) becoming familiar with the data by repeatedly reading transcripts, (2) generating initial inductive codes directly from the data; (3) mapping inductive codes onto deductive categories guided by the HISB framework (type of information sought, amount of information sought, and preferred information delivery method), (4) grouping codes into subthemes within each category, (5) reviewing and refining subthemes to develop coherent themes, and (6) defining and naming the final themes with representative quotations [19, 20]. In this study, the HISB framework served as a descriptive framework for structuring participants’ information-seeking experiences, rather than as an interpretive lens for explaining underlying processes. All inductively generated codes were fully mapped onto the three HISB dimensions, with no residual or cross-cutting concepts identified. The analysis was iterative and included member checking to enhance credibility. We considered data saturation to have been achieved when no new codes or subthemes were identified across the focus groups. Coding and data management were performed using MAXQDA software (VERBI GmbH, Berlin, Germany).

Methodological rigor

We enhanced the rigor of this study by applying Lincoln and Guba’s [21] four criteria of trustworthiness. A qualitative research expert (J.H.S.) and a physician specializing in hereditary breast cancer (S.Y.J.) conducted peer review to reduce interpretive bias and errors and ensure credibility. Transferability was addressed using purposive sampling to collect rich experiences and providing thick detailed descriptions of participant experiences, enabling readers to assess the applicability of the findings to other settings. Dependability was achieved by maintaining field notes that recorded participant responses and researcher impressions to enhance the transparency and tracking of the research process. We further ensured consistency by using the same interview guide across all FGIs and rigorously following Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis framework.

Results

Participants characteristics

Twenty patients expressed interest in participating in the FGIs; however, one withdrew due to health issues and two were excluded because they were family members of other participants. Consequently, 17 participants (mean age: 49.1 years; range: 35–64) joined four FGIs (duration: 60–92 min, with 4–5 participants per group) (Table 2).

Most participants were married (n = 14), were unemployed (n = 13), had children (n = 14), and were college or university graduates (n = 12) (Table 2). All participants had relatives with cancer. Regarding the genetic test results, five participants had BRCA1 or BRCA2 PV, one had MSH6 PV, seven had VUS, and six had negative results. Theme 1. Type of information sought: testing stage-specific information needs

This theme captures how information content and scope vary across the genetic testing trajectory for patients at high risk for hereditary breast cancer.

At the pre-testing stage, the participants primarily sough decision-critical information that clarified the testing necessity, such as its purpose, benefits, and available options. At the post-testing stage, their information needs shifted and broadened toward interpretation of their results, individualized prognosis, results-contingent management plans, and implications for their children (Table 3).

Subtheme 1.1. Seeking information on testing necessity for decision-making before testing

This subtheme illustrates the participants’ need to understand the necessity and purpose of genetic testing to make informed decisions. Before undergoing genetic testing, participants sought information related to “why testing is necessary.” To understand testing necessity, they demanded information regarding its purpose, potential benefits, and available options to make informed decisions about genetic testing. One participant stated:

“(When deciding on the genetic testing) I wished the doctor had explained why the test was necessary and how it helps me.” (Participant 3, who chose NGS).

Some participants (3, 10, and 15) sought additional information regarding testing options between NGS panels and BRCA1/2 testing, including the tests’ clinical relevance, gene coverage, and potential benefits, because they lacked sufficient knowledge about these differences. However, they remained confused regarding the differences between these options and chose their testing type based on cost rather than a clear understanding of the implications. This lack of information and limited understanding of the necessity and implications of testing led participants to experience uncertainty and, in some cases, decision regret. One participant recalled the following:

“The doctor just recommended testing without explaining why. If I had known its impact on future decisions, I would have chosen more expensive testing (NGS panel).” (Participant 10, who chose BRCA1/2 testing).

Subtheme 1.2. Seeking results-based prognosis and management after testing

This subtheme describes participants’ need for information to understand the implications of their testing results and develop management strategies for themselves and their families, representing the broader spectrum of information required at the post-testing stage than at the pre-testing stage.

Following disclosure, participants’ information needs shifted from understanding testing necessity to interpreting the results and their implications for prognosis and clinical management. Specifically, they wanted to know the meaning of their results (e.g., PV, VUS, or negative), what treatment decisions were needed (e.g., chemotherapy, radiotherapy, mastectomy, and breast reconstruction), whether other specialist consultations were necessary, and which cancer-prevention strategies would be appropriate (e.g., RRSO, CPM, or surveillance). One participant stated the following regarding the information she needed during post-testing counseling:

“(After testing) I wish they (HCPs) had given specific information like ‘patients with your results typically need this type of plan,’ or ‘our hospital provides these consultations for you’” (Participant 12, tested negative).

Participants also reported the need for information regarding their children. Some expressed concern about the implications of their genetic results for their children and wanted information regarding inheritance probabilities and practical guidance, such as when and how their children should be tested, lifestyle recommendations, and appropriate screening schedules. One participant stated:

“I am extremely worried about my (18-year-old) son inheriting the same genes. I wish I had known the inheritance probability and guidance for managing my children’s diet.” (Participant 5, detected BRCA1 PV).

Across result types, participants with negative or VUS results often expressed greater confusion, uncertainty, and dissatisfaction with post-test counseling. Their counseling sessions often ended abruptly after a brief label “negative” or “uncertain” without risk clarification or follow-up management. Consequently, they sought further information, such as whether additional testing was needed, what lifestyle modifications were recommended, or what cancer surveillance methods were appropriate for them. One participant emphasized her need for management guidance as follows:

“They (HCPs) strongly pushed for genetic testing, but after getting a negative result, they just said, ‘No mutations.’ If they had explained what management would be needed afterward, I would have understood the test’s value.” (Participant 12, tested negative on BRCA 1/2 testing).

Theme 2. Amount of information sought: varied information preferences based on coping with information load and uncertainty

This theme illustrates the amount and level of detail in the information patients sought, showing the variation in the depth of information they wanted, from essential and concise to comprehensive and detailed based on individual coping strategies with information burden and manage to uncertainty.

Subtheme 2.1. Preferring key information under information burden

This subtheme describes participants’ preferences for concise and essential information as a method for coping with cognitive and emotional overload.

Some participants (1, 3, 4, and 9) preferred brief, decision-enabling summaries, citing limited capacity to process and retain dense genetics content. One participant exemplified this as follows:

“(After testing) I just asked the doctor to tell me whether I had the gene (PV) or not. I wish they had explained more by focusing on the important point, rather than details.” (Participant 4, 40 years old, tested negative on BRCA 1/2 testing).

Participants explained that these preferences stemmed from their concurrent cognitive and emotional burdens related to cancer diagnosis and treatment planning, which diminished their ability to comprehend and retain the information provided. During this period, they were overwhelmed by the need to make multiple complex medical decisions, including selecting diagnostic tests and treatment options, while attending consultations with various specialists (e.g., pathologists, gynecologists, and oncologists). Amid this cognitively and emotionally demanding context and considering the information received from multiple HCPs, participants reported difficulties with both understanding and recalling the information provided. Consequently, they preferred concise and essential information that directly facilitated their immediate medical decisions, thereby reducing the cognitive burden associated with complex genetic information. One participant stated the following:

“After being diagnosed with cancer, too many things were happening at the same time. Even though the HCPs explained many things, I couldn’t remember any of them. I wanted to choose the correct test but didn’t really know what was right. So, I wished the doctor would just tell me simply that the test is necessary instead of giving long explanations.” (Participant 8, 41 years old).

Subtheme 2.2. Preferring comprehensive information to manage uncertainty

In contrast, others actively sought in-depth detail to regain a sense of control and optimize decisions about test selection and management.

Some participants (5, 10, 12, and 13) wanted to receive comprehensive and in-depth information beyond basic explanations. These participants believed that detailed information would enable them to identify the most appropriate testing options and health management strategies, thereby optimizing their health outcomes. Thus, acquiring detailed information was their coping strategy for managing uncertainty regarding health outcomes.

Some participants were dissatisfied because they felt that the information they received from HCPs did not go beyond their existing knowledge. Therefore, they sought more in-depth explanations that would provide them with new knowledge and enable them to make more informed decisions. One participant explained why she valued comprehensive information and how it could have influenced her test choice:

“I need more in-depth information. When the explanation doesn’t go beyond what I already know, I feel less satisfied… If the doctor had explained the details more concretely, I would have more carefully considered which test (NGS or BRCA 1/2 test) to choose.” (Participant 10, who chose BRCA1/2 testing).

Theme 3. Preferred methods of information delivery: desire for clear, reliable, and supportive communication from providers

This theme illustrates the participants’ preferred methods of information delivery during genetic testing and counseling, showing that they valued clear and comprehensible explanations, perceived HCPs as the most trusted sources, and required empathic delivery and supplemented by take-home materials (Table 3).

Subtheme 3.1. Need for clear and plain explanations

This subtheme highlights that genetic terminology often hindered comprehension and question asking during genetic testing and counseling. Participants consistently emphasized the need for simple, plain, and comprehensible explanations of genetic testing and counseling. Several participants (9, 10, 11, and 13) found genetic terminology and concepts difficult to understand, making it challenging to follow discussions or ask informed questions. Consequently, several participants passively agreed with HCPs without fully understanding the information provided during genetic testing. One participant stated:

“When I had to choose genetic testing, I just nodded along, saying ‘yes, yes, yes,’ as my doctor talked about it. Because it was difficult to understand what the doctor said, I didn’t know what questions I should ask about genetic testing.” (Participant 12, 59 years old, who chose BRCA 1/2 testing).

Conversely, participants could better comprehend information when it was tailored to their level of understanding and delivered using plain language. This made them feel respected and supported, leading them to appreciate the efforts of HCPs. One participant recalled the following:

“(At the post-testing counseling) The (genetic) terminology was difficult. But when they (HCPs) explained it in a simple way, I thought, ‘They truly care about patients.’” (Participant 1, 50 years old, who had a detected VUS from BRCA 1/2 testing).

Subtheme 3.2. Need for reliable information through empathic counseling

This subtheme describes participants’ desire to receive trustworthy information from HCPs through empathic and supportive communication. Participants expressed the need for HCPs to deliver reliable information through empathic communication. Several participants sought information from online sources or patient communities, as well as HCPs. However, they often encountered inaccurate or contradictory information between what HCPs and other sources provided. This inconsistency caused confusion and reinforced their desire to receive reliable information directly from HCPs, who they perceived as the most credible and reassuring sources. One participant explained:

“Patient community information is hard to verify. One source says, ‘Don’t drink coffee or alcohol,’ but the hospital (HCPs) says, ‘It’s fine, you can eat everything.’ It’s so confusing. I hope the HCPs gives this information, so that I can rely on it.” (Participant 7).

Beyond reliable information, participants emphasized the need for HCPs to deliver information through empathic and warm communication. They reported that HCPs’ kind and caring manner encouraged them to ask questions more actively. However, one participant recalled the following:

“When I tried to ask a question, the doctor said, ‘I already explained it.’ I felt I shouldn’t ask any more questions.” (Participant 12, tested negative on the NGS panel).

Subtheme 3.3. Need for supplementary supports

This subtheme highlights participants’ need for supplementary materials and guidance to compensate for limited consultation time and support better comprehension and recall of genetic information.

Several participants (1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 10, and 17) reported difficulties in understanding and retaining the information provided during short consultations, which typically last only 3–10 min in Korea. This limited consultation time made it challenging for participants to process information, ask questions, and clarify uncertainties, as many felt guilty about taking up their HCPs’ time. One participant stated:

“I feel like asking questions is a burden on the HCPs. With a bunch of patients waiting, I know my consultation time is limited, and I worry about taking up consultation time from the next patient when I ask a further question of the doctor.” (Participant 17, 51 years old).

They expressed the need for supplementary materials, including pre-counseling guidance, written summaries of key points, and examples based on other patients’ experiences, to compensate for this issue. Participants suggested that such supplementary materials would allow them to review and comprehend the information more effectively once they felt calmer and more reflective. Another participant noted:

“It would be helpful if they wrote down the test results and counseling content by hand. That way, I could review it later when I’m more clear-headed.” (Participant 1, 50 years old).

Discussion

This qualitative study explored the information-seeking experiences during genetic testing and counseling of patients at high risk for hereditary breast cancer. Three themes revealed the (1) type of information sought—stage-specific information needs, (2) amount of information sought—varied preferences for information amount based on coping with information load and uncertainty, and (3) preferred methods of information delivery—desire for clear, reliable, and supportive communication from providers. These findings provide insights for developing optimized genetic information delivery strategies and supporting informed decision-making.

This study’s findings demonstrate that information-seeking is a dynamic process influenced by contextual factors, thereby supporting and enriching the HISB framework [7]. Our findings provide contextual insights into patients’ information-seeking experiences during genetic testing and counseling in South Korea. Specifically, patients’ information needs varied according to the stage of genetic testing and their cognitive burden, emotional load, and coping strategies. These findings highlight the need for personalized information-delivery strategies that account for variations in content and depth according to patients’ genetic testing stage, cognitive burden, and coping type in genetic testing and counseling.

Notably, we observed a mismatch between patients’ preferred information type and that recommended by international guidelines at the pre-testing stage [3]. This study showed that some patients sought only essential information because of their emotional and cognitive burdens, whereas the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines recommend a broader range of information covering possible outcomes, testing options, costs, and ethical or legal considerations, to ensure informed decision-making [3]. These findings indicate that providing comprehensive information, as recommended by international guidelines, may overwhelm patients when preparing for genetic testing. Therefore, adopting a phased information-delivery approach that considers patients’ cognitive and emotional burdens is essential. For example, during the pre-testing stage, key decision-making information should be prioritized, while additional details can be provided later through written or digital supplementary materials to enhance patients’ understanding.

At the post-testing stage, patients expressed a range of information needs that were generally similar across test result types (i.e., PV, VUS, and negative) and aligned with findings from previous systematic reviews on the information needs of individuals at risk for hereditary cancer [9]. However, patients who received negative or VUS results reported unmet information needs. They sought specific and detailed explanations regarding their cancer risk and appropriate follow-up management based on their results. These findings indicate that management guidelines for patients with negative or VUS results remain unclear, which is supported by previous studies [4–6]. Therefore, this study reinforces the importance of providing comprehensive post-testing information, particularly for patients with negative or VUS results. Systematic post-test counseling with tailored support for patients with negative or VUS results could effectively address their unmet needs.

Notably, this study suggests refinement to the HISB framework. Although the HISB framework acknowledges that individual variations in information-seeking arise from personal needs, emotions, and information-processing capacity [7], it does not fully explain the specific mechanisms underlying them. Our study advances this understanding by illustrating that individual differences in information-seeking are shaped by patients’ cognitive and emotional burdens and their coping strategies for managing disease-related uncertainty. Those with a higher cognitive burden prefer concise information, whereas those seeking control and reduced uncertainty prefer comprehensive information. These results deepen the HISB framework by conceptualizing information-seeking as a coping mechanism, demonstrating that coping styles differ according to patients’ cognitive burden and uncertainty management strategies.

Our findings indicate that information provision alone is insufficient and should be complemented by supplementary educational materials. In the Korean outpatient context, consultation times were typically brief (3–10 min), limiting patients’ ability to process complex information during visits [6]. Accordingly, a time-redesign approach such as pre-visit priming to prepare patients, in-visit delivery of key messages by HCPs using plain language, and post-visit results-contingent summaries. These strategies should be integrated with multimodal education, including printed materials, interactive websites, and patient-facing applications (e.g., chatbots or mobile apps), which enable self-paced review and enhance long-term recall [6].

This study highlights the pivotal role of HCPs’ communication skills in shaping patients’ information-seeking experiences. Patients at high risk for hereditary breast cancer emphasized the importance of plain and comprehensive explanations delivered with empathy and a supportive attitude, viewing HCPs as their most credible and reassuring information source. These findings are consistent with those of previous research showing that interpersonal communication skills, such as empathy, respect, and avoidance of medical jargon, enhance patient engagement, comprehension, and informed decision-making [5, 8, 10]. Therefore, patient-centered communication from HCPs is essential to enhance their comprehension and ultimately improve health outcomes among patients at high risk for hereditary breast cancer.

This study has some limitations. First, this study was conducted at a single institution in South Korea; thus, the findings may have limited transferability to healthcare settings in other geographical regions. Second, the study population comprised women diagnosed with breast cancer, which may limit the applicability of the findings to individuals with other types of cancer or those without a cancer diagnosis. Finally, the study relied on retrospective interviews on genetic testing and counseling experiences, introducing a potential risk of recall bias affecting participants’ responses.

Conclusion

This qualitative study explored the information-seeking experiences during genetic testing and counseling of patients at high risk for hereditary breast cancer. Information needs among patients at high risk for hereditary breast cancer are testing stage-specific and sensitive to cognitive load or coping strategies. Preferences range from concise essentials to comprehensive detail, and patients prioritize clear, reliable, empathic communication from HCPs, supported by supplementary materials. These findings highlight the need for personalized information delivery-core decision content pre-test, and results-contingent plans post-test. Healthcare professionals should deliver clear and comprehensive information through empathetic communication supported by supplementary materials. Such implementation strategies could enhance comprehension, reduce unmet informational needs and strengthen informed decision-making.
