Microstrain-engineered platinum nanoclathrins for fuel cells
Authors: Zhiyong Yu, Qing Yao, Chen Sun, Liangqun Zhao, Yonggang Feng, Rui Qin, Fei Xue, Xueli Yan, Zhiwei Hu, Wei-Hsiang Huang, Chih-Wen Pao, Lili Han, Nanjun Chen, Xiaoqing Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificancePlatinum (Pt)-based catalysts are vital yet costly for proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFC). While well-defined nanostructures enhance Pt utilization and stability, their dense structures impede mass transport, limiting practical performance. Inspired by clathrin-mediated endocytosis in biology, we develop Pt nanoclathrins (NCLs) featuring nanoporous shells and internal cavities. This unique architecture combines channel-rich structures for efficient fuel transport with structural robustness against degradation. The Pt NCLs (0.1 mgPt cm−2, 80 °C, 200 kPaabs) achieve rated power densities of 1.25 W cm−2 in H2/O2 (500/500 sccm) and 0.83 W cm−2 in H2/Air (500/1,500 sccm) as well as remarkable stability (95.7% activity retention after 30,000 accelerated stress test) in fuel cells. This bioinspired design advances durable and high-performance PEMFC catalysts.

Proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) have emerged as pivotal sustainable energy technologies for mitigating global energy crises and environmental degradation (1–5). While platinum (Pt)-based catalysts maintain their dominance in practical PEMFC applications (6–8), their commercial scalability remains constrained by inherent material scarcity and prohibitive costs (8, 9). To address these challenges, extensive research has been directed toward optimizing the atomic utilization, intrinsic activity, and stability of Pt-based catalysts through rational nanostructuring (10–14). Currently, researchers have fabricated Pt-based nanostructures with precisely controlled sizes and shapes through a variety of synthetic techniques and systemically investigated their influences on catalytic properties (15–21). Despite that significant advancements in catalytic performance have been achieved (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), these well-defined nanocrystals frequently undergo agglomeration or structural deformation during electrochemical cycling, leading to progressive performance degradation under prolonged operational conditions (18, 22). Hollow nanostructures, the three-dimensional architecture with an internal cavity, exhibit features such as larger specific surface area, higher density of catalytic active sites, and resistance to agglomeration during catalytic reactions (23–27). These attributes not only reduce noble metal consumption but also synergistically enhance catalytic durability and reaction kinetics (23, 27). Nevertheless, conventional hollow configurations exhibit inherent limitations in shell permeability, resulting in suboptimal mass transport efficiency (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). This limitation becomes particularly pronounced in practical fuel cell, where fuels need to transport across the interfaces to reach the reaction zones, leading to significantly higher mass transfer resistance compared to liquid half-cells utilizing a rotating disk electrode (RDE) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) (28).

Within the intricate biological systems of living organisms, sophisticated material transport mechanisms and metabolic processes continuously occur, facilitated by specialized channel and cavity architectures that ensure efficient biochemical operations (29, 30). Notably, the clathrin-mediated endocytosis system, where a porous and hollow network formed by fundamental structural units effectively encapsulates cellular vesicles during membrane invagination, thereby maintaining vesicles shape and reducing the unstable factors during its formation process (29, 31, 32). The evolutionary optimization of such channel-rich architectures presents compelling biomimetic potential for engineering advanced reaction systems requiring enhanced mass transfer capabilities. Therefore, the development of clathrin-mimetic Pt nanostructures emerges as a promising strategy to overcome the critical mass transport limitations in operational fuel cell systems (Fig. 1A). Nevertheless, the fabrication of clathrin-like architectures in noble metals presents substantial synthetic challenges. The strong metallic bonding interactions and anisotropic crystallization tendencies characteristic of noble metals favor the formation of thermodynamically stable, symmetrical configurations. These inherent properties drive noble metal nanomaterials to assemble into highly ordered crystalline structures rather than the desired porous architectures with engineered channels and cavities (33–35).

Herein, we demonstrate the successful fabrication of clathrin-mimetic noble metal nanostructures featuring nanoporous shells and internal cavities via a selenium (Se)-mediated self-assembly strategy (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). The creation of such nanoclathrin relies on the in situ formed Se, where the amorphous structure induces the disordered growth of noble metals on the surface, ultimately establishing the nanoclathrin architecture. This approach enables precise control over the macroscopic structure of nanoclathrins, including size, shell thickness, and composition. Crucially, microstrain engineering through grain boundaries manipulation allows performance optimization of platinum nanoclathrins (Pt NCLs) for half reactions in fuel cells. When implemented as both cathode and anode catalysts in practical fuel cells, the Pt NCLs-based membrane electrode assembly (MEA) achieves rated power densities of 1.25 W cm−2 in H2/O2 and 0.83 W cm−2 in H2/Air due to the promoted mass transfer and optimized microstrain. This performance positions Pt NCLs as one of the best-performing pure-Pt catalysts and makes them even competitive with the state-of-the-art Pt-alloy catalysts. The interconnected channel structure endows the Pt NCLs catalyst with outstanding stability, where the initial activity retains 95.7% after 30,000 accelerated stress test (AST) cycles and the working voltage loss is negligible over 1,000 h operation at 1.5 A cm−2 in a single cell.

Results and Discussion

As shown by the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3), the fabricated Pt NCLs exhibit internal cavities characteristic with uniform distribution. Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (AC-HAADF-STEM) images on Pt NCLs further confirm the structural characteristics of Pt NCLs, which are internally hollow and possess a nanoporous shell (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). The AC-HAADF-STEM images of individual Pt NCL vividly show that the clathrin-like structure is assembled from ultrasmall Pt nanoclusters closely packed on the shell (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and C). Additionally, it can be observed from the corresponding chromatic image that there are abundant nanopores existing on the shell (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B and D). These structural properties closely resemble those of clathrin in living organisms, which also feature an internal hollow cavity and a porous surface (29, 32). To acquire a more comprehensively understanding of the local structures, three-dimensional (3D) electron tomography was conducted for Pt NCLs (Movie S1). The 3D structural visualization clearly depicts the 3D configuration of a single Pt NCL, revealing a distorted and rugged shell with numerous nanoporous structures (Fig. 1F1). The laminated cross-sections shown in Fig. 1 F2 and F3 disclose the hollow cavity within the Pt shell as well as the pore structure that bridges the inner and outer spaces (36). This channel-rich structure is further elucidated by the N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm and pore size distribution (Fig. 1G), where Pt NCLs exhibit abundant nanopores with diameters of approximately 4 nm, serving as channels for efficient gas transport throughout the entire structure (37, 38).

High-resolution lattice images were collected to elucidate the detailed crystallographic structures (Fig. 2). Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 reveal that the Pt NCLs consist of numerous tiny Pt fragments accompanied by extensive grain boundaries, as highlighted by the dashed lines. These nanoclusters are densely packed in diverse orientations and spacings (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Correspondingly, the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern confirms the polycrystalline nature, with diffraction rings corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of face-centered cubic Pt (Fig. 2B). The AC-HAADF-STEM image and corresponding 3D atom-overlapping Gaussian-function fitting mapping illustrate the presence of nanopore structures and the atomic configuration surrounding the nanopores (Fig. 2C). Additionally, continuous grain boundaries are evident, as depicted in Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S7. Besides the boundary formed by the (220) and (111) planes with rotation angle of 29°, the presence of grain boundary structure between the (111) planes with 61° rotation angle can also be observed (Fig. 2D). A specific grain boundary depicted in Fig. 2E displays two detached Pt (111) and Pt (200) nanograins with lattice spacings of 2.38 and 1.92 Å, and the rotation angle of these two nanograins is estimated to be approximately 21°. The interactions between two nanograins with different orientations are also illustrated in SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and C. The aforementioned grain boundaries exhibit significant atomic distortions and will give rise to local lattice strain in the surrounding regions (39–41). Consequently, the geometric phase analysis (GPA) was utilized to analyze the strain distribution of grain boundaries in Pt NCLs (42). As depicted in Fig. 2F, the strain mapping derived from Fig. 2C reveals the heterogeneous distribution of lattice strain, where Area 1 is compressed due to the compressive strain and Area 2 is under tensile strain. The corresponding strain mapping of SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and C also indicate the inhomogeneous distribution of lattice strain (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 B and D). In these grain-boundary-dense nanoclathrins, the clusters rotate randomly according to the accommodation of orientation attachment, thereby inducing localized lattice strain at all the interfacial boundaries within the entire nanoclathrin structure. Different from the lattice strain (so-called macrostrain) that refers to the global change in the lattice parameters compared to the equilibrium crystal structure, local lattice strain, also known as microstrain, quantifies the local deviation of atoms from their ideal position in the crystal structure and can effectively modulate the electronic properties (41).

To understand the formation mechanism of the unique nanoclathrins architecture, we tracked the evolution process via collecting products from different reaction time (SI Appendix, Figs. S9 and S10). According to the analysis results, the transformation process from amorphous structure to crystalline plays a crucial role in the formation of Pt NCLs (SI Appendix, Figs. S11–S13). Specifically, the disordered structure of in situ formed amorphous Se (a-Se) core can overcome the lattice mismatch, facilitating the disordered growth of Pt on its surface (SI Appendix, Figs. S11–S15). The ultrasmall Pt nanocrystals are closely packed on the surface of a-Se core with varying orientation, therefore shaping an a-Se@Pt core–shell structure (SI Appendix, Figs. S12 and S13). The corresponding Pt NCLs with abundant porous-channel structures can be successfully derived from such core–shell architecture (Fig. 3A).

With the deep understanding of the formation mechanism, we have achieved precise regulation of the macroscopic structures of nanoclathrin architecture, including size, shell thickness, noble metal, and heterometallic composition. For instance, by controlling the size of a-Se cores during the nucleation and growth stage, Pt NCLs with sizes ranging from ~87 to ~325 nm can be obtained (Fig. 3 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S16). Furthermore, the thickness of the nanoporous shell can be manipulated by altering the amount of Pt that grown on the surface of the a-Se cores. As shown in Fig. 3 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S17, the shell thickness of Pt NCLs can be controlled within the range of ~6.1 to ~13.9 nm. While reducing the Pt precursor to half of the optimal amount results in insufficient surface coverage, yielding an incomplete, bowl-shaped, or hemispherical morphology (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). In addition to the morphology regulation, the composition of the nanoporous shell can also be modulated. Introducing a second metal enables the expansion of the nanoporous shell’s components. Fig. 3 F and G and SI Appendix, Figs. S19–S21 show that we have successfully achieved the controlled synthesis of dual metal components, including Pt–Pd nanoclathrins as well as Pt–Ni nanoclathrins and Pt–Co nanoclathrins. Moreover, this strategy is universal and can be applied to synthesize nanoclathrin structures of various noble metals. By replacing Pt with other noble metals, Pd, Rh, and Ir nanoclathrins featuring internal cavities and nanoporous shell can be obtained (Fig. 3 H and I and SI Appendix, Fig. S22).

As discussed above, microstrain (local lattice strain) arises at interfacial boundaries within the entire nanoclathrin and can effectively modulate the electronic properties. Therefore, by continuously controlling the density of grain boundaries in Pt NCLs, the degree of the microstrain can be precisely regulated. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S23, the macroscopic structures of Pt NCLs with different degrees of grain boundaries remain well-preserved during microstrain modulation. Fig. 4A1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S24A reveal the microscopic structure of initial Pt NCLs, where abundant grain boundaries with various atom orientations exist, as highlighted by the dotted lines (denoted as Pt NCLs-MS1; MSx, x represents the microstrain degree). With the ordering of structures, short-range grains with diverse orientation gradually transform into long-range ordered grains, demonstrating a continuous reduction in grain boundaries (Fig. 4 B1–D1 and SI Appendix, Figs. S25A–S27A). In contrast to Pt NCLs-MS1, AC-HAADF-STEM images of Pt NCLs-MS4 show that long-range grains with ordered atom orientation are prevalent, as highlighted by the dotted lines (Fig. 4D1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S27A). Correspondingly, strain mapping via GPA shows that the color intensity, representing strain degree, varies significantly across the structure in Pt NCLs-MS1, indicating intense microstrain (Fig. 4A2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S24B). With the transformation to long-range ordered grains, the microstrain gradually decreases as evidenced by Fig. 4 B2–D2 and SI Appendix, Figs. S25B–S27B. Additionally, XRD analysis was conducted on Pt NCLs to investigate the evolution of microstrain. The measured full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the diffraction peak narrows as the structural order increases (2.691 for Pt NCLs-MS1, 2.565 for Pt NCLs-MS2, 2.450 for Pt NCLs-MS3, and 2.238 for Pt NCLs-MS4), indicating a reduction in microstrain degree (Fig. 4E) (41, 43). To quantitatively characterize the density of grain boundaries, XAFS spectroscopy was conducted to analyze the coordination environment of Pt NCLs. As depicted in Fig. 4F and SI Appendix, Fig. S28, the intensity of the Pt–Pt scattering peak increases with the structural ordering. Fitting results reveal that the Pt NCLs-MS1, with the most grain boundaries, exhibits the lowest coordination number of 6.39. The coordination number progressively increases with structural ordering (7.07 for Pt NCLs-MS2 and 8.02 for Pt NCLs-MS3), reaching 8.74 for Pt NCLs-MS4 (SI Appendix, Figs. S28 and S29 and Table S1). These findings demonstrate a positive correlation between microstrain degree and grain boundary density. Specifically, the FWHM (x-axis), representing microstrain, shows positive relationship with the coordination number (y-axis), representing grain boundary density (Fig. 4G), thereby demonstrating continuous control over the microscopic structure of Pt nanoclathrin.

XPS and surface valence band spectra were utilized to evaluate the influence of microstrain degree on the electronic structure of Pt (Fig. 5 A and B). The d-band centers of Pt NCLs-MSx are highly sensitive to the microstrain degree, shifting negatively with the decrease of microstrain degree (Fig. 5B). Considering the widely tunable electronic structures, it is expected to correspondingly regulate the absorption of chemical species on active sites and finally reach the optimized performance of specific electrochemical reactions (44). Consequently, the Pt NCLs with controllable microstrain were employed in the half reactions of fuel cells, oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) and hydrogen oxidation reaction (HOR). Fig. 5C presents the ORR polarization curves of Pt NCLs-MSx, Johnson Matthey Pt/C (JM Pt/C), and Pt NPs tested in O2-saturated 0.1 M HClO4. Specifically, Pt NCLs-MSx demonstrate significantly higher half-wave potential compared to both JM Pt/C and Pt NPs, highlighting the crucial role of the nanoclathrin structure in facilitating the catalysis. The mass and specific activities of Pt NCLs at different microstrain degree are illustrated in Fig. 5D (SI Appendix, Fig. S30 and Table S2). The curves exhibit volcano-shaped microstrain–activity relationship, with peak ORR activity observed at Pt NCLs-MS2 (Fig. 5D). This indicates that microstrain modulation can achieve the optimization of catalytic activity, with an appropriate microstrain degree yielding the best performance. Pt NCLs-MS2, the most active catalyst, exhibit mass and specific activities as high as 0.732 A mgPt−1 and 2.96 mA cmPt−2 at 0.9 V vs. reversible hydrogen electrode (vs. RHE), which are 4.5/11.4 times greater than those of JM Pt/C and 6.6/7.5 times greater than those of Pt NPs, respectively. Additionally, Fig. 5E shows that Pt NCLs also exhibit strongly microstrain-dependent activities when catalyzing HOR in 0.1 M HClO4. For this reaction, volcano-shaped curves are observed from both mass activities and specific activities (Fig. 5F). The best-performing catalyst, Pt NCLs-MS2, achieves mass and specific activities up to 0.513 A mgPt−1 and 2.08 mA cmPt−2 at 0.02 V vs. RHE. These values are substantially improved compared with JM Pt/C and Pt NPs, being 4.3/11.0 times greater than those of JM Pt/C and 5.9/6.8 times greater than those of Pt NPs, respectively (Fig. 5F). Additionally, we evaluate the highly porous nature and large accessible surface area of the studied catalysts using different gas sorption isotherms. Based on the N2 sorption isotherm and pore size distribution in Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Fig. S31, the Pt NCLs possess numerous nanopores with diameters of approximately 4 nm, which can function as channels for gas transmission throughout the entire structure. Furthermore, the enhanced CO2 uptake of Pt NCLs has been revealed by CO2 sorption isotherms (Fig. 5G), indicating a more developed porous structure compared to JM Pt/C and Pt NPs. This superior porosity facilitates the accessibility and transport of reactant gases (e.g., O2) within the catalyst structure (37, 38, 45). Therefore, a mechanistic model for enhanced performance during catalysis was proposed. From a macroscopic structural perspective, the interconnected channel structures of Pt NCLs facilitate efficient mass transport; from a more microscopic structural viewpoint, the high-density grain boundaries within Pt NCLs accelerate reaction kinetics, highlighting significant potential for practical fuel cell applications (Fig. 5H).

The MEA fabricated with Pt NCLs as both anode and cathode demonstrates outstanding activity and stability in practical fuel cell (SI Appendix, Figs. S32 and S33). The Pt NCLs-based MEA was first evaluated under H2/O2 conditions with pressure of 200 kPaabs, 100% relative humidity (RH), and cathode and anode Pt loading of 0.1 mgPt cm−2. Remarkably, the rated power density of Pt NCLs reaches up to 1.25 W cm−2, which is substantially higher than JM Pt/C (H2/O2: 1.02 W cm−2) and Pt NPs (H2/O2: 0.50 W cm−2) (SI Appendix, Fig. S34). To better simulate practical operating conditions, the catalytic performance was systematically evaluated under a H2/Air (CO2-free) condition. In specific, the Pt NCLs demonstrate a rated power density of 0.83 W cm−2 in H2/Air, outperforming both JM Pt/C (0.57 W cm−2) and Pt NPs (0.28 W cm−2) under the same conditions (Fig. 6A and SI Appendix, Table S3). We also performed the polarization curves under varied conditions to verify the robustness of catalyst. The Pt NCLs still demonstrate superior rated power densities of 0.73 W cm−2 at 500 sccm (Air), 200 kPaabs, and 0.64 W cm−2 at 500 sccm (Air), 150 kPaabs, representing one of the best-performing pure-Pt catalysts and rivaling even some state-of-the-art Pt-alloy catalysts (SI Appendix, Fig. S35 and Table S3). The mass activity (MA) of Pt NCLs is calculated to be 0.887 A mgPt−1 at 0.9 V, approximately 8.3, 5.3, and 2.3 times greater than those of JM Pt/C (0.166 A mgPt−1), Pt NPs (0.106 A mgPt−1), and Tanaka Pt/C (TKK Pt/C, 0.391 A mgPt−1) (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Figs. S36 and S37). The Pt NCLs catalysts deliver much higher cell voltage at 0.8 A cm−2 (0.727 V) than those of JM Pt/C (0.682 V) and for Pt NPs (0.542 V) (Fig. 6 A and B). In addition, we have also demonstrated the crucial role of enhanced mass transport in Pt NCLs-based performance enhancement (SI Appendix, Figs. S38 and S39). The total O2 transport resistance (Rtot) comprises pressure-dependent resistance (RP) and pressure-independent resistance (RNP), which are represented by slope and intercept, respectively (Fig. 6C) (45–48). The calculated RNP for Pt NCLs (16.0 s m−1) is significantly lower than those for JM Pt/C (30.8 s m−1) and Pt NPs (34.0 s m−1), demonstrating a notably reduced O2 transport resistance. Similarly, the lower proton transport resistance (RH+) of Pt NCLs (10.6 mΩ), evaluated by impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements (SI Appendix, Fig. S39), demonstrates the superior proton transport capacity over JM Pt/C (12.5 mΩ) and Pt NPs (14.5 mΩ) (45, 46). MEAs fabricated with Pt NCLs, JM Pt/C, and Pt NPs were then subjected to accelerated durability test (ADT) with voltage cycles from 0.60 to 0.95 V using the US Department of Energy (DOE) protocols to estimate their stability. The rated power density of Pt NCLs shows negligible change (merely 7.2% decay) after 30,000-cycle ASTs, which is superior to that of JM Pt/C (63.2% decay) and Pt NPs (35.7% decay) (SI Appendix, Fig. S40). More significantly, the Pt NCLs show merely 4.6% decay in MA after stability test. In contrast, JM Pt/C and Pt NPs experience severe performance degradation with MA declines of 64.4% and 61.3%, respectively (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S36). The initial MA and stability of Pt NCLs exceed the DOE 2020 targets (MA: 0.44 A mgPt−1 and MA loss: <40% after 30,000 cycles) and outperform most reported catalysts (Fig. 6D and SI Appendix, Table S3). The I–V curve of Pt NCLs also shows negligible changes with voltage drop of 8 mV at current density of 0.8 A cm−2 after 30,000 cycles, being superior than counterparts (251 mV for JM Pt/C and 127 mV for Pt NPs) and well within the DOE target of <30 mV decay (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S40). The comprehensive characterizations after stability test collectively confirm the high structural and electrochemical stability of the nanoclathrin structures (SI Appendix, Figs. S41 and S42). In addition, the Pt NCLs were tested at high current density of 1.5 A cm−2 for 1,000 h in H2/O2 fuel cells, showing negligible working voltage loss across all testing ranges, demonstrating great potential of practical application (Fig. 6E).

Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated that the construction of bioinspired Pt NCLs holds great potential for practical fuel cell applications. The in situ formed amorphous Se at early reaction stage facilitates disordered growth of noble metals on its surface, ultimately shaping the clathrin-like structure. Furthermore, this strategy enables precise control of the macroscopic structure of nanoclathrins, including size, shell thickness, noble metal, and heterometallic composition. In terms of microscopic structure, microstrain engineering through grain boundaries manipulation enables performance optimization of Pt NCLs for half reactions in fuel cells. When further employed in practical fuel cells, given the optimized microstrain degree and enhanced mass transfer, Pt NCLs deliver high rated power density up to 1.25 and 0.83 W cm−2 in H2/O2 and H2/Air, respectively. These performances place Pt NCLs as one of the best pure-Pt catalysts and even rival some state-of-the-art Pt-alloy catalysts. The integrated network structures of Pt NCLs confer remarkable stability against aggregation, as evidenced by 95.7% activity retention after 30,000 AST cycles, coupled with stable operation for over 1,000 h at 1.5 A cm−2. This rational nanostructuring based on clathrin-like architectures offers a perspective for the design of nanostructures that intended to address the issues of mass transfer efficiency in practical fuel cells and other sustainable energy applications.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of Pt Nanoclathrins.

In a typical synthesis of Pt nanoclathrins, 9.8 mg Pt(acac)2, 4.2 mg C14H14Se2, 36.0 mg AA, 100.0 mg PVP, and 5/5 mL EG/H2O were firstly added into a glass vial (volume: 30 mL). The mixture in the capped vial was ultrasonicated for 1 h. After that, the homogeneous mixture was transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and then heated from room temperature to 180 °C and maintained at 180 °C for 3 h in an oven. After cooling to room temperature, the products of a-Se@Pt nanoparticles were washed with ethanol/acetone mixture and then collected by centrifugation. Subsequently, the a-Se@Pt nanoparticles were redispersed in the mixture solution of NaClO/NaOH with stirring for the selective removal of Se core. After being stirred for 12 h, the products of Pt nanoclathrins were washed with H2O and then collected by centrifugation for further use.

Preparation of Pt Nanoclathrins with Different Sizes.

The synthetic procedures for Pt nanoclathrins with different sizes are the same as that of Pt nanoclathrins except for adjusting the amount of PVP to 0 mg for size of ~325 nm and 250 mg for size of ~87 nm.

Preparation of Pt Nanoclathrins with Different Shell Thicknesses.

The synthetic procedures for Pt nanoclathrins with different shell thicknesses are the same as that of Pt nanoclathrins except for adjusting the amount of Pt(acac)2 to 19.8 mg for thickness of ~9.6 nm and 29.4 mg for size of ~13.9 nm.

Preparation of PtPd Nanoclathrins, PtNi Nanoclathrins, and PtCo Nanoclathrins.

The synthetic procedures for PtPd nanoclathrins, PtNi nanoclathrins, and PtCo nanoclathrins are the same as that of Pt nanoclathrins except for extra adding 1.9 mg Pd(acac)2, 1.6 mg Ni(acac)2, and 2.2 mg Co(acac)2, respectively.

Preparation of Pd Nanoclathrins, Rh Nanoclathrins, and Ir Nanoclathrins.

The synthetic procedures for Pd nanoclathrins, Rh nanoclathrins, and Ir nanoclathrins are the same as that of Pt nanoclathrins except for changing 9.8 mg Pt(acac)2 to 7.6 mg Pd(acac)2, 10.0 mg Rh(acac)3, and 12.2 mg Ir(acac)3, respectively.

Preparation of Pt Nanoparticles.

The synthetic procedures for Pt nanoparticles are the same as that of Pt nanoclathrins except for the adding of C14H14Se2 and removal of Se core.

Continuous Microstrain Engineering of Pt Nanoclathrins.

The continuous modulation on microstrain of Pt nanoclathrins is same as that of Pt nanoclathrins except extra subjecting the a-Se@Pt nanoparticles to Ar atmosphere at different temperature for 1 h to modulate the density of grain boundaries (original condition for Pt nanoclathrins-MS1, 100 °C for Pt nanoclathrins-MS2, 200 °C for Pt nanoclathrins-MS3, 300 °C for Pt nanoclathrins-MS4).

Strain Analysis on the Microstrain Degree.

The GPA method is used for quantifying displacements and strain fields at atomic resolution, which is based on the reciprocal space of the AC-HAADF-STEM image and ER-C plugin in DigitalMicrograph software (42).

CO2 Adsorption–Desorption Isotherms.

CO2 adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured at 298 K using Micromeritics ASAP 2010 instrument, with CO2 pressure ranging from 0 to 770 mmHg. Prior to analysis, each sample (approximately 80 mg) was degassed under high vacuum (<10−2 mbar) at 100 °C for 4 h to remove preadsorbed moisture and contaminants. After degassing and cooling to room temperature, the sample tube was precisely weighed to determine the dry mass of each sample. During the measurement, the adsorption branch was obtained by incrementally introducing CO2 and monitoring the equilibrium pressure and uptake at each step. The desorption branch was then recorded by progressively reducing the CO2 pressure and measuring the amount of CO2 released until the initial low pressure was reached.

The electrocatalytic measurement information is described in SI Appendix.
