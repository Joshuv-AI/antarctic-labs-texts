Case 22: A 65-Year-Old-Woman With Neutropenia
Authors: Min Gon Kang, Yoohee Chung, Jinhang Kim, Kyoung Il Min
Published: 2026

CASE PRESENTATION

Dr. Min Gon Kang: A 65-year-old woman was referred to our hematology clinic for persistent, unexplained leukopenia that had been intermittently noted over several years. She denied recurrent infections, fever, weight loss, or other constitutional symptoms, though she occasionally experienced mild dizziness and fatigue. She had no significant past medical history, took no regular medications. Regarding her occupational history, she was a retired elementary school teacher, and denied any known exposure to toxins or herbal medicines. Her primary clinic had previously remarked that her white blood cell (WBC) count was “slightly low,” but no further evaluation had been conducted.

Her vital signs were stable on presentation. She was afebrile, with normal blood pressure, pulse, and respiratory rate. Physical examination revealed no lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, or skin abnormalities.

A complete blood count (CBC) performed two years earlier had already shown leukopenia, with a WBC count of 3.90 × 109/L and an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of 1.45 × 109/L. Hemoglobin and platelets were within normal limits. At our clinic, repeat CBC again demonstrated leukopenia with WBC (3.18 × 109/L) and ANC (1.12 × 109/L). Red cell indices were stable, except for mild macrocytosis (mean corpuscular volume [MCV], 98.6 fL), without overt anemia. CBC results are summarized in Table 1.

While reviewing potential causes of neutropenia, the patient reported that her oldest sister had recently been hospitalized elsewhere for syncope, cytopenia, and neuropathy. Her sister was found to have a markedly elevated urine arsenic level. Given this history, we performed a urine arsenic test for our patient, which revealed a significantly elevated level of 767 µg/L (reference < 150 µg/L).

CLINICAL IMPRESSION

Dr. Min Gon Kang: Chronic cytopenia possibly due to arsenic intoxication. Other causes such as viral, autoimmune, and nutritional etiologies should be considered in the differential diagnosis.

ADDITIONAL LABORATORY FINDINGS

Dr. Yoohee Chung: Additional laboratory tests were performed to evaluate potential causes of neutropenia, including nutritional deficiencies, autoimmune disorders, chronic infections, medication effects, and bone marrow disorders. Laboratory results revealed normal levels of vitamin B12 and folate, normal thyroid function, and no evidence of hemolysis. Liver and renal function tests were within normal limits. Autoimmune markers, including antinuclear antibodies and rheumatoid factor, were negative. Viral serologies for hepatitis viruses, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV), and cytomegalovirus (CMV) returned negative. Serum protein electrophoresis showed no monoclonal protein.

While the neutropenia was mild (ANC, 1.12 × 109/L), further evaluation was initiated due to the patient’s significant concern regarding her sister's recent hospitalization for arsenic intoxication and neuropathy. Additionally, the borderline macrocytosis (MCV, 98.6 fL) in the absence of overt anemia suggested a potential metabolic disturbance in the folate or B12 pathways, warranting a closer look at the patient's one-carbon metabolism.

DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

Dr. Yoohee Chung: Neutropenia is generally categorized into two major etiologic groups: decreased production and peripheral destruction.

Decreased production

Dr. Min Gon Kang: Decreased production encompasses drug-induced marrow suppression, primary bone marrow disorders, nutritional deficiencies, and infections. In this case, the patient was not taking marrow suppressive agents such as alkylating agents, antithyroid medications, or clozapine. Primary marrow failure was considered less likely given the isolated chronic course, stable hemoglobin and platelet counts, and absence of pancytopenia. Nutritional causes were also initially excluded as serum Vitamin B12 and folate levels were within normal limits. Furthermore, viral markers including HIV, EBV, CMV, and hepatitis were negative, and the patient lacked systemic symptoms suggestive of an active infection.

Peripheral destruction

Dr. Min Gon Kang: Peripheral destruction was also considered. However, autoimmune causes were unlikely as anti-nuclear antibody and rheumatoid factor were negative, and no clinical signs of systemic lupus erythematosus or rheumatoid arthritis were present. There was also no history of exposure to drugs that typically cause hapten-mediated destruction.

Heavy metal associated cytopenias

Dr. Yoohee Chung: Heavy metal exposure is an important yet often overlooked etiology of cytopenias. It may cause neutropenia by decreased production. Cadmium, lead, arsenic, mercury, manganese, and other trace metals may cause bone marrow suppression through direct toxicity, inhibition of enzymatic pathways, oxidative stress, or interference with heme synthesis.

In the evaluation of unexplained cytopenia, particularly when there is potential exposure history or accompanying neurologic, gastrointestinal, or dermatologic findings, targeted heavy-metal testing should be considered. Accurate measurement requires use of metal-free collection tubes, such as trace element tubes or K2EDTA (metal-free) tubes, as contamination from standard tubes can result in misleading values.

A thorough evaluation by the Occupational and Environmental Medicine department revealed no history of exposure to heavy metals or environmental toxins, and her residence utilized city-supplied water rather than locally supplied water.

Arsenic intoxication

Dr. Yoohee Chung: Arsenic exposure is a well-recognized cause of bone marrow suppression, potentially leading to leukopenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia. In addition to hematologic toxicity, chronic arsenic exposure may manifest with peripheral neuropathy, gastrointestinal symptoms, skin hyperpigmentation, and characteristic nail changes such as Mees lines. Arsenic exists in both organic and inorganic forms, with the inorganic species being responsible for most toxic effects. However, routine screening tests using K2EDTA tubes measure only total arsenic and cannot differentiate between these chemical forms. Importantly, dietary intake of seafood, particularly crustaceans such as crab, lobster, shrimp, and crayfish, can result in transient elevation of organic arsenic, leading to falsely elevated total arsenic levels without true toxicity.

Therefore, when an elevated total arsenic level is identified, clinicians should first carefully review recent dietary history and recommend abstinence from seafood followed by repeat testing. Persistent elevation despite dietary modification raises concern for inorganic arsenic exposure and warrants further confirmatory evaluation, such as arsenic speciation analysis using liquid chromatography–inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry.

Because the patient’s sibling also demonstrated cytopenia and confirmed arsenic elevation, genetic or enzymatic susceptibility was also considered. Whole-genome sequencing was performed to evaluate potential germline variants given the family history and the patient’s strong will to undergo genetic testing. While awaiting the results, she was advised to strictly avoid seafood. Over the following months, her urine arsenic levels declined steadily. From the initial concentration of 767 µg/L, levels dropped to 190 µg/L at three months and normalized to 97 µg/L at six months. This significant decline following dietary restriction indicated that the initial elevation was largely due to organic arsenic species found in seafood.

GENETIC FINDINGS

Dr. Jinhang Kim: Whole-genome sequencing was performed to evaluate germline variants involving one-carbon metabolism and detoxification pathways (Table 2). The analysis revealed clinically significant variants in key folate metabolism genes. Specifically, the patient carried the methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) Ala222Val (rs1801133) and 5-methyltetrahydrofolate homocysteine methyltransferase reductase (MTRR) Ile22Met (rs1801394) variants, both classified as deleterious (Fig. 1). Additionally, a methionine synthase (MTR) Asp919Gly (rs1805087) variant was identified and classified as deleterious with low confidence. These variants collectively compromise the methylation cycle at multiple steps: the MTHFR defect hinders the conversion of folate to its active form, while the MTR and MTRR defects impair the utilization of that active folate for homocysteine remethylation. This synergistic impairment creates a metabolic bottleneck, leading to the conclusion that the patient suffers from a functional deficiency despite normal serum vitamin levels.

FINAL DIAGNOSIS

Dr. Kyoung Il Min: Neutropenia due to functional folate deficiency with impaired methylation capacity.

GENERAL INTRODUCTION TO THE DISEASE MANAGEMENT

Dr. Jinhang Kim: MTHFR and MTRR are essential enzymes in the folate and methionine cycles, both fundamental components of one-carbon metabolism.

In the folate cycle, dietary folate is converted through dihydrofolate and tetrahydrofolate (THF) to enter the intracellular folate pool. MTHFR catalyzes the reduction of 5,10-methylene-THF to 5-methyl-THF, the active folate form required for methylation reactions. When MTHFR function is reduced, the production of active folate decreases.

MTR and MTRR orchestrate the transfer of a methyl group from 5-methyl-THF to homocysteine, forming methionine. Impairment of these reactions leads to stagnation of the folate cycle and reduced availability of methyl groups necessary for essential methylation reactions. Thus, even when serum folate appears normal, intracellular levels of active folate may be insufficient, resulting in functional folate deficiency.

Therapeutic approaches include increasing folic acid intake to overcome reduced MTHFR efficiency, supplying 5-methyl-THF directly, supplementing vitamins B12 and B6 to support MTR function, and using betaine to bypass impaired MTR activity.

PROGNOSIS

Dr. Min Gon Kang: Serum homocysteine was elevated at 20.5 μmol/L (reference, 0–15 μmol/L), confirming the functional block in methylation. Following supplementation with folate and cobalamin, the patient showed substantial clinical improvement. DISCUSSION

Dr. Min Gon Kang: The initial finding of elevated total urine arsenic is a frequent confounding factor in clinical practice. Epidemiological data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey demonstrated that seafood consumption is the primary determinant of total urinary arsenic concentrations in populations without industrial exposure.1 Organic arsenic species like arsenobetaine are rapidly excreted and are non-toxic, yet they can falsely elevate total arsenic levels by several-fold. This underscores the necessity of arsenic speciation or a 48-hour seafood abstinence period prior to diagnosing true inorganic arsenic toxicity.

Dr. Min Gon Kang: This case illustrates how genetically driven disturbances in one-carbon metabolism can manifest as subclinical cytopenias, and rescued by folate and vitamin B12 replacement. The patient’s long-standing neutropenia, repeatedly normal nutritional studies, and supportive family history prompted a metabolic and genetic evaluation beyond conventional etiologies. One-carbon metabolism plays a central role in nucleotide synthesis and methylation reactions; impairment of this system can impair hematopoiesis even when serum folate concentrations appear normal.

Dr. Yoohee Chung: A key laboratory abnormality in this case was the elevation of homocysteine. Homocysteine accumulates when the folate and methionine cycles fail to efficiently regenerate methionine via methylcobalamin-dependent remethylation. Elevated homocysteine itself has pathophysiologic significance: it induces oxidative stress, promotes endothelial dysfunction, and contributes to systemic toxicity, thereby linking metabolic abnormalities with broad clinical consequences.23 Although the patient’s level was modestly elevated at 20.5 μmol/L, it nevertheless signals functional inefficiency of the remethylation pathway, a defect linked to her genetic profile.

Dr. Jinhang Kim: The elevated homocysteine level is explained by the genetic finding discovered in the patient. Genetic findings such as MTHFR polymorphisms, particularly the C677T variant (Ala222Val), has been shown in meta-analyses to affect clinical outcomes in pathways linked to homocysteine metabolism in a meta-analysis.4 The functional consequences of this variant, compounded by deleterious changes in MTR and MTRR, may aggravate methylation efficiency. Studies have suggested how combined deficiencies in folate and vitamin B12, or the presence of compound genotypes (e.g., MTHFR C677T with MTRR A66G), synergistically elevate homocysteine levels.5

In the Korean population, the MTHFR C677T genotype frequencies for CC, CT, and TT genotypes were 34, 49, and 17%, respectively.6 Because of relatively high prevalence of CT heterozygous and TT homozygous, clinicians should consider potential pharmacogenetic risks when prescribing medications that interfere with folate metabolism.

Dr. Kyoung Il Min: Disruptions of one-carbon metabolism often lead to hematological consequences, as folate-dependent pathways are essential for nucleotide synthesis, methylation reactions, and proper cell-cycle progression within the bone marrow. A well-recognized example is seen in the context of antifolate chemotherapy such as pemetrexed, where impaired folate cycling significantly aggravates hematological toxicity in patients harboring MTHFR C677T homozygosity.7 Beyond these chemotherapy-related settings, abnormal folate handling associated with MTHFR polymorphisms has been linked to macrocytosis, anemia, and occasionally neutropenia, even when serum folate concentrations remain within reference ranges.8

Another clinically vital example is methotrexate (MTX), which is utilized across broad range of medical specialties. Because MTX acts by inhibiting dihydrofolate reductase and subsequently MTHFR, individuals with the C677T variant are at an increased risk for severe adverse drug reactions. Meta-analyses have shown that the TT genotype is significantly associated with an increased risk of MTX-induced hepatotoxicity and hematologic toxicity, such as acute pancytopenia.9 Consequently, identifying these variants can assist clinicians in personalizing MTX-containing regimens, such as high-dose MTX chemotherapy for CNS lymphoma,10 disease-modifying antirheumatic drug therapy for rheumatoid arthritis,9 and non-surgical management for ectopic pregnancy.11 Recognizing a patient's genetic predisposition allows for proactive strategies, including dose modifications or more aggressive folate supplementation such as the use of leucovorin, to mitigate potentially life-threatening toxicities.

Dr. Kyoung Il Min: Regarding management, the patient’s improvement after folate and vitamin B12 supplementation supports the diagnosis of functional folate deficiency, in which intracellular folate utilization is inadequate despite normal serum levels. Current approaches emphasize that patients with mutations affecting folate and one-carbon metabolism may benefit more from active folate derivatives, such as 5-methyl-THF or folinic acid, rather than folic acid alone, because these forms bypass enzymatic bottlenecks created by MTHFR dysfunction.12 Additionally, adequate vitamin B12 replacement is essential, as cobalamin deficiency unmasks or exacerbates defects in methylation and can independently produce cytopenias.13 In this case, because active folate derivatives were not available, folic acid and cobalamin were administered together, effectively compensating for the reduced functional activity of the patient’s MTHFR and MTRR enzymes.

The clinical response following supplementation provides functional validation of the diagnosis. Restoration of intracellular methylation capacity alleviated marrow suppression, resulting in recovery of neutrophil counts. This hematologic improvement underscores the importance of identifying functional deficits in the folate pathway, even when conventional nutritional assays appear normal.

Dr. Kyoung Il Min: As a limitation of this study, it is important to note that whole-genome sequencing is not a cost-effective or standard diagnostic tool for mild, asymptomatic neutropenia. In a routine clinical setting, empirical folate and vitamin supplementation would likely precede genetic testing. However, in this case, the confluence of family history prompted the use of research funding for academic purposes, and genetic testing allowed for the identification of a complex “compound” genetic landscape (MTHFR, MTR, and MTRR variants), serving as a proof of concept for identifying a functional deficiency that might otherwise only be suspected.
