Pregnancy adverse events with immune checkpoint inhibitors in monotherapy and combination therapy in cancer: a disproportionality analysis
Authors: Eman A. Alghamdi, Nouf S. Alfadel, Ahlam A. Alghamdi, Basmah A. Albabtain, Muneerah M. Aleissa, Ghadah H. Alshehri, Nada Alsuhebany, Edward B. De Vol, Haifa Aldakhil, Waleed M. Altowayan, Mohammed Alghamdi, Ali Y. Alshahrani, Fawaz F. Alharbi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAlthough immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer treatment, their potential to disrupt maternal–foetal immune tolerance raises important safety concerns during pregnancy. This study used global pharmacovigilance data to compare pregnancy-related adverse events (PRAEs) following ICI monotherapy vs combination therapy.MethodsA cohort based case–noncase disproportionality analysis was conducted, using VigiBase®, the WHO's global Individual Case Safety Report database through January 21, 2025. ICI exposure was classified as monotherapy or combination therapy. Cases were defined as pregnancy reports including at least one PRAE from the nine predefined pregnancy-related adverse event categories (premature birth, abortion/stillbirth, gestational age or birth weight abnormalities, congenital anomalies, neurodevelopmental disorders, neonatal respiratory events, eclampsia, delivery complications, and other pregnancy-related adverse events), while noncases were defined as pregnancy reports with ICI exposure that did not include the specific PRAE category under analysis, although they may have included other reported adverse events. Reporting odds ratios (RORs) and 95% CIs were estimated to compare PRAE reporting between combination and monotherapy exposures. Multivariable Firth logistic regression was used to calculate adjusted RORs, controlling for reporter qualification and report completeness score. Generalised estimating equations with a binomial distribution were applied to account for clustering of multiple adverse events within the same pregnancy. Levels of significance are unadjusted for multiple testing.Findings337 case reports were initially retrieved from VigiBase, of which 164 met the inclusion criteria. Of these, 22 (13%) involved combination ICI therapy and 142 (87%) monotherapy exposure. Overall, 72 (43.9%) of 164 reported at least one PRAE, with a significantly higher frequency among combination therapy cases compared with monotherapy (17 [77.3%] of 22 vs 55 [38.7%] of 142; p < 0.001). Premature delivery was the most common pregnancy-related adverse event across all subjects, occurring in (14 [63.6%] of 22) of the combination therapy group and (23 [16.2%] of 142) of the monotherapy group (p < 0.001). Gestational or weight abnormalities followed, occurring in (4 [18.2%] of 22) and (7 [4.9%] of 142) of the combination therapy and Monotherapy groups, respectively (p = 0.04). In multivariable analyses adjusted for reporter qualification and completeness score, combination therapy remained strongly associated with foetal and neonatal complications, particularly premature birth (adjusted ROR 9.10, 95% CI 3.38–24.55). Reports with higher completeness scores were significantly more likely to include adverse pregnancy outcomes across several categories. Findings were consistent in generalised estimating equations models, where combination therapy showed a twofold higher population-averaged risk of any PRAE (ROR 2.26, 95% CI 1.39–3.68; p < 0.001).InterpretationICI combination therapy was associated with a higher reporting of PRAEs compared with monotherapy, particularly premature delivery and gestational or foetal growth abnormalities.FundingThis study received no external funding.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library for pharmacological and clinical studies published from database inception through January 21, 2025. Search terms included “immune checkpoint inhibitors,” “pregnancy,” “foetal outcomes,” “maternofetal toxicity,” and “pharmacovigilance.” Early evidence from Xu et al. (2021) first signalled a potential increase in congenital anomalies and foetal loss associated with immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) exposure. However, subsequent analyses by Noseda et al. and Gougis et al. expanded the evidence base by evaluating pregnancy and neonatal outcomes, including prematurity and foetal growth abnormalities. However, findings across studies remained limited and often statistically inconclusive, largely because of sparse exposed pregnancy cases, heterogeneous outcome definitions, and limited data granularity. Gougis et al. provided important comparative evidence by evaluating pregnancy outcomes after ICI exposure versus non-ICI antineoplastic therapy and included a subgroup comparison of ICI monotherapy versus combination therapy. However, this subgroup analysis was limited by the small number of exposed cases and was not the primary focus of their study. Therefore, the regimen-specific reporting profile of pregnancy-related adverse events after ICI monotherapy versus combination therapy remains insufficiently characterised.

Added value of this study

This study provides the largest and most contemporary analysis of pregnancy-related adverse events (PRAEs) associated with ICI exposure to date. By using an expanded global dataset from VigiBase (N = 164) and Generalised Estimating Equations (GEE) to account for report clustering and multivariable confounding, we resolve previous scientific uncertainties. Our findings demonstrate a potent, non-linear escalation in maternofetal toxicity when transitioning from monotherapy to combination regimens. Most notably, we identified a nearly 10-fold increased reporting odds of premature birth (aROR 9.96) for combination therapies compared to monotherapy. This research effectively identifies combination checkpoint blockade as the primary driver of abbreviated gestation, a signal that was previously masked by underpowered cohorts.

Implications of all the available evidence

The evidence now indicates that the safety profile of ICIs in pregnancy is regimen-dependent. While all ICI exposures during pregnancy necessitate high-risk obstetric surveillance, the use of combination therapy represents a critical threshold for maternal and foetal risk. These findings provide an essential evidence-based foundation for clinicians to perform nuanced risk-benefit assessments and stratified patient counselling. Furthermore, the high prevalence of prematurity identified in this study mandates that patients receiving combination ICI therapy be managed within multidisciplinary frameworks that prioritise neonatal preparedness and access to tertiary-level intensive care units.

Introduction

Cancer during pregnancy is rare, with an estimated incidence of approximately 0.1%.1 However, its incidence is increasing, mainly due to the growing trend of women delaying childbearing.2 The most commonly diagnosed cancers during pregnancy include breast cancer, melanoma, cervical cancer, and lymphoma, which reflect the prevalence of these malignancies in women of reproductive age.1, 2, 3 Additionally, advancements in prenatal screening, such as noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT) for foetal aneuploidies, have inadvertently contributed to the early detection of previously undiagnosed malignancies. This occurs because NIPT may reveal abnormal genomic patterns, such as somatic copy number variations, which may indicate the presence of an underlying maternal cancer.4

Managing cancer during pregnancy presents a complex challenge, requiring a delicate balance between maternal treatment efficacy and foetal safety. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed the landscape of cancer therapy by enabling the immune system to recognise and eliminate tumour cells. However, their application in pregnancy raises critical concerns as they disrupt immune checkpoints that play a crucial role in maintaining maternal-foetal immune tolerance, risk of autoimmunity, and altered foetal development.5,6

Emerging evidence indicates that ICIs can cross the placental barrier, potentially influencing foetal immune maturation and increasing the risk of neonatal complications. Mechanistically, ICIs antagonise inhibitory regulators of T-cell activation, including programmed death-1 (PD-1), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), which are physiologically upregulated at the maternal–foetal interface to sustain immune equilibrium. Theoretical and preclinical studies suggest that pharmacologic blockade of these pathways during gestation may tilt the immune balance toward excessive activation, resulting in loss of maternal–foetal tolerance and worsen pregnancy outcomes. Together, these findings underscore the need for systematic evaluation of the safety of ICIs during pregnancy.7,8

Pregnancy specific clinical guidance for ICI use is limited; however, available preclinical evidence and product label precautions generally support avoiding ICI exposure during pregnancy because of potential foetal toxicity and disruption of maternal–foetal immune tolerance.9, 10, 11 Recent human studies indicate that the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes associated with ICI exposure may not be substantially higher than that observed with other anticancer therapies. Building on this evolving evidence, Gougis et al. (2024) conducted a comprehensive evaluation of pregnancy outcomes among individuals treated with ICIs compared with non-ICI antineoplastic agents. Within that study, a subgroup analysis explored the comparative safety of ICI monotherapy versus combination therapy; however, the limited number of exposed cases restricted statistical power and precluded firm conclusions.12,13

Given the limited data and conflicting findings from clinical studies, this study aimed to conduct a large-scale descriptive analysis of maternal and foetal adverse outcomes following ICI exposure during pregnancy. The primary objective was to investigate whether the incidence of pregnancy-related adverse events (PRAEs) differs between patients who received monotherapy ICI treatment and those for whom exposure to two or more ICI agents was stated within the same pregnancy report. The secondary objectives were to explore differences in reporting patterns of adverse events across subgroups defined by maternal age group, cancer indication, geographic region, and treatment dose or regimen, characterise and compare the profiles of reported adverse events across therapy groups, and to evaluate whether specific classes of adverse events are more frequently reported with combination therapy compared to monotherapy.

Methods

Study design and database

A cohort-based case–noncase disproportionality analysis was conducted, applied to pharmacovigilance data from WHO. The data source was VigiBase, the global database of individual case safety reports (ICSRs), maintained by the Uppsala Monitoring Centre. Each ICSR includes detailed information on patient demographics (age, sex, reporting date, reporter type, and geographical origin), drug characteristics (route of administration, concomitant medications, dosage, and frequency), and adverse event data (preferred terms, time to onset and resolution, and seriousness). Report completeness and the likelihood of establishing causal associations vary across submissions. Race and ethnicity data were not analysed in this study because these variables are not routinely collected, standardised, or consistently available within VigiBase. Consequently, assessment of outcomes according to race or ethnicity was not feasible.

VigiBase, is managed by the Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) and represents the largest pharmacovigilance repository globally, containing over 30 million reports from more than 150-member countries. Its extensive global coverage provides a unique opportunity to detect rare safety signals in specialised populations, such as pregnant women receiving immunotherapy, who are typically excluded from prospective clinical trials. To ensure high data quality and standardisation, VigiBase uses the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) for event coding and employs the vigiGrade system to assess report completeness based on the presence of critical clinical variables, including patient demographics, drug dosage, and event outcomes. While spontaneous reporting systems are inherently subject to limitations such as underreporting and variable data precision, VigiBase is a clinically validated tool for signal detection; research has demonstrated that its data often serve as the primary evidence for regulatory safety updates and label changes worldwide. In our methodology, we further accounted for potential data variability by incorporating report completeness as a covariate in our multivariable models, thereby ensuring that the identified association between combination ICI therapy and maternofetal toxicity is based on the most robust evidence available within the system.14,15

Ethics

The data in the VigiBase database are anonymised, and it is not possible to access personal information about the patients or the individuals reporting the cases; thus, ethical approval and informed consent were not required. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for case-noncase studies.16

Procedures

The study period encompassed all ICSRs available in VigiBase from database inception through January 2025 using the MedDRA version 27.1. Although the database contains reports dating back to 1967, the first pregnancy-related reports for ICIs appeared post-2011, following regulatory approvals. The specific data query used for this analysis was finalised on January 21, 2025, allowing for a sufficient data-lag period to ensure the capture of follow-up information regarding neonatal outcomes and the calculation of stable Reporting Odds Ratios (RORs).

The search included reports involving antineoplastic ICIs, defined according to the Standardised Drug Grouping (SDG – Narrow) classification, and adverse reactions coded under a comprehensive set of MedDRA terms related to pregnancy, foetal, and neonatal complications (Supplement, Table S1).

To ensure the inclusion of only relevant reports, we retained those that explicitly mentioned one or more PRAEs or exposure and excluded those containing only secondary pregnancy associations.

In this study, secondary pregnancy associations were defined as reports in which pregnancy was mentioned only indirectly or contextually, without clear evidence that the pregnant individual was exposed to an ICI during pregnancy or that the reported adverse event represented a maternal, foetal, or neonatal pregnancy-related outcome. These reports were excluded to minimise exposure misclassification and because they could not reliably contribute to the assessment of pregnancy-related safety following in utero ICI exposure. Specifically, only reports with pregnancy-related terms classified under the System Organ Class (SOC), High-Level Group Term (HLGT), High-Level Term (HLT), Preferred Term (PT), or lower-Level Term (LLT) were included.

Reports were subsequently screened to exclude those involving nonpregnant adults, pregnancies in women older than 45 years, cases with no recorded administration of an ICI, and reports describing paternal or breastfeeding or skin exposures. Reports indicating a male biological sex were classified as exposure via semen. Reports specifying exposure through semen were excluded (Supplement, Table S2). Similarly, reports describing exposure through breast milk or prior to pregnancy without explicit mention of exposure during pregnancy were also excluded. All remaining reports were considered to represent in utero exposure to anticancer agents during pregnancy.

The analysis included US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)–approved ICI drugs: PD1 inhibitors (nivolumab, pembrolizumab, cemiplimab, and dostarlimab), PD-L1 inhibitors (atezolizumab, avelumab, and durvalumab), and other FDA-approved ICIs (anti-CTLA4: ipilimumab and tremelimumab; anti-LAG3: relatlimab).

Reports that listed exposure to a single ICI agent were categorised as monotherapy, whereas reports that listed two or more ICI agents within the same pregnancy ICSR were categorised as combination therapy. Each ICI-exposed report was reviewed and analysed individually to ensure accurate classification.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the presence of at least one pregnancy-related adverse event (PRAE) documented in VigiBase reports among pregnancies exposed to ICIs. PRAEs included maternal, foetal, and neonatal outcomes and were grouped into nine predefined categories. Cases were defined as reports mentioning at least one PRAE, while noncases were defined as pregnancy reports with ICI exposure that did not include that specific PRAE category, although they may have included other reported adverse events.

Secondary outcomes included the distribution of individual PRAE categories by therapy type, including premature birth, abortion or stillbirth, gestational age or birth weight abnormalities, congenital anomalies, neurodevelopmental disorders, neonatal respiratory events, eclampsia, delivery complications, and other selected pregnancy-related adverse events. Subgroup analyses were also conducted according to reporter qualification, maternal age group, cancer indication, geographic region, and treatment regimen where data were available.

Pregnancy reports were classified as cases if they included at least one PRAE, as defined by the predefined MedDRA-based outcome categories listed in Supplement, Table S3. These categories were selected to capture clinically relevant maternal, foetal, and neonatal outcomes that are biologically plausible following immune checkpoint inhibition during pregnancy and consistently identifiable in spontaneous pharmacovigilance reports.

The nine predefined PRAE categories were organised as follows: maternal outcomes included eclampsia and delivery complications; foetal outcomes included premature birth, abortion or stillbirth, gestational age or birth weight abnormalities, congenital anomalies, and neurodevelopmental disorders when explicitly reported; neonatal outcomes included neonatal respiratory disorders and selected neonatal complications captured under the “other pregnancy-related adverse events” category. Reports without any of these predefined outcomes were classified as noncases for the analysis, even if they included other adverse events.

Long-term outcomes were not systematically assessed because VigiBase reports generally do not include standardised longitudinal follow-up after birth. Therefore, delayed developmental or long-term childhood outcomes could not be reliably evaluated unless they were explicitly reported as part of the original ICSR.

Statistics

We conducted a case–noncase study using disproportionality analysis to evaluate the association between the type of ICI exposure and pregnancy-related adverse events of interest. In the primary analyses, the unit of analysis was the individual pregnancy report. Reporting odds ratios were calculated as the odds that a report involving combination ICI therapy contained at least one PRAE (case) rather than no PRAE (noncase), relative to reports involving ICI monotherapy. Separate report-level RORs were also estimated for each predefined PRAE category.

The study population was summarised using frequencies and percentages for qualitative variables and medians with IQRs for quantitative variables. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test when any cell contained fewer than five observations in expectation. A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.1.2.

To reduce potential bias, two key confounding factors were identified based on data availability and clinical relevance: the reporter's qualifications and the report completeness score. Multivariable logistic regression models were subsequently used to estimate adjusted reporting odds ratios (aRORs) for each PRAE category, controlling for these covariates. Because several adverse event categories contained sparse data or complete separation, multivariable Firth penalised logistic regression was applied to reduce small-sample bias and obtain stable parameter estimates and confidence intervals. This approach is particularly suitable for disproportionality analyses involving rare adverse events or zero-cell counts. Variables with missing information were assessed prior to analysis. Two ICSRs with missing data on reporter qualification were identified. As reporter qualification was included as a covariate in the adjusted models, these two reports were omitted from the adjusted analyses only.

Because multiple PRAEs could be reported within the same pregnancy report, generalised estimating equations (GEE) with a binomial distribution and logit link function were additionally applied to extend the report-level disproportionality analyses. In these models, individual adverse events were treated as correlated observations clustered within the same pregnancy report, using an exchangeable working correlation structure to account for within-report dependence. This approach generated population-averaged estimates of the association between ICI therapy type (combination vs. monotherapy) and the overall likelihood of reporting pregnancy-related adverse events while accounting for multiple events within the same report. Robust standard errors were used to obtain valid confidence intervals and p-values, even in the presence of intra-cluster correlation.

Most reports originated from the USA (91 [55.5%] of 164), followed by France (11 [6.7%]), Germany (11 [6.7%]), Italy (7 [4.3%]), and the UK (7 [4.3%]). Additional submissions were received from Argentina (4 [2.4%]) and other countries in smaller numbers. Most reports were submitted by physicians (69 [42.1%]), followed by other health professionals (40 [24.4%]), consumers or non–health professionals (26 [15.9%]), and pharmacists (10 [6.1%]). A small number of reports were jointly submitted by physicians and other health professionals (7 [4.3%]), while two reports did not specify the reporter qualification. No statistically significant differences were found between the two groups in country of origin, reporter type, or report completeness.

PD-1 inhibitors were the most commonly reported ICI class, accounting for 147 (89.6%) of 164 reports overall. All patients in the combination therapy group received a PD-1 inhibitor, whereas 125 (88%) of 142 of those on monotherapy did (p = 0.13). In contrast, PD-L1 inhibitors were uncommon, accounting for only five (3%) of 164 cases, and appeared exclusively in the monotherapy group. CTLA-4 inhibitors (mainly ipilimumab) were reported in 33 (20%) of 164 cases, with a marked predominance in combination therapy (95.5% vs 8.5%; p < 0.001). Finally, LAG-3 inhibitors were rarely observed (1 [<1%] of 164), limited to a single combination-therapy case, consistent with their recent approval and limited real-world use. Regarding the reported therapeutic indications, melanoma was the most frequently reported malignancy overall, accounting for 42/164 (25.6%) of all cases, and was significantly more common among reports involving combination therapy compared with monotherapy (54.5% vs 21.1%, p < 0.001).

Concomitant use of additional antineoplastic agents was reported in 21 of 142 monotherapy reports (14.8%) and was not observed in any of the 22 combination-therapy reports (0%) (Supplement, Table S4).

72 (44%) of 164 reports included at least one pregnancy-related adverse event. The PRAE spectrum was primarily dominated by foetal complications (45 [27.4%]), with premature birth emerging as the most frequent individual signal, affecting 37 (22.6%). Spontaneous abortion was reported in 26 cases (15.9%), whereas congenital anomalies and conditions related to gestational weight/age occurred in 13 (7.9%) and 11 (6.7%), respectively. Maternal-specific complications were notably less frequent, accounting for only (8/164) 4.9% of the reports. This comprehensive landscape highlights that while the safety signals span multiple categories, the burden of toxicity is most heavily concentrated in neonatal outcomes and the maintenance of pregnancy term.

Preterm birth was the most commonly reported outcome and occurred significantly more often in the combination therapy group than in the monotherapy group (63.6% vs 16.2%; p < 0.001). Gestational age or birth weight–related conditions were also more frequently reported with combination therapy (18.2% vs 4.9%; p = 0.04). Abortion was reported exclusively in the monotherapy group (18.3% vs 0%), with a statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.03). Congenital anomalies were numerically more frequent among combination therapy reports (18.2% vs 6.3%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.08). Less frequent outcomes including neurodevelopmental disorders, eclampsia, delivery-related complications, neonatal respiratory conditions, and other outcomes did not differ significantly between treatment groups.

Foetal events represented the most frequently reported category, occurring more often in the combination therapy group than in the monotherapy group (p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this disproportionality analysis using the world's largest database of ICSRs, we applied a case–noncase design to evaluate pregnancy-related outcomes following ICI therapy. Our findings revealed a clear disproportionality signal for combination therapy, particularly regarding maternofetal complications such as preterm delivery and abnormal gestational age or birth weight, when compared with monotherapy.

After adjusting for both reporter qualification and report completeness score, the association between combination therapy and foetal or neonatal complications remained strong, highlighting the consistency of this relationship. Notably, our study identifies higher report completeness, substantially increased the likelihood of detecting significant associations, implying that well-documented cases are more likely to uncover true safety signals. In contrast, the reporter type (consumer versus healthcare professional) exerted minimal influence on the observed outcomes, reinforcing that the reliability of pharmacovigilance findings is driven primarily by data quality rather than by the source of the report. This observation aligns with previously published studies, which have demonstrated that the validity and interpretability of disproportionality analyses depend chiefly on the completeness and accuracy of individual case reports, while the reporter's identity or professional background exerts little to no intrinsic effect.17, 18, 19

Although concomitant antineoplastic medications were reported exclusively in the ICI monotherapy group, residual confounding related to disease severity, and reporting behaviour cannot be fully excluded given the limitations of spontaneous reporting data.20 Nevertheless, the discordance between concomitant antineoplastic exposure and the higher reporting of PRAEs in the combination ICI group suggests that the excess reporting of PRAEs cannot be attributed solely to concomitant antineoplastic therapy. This observation is consistent with evidence that combination ICI regimens induce more intense and broader immune activation than monotherapy, potentially increasing the risk of immune-mediated placental dysfunction, foetal intolerance, or other pregnancy complications independent of additional antineoplastic exposure. In support of this interpretation, corticosteroid use often reflecting the management of immune-related adverse events was significantly more frequent in the combination ICI group than in the monotherapy group (p < 0.01), indicating a higher burden of immune-mediated toxicity21,22 (Supplement Table S4).

Previous research on pregnancy outcomes following ICI exposure has yielded mixed and often inconclusive results. Several pharmacovigilance and observational studies reported no detectable safety signal for PRAEs associated with ICI use, suggesting a limited or uncertain risk profile in this population.12,13,23 On the other hand, a case series reported that ICI exposure during pregnancy was associated with an increased incidence of PRAEs, prematurity and low birth weight, indicating possible maternofetal vulnerability.24

Consistent with the overall trends observed by Gougis et al., the present study demonstrates that combination ICI regimens are significantly associated with an increased risk of preterm birth and abnormalities in gestational age or birth weight compared with monotherapy. This observation aligns with a growing body of evidence from meta-analyses, systematic reviews, and large pharmacovigilance studies, all indicating that combination ICI therapy carries a higher overall toxicity burden and a greater frequency of serious adverse events than monotherapy, affecting multiple organ systems. Reported immune-related adverse events (irAEs) such as myocarditis, pericarditis, and hepatotoxicity may further contribute to maternal or foetal physiological stress, potentially predisposing to adverse pregnancy outcomes.25, 26, 27, 28

A crucial consideration in interpreting our findings is the aetiology of premature birth, which may be spontaneous or iatrogenic. The PD-1/PD-L1 pathway is a known regulator of maternal–foetal tolerance; thus, its blockade may directly trigger a pro-inflammatory cascade leading to spontaneous labour. Alternatively, the high incidence of preterm birth observed especially with combination therapy may reflect clinical decisions to deliver early in response to severe maternal immune-related adverse events (irAEs) or foetal distress. While pharmacovigilance data often do not distinguish between these two pathways, both highlight the clinical reality that ICI exposure in pregnancy carries a high risk of abbreviated gestation, necessitating multidisciplinary care and neonatal preparedness.

Preclinical evidence indicates that adverse pregnancy outcomes are largely driven by immune dysregulation at the maternal foetal interface. Blocking essential ICIs such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 normally responsible for maintaining fetomaternal tolerance,5,6 blockade of these pathways has been shown to increase the likelihood of foetal loss and pregnancy complications in animal models. In particular, murine experiments demonstrated that PD-L1 inhibition resulted in a fivefold rise in spontaneous abortion during allogeneic pregnancies, underscoring the critical role of these pathways in sustaining immune tolerance to the genetically distinct foetus. Similarly, studies in cynomolgus monkeys exposed to high doses of anti–PD-1 antibodies revealed higher rates of foetal growth restriction, premature delivery, and embryonic, foetal, and neonatal death.9,10,29, 30, 31

In addition to the observed signals for preterm birth and gestational age or birth weight abnormalities, congenital anomalies were reported more frequently in the combination therapy group than in the monotherapy group, although the difference was not statistically significant. The observed reporting of congenital malformations appears higher than the estimated background rate of approximately 3% in the general population32; however, this should be interpreted cautiously. This elevation may reflect reporting enrichment in pharmacovigilance databases, where severe or unusual pregnancy outcomes are preferentially reported, as well as the complexity of this high-risk population, including underlying malignancy, possible prior exposure to embryotoxic therapies, and intensive foetal surveillance. Accordingly, these findings should be viewed as a signal of disproportionate reporting within a high-risk cohort rather than a precise estimate of teratogenic risk attributable to ICIs.

Among the predefined categories of PRAE, the “other” category comprised four reports; gastroenteritis, immune-mediated enterocolitis, necrotising colitis, and neonatal necrotising enterocolitis, all of which occurred in pregnancies exposed to ICI monotherapy. Although infrequent, these events share an inflammatory and immune-mediated profile consistent with the recognised spectrum of gastrointestinal toxicities associated with checkpoint inhibitors. A comparable pattern has been described in the literature: Baarslag et al. reported a severe case of immune-related gastroenterocolitis in a 4-month-old infant following in-utero exposure to pembrolizumab at 16 weeks' gestation, presenting with persistent watery diarrhoea, weight loss, and vomiting.33 Likewise, another study documented a possible immune-related maternofetal event involving pneumonitis that resulted in neonatal respiratory distress and death.12 While the true incidence of immune-mediated neonatal complications after maternal ICI exposure remains uncertain, these findings collectively suggest a rare but plausible risk. Such evidence underscores the importance of vigilant post-marketing surveillance and close monitoring of infants with prenatal ICI exposure to identify potential immune-related adverse outcomes early.

Taken together, these findings emphasise that the risk profile of combination ICI therapy extends beyond classic irAEs to include pregnancy-specific complications. From a regulatory perspective, such evidence supports the need for enhanced pregnancy risk communication in product labelling, post-marketing surveillance through pregnancy registries, and active monitoring systems that integrate spontaneous and real-world data.

To the best of our knowledge, this study includes the largest cohort of patients exposed to ICIs during pregnancy evaluated to date. This strengthens the reliability of the findings by improving statistical power, enabling more stable subgroup analyses, and allowing the assessment of rare but clinically meaningful PRAEs. In addition, the size of the cohort enhances the generalisability of the results, as it reflects real-world reporting across multiple regions and clinical settings.

This study has several caveats & limitations inherent to spontaneous pharmacovigilance data and disproportionality analyses. VigiBase is subject to underreporting, selective reporting, stimulated reporting, and notoriety bias, whereby severe, unusual, or clinically concerning pregnancy outcomes may be preferentially reported, while early pregnancy losses or milder complications may be under documented. In addition, the absence of a known denominator for all ICI-exposed pregnancies prevents estimation of absolute incidence or clinical risk; therefore, reporting odds ratios should be interpreted as measures of disproportionality in reporting frequency rather than true risk estimates or evidence of causality.

Important clinical details were frequently missing or inconsistently reported, including gestational age at ICI exposure, timing and duration of treatment, gestational age at preterm birth, cancer stage and severity, treatment setting, prior or concomitant anticancer therapies, obstetric history, race and ethnicity information, and whether preterm birth was spontaneous or medically indicated. Although concomitant antineoplastic medications were included in the study population regardless of treatment regimen, all reports involving concomitant antineoplastic therapy occurred within the ICI monotherapy group. While this pattern would be expected to bias findings toward the null and therefore strengthen the observed association with combination therapy, residual confounding related to concomitant treatments, disease severity, and reporting behaviour cannot be fully excluded.

Furthermore, sensitivity and stratified analyses according to cancer type, ICI class, geographic region would have been clinically informative but were not feasible because of limited sample size and sparse subgroup data.

The observed reporting of congenital malformations, although higher than the general population background rate, should also be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect reporting enrichment, high-risk maternal disease, prior embryotoxic therapies, and intensive foetal surveillance rather than a precise estimate of teratogenic risk. Accordingly, these findings should be considered signal-generating and require confirmation through prospective observational studies, pregnancy registries, active surveillance systems, and standardised reporting protocols with more complete maternal, foetal, neonatal, and treatment-level information.
