Covalency stabilizes high energy vanadium oxide positive electrode for sustainable aqueous zinc-ion batteries
Authors: Fan Zhang, Hanqin Yin, Aijun Du, Qianqin Zhou, Chao Zhang, Juan Bai, Ziqin Tan, Ting Liao, Yanhui Song, Ziqi Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aqueous zinc-ion batteries are promising sustainable energy-storage systems, yet their practical deployment is hindered by positive electrode structural instability during cycling, which has received less attention. Recognizing the critical role of orbital coupling in metal oxides, we regulate the high-voltage stability of vanadium oxide positive electrodes by modulating the electronic state of V in V2O3. Fluorine incorporation into the V-O framework induces asymmetric electron distribution, enhancing V-O orbital overlap and strengthening covalent interactions through reinforced π- and σ-bonding. This increased covalency reduces the basicity of axial oxygen atoms, suppressing proton attack and mitigating structural distortion at high charging voltages. F-V2O3 exhibits enhanced cycling stability, retaining 80% capacity over 60,000 cycles at 3 A g−1. Furthermore, Ah-level pouch cell delivers specific energy (based on all electrode materials) of 95 Wh kg−1 at 50 mA g−1. A practical pouch-cell pack with a 3.7 V output successfully powers drones. Here, we show the importance of covalency engineering for stabilizing oxide positive electrodes.

Introduction

The growing energy crisis and escalating environmental pollution necessitate the development of sustainable energy storage solutions1,2. Aqueous Zn-ion batteries (AZIBs) have emerged as a class of promising alternative to lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), offering advantages of abundant raw materials, low cost, and inherent safety3,4. Among potential positive electrode materials, vanadium-based compounds stand out for their wide redox range (V3+/V4+/V5+), enabling high specific energy5–10. Since the pioneering work by Nazar et al. on V2O5, layered vanadium oxides have been extensively explored for AZIBs11–14. However, a broader application is hindered by structural degradation during cycling, leading to rapid capacity fading and instability in aqueous environments. Specifically, V2O3 has a 3D tunnel structure, which facilitates faster ion diffusion, yet faces challenges of vanadium dissolution from the instability of V-O bonds when exposed to prolonged electrochemical cycling. The deficient engineering in the layer oxides plays a key role in electrochemically activating the redox15–18, but the local oxygen configuration suffers from instability during the ion interaction process, causing structure distortion during long-term cycling. Some strategies, such as vanadium defect engineering in V2O3, although achieved long cyclability (e.g., 30,000 cycles), often come at the cost of capacity, as the number of redox-active sites is reduced19. A fundamental understanding of these trade-offs is critical to designing vanadium-based positive electrodes that effectively balance high stability, capacity, and longevity.

From a molecular orbital coupling perspective, the hybridization of V 3d orbitals with O 2p orbitals in V2O3 results in the splitting of energy levels into bonding, antibonding, and nonbonding states. The covalent interactions, primarily through π- and σ-bonding, between vanadium and oxygen atoms significantly influence the stability of the V-O bond. Localized holes in the oxygen atoms help stabilize the vanadium structure by facilitating V-O bonding, adhering to the octet rule20. The orbital coupling between V and O, however, is not always preserved during redox processes, leading to instability in the coordination environment. Specifically, during electrochemical cycling, the initial redox process begins with the extraction of electrons from antibonding states within the V-O framework, where the V-O bond remains stable. As the antibonding orbitals are depleted, subsequent redox activity shifts to nonbonding or oxygen-centered orbitals, reducing the overlap between V 3d and O 2p orbitals. This diminished orbital coupling weakens the V-O covalency, making the structure more sensitive to changes in the local oxygen coordination during high-voltage redox cycling. Under these conditions, the weakened V-O bonds can facilitate side reactions, such as proton-coupled electron transfer (e.g., V2O3 + H+↔VO2+ + H2O), leading to vanadium evolution and structural degradation. Ultimately, these effects limit the reversibility and long-term stability of vanadium-based electrode materials21.

To reveal how orbital coupling affects redox chemistry as well as electrochemical stability, this study explores two strategies to modulate the vanadium electron state: (i) introducing oxygen vacancies and (ii) more electronegative anionic substitutional atoms to examine the degree of covalency on the stability of the positive electrode and thus the sustainability of AZIBs. When oxygen vacancies are introduced in V2O3 (OV-V2O3), when oxygen vacancies are introduced in V2O3 (OV-V2O3) via chemical redox with NaBH4 (SI), the electrons left behind after oxygen removal are transferred to vanadium 3d orbitals. This process disrupts the V-O-V bridging bonds, weakening the π- and σ-bonding interactions and diminishing orbital coupling between vanadium and oxygen, which ultimately reduces the overall covalency. In contrast, by incorporating a more electronegative anionic element, such as fluorine (F), it raises the vanadium oxidation state to reach a higher oxidation state of vanadium, such as V4+ (d1) and results in a lower energy of the 3d orbitals, enhancing the orbital overlap between the V 3d and O 2p orbitals and strengthening orbital coupling for stronger interactions between the empty vanadium eg orbitals and the oxygen 2p orbitals. Considering fluorine is the only anion that is more electronegative than oxygen for enhancing the covalency of V2O3, a concentration on F-substitution is paid in this work. The reduced antibonding competition in the V 3d orbitals allows for stronger σ-bonding, while the emptier t2g orbitals accept electron density from oxygen, enhancing π-bonding interactions as well.

In this work, through efficient F-substitution of the oxygen vacancies, operando X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) revealed that stronger covalency can be successfully introduced to ensure robust structural stability of the V2O3 positive electrode at high charge voltage. As a result, the F-doped V2O3 (F-V2O3) positive electrode demonstrated long-term capacity retention, maintaining 80% of its capacity after 60,000 cycles at 3 A g−1, and an Ah-level pouch cell delivered specific energy of 95 W h kg−1 at 50 mA g−1. The practical trial of a pouch cell pack with an output voltage of 3.7 V successfully drove a remote-controlled drone, highlighting the real-world application perspective of this promising strategy in this sustainable energy storage technology. Here, we show the importance of orbital coupling in stabilizing vanadium-based positive electrodes and offer valuable insights into designing more stable aqueous vanadium-based redox chemistry, which remains a challenging area to optimize in sustainable energy storage technologies.

Results

Relationship between the covalency properties and the mechanism

We deduce that lattice-oxidized vanadium exists in one of the following three configurations: (i) non-stabilized V2O3 with localized holes, (ii) π-stabilized V2O3, or (iii) σ-stabilized V2O3, as shown in Fig. 1a. Generally, the electronic energy of vanadium cations is stabilized by the surrounding O anions. According to the ligand field theory, the d orbitals of the octahedral V are split into three low-lying t2g orbitals and two high-lying eg orbitals. Specifically, the V cations have d orbitals electrons to overlap with the oxygen p orbitals, and the hybridization results in the energy splitting into antibonding states, bonding states, and nonbonding states (Fig. 1b). If the O sites in V2O3 are partially replaced by F, density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed that the bandgap is decreased from 0.92 eV to 0.55 eV, indicating an increased conductivity (Fig. S1). As depicted in Fig. 1c, d, the F substitution-induced asymmetric electron redistribution could boost the V-O orbital coupling by increasing the σ and π interactions. The morphology of V2O3 before and after the F substitution are shown in Fig. S2. The high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) characterization (Figs. 2b and S3, 4) shows a single crystal structure morphology of F-V2O3, where the brighter spots are deemed to be V atoms for their heavier mass and the quantitative intensities for both line 1 and 2 and don’t show the existence of another atoms (Fig. S5), indicating that the V atoms were not replaced. While F and Na atoms, which come from the NaF during the synthesis process, are too light to be detected by the HAADF technique, local electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) performed over the entire TEM region shown in Fig. S6 and the corresponding elemental signal indicates the presence of V, O, F, and Na (Figs. 2c and S7).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirms that the fluorine substitution or the oxygen vacancy does not induce significant structural changes to the V2O3 lattice (Fig. S8 and Table S1). Synchrotron-based X-ray absorption spectroscopy, including X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS), was employed to probe the local structural and electronic environments of V and O atoms. XANES provides valuable insights into the oxidation states and local coordination symmetry of V atoms (Fig. 2d), where the energy absorption positions of V K-edge (~5485 eV) for V2O3, OV-V2O3, and F-V2O3, falling between that of VO (V2+) and V2O5 (V5+), with metallic V foil (V0) at the lowest edge energy. It can be noted that the V k-edge of OV-V2O3 is located at the lowest energy, which is 1.01 eV lower than pristine V2O3 (Fig. 2d), indicating a more reduced V oxidation state. The two electrons associated with the formation of the O vacancy are localized in the empty V 3d orbitals, thereby reducing the vanadium valence and reducing the V-O covalency. Upon the substitution of O by a more electronegative F, electrons would be withdrawn from the V 3d orbitals, resulting in increased V oxidation state and a 2.87 eV V K-edge energy enhancement to pristine V2O3. Figure S9 illustrates the electron transfer from V to F in F-V2O3 corresponds to the calculated electron populations. Besides, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) also validated the electronic distribution asymmetry and the enhanced V oxidation states in F-V2O3 (Figs. S10 and S11). The elevated charge density associated with the higher oxidation state indicates a more extensive overlap between the V 3d and O 2p orbitals, thereby enhancing the V-O covalent bonding in F-V2O3. Additionally, the F substitution can alleviate electron-electron repulsion within the V 3d orbitals, thereby promoting more efficient orbital hybridization and further strengthening the V-O interaction. Wavelet transform (WT) analysis of the k3-weighted EXAFS data provides more information on the local coordination environment. The WT contours (Fig. S12) revealed the coordination variations in both V-O and V-V coordination for OV-V2O3 and F-V2O3, reflecting subtle changes in bond lengths and disorder.

Figure 2e presents the deconvoluted pre-peaks of the V K-edge XANES. The pre-edge feature of pristine V2O3 corresponds to the 1s-3d electric dipole-forbidden transition of V(6) in an octahedral symmetry. OV-V2O3 with a lower pre-peak energy supports the transfer of electrons into V 3d orbitals upon oxygen vacancy formation, and the lowest energy of the pre-peak of F-V2O3 suggests that more electrons are readily transferred into the V 3d orbitals and a lower charge transfer resistance during the electrochemical process. Apart from the main octahedral symmetry feature, a slight pre-edge signal appeared at a lower energy in pristine V2O3 and more distinct in OV-V2O3 should be from the lower-coordinated V(3) symmetry, confirming the presence of lower coordination, structural distortion, and symmetry breaking with the formation of O-deficit induced V(3) centers. In F-V2O3, however, the contribution of V(3) is negligible, as the F substitution to O maintains the predominantly coordinated V(6) centers. Based on the structural characterizations (inset of Fig. 2e), it is clear that V(3) with low coordination formed on the surface of pristine V2O3 and OV-V2O3, while V(6) dominates the surface of F-V2O3.

It is expected that the highly coordinated V(6) plays a dominant role in maintaining robust structural and chemical stability in electrochemical processes23. The intensity decrease could also be attributed to a reduction in electron scattering around the V orbitals caused by increased cationic disorder and a diminished availability of O ligands that usually facilitate scattering with the formation of O vacancies. Therefore, the charge compensation at high voltages in OV-V2O3 is primarily governed by V-O redox processes, which further leads to V-O decoordination, element dissolution, and progressive structural distortion, as supported by the fluctuations of the differential intensity (ΔI = In − I1) observed in the pre-edge region (pink area in bottom part of Fig. 3a). These spectral features an oxidation-driven oxygen-to-metal charge transfer at V sites, resulting in the formation of O-V-O species. In contrast, the presence of F in F-V2O3 significantly promotes the V-O orbital coupling strength, thereby stabilizing the redox behavior under high voltages, as observed the gradual shifts of V K-edges to higher energies with increasing voltages (Fig. 3b, c). Correspondingly, no obvious structural changes were observed in the pre-edge region (pink area in the bottom part of Fig. 3b), suggesting the minimal local atomic distortion and the extra structural stability for the V-centered redox process24,25. Compared with OV-V2O3, F-V2O3 exhibits an enhanced V K-edge pre-edge intensity and a subtle centroid upshift, indicating strengthened 3d-4p hybridization and increased V-O covalency induced by fluorine incorporation (Fig. 3c bottom). The XRD patterns collected on the samples after charging at different voltages also demonstrated that serious structural changes occurred in OV-V2O3 but not in F-V2O3 (Fig. S13).

It has been explored that redox chemistry strongly correlates with the structural transformation in layered oxides26,27. Herein, we employed the Fourier-transformed EXAFS, k³-weighted EXAFS data, for providing further structural insights into the transition metal ions, including atomic disorder, coordination environments, and bond distances, upon high-voltage charges (Figs. 3d, e, and S14a, b). In the contour maps for OV-V2O3 (Figs. 3d and S14a), where the peak at approximately 1.54 Å corresponds to the V-O distance in the first shell and the peak at around 2.75 Å represents the V-V distance in the second shell, the intensity and distances of both V-O and V-V bonds in OV-V2O3 significantly varied during the charging process and then partial recovered during the discharging, indicating a partly irreversible crystal disordering. In contrast, for F-V2O3 (Figs. 3e and S14b), no significant intensity and bond length variations were detected in both the charging and discharging processes, indicating that the robust structural stability contributed by the enhanced V-O covalency from the F-substitution. Besides, operando soft XAS measurements were performed to directly probe the V L2, L3 of OV-V2O3 and F-V2O3 (Fig. 3f, g). Clear spectral evolution is observed during charge and discharge. Specifically, the main V L3 peaks of both electrodes shift to lower energy during discharge, indicating a reduction of the V oxidation state. During charging, however, distinct behaviors are observed. For F-V2O3, the V L3 peak continuously shifts to higher energy with increasing voltage, consistent with a reversible V-centered redox process. In contrast, for OV-V2O3, the V L3 peak initially shifts to higher energy from 0.3 to 1.3 V but subsequently exhibits a reverse trend above 1.3 V, which reduces the V chemical valence. Figures 3h and S15 provide how the crystal structures of V2O3 respond towards redox chemistry upon charging at high voltages. The lower coordinated V(3) centers and the V-O-V configuration in OV-V2O3 are very vulnerable to redox chemistry at high voltages, and significant structural distortion was observed at 1.3 V. The local structure of V(6) in F-V2O3 remained unchanged up to 1.5 V. Based on the operando characterizations, therefore, the stronger V-O orbital coupling contributed by more electronegative F-substitution plays a key role in stabilizing both the structure and the redox chemistry at high voltages.

In a weakly acidic aqueous electrolyte, both H+ insertion/extraction and Zn2+ intercalation contribute together to the higher capacity of the battery28. However, the H+ insertion also leads to V-O dissolution. The uptake of H+ in OV-V2O3 resulted in a first discharge capacity of 45 mA h g−1 at 0.5 A g−1, corresponding to a 9.8% contribution to the total capacity (Fig. 4b). The initial high H+ uptake indicates that the O vacancies could be rapid diffusion pathways of H+ into the deeper lattice. With H+ insertion into the F-V2O3 lattice during discharging, as shown in the pH measurements conducted during electrochemical cycling (Fig. 4c), the pH value of the aqueous electrolyte rose for the decrease of protons. Upon charging, H+ were released back into the electrolyte, resulting in a pH decrease. In monitoring the local pH value changes on the positive electrode surfaces (Fig. S17), OV-V2O3 exhibited a much more pronounced increase in interfacial pH in the discharge process than that of F-V2O3. This behavior indicates enhanced proton consumption associated with parasitic proton-coupled reactions at the OV-V2O3/electrolyte interface, where intensified hydrogen evolution and interfacial instability are present. Operando voltage-resolved differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) was employed to monitor gas evolution during electrochemical polarization (Fig. S18). F-V2O3 exhibited strongly suppressed gas signals across the whole potential window, indicating mitigated interfacial water activity and enhanced electrochemical stability on the F-V2O3 surface. In the ZnSO4 electrolyte, the charge-discharge profiles of OV-V2O3 displayed an extended charge plateau at around ~1.33 V, and the corresponding dQ/dV plot revealed a redox peak at the same potential, which is attributed to the reaction of V2O3 + H+ ↔ VO2+ + H2O, for the formation of yellowish VO2+ as well as the dissolution of V from the electrode (Fig. 4d). This redox reaction and the V dissolution are not observed on the F-V2O3 electrode. From the viewpoint of molecular orbit theory, the O-V-O bonds and V(3) in OV-V2O3 with a weaker overlap are less covalent, leading to more negative charge accumulations on the axial oxygen atoms and enhancing the basicity for increased H+ reactivity and promoted V dissolution. On the contrary, the stronger covalency of F-V-O bonds in F-V2O3 alters oxygen atoms with less basicity and thus reduces the reactivity with protons. Compared to H+ insertion, the Zn2+ intercalation is more susceptible to structural distortion, which is significantly ascribed to the overall capacity fading of positive electrodes29. To investigate the influence of Zn2+ insertion on structural integrity, in-situ TEM was employed (Supplementary Movie 1). As shown in Figs. 4e and S19, the F-V2O3 electrode remained intact during the discharge process, exhibiting neither significant volume changes nor structural collapse. Post-cycling analysis by using TEM (Figs. 4f and S20) further revealed that OV-V2O3 displays pronounced crystal distortion, and the formation of V deficiencies resulted by V losses. The strain fields mapped by geometric phase analysis (GPA) confirmed the existence of significant tensile strains εxx in OV-V2O3 after 500 cycles, with the loss of V-O species, but negligible strain presented F-V2O3 (Fig. 4g). The operando ultraviolet-visible (UV–vis) analyses upon charging/discharging (Fig. S21) also reveal the appearance of absorption at ~250 nm associated with V-dissolution in OV-V2O3 but not in F-V2O330. Furthermore, operando Raman spectroscopy was conducted to provide complementary structural information. As shown in Fig. S22, the characteristic V-O vibrational modes in OV-V2O3 exhibit pronounced weakening and eventual disappearance during charging, indicating instability of the V-O coordination environment. In contrast, the V-O Raman features of F-V2O3 remain nearly unchanged throughout cycling, reflecting the enhanced stability of the V-O framework arising from strengthened covalency. In addition, inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis was performed in a three-electrode configuration on the electrolytes collected after defined charge-discharge cycling at 1 A g−1 (Fig. S23). As cycling proceeds, the V concentration in the electrolyte increases markedly for OV-V2O3, whereas only negligible and stable V levels were detected for F-V2O3.

The battery performance of positive electrodes were further evaluated in full coin cells with a Zn|2 M ZnSO4|F-V2O3 configuration to elucidate the role of strong V-O orbital coupling in electrochemical kinetics. The cyclic voltammetry (CV) mapping of F-V2O3 scanned at rates from 0.1 to 0.5 mV s−1 (Fig. 5a) reveals the cathodic processes associated with Zn2+ insertion (peaks 1 and 2) and anodic processes related to Zn2+ extraction (peaks 3 and 4), indicating a predominantly capacity-controlled behavior, i.e., 49% to 83% as the increase of scan rates (Figs. S24–S27), and the battery shows a pseudocapacitive capacity (Fig. S28). For the rate performance of F-V2O3 positive electrode (Figs. 5b and S29), discharge capacities of 419 and 201 mA h g−1 were reached with the specific current increased from 0.5 to 15 A g−1, highlighting its fast kinetic properties. The EIS measurements after the rate performance tests (Fig. S30) indicate that the F-V2O3 positive electrode has better conductivity. The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) was employed to evaluate the Zn2+ diffusion kinetics (Figs. 5c and S31). The Zn2+ diffusion coefficients in F-V2O3 are in orders of 10−10 to 10−7 cm2 s−1, which are one to two orders of magnitude higher than those of pristine V2O3 and OV-V2O3. Leveraging the stronger V-O covalency of F-V2O3 at high-voltage level, the battery could be charged to a cut-off potential of 1.5 V vs. Zn/Zn2+, corresponding to nearly 100% state of charge (SOC), wherein the inserted Zn2+ ions were fully extracted from the positive electrode. As illustrated in Fig. 5d, the coin cell with F-V2O3 achieved a long lifespan of over 60,000 cycles at 3 A g−1, with 80% capacity retention. In comparison, pristine V2O3 maintained 80% of its initial capacity within only 4000 cycles, and OV-V2O3 displayed obvious fading after 15,000 cycles. The introduction of oxygen vacancies can enhance electrochemical performance. However, over-extended cycling, reduced V-O covalency may compromise structural integrity and thus limit long-term cycling stability. Even at a low specific current of 0.5 A g−1 (Fig. 5e), the coin cell with F-V2O3 maintained a high specific capacity of 378 mA h g−1 over 5000 cycles, corresponding to 85% retention of the initial capacity. In contrast, OV-V2O3 and pristine V2O3 lost 37% and 55% of their capacities, respectively, after 1000 cycles. These results clearly demonstrate that F-V2O3 can sustain high-voltage operations without structural collapse. The Zn2+ concentration distributions within OV-V2O3 and F-V2O3 positive electrodes at a depth of discharge (DOD) from 0 to 100% were simulated by finite element analysis (FEA) (Fig. 5f). Compared to OV-V2O3, F-V2O3 exhibited a more homogeneous and concentrated Zn2+ distribution at high DODs, indicating that F-substitution enhances electronic conductivity and facilitates faster ion transport. Practically, under partial DOD conditions of 65% and 85% (Fig. S32), F-V2O3 also delivered higher capacities (368 mA h g−1 at 85% and 297 mA h g−1 at 65%) than those of OV-V2O3 (343 mA h g−1 at 85% and 263 mA h g−1 at 65%). To validate the practical applicability of the F-V2O3 positive electrode, pouch cells with a configuration of Zn|2 M ZnSO4|F-V2O3 was assembled at a high mass loading of ~18 mg cm−2 and an N/P ratio of 1.6, as the parameters shown in Table S3. The Zn anode was coated with a poly-dopamine layer to retain the self-reaction and was associated with HER (Fig. S33). Operated within a voltage window of 0.3–1.5 V, the 8 × 8 cm double-layer pouch cell delivered an Ah-level discharge capacity at a specific current of 50 mA g−1, retaining 83% of the capacity after 250 cycles without swelling (Fig. 5h). The specific energy of the pouch cell is about 95 W h kg−1 based on the total mass of electrodes, which is higher than some commercial Zn-based batteries and some recently reported V-based ZIB pouch cells (Fig. 5i and Table S4)36–41. Even at a higher specific current of 0.5 A g−1, the pouch cell maintained a robust discharge capacity of approximately 750 mA h (Fig. S34). Besides, a higher mass loading of ~23 mg cm−2 Ah-level pouch cell remain the 91% capacity retention and over 99% coulombic efficiency over 200 cycles (Fig. S35). In addition, the single-layer pouch cell with a N/P ratio of 2.8 achieved a specific energy of 90 W h kg−1 and retained 86% of the capacity after 350 cycles (Fig. S36). Furthermore, a customized battery pack composed of four pouch cells connected in series achieved an output voltage exceeding 3.7 V after undergoing a self-discharge process and supplied sufficient power to drive a 14 × 14 cm remote-controlled drone, highlighting its potential for real-world energy storage and portable electronics applications (Figs. 5j, S37, and Supplementary Movie 2).

As a further atomic-level insight into the covalency for the outperforming performance of F-V2O3 positive electrode, the density of states (DOS) and crystal orbital Hamilton population analysis (COHP) and integrated COHP (ICOHP) were employed to clarify the bond properties between V and O (Fig. 6). It is interesting that the ICOHP values, which quantifies the total bond strength between two atoms, of the V-O bonds in F-V2O3, i.e., −6.27 eV for non-charged (Fig. 6a) and −5.56 eV for charged F-V2O3 (Fig. 6b), are much higher than −3.71 eV for non-charged (Fig. 6c) and −1.16 eV for charged OV-V2O3 (Fig. 6d), approving a much stronger V-O orbital coupling with the formation of asymmetric O-V-F configurations with F institution. More negative ICOHP, indicating a stronger V-O bond. As a result, the electrode framework remains structurally more intact, which mitigates local lattice distortion and preserves continuous ion transport pathways, contributing to a higher Zn2+ ion diffusion rate. The optimal F substitution site and content in the F-V2O3 were optimized via DFT investigations (Figs. S38, 39, Table S5, and Supplementary Data 1). Particularly for the high voltage charging state, corresponding to a higher oxidation state of V (+5)42, the DOS for bonding is mainly contributed by V-dominant states, or a V-centered redox (Fig. 6b), while it is more O-dominant states in charged OV-V2O3 in a O-dominated redox (Fig. 6d). Intrinsic covalent orbital bonding index (ICOBI) was used to quantitively assess the V-O covalency, by which a higher ICOBI value close to 1 stands for a bond with strong covalency and a lower ICOBI values means a weaker bonding susceptible to bonding breaking or structure instability. F-V2O3 has an even stronger V-O covalency upon high-voltage charging/discharging states with an ICOBI of 1.14 vs. 1.08, but OV-V2O3 has a lower V-O covalency upon charging (0.58 vs. 0.62), indicating susceptible bonding states. The bond-length changes also match well with the covalency variations upon charging (Fig. S40). Discussion

This study demonstrated the relationship between orbital coupling or covalency and structural stability in vanadium oxides upon high-voltage and high-density sustainable energy storage. From the viewpoint of electronic coupling, the introduction of electronegative anions into V2O3 can induce an asymmetric F-V-O configuration for reaching stronger V-O covalency, by which V-centered redox reactions with less crystal structure disturbance occur to suppress irreversible VO-contained redox reactions upon wide-voltage electrochemical cycling and maintain robust structural stability in high-voltage charging/discharging processes. The F-V2O3 with stronger covalency as the positive electrode in Zn|2 M ZnSO4|F-V2O3 full cells reached a prolonged lifespan up to 60,000 cycles. Ah-level pouch cells exhibited a 95 W h kg−1 specific energy, and a pouch cell pack with an output voltage of 3.7 V successfully drove a 14 × 14 cm remote-controlled drone, demonstrating its potential for real-world energy storage and portable electronics applications of this class of sustainable aqueous batteries. This work provides a universal principle of strengthening the covalency of metal oxide positive electrodes for solving the issues of structural instability and element dissolution during service and offers a feasible way to design stable and high-density sustainable aqueous active metal batteries.

Methods

Materials synthesis

Preparation of V2O3

One hundred milligrams of vanadium (IV) oxy acetylacetonate was added into 25 mL tetrahydrofuran (THF) until it is completely dissolved. Then, 1.5 mL H2O2 was added into the solution drop by drop, keeping the mixture stirring for 5 h at 25 °C ± 1. The solution was transferred into a 50 mL polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) hydrothermal reactor for conducting the reaction at 200 °C for 24 h. After cooling down, collecting the precipitate via centrifugation and washing with THF, ethanol and DI-water for three times, respectively, and freezing the production for 24 h. The collected powders were calcined at 650 °C for 10 h with 5 °C /min heating rate at Ar gas atmosphere.

Preparation of OV-V2O3

After the hydrothermal reaction under the same conditions with the preparation of pristine V2O3, the washed products were added into a solution of 30 mL 4 M NaHB4 and stirred for 5 h at 25 °C ± 1 to generate oxygen vacancies. The treated products were washed again by centrifugation with DI water 15 times to remove any reactants and performed freeze-drying for 24 h. The dried powders were then calcined at 650 °C for 10 h with 5 °C /min heating rate at Ar gas atmosphere.

Preparation of F-V2O3

By applying the same procedures to the preparation of Ov-V2O3 till the freeze-drying, the dried powders were mixed with 12.5 mg NaF homogeneously and then calcined at 650 °C with 5 °C /min heating rate for 10 h at Ar gas atmosphere.

Preparation of dopamine-coated Zn anode

Zero point five grams of dopamine was dissolved in 50 mL DI water to form an aqueous solution, and the Zn foil was then soaked in the solution for 6 h. The coated Zn anode was obtained by drying in air.

Material characterizations

The crystal structures of the obtained three types of V2O3 were examined by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Bruker D8 Advanced) with Cu Kα radiation. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images were collected on a JEOL 7001 instrument. The atomic-scale structures were observed by an Aberration-corrected electron microscope—scanning transmission electron microscopy (JEOL ARM200F at 200 kV). X-ray absorption (XAS) characterization for V K-edge was processed by the soft X-ray beamline at the Australian Synchrotron. The electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) was collected with STEM, JEM-ARM300F (Grand ARM). Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra were collected on a Bruker A300-10/12 EPR spectrometer at 25 °C ± 1. The Elemental analysis was performed on an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS, Thermo ESCALAB 250 Xi, Al Kα radiation, hv = 1486.6 eV, America). Ultraviolet–visible (UV/vis) spectra were collected on Cary 60 with a wavelength range of 200–800 nm. Elemental analysis was performed on an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES, PerkinElmer Optima 8300 DV).

V K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) measurements were carried out at the Australian Synchrotron using the hard X-ray beamline operating at 3 GeV. The spectra were collected in fluorescence mode using a home-made cell; the coin cell case was punched with an 8 mm diameter hole to allow X-ray transmission during measurement, a Kapton film was used as the X-ray transparent window to seal the opening in the coin cell. The Kapton film had a thickness of 0.008 mm and was purchased from Okay Technologies. The film was securely attached to ensure both airtight sealing and X-ray transmission during the measurements. Specifically, the same active material, conductive additive, binder composition, mass loading, and electrode fabrication protocol were employed. The incident X-ray beam was monochromatized using a water-cooled Si (111) double-crystal monochromator. All V K-edge spectra were acquired in fluorescence mode, and a V metal foil was simultaneously measured as a reference for energy calibration. The XAS data were processed and analyzed using the Athena and Artemis software packages. Background subtraction and normalization procedures were applied to extract the EXAFS signal. The resulting χ(k) data were weighted and Fourier transformed into R-space using a Hanning window. Quantitative structural parameters around the central atoms were obtained by least-squares fitting of the EXAFS χ(k) data in R-space. In situ soft XAS measurements were also performed at the Australian Synchrotron, using the soft X-ray beamline in fluorescence mode with the same home-made cell.

Raman spectroscopy was conducted on a Renishaw Raman microscope with a 532 nm solid laser as an excitation source. An in-situ two-electrode Raman cell (Gaoss Union, B002-RM) was used.

The online DEMS system of HPR40 (HIDEN) was employed to monitor gas evolution during electrochemical polarization. A dedicated flow-through electrochemical cell was continuously flushed using a peristaltic pump, while Ar was introduced for 20 min prior to and during measurements to remove dissolved gases and stabilize the background signal. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed from −1.5 to 0.5 V (vs Ag/AgCl) at a scan rate of 10 mV s−1, with the active materials serving as the working electrodes.

The in situ TEM experiments were performed using an open-cell configuration with a Zues in situ TEM biasing holder (Zeptools) inside a JEOL NeoARM microscope equipped with a cold FEG. The working electrode consisted of the active material directly attached to a gold rod via gentle mechanical contact. A freshly cleaved zinc metal piece was mounted on a sharpened tungsten probe and served as the counter/reference electrode. The probe was positioned using the nanomanipulator until contact with the sample was established. A bias of −1 V vs. Zn was applied to the working electrode during zinc deposition, enabling real-time imaging and diffraction characterization. The electron dose rate was maintained at 0.1–0.5 pA nm−2 to minimize beam-induced damage.

In-situ pH measurements were performed using a calibrated micro pH electrode inserted into a home-made three electrodes cell with 10 mL 2 M ZnSO4 electrolyte under 25 °C, the pH sensor electrode is close to working electrode (F-V2O3 or OV-V2O3), with Pt and Ag/AgCl as counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively. The charge/discharge tests were performed at a specific current of 0.1 A g−1, and pH value were collected at potentials spaced at 0.1 V intervals.

For the ex-test of the electrodes, the coin cell is disassembled in air atmosphere at 25 °C, the electrode is gently washed by deionized water to remove the surface remaining electrolyte, the electrode is dried at vacuum oven at 50 °C for 24 h, and the prepared electrodes are used for ex XRD test. Electrochemical measurements

To investigate the electrochemical stripping/plating behaviors and the cycling stability of the materials, coin batteries were assembled into standard CR2032-type coin cells by using a glass fiber (Whatman, GF/A, diameter: 16 mm, thickness: 200 µm, average pore diameter: 1.6 µm) separator, Zn foil (d = 12 mm, 99.999% thickness = 50 µm, polished with 2000 and 5000 mesh sandpaper before cutting with the Kejing Disc Cutter with 12 Dia.), and 70 µL 2 M ZnSO4 aqueous electrolyte. For the full battery test, the electrodes were prepared by mixing active materials, acetylene black, and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) at a weight ratio of 80%:10%:10%, and the loading mass of active materials is about 1.7 mg cm−2. The mixture was dispersed into a small amount of methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP) solvent by grinding in an agate mortar to achieve a stably homogeneous paste. The slurry was pasted onto carbon cloth (thickness = 0.38 mm, m = 180 gm−2, resistance < 5 mΩ cm2) and dried at 100 °C for 12 h in a vacuum oven. The two stacked layers pouch-type batteries were assembled by sandwiching the electrolyte and the separators between two 80 mm × 80 mm cathodes and the Zn anode (82 mm × 82 mm) with the polydopamine as coating layer, and sealed by Al-plastic films, where the slurry was pasted onto stainless steel mesh (300 mesh, diameter: 12 mm, thickness: 0.1 mm) and dried at 100 °C for 12 h in a vacuum oven. The dosage of the electrolyte was about 4 mL. The sealed pouch cell was sandwiched between the home-made fixtures. The specific capacity of a coin cell is calculated based on the cathode active material’s mass, while the energy/power densities of the pouch cell are based on the weight of all electrodes. All assembly procedures were conducted in an atmosphere at 25 °C ± 1. The cells were galvanostatically charged/discharged over a voltage range of 0.3–1.5 V vs. Zn/Zn2+ at different specific currents. Galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) and cycling tested by using Neware Battery Testers. The CVs were tested using a CHI-760 electrochemical workstation. The three-electrode tests were performed in a home-made glass beaker with 10 mL 0.02 M H2SO4 electrolyte with Pt and Ag/AgCl as counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively. All the electrochemical tests are conducted under 25 °C room.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy

The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests were conducted with a CHI-760 electrochemical workstation. EIS spectra were collected at potentials spaced at 0.2 V intervals. The EIS spectra were recorded using a potentiostatic signal with an amplitude of 5 mV over a frequency range from 0.1 to 105 Hz, with 85 data points, 2 s open-circuit voltage time is applied before carrying out the EIS measurement.

Theoretical calculations

DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package43,44. The interactions between electron and ion were described using the projector augmented wave method, and the generalized gradient approximation was adopted by the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional45,46. The cutoff energy of plane-wave basis sets was set to be 400 eV, and convergence criteria for structural optimization were 10−5 eV (energy) and 0.01 eV/Å (force), respectively. Four-layer slab models were built as 2 × 2 supercells of V2O3(001) surface with a total number of 80 atoms (32 V and 48 O), where the bottom two layers were fixed. The Brillouin zones were sampled by γ-centered meshes of 3 × 3 × 1 grids for structural optimization and 6 × 6 × 1 for electronic analysis. Vacuum layers of around 16 Å were added to all slab models. In the COHP calculations, the energetic window was chosen as [−12 eV, 15 eV] based on DOS calculations. The 3p, 3d and 4s orbitals were included for vanadium, and the 2s and 2p orbitals were considered for oxygen and fluorine. COMSOL Multiphysics 5.6 was used to simulate the modeling of Zn2+ insertion uniformity of F-V2O3 and OV-V2O3 (Supplementary Data 2). The model was based on the calculations of currents in the electrolyte and electrodes and the “cubic current distribution” interface. In this case, the electrochemical reactions, current, and ion transport were fully coupled to accurately describe the system behavior.
