Debates and progress in the management of intermediate-stage (BCLC stage B) hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Jianan Jiang, Yinbiao Cao, Shang Gao, Yanqin Hu, Shizhong Yang, Haowen Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the global incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) on the rise, precise staging has become critical for guiding treatment decisions and improving long-term outcomes. The Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system, widely used for HCC classification, incorporates factors such as Child–Pugh A or B liver function, tumor size and number, absence of cancer-related symptoms, and lack of vascular invasion or extrahepatic metastasis. For intermediate-stage HCC, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) remains the globally recommended standard. However, the substantial heterogeneity in tumor burden and liver function among patients means that not all individuals benefit equally from TACE. As a result, a uniform treatment approach is insufficient. Preserving liver function is now recognized as equally important as achieving high objective response rates, with the ultimate goal of prolonging overall survival. In response, researchers have proposed advanced stratification methods for stage B HCC to optimize therapeutic outcomes. While these stratification criteria remain under debate, there is a growing shift from TACE-centric strategies toward personalized targeted therapies for specific subpopulations. This review explores advanced stratification concepts, evaluates corresponding treatment strategies, analyzes ongoing clinical trials, and assesses their potential to transform the management of intermediate-stage HCC—while also outlining future directions for its treatment.Keywords: Intermediate-stage hepatocellular carcinoma, Heterogeneity, TACE, Precise treatment

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, and its incidence is continuing to rise.1, 2, 3 Despite treatment advances, its long-term prognosis remains unfavorable.4 Accurate staging is essential for guiding therapy and significantly impacts patient outcomes. Among available systems, the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system is the most widely used.5 Patients classified with intermediate-stage HCC under BCLC exhibit a wide range of tumor burdens and liver function levels. This group is heterogeneous, differing substantially from both early and advanced stages.6 Subclassifying these patients and tailoring treatment accordingly is therefore critical.7 However, the current BCLC system does not differentiate subgroups within this stage, limiting the development of personalized treatment strategies.5

Traditionally, the BCLC system limits treatment for intermediate-stage HCC to transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) alone.8,9 However, the efficacy and safety of TACE remain debated, particularly for certain patients within this group.10,11 This underscores the urgent need for novel treatment strategies with well-defined patient selection criteria to improve management of this heterogeneous population. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and targeted therapies have shown significant progress, offering promising new options for liver cancer treatment. These developments have also driven ongoing evolution in liver cancer management concepts and protocols. Although several new scoring and classification systems for liver function and tumor staging have been proposed, none have gained universal acceptance.

Given the complexity of intermediate-stage liver cancer and the need for precise treatment, expert panels have proposed updated BCLC classifications. These revisions incorporate clinical decision-making and account for treatment stage migration.12 They highlight the importance of stratifying patients with intermediate-stage HCC into substages to enable tailored therapies. This review summarizes several key classification concepts for intermediate-stage liver cancer and explores the challenges in applying current guidelines, offering a foundation for future research.

Subclassification criteria for intermediate-stage HCCs

Heterogeneity of intermediate-stage HCCs and subclassification controversy

Intermediate HCC, classified as BCLC stage B, includes a diverse group of patients with preserved liver function (Child–Pugh class A or B) but large or multifocal tumors, without cancer-related symptoms, macrovascular invasion, or extrahepatic spread.13, 14, 15

This group is highly heterogeneous, with wide variation in tumor size, number, liver function, age, and comorbidities.6 Such complexity complicates treatment decisions, requiring therapies such as liver transplantation (LT), TACE, or systemic treatments to be tailored to each patient's unique profile. Combination of these factors.7 Although no universally accepted treatment standard exists, several efforts have been made to address this gap.

In 2012, Bolondi et al.6 introduced a classification system dividing intermediate HCC into four subgroups—B1 to B4—based on criteria such as the Child–Pugh score, Milan criteria, and the presence of portal venous thrombosis. In 2015, Kudo7 proposed an updated version, known as the Kinki Criteria, refining this into three subgroups—B1, B2, and B3. Additional classification systems include the China Liver Cancer (CNLC) staging system,16 the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) guidelines,17 the Hong Kong Liver Cancer (HKLC) classification of China,18 and the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines.19 All aim to personalize treatment and maximize therapeutic benefit. The following sections provide a detailed analysis of these approaches.

A notable issue across these systems is the ambiguity in categorizing single large tumors, particularly those over 5 cm in diameter. The original BCLC system, updated in 2011, classified single tumors of >5 cm as stage A, based on the assumption of more favorable biological behavior.20 However, this categorization remains under debate21 because large tumors are more likely to show microvascular invasion and intrahepatic metastasis—both independent risk factors for recurrence and mortality.22, 23, 24 Some experts, including the Spanish group that developed the BCLC system, suggest these tumors may be more appropriately classified as stage B.25 The current EASL guidelines continue to classify single tumors of >5 cm as stage A, but the controversy persists.17

Progress in multi-omics research on the staging of HCC

Conventional staging systems group patients with similar characteristics (e.g., tumor size and number), yet these patients often show substantial differences in biological behavior, treatment response, and prognosis. Although updated systems aim for greater precision, there is still significant potential in developing more refined, multidimensional models—particularly through multi-omics technologies that reveal intrinsic tumor heterogeneity at molecular, cellular, and imaging levels.

Patients with HCC exhibit diverse genomic, epigenomic, and transcriptomic profiles, resulting in varied responses to treatment regimens.26,27 Whole-genome analysis advances precision oncology by identifying somatic mutations and gene amplifications, enabling the use of inclusion and exclusion biomarkers to improve care quality.28 Liquid biopsy, a non-invasive alternative to tissue biopsy, monitors tumor progression, metastasis, and recurrence. Next-generation sequencing enables accurate profiling of liquid biopsy components, including cell-free circulating tumor DNA, circulating tumor cells, and genetic contents of extracellular vesicles, such as microRNA, long non-coding RNA, and circular RNA.29 Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest that genomic analysis via liquid biopsy is a promising strategy for identifying individual mutation patterns in HCC. However, these assays tend to show relatively low sensitivity and high specificity for early HCC detection and carry a risk of bias.30, 31, 32

Transcriptomics provides functional insights, linking genomic alterations to phenotype.29 Tumor-derived exosomes can reprogram target cells, promoting proliferation, growth, and metastasis.33 The content and dynamic changes in exosomal cargo mirror those of their parental tumor cells, supporting their use in diagnosis, prognosis, and prediction of therapeutic response.34 Xue et al. identified eight exosomal miRNAs (miR-122, miR-125b, miR-145, miR-192, miR-194, miR-29a, miR-17-5p, and miR-106a) showing significantly different expression between HCC and normal serum samples.35

Deep learning effectively handles complex data such as medical imaging, molecular networks, and gene sequences, with growing application in oncology, especially genomics, transcriptomics, and radiomics.36 Radiomics, introduced by Lambin et al., in 2012,37 extracts high-throughput imaging features (e.g., shape, texture, intensity) to reflect underlying tissue physiology and pathology. By integrating these features with clinical data via artificial intelligence models, radiomics addresses tumor heterogeneity and contributes to diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis. Studies by Yasaka, Hamm et al., and Yanqi et al. have validated the value of radiomics in HCC diagnosis and differential diagnosis.38, 39, 40 Research indicates that magnetic resonance imaging radiomics shows promise for early HCC detection and personalized preoperative planning to optimize outcomes.41 Advances in radiomics have enabled accurate prediction of HCC pathological subtypes in multiple studies.42,43 However, deep learning models require large, curated datasets—typically thousands of cases—and still face challenges in external validation.44, 45, 46

Integrating multi-omics data—characterized by high dimensionality and mixed data types (e.g., continuous, categorical, imaging)—requires advanced bioinformatics and artificial intelligence tools. This integration reveals tumor heterogeneity overlooked by conventional approaches, enabling more precise prognostic assessment, individualized treatment selection,47 dynamic monitoring, early intervention, and biomarker discovery through comprehensive characterization across multiple levels of variation.48 Still, most current studies are retrospective or based on small prospective cohorts, highlighting the need for large-scale, multicenter prospective trials to validate these models’ predictive value and clinical utility. Furthermore, because this research involves sensitive, multidimensional patient data, it demands strong ethical frameworks, privacy protection, and improved data-sharing practices to support continued progress.

Staging progression of intermediate-stage HCCs

In 2012, an expert panel proposed a revised classification of intermediate-stage HCC, subdividing it into four subgroups (B1–B4) based on criteria such as the Child–Pugh score, tumor burden relative to the Milan criteria, performance status (PS), and presence of portal venous thrombosis.6 For example, patients in subgroup B1 are defined by a Child–Pugh score of 5–7, normal PS, and a tumor burden within the “up-to-7” criteria, with a median overall survival (OS) of 41 months. The “up-to-7” criterion is a key marker, indicating potential eligibility for LT under extended criteria or through downstaging strategies.12

While Bolondi's subclassification of BCLC B is both feasible and widely recognized, it has limitations. Subgroups B1, B2, and B3 correspond to Child–Pugh scores of 5–7, 5–6, and 7, respectively, which complicates stratification. Furthermore, treatment recommendations for these groups primarily rely on TACE, and in practice, treatment outcomes show little distinction compared with the original BCLC staging. This raises concerns about the classification's effectiveness in guiding clinical decisions.7 In response, Kudo et al. proposed the Kinki criteria, simplifying the system by integrating Child–Pugh scores with tumor burden assessments based on the Milan and up-to-7 criteria.49,50 Under this model, patients are grouped into three categories: B1 (Child–Pugh score of 5–7, within up-to-7 criteria), B2 (Child–Pugh score of 5–7, beyond up-to-7 criteria), and B3 (Child–Pugh score of 8–9, any tumor burden).7

In China, the CNLC staging system offers an alternative approach. Its latest version (2022) classifies intermediate-stage HCC into stages IIa and IIb based on tumor characteristics. Stage IIa includes patients with PS 0–2, Child–Pugh A or B liver function, two or three tumors with a maximum diameter of >3 cm, and no vascular invasion or extrahepatic spread. Stage IIb includes patients with PS 0–2, Child–Pugh A or B, at least four tumors (regardless of size), and no evidence of vascular invasion or extrahepatic disease.51, 52, 53 For stage IIa, surgical resection remains the primary treatment, while stage IIb patients are generally not surgical candidates.54 Instead, non-surgical treatments like TACE are often recommended, although surgery may still be considered in select cases—e.g., when tumors are confined to one segment or extrahepatic lesions are treatable with intraoperative radiofrequency ablation (RFA).55 The CNLC system has also expanded to incorporate newer therapies, offering more comprehensive treatment guidance.53

Building on this, the 2022 update of the BCLC guidelines further refines intermediate-stage HCC stratification, acknowledging the group's heterogeneity. Patients with BCLC B are now categorized into three subgroups based on tumor burden and liver function,12 as shown in Fig. 1. The first subgroup includes candidates for LT who meet “Extended Liver Transplant criteria,” typically incorporating tumor size and alpha-fetoprotein levels.56 The second group comprises patients ineligible for LT but with well-defined nodules and preserved portal flow, making them suitable for TACE. The third group includes patients with diffuse, infiltrative liver disease affecting both lobes, for whom TACE is generally ineffective; systemic treatment is usually recommended.12 Compared with the 2018 version, LT is now considered for a subset of BCLC B patients following successful downstaging with TACE or transarterial radioembolization (TARE). This updated framework integrates clinical decision-making to support more personalized treatment, based on both patient characteristics and institutional expertise.12

The latest Japan Society of Hepatology (JSH) guidelines emphasize the extreme heterogeneity of intermediate-stage HCC in terms of liver function, tumor size, and tumor number.49 Within this group, liver function ranges from Child–Pugh class A (5 points) to class B (9 points), tumor sizes range from <1 to >10 cm, and tumor numbers range from four to dozens of nodules. The BCLC staging system classifies HCC into five stages: 0 (very early), A (early), B (intermediate), C (advanced), and D (terminal). JSH endorses the Japan Integrated Staging (JIS) score—combining the Liver Cancer Study Group of Japan TNM classification with the Child–Pugh score—as the most practical and versatile tool for staging HCC.57 The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines adopt a treatment-oriented approach, stratifying patients into four groups: potentially resectable or transplantable, operable by PS or comorbidity; unresectable disease; liver-confined, inoperable due to PS, comorbidity, or limited extrahepatic disease; and metastatic disease or extensive intrahepatic tumor burden.58 The European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines continue to follow BCLC-B staging criteria. These systems remain subject to revision as new evidence emerges to optimize treatment strategies and improve prognosis. Importantly, throughout this review, the term “intermediate-stage” refers specifically to BCLC stage B, not to equivalent categories defined by other guidelines.

Evolving paradigm: TACE toward more aggressive surgical approaches

The BCLC staging system has traditionally excluded surgical resection as a primary treatment for intermediate-stage (BCLC B) HCC, favoring TACE as the standard approach. Innovations such as superselective conventional TACE and drug-eluting bead TACE (DEB-TACE) have improved safety and efficacy, with DEB-TACE demonstrating better survival outcomes and less liver dysfunction,59,60 particularly for tumors of >5 cm,7 although appropriate use depends on careful patient selection based on hepatic reserve and tumor burden heterogeneity.61,62

Meanwhile, advances in surgical techniques have expanded the feasibility of resection and transplantation for select cases of BCLC B/C.63, 64, 65, 66 Increasing evidence supports resection over TACE for multifocal HCC in patients with preserved liver function, showing superior long-term survival in those with Child–Pugh A status, Model for End-Stage Liver Disease score of <9, and PS of 0–1,25,67 especially for patients with two or three lesions, regardless of size.66

Transformation of treatment ideas for intermediate-stage HCC in the updated guidelines

The 2022 revision of the BCLC guidelines emphasizes the importance of tailoring therapy for BCLC-B patients based on individual tumor burden.68 Adequate liver function remains essential for TACE eligibility.61 For patients unsuitable for TACE, systemic therapy is the recommended alternative,69, 70, 71, 72 and effective downstaging may allow for subsequent LT.

The latest JSH guidelines challenge the idea of TACE as the sole standard for intermediate-stage HCC, highlighting the availability of up to six systemic treatment options.73 As a result, various subclassification systems and associated therapeutic strategies have been proposed to address the heterogeneity of this stage.7,49,74 The fifth revised JSH-HCC guidelines introduce notable changes to the treatment algorithm: liver resection and RFA are prioritized for patients with up to three HCC lesions of ≤3 cm, and a separate treatment sequence is outlined for HCC with vascular invasion. Based on newly included randomized controlled trials from Hong Kong, China and Japan, which showed comparable outcomes between resection and ablation for small tumors, liver resection is now recommended as first-line therapy for up to three lesions of >3 cm, with TACE as second-line. For patients with four or more lesions, TACE remains first-line, followed by hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) or systemic therapy. Systemic therapy is indicated for patients with Child–Pugh A liver function and extrahepatic metastasis. In cases of vascular invasion, major revisions recommend liver resection for resectable disease and systemic therapy for unresectable cases, followed by TACE or HAIC—superseding previous equal recommendations among TACE, resection, HAIC, and targeted therapy.73

The 2025 ESMO guidelines strongly advocate multidisciplinary team management for intermediate-stage HCC.75 Liver resection is supported for BCLC-B patients not suited for intra-arterial therapies, with a preference for minimally invasive surgery in patients with cirrhosis. Orthotopic LT (OLT) is recommended for patients meeting the Milan criteria (expected recurrence of <10%, 5-year survival of approximately 70%), and University of California San Francisco criteria may be considered for those exceeding Milan limits. OLT may be preferred over locoregional therapies when the expected 5-year OS exceeds 50%. DEB-TACE or conventional lipiodol TACE is recommended for BCLC-B patients, with TARE or bland embolization as alternatives. Adjuvant systemic therapy—whether tyrosine kinase inhibitors or ICI-based combinations—following resection, ablation, or OLT is not recommended at this stage.

Reflecting expert consensus, the 2025 NCCN guideline update prioritizes liver resection or LT as established, potentially curative options for eligible patients meeting surgical or transplant criteria. Locoregional therapies (ablation, arterial-directed therapy, radiotherapy) remain the typical first-line approach for patients unsuitable for surgery or LT. Ablation may serve as definitive treatment for carefully selected patients with small, well-positioned tumors following multidisciplinary review. Transarterial therapies (TACE, DEB-TACE, or TARE with Y-90 microspheres) are recommended for unresectable or inoperable tumors not amenable to ablation.58 All guidelines stress multidisciplinary evaluation before treatment initiation and highlight the importance of meticulous patient selection and engagement.

Together, these internationally recognized guidelines reflect a clear shift toward expanding surgical indications to improve clinical outcomes for patients with intermediate-stage HCC.

Concept of TACE unsuitability/ineligibility in intermediate-stage HCC and treatment strategy

The 2022 BCLC guideline update recommends TACE for non-transplant candidates with well-defined tumors and preserved portal perfusion.12 However, patients considered “TACE-unsuitable”—those exceeding the up-to-7 criteria, with albumin–bilirubin (ALBI) grade 2 liver function, or with aggressive tumor biology—require alternative approaches.76 The Asia–Pacific Primary Liver Cancer Expert (APPLE) consensus defines TACE unsuitability based on three main criteria:

In alignment with this, the updated JSH guidelines73 recommend initiating molecular targeted agents with high objective response rates (ORRs) as first-line therapy for TACE-unsuitable patients, instead of repeated TACE—which can impair liver function and compromise future systemic treatment eligibility. For intermediate-stage HCC patients with preserved liver function who are refractory or unlikely to respond to TACE, introducing systemic therapy may prolong survival.73 Strategies such as lenvatinib pretreatment followed by selective TACE have shown potential benefit in this population.76,78 Defining TACE unsuitability and establishing novel therapeutic strategies, including combination immunotherapy, remain critical challenges in the era of expanded systemic options.77 For Child–Pugh A patients, molecular targeted agents are recommended as first-line therapy after TACE failure or refractoriness, while HAIC may be considered on an individual basis for Child–Pugh B patients or those with more compromised liver function.79 Emerging evidence suggests that delaying the switch to systemic therapy until after TACE failure may be suboptimal, underscoring the need for earlier transition.

The revised ESMO Clinical Practice Guideline identifies combination therapy as the preferred first-line approach, with all four ICI-based combinations considered viable. However, atezolizumab–bevacizumab is contraindicated in patients with a high bleeding risk. For patients ineligible for immunotherapy, sorafenib or lenvatinib remain appropriate first-line options.80 In the second-line setting, regorafenib, cabozantinib, and ramucirumab are the only agents with evidence-based sequencing following sorafenib. No clear efficacy differences have been established among them, although ramucirumab is indicated only for patients with alpha-fetoprotein levels of >400 ng/mL.75

The NCCN Guidelines Version 1.202558 designate atezolizumab plus bevacizumab (for Child–Pugh class A) and tremelimumab–actl plus durvalumab as preferred first-line systemic therapies. Other recommended first-line options include sorafenib (Child–Pugh A or B7), lenvatinib (Child–Pugh A), durvalumab, and pembrolizumab. Conditionally recommended options include nivolumab (Child–Pugh B), atezolizumab–bevacizumab (Child–Pugh B), and nivolumab–ipilimumab for tumors with a high mutational burden. Several agents are also recommended for subsequent-line therapy upon disease progression.

Application of portal vein embolization (PVE) and portal vein embolization with stem cell augmentation (PVESA)

Conventional PVE is used to induce hypertrophy of the future liver remnant (FLR), allowing patients initially deemed ineligible for resection due to insufficient FLR volume to become surgical candidates.81 However, its success may be limited, particularly in patients with liver dysfunction or comorbidities.82 To address this, PVESA combines PVE with stem cell therapy, specifically aimed at enhancing FLR growth in patients with significant liver fibrosis or comorbid conditions. Initial evidence supporting PVESA came from a pilot study by Schulte am Esch et al., involving three patients treated with intraportal infusion of autologous CD133+ bone marrow-derived stem cells (BMSCs) following PVE, compared with three PVE-only controls. The BMSC group showed a 2.5-fold greater mean daily increase in FLR volume (p < 0.01), and all underwent successful resection. By contrast, two control patients were ineligible for surgery because of disease progression within the FLR.83 Subsequent studies have evaluated PVESA in broader clinical settings, indicating it is generally safe and can enhance hepatic regeneration post-PVE.84, 85, 86, 87 However, optimizing stem cell selection, harvesting, and delivery remains key to maximizing benefit and minimizing risk. To date, PVESA studies have used BMSCs and hematopoietic stem cells.82 New approaches—such as cell priming, genetic engineering, and extracellular vesicle-based therapies—are being developed to further improve liver regenerative capacity, with mesenchymal stem cells as a current focus.88,89 Despite its potential, PVESA requires further investigation, including mechanistic studies in orthotopic liver cancer models with coexisting liver injury and larger human trials.

Beyond their role as preoperative bridging strategies for patients with insufficient FLR, both PVE and PVESA can be incorporated into sequential, integrated treatment regimens combining targeted therapy and immunotherapy. Multicenter studies have shown that laparoscopic right hepatectomy following sequential TACE and PVE is safe and effective, increasing the likelihood of achieving textbook outcomes and improving 5-year OS.90 Furthermore, a case report demonstrated that combined HAIC, lenvatinib, and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibody therapy followed by concurrent TACE and PVE can serve as an effective conversion therapy—promoting tumor necrosis and increasing functional liver volume in patients with advanced HCC.91

Contemporary clinical practice increasingly favors surgical modalities—including resection, RFA, and LT—over TACE-centered approaches, although optimal patient selection remains a critical challenge requiring multidisciplinary evaluation.

Exploration of systemic therapy

Systemic therapy is the recommended treatment for BCLC B patients who are not candidates for TACE because of various factors, including diffuse, infiltrative, or extensive bilobar liver disease or progression following TACE.12 In recent years, significant advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapy have emerged,92 rapidly gaining attention for their potential in liver cancer treatment. This section explores recent developments in systemic therapies for HCC, focusing on clinical trials and their implications for patient management.

Advancements in targeted therapeutics and immunotherapy

The role of systemic therapy in HCC has evolved considerably since the landmark SHARP and Asia–Pacific trials in 2007, which demonstrated the survival benefit of sorafenib, a molecularly targeted agent.93 Sorafenib, an oral multikinase inhibitor, suppresses tumor growth by inhibiting serine–threonine kinases C-Raf, wild-type B-Raf, and mutant (V600E) B-Raf—key components of the Raf/MEK/ERK signaling pathway. Both trials reported significant OS improvement with sorafenib over placebo, establishing it as the standard of care for advanced HCC.94,95 Despite its efficacy, sorafenib has limitations, including modest tumor shrinkage and notable systemic toxicity such as hand–foot skin reactions.94, 95, 96, 97

To address these shortcomings, ongoing efforts have focused on developing targeted therapies with greater efficacy and fewer side effects. Lenvatinib, an oral kinase inhibitor, has emerged as a promising alternative.93 Lenvatinib inhibits fibroblast growth factor receptor 4, a key driver of HCC progression, and has demonstrated strong anti-tumor activity.80,98 The REFLECT trial comparing lenvatinib to sorafenib showed a higher ORR of 40.6% versus 12.6%, as well as improvements in progression-free survival (PFS) and time to progression.99 Moreover, the median OS was slightly longer in the lenvatinib group than in the sorafenib group (13.6 vs. 12.3 months).80 Additionally, lenvatinib was associated with fewer adverse events, including lower rates of hand–foot syndrome and diarrhea, making it an attractive option for patients with advanced HCC.80 Lenvatinib has since gained regulatory approval in the United States (U.S. Food and Drug Administration),100 Europe,101 China,102 and Japan.93

Regorafenib, another oral kinase inhibitor, is structurally similar to sorafenib but exhibits a more potent toxicity profile.103 In the RESORCE trial, regorafenib significantly improved OS in patients with advanced HCC who had tolerated and subsequently progressed on sorafenib. Median OS was 10.6 months in the regorafenib group, compared with 7.8 months in the placebo group.70 In addition to OS, regorafenib improved both PFS and time to progression, establishing it as a viable second-line treatment option. Importantly, however, regorafenib may not be suitable for patients who were intolerant to sorafenib because its use is approved only for those who previously tolerated sorafenib treatment. Other promising targeted agents, such as cabozantinib104 and ramucirumab,72 have also emerged as viable options for patients who do not respond to sorafenib, providing alternative treatment avenues in the second-line setting.103

In addition to targeted therapies, immunotherapy has gained significant traction in HCC treatment, particularly through the use of ICIs. HCC is characterized by an immunosuppressive microenvironment that enables tumor cells to evade immune detection, with upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules playing a central role. ICIs targeting cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4), PD-1, and its ligand PD-L1 have shown promise in reversing this suppression and enhancing anti-tumor immunity.105

The recent large phase III IMbrave150 study69,106 further underscored the potential of combining ICIs with targeted therapies in HCC management.107,108 However, the lack of reliable biomarkers to predict treatment response remains a major unmet need, driving ongoing research. As systemic options continue to expand, treatment selection is becoming increasingly complex,93 a topic discussed later in this review. These advancements represent a paradigm shift in HCC therapy across all stages, with further significant changes expected in the near future.

The advent of combination therapy: optimal approaches to integrating treatments

In recent years, HCC treatment has increasingly shifted toward combination strategies that integrate locoregional therapies with systemic agents. Locoregional treatments—such as TACE, HAIC, selective internal radiotherapy, and external beam radiotherapy—have traditionally been used in patients without extrahepatic metastases to improve resectability and disease control.109 Historically, systemic therapies such as sorafenib have shown limited ORRs, typically around 10% in advanced HCC.95 However, emerging systemic combinations, particularly those pairing anti-angiogenic agents with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies, have demonstrated markedly improved response rates. For instance, lenvatinib plus nivolumab achieved an ORR of 54.2%,110 lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab achieved 36.0%, and atezolizumab plus bevacizumab achieved 30.0%.111,112 These combinations not only increase the ORR but also improve survival outcomes, making the integration of local and systemic therapies a leading strategy in advanced liver cancer management.

Evidence consistently shows that combination therapy outperforms single-modality approaches, including surgery or TACE alone. A key example is the “Upfront Systemic Therapy Followed by Curative Conversion” strategy proposed by Kudo's team, targeting patients with intermediate-stage HCC unsuitable for TACE.113 This approach uses systemic therapy—alone or in combination with TACE—to shrink tumors, enabling curative resection or ablation. Supporting this, the prospective randomized controlled TACTICS trial conducted across 33 centers in Japan demonstrated significantly longer survival with TACE plus sorafenib versus TACE alone (median OS of 25.2 vs. 13.5 months, respectively).114 Similarly, sequential therapy with lenvatinib and TACE (LEN-TACE) has shown notable efficacy in patients exceeding the up-to-7 criteria.76,78 A proof-of-concept study reported a 73.3% ORR and 37.9-month median survival for LEN-TACE, compared with 33.3% ORR and 21.3 months for TACE alone.78 This synergistic effect113 is believed to result from lenvatinib's ability to normalize the tumor vasculature, thereby enhancing the intratumoral distribution of chemotherapeutic agents during TACE.115,116

Moreover, the combination of atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) and bevacizumab (anti-vascular endothelial growth factor) with TACE—termed “ABC Transformative Therapy”—has been gaining attention for its potential to revolutionize treatment of unresectable HCC, as shown in Fig. 2.117 Atezo/Bev plays a central role by promoting tumor shrinkage and exerting anti-vascular endothelial growth factor effects prior to TACE. This approach not only enhances TACE efficacy but also facilitates conversion of previously unresectable tumors into candidates for curative therapies such as resection or ablation following tumor reduction.113,118 Atezo/Bev also induces tumor antigen release and activates the cancer immunity cycle; when paired with TACE-induced tumor necrosis, this synergy enhances overall treatment efficacy.119 The rate of curative conversion has reached 30%, underscoring its therapeutic potential. In the IMbrave 150 trial,69 Atezo/Bev significantly improved OS and median PFS compared with sorafenib, further establishing its role as a frontline therapy.120 Following these results, Atezo/Bev received global regulatory approval.69 Indications for curative conversion with this approach include achieving tumor shrinkage and enhancing the treatment response, even without shrinkage, among others.117 Kudo has proposed prioritizing the administration of targeted therapies before TACE in this context.76

Recent advances in conversion therapy—aimed at enabling curative resection in initially unresectable tumors—have contributed to improved outcomes in patients with HCC.112 Conversion therapy, which utilizes locoregional or systemic treatments to render unresectable tumors resectable,109 has shown notable promise. At ESMO 2023, Professor Lu Shichun's team from the Chinese PLA General Hospital121 presented a prospective phase II extension study demonstrating that lenvatinib combined with PD-1 inhibitors converted 52% of unresectable intermediate-stage HCC to a resectable status, with favorable prognostic outcomes. Key results included an 85.7% R0 resection rate post-conversion, a 38.1% pathological complete remission rate, and a 1-year recurrence-free survival rate approaching 50%. Both ORR and OS data suggested durable survival benefits, reinforcing the value of combined therapies in sequential conversion strategies for intermediate-to-advanced HCC. Although the study included mostly intermediate-advanced cases, the absence of a subgroup analysis specific to intermediate-stage HCC underscores the need for further investigation. These findings provide a solid basis for refining conversion protocols and expanding curative options for patients with advanced disease. Future studies should prioritize large-scale randomized trials and biomarker discovery to improve patient selection and optimize treatment outcomes.

As ongoing clinical trials continue to evaluate various local–systemic combinations, this integrative approach offers not only theoretical rationale but also growing clinical evidence. With these encouraging results, treatment paradigms for intermediate and advanced HCC are likely to continue shifting toward multimodal strategies aimed at maximizing survival and long-term prognosis.

Emerging anti-tumor strategies

Beyond conventional targeted and immunotherapeutic approaches, several emerging anti-tumor strategies show potential for treating intermediate-to-advanced stage HCC. This section outlines recent research findings on these novel therapies for intermediate-stage disease.

Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy and oncolytic viruses represent promising new avenues for advanced HCC, although their long-term efficacy in solid tumors remains under investigation.122 Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy represents a revolutionary advance in cancer treatment.123 Both clinical and preclinical studies have demonstrated its notable efficacy against HCC. The approach involves isolating a patient's T cells, genetically modifying them ex vivo to recognize and kill cancer cells, and then reinfusing the engineered cells.124 Several molecular targets—such as GPC3, c-Met, and NKG2D—have shown considerable promise in this context as immunotherapeutic targets for HCC.125

Oncolytic virus therapy is an emerging adjunct in the management of intermediate-to-advanced HCC, with its primary value lying in converting immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors. VG161, an oncolytic virus engineered to express IL-12, IL-15, and a PD-L1 blocking peptide, was evaluated in a phase I trial involving patients with HCC refractory to at least two prior lines of therapy. The study reported an ORR of 17.14%, a disease control rate of 60.00%, and a median OS of 9.40 months.126 A separate multicenter phase II trial, presented at the 2024 American Society of Clinical Oncology conference, assessed the safety and efficacy of the oncolytic immunotherapy RP2 in combination with atezolizumab and bevacizumab as a second-line treatment for advanced HCC, with ORR and PFS as primary endpoints.127 While oncolytic viruses offer a novel option for patients who have failed prior therapies, their modest response rates highlight the need for improved delivery strategies and enhanced immune activation.122 No oncolytic virus is currently approved globally for HCC; however, combining them with ICIs or other systemic agents may boost efficacy. Further large-scale trials are needed to validate these approaches.

While monotherapy with ICIs yields limited response rates, strategic combination approaches have demonstrated enhanced efficacy. Clinical evaluation of tremelimumab (anti–CTLA-4) combined with durvalumab (anti-PD-L1) has shown favorable oncological outcomes with manageable toxicity in HCC treatment.128 The rationale for combining CTLA-4 and PD-L1 inhibitors lies in their complementary mechanisms of action, which collectively enhance immune responses against tumor cells.105 The HIMALAYA study demonstrated that the STRIDE regimen significantly improved OS compared with sorafenib (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.78, 96% confidence interval [CI]: 0.65–0.92, p = 0.0035), while durvalumab monotherapy was noninferior (HR: 0.86, 96% CI: 0.73–1.03, p < 0.001).129 Longitudinal data showed sustained survival benefits with STRIDE, with 36-month OS rates of 30.7% versus 19.8% and 48-month OS rates of 25.2% versus 15.1%. Notably, 57.3% of STRIDE-treated survivors (n = 103) remained treatment-free during follow-up, and the safety profile remained consistent, with treatment-related adverse events reported in 17.5% of patients (68/388).130 These results, combining durable efficacy with acceptable toxicity, further reinforce the growing role of immunotherapy in HCC management.

Research into cancer epigenetics and its clinical applications has expanded rapidly in recent decades, with numerous studies establishing a causal link between epigenetic alterations and cancer initiation and progression.131,132 Epigenetic modulators represent a novel class of drugs that influence tumor development by altering gene expression. Given the complex tumor microenvironment and widespread epigenetic dysregulation of HCC,133 these agents may help create a more receptive cellular context for anti-tumor therapies to act effectively.134 Recent preclinical and clinical studies highlight the potential of combining epigenetic therapies with ICIs in HCC. For example, HDAC8 inhibitors have been shown to enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy.135 Likewise, HDAC6 inhibition has demonstrated promise in sensitizing advanced HCC to ICIs by reprogramming immune cells and upregulating PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, thereby boosting CD8+ T-cell activity and promoting a more favorable tumor microenvironment.136 Furthermore, the selective class I HDAC inhibitor CX101 has been found to improve the response to anti-PD-1 inhibitors in HCC with high HDAC1/2/3 expression.137 Despite their therapeutic promise, epigenetic drugs often pose safety concerns due to their broad, genome-wide effects, which may lead to off-target and non-tumor-specific consequences.138 A further challenge is the lack of robust, validated predictive biomarkers in HCC, particularly for assessing response to systemic therapies in advanced disease.139

While not yet part of routine clinical practice and not specific to BCLC-B stage HCC, these emerging therapeutic strategies offer valuable insights into managing intermediate-to-advanced HCC. Their ongoing clinical investigation reflects progress in the field and emphasizes the need for continued innovation. Future research should prioritize overcoming resistance mechanisms, optimizing combination regimens, and integrating biomarker-driven approaches to improve outcomes and advance personalized treatment strategies.

Downstaging strategies for intermediate-to-advanced HCC

Advances in surgical techniques and systemic therapies have increased interest in the potential of downstaging strategies for BCLC stage B HCC.

A retrospective cohort study across three Chinese centers showed that TACE combined with targeted therapy or with targeted-immunotherapy significantly improved DFS compared with TACE alone in patients with microvascular invasion. Median DFS was 16 months with TACE alone, versus 22 months and 21 months with the respective combinations (p = 0.027), highlighting the role of combination therapy in reducing recurrence.140 Similarly, the multicenter, randomized, double-blind phase 3 LEAP-012 trial demonstrated clinically meaningful improvements in PFS with TACE plus lenvatinib and pembrolizumab versus TACE plus placebo in patients with unresectable, non-metastatic HCC. Median PFS was 14.6 months (95% CI: 12.6–16.7) for the combination group, compared with 10.0 months (95% CI: 8.1–12.2) for placebo (HR: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.51–0.84, one-sided p = 0.0002).141 The EMERALD-1 study also reported a significant PFS benefit with durvalumab plus bevacizumab combined with TACE versus placebo plus TACE in patients with unresectable HCC.142 However, additional follow-up, OS data, and further analysis are needed to determine whether TACE combined with targeted or immunotherapy can be established as a new standard of care for this population.

TACE combined with camrelizumab and apatinib (“TACE + dual AI”) represents a highly promising strategy for intermediate-to-advanced HCC, leveraging synergistic local control and systemic therapy to significantly improve ORRs and survival outcomes.143,144 This regimen is suitable for BCLC B/C patients—particularly those with a high tumor burden, with portal vein tumor thrombosis, or considered potential candidates for conversion therapy. However, it requires close monitoring for adverse events, administration by experienced teams, and evidence-based, dynamic treatment adjustments.145,146 A propensity score-matched study reported significantly longer median PFS and OS with TACE plus immunotherapy and molecular targeted therapy compared with monotherapy. Median PFS was 13.5 months (95% CI: 9.0–18.0) versus 7.7 months (95% CI: 6.8–10.3, p = 0.003), and median OS was 24.1 months (95% CI: 20.0–NR) versus 15.7 months (95% CI: 13.2–22.7, p = 0.008). The combination group also achieved a significantly higher ORR (59.5% [50/84] vs. 37.4% [55/147], p = 0.002), indicating superior clinical efficacy.147 However, these survival benefits require validation in prospective clinical trials. Further research is needed to explore the synergistic enhancement of TACE when combined with systemic therapy in HCC.

We have incorporated data from the landmark TALENTACE trial—a 5-year international, multicenter phase III study led by Professor Jiahong Dong's team at Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital, Tsinghua University—which specifically addresses HCC with an intermediate-high tumor burden (exceeding the up-to-6 criteria) in China's hepatitis B virus (HBV)-associated population. This pivotal study demonstrates that combining atezolizumab plus bevacizumab (“T + A”) with on-demand TACE significantly outperforms TACE alone in patients with systemic treatment-naïve, unresectable HCC with an intermediate-high tumor burden. Key outcomes include superior TACE-specific PFS (median: 11.3 vs. 7.03 months), clinically meaningful improvement in RECIST v1.1-assessed PFS (median: 10.32 vs. 6.37 months), and higher ORRs (81.3% vs. 66.7% by RECICL, 49.1% vs. 33.9% by RECIST v1.1). These findings establish a new paradigm for conversion therapy and long-term survival in a population historically considered unresectable.148

Evidence suggests that patients with unresectable HCC may derive greater benefit from HAIC than from conventional TACE.149 A meta-analysis demonstrated superior OS with HAIC for both BCLC A-B patients (HR: 0.58, 95% CI: 0.43–0.78) and BCLC C patients (HR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.22–0.81). PFS was also improved (HR: 0.56, 95% CI: 0.45–0.70 for A-B; HR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.21–0.86 for C), along with higher partial response rates (RR: 3.23, 95% CI: 1.63–6.38 for A-B; RR: 2.51, 95% CI: 1.65–3.81 for C), lower progressive disease rates (RR: 0.38, 95% CI: 0.26–0.58 for A-B; RR: 0.60, 95% CI: 0.36–0.98 for C), and higher subsequent resection rates (RR: 2.15, 95% CI: 1.35–3.42) compared with TACE.150 These findings support a re-evaluation of HAIC as a treatment option for intermediate-to-advanced HCC, particularly with further validation needed across diverse liver disease etiologies and Western populations. The phase III FOHAIC-1 trial confirmed a median OS of 13.9 months for HAIC-FO versus 8.2 months for sorafenib (HR: 0.408, 95% CI: 0.301–0.552, p < 0.001). Tumor downstaging was achieved in 12.3% (16/130) of patients in the HAIC-FO group, with 15 undergoing curative resection or ablation and reaching a median OS of 20.8 months (1-year OS rate: 93.8%). HAIC-FO also demonstrated significantly longer OS than sorafenib in high-risk subgroups (10.8 vs. 5.7 months; HR: 0.343, 95% CI: 0.219–0.538, p < 0.001).151 However, the benefit of HAIC-FO remains uncertain in patients with extensive liver involvement due to etiologies such as alcohol-related liver disease or hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection. Head-to-head comparisons with established ICI combinations are needed, especially in patients contraindicated for atezolizumab–bevacizumab and those with high intrahepatic tumor burden.

Integrated antiviral and anticancer therapy for intermediate-to-advanced HCC with viral hepatitis

Antiviral therapy for viral hepatitis aims to improve survival and quality of life by preventing disease progression and reducing the risk of HCC development.152 Suboptimal antiviral use worsens the burden of HBV-related HCC: in Wu et al. study, 75% of patients were untreated prior to diagnosis, presenting with poorer laboratory profiles and significantly shorter median survival (2.1 vs. 4.4 years, p < 0.001).153 Nucleos(t)ide analog (NA) therapy significantly lowers HCC risk (HR: 0.37, p < 0.001),154 and both NAs for HBV and direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) for HCV provide substantial clinical and quality-of-life benefits.155

The VIRGIL study demonstrated that a sustained virologic response to entecavir significantly reduces the risks of hepatic decompensation, HCC, and death compared with partial responders,156 supported by retrospective studies showing reduced HCC incidence with entecavir use.157 While antiviral therapy may suppress hepatitis B x protein expression or inhibit HBV DNA integration into host chromosomes—mechanisms that mitigate malignant potential158—it does not fully eliminate the risk of HCC, even in HBeAg-negative or HBsAg-negative individuals.159, 160, 161, 162 Persistent NA therapy is recommended for patients with HCC regardless of cirrhosis status. LT is advised as a first-line option for select BCLC A/B patients meeting expanded criteria when resection is not feasible.156 Emerging molecular mechanisms in HBV-related HCC offer novel therapeutic targets. These include reduced SLFN11 expression, which may suppress tumor progression,163 and HBV-derived glycans that modulate immune responses via SIGLEC binding. In preclinical models, combining anti-SIGLEC-3 monoclonal antibody with GS-9620 activated host immunity, cleared HBsAg, and lowered the incidence of HCC in chronic HBV infection.164 Systemic therapies—including ICIs, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and monoclonal antibodies—have advanced personalized treatment and improved survival in patients with advanced HBV-related HCC compared with conventional therapies.165

A DAA-induced sustained virologic response in patients with HCV significantly reduces the risk of HCC, particularly within the first 3 years post-treatment.166 However, continued surveillance remains essential for younger patients with advanced liver disease, given the aggressive nature and recurrence risk of HCV-related HCC.167 Co-infection with HBV and HCV further accelerates liver-related complications.168 Although a direct causal link between HBV reactivation during DAA therapy and increased HCC risk is not firmly established, the high rate of reactivation warrants antiviral prophylaxis in co-infected patients.167,169

Despite advances in optimized disease management and preventive strategies, hepatitis viruses—particularly HBV, due to its high prevalence and complex pathogenesis—remain significant contributors to HCC development.167 These challenges are further compounded by strained healthcare resources during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Conclusion: reaching progress amidst controversy

In summary, the considerable heterogeneity within BCLC stage B HCC continues to drive ongoing debate around its classification and treatment strategies. This highlights the critical need for scientifically validated stratification systems to inform personalized therapeutic approaches. Notably, clinical practice is undergoing a paradigm shift in the management of BCLC B-stage HCC, with growing emphasis on integrating locoregional treatments—such as surgical resection, RFA, and TACE—with systemic modalities including targeted therapies and immunotherapy. This trend holds significant promise for improving outcomes. For patients who are not candidates for surgical or locoregional approaches, systemic therapies—comprising targeted agents and immunotherapeutics—are increasingly recognized as effective alternatives. Ultimately, managing BCLC B-stage HCC requires a comprehensive, multidisciplinary strategy that accounts for a broad range of clinical variables. As our understanding of the disease continues to advance, treatment protocols must be regularly updated, with tailored plans developed through collaborative decision-making based on each patient's unique clinical profile.
