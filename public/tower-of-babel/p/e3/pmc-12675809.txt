Artificial Intelligence Can Answer Postoperative Questions About Distal Radius Fractures—But Can Patients Understand the Answers?
Authors: Rae Tarapore, Suhasini Gupta, Kenneth R. Means, Aviram M. Giladi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to assess the validity, reliability, and readability of responses to common patient questions about postoperative from ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, and Google Gemini.MethodsTwenty-seven thoroughly vetted questions regarding distal radius fractures repair surgery were compiled and entered into ChatGPT 4, Gemini, and Copilot. The responses were analyzed for quality, accuracy, and readability using the DISCERN scale, the Journal of the American Medical Association benchmark criteria, Flesch-Kincaid Reading Ease Score, and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level. Citations provided by Google Gemini and Microsoft Copilot were further categorized by source of reference. Five questions were resubmitted, requesting response simplification. The responses were re-evaluated using the same metrics.ResultsAll three artificial intelligence platforms produced answers that were considered “good” quality (DISCERN scores >50). Copilot had the highest quality of information (68.3), followed by Gemini (62.9) and ChatGPT (52.9). The information provided by Copilot demonstrated the highest reliability, with a Journal of the American Medical Association benchmark criterion of 3 (of 4) compared with Gemini (1) and ChatGPT (0). All three platforms generated complex texts with Flesch-Kincaid Reading Ease Scores ranging between 35.8 and 41.4 and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level scores between 10.5 and 12.1, indicating a minimum of high-school graduate reading level required. After simplification, Gemini’s reading level remained unchanged, whereas ChatGPT improved to that of a seventh-grade reading level and Copilot improved to that of an eighth-grade reading level. Copilot had a higher number of references (74) compared with Gemini (36).ConclusionsAll three platforms provided safe and reliable answers to postoperative questions about distal radius fractures. High reading levels provided by AI remain the biggest barrier to patient accessibility.Clinical relevanceFor the current state of mainstream AI platforms, they are best suited as adjunct tools to support, rather than replace, clinical communication from health care workers.

Modern surgical health care often requires that providers attend to patient questions or concerns via telephone, audiovisual, or written text platforms. Many surgeons delegate these tasks to medical assistants, resident/fellow physicians, physician associates, or nurse practitioners. Regardless, such responses can be difficult to provide in a timely manner in the course of practice and thus often occur outside of regular business hours and even days later.1,2 This can be frustrating and exposes patients and providers to added risks, especially as the response burden continues to increase.3

There has been a recent focus on artificial intelligence (AI) and its potential roles in health care. Many groups have studied its ability to answer medical questions in test-taking settings and certain patient care scenarios.4, 5, 6 Artificial intelligence has already begun to enter medical practices, with applications that can generate clinic notes based on in-person conversations and examination interactions between patients and providers.7 A further potentially readily available use of AI would be to improve patients’ nonoffice visit query access and efficiency while lessening the provider response burden. This could be particularly helpful during the early postoperative period, when patient questions, concerns, and anxiety are particularly high and pressing.

Distal radius fractures (DRFs) are one of the most common injuries that patients present with to hand, orthopedic, and plastic surgery providers.8 Some groups have already begun to examine the ability of AI to answer DRF postoperative patient questions.6,9 Most studies have led the investigators to conclude that large language models hold promise and are not yet ready to be used in isolation as a substitute for clinicians.5,6,9 The majority of these studies focus on a single iteration of AI, most commonly ChatGPT, and have not focused specifically on commonly asked postoperative questions. Given the rapidly evolving nature of AI and the variety of options now available, it would be useful for physicians to possess updated data regarding the current ability of multiple platforms to answer such questions. Our purpose was to assess the validity, reliability, and readability of responses to common patient questions about postoperative DRFs from ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, and Google Gemini.

Materials and Methods

We compiled an initial list of the most frequently asked postoperative DRF questions. The list was initially drafted by a hand surgery resident and was then reviewed by a hand surgery fellow and a hand surgery physician associates. It was then vetted by two fellowship-trained hand surgeons before using our center’s patient/family advisory council (PFAC). The PFAC reviewed these questions to ensure that they were written in a manner that most closely reflects the language that patients typically use (Table 1). The PFAC members also suggested additional questions that patients would likely ask after DRF surgery. Two fellowship-trained attending hand surgeons, each holding the Subspecialty Certificate in Surgery of the Hand, then reviewed the questions again to approve the study’s ultimate list of 27 questions (Table 1). We then input the questions en masse into ChatGPT version 4.0, Microsoft Copilot (a free iteration that is available publicly), and Google Gemini in January of 2025. The DISCERN scale is used by other orthopedic investigators to establish the validity of AI models for various purposes.10,11 It consists of 16 queries that allow reviewers to systematically validate information provided by models. Reviewers give each query a score from 1 to 5, with one representing the poorest quality and five representing the highest quality. The maximum score is 80, and scores > 50 are labeled as “good,” whereas scores ≥ 63 are labeled as “excellent.”12 For our study, two reviewers completed DISCERN scale assessments of all AI responses to our DRF postoperative 27-question list. One reviewer was a postgraduate year 3 (PGY-3) hand surgery resident, and the other was a fourth-year medical student. Both reviewers independently scored each AI response. We determined the final score for each response as the average of the two reviewers’ scores.

The JAMA benchmark criteria are used to assess the informational reliability of health-related publications.5 The system has four parts, and the maximum total score is four points. In the first section, “authorship,” a reviewer identifies the person or group that produced the information. In the “disclosures” section, a reviewer assesses if information regarding who “owns” the information is available. In the “attribution” section, they judge if citations and references are appropriately provided. Finally, in the “currency” section, the reviewer determines whether dates for when the information was last reviewed or posted are provided.13

The FKRES and FKGL are both calculated using ratios of words per sentence and syllables per word in written text.14 The FKRES and FKGL have been used to assess the readability of AI responses.5 The FKRES has a maximum score of 100, with lower scores corresponding to more complex, less easily readable responses. The FKGL is meant to indicate the estimated minimum grade school reading level needed to understand a response. For reference, the American Medical Association (AMA) and National Institutes of Health both state that readability of general patient-facing educational materials should be at the sixth-grade reading level or lower.15

Microsoft Copilot and Google Gemini both provided internet-based references for their responses to our DRF postoperative questions. We recorded the source type for each of their references.5,16 ChatGPT did not provide references for its responses.

Poststudy additional analysis

Our FKRES and FKGL assessments revealed that all three AI platforms generated responses with insufficient ease of readability and excessively high reading grade levels for the general patient population. The overcomplexity of AI responses to patient queries has been well described, and the results for our study were similar.5 Therefore, we selected five of our 27 queries with longer responses and asked each platform to “please simplify” their initial response (Appendix S1, available on the Journal’s website at www.jhsgo.org). All simplified responses were re-scored for validity and readability.

Reliability scores

The JAMA benchmark criteria score for ChatGPT was 0 of 4, 3 of 4 for Copilot, and 1 of 4 for Gemini. Copilot only lacked information on the disclosure of ownership of the responses generated.

Readability scores

Across all 27 questions, the average FKRES scores for all three platforms corresponded to a “difficult” readability rating (Table 2). Based on the average FKGL scores, each platform’s responses required at least an 11th-grade reading level, with ChatGPT averaging 11th-grade and the other two averaging 12th-grade or higher (Table 2).

Discussion

All three AI platforms produced relatively high-quality responses to our list of 27 most-anticipated DRF postoperative patient questions. Microsoft Copilot performed best overall, followed by Google Gemini and then ChatGPT. Our reviewers regarded all responses as safe and appropriate. Although other authors have used quantitative statistical analyses to compare AI sources, our study is more descriptive and qualitative.5,17,18 We thought this was suitable, especially given the fact that our validity assessment tool, the DISCERN scale, involves subjective interpretations that can vary across reviewers. Consequently, any findings of statistical differences between AI platform evaluations could lead to an overvaluation of the importance of those differences.

Prior investigators used the same three platforms as we did to generate responses to questions about anterior cruciate ligament injury.5 They had similar validity results, with some of Copilot’s responses deemed as “excellent.” Regarding readability, other authors have also reported that the current routine responses for all three platforms are more complex and at a higher reading grade level than is typically recommended for patient-facing materials.5,15,18 Since the average United States patient reads at an eighth-grade level and the AMA recommends that health-related materials be written at a sixth-grade level or lower, the current complexity of AI-generated content is a key barrier to its broader adoption in health care for the purposes we studied.19 Our reliability findings align with previous research on the same AI platforms for other common orthopedic scenarios, with Copilot scoring best and ChatGPT scoring worst.5,20 Regarding the generation of informational references, although previous authors raised concerns for AI platforms “hallucinating” sources, we did not encounter any that were completely erroneous or fabricated. For our study, one citation was misrepresented, which is a notable improvement overall.

As clinicians contemplate how to initially employ AI in their practices for these types of roles, they will have to consider the risks and benefits appropriately. One option would be for AI to serve as an immediately available resource, with the understanding that a provider would review all responses within a certain time window. Even this limited approach would likely be a benefit to patients and lessen provider workload. As AI technology advances, it is likely that these programs will be able to tailor responses according to the individual preferences of each provider in both substance and delivery. The medico legal aspects of formally implementing AI as a first-response option for patients would also have to be addressed. One concern is that formal practice integration could expose clinicians to excessive risk so long as they are ultimately responsible for the AI platform’s responses. An important counterargument is that failing to address patient inquiries completely, in a timely manner, or at all, may pose a greater relative risk to patients. Furthermore, patients are increasingly likely to use AI on their own, regardless of provider recommendations; having a monitored AI system would feature more oversight and therefore safety. These are just a few of the key considerations as we contemplate the future of AI in health care.

Our study has several limitations. The world of AI is rapidly evolving in real time. Consequently, the exact results of our AI queries are likely already dated. We also only included three of the most mainstream AI sources. Our list of potential DRF postoperative patient questions could also be incomplete or inaccurate and would benefit from further real-world evaluations. Our exploratory trial of asking each platform to simplify responses to a handful of our complete list of questions was limited and yielded mixed results, requiring further study.

We found that the quality and accuracy of the AI-generated information for our study were acceptable, and instead, the major challenge was achieving an optimal balance between readability and providing appropriately detailed answers for a broad patient population. This leads us to suggest that, presently, the best use for these platforms may be as supplemental informational tools, improving the response times and access that patients seek while enhancing provider efficiency and reducing their burden.2,21 We conclude that for the current state of mainstream AI platforms, they are best suited as adjunct tools to support, rather than replace, clinical communication from health care workers.
