Introduction

Sarcomatoid squamous cell carcinoma (SSCC), also referred to as spindle cell carcinoma, is a rare and aggressive variant of squamous cell carcinoma characterized by a biphasic proliferation of epithelial and sarcomatoid components. While SSCC most commonly arises in the upper aerodigestive tract, primary involvement of the mandible remains exceptionally uncommon, with only a limited number of cases reported [1].

From a radiologic perspective, mandibular SSCC typically presents as an ill-defined osteolytic lesion with cortical destruction and soft-tissue extension, features that are highly suggestive of malignancy but largely non-specific. As a result, imaging findings may overlap with a wide spectrum of aggressive odontogenic tumors and primary bone malignancies, posing significant diagnostic challenges [2].

In this context, radiologic–pathologic correlation plays a pivotal role in establishing the diagnosis and avoiding potential misinterpretation. Highlighting the imaging characteristics and associated diagnostic pitfalls of this rare entity is therefore essential for improving diagnostic accuracy [3].

We report a rare case of mandibular SSCC with emphasis on its imaging features, differential diagnosis, and radiologic–pathologic correlation.

Case presentation

A 60-year-old woman with no significant past medical history presented with a two-week history of progressively increasing swelling and pain in the right mandibular region, associated with difficulty in mastication. No history of trauma, dental procedures, fever, or constitutional symptoms was reported.

Clinical examination revealed facial asymmetry due to a firm, tender swelling involving the right mandibular body, extending to the submandibular and jugal regions. Intraoral inspection demonstrated a large exophytic, ulcerated, and friable mass arising from the right mandibular alveolar ridge, measuring approximately 5 cm and extending from the premolar to the second molar region (Fig. 1). The lesion was associated with severe periodontal destruction, tooth mobility, and spontaneous bleeding.

Clinical photograph showing a large exophytic intraoral mass involving the right posterior mandibular alveolar ridge. The lesion appears ulcerated with irregular necrotic and hemorrhagic areas and is associated with marked dental destruction, tooth displacement, and gingival infiltration.

Computed tomography (CT) of the facial bones was performed for further evaluation. CT imaging revealed an ill-defined, aggressive osteolytic lesion cantered in the right mandibular body, measuring approximately 60 × 50 mm. The lesion demonstrated extensive destruction of both buccal and lingual cortices with areas of cortical breakthrough (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4). The internal architecture was heterogeneous, with multiloculated hypodense areas and irregular enhancing soft-tissue components following contrast administration. The lesion extended into adjacent spaces, including the masseteric and sublingual regions, with displacement of surrounding soft tissues but no clear evidence of muscle invasion. No periosteal reaction or matrix mineralization was identified. Multiple small (<1 cm) cervical lymph nodes were noted, without radiologic features suggestive of pathological involvement.

Axial computed tomography (CT) scan (bone window) demonstrating an ill-defined aggressive osteolytic lesion centered in the right mandibular body (white arrows). There is extensive destruction of both buccal and lingual cortices with cortical breakthrough and irregular bone erosion.

Coronal CT reconstruction (bone window) showing extensive osteolysis of the right mandibular body (white arrows), with cortical disruption and extension into adjacent soft tissues.

Three-dimensional volume-rendered CT reconstruction illustrating a large destructive defect involving the right mandibular body and angle (white arrows), with marked distortion of mandibular architecture and displacement of adjacent teeth.

Laboratory investigations showed mild inflammatory markers elevation, with leukocytosis (12,500/mm3) and elevated C-reactive protein (42 mg/L).

An incisional biopsy was performed. Histopathological examination demonstrated a biphasic malignant neoplasm composed of conventional squamous epithelial carcinoma and a spindle-cell sarcomatoid component. Immunohistochemical staining revealed strong cytoplasmic positivity for cytokeratin AE1/AE3 and vimentin, confirming the diagnosis of sarcomatoid squamous cell carcinoma (Fig. 5). No histopathological evidence of lymph node metastasis was identified.

Immunohistochemical staining demonstrating strong cytoplasmic positivity of tumor cells for cytokeratin AE1/AE3 (brown staining, black arrowheads), confirming epithelial differentiation and supporting the diagnosis of sarcomatoid squamous cell carcinoma.

Following multidisciplinary tumor board discussion, the tumor was considered locally advanced and not amenable to primary surgical resection. The patient was treated with concurrent chemoradiotherapy, combining external beam radiotherapy with platinum-based chemotherapy. Early follow-up showed partial clinical regression of the lesion and improvement of symptoms.

Discussion

Sarcomatoid squamous cell carcinoma (SSCC) is a rare and highly aggressive variant of squamous cell carcinoma characterized by a biphasic epithelial and sarcomatoid differentiation. Although it predominantly arises in the upper aerodigestive tract, primary involvement of the mandible remains exceptionally uncommon, with only a limited number of cases reported. This rarity, combined with non-specific imaging features, makes diagnosis particularly challenging and prone to misinterpretation [4].

From a radiologic perspective, SSCC of the mandible typically presents as an ill-defined osteolytic lesion with aggressive bone destruction, cortical breakthrough, and associated soft-tissue mass. In the present case, CT demonstrated extensive cortical destruction involving both buccal and lingual cortices, with heterogeneous enhancement and extension into adjacent spaces, reflecting a high-grade malignant process. Similar CT findings have been described in the literature, particularly in cases involving primary bone or mandibular localization, where lesions appear poorly marginated and infiltrative [5]. Unlike some primary bone malignancies, no periosteal reaction or matrix mineralization is usually identified, which may help narrow the differential diagnosis.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), although not performed in this case, may provide additional information regarding marrow infiltration, soft-tissue extension, and perineural spread. Reported MRI findings of SSCC include heterogeneous signal intensity with T2 hyperintensity and variable contrast enhancement, reflecting tumor heterogeneity, necrosis, and mixed cellular composition. MRI can therefore be valuable in preoperative assessment and local staging of mandibular tumors.

A key challenge highlighted by this case is the broad radiologic differential diagnosis of aggressive osteolytic mandibular lesions. Benign odontogenic tumors such as ameloblastoma may present with multilocular radiolucency; however, they typically demonstrate well-defined margins and slow expansile remodeling rather than aggressive cortical destruction. Odontogenic keratocysts also tend to have well-circumscribed borders and limited soft-tissue extension [6]. In contrast, malignant lesions such as osteosarcoma may show aggressive bone destruction but are often associated with periosteal reaction and osteoid matrix mineralization, features that were absent in our case. Other malignant entities, including fibrosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, and metastatic disease, may also present with osteolytic patterns, further complicating the radiologic assessment.

These overlapping imaging features constitute a major diagnostic pitfall, as SSCC may be misinterpreted as a primary bone sarcoma or an aggressive odontogenic lesion. Therefore, recognition of a rapidly progressive, ill-defined osteolytic lesion with extensive cortical destruction and soft-tissue extension, in the absence of matrix mineralization, should raise suspicion for an aggressive epithelial malignancy, including sarcomatoid squamous cell carcinoma.

Histopathological examination remains the gold standard for diagnosis. The tumor typically demonstrates a biphasic architecture combining malignant squamous epithelial cells and spindle-shaped sarcomatoid elements. Immunohistochemistry is essential to confirm epithelial differentiation of the spindle component, with co-expression of cytokeratin and vimentin supporting the diagnosis and helping distinguish SSCC from true mesenchymal sarcomas [7].

Radiologic–pathologic correlation is crucial for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management. In this case, the extensive osteolysis and soft-tissue extension observed on CT closely correlated with the infiltrative growth pattern and biphasic histological composition. Such correlation helps avoid diagnostic errors and guides biopsy targeting as well as therapeutic planning [8].

Management of SSCC remains challenging due to its aggressive behavior and high risk of local recurrence. Surgical resection with wide margins is generally considered the treatment of choice when feasible. However, in advanced cases with extensive local invasion, as observed in our patient, combined chemoradiotherapy may represent an appropriate alternative.

The prognosis of SSCC is generally poor compared to conventional squamous cell carcinoma, particularly in advanced stages. Prognostic factors include tumor size, local invasion, margin status, and nodal involvement. Early recognition based on imaging findings and prompt multidisciplinary management are therefore essential to improve outcomes. Given the rarity of mandibular SSCC, reporting such cases remains important to better define its radiologic characteristics and avoid potential diagnostic pitfalls [9].

Conclusion

Sarcomatoid squamous cell carcinoma of the mandible is a rare and aggressive malignancy that may present as a non-specific osteolytic lesion on imaging, mimicking both odontogenic and primary bone tumors. This case highlights key diagnostic pitfalls related to its overlapping radiologic features. Recognition of an ill-defined destructive lesion with extensive cortical involvement and soft-tissue extension, in the absence of matrix mineralization, should raise suspicion for an aggressive epithelial malignancy. Accurate diagnosis relies on radiologic–pathologic correlation, which is essential to guide appropriate management and avoid misinterpretation.
