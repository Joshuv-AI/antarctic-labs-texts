Serum 25(OH)D and HDL-C as site-specific metabolic markers of cervical lymph node metastasis in papillary thyroid carcinoma
Authors: Shanyu Yin, Nannan Lv, Guo Tian, Jianjian Xiang, Danlei Lu, Tian’an Jiang, Ying Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreoperative risk stratification for cervical lymph node metastasis (LNM) is challenging in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), metabolic disturbances may contribute to tumor progression and metastasis. This large retrospective cohort study investigated clinical and metabolic profiles to identify independent metabolic correlates associated with cervical LNM, which may complement preoperative risk assessment.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis of 1,003 PTC patients was performed. Preoperative variables (demographics, clinicopathological features, serum biochemical markers) were profiled; univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were utilized to identify independent metabolic factors associated with central (CLNM) and lateral LNM (LLNM).ResultsAmong 1,003 patients, 35.19% and 3.79% presented with CLNM and LLNM, respectively. The LLNM subgroup was small (n = 38), which limited statistical power and generalizability of the corresponding findings. Multivariable analysis identified HDL-C (OR = 0.63, P = 0.040) as a factor independently associated with CLNM, and 25(OH)D (OR = 0.97, P = 0.012) with LLNM. Patients with 25(OH)D deficiency (< 50 nmol/L) had a higher LLNM incidence than those with sufficient levels (5.07% vs. 1.95%, P < 0.05). Tumor size remained a robust risk factor for both compartments, whereas BRAF V600E mutation showed no significant independent association with metastasis in this dataset (P > 0.05).ConclusionOur findings suggest that preoperative serum 25(OH)D and HDL-C are associated with site-specific LNM in PTC. These markers may complement preoperative risk stratification, although current evidence remains inconsistent. Given the retrospective, single-center design and small subgroup sizes, our results are preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Background

In recent years, the global incidence of thyroid cancer has escalated markedly, accounting for nearly 10% of all newly diagnosed malignancies and drawing substantial attention in public health and clinical practice [1–5]. Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) constitutes the most prevalent subtype of differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC), accounting for approximately 80–90% of all thyroid malignancies [6]. A prominent clinical feature of PTC is its high propensity for cervical lymph node metastasis (LNM), with central lymph node metastasis (CLNM) occurring in 40%–70% of cases and lateral lymph node metastasis (LLNM) in a smaller proportion [7–9]. Notably, CLNM is widely recognized as a key predictor of poor prognosis in PTC patients, while LLNM, though less common, is strongly associated with an elevated risk of disease progression and distant metastasis [10–12]. Despite the clinical significance of LNM, standardized diagnostic methods and authoritative clinical guidelines for the preoperative identification of CLNM and LLNM in PTC remain lacking, posing a major challenge for individualized clinical decision-making [13, 14].

Currently, the preoperative assessment of cervical LNM relies heavily on high-frequency neck ultrasound, ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA), and FNA-thyroglobulin (Tg) detection, with enhanced computed tomography (CT) serving as a supplementary tool for evaluating lateral and mediastinal involvement [15–17]. However, these conventional modalities are fraught with inherent limitations: ultrasound exhibits suboptimal sensitivity for micrometastases and deep-seated nodes; FNA is susceptible to sampling bias; and enhanced CT often yields false-positive results due to inflammatory lymphadenopathy [18, 19]. Consequently, occult micrometastases are frequently encountered intraoperatively, leading current clinical guidelines to often rely on primary tumor characteristics—such as size and extrathyroidal extension—as proxies for recommending prophylactic central neck dissection [20, 21]. This underscores an urgent unmet need for reliable, non-invasive biomarkers to complement existing tools and refine preoperative LNM risk stratification.

Accumulating evidence has highlighted that tumor growth and metastatic progression are not only driven by genetic mutations but also closely associated with metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells [22–24]. Metabolic dysregulation modulates the tumor microenvironment by regulating angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and immune cell infiltration, thereby promoting the invasive and metastatic potential of tumor cells [25, 26]. While various biochemical parameters—including those involved in micronutrient homeostasis and lipid metabolism—have been implicated in tumor biology, their specific independent predictive value for distinct cervical LNM patterns (CLNM vs. LLNM) has not been fully elucidated, particularly within large-scale clinical cohorts.

To bridge this research gap, we conducted a large-scale retrospective cohort study to systematically explore the association between multidimensional preoperative markers and LNM status in PTC patients. By identifying independent metabolic markers associated with nodal involvement, this study seeks to provide preliminary clinical evidence that could potentially inform preoperative risk stratification and complement individualized preoperative evaluation.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a retrospective cohort study of consecutive patients who undergo thyroidectomy with neck lymph node dissection for PTC.

Study population

Between October 2023 and January 2025, patients who underwent surgical resection for PTC at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine were retrospectively enrolled. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Inclusion criteria

Patients were included if they met all of the following conditions: (1) Histologically confirmed diagnosis of papillary thyroid carcinoma; (2) Underwent curative-intent surgery with formal central and/or lateral neck lymph node dissection; (3) Had definitive postoperative histopathological documentation of lymph node status (positive or negative for metastasis); (4) Possessed complete preoperative laboratory data—including 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], lipid profiles, and parathyroid hormone levels—obtained within one month prior to surgery; (5) Had available complete clinical and follow-up records. These criteria ensure diagnostic accuracy, data availability, and result reliability for the study population.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded for any of the following reasons: (1) History of any malignancy prior to the PTC diagnosis; (2) Presence of concurrent thyroid disorders that could significantly confound metabolic parameters (e.g., active Graves’ disease, subacute thyroiditis); (3) Received preoperative treatments that could alter thyroid function or tumor biology (e.g., levothyroxine suppression therapy, targeted therapy, or neck irradiation); (4) Used medications known to affect 25(OH)D or lipid metabolism (e.g.,, high-dose vitamin D supplements, statins) within three months before surgery; (5) Had incomplete or missing key data required for analysis (such as incomplete surgical pathology reports or undetermined BRAF V600E mutation status). These exclusions effectively control for confounding factors and enhance the study’s internal validity.

Data collection

Throughout the study, patient information was collected, including demographic and anthropometric data (age, sex, height, weight, body mass index [BMI]); hematological and biochemical parameters (triiodothyronine [T3], thyroxine [T4], thyroid-stimulating hormone [TSH], free triiodothyronine [FT3], free thyroxine [FT4], thyroid peroxidase antibody [TPOAb], thyroglobulin antibody [TgAb], parathyroid hormone [PTH], triglycerides [TG], total cholesterol [TC], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C], direct low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [DLDL-C], calcium [Ca], phosphorus [P], 25(OH)D, homocysteine [HCY]); These tests were part of our institution’s routine preoperative assessment; and tumor-related characteristics (maximum tumor size, BRAF mutation status, tumor number, lymph node metastasis [central and lateral], pathological tumor-node-metastasis [pTNM] stage).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) for continuous variables, depending on the normality of the data distribution, and as frequencies and percentages (n, %) for categorical variables. For univariate group comparisons, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed to assess differences in continuous variables, while the Chi-square (χ²) test was applied for categorical variables. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analyses were subsequently incorporated into multivariable logistic regression models to identify independent predictors for CLNM and LLNM. To ensure the robustness of the multivariable analysis, multicollinearity among the candidate independent variables—including both continuous and categorical parameters—was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a VIF < 5 indicating the absence of significant collinearity. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). All statistical analyses were two-tailed, and a P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), R (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and MATLAB (version R2021b; MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA).

Results

Basic clinical features

This study is a retrospective cohort study that initially collected clinical data from 1,483 patients. According to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 317 patients who did not meet the study criteria were excluded. Subsequently, to ensure dataset completeness and reliability, an additional 163 patients with missing or incomplete clinical information were excluded. Ultimately, 1,003 patients with complete data were included in the statistical analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of screened patients

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the 1,003 enrolled PTC patients. Among them, 747 (74.48%) were female, and 256 (25.52%) were male. Postoperative pathological results confirmed 353 patients (35.19%) with CLNM and 38 patients (3.79%) with LLNM. The cohort had a mean age of approximately 45 years, a mean BMI of 24.00 kg/m², and a mean maximum tumor diameter of 0.77 cm.

Group differences in continuous variables by CLNM/LLNM

Continuous variables were compared between CLNM/LLNM groups and non-metastatic group using the Mann–Whitney U test, with results summarized as median (interquartile range, IQR) and presented in Table 2. Compared with non-metastatic patients, the CLNM group exhibited significant differences in height (P = 0.040), age (P < 0.001), maximum tumor size (P < 0.001), PTH (P = 0.029), and 25(OH)D levels (P = 0.014). For the LLNM group, statistically significant differences were observed in maximum tumor size (P < 0.001), PTH (P = 0.024), 25(OH)D (P = 0.004), and DLDL-C levels (P = 0.035) relative to non-metastatic patients. Notably, both CLNM and LLNM patients had larger maximum tumor size and lower 25(OH)D levels compared with non-metastatic patients (all P < 0.05). Multivariable logistic regression analysis

To identify independent predictors of CLNM and LLNM, variables with P < 0.10 in the above univariate analyses (Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, χ² test for categorical variables) were included in separate multivariable logistic regression models, which is a widely accepted strategy to minimize the risk of excluding potential predictors. Results are presented as OR with 95% CI, and a two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Table 4 presents the results for CLNM. Age (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.97, P < 0.001) and HDL-C (OR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.40–0.98, P = 0.040) were identified as factors associated with reduced odds of CLNM (all P < 0.05). In contrast, maximum tumor size (OR = 3.38, 95% CI: 2.27–5.03, P < 0.001), tumor multiplicity (OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.03–2.02, P = 0.031), and microscopic capsular invasion (OR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.20–2.31, P = 0.002) were confirmed as independent risk factors for CLNM (all P < 0.05). No other variables reached statistical significance in the CLNM model (all P > 0.05).

Discussion

PTC is characterized by a high propensity for cervical LNM, a key determinant of disease progression and prognosis [13, 14]. CLNM and LLNM carry distinct clinical implications, with CLNM being more common and LLNM indicating a higher risk of disease progression [27]. Preoperative prediction of LNM remains challenging, as conventional imaging has limitations in detecting occult metastases [28]. In this large retrospective cohort study, we identified preoperative serum 25(OH)D and HDL-C as metabolic markers associated with site-specific lymph node metastasis in PTC. Notably, 25(OH)D deficiency was associated with an increased prevalence of lateral lymph node metastasis in this dataset. While these findings, particularly regarding lateral metastasis, are limited by subgroup size and require prospective validation, they highlight the potential adjunctive value of these readily available metabolic markers in refining preoperative risk stratification.

Consistent with prior evidence [29], our data confirm that the maximum tumor size is the strongest predictor of both CLNM and LLNM, with a particularly pronounced effect on LLNM (OR = 5.16 vs. 3.38). This reaffirms tumor size as a cornerstone of preoperative risk stratification for PTC.

For the central compartment, HDL-C was independently associated with reduced odds of CLNM (OR = 0.63, P = 0.040), complementing established factors such as tumor multiplicity and microscopic capsular invasion. While the exact mechanisms warrant further investigation, this association may have been linked to the anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties of HDL-C [30], supporting its potential as an adjunctive biomarker for refining preoperative risk stratification.

In contrast to central metastasis, lateral compartment involvement was independently associated with lower 25(OH)D levels (OR = 0.97, P = 0.012). Patients with 25(OH)D deficiency (< 50 nmol/L) had a more than twofold higher LLNM prevalence (5.07% vs. 1.95%, P = 0.011). However, given the small LLNM subgroup (n = 38), these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require external validation. Biologically, 25(OH)D may have influenced metastasis via VDR-mediated immune regulation and EMT inhibition, although this remains speculative. The literature on 25(OH)D and LNM is inconsistent [31–33]; These discrepancies may reflect differences in geographic latitude, seasonality, and 25(OH)D cutoff definitions. Importantly, these inconsistencies may also arise from combining central and lateral nodal metastases as a single endpoint, whereas our data suggest a site-specific association. Our results align with studies supporting an inverse relationship [34], but further mechanistic validation is required.

These findings suggest that preoperative HDL-C and 25(OH)D provide a novel perspective for refining PTC risk stratification. For instance, low HDL-C might warrant more thorough central compartment evaluation, while 25(OH)D deficiency could serve as an indicator of higher risk of LLNM. While these markers should not currently guide routine neck dissection decisions, they provide a rationale for future interventional studies to determine whether metabolic optimization could influence metastatic outcomes. Identifying non-informative markers also helps establish a more parsimonious predictive model for LNM [35]. Despite conflicting prior reports [36, 37], thyroid autoantibodies (TPOAb/TgAb) showed no independent association with either CLNM or LLNM in multivariable analysis. Although BRAF V600E contributes to PTC tumorigenesis [38], it was not an independent predictor of LNM in our adjusted models, suggesting its effects may be largely mediated by tumor size and other aggressive features. Similarly, with the exception of HDL-C, other lipid markers lacked independent predictive value, further supporting a selective approach to biomarker integration in risk stratification.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design limits the generalizability of the results. Second, the small LLNM subgroup (n = 38) reduces statistical power; these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require external multi-center validation. While we performed both continuous and categorical analyses to ensure transparency, this consistency did not mitigate the inherent sample size limitation. Third, single-time-point measurements cannot reflect longitudinal changes in metabolic status, which may fluctuate over time [39]. Fourth, data on potential confounders, including 25(OH)D supplementation, statin use, diet, and physical activity, were unavailable. Fifth, the observational design precludes causal inference. Sixth, Although most patients underwent these tests as part of our institutional routine, they are not standard preoperative tests in all centers, and data were not available for every patient, which may introduce selection bias. Finally, the absence of mechanistic studies limits our understanding of the underlying molecular pathways.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study indicates that preoperative 25(OH)D and HDL-C are independently associated with site-specific lymph node metastasis in PTC. Despite inconsistent reports in the literature, these accessible metabolic markers -especially 25(OH)D in relation to LLNM—may serve as adjuncts to conventional preoperative risk stratification. Given the retrospective design and relatively small subgroup size for lateral nodal disease, these hypothesis-generating findings remain preliminary and require confirmation in well-designed prospective validation and multi-center studies. Overall, these results underscore the potential role of metabolic profiling in stratifying risk for patients with PTC.
