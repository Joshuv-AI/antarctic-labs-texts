Alveolar bone changes and root resorption in adult patients undergoing mandibular molar distalization with clear aligners: a retrospective CBCT study
Authors: Ebrahim Ebadi, Pengyu Chen, Zhongyi Xiao, Yi Li, Almustafa Osama, Yukun Gou, Xi Chen, Yunshan Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis retrospective study aims to determine the changes in alveolar bone height, thickness, and root resorption after mandibular molar distalization (MMD) using clear aligner therapy (CAT).MethodsA total of 92 distalized mandibular molars, corresponding to twenty-three adult patients (15 females and 8 males), were included. Conebeam computed tomography (CBCT) images were evaluated for bone height, bone thickness, and root length. Paired-sample t-test and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test were used to compare the changes before and after molar distalization. Bivariate Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationship between age and treatment duration. Multiple linear regression (MLR) was applied to predict the alveolar bone loss and root resorption based on age and duration of treatment.ResultsSignificant alveolar bone height reduction was observed on the lingual side of the left second molar (L-2nd M) and the buccal side of the right first molar (R-1st M) (L-2nd M = 1.29 ± 1.14, 1.93 ± 1.22, P < 0.001; R-1stM = 1.80 ± 0.74 2.69 ± 1.36;, P < 0.001). Furthermore, root length reduction was significantly different in the L-2nd M (3.61 ± 1.34, 4.49 ± 1.54, P < 0.001) and the R-2nd M (3.06 ± 0.97, 4.10 ± 1.61, P < 0.001). However, bone thickness showed site-specific changes. Pearson correlation analysis revealed no significant correlation between the age and treatment duration across all the distalized molars. MLR demonstrated that age and treatment duration are weak predictors of post-treatment changes.Conclusionsignificant alveolar bone height reduction and thickness changes were observed in mandibular molars after distalization with clear aligners, alongside weak associations with age and treatment duration. These findings enhance the understanding the changes that occur in periodontal tissues after MMD using CAT. Furthermore, these findings highlight the need for further research for better understand of these changes and improve of predictive factors.Keywords: Clear aligners, Molar distalization, Mandibular molars, Alveolar bone thickness, Root resorption, Orthodontic treatment, CBCT, Adult orthodontics

Background

Clear aligner therapy (CAT) has gained popularity as an alternative treatment modality to the traditional fixed appliance therapy (FAT), offering enhanced comfort, fewer office visits, improved aesthetics, and better oral hygiene maintenance [1, 2]. CAT was originally developed to address mild to moderate dental crowding or minor spacing issues [3]. Recent advancements have expanded its application to include more complex orthodontic treatments, driven by improved control over tooth movement [4, 5]. Mandibular molar distalization (MMD) is a technique used in non-extraction therapy to create space by repositioning mandibular molars distally [6–8]. MMD is a challenging orthodontic movement due to the dense mandibular bone and the complex morphology of the lower molars compared with their maxillary counterparts [9]. Despite these difficulties, studies demonstrate CAT’s effectiveness in achieving MMD. For instance, Dongxue Wu et al. [10] and Jie Han et al. [11] both reported that clear aligners can successfully distalize the mandibular molars, primarily through tipping movements.

Orthodontic forces applied to correct malocclusions influence the alveolar bone remodeling, and understanding these physiological changes is essential to prevent periodontal complications such as dehiscence and fenestration [12, 13]. Another concern is root resorption, a multifaceted side effect of orthodontic treatment, that varies in occurrence and undermines the structural stability of the root and crown by affecting the dentin and cementum [14, 15]. While typically localized, root resorption can lead to irreversible damage under certain conditions [16].

Factors contributing to root resorption and alveolar bone loss include treatment duration, force magnitude, extent of tooth movement, and appliance type [17, 18]. Notably, clear aligners may offer advantages over fixed appliances in mitigating these risks. Studies report a lower prevalence of root resorption with CAT (56.30%) compared to FAT (82.11%) [19]. Similarly, Naseem Ali Al-Worafi et al. [20] found that CAT reduced the incidence of alveolar bone loss and root resorption in Class II malocclusion treatments. Furthermore, Leena Ali Al-Warafi et al. [21] found that buccal alveolar bone changes are the most affected surfaces following maxillary and mandibular molars’ intrusion and extrusion movements using clear aligners, with mandibular molars being more affected than the maxillary ones.

These findings suggest CAT’s potential for safer periodontal outcomes, yet specific data on MMD’s impact are scarce. While prior research has focused on MMD’s efficacy [10, 11, 22, 23], little attention has been paid to its effects on periodontal health, particularly alveolar bone levels and root resorption. Ibtehal Almagrami et al. [24] compared the alveolar bone changes, and prevalence and severity of root resorption of maxillary incisors. Moreover, Silvia Izabella Pop et al. [25] also evaluated the height of the alveolar bone on the four surfaces of specific teeth and the root length of the central incisors before and after orthodontic treatment. However, to the authors’ knowledge, no studies have evaluated alveolar bone changes and root resorption in mandibular first and second molars following sequential MMD using CAT.

CBCT offers a robust solution for addressing this gap. Unlike 2D cephalometric imaging, CBCT provides precise, lower radiation dose exposure than conventional computed tomography, and a magnification-free quantitative data, enabling detailed assessments of bone height, thickness, and root length, as well as volumetric and contour changes [26, 27]. CBCT has emerged as an advanced imaging technique for diagnosing and evaluating multiple craniofacial and orthodontic conditions [28, 29]. Studies have explored its applications, including monitoring alveolar bone changes and root resorption post-orthodontic treatment, performing cephalometric assessments, and analyzing the upper airway in patients with sleep-disordered breathing.

Although many studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of CAT in MMD, its effects on alveolar bone changes and root resorption remain largely unexplored. This study utilized pre- and post-treatment CBCT scans to evaluate changes in alveolar bone height, bone thickness, and root length of mandibular first and second molars in adults undergoing sequential MMD with CAT. Additionally, it examined the influence of age and treatment duration on these changes. By addressing this understudied area, this research aims to enhance the understanding of the periodontal safety of CAT in complex orthodontic treatments.

Methods

Ethics statement and study design

This retrospective clinical study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Department of Stomatology, First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (XJTU1AF2022LSK-186). Written informed consents were obtained from all participants prior to the data collection. All procedures strictly adhered to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample size calculation

We conducted a power analysis using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Franz Faul University, Kiel, Germany) to calculate the minimum required sample size, drawing from previous study [30]. A minimum of 23 participants was necessary to achieve a 95% power level with a significance level of 5% (α = 0.05) and an effect size of 0.9.

Participant selection

CBCT images from 23 adult patients [15 females (mean age 26.3 ± 8.9 years) and 8 males (mean age 22.2 ± 5.7 years)] who attended the Department of Stomatology at First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University from 2017 to January 2025 were enrolled.

Collection of patient treatment data and ClinCheck design and production

All the patients received CAT using Invisalign aligners (Align Technology, San Jose, California, USA). Before and after treatment (T0 and T1), CBCT scans were taken, and routine initial data were collected. Based on the patient’s oral condition, it was determined whether scaling was needed. If mandibular third molars were present, they were uniformly extracted before the CAT started. Silicone rubber impressions and bite registrations were then taken, and the patient’s clinical examination data and treatment plan were submitted to the company to generate a 3D animation plan. In this plan, MMD was performed using the “V-Pattern” mode, and no intrusion movement was designed for the mandibular molars. After the plan was returned, the clinician modified the treatment plan using the 3D module of ClinCheck software until the final reasonable treatment target position was reached, and then submitted it for production.

Aligner wearing process and data collection

The duration of mandibular molar distalization for all included cases was between 4 and 8 months (mean: 5.9months). The aligner wearing requirements for each patient were standardized: wearing time was more than 22 h per day, a new aligner was changed every 14 days, and chewing gum was used as needed . Patients were required to return for follow-up every two months. During follow-ups, intraoral photographs were taken (Fig. 1), and aligner wearing compliance was assessed. The progress of tooth movement and the status of all intraoral attachments were checked, and repairs were made promptly if needed. The time point when the patient’s mandibular first molar had completed distalization was defined as T1. CBCT scans was taken when the patient had just finished wearing the aligner corresponding to this stage.

CBCT imaging and 3D reconstruction

All patients underwent CBCT imaging using the same machine, KaVo 3D imaging system (KaVo GmbH, Germany) at the same location, performed by the same professional radiologist. All patients were scanned using a standard protocol: field of view (FOV) was 23.0 × 17.0 cm, the setting of exposure parameter was 37.10 mAs; 17.8 s; 120 kV, and image acquisition at 0.3 mm voxel size. Moreover, head position was standardized with the Frankfort horizontal plane (FHP) parallel to the floor and maximum occlusal intercuspation. According to the imaging protocol, the patients were instructed to avoid swallowing or movingthroughout the scanning process. Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) files from T0 and T1 stages were saved and imported into Dolphin imaging software (Version 11.9, Dolphin Imaging, Chatsworth, CA, USA, https://www.dolphinimaging.com/) for further analysis.

Three-dimensional measurements

Coronal slices from each CBCT image were used to assess alveolar bone height, bone thickness, and root resorption of the mandibular first and second molars. h1 and h2 represent the buccal and lingual vertical distances, respectively from the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to the alveolar bone crest, and “r” indicates the root length measured from the midpoint of the CEJ to the root apex (Fig. 2A). Three reference lines were drawn perpendicular to the tooth’s long axis: Line A was positioned 1 mm apical to the alveolar crest, Line B drown at the midpoint between the CEJ and root apex, and Line C positioned tangent to the root apex (Fig. 2B). Alveolar bone thickness was measured in millimeters. The average thickness of buccal and lingual bone was calculated across the three levels (Fig. 2C). Differences in alveolar bone height, thickness, and root resorption were then compared between pre- and post-treatment. All measurements were performed by 2 blinded experts (C.P. & X.Z.).

Statistical analysis

SPSS software (Version 26; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics, including each variable’s mean and standard deviation (SD), were calculated and presented as such. Data were checked for normal distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Subsequently, Paired-sample t-test and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test were used to compare changes in alveolar bone height, thickness, and root length of the mandibular first and second molars between T0 and T1 ( significance level set at < 0.05). Pearson bivariate correlation test was used to examine the correlation between age, duration of treatment, and alveolar bone and root changes. Multiple linear regression (MLR) analysis was performed to predict the alveolar bone loss and root resorption of the mandibular molars based on the age and duration of treatment as independent variables.

Reliability assessment

To ensure intra- and inter-examiner reliability, two independent observers repeated the CBCT measurements on a randomly selected subset of 10 patients at a two-week interval. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated using a two-way random-effects model based on absolute agreement. ICC values ranged from 0.90 to 0.95, demonstrating strong reproducibility of the results. Measurement errors ranged from 0.35 to 0.58 mm.

Results

A total of 92 molar teeth corresponding to 23 patients [15 females (mean age 26.3 ± 8.9 years) and 8 males (mean age 22.2 ± 5.7 years)] were included.

Alveolar bone height

The CBCT analysis revealed significant changes in alveolar bone height following sequential molar distalization with clear aligners. As shown in Table 1, both buccal and lingual vertical distances from the cementoenamel junction to the alveolar bone crest increased after treatment (T1) compared to baseline (T0), indicating a reduction in alveolar bone height. All mandibular molars exhibited a statistically significant reduction in both buccal (h1) and lingual (h2) alveolar bone heights (P < 0.05), except on the lingual side of right mandibular first molar. The greatest reduction was observed on the buccal side of the left and right mandibular first molars (1.80 ± 0.89, 2.70 ± 1.40, P =0.004 and 1.80 ± 0.74, 2.69 ± 1.36, P < 0.001, respectively), while the smallest reduction occurred on the lingual side of the right mandibular first molar, although it was not statistically significant (1.96 ± 1.34, 2.23 ± 1.44, P =0.082).

Alveolar bone thickness

Comparing pre- and post-treatment bone thickness, it was found that in the left mandibular first molar, the average buccal and lingual bone thickness increased; however, these changes were not statistically significant (1.77 ± 0.66, 1.80 ± 0.61, P = 0.476 and 2.96 ± 0.85, 2.97 ± 0.78, P = 0.694, respectively). In contrast, the left mandibular second molar showed a significant increase in average buccal bone thickness (3.61 ± 1.34, 4.49 ± 1.54, P < 0.001) and a significant decrease on the lingual surface (2.78 ± 0.52, 2.44 ± 0.79, P = 0.021). A similar pattern was observed on the right side of the mandible. The right mandibular first molar exhibited an increase in average buccal (1.95 ± 0.61, 2.16 ± 0.78, P =0.299) and lingual (3.14 ± 0.79, 3.25 ± 0.62, P =0.196) bone thickness, though neither change was statistically significant. The right mandibular second molar demonstrated a statistically significant increase in buccal bone thickness (3.06 ± 0.97, 4.10 ± 1.61, P < 0.001), while the decrease on the lingual side (2.96 ± 0.68, 2.73 ± 0.73, P =.103) was not statistically significant (Table 2).

Pearson correlation results

For the L-1stM , treatment duration significantly correlated with lingual bone height changes (r =0.497, P =0.016), while age was associated with buccal bone thickness at the root apex level(t5) (r =0.463, P =0.026). the L-2ndM showed no significant correlations with age or treatment duration for alveolar bone height, thickness, or root length. In the R-1stM, near-significant correlations were observed between buccal bone height and both age (r = -0.363, P =0.089) and treatment duration (r =0.363, P =0.088), although no other significant associations were detected. For the R-2ndM, demonstrated a significant correlation between lingual bone height (h2) and age (r =0.423, P =0.044).

Multiple linear regression results

MLR was performed to predict changes in alveolar bone height (h1, h2), root length (r), and bone thickness (t1–t6) for mandibular molars (L-1stM, L-2ndM, R-1stM, R-2ndM) after clear aligner treatment, with age and treatment duration as predictors. Significant models were observed only at the buccal midpoint (t3) level for the L-1stM (r2 = 0.25, P =0.045), and R-2ndM (r2 = 0.25, P =0.047), indicating that age and treatment duration modestly predicted changes in buccal bone thickness at these sites. All other models were non-significant (P-values ranging from 0.072 to 0.960). (Figs. 3, 4, 5 and 6)

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate changes in alveolar bone height and thickness, as well as root resorption, associated with mandibular molar distalization (MMD) using clear aligner therapy (CAT). Periodontal health is crucial for safe orthodontic movement; thus, evaluating alveolar bone conditions is essential to prevent complications such as fenestrations and dehiscence, which are frequently reported [33].CBCT was used to evaluate the anatomical thickness of the alveolar bone after molar distalization with CAT, providing high-quality, three-dimensional images free from distortion and superimposition. This imaging technique allows for a more comprehensive assessment of alveolar bone morphology and enables evaluation of more tooth surfaces compared to traditional radiographic methods [12, 34].

This study found a consistent reduction in alveolar bone height on both buccal and lingual aspects of the molars, with greater loss on the buccal surface. This aligns with Strippoli et al. [35], who observed that crestal bone reduction was three times greater buccally than lingually after orthodontic treatment with piezocision. In contrast, Un-Bong Baik et al. [36] reported significant changes on the lingual surface and minimal buccal bone loss following second molar protraction. These findings are further contextualized by the study of Silvia Izabella Pop et al. [25], who reported alveolar bone apposition in the region of lower left first molar following orthodontic treatment. Nicolas Göllner et al. [37] also reported that non of the patients in their sample developed a severe bone level height defect at any point after mandibular first molar mesialization, which contrasts with our findings. However, they reported that the bone height distance from CEJ was less than 3 mm in all cases which aligns with our finding. These contrasting outcomes highlight the variability in alveolar bone response to orthodontic forces, potentially influenced by factors such as treatment modality, individual anatomical differences, and the magnitude or direction of applied forces [38].

Changes in alveolar bone thickness were site-specific. Mandibular first molars showed slight increases in both buccal and lingual thickness, suggesting localized bone apposition potentially stimulated by aligner forces. This might help protect against dehiscence, as increased buccal bone can reduce susceptibility to gingival recession [39]. Conversely, mandibular second molars exhibited increased buccal but decreased lingual thickness, possibly due to their greater distal movement or tipping forces. These findings are consistent with Di Sun et al. [40], who reported lingual cortical thinning after molar distalization using microimplants, which may result from compressive forces on the lingual aspect during distalization.

All molar roots exhibited significant root resorption following treatment, indicating orthodontically induced inflammatory root resorption (OIIRR). This aligns with findings from several previous studies [35, 40]. Similarly, Kurnaz et al. [41] reported root resorption in both fixed appliance and clear aligner treatments, with greater severity in the former, likely due to longer treatment durations. Despite measurable root resorption, the overall reduction in root length was within the range considered acceptable for orthodontic treatment.

Although the differences in alveolar bone height and root resorption between T1 and T0 were statistically significant, the actual mean changes were relatively small in magnitude. For alveolar height, the reductions ranged from 1.93 mm to 2.70 mm, which falls within clinically acceptable limits (1–3 mm) and is unlikely to compromise periodontal health. Similarly, while root resorption was significant, the mean reduction in root length was minimal and within expected norms for orthodontic treatment (ranging from 1.21 mm to 1.41 mm). Given these modest changes, the observed differences, though statistically significant, may not hold substantial clinical relevance. This suggests that while orthodontic treatment induces measurable biological changes, their impact on long-term tooth stability and periodontal health is likely negligible in most cases [42, 43].

Although no strong overall link was found between age or treatment duration and bone or root changes, some site-specific trends were noted. For example, longer treatment time was associated with increased lingual bone height in the left first molar, and older age correlated with greater apical buccal bone thickness in the same tooth. A similar age-related increase in lingual bone height was observed in the right second molar. While other teeth showed trends, these did not reach statistical significance. Overall, age and treatment time had minimal impact, and bone remodeling appears to be driven more by localized, tooth-specific biomechanical factors.

Age and treatment duration could predict changes in alveolar bone and root structure during CAT. The analysis revealed limited predictive value, with only a few site-specific effects reaching significance. Notably, certain regions of the buccal bone in the first and second molars showed a modest response to these variables. However, the majority of outcomes were not significantly influenced by either age or treatment duration. These findings suggest that age and treatment duration are generally weak predictors of post-treatment changes, with other biomechanical or patient-specific factors likely driving most alveolar bone and root length outcomes. Future studies may incorporate nonlinear models to improve predictive accuracy.

CAT-induced molar distalization can cause alveolar bone resorption and root resorption, but these changes generally remain within safe limits for healthy, compliant patients. However, clinicians should carefully monitor high-risk individuals and apply conservative forces. Bone thickness changes vary by site, influenced by tooth position and movement direction, which must be considered to avoid complications such as dehiscence or recession, especially in patients with pre-existing thin cortical bone. Root resorption was minimal and acceptable. Age and treatment duration had little impact, emphasizing the need for individualized, biomechanics-based treatment planning.

The clinical significance of our study lies in its potential to improve the understanding of periodontal changes following molar distalization with clear aligners. Orthodontists should regularly monitor alveolar bone height and thickness, especially in patients with initially thin cortical bone, to identify early bone loss or root resorption. To reduce periodontal risks during mandibular molar distalization, it is advisable to apply conservative forces and tailor treatment plans to individual patient needs.

Despite the strength of the current study, it is not without limitations. Root resorption was measured only in the coronal view in this study. Furthermore, volumetric evaluation of the root resorption and bone density was not considered, thus future studies should include these variables. Since mandibular molars have two roots, additional measurements in sagittal and axial plane of the mandibular posterior teeth, as well as assessments of tooth inclination, may provide more comprehensive information for a better understanding of the periodontal condition of these teeth. Additionally, future research should adopt a prospective study design to address the limitations of retrospective analyses. Our current study was limited by a small sample size; therefore, subsequent investigations should include larger cohorts and incorporate control groups (e.g., fixed appliance distalization groups) for more robust comparisons. Furthermore, potential variations in aligner wear compliance among study participants should also be examined.

Conclusion

This study found a significant reduction in alveolar bone height of mandibular molars particularly on the buccal side, following sequential molar distalization with clear aligners. The alveolar bone thickness of the second molars increased on the buccal side and decreased on the lingual side after treatment. All mandibular molars underwent significant shortening after distalization. These results enhance understanding of the periodontal effect after molar distalization using clear aligners, though future prospective studies with larger sample sizes and long-term follow-up periods are required to better evaluate potential periodontal outcomes at various stages of treatment. Orthodontists should closely monitor alveolar bone height and thickness, particularly in patients with pre-existing thin cortical bone, to detect early signs of bone loss or root resorption. Additionally, conservative force application and individualized treatment planning are recommended to minimize periodontal risks during mandibular molar distalization.
