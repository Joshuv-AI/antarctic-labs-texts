Systematic Review of Emergency Severity Index (ESI) triage tool's utility in predicting mortality and critical care unit admission in Emergency Departments
Authors: Khaled Abdelrazek, Fatma Elzahraa Elsayed Soliman, Mohamed Hassan, Alaa Ewida, Wesam Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Emergency Severity Index (ESI) triage tool is a commonly used triage tool in Emergency Departments (EDs). Use of the ESI is associated with challenges emanating from such variables as patients’ presentations, chief complaints, disease severity and patient ages. This systematic review aims to examine the utility of the ESI triage tool in the prediction of patient’s mortality rate and admission to critical care units.MethodsA systematic search was conducted, covering the period from 1 April 2009 to 28 February 2023. Only studies published in English were included. The systematic review included primary research studies assessing and discussing the impact of using the ESI triage tool on mortality rates and admission to critical care units. Studies that reported the validity and reliability of the ESI triage tool were also included. Eligible studies specifically defined variable outcomes, such as death and admission to critical care units, in adult patients (aged 15 years and older). Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies were included, while retrospective studies were excluded.ResultsOf 2159 records screened, six studies were included (five cohort studies, one RCT), with sample sizes ranging from 580 to 9798 participants. ESI showed high inter-rater reliability (κ = 0.75), sensitivity (74.3 %), and specificity (94.4 %), with lower under-triage (10.7 %) and over-triage (6.2 %) compared with other triage systems. The predictive accuracy for mortality and ICU admission ranged from AUC 0.64–0.75, improving to AUC 0.91 when combined with additional clinical parameters. Studies consistently reported that ESI outperformed or equaled comparator triage tools.ConclusionsThe ESI triage tool demonstrated strong validity and reliability in predicting mortality rates and critical care admissions across diverse adult populations. Studies showed that ESI performs comparably or better than other triage systems and can be enhanced when combined with clinical parameters. Consistent application and proper training were identified as crucial factors for optimizing its predictive accuracy and clinical utility in emergency settings.Keywords: ESI, Emergency Severity Index, Triage, Emergency treatment, Mortality, Critical care units

African relevance

• The ESI triage scale, although commonly used in High-Income settings may not be fully applicable to African settings due to differences in healthcare infrastructure and patient demographics

• The utility of the ESI tool in predicting mortality and ICU admissions in resource-limited environments remains an important area of investigation, as differences in healthcare infrastructure and resource availability may impact its accuracy and effectiveness.

• It also underscores the need for further training to enhance the use of the ESI tool and improve emergency care outcomes across African emergency departments.

• Implementing objective triage systems like ESI is essential for the ongoing development and quality improvement of emergency medicine in Africa.

Introduction

The triage system is an approach used in Emergency Departments (EDs) to guide clinicians in categorizing patients based on clinical acuity and degree of urgency. There are many scales used in triage [1]. Triage systems classify patients by the severity of their condition and the resources required for care [2]. One of the most important scales used in triaging is the Emergency Severity Index (ESI). Developed in the late 1990s, it was commonly used in the United States as well as in non-English speaking countries such as Iran, Korea, Japan, and Switzerland [3]. ESI triage tool is widely recognized as a valid and reliable method for prioritizing patients in ED. It enables more accurate decision-making, facilitates appropriate treatment, and helps predict key outcomes such as mortality rates and admissions to critical care units. Previous studies have demonstrated that ESI scoring is associated with improved identification of patients at high risk of death or requiring intensive care [4]. Given the importance of these outcomes, this systematic review aims to evaluate the effectiveness of the ESI tool specifically in predicting mortality and admissions to critical care units. It enables accurate decision making in EDs, subsequently, facilitating appropriate treatment, and predicting outcomes. The most important outcomes are mortality and admission into critical care units. This also helps to decrease patient waiting time in the ED while reducing congestion.

The ESI is a tool comprising five levels, 1–5, based on clinical presentation and needs. The patient’s condition, whether critical or not, is inversely proportional to the ESI. Level 1, therefore is the highest level of severity / urgency and it requires immediate interventions, while a patient with level 5 requires the least urgent care [5]. It is a scientific and systematic approach, that saves time, which is also a significant determinant of patient outcomes in the ED and it replaces the old ‘first come is first served’ approach [6]. Some studies have reported improved patient satisfaction with the use of the ESI [7]. Other studies have examined the association of ESI with survival rate and patients’ mortality after one week, one month and six months of ED visits. There was evidence that patients in level 1 of the ESI had less than six months of survival while those in level 5 had >6months [8]. Though there are variations in the use of the ESI in different organizations, the goal of all is prioritization of patients based on clinical needs. The ESI is an excellent validated tool with good reliability [9]. While the ESI is primarily used to assess patient acuity in EDs, some studies indicate that ESI levels may not directly correspond to overall hospital admission rates. However, the tool remains effective for predicting longer-term outcomes, such as patient survival up to six months after triage, high lighting its utility beyond immediate care decisions [10]. The likelihood of dying in EDs is very low; however, an association between ESI triage levels and the risk of death still exists. Many patients who died in EDs did so due to under-triaging, where patients who should have been categorized as Level 2 or 3 were incorrectly assigned to Level 5. In some cases, patients were inappropriately transferred from Level 1 to Level 5 on the ESI scale due to misclassification or neglect of patients triaged at Level 5. The ESI is strongly associated with patient outcomes by estimating the number of resources needed. Moreover, the highest levels of severity of the ESI triage scale, which correspond to the most critical patient condition, are significantly associated with increased ED mortality rate. Both the ESI and the Manchester Triage System (MTS) are significantly associated with predicting hospital admissions based on patient categorization; however, the ESI has demonstrated greater reliability in its predictions [11]. Various triage scales are currently used across healthcare systems worldwide. However, the American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP) and the Emergency Nurses Association (ENA) have clearly recommended the use of the ESI scale. Moreover, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) allocated funding to develop and publish ESI training materials to educate a growing number of clinicians [12]. One of the most important considerations in utilizing the ESI scale is its application to special populations, particularly the elderly and pediatric patients. This has been identified as one of the scale’s limitations, as it may result in under-triage of these patients into lower urgency categories, most commonly at ESI level 3 [13]. Furthermore, version 4 of the ESI has demonstrated a high level of agreement between nurses and both adult and pediatric emergency physicians [14]. One study reported the reliability of the ESI in pediatric patients, recommended further studies on ESI from different perspectives and emphasized the importance of specialization of emergency physicians and pediatric emergency physicians [15]. ESI has yielded strong reliability not only with special populations but also in people with cancer [16]. Imparting nurses with skills and knowledge will promote efficiency in the use of the ESI triage and will lead to proper management [17,18].

Methods

The systematic review was conducted according to the JBI SUMARI methodology for systematic reviews. Original research studies published in English, between 1 April 2009 and 28 February 2023, and involving participants aged 15 years and older were included. The search strategy was conducted across seven databases: British Nursing Index, Business Source Premier, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, EMBASE (replaced by Medline and Scopus), and PubMed. Specific keywords were used for each database, and text terms were identified from the titles and abstracts of relevant studies. The search was limited to articles published in English between 1 April 2009 and 28 February 2023.

Individual searches were conducted in each of the databases. All citations were then imported into EndNote 20.4.1 for identification and merging of duplicate records. The records were screened by the title and abstract. Full text screening was then conducted via JBI SUMARI by two independent reviewers (Authors) to assess the eligibility according to set inclusion criteria and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) tool used for reporting the results. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer.

Inclusion criteria Studies were eligible for inclusion if they evaluated the effect of the ESI triage scale on patient mortality and admission to critical care units, involved patients older than 15 years who experienced death within 30 days of an ED visit or during hospitalization, examined presenting complaints and vital signs, and assessed the validity and reliability of the ESI system.

Exclusion criteria: Retrospective studies were excluded due to their higher susceptibility to selection bias, incomplete data, and confounding, which weaken causal inference. Therefore, only prospective studies and randomized controlled trials were considered to ensure a higher level of evidence.

Assessment of methodological quality

This was done by two independent reviewers. The JBI Standard Critical Appraisal Tool was used to assess the risk of bias in cohort studies, observational studies, and randomized controlled trials. The findings of the studies included addressed the research question to a significant extent. However, for the cohort studies, the appraisal tool contained some questions that were not applicable and were therefore marked as "Not Applicable (NA)." It is recommended that more specific tools be used within the JBI SUMARI platform to appropriately appraise different study methodologies.

Data extraction and analysis

Data extraction was conducted from JBI Software by two reviewers. The characteristics of studies extracted were study design, countries, year of publication, settings, participants characteristics and outcomes measured. This review being mixed method, statistical analysis (metanalysis) was not done but quantitative and qualitative outcomes were extracted from included studies. Results were examined alongside the findings to measure the mortality rate and admission to critical care units, in accordance with the systematic review criteria. JBI SUMARI software was also used to review the outcomes of the systematic review and to categorize the findings for the development of meta-aggregative flowcharts. The GRADE (Grading of Recommendation, Assessment, Development and Evaluation) Approach was used to generate the Summary Of findings and for reviewing outcomes mortality rate and admission to critical care units.

Results

The research results from each database were as follows: British Nursing Index (149), Business Source Premier (151), CINAHL (256), Cochrane Library (422), Medline (385), Scopus (436), and PubMed (360). This resulted in a total of 2159 records which were imported. After removing 122 duplicated records, 2037 studies remained for title and abstract screening. During the screening phase, 1973 studies were excluded. A total of 64 studies were selected for full-text screening. After applying the eligibility criteria, 58 were excluded, while 6 studies were included in the final review (Figure 1).

Across the included cohort studies, the 30-day mortality rate was the most consistently reported outcome. Reported AUC values for ESI ranged from 0.64 to 0.75 in predicting short-term mortality, with improvements up to 0.91 when combined with disease severity ratings or machine learning models. ICU admission prediction showed similarly variable accuracy, ranging from moderate discrimination (AUC ∼0.70) to excellent when enhanced models were applied.

Significant heterogeneity was observed across the included studies in terms of study design, populations, settings and outcomes. This heterogeneity precluded formal meta-analysis but highlights the broad applicability and variable performance of the ESI. This degree of variability precluded formal meta-analysis, as statistical pooling would have risked producing misleading or non-comparable estimates. Instead, a narrative synthesis was used to summaries performance trends across contexts. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted with caution, recognizing that ESI accuracy and reliability may differ substantially depending on population characteristics, clinical workflow, and outcome definitions

Several methodological limitations were noted: small sample sizes in some cohorts, variability in outcome definitions (e.g., mortality measured at different time points, single-center bias in two studies, potential under-reporting of confounding factors, and limited RCT evidence (only one trial available).

Findings suggest that while the ESI is a valid and reliable triage tool for predicting admissions and ED resource utilization, its accuracy in predicting short-term mortality varies. Performance improves when the ESI is supplemented with additional clinical variables, disease severity ratings, or machine learning models. Older adults and patients with complex presentations (e.g., cancer, nonspecific complaints) particularly benefit from these enhanced approaches.

Discussion

This systematic review is among the few that have evaluated the impact of the ESI on mortality rates and admissions to critical care units. The reviewed evidence indicates that the ESI is a valid predictive tool for these outcomes, particularly for patients assigned to the highest acuity levels (ESI levels 1 and 2). The studies included demonstrated the validity and reliability of the ESI tool.

One study reported the importance of machine learning and natural language processing in assisting healthcare providers and clinicians in the triage process. It helps in identification of patients at risk of admission in critical care units. Respiratory and cardiovascular parameters such as (heart rate, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure) were the most predictive of ICU admission. According to Lim et al. [20], the ESI triage tool is more reliable, valid and superior to the three-tier triage system which was used in Malaysia. The ESI showed better sensitivity and specificity than three-tier triage system. ESI compared with three-tier triage system to identify the under triage and over triage favorably and emphasized that high levels of ESI triage system associated with increase resources used and admission rate [20]. Categorizing patients based on objectivity and ESI Levels and components provides high agreement in low ESI level 1, and high level of ESI level 5 and the levels in between provides less agreements between the clinicians but level 2 was considered the highest agreement level among ESI five levels. ESI triage tool is adaptable across various healthcare systems regardless of context or background and ESI tool contributes to a better understanding of the triage concept [21].

Age plays a crucial role in ESI triage tool according to Ginsburg’s [22] detailed risk assessment for elderly is required and might be other variables interfere with age to increase the level of mortality in old age even for those who are younger and in same ESI level. Old age requires more resources in EDs compared with younger adults - short-term mortality rate, admission to critical care units and ED resources are higher in old age than young adults [7]. The ESI triage tool is an excellent predictor of mortality rates, admissions to intensive care units, and ED resource utilization. It has been validated for use across different age groups, disease conditions, healthcare settings, and systems. ESI is also effective in special populations with specific conditions, such as patients with active cancer. A key limitation of this review is the limited representation of studies from African and other resource-constrained settings. Because most included data derive from high-income countries, the applicability of our findings to African EDs remains uncertain. Variations in infrastructure, staffing, and triage training may influence ESI performance in these environments. Further validation studies conducted within African settings are therefore essential. These findings highlight not only variability in ESI performance across settings but also practical considerations for its implementation, particularly in low-resource environments. Implementing the ESI in low-resource settings presents several practical challenges beyond simply adopting a triage tool. Many EDs operate with limited infrastructure, including insufficient monitoring equipment, constrained space, and a lack of digital documentation systems [24]. Staff shortages and high patient-to-provider ratios can compromise adherence to structured triage protocols, and variability in triage training, particularly among junior or rotating staff, may reduce consistency and reliability [25]. Cultural and systemic workflow differences may also affect the practical application of the ESI [24]. To address these challenges, strategies such as phased implementation, competency-based training, and locally adapted triage charts are recommended based on ESI guidance and WHO recommendations [24,25]. Additionally, ongoing evaluation and feedback can help ensure sustainable and reliable triage practices. Future research should assess both the predictive accuracy and operational feasibility of the ESI in African and other resource-constrained emergency care systems

Conclusions

The reasons behind mortality and admission to critical care units must be thoroughly addressed to improve emergency care outcomes. This review found that the ESI consistently demonstrated efficiency and efficacy in predicting both short- and long-term mortality, as well as critical care admissions among ED patients. However, due to the heterogeneity of study populations, healthcare settings, and outcome definitions across the included literature, these findings should be interpreted as general performance trends rather than universally applicable estimates. Ongoing training in ESI-based triage remains essential for healthcare professionals, particularly those working in ED, to ensure reliability and consistency in their application. The ESI remains a valuable triage tool, whether implemented independently or in conjunction with complementary systems such as the Physician Disease Severity Rating (DSR). We encourage conducting further research on the use of the ESI tool in special populations and for specific diseases to better predict patient outcomes. Future studies should explore the tool's predictive validity in vulnerable groups such as the elderly, pediatrics patients, and individuals with chronic conditions or complex comorbidities. Additionally, research should examine the integration of ESI with other clinical assessment tools to enhance triage accuracy and decision-making in diverse healthcare settings. Evaluating the impact of continuous staff training on the effective use of ESI may also contribute to improved clinical outcomes and resource utilization.
