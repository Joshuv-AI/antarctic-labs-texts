A ferroptosis-suppressive state drives resistance to bladder-preserving chemoradiotherapy in muscle-invasive bladder cancer

ABSTRACT
Bladder-preserving trimodality therapy (TMT) incorporating concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CRT) provides a curative-intent alternative to radical cystectomy for muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), yet its efficacy is frequently limited by intrinsic treatment resistance, the molecular basis of which remains poorly defined. To address this, we performed bulk transcriptomic profiling of pretreatment tumors from 179 patients uniformly treated with bladder-preserving CRT and systematically integrated gene expression data with tumor immune features and clinical outcomes. We identified a ferroptosis-suppressive transcriptional signature (FSS) associated with a distinct resistance-associated tumor state that independently stratified radiographic progression-free survival and overall survival following CRT. FSS-high tumors were characterized by inferior outcomes, enrichment of basal/squamous and immune-excluded phenotypes, and reduced intratumoral immune infiltration, whereas FSS-low tumors preferentially exhibited luminal unstable and immune-inflamed features. Consistent with clinical observations, a ferroptosis-suppressive transcriptional program was recapitulated in CRT-resistant bladder cancer cell line models. Genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 loss-of-function screening further identified core ferroptosis suppressors as functionally relevant dependencies specifically under irradiation stress, and pharmacologic induction of ferroptosis effectively restored radiosensitivity in otherwise resistant cells. Together, these findings support ferroptosis suppression as a biologically relevant resistance-associated state that mechanistically links tumor-intrinsic transcriptional programs to immune contexture and therapeutic vulnerability and provide a translational framework for improved risk stratification and future treatment refinement in bladder-preserving therapy for MIBC.

Introduction

Radiation-based therapy, particularly chemoradiotherapy (CRT), remains a cornerstone of curative-intent treatment for solid tumors, including muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC). MIBC represents a clinically aggressive malignancy accounting for approximately 25% of all newly diagnosed bladder cancers and associated with significant morbidity and mortality; five-year cancer-specific survival remains below 50% in patients with locally advanced disease. Radical cystectomy (RC) with perioperative chemotherapy has historically been considered the standard of care,1 yet RC carries substantial perioperative morbidity and permanently impairs urinary function and quality of life. In the context of MIBC, trimodality therapy (TMT), comprising maximal transurethral resection of the bladder tumor (TURBT) followed by CRT, offers a bladder-preserving alternative to RC for appropriately selected patients, with retrospective and selected prospective studies reporting comparable oncologic outcomes.1–3 Molecular profiling studies have further characterized the transcriptional landscape of MIBC, identifying consensus subtypes, including luminal, basal/squamous, and neuroendocrine-like classes, with distinct biological properties and therapeutic implications, yet their predictive utility in the CRT setting remains incompletely defined. Despite its clinical promise, resistance to CRT limits the long-term efficacy of TMT. While alterations in DNA damage response (DDR) genes have been proposed as candidate biomarkers of radiosensitivity,4–6 additional molecular determinants of treatment resistance remain poorly defined.

Recent studies have implicated ferroptosis, a distinct form of iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation–driven cell death, as a modulator of therapy resistance across multiple tumor types, including resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy.7–12 Mechanistically, ferroptosis is governed by a balance between pro-oxidant drivers—including iron accumulation, polyunsaturated fatty acid–containing phospholipids, and lipoxygenase activity—and antioxidant defenses centered on the GPX4–glutathione axis and the FSP1–CoQ10 pathway.13 In particular, suppression of ferroptosis has been associated with impaired tumor cell death under oxidative stress and may attenuate responses to DNA-damaging treatments7,12,14 and rewired lipid metabolism, contributing to resistant phenotypes in several cancers, including bladder cancer (BC).13,15 Furthermore, ferroptosis sensitivity is intimately coupled to the tumor immune microenvironment; immune-excluded and immunosuppressive contexts have been associated with ferroptosis evasion, potentially reinforcing resistance to both radiotherapy and immunotherapy,12.16 Notably, IFNγ released by CD8⁺ T cells has been shown to sensitize tumor cells to ferroptosis by downregulating SLC7A11, such that immune exclusion—by limiting T cell infiltration—may simultaneously impair ferroptosis induction and attenuate therapeutic efficacy.16 However, the clinical significance of ferroptosis-related transcriptional programs in CRT response and survival outcomes in MIBC has not been systematically investigated.

Because CRT resistance is likely shaped by multifactorial genetic programs, unbiased genome-scale functional genetic screens offer a powerful approach to identify genes associated with tumor cell survival under therapeutic stress and to uncover treatment-sensitizing vulnerabilities. CRISPR/Cas9-based loss-of-function screening has been successfully applied across diverse cancer models to elucidate mechanisms of therapy resistance and to nominate druggable targets.17,18 These approaches have demonstrated that therapeutic resistance is frequently driven by coordinated adaptive transcriptional and metabolic programs rather than by single-gene alterations alone.19 In particular, genome-wide CRISPR screens conducted under specific therapeutic stress conditions, such as irradiation or chemotherapy exposure, have proven especially informative, as they capture context-dependent genetic dependencies that are not apparent under baseline growth conditions and would otherwise remain undetected by standard essentiality screens. Integrating such functional genomic data with clinical transcriptomic profiling provides a multilevel framework for connecting molecular observations in patient tumors to experimentally validated mechanisms, a critical step toward translating biological insights into refined therapeutic strategies.20 To date, however, such an integrated approach combining genome-wide CRISPR screening under irradiation stress with clinical transcriptomic profiling has not been applied to bladder-preserving CRT in MIBC, representing a critical gap in our understanding of treatment resistance in this disease.

Here, we investigated whether ferroptosis-suppressive transcriptional programs are linked to resistance biology in bladder-preserving CRT. In a clinically annotated cohort of 179 patients uniformly treated with cisplatin-based concurrent CRT, bulk RNA sequencing of pretreatment tumors was performed, and ferroptosis suppressor signatures reflecting antioxidant and lipid metabolic stress were evaluated for association with radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS) and overall survival (OS). To orthogonally connect these tissue-level observations with mechanisms, CRT-resistant bladder cancer cell models were established and characterized by parallel molecular readouts, including RNA-seq, lipidomic profiling, and genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screening, to identify context-dependent vulnerabilities. Collectively, this study provides several important insights into the role of ferroptosis suppression in bladder-preserving CRT resistance: first, the clinical validation of a ferroptosis-suppressive transcriptional signature as an independent prognostic factor in bladder-preserving CRT; second, the mechanistic characterization of ferroptosis suppression as a functionally relevant resistance state in CRT-resistant bladder cancer models; and third, the identification of ferroptosis induction as a potential therapeutic vulnerability associated with radiosensitization. These findings support the concept that ferroptosis suppression as a clinically relevant resistance-associated state linking tumor-intrinsic transcriptional programs to immune contexture and therapeutic vulnerability, providing a novel translational framework for risk stratification and treatment refinement in bladder-preserving therapy for MIBC.

Results

Transcriptomic profiling identifies immune-related differences associated with CRT response in MIBC

To investigate transcriptomic correlates of CRT response in MIBC, we analyzed a multi-institutional cohort of 179 patients who underwent TMT consisting of cisplatin-based chemotherapy and concurrent pelvic irradiation. For the primary clinical stratification, a prespecified eligibility criterion required a minimum of 12 months of radiographic follow-up to mitigate false-negative classification due to insufficient observation. Patients were classified as progression-free (PF; n = 88) if they had ≥12 months of radiographic follow-up without evidence of radiographic progression, with intravesical non–MIBC recurrences not considered progression events. Patients were classified as progressors (P; n = 91) if radiographic progression occurred at any time during follow-up. This endpoint was intended to capture treatment failure of bladder-preserving CRT rather than simple intravesical relapse. The median follow-up duration was 51 vs 47 months (P = 0.416; Table 1), respectively, as estimated by the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. Patients in the P group had significantly shorter OS following CRT (hazard ratio [HR] 8.99, 95% confidence interval [CI] 5.50–14.69, P < 0.001; Fig. 1a), consistent with the expected clinical course of tumors that develop radiographic progression. In the clinicopathologic comparison, the P group was characterized by a significantly younger median age at baseline (68 vs 72 years; P = 0.008), more advanced clinical T stage (P = 0.004) and N stage (P = 0.002), and a higher incidence of lymphovascular invasion (LVI). In contrast, baseline histologic composition was comparable between groups, with similar proportions of pure UC and tumors harboring any variant differentiation (P = 0.527). Other conventional tumor factors, including tumor grade and concomitant carcinoma in situ (CIS), also showed no meaningful intergroup differences (Table 1, Fig. 1b). To identify transcriptomic features associated with CRT response beyond clinicopathologic factors, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on pretreatment tumor specimens from all 179 patients. Notably, the distribution of MIBC consensus molecular classifications21 showed no significant differences between the PF and P groups (P = 0.642; Table 1). As shown in Fig. 1c, unsupervised clustering of single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) pathway activity scores revealed distinct transcriptomic patterns between the two groups. Pathways related to immune signaling and lipid metabolism were among the most differentially activated. Consistently, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) independently confirmed significant enrichment of immune response-associated pathways (e.g., interferon signaling) and lipid metabolic processes (e.g., fatty acid oxidation) in the PF group compared with the P group, as illustrated in the enrichment plots (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table S1).

To further explore these immune-related transcriptomic differences, we profiled the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) using complementary computational approaches, including EcoTyper,22 quanTIseq,23 and MCPcounter.24 EcoTyper analysis revealed that the overall distribution of cell ecotypes (CEs) differed significantly between the PF and P groups (P = 0.049; Fig. 2a). Specifically, CE3 and CE5, together with CE9 and CE10, were enriched in the PF group, whereas CE1, CE2, and CE6 were enriched in the P group (P = 0.003). Among these ecotypes, CE9 and CE10 most closely correspond to the proinflammatory or immune-inflamed-like ecotypes described in the original EcoTyper atlas, whereas CE1 and CE2 are lymphocyte-deficient adverse ecotypes, CE3 is a myeloid-enriched ecotype, CE5 has been linked to smoking-related mutational features, and CE6 is notable for relative enrichment in normal tissue.22 Consistently, MCPcounter analysis demonstrated significantly lower inferred CD8+ T-cell abundance in P tumors, and quanTIseq estimated a higher fraction of uncharacterized cells in the same tumors (Fig. 2b). To orthogonally validate these RNA-based findings at the tissue level, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of CD8⁺ T cells in 155 patients. Of these, 94 cases with evaluable stromal CD8⁺ cells were included in the intratumoral-to-stromal ratio analysis. The intratumoral/stromal ratio of CD8⁺ cells was significantly lower in the P group than in the PF group (P = 0.010; Fig. 2c), indicating impaired intratumoral CD8⁺ infiltration in P tumors. In contrast, H&E-based total TIL scoring did not differ significantly between the PF and P groups (P = 0.087; Supplementary Fig. S1), suggesting that the immune-related differences were better reflected by spatial CD8⁺ distribution than by total lymphocyte burden alone. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significant survival heterogeneity across CEs (P = 0.003; Supplementary Fig. S2). Guided by this heterogeneity, we stratified cases by ecotype composition into P-enriched (CE1/CE2/CE6) and PF-enriched (CE3/CE5/CE9/CE10) groups. P-enriched tumors were associated with inferior survival relative to PF-enriched tumors (rPFS: HR 3.06, 95% CI 1.63–5.74, P < 0.001, OS: HR 3.61, 95% CI 1.78–7.35, P < 0.001; Fig. 2d). Ferroptosis suppression is associated with CRT resistance and adverse survival outcomes in MIBC

GSEA revealed that, in addition to immune-related programs, lipid-metabolic pathways were significantly dysregulated between the PF and P groups (Fig. 1d). Given the mechanistic link between lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death, we hypothesized that suppression of ferroptosis may contribute to CRT resistance. Consistent with this premise, ferroptosis has been implicated in therapy resistance, including resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy, and in remodeling of the TIME toward an immune-excluded state.12,15,25

To test this hypothesis, we curated ferroptosis-related genes from FerrDbV2,26 restricted them to entries with validated status and evidence score ≥3, and defined a 26-gene FSS. At the gene level, most FSS genes were significantly upregulated in P tumors (Fig. 3a). At the pathway level, we evaluated the same FSS using complementary approaches: GSEA on the genome-wide differential expression vector (P vs PF) demonstrated positive enrichment of the FSS (Fig. 3b), and ssGSEA provided per-sample FSS scores, which were higher in P tumors (Fig. 3c). We next evaluated whether the FSS was associated with radiographic progression and survival outcomes after CRT. When analyzed as a continuous variable, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated that FSS showed consistent discrimination for rPFS across multiple time points, with areas under the curves (AUCs) of approximately 0.65–0.68 at 12, 24, and 36 months (Supplementary Fig. S3), indicating modest but stable prognostic performance. Based on this time-consistent prognostic association, patients were subsequently dichotomized into FSS-high (n = 104) and FSS-low (n = 75) groups using the optimal cutoff determined by the Youden index.

Baseline characteristics stratified by the FSS are shown in Table 2 and Fig. 3d. The delivered radiation dose and radiographic progression pattern were comparable across strata throughout a median follow-up of 48 and 50 months, respectively, as estimated by the reverse Kaplan–Meier method (P = 0.415). With regard to clinicopathological background, the FSS-high group was associated with a more advanced cT stage, reflected by a higher proportion of cT3/4 disease (50.7% vs 33.7%, P = 0.037) and a greater prevalence of clinical nodal involvement (cN1: 22.7% vs 10.6%, P = 0.037). The FSS-high stratum also showed more frequent histologic variant differentiation, particularly squamous differentiation (17.3% vs 2.9%), with overall variant presence differing significantly between groups (21.3% vs 9.6%, P = 0.033). In contrast, concomitant CIS was more common in the FSS-low group (22.1% vs 10.7%, P = 0.048). Other clinicopathological factors, including age, sex, smoking status, LVI, and tumor grade, were comparable across FSS strata. The consensus molecular subtype distribution also differed between groups, with a higher prevalence of Ba/Sq tumors in the FSS-high stratum. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that FSS-high patients experienced significantly shorter rPFS (HR 2.74, 95% CI 1.76–4.27, P < 0.001) and OS (HR 3.31, 95% CI 2.02–5.45, P < 0.001) following CRT than FSS-low patients (Fig. 3e). Consistently, lower progression-free rates were observed in the FSS-high group (P < 0.001, Fig. 3f). In multivariable Cox models adjusting for age, cT stage, cN stage, and LVI status, FSS-high remained associated with inferior rPFS (HR 2.22, 95% CI 1.44–3.44, P < 0.001) and OS (HR 2.82, 95% CI 1.71–4.63, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Table S2). LVI positivity also emerged as an independent adverse factor for both rPFS and OS, whereas positive lymph node metastasis was significant only for rPFS. To further assess the incremental prognostic value of FSS beyond standard clinicopathological factors, we constructed nested Cox models using a parsimonious clinical backbone consisting of age ≥75 years, cT stage ≥T2, cN+, and LVI. The addition of FSS to this clinical model significantly improved the model fit for both rPFS and OS (likelihood-ratio test: rPFS, P = 3.8 × 10⁻⁵; OS, P = 2.3 × 10⁻⁵; Fig. 3h and Supplementary Table S3), supporting the interpretation that the FSS provides prognostic information beyond clinical risk factors. To translate the multivariable Cox model into a clinically interpretable format, we constructed a nomogram incorporating FSS together with key clinicopathologic variables (Supplementary Fig. S4). Time-dependent ROC analysis of the integrated clinical + FSS model showed consistent discrimination with AUCs in the ~0.73–0.78 range for both rPFS and OS across time points (Supplementary Fig. S4), supporting the performance of the integrated clinical risk model incorporating FSS. To further evaluate the external validity of the FSS, we analyzed TCGA-based cohorts with different treatment contexts.27 In radiotherapy-treated MIBC cases, despite the small sample size, FSS-high tumors showed significantly shorter OS than FSS-low tumors (HR 6.02, 95% CI 1.33–27.24, P = 0.020, Fig. 3i). In contrast, in a nonradiotherapy-treated RC-dominant cohort,27 FSS did not significantly stratify PFS or OS (PFS: HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.59–1.08, P = 0.150; OS: P = 0.147; Supplementary Fig. S5), suggesting that the prognostic relevance of FSS may be treatment context dependent rather than uniform across disease settings. Because descriptive analyses showed that FSS-high tumors were more frequently classified as basal/squamous (Ba/Sq), we next asked whether FSS simply reflected molecular subtype or whether it was associated with additional biological and prognostic information beyond subtype context.

To delineate the biological features associated with FSS status, we analyzed molecular subtypes and immune-related pathways. GSEA revealed Ba/Sq-related pathways enriched in FSS-high tumors and LumU-related pathways enriched in FSS-low tumors, consistent with the expression of subtype-specific marker genes (Fig. 4a). As a sensitivity analysis, LundTax 5-group classification28–30 yielded directionally similar subtype associations, with FSS-high tumors remaining enriched for Ba/Sq-like disease (Supplementary Table S4). Immune signaling programs were enriched in FSS-low tumors (Fig. 4b), suggesting a more immunologically active TIME. Deconvolution supported this observation: EcoTyper revealed distinct ecotype distributions (P = 0.012), with P-enriched CEs (CE1, CE2 and CE6) being more frequent in FSS-high, PF-enriched CEs (CE3, CE5, CE9 and CE10) occurring at similar frequencies in both groups, and CE8 being markedly enriched in FSS-low tumors (Fig. 4c). CE8 tumors have been described as being associated with moderately favorable outcomes and enriched in B cells, plasma cells, and T follicular helper cells.22 These tumors exhibited markedly higher levels of IFN-γ and cytotoxic T-cell signatures than CE1/2/6 tumors.22 These findings suggest that FSS-high tumors are enriched for immune-excluded-like ecotypes, whereas FSS-low tumors are dominated by immune-inflamed-like ecotypes. MCPcounter analysis inferred lower CD8⁺ T-cell abundance in FSS-high tumors, and quanTIseq estimated a higher fraction of uncharacterized cells in FSS-high tumors (Fig. 4d). Immunohistochemistry corroborated these findings: the intratumoral/stromal ratio of CD8⁺ T cells was significantly lower in FSS-high tumors than in FSS-low tumors (P = 0.029, Fig. 4e), providing orthogonal evidence of impaired intratumoral CD8⁺ infiltration in FSS-high tumors. In contrast, H&E-based total TIL scoring did not differ significantly between the FSS-low and FSS-high groups (P = 0.139; Supplementary Fig. S6), suggesting that total TIL burden and spatial CD8⁺ distribution may capture partially distinct aspects of the TIME. Given the observed enrichment of Ba/Sq-related programs in FSS-high tumors and the distinct ecotype distribution according to FSS status, we next examined whether FSS retained prognostic relevance beyond molecular subtype and immune context. In multivariable models including clinical factors, subtype (Ba/Sq vs others), and ecotype group, the addition of FSS significantly improved model fit for both rPFS and OS in the EcoTyper-assigned subset (likelihood-ratio test: both P < 0.001; Fig. 4f and Supplementary Table S5). FSS-high also remained independently associated with inferior rPFS and OS in the full model. Collinearity among FSS, subtype, and ecotype was low (maximum VIF 1.49; Supplementary Table S6). These findings indicate that although FSS-high tumors are biologically linked to Ba/Sq-like and immune-excluded tumor states, FSS captures clinically relevant information that is not fully explained by subtype or ecotype alone. In subgroup analyses, FSS-high retained prognostic relevance in both Ba/Sq and non-Ba/Sq tumors, as well as in the P-enriched ecotype subgroup, whereas in the PF-enriched ecotype subgroup, the association was directionally similar but did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Figs. S7 and S8). In summary, transcriptome-wide analysis of pretreatment tumors from 179 patients demonstrated that ferroptosis suppression is associated with CRT resistance, an immune-excluded TIME, and poor survival. These findings support the FSS as a candidate biomarker associated with CRT response and guiding patient stratification in bladder-sparing therapy, with prognostic value that is not fully explained by molecular subtype or immune context alone. As a complementary context, reanalysis of a public bladder cancer single-cell dataset suggested that FSS-related expression was detectable across multiple annotated cellular compartments while remaining clearly represented in the tumor/epithelial compartment (Supplementary Fig. S9).

Ferroptosis suppression is associated with CRT resistance in bladder cancer cell models

To validate our clinical findings in an experimental setting, we performed a comprehensive mechanistic analysis using BC cell line models. CRT-resistant sublines were established through prolonged exposure to cisplatin and irradiation (Fig. 5a). We confirmed reduced sensitivity to cisplatin and irradiation in the resistant cell lines compared to their parental counterparts (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. S10). Given the known diversity of resistance adaptations, we initially focused on T24R cells as a model that captures selected transcriptional features observed in patient tumors. We note, however, that the parental T24 line has a relatively mesenchymal/EMT-like phenotype and therefore should be interpreted as representing one biologically relevant resistance trajectory rather than a universal model of MIBC. In addition, because T24R cells were generated through therapeutic selection in vitro, this model reflects acquired rather than preexisting resistance and should not be interpreted as a direct surrogate of intrinsic clinical resistance. RNA sequencing revealed that CRT-induced transcriptomic changes were markedly attenuated in T24R relative to T24 (Fig. 5c), with substantially fewer differentially expressed genes, indicating attenuated transcriptional responsiveness. At baseline, T24R cells showed increased enrichment of canonical resistance-associated pathways, including E2F targets and DNA damage checkpoints, along with metabolic programs such as cholesterol homeostasis, oxidative phosphorylation, and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 5d). Conversely, immune signaling pathways were markedly suppressed. Upon CRT, parental T24 cells upregulated these resistance-associated and metabolic pathways, whereas T24R cells displayed limited additional changes, restricted to immune-related pathways such as interferon signaling. These data suggest that resistant cells are preconditioned with resistance-associated transcriptional programs and exhibit limited transcriptional plasticity under CRT stress. To evaluate ferroptosis suppression in this model, we focused on pathway-level rather than gene-by-gene concordance. Gene set enrichment analysis demonstrated significant enrichment of the predefined 26-gene FSS in T24R relative to parental T24 cells (Fig. 5e), supporting activation of a ferroptosis-suppressive program at the enrichment level in this resistant model. This interpretation was further supported by subsequent functional experiments. In contrast, gene-level changes across the individual FSS components were not uniformly directional and are therefore presented as descriptive data in the Supplementary Information rather than as the primary basis for inference (Supplementary Fig. S11). Consistent with a functional contribution of ferroptosis in T24 cells, ferrostatin-1 significantly attenuated the cytotoxic effect of cisplatin plus irradiation (Supplementary Fig. S12). To investigate how alterations in ferroptosis suppressor genes influence lipid metabolism, we performed lipidomic profiling using liquid chromatography–electrospray ionization–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–ESI/MS/MS) under CRT conditions. CRT increased total phosphatidylcholine (PC) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) pools—particularly highly unsaturated species (e.g., PC 36:4/36:5)—in parental T24 cells, whereas resistant T24R cells showed no such increase and instead accumulated lactosylceramide (LacCer)/lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) (Fig. 5f–h). This pattern suggests that parental cells expand oxidizable phospholipid pools under CRT, whereas resistant cells undergo lipid remodeling that may limit lipid peroxidation.

Collectively, these findings suggest that CRT-resistant BC cells are transcriptionally preprogrammed with resistance-associated pathways, including ferroptosis suppressor gene networks, and exhibit attenuated transcriptomic responsiveness to CRT stress. The concomitant absence of CRT-induced expansion of oxidizable phospholipid pools, together with the accumulation of nonoxidizable lipid species such as LacCer and LPC, suggests that ferroptosis suppression may be mechanistically coupled to adaptive lipid remodeling. Such remodeling may limit lipid peroxidation and thereby contribute to CRT resistance.

We first focused on radiosensitizing genes, as these are most directly relevant to treatment-specific vulnerabilities. The screen identified 2753 genes whose loss increased radiosensitivity (Fig. 6b). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed strong overrepresentation of programs directly tied to CRT biology, including DNA replication and repair (ATM, CHEK2, RAD51), replication initiation and elongation (PCNA, RPA), and mitotic control (PLK4, KIF11), along with mitochondrial organization, mTOR-linked translational regulation, and redox homeostasis (Fig. 6c). These findings suggest that CRT-resistant cells harbor radiosensitizing dependencies across genome maintenance, replication stress, mitotic, and metabolic pathways. To further contextualize these findings relative to canonical DNA damage response (DDR) pathways, we examined representative DDR genes using the same predefined screening criteria. Several expected positives, including PRKDC, LIG3, FEN1, and PCNA, were classified as both essential and radiosensitizing hits, while other DDR genes were identified as essential-only or radiosensitizing-only dependencies (Supplementary Table S7). These results support the technical robustness of the screen and show that ferroptosis-related hits emerged against an expected background of canonical genome maintenance dependencies.

Negative selection also identified 2908 essential genes whose loss impaired the basal growth of CRT-resistant cells (Fig. 6b). As expected, these genes were enriched for core biosynthetic and RNA-processing pathways, including ribosome biogenesis, translation, RNA splicing, and proteasome function, supporting the technical robustness of the screen (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. S16). Because many of these reflect general growth requirements rather than treatment-specific liabilities, detailed pathway annotations are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Ferroptosis-related hits and functional validation

Notably, several ferroptosis suppressor genes emerged among the functionally relevant hits in the CRISPR screen, including genes classified as essential and radiosensitizing, although individual nonessential FSS genes were not uniformly assigned to a single irradiation-context category (Fig. 6d). In particular, GPX4, a central regulator of lipid peroxidation,31,32 and SLC7A11, a key component of the cystine/glutamate antiporter,33 were repeatedly identified, supporting the relevance of ferroptosis-suppressive dependencies in CRT-resistant cells. Several additional ferroptosis suppressors, including NFE2L2, also emerged, further supporting the biological relevance of ferroptosis suppression in this resistant state (Supplementary Table S8).

To functionally validate the role of ferroptosis suppression in CRT resistance, we treated resistant cells with ferroptosis inducers in combination with radiotherapy. Specifically, we employed the GPX4 inhibitor RSL3 and the SLC7A11 inhibitor erastin. As expected, T24R cells exhibited resistance to cisplatin relative to parental T24 cells; however, RSL3 treatment abolished the differential sensitivity, rendering T24R as sensitive as T24 (Fig. 6e). Likewise, while T24R cells remained resistant to cisplatin and irradiation, cotreatment with erastin effectively resensitized them to irradiation, restoring radiosensitivity (Fig. 6f). Notably, this radiosensitizing effect was substantially abrogated by cotreatment with ferrostatin-1, consistent with ferroptosis-dependent resensitization to irradiation. These observations further corroborate the high Bliss independence model score observed for the combination treatment (Fig. 6g).

To further assess lipid peroxidation directly, we performed C11-BODIPY(581/591) imaging in T24R cells under 3 Gy irradiation.34 Cisplatin plus irradiation alone did not produce a marked additional increase in the oxidation ratio over the basal state, whereas erastin plus irradiation increased C11-BODIPY oxidation, and this effect was attenuated by ferrostatin-1 (Fig. 6h). As supportive evidence, parental T24 cells showed increased C11-BODIPY oxidation after cisplatin plus irradiation under the same irradiation conditions (Supplementary Fig. S17).

Together, these findings provide functional support for the interpretation that disruption of ferroptosis-suppressive defenses unmasks ferroptosis-associated lipid peroxidation vulnerability in CRT-resistant cells.

In summary, our integrative in vitro analyses support a mechanistic role for ferroptosis suppression in CRT resistance. These results not only corroborate the clinical observations but also provide a rationale for therapeutic strategies targeting ferroptosis pathways to improve CRT efficacy in bladder-sparing approaches.

Discussion

This study provides integrative clinical and functional evidence that ferroptosis suppression represents a clinically relevant resistance-associated program in MIBC. By analyzing pretreatment tumors from 179 patients undergoing TMT and validating findings in CRT-resistant BC cell models, we showed that FSS-high tumors exhibit poor CRT response, immune-excluded TIME, and inferior rPFS and OS. Importantly, this association was independently recapitulated in radiotherapy-treated cases from the TCGA bladder cancer cohort, supporting the robustness of FSS as a prognostic stratifier. In contrast, FSS did not significantly stratify outcomes in a nonradiotherapy-treated, RC-dominant cohort, suggesting that its prognostic relevance may be more context-dependent and potentially stronger in the setting of bladder-preserving CRT. However, we could not determine whether this association is attributable primarily to radiotherapy, cisplatin, or their combination. Mechanistically, resistant cells displayed transcriptional preconditioning with resistance-associated programs, attenuated CRT-induced transcriptional remodeling, rewired lipid metabolism limiting oxidizable phospholipid pools, and functional dependence on ferroptosis suppressors such as GPX4, SLC7A11, and NFE2L2. Genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screening identified these genes as essential and radiosensitizing hits, and pharmacologic inhibition with RSL3 and erastin restored radiosensitivity. In addition, C11-BODIPY imaging showed erastin-inducible and ferrostatin-reversible lipid peroxidation in resistant cells, providing additional mechanistic support for ferroptosis-associated vulnerability in CRT resistance.

Molecular classification has markedly advanced our biological understanding of MIBC, delineating reproducible subtypes such as Ba/Sq, LumP, and LumU.21 Beyond subtype assignment, specific genomic and transcriptomic biomarkers have emerged as predictors of treatment response.35 Alterations in DDR genes—particularly ERCC2—are implicated as predictive markers of improved outcomes after TMT6 and are under prospective investigation, such as RETAIN I (NCT02710734)36 and RETAIN II (NCT04506554). Furthermore, our recent study demonstrated that within the LumP subtype, FGFR3-mutated tumors showed higher objective response rates to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) than FGFR3-wildtype LumP tumors, overturning the traditional view of FGFR3-altered tumors as immunologically “cold”.37 These findings emphasize the importance of considering molecular and immunogenomic contexts when evaluating biomarkers.

Our results extend these insights to the TMT setting by showing that FSS-high tumors are enriched for the Ba/Sq subtype and immune-excluded TIME, whereas FSS-low tumors are predominantly LumU with an immune-inflamed TIME. Consistently, MCPcounter analysis revealed a marked reduction in CD8⁺ T cells in FSS-high tumors. Ferroptosis has been reported to be less efficiently triggered in tumors with low CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, likely due to reduced IFN-γ–driven lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic signaling from cytotoxic lymphocytes,16,38 consistent with our immunohistochemistry (IHC) findings. Notably, within FSS-high tumors, the persistence of poor outcomes in CE2- and CE6-enriched cases indicates that CRT resistance may be related to coordinated cellular states rather than the abundance of individual immune cell types, supporting the relevance of ecosystem-centric profiling as a readout of CRT responsiveness. Among these, CE6 emerged as a potential CRT progressor-associated ecotype. In the pancancer atlas by Luca et al.,22 CE6 was described as a normal-like ecosystem composed mainly of epithelial, fibroblast, and endothelial cells with very low lymphoid or myeloid content and was one of the few CEs frequently detected in both tumors and adjacent normal tissues. Consistent with this feature, CE6-enriched tumors in our cohort showed poorer outcomes within the FSS-high group. Collectively, these findings suggest that incorporating ferroptosis-suppressive status alongside molecular subtype and immune contexture may improve risk stratification for bladder preservation. These results further support the view that FSS provides prognostic information beyond consensus subtype assignment rather than simply reflecting Ba/Sq-enriched biology. Public single-cell reanalysis further suggested that the FSS program is not restricted to a single cellular compartment but is distributed across multiple populations in the tumor microenvironment, including tumor/epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells. The signal was also clearly represented in the tumor/epithelial compartment, supporting the interpretation that bulk FSS reflects, at least in part, a tumor-associated ferroptosis-suppressive state rather than a purely stromal signal. In contrast, FSS did not significantly stratify outcomes in a nonradiotherapy-treated, RC-dominant cohort, suggesting that its prognostic relevance may be context-dependent and potentially stronger in the setting of bladder-preserving CRT. However, we could not determine whether this context dependence is attributable primarily to radiotherapy, cisplatin, or their combination.

Ferroptosis has been implicated in therapy resistance across tumor types.8,11,15,25,39 Suppression of ferroptosis—via upregulation of antioxidant systems such as GPX4 and SLC7A11 or master regulators such as NFE2L2—may enable tumor cells to withstand oxidative stress and evade DNA-damaging therapies. We found that CRT-resistant tumors and cells display transcriptional activation of ferroptosis suppressor genes, lack CRT-induced phospholipid remodeling, and depend on these suppressors for survival under therapeutic stress. Lipidomics showed that parental cells expanded PC and PE pools, particularly highly unsaturated species, under CRT, whereas resistant cells failed to do so and instead accumulated LacCer and LPC. This raises the possibility that resistant cells evade ferroptotic pressure by restricting the formation of oxidizable phospholipid pools—a mechanism not previously linked to CRT resistance. Importantly, our CRISPR screen identified multiple ferroptosis suppressors as essential and radiosensitizing hits, and pharmacologic inhibition of GPX4 and SLC7A11 using RSL3 and erastin attenuated the resistance phenotype. Consistent with this, C11-BODIPY imaging demonstrated erastin-inducible and ferrostatin-reversible lipid peroxidation in resistant cells under irradiation, providing additional support for a mechanistic link between ferroptosis-suppressive defenses and CRT resistance. At the same time, our findings underscore heterogeneity in resistance mechanisms across CRT-resistant models. While ferroptosis suppression emerged as a dominant resistance program that closely mirrors clinical tumors and is functionally linked to resistance in T24R cells, an alternative resistant subline exhibited CRT resistance without activation of ferroptosis-associated pathways, indicating the existence of ferroptosis-independent adaptive states. These observations suggest that ferroptosis suppression represents one major, but not universal, mechanism of CRT resistance and that distinct transcriptional trajectories can arise under similar therapeutic pressure. Clinically, the immediate implication of these findings is not the direct use of RSL3- or erastin-like compounds but the identification of ferroptosis-suppressive biology as a source of biological insight that may support biomarker-guided risk stratification and future radiosensitizer development in bladder-preserving therapy. Recent reviews support the translational relevance of ferroptosis-related therapeutic strategies, including radiotherapy-based sensitization and combination approaches,40,41 while prior work with oxyfedrine provides a mechanistic rationale for clinically feasible xCT-targeted combination strategies.42

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design and limited size of certain molecular subgroups require external validation. The radiotherapy-treated TCGA subset used for external comparison was small and should therefore be interpreted as supportive context rather than definitive validation. An additional limitation is that the T24/T24R system reflects a urothelial model with a relatively mesenchymal/EMT-like phenotype, which is not representative of all MIBC states and should not be interpreted as a universal representation of clinical CRT resistance. In addition, the T24R model represents an acquired resistance state, whereas our clinical analyses address preexisting resistance programs present before CRT. A further limitation is inherent to the screening platform itself. Genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screening is well suited to identify vulnerabilities in resistant cells but does not provide a comprehensive map of resistance origins and may not fully capture resistance mechanisms driven by preexisting loss-of-function alterations, copy-number loss, or nongenetic adaptive plasticity. We therefore interpret the screen primarily as a tool to identify vulnerabilities associated with the resistant state rather than to define all initiating mechanisms of CRT resistance. Future work integrating spatial multiomics and in vivo validation will be essential to translate these findings into clinical tools and to test ferroptosis-inducing agents as radiosensitizers in prospective bladder preservation trials.

This study provides integrative clinical and experimental evidence that ferroptosis suppression is associated with a clinically relevant resistance-associated program in MIBC treated with CRT. By linking ferroptosis-suppressive transcriptional features to immune-excluded tumor ecosystems, adverse clinical outcomes, and functional dependencies in resistant models, our findings support ferroptosis-suppressive activity as a candidate biomarker for risk stratification. Incorporating ferroptosis-related biomarkers with molecular subtype and immune contexture may help refine patient selection for bladder-preserving therapy.

Materials and methods

Clinical records and patient care

Clinical and pathological data were retrospectively collected from 201 consecutive patients with BC treated within the OMPU-NCC Consortium Japan (a collaborative bladder cancer consortium between Osaka Medical and Pharmaceutical University and the National Cancer Center of Japan). All patients underwent TMT, consisting of TURBT followed by CRT at Osaka Medical and Pharmaceutical University between August 2016 and July 2022. Detailed treatment and surveillance protocols are described below. Among these, 3 patients who remained alive but had less than 12 months of follow-up without radiographic progression were excluded to avoid misclassification due to immature follow-up. In addition, 19 patients who had received systemic chemotherapy prior to tumor sampling were excluded because prior treatment could affect tumor transcriptomic profiles. After these exclusions, a total of 179 patients constituted the final analytic cohort for clinical and transcriptomic analyses. Response evaluation was based on post-CRT cystoscopic and radiographic assessments. Radiographic progression was defined as local progression of the primary bladder lesion and/or regional or distant metastatic progression detected on cross-sectional imaging according to institutional radiology review (and/or RECIST [Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors] where applicable). Salvage cystectomy was performed in 12 patients. In all cases, radiographic local progression had been documented before cystectomy, except for one patient with metastatic progression who underwent cystectomy for local symptom control. Accordingly, salvage cystectomy was treated as post-progression management and did not itself define or censor the rPFS event. rPFS was defined as the interval from the initiation of CRT to radiographic progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Patients without an event were censored at the date of the last radiographic assessment. OS was defined as the interval from CRT initiation to death or last follow-up. The distribution of follow-up time was estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method.

Treatment and surveillance protocols

Maximal TURBT

An initial TURBT and a planned second-look TURBT were performed for maximal tumor debulking and pathological confirmation, with tumors resected as completely as possible prior to CRT.

Chemoradiotherapy

Concurrent cisplatin-based CRT was delivered to the whole pelvis using external-beam radiation therapy. Radiation was administered in daily fractions of 2 Gy, with a planned total dose of up to 60 Gy. Selective bladder boost was applied at the discretion of the treating radiation oncologist based on tumor characteristics and residual disease after transurethral resection.

Surveillance

After completion of CRT, patients were followed according to an institutional surveillance protocol incorporating regular cystoscopic evaluation, urine cytology, and cross-sectional imaging to assess local and distant disease status.

Statistical analysis

To define an optimal threshold for dichotomizing the ferroptosis suppressor signature (FSS), we applied the Youden index on a prespecified binary endpoint of 2-year rPFS. The 24-month time horizon was selected to capture clinically meaningful early treatment failure based on prior bladder-preservation studies demonstrating that the majority of recurrences occur within the first two years after CRT, with a subsequent decline in recurrence risk.2,43 Patients with events within 24 months were coded as positive; those event-free with ≥24 months of follow-up were coded as negative, and cases censored before 24 months were excluded from this analysis. ROC curves were generated using the pROC package in R,44 with bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) to estimate 95% confidence intervals for the optimal cutoff and AUC. The primary cutoff was prespecified as the Youden-optimal threshold at 24 months; consistency was evaluated at 36 months as a sensitivity analysis. Time-dependent discrimination of continuous FSS was additionally evaluated using time-dependent ROC analysis (timeROC package45; marginal inverse probability of censoring weighting), with AUCs reported at 12, 24, and 36 months. Pointwise 95% confidence intervals were obtained using influence-function–based variance estimation (iid = TRUE). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.3.1), JMP (version 13; SAS Institute), and R (version 4.3.1). Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test was used for categorical variables, and Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test was used for continuous variables, as appropriate. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Survival curves were estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by the log-rank test, and HRs were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. The schematic illustrations were generated using BioRender.com.

Biospecimen collection

Pretreatment tumor tissues were obtained from TURBT specimens prior to CRT. Samples were immediately placed in RNAlater (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All hematoxylin and eosin-stained slides were reviewed by board-certified pathologists to confirm the diagnosis of urothelial carcinoma (UC) and ensure ≥60% tumor cell content with ≤20% necrosis. RNA was extracted using the AllPrep DNA/RNA Kit (QIAGEN), quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and assessed for integrity using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent); samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) < 7.0 were excluded.

Cell lines and materials

The BC cell lines, including T24 and BOY12E cells, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and RIKEN BRC (Tsukuba, Japan). Cell line authentication was verified by the Human STR Profiling Cell Authentication Service, and all cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination. Cells were maintained in medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 2 mM L-glutamine at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. All reagents, including small-molecule inhibitors and plasmids, are listed in Supplementary Table S9.

Cellular viability assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 1000–1500 cells per well (n = 6 per condition as biological replicates) and treated as indicated. Cellular viability was measured using AlamarBlue (Thermo Fisher Scientific) 5–10 days after treatment.

Drug synergy analysis

Cells were seeded in 12-well plates and treated with a matrix of 16 combinations of irradiation (1, 2, 3 Gy) and erastin (0.2, 0.3, 0.5 μM) or vehicle. Colonies were fixed and stained on day 14 and quantified using ImageJ. Synergy scores were calculated using the Bliss independence model implemented in SynergyFinder Plus.46

RNA sequencing

For cell lines, BC cells were treated with or without combined irradiation (3 Gy) and cisplatin (3 μM) in biological duplicates. Total RNA was extracted using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). In the present study, all the procedures for library preparation were handled by MaholoLabDroids (humanoid robotic crowd biology) at Robotic Biology Institute Inc. (Tokyo, Japan).47 With regard to RNA-seq library preparation, the NEBNext rRNA Depletion Kit was used for rRNA depletion, followed by library amplification using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturers’ protocol. All RNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a paired-end flow cell configuration (2 × 100 bp) to ensure a minimum depth of 50 million base reads for RNA sequencing.

Signature analysis for ferroptosis

Ferroptosis-related genes were curated from the FerrDbV2 database, restricted to entries with a relevance score ≥3. This yielded a final panel of 26 ferroptosis suppressor genes (e.g., NFE2L2, GPX4, FTH1, AIFM2). RNA-seq data were normalized as transcripts per million (TPM) and log-transformed where appropriate to ensure comparability across samples.

Immunohistochemistry

Public single-cell RNA-seq reanalysis

To explore the cellular distribution of FSS within bladder cancer tissues, we reanalyzed a public bladder cancer single-cell RNA-seq dataset48 comprising seven tumor samples. Gene-by-cell expression matrices were imported into Seurat, and genes detected in fewer than three cells were excluded. Cells were filtered using the following quality-control criteria: 500–6000 detected genes per cell and mitochondrial transcript fraction <10%. After log-normalization and variable-feature selection, samples were merged, and batch effects were reduced using Harmony based on sample identity. UMAP embedding, neighbor graph construction, and clustering were performed on the Harmony-reduced space. Major-lineage annotation was performed using canonical marker gene sets together with AddModuleScore-based scoring for tumor/epithelial cells, myeloid cells, T cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, B cells, and plasma cells. An epithelial candidate subset was then reanalyzed separately using Harmony. The predefined 26-gene FSS was evaluated using both gene-level expression and UCell-based signature scoring across annotated cellular compartments. Because the original study was designed primarily to resolve epithelial cell heterogeneity in bladder cancer and because epithelial subsets were also specifically reanalyzed in that framework, the present reanalysis was intended as supportive contextualization of the bulk FSS signal rather than definitive cell-type attribution. Because malignant-cell annotation was not further refined using inferCNV- or copy-number-based approaches and some nonmalignant populations were limited in representation, this reanalysis was interpreted as exploratory supportive analysis rather than definitive cell-type attribution.

Lipidomic analysis by LC–ESI/MS/MS

Comprehensive lipidomic profiling was performed by Human Metabolome Technologies (HMT; Tsuruoka, Japan) using LC–ESI/MS/MS. Total lipids were extracted from cell pellets (2 × 106 cells) using a modified Bligh and Dyer method. A portion of the total lipid extract was further fractionated by anion-exchange chromatography to isolate acidic phospholipids. Dried lipid fractions were evaporated under nitrogen, reconstituted in methanol, and subjected to LC–MS analysis. Lipids were separated on an ACQUITY UPLC H-Class system (Waters) coupled to a Xevo TQ-XS triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source and operated in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. Peak detection and alignment were performed using MetaboAlign v1.0.5 (Mitsui Knowledge Industry). Quantification was based on internal standard normalization, and the results were normalized to the cell number (pmol per 1 × 106 cells). Relative compositional ratios among lipid species were also calculated.

Genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen

Genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout H3 libraries (~118,000 sgRNAs; 5-6 sgRNAs per gene) were provided by the laboratory of X. Shirley Liu. A total of 2 × 10⁸ BC cells were transduced at a low multiplicity of infection (~0.3) to ensure single-sgRNA integration, followed by 5 days of puromycin selection. Surviving cells were expanded, and 6 × 10⁷ cells per condition were allocated to the day 0 (T0) reference or cultured for 21 days under irradiation or nontreatment conditions. For the irradiation arm, cells were subjected to fractionated irradiation at a dose of 3 Gy three times per week throughout the 21-day screening period, whereas control cells were cultured without irradiation. Genomic DNA was extracted at the indicated time points, and PCR-amplified sgRNA libraries were sequenced at 30–40 million reads per sample, ensuring ≥300-fold coverage of the library.

CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen data analysis

Raw read counts were processed using MAGeCK to quantify sgRNAs, and gene-level β-scores were computed using MAGeCK-MLE, with AAVS1 serving as a negative control. Differential β-scores between treatment and control arms were calculated using MAGeCKFlute. Essential genes were defined by β scores ≤ −0.64 in the control arm, a threshold supported by distributional analysis and validated against proxy-essential sets (ribosomal, proteasomal, replication-licensing factors). This cutoff closely matched the optimal data-driven value (β = −0.64, ROC AUC = 0.937; Supplementary Fig. S18), indicating a stringent yet empirically justified criterion. Radiosensitizing and resistance genes were identified by differential β-scores. Based on the internal consensus from our four prior CRISPR screening studies,18 in which Δβ ≤ −0.40 consistently captured robust negative selection hits, we predefined Δβ ≤ −0.40 as a minimum important difference threshold for negative selection in the present analysis. Genes with Δβ ≥ +0.40 were analogously considered positively selected. The identified genes were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis using DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov).

Bioinformatic analysis

All analyses were performed using the supercomputing resource provided by SHIROKANE (Human Genome Center, the Institute of Medical Science, the University of Tokyo). For the RNA-seq analysis, STAR:2.5.2a was used for the mapping of FASTQ on GRCh38. Then, featureCounts (SUBREAD): 2.0.1 was adopted to count the number of reads mapped on exon regions by gene symbol. Raw counts were modeled by DESeq249; TPM (transcripts per million) was computed only for expression display. Regarding the molecular subtypes, we utilized the consensus MIBC subtype.21 For the deconvolution of the tumor microenvironment from the bulk RNA-seq data, EcoTyper was adopted.22 For differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis, the DESeq2 platform49 was conducted using a raw read count matrix from the present cohort.

C11-BODIPY lipid peroxidation imaging

Lipid peroxidation was assessed using C11-BODIPY (581/591) fluorescence imaging. T24R cells were treated under 3 Gy irradiation with vehicle (DMSO), cisplatin, erastin, or erastin plus ferrostatin-1, and parental T24 cells were analyzed under the same irradiation conditions as supportive comparison. Fluorescence images were acquired under identical microscope settings across all groups. Green fluorescence was interpreted as oxidized C11-BODIPY, and red fluorescence was interpreted as nonoxidized C11-BODIPY. Images were analyzed using ImageJ, and lipid peroxidation was quantified as the green/red fluorescence intensity ratio using matched regions of interest or masks. Each dot in the quantitative plots represents one biological replicate. Quantification broadly followed published C11-BODIPY oxidation-ratio imaging approaches.34
