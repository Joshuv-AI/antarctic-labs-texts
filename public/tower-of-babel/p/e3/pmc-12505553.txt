Sex-specific associations between type 2 diabetes and muscle health in middle-aged adults
Authors: Yijia Wang, Songting Gao, Qiang Xu, Zhongxin Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and sarcopenia have emerged as significant public health challenges that disproportionately affect middle-aged adults. However, the relationship between T2DM and muscle parameters in this critical age remains inadequately elucidated.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive analysis of cross-sectional data derived from 2,446 middle-aged participants (aged 40–59 years; 1,230 men and 1,216 women) enrolled in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011–2014. T2DM was defined by physician-confirmed diagnosis and/or glycohemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%. We systematically evaluated appendicular lean mass index (ALMI) and grip strength in relation to T2DM status, employing multiple linear regression models with hierarchical adjustment for demographic, anthropometric, and clinical covariates.ResultsFollowing comprehensive covariate adjustment, T2DM exhibited a significant inverse association with combined grip strength (β=-3.14; 95% CI: -4.55, -1.73) but showed no significant association with ALMI (β=-0.05; 95% CI: -0.13, 0.02). Sex-stratified analyses revealed significant reductions in both muscle mass and strength exclusively among men with T2DM, whereas no significant associations were observed in women. The adverse impact of T2DM on grip strength was particularly pronounced among individuals with BMI < 25 kg/m² (β=-6.72; 95% CI: -10.38, -3.06). Furthermore, glycohemoglobin demonstrated positive associations with ALMI in non-diabetic individuals, while serum glucose levels exhibited inverse associations with ALMI across the total study population.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrated negative associations between T2DM and muscle mass and strength in middle-aged men, but not in women. These observations underscore the critical importance of early muscle health surveillance and targeted therapeutic interventions in T2DM patients, particularly among men and those with BMI < 25 kg/m².

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has emerged as a global health priority, constituting 90% of the 537 million diabetes cases worldwide and imposing a substantial burden on morbidity and mortality [1]. The recent Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study shows that the 55–59 age group had the highest global incidence and prevalence, with other middle-aged groups also exhibiting elevated rates [2]. Concurrently, sarcopenia—a progressive decline in muscle mass and function—has gained recognition as a parallel epidemic, linked to elevated risks of falls, fractures, and disability [3–5]. While traditionally linked to advanced age, recent evidence demonstrates midlife onset before age 60, with prevalence remaining consistently elevated across studies in this population [6]. The complex, bidirectional interrelationship between T2DM and sarcopenia has emerged as a critical focus in metabolic research [7, 8].

Evidence indicates an amplified prevalence of sarcopenia among T2DM patients, with accelerated decline in skeletal muscle function compared to healthy counterparts [9, 10]. This association is influenced by multiple diabetes-related factors, such as chronic hyperglycemia, disease duration, and microvascular complications, which collectively contribute to progressive muscle dysfunction [11]. Notably, sex-specific differences in muscle composition changes have been observed, with men and women exhibiting distinct trajectories of muscle mass and strength decline [12, 13].

While traditionally considered age-related disorders, both T2DM and sarcopenia are increasingly diagnosed in younger populations, driven by sedentary lifestyles and obesity epidemics [8, 14]. The decline in muscle mass is frequently accompanied by a reduction in muscle strength, which follows a trajectory characterized by an increase to peak levels in early adulthood, maintenance through midlife, and progressive decline thereafter [15, 16]. This early deterioration lays the groundwork for severe functional impairment in later life, underscoring the need for timely intervention.

Recent clinical evidence emphasizes early detection and targeted strategies to mitigate sarcopenia in T2DM [17]. Assessments of muscle mass and strength are pivotal for evaluating diabetes-related health outcomes [18, 19], yet two key gaps remain unresolved. First, although the association between T2DM and muscle health has been extensively investigated in older populations, its manifestations in middle-aged adults—a demographic where early interventions could yield the greatest clinical impact—remain poorly elucidated. Second, potential sex-specific differences in this relationship are undercharacterized, despite compelling biological plausibility for divergent effects between men and women.

To address these gaps, our study leverages two validated sarcopenia indicators aligned with the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2 criteria [20]: appendicular lean mass index (ALMI) and grip strength. ALMI offers a robust, height-adjusted measure of muscle mass with well-established reliability [21], while grip strength serves as both a diagnostic marker and a predictor of morbidity [22]. By focusing on middle-aged adults, we aimed to elucidate sex-specific associations between T2DM and muscle health, thereby informing targeted, precision interventions.

Methods

Study design and population

We analyzed cross-sectional data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a comprehensive program assessing health and nutritional status in the United States. Detailed methodological protocols are publicly available (http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.htm) [23]. We analyzed data from the NHANES 2011–2014 cycles, as grip strength, a critical diagnostic measure for sarcopenia, was systematically assessed only during these survey periods.

From an initial pool of 3,786 middle-aged adults (40–59 years), participants were excluded based on the following criteria: absence of glycohemoglobin (HbA1c) or serum glucose measurements (n = 337); indeterminate diabetes status (n = 3); missing appendicular lean mass, height, or weight data (n = 731); incomplete combined grip strength measurements (n = 165); early-onset diabetes mellitus diagnosed before age 30 (n = 24); and undefined menstrual status (n = 80). The final analytical sample comprised 2,446 participants (Fig. 1). The study protocol adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, with all participants providing written informed consent.

Definition of T2DM

The primary exposure variable was T2DM status, defined by physician-confirmed diagnosis (age of onset ≥ 30 years) and/or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% according to American Diabetes Association guidelines [24].

Outcome variables

Primary outcomes included ALMI and combined grip strength. ALMI was derived from dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scans (Hologic QDR-4500 A) as appendicular lean mass [kg] divided by height squared [m²]. Grip strength was assessed using a calibrated Takei Digital dynamometer (T.K.K.5401), with combined strength calculated as the sum of the maximum force measurements obtained from each hand.

Covariates

Our analyses incorporated multiple demographic and clinical covariates: age, sex, race/ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), educational level, marital status, history of hypertension, history of cancer or malignancy, moderate recreational activities, total protein, blood urea nitrogen, and serum calcium.

Statistical analyses

Analyses were performed using R (version 3.4.3) and EmpowerStats software, with significance set at P < 0.05 (two-sided). Following STROBE guidelines [25], we employed multivariable linear regression with sequential covariate adjustment: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (demographic factors), Model 3 (Model 2 plus BMI), and Model 4 (fully adjusted for all potential confounders).

We conducted comprehensive stratified analyses by sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, moderate recreational activities, and comorbidities. Additionally, we examined associations between glycemic parameters (HbA1c and serum glucose) and muscle metrics.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population stratified by T2DM status are presented in Table 1. Among 2,446 participants, 364 had T2DM, showing distinct demographic and clinical profiles. Participants with T2DM were older (50.6 ± 5.5 vs. 49.0 ± 5.7 years) with higher proportions of men (57.1% vs. 50.2%) and postmenopausal women (28.8% vs. 24.5%). Notable racial/ethnic disparities emerged: participants with T2DM comprised fewer non-Hispanic Whites (28.6% vs. 42.2%) but higher proportions of non-Hispanic Blacks (29.1% vs. 21.4%) and Mexican Americans (15.9% vs. 11.8%). Clinical parameters revealed significantly higher BMI (33.2 ± 7.3 vs. 28.7 ± 6.2 kg/m² and hypertension prevalence (57.4% vs. 31.1%) among participants with T2DM. As expected, glycemic indices were elevated in participants with T2DM (HbA1c: 7.8 ± 2.1% vs. 5.5 ± 0.4%; serum glucose: 9.3 ± 4.9 vs. 5.3 ± 0.9 mmol/L). While ALMI was higher in participants with T2DM (8.8 ± 1.7 vs. 7.8 ± 1.6 kg/m²), combined grip strength showed no significant between-group differences.

The relationship between T2DM status and muscle parameters demonstrated complex patterns (Table 2). Initial models showed positive associations between T2DM and ALMI; however, after BMI adjustment, this association reversed but remained non-significant (Model 4: β=−0.05; 95% CI: −0.13, 0.02). Similarly, combined grip strength showed no association in unadjusted analyses but demonstrated a substantial negative relationship with T2DM in fully adjusted models (β=−3.14; 95% CI: −4.55, −1.73).

The impact on grip strength was most pronounced in individuals with BMI < 25 kg/m² (β=−6.72; 95% CI: −10.38, −3.06).

Discussion

Our cross-sectional analysis reveals that T2DM exhibited a significant inverse association with combined grip strength, but showed no significant association with ALMI. Sex-stratified analyses revealed significant reductions in both muscle mass and strength among men with T2DM, whereas no significant associations were observed in women. The relationship between glycemic parameters and muscle characteristics suggests intricate mechanistic pathways linking T2DM and muscle health.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that T2DM accelerates the loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength, particularly in older adults [26, 27]. This muscle deterioration manifests not only as reduced mass but also as impaired quality, crucial factors in maintaining physical function and preventing disability [28]. The association extends to functional outcomes, with T2DM patients showing muscle weakness particularly at the ankle and knee, often correlating with peripheral neuropathy severity [29].

However, the relationship between muscle parameters and T2DM shows considerable heterogeneity across populations [30]. In our study, the initially higher ALMI in T2DM participants reversed after BMI adjustment, suggesting that T2DM patients may have relatively lower muscle mass than expected for their body size. This finding highlights the critical importance of appropriate adjustment for confounding factors when evaluating muscle-related outcomes in T2DM.

The impact of T2DM on muscle parameters demonstrates notable sex-specific patterns. Previous studies have reported that older women with T2DM show accelerated loss of skeletal muscle mass [31], while older men exhibit more pronounced declines in muscle strength [32]. Consistent with these findings, our sex-stratified analyses revealed significant reductions in both muscle mass and strength among men with T2DM, whereas no significant associations were observed in women, regardless of menopausal status. This sexual dimorphism likely reflects underlying differences in hormonal profiles, body composition, and muscle metabolism between sexes.

Our analyses revealed that individuals with BMI < 25 kg/m² exhibited the most substantial decrements in grip strength, suggesting particular vulnerability in normal-weight T2DM patients. This unexpected finding may reflect reduced metabolic reserve or distinct pathophysiological mechanisms in lean individuals compared to their overweight counterparts. Previous studies have identified metabolically obese but normal weight individuals, who exhibit insulin resistance and visceral fat accumulation despite normal BMI, potentially contributing to muscle dysfunction and strength decline [33, 34].

The bidirectional relationship between muscle mass and glucose regulation is well-established, with extensive evidence demonstrating that greater muscle mass improves insulin sensitivity and reduces diabetes risk [35, 36]. Our results show distinct patterns: serum glucose levels correlate inversely with ALMI, while elevated HbA1c is associated with higher ALMI in non-diabetic individuals—an association not observed in those with T2DM. The observed positive association between HbA1c and ALMI in non-diabetic individuals may reflect compensatory muscle hypertrophy in response to early insulin resistance, as skeletal muscle serves as a primary site for glucose disposal. Alternatively, this could represent a prediabetic state where mild hyperglycemia stimulates muscle protein synthesis before the onset of overt diabetes-related muscle atrophy.

The pathophysiology may involve a complex interplay of mechanisms. Insulin resistance impairs proteostasis by concurrently suppressing muscle protein synthesis and accelerating proteolysis [37], while chronic low-grade inflammation triggers proteolytic pathways that promote muscle catabolism [38]. Concurrently, metabolic inflexibility in T2DM compromises fatty acid β-oxidation, resulting in ectopic lipid deposition that exacerbates muscle dysfunction [39]. Additionally, the pathophysiology of diabetic complications involves interconnected inflammatory networks that transcend individual organ systems [40, 41]. Our findings may have critical implications for early intervention strategies. First, the marked deficits in muscle mass and strength observed in men with T2DM highlight the necessity of sex-specific screening protocols, including routine assessments of muscle mass and grip strength in middle-aged diabetic men, to enable early sarcopenia detection. Second, the absence of significant associations in women, irrespective of menopausal status, underscores the need to avoid overgeneralization in clinical guidelines. Third, the pronounced vulnerability of individuals with a BMI < 25 kg/m² suggests that lean T2DM patients may require prioritized interventions, such as resistance training and tailored nutritional support, to counteract muscle deterioration. Incorporating these targeted measures into diabetes management may mitigate functional decline and enhance long-term outcomes.

The study’s primary strength lies in its utilization of the NHANES, a database characterized by standardized protocols and stringent quality control measures. Additionally, our strategic focus on middle-aged adults aged 40–59 years addresses a critical knowledge gap in the extant literature, as previous investigations have predominantly examined elderly populations. However, several methodological constraints warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design inherently limits causal inference between T2DM and muscle parameters, underscoring the necessity for prospective longitudinal investigations. Second, our reliance on HbA1c measurements and physician-documented diagnoses for T2DM classification may have introduced potential classification bias, particularly regarding undiagnosed cases or individuals meeting alternative diagnostic criteria. Third, the NHANES database did not differentiate between diabetes mellitus subtypes. To minimize the potential inclusion of participants with Type 1 diabetes mellitus, we adopted an established methodological approach from previous studies by excluding individuals with diabetes onset prior to 30 years of age [42, 43]. Fourth, the study did not account for diabetes duration, medication use, or the presence of diabetic complications, which may influence muscle mass and strength outcomes. Fifth, while the inclusion of both ALMI and BMI in the same model may introduce conceptual overlap given their shared body size adjustment, future studies should explore alternative methods to better delineate their independent effects.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that T2DM is independently associated with reduced grip strength in middle-aged adults, with particularly pronounced effects in men and individuals with BMI < 25 kg/m². By focusing on these at-risk subgroups, healthcare providers may delay functional decline and improve long-term outcomes in T2DM. Future longitudinal studies are needed to validate these targeted approaches.
