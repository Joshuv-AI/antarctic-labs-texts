Randomized clinical trial for pop titrated versus the slow coagulation cyclophotocoagulation in treating dark irises neovascular glaucoma
Authors: Weerawat Kiddee, Kaneungnit Kittigoonpaisan, Sakhanit Leelaprasasne, Arunee Tanjana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo compare the efficacy and safety of transscleral cyclophotocoagulation (TSCPC) using the pop-titrated (PT-TSCPC) versus the slow-coagulation (SC-TSCPC) energy delivery technique in the treatment of neovascular glaucoma (NVG) patients with dark irises. Randomized clinical trial. Ninety-seven Asian NVG patients. Forty-eight and 49 eyes were randomized to receive PT-TSCPC (1,500-2,500 mW for 2 s) and SC-TSCPC (1,250 mW for 4 s), respectively. Patients were followed up for five years. The success rate, the response rate, the retreatment rate, the cyclodiode efficacy index, the numerical pain rating scale (NPRS), visual acuity (VA), and complications. The preoperative parameters were comparable between both groups. The SC technique required 99.7 ± 35 joules, whereas the PT technique needed 78.3 ± 18.2 joules, P < 0.001). The PT method caused more eye pain during treatment than the SC method (NPRS; 6 vs. 1.8, P < 0.001). The mean intraocular pressure (IOP) at the last visit was 18.8 ± 5.3 mm Hg in the PT-TSCPC group (P < 0.001; 45% reduction from the baseline level) and 15.5 ± 6.7 mm Hg in the SC-TSCPC group (P < 0.001; 56% reduction), P = 0.02). The success rates were 60.4% in the PT-TSCPC group and 71.4% in the SC-TSCPC group (P = 0.14). The response rates were 85.4% and 79.6% for the PT- and SC-TSCPC groups, respectively (P = 0.46). The cumulative probability of success at 5-year follow-up was 48.6% and 69.3% in the PT- and SC-TSCPC groups, respectively (P = 0.03). The SC-TSCPC group required > 1 laser session than the pop-titrated technique (22.4% vs. 6.3%, P = 0.002). VA was worsening in 54.2% of PT-treated eyes and 51% of SC-treated eyes (P = 0.13). Worsening of intraocular inflammation (HR 2.63; 95%CI,1.11–6.26; P = 0.028), PT technique (HR 2.17; 95% CI,1.01–4.71; P = 0.048), male gender (HR 2.13; 95% CI,1.01–4.55; P = 0.048), were the risk factors for TSCPC failure in NVG. The slow-coagulation TSCPC may achieve more efficacious IOP reduction and has better safety profiles than the pop-titrated technique for treating NVG patients with dark irises. Nevertheless, it requires more laser sessions and energy than the power titrating protocol.Trial registration Clinicaltrials.gov Identifier NCT01774227 (23/01/2013). Introduction

Neovascular glaucoma (NVG) remains the leading cause of refractory glaucoma in developing countries1. Diode transscleral cyclophotocoagulation (TSCPC) has become the procedure of choice for patients who are unsuitable for or have failed trabeculectomy and glaucoma drainage device implantation2. Although the laser equipment is easy to access even in a limited resources setting without access to a micropulse laser, complications, including intraocular inflammation, hypotony, phthisis bulbi, and loss of vision, have limited the use of TSCPC3,4. Various factors have been reported to affect the outcome of TSCPC treatment, including the underlying cause of glaucoma, the type of laser, the degree of iris pigmentation, the laser parameters, and the total applied energy5–7.

There are two main approaches to delivering the non-micropulse diode laser energy: the energy titrating method (pop-titrated; PT-TSCPC) and the fixed energy method. The fixed power techniques are sub-categorized into the fixed high-energy and the fixed-low energy (Gassterland’s slow-coagulation; SC-TSCPC) method8. In the PT-TSCPC method, the power is titrated upward or downward according to whether there are audible “pops” during the treatment (the power settings of 1.5–2.5 W, 2-second duration)9. The fixed high-energy delivers high power in a short duration (2–2.25 W for 2 s)10,11. The SC-TSCPC utilizes lower power and delivers a longer continuous duration (1.25 W for 4 s) for eyes with a heavily pigmented iris7,12–14. Although the TSCPC has been used to treat NVG for many years, no standard laser delivery technique exists for the optimum treatment protocol15. Studies that evaluated the effect of the energy delivery technique of TSCPC were retrospective6,12–14,16, prospective non-comparative studies9,17,18, and a non-randomized case-control study10. The only study is a small, short-term, randomized pilot study18. The previous articles included cases with non-uniform underlying glaucoma causes, and the follow-up period was relatively short. A recent meta-analysis of cyclodestructive procedures, including TSCPC, stated that a well-designed long-term study is needed to elucidate the effect of different treatment protocols15.

This randomized clinical trial evaluated which laser energy delivery method provides the optimal coagulation dose needed to effectively lower long-term intraocular pressure (IOP) while reducing procedure risks for a specific type of glaucoma. We compared the 5-year efficacy and safety of diode TSCPC using the pop-titrated versus the slow-coagulation energy delivery technique to treat NVG in Asian patients with dark irises.

Materials and methods

Study design

A single-center, 60-month, assessor-masked, randomized study was carried out at the Department of Ophthalmology, Prince of Songkla University, Thailand, from October 2013 to August 2023. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. It was performed according to the principles of Good Clinical Practice and the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement. The institutional review board of Prince of Songkla University approved the study. All patients provided written informed consent before participation in the study. The trial was prospectively registered at the Clinical Trials Registry, NCT01774227 (23/01/2013).

Participant selection

One hundred and twelve patients aged between 18 and 85 were assessed for eligibility. The inclusion criteria were the NVG cases with a high probability of failure or previously failed trabeculectomy with mitomycin-C and/or drainage implants, who refused to undergo more aggressive intraocular surgery. The best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in the study eye was counting fingers or better. We excluded the patients with one functional eye or who had previously been treated by the cyclodestructive procedure. Only one eye of eligible participants was included. The underlying cause of NVG, history of receiving pan-retinal photocoagulation, prior intraocular surgery, trabeculectomy, and glaucoma drainage device implantation, were recorded. Recruited cases underwent baseline assessment of BCVA, IOP measured by calibrated Goldmann applanation tonometer (Haag-Streit AG, Koeniz, Switzerland), and anterior segment examination, including gonioscopy for disease staging. The corneal endothelial cell density measurement (ConfoScan 4, Nidek Inc., Japan) and dilated fundoscopy were performed whenever possible. The number of topical and oral antiglaucoma medications used was recorded. Fixed combinations were noted according to the active medications.

Randomization, allocation, and masking

The statistical coordinator generated randomization using a computer-generated stratified randomization list. Patients were assigned in equal proportions (1:1) into one of two study groups: those treated with the pop-titrated (the PT-TSCPC group) and those treated with the slow-coagulation technique (the SC-TSCPC group). Each eligible participant was assigned a number according to their sequence of enrollment. The allocation was performed using a sealed envelope system. A nurse opened the envelope to assign participants to the intervention in the operating room. Patients and outcomes assessors (KK, SL, AT) were masked to allocate treatment during the follow-up assessment. The single experienced physician who gave the laser treatment (WK) was non-masked.

Laser procedure19

All TSCPC was performed in the operating room using the Oculight SLx diode laser system (Iris Medical Instrument Inc., Mountain View, CA, USA) with the G-probe delivery (IRIDEX Corporation, CA, USA), by a single experienced surgeon (WK). Anesthesia was obtained by topical instillation of tetracaine hydrochloride 0.5% (Alcon Laboratories Inc., Fort Worth, TX) and a retrobulbar injection of a 3–5 mL mixture of lidocaine 2% with 1:80,000 epinephrine (AstraZeneca Inc., Cambridge, UK) plus bupivacaine 0.5% (AstraZeneca Inc., Cambridge, UK). A lid speculum was then placed. The footplate of the G-probe handpiece was aligned to center the fiberoptic tip 1.2 mm from the corneoscleral limbus. Each subsequent application is spaced by one-half the width of the footplate. Seventeen to 19 applications were placed over 270 degrees, avoiding the 3 and 9 o’clock meridians. The laser parameters were adjusted or fixed during the procedure according to the assigned group.

The PT-TSCPC method used power titrated according to the audible pop. It was started at 1,500 mW for 2 s and increased by 250 mW intervals until a pop was heard (2,500 mW maximal power was allowed). Following this, the power was reduced by 250 mW downward until the pop was no longer audible. The rest of the treatment was completed at these parameters. For the SC-TSCPC group, the laser power was set and fixed at 1,250 mW for 4 s during the treatment7,12,14,22. The post-laser management was performed in the same manner in both groups, which included a week of four times daily topical antibiotic eyedrops (Vigamox, Alcon Pharmaceuticals, Fort Worth, TX) and topical steroid eye drops (1% Pred forte, Allergan USA, Inc. Madison, NJ) were instilled every two hours daily for at least four weeks, then tapered over 6–8 weeks as the inflammation subsided. A topical cycloplegic agent (1% Atropine, Alcon Pharmaceuticals, Fort Worth, TX) was administered twice daily for four weeks. Glaucoma medications, including an oral carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, were continued unchanged until the 1-week post-treatment visit. Medication adjustment was permitted, depending on the IOP, and based on the physician’s discretion. A repeated laser procedure, if needed, was performed 3 months after the prior procedure. It was done 270 degrees over the previously treated area. Postoperatively, follow-up visits were scheduled at the 1 week and months 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48, 54, and 60.

Outcome measures

BCVA, IOP, total energy, complications, additional glaucoma procedures, retreatment time with TSCPC, and the number of medications were recorded. Postoperatively, follow-up visits were scheduled at the 1-week and months 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48, 54, and 60. The primary outcome measure was treatment success. The success rate was defined as the proportion of eyes that achieved the desired IOP range of 6–21 mm Hg without oral carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (CAIs), either with or without topical medication. Secondary outcome measures included response rate, retreatment rate, cyclodiode efficacy index, total energy applied (joules), number of treatment sessions, BCVA, pain score according to the numerical pain rating scale (NPSR) while receiving laser, and 1-week to 6-month follow-up, and complication. Failure was defined as hypotony, phthisis bulbi, visual acuity of NLP, or needing the oral CAI for IOP control at the last follow-up visit.

The response rate was the proportion of eyes that experienced a reduction in IOP to either below 22 mm Hg or a 30% decrease from the baseline IOP level without using oral CAIs20. This includes eyes with hypotony (IOP < 6 mm Hg). Retreatment rate was the proportion of eyes that required more than one treatment session to achieve the target IOP of less than 22 mm Hg or a 30% reduction from the baseline IOP level20. The cyclodiode efficacy index was the response rate ratio to the mean number of treatment sessions21. It provides a quantitative measure of the effectiveness of the treatments in achieving the desired IOP reduction. A higher index value indicates that the treatment is more effective in producing a positive response per treatment session on average. Conversely, a lower index value suggests that the treatment may be less effective or require more sessions to achieve the desired response.

Sample size calculation and statistical analyses

The aim was to compare the success rate of the two energy delivery protocols. Given the categorical variable with independent data collection, the following formula was applied: N= [2(Z1−α/2- Z 1−β)2 p (1-p)]/d2. Taking a reported success rate of 79% from the study in pigmented Asian Indian eyes by Kaushik et al., a difference of at least 25% is significant20. A study fixing the level of significance as 5% and the power to 80% required a minimum of 42 participants, which was calculated from [2 × (1.96 + 0.84)2 × 79 × (100 − 79)]/252. Thus, allowing for a 20% dropout rate at 60 months, we aimed for 50 participants per group.

The statistical analyses were conducted based on intention to treat and were done at the end of the study. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics and baseline ocular characteristics. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s Chi-square test. Fisher’s exact test was used for the comparison of proportions. Paired and independent t-tests were used to evaluate within-group and between-group mean differences. Non-parametric data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney test. The cumulative probability of success was assessed using the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with a log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis determines the likelihood of laser failure. The analyses were performed with R Statistical Software (version 3.3.0; Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and MedCalc statistical software (version 13.0.2; MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium). P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics and ocular parameters

Figure 1 listed the CONSORT flow of patients throughout the study. Of the initial 48 eyes in the PT-TSCPC group, 39 (81.3%) completed the 60-month follow-up, while out of the initial 49 eyes in the SC-TSCPC group, 43 eyes (87.8%) were completed. Demographic and baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Diabetic retinopathy (47.4%) was the most common underlying cause of NVG in both groups, followed by central retinal vein occlusion (23.7%) and ocular ischemic syndrome (11.3%). The mean initial IOP was slightly higher in the SC-TSCPC group (35.2 mm Hg) compared to the PT-TSCPC group (34.2 mm Hg), but the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.47). Seven eyes (14.6%) in the PT-TSCPC group and six eyes (12.2%) in the SC-TSCPC group had prior failed trabeculectomy or glaucoma drainage device implantation (P = 0.78). The PT-TSCPC group had a slightly higher mean corneal endothelial cell density than the SC-TSCPC group (P = 0.05). The SC-TSCPC group had higher energy applied during the initial cyclophotocoagulation session, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.001). There was no significant difference in age, gender, mean BCVA, duration of follow-up, and underlying causes of neovascular between the two groups (Table 1).

Intraocular pressure and antiglaucoma medication

Both treatment groups showed a high percentage of eyes with a significant IOP reduction of at least 30% from baseline [85.4% and 81.6% for the PT- and SC-TSCPC groups, respectively (P = 0.62)]. Twenty-nine eyes (60.4%) in the PT-TSCPC group and 34 (69.4%) in the SC-TSCPC group had a final IOP of 6–21 mm Hg, P = 0.36. The mean IOP at the last visit was 18.8 ± 5.3 mm Hg in the PT-TSCPC group (P < 0.001; 45% reduction from the baseline level) and 15.5 ± 6.7 mm Hg in the SC-TSCPC group (P < 0.001; 56% reduction), P = 0.02). 10% of eyes in the PT-TSCPC group and 18% in the SC-TSCPC still had an IOP of > 21 mm Hg at the end of follow-up, P = 0.88.

Figure 2a showed the IOP reduction between groups at each follow-up visit. At the 3- and 6-month visits, the PT-TSCPC group had a significantly lower IOP than the SC-TSCPC group. However, at the last three follow-up visits, at 48-, 54-, and 60-month visits, the SC-TSCPC group had a significantly lower IOP level (Table 3). Topical antiglaucoma was significantly reduced throughout the follow-up period compared with the baseline in both groups. The mean number of eyedrops was slightly higher in the SC-TSCPC group than in the PT-TSCPC group at 3 through 36 months of follow-up, but the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 2b). Only 5% of eyes in both groups required oral CAI to control IOP at the 5-year visit, P = 0.92.

VA outcomes

The mean logMAR VA in the PT-TSCPC group changed from 1.2 ± 0.6 at baseline to 1.5 ± 0.5 at the last follow-up. In comparison, it changed from 1.2 ± 0.5 to 1.4 ± 0.6 in the SC-TSCPC group. The change was statistically insignificant within and between groups (Table 2).

VA remained stable or improved in 45.8% of PT-treated eyes and 49% of SC-treated eyes (P = 0.09).

The success of treatment, the retreatment rate, and the cyclodiode efficacy index

The success rates were 60.4% in the PT-TSCPC group and 71.4% in the SC-TSCPC group (P = 0.14). Both treatments showed high response rates for managing IOP without oral CAIs, which were 85.4% and 79.6% for the PT- and SC-TSCPC groups, respectively (P = 0.46). The success and the response rates did not significantly differ between groups. Eyes in the PT-TSCPC group had an average of 1.1 treatment sessions, while eyes in the SC-TSCPC group had an average of 1.3. The difference in the number of treatment sessions between the groups was statistically significant (P = 0.03). The lower retreatment rate in the PT-TSCPC group (6.3% vs. 22.4% in the SC-TSCPC group, P = 0.002) suggested that the PT-TSCPC technique required fewer retreatment sessions to maintain the target IOP. The mean time of undergoing a repeat TSCPC was 22.3 ± 11.9 weeks (range, 16–36 weeks) from the first procedure in the PT-TSCPC and 20.6 ± 7.0 weeks (range, 12–35 weeks) in the SC-TSCPC group (P = 0.82). The cyclodiode efficacy index of the PT-TSCPC group was 0.78, meaning that 78% of patients in the pop technique achieved satisfactory IOP control following a single treatment session. Meanwhile, 61% of the SC-TSCPC group reached this target, P = 0.01 (Table 2). While SC-TSCPC was more effective in IOP reduction, it required more laser sessions and higher laser energy than PT-TSCPC.

Safety outcomes

Pain score

Patients who underwent PT-TSCPC reported higher eye pain during treatment, with scores of 6 compared to 1.8 in the SC-TSCPC group (P < 0.001, Table 4). This indicated increased discomfort associated with the PT technique. However, both groups had low pain levels in follow-up visits and remained stable for up to six months post-laser.

Risk factors for laser failure

Treatment failure was defined as the eye with hypotony, phthisis bulbi, visual acuity of NLP, or needing the oral CAI for IOP control at the last follow-up visit. Failure criteria were met in 16.7% of PT-treated eyes and 12.2% of SC-treated eyes (P = 0.58). The cumulative probability of success at the 5-year follow-up was higher in the SC-TSCPC group compared to the PT-TSCPC group, 69.3% versus 48.6%, respectively (P = 0.03), Fig. 3. Table 6 provided a regression analysis to determine the likelihood of laser failure. Worsening of intraocular inflammation after receiving laser (HR 2.63; 95%CI,1.11–6.26; P = 0.028), PT technique (HR 2.17; 95% CI,1.01–4.71; P = 0.048), and male gender (HR 2.13; 95% CI,1.01–4.55; P = 0.048), were the significant risk factors for TSCPC failure in NVG according to the multivariable-adjusted hazard ratio.

Discussion

The treatment of NVG includes IOP-lowering therapy and controlling retinal ischemia. It is usually refractory to medical treatment, and additional interventions, including TSCPC, are often required to control IOP. Cyclophotocoagulation aims to reduce the IOP while minimizing collateral damage to adjacent tissues. The underlying cause of refractory glaucoma, the degree of iris pigmentation, the laser technique related to success, and serious complications such as hypotony and phthisis15,22. A fixed lower amount of laser energy delivered over a more extended period possibly decreases the risk of ciliary body damage and associated postoperative complications, especially in cases with heavy iris pigmentation23.

The efficacious outcomes of cyclophotocoagulation have been reported in various aspects. Khodeiry et al. retrospectively studied the NVG patients who underwent slow-coagulation TSCPC (1,250 mW for 4 s) with a follow-up of 2 years without the comparative group. The mean reduction in IOP was 22.3 ± 13.9 mm Hg at 2 years, 53% from baseline13. 85% of patients had a ≥ 20% reduction of IOP, and 72% had a final IOP < 21 mm Hg. The proportion of eyes in the study that had IOP < 21 mm Hg after the slow coagulation treatment was 69%. They found that with slow coagulation, IOP decreased from 40.7 ± 8.6 mm Hg preoperatively to 18.4 ± 12.2 mm Hg postoperatively (P < 0.001). The mean number of glaucoma medications significantly reduced from 3.3 ± 1.1 at baseline to 2.0 ± 1.5 at the final visit, with an overall mean change of − 1.4 ± 1.5 (− 41.7%) from the pre-TSCPC value.

Compared to our results in the SC-TSCPC group, the preoperative mean IOP was 35.2 ± 6.6 mm Hg, which decreased to 15.5 ± 6.7 mm Hg after 5 years. We have found that in our dark iris color, 70% of eyes in the SC-TSCPC group had a final IOP of 6–21 mm Hg. The IOP reduction efficacy in their study was not different from ours, despite the difference in the basis of iris color. Because their research had no comparative group, it was impossible to determine its efficacy with the conventional technique. In our investigation, we compared the effectiveness of two treatment methods for IOP reduction. Initially, up to six months, the PT-TSCPC group showed significantly lower IOP levels. Yet, by the last three follow-ups at 48, 54, and 60 months, the SC-TSCPC group displayed a substantially lower IOP. This shift can likely be explained by the necessity for retreatment, which is typically required later with the fixed low-energy approach.

The 3-month prospective randomized study by Koronis et al. compared the pop-titrated and slow coagulation TSCPC techniques in refractory glaucoma, including NVG18. Baseline IOP decreased from 37.9 ± 12.7 mmHg to 20.3 ± 13.9 mmHg (45.8%reduction) and from 41.2 ± 9.6 mmHg to 21.3 ± 13.4 mmHg (46.3% reduction), in the PT and SC-TSCPC group, respectively. We have found that the mean IOP at the last visit was 18.8 ± 5.3 mm Hg in the PT-TSCPC group (45% reduction) and 15.5 ± 6.7 mm Hg in the SC-TSCPC group (56% reduction). The slow coagulation technique in our study seemed to result in more IOP reduction. The difference in the study population and the high cumulative laser energy used in the slow coagulation technique in our study might have caused this effect. Throughout the follow-up period, there was a significant reduction in the number of topical antiglaucoma medications compared with the baseline in both groups in our study. The preoperative number of glaucoma medications in the PT-TSCPC group was 3.5 ± 0.5 and 1.3 ± 1.1 post-laser (P < 0.001). The reduction of the preoperative number of glaucoma medications in the SC-TSCPC group was 3.4 ± 0.5 to 1.2 ± 1.3 post-treatment at 60 months (P < 0.001). Only 5% of eyes in both groups required oral CAI to control IOP at the 5-year visit.

Previous studies have used both TSCPC algorithms with good results in various kinds of refractory glaucoma. Khodeiry used the slow coagulation TSCPC (1,250 mW for 4 s) in medically uncontrolled pseudophakic glaucoma patients who had no previous glaucoma surgeries24. IOP decreased from 27.5 ± 9.8 mm Hg to 16.1 ± 6.3 mm Hg postoperatively (P < 0.001). The preoperative number of glaucoma medications was 4.1 ± 0.9 and 3.1 ± 1.3 post-TSCPC (P < 0.001). Elhusseiny et al. reviewed adult aphakic eyes with medically uncontrolled glaucoma that underwent slow-coagulation TSCPC14. The success rate (IOP 6–21 mm Hg with ≥ 20% reduction) was 63% a one year. The mean IOP was statistically significant, decreasing from 29.6 ± 5.8 mm Hg with a mean of 3.9 ± 1.0 medications at baseline to a mean of 19.0 ± 6.4 mm Hg with a mean of 2.5 ± 1.2 medications at 12 months. Khodeiry et al. reported the outcomes of slow coagulation TSCPC (power of 1,250 mW for 4 s) in patients with medically uncontrolled silicone oil glaucoma12. The mean IOP significantly decreased from 29.7 ± 9.6 mm Hg to 14.6 ± 6.5 mm Hg at 12 months postoperatively. Glaucoma medications were reduced from 4.2 ± 0.9 to 1.9 ± 1.3 12 months after TSCPC (P < 0.001).

We found that the success rates for achieving the IOP range of 6–21 mm Hg without oral CAIs were 60.4% in the PT-TSCPC group and 71.4% in the SC-TSCPC group. Although the SC-TSCPC group had a numerically higher success rate, the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant. Suggests that both techniques are similarly effective in achieving the desired IOP range in most patients. However, the slightly higher success rate in the SC-TSCPC group may indicate a trend toward better efficacy, even if it is not statistically significant in this study. Additionally, both treatments demonstrated comparable high response rates for managing IOP without oral CAIs, with 85.4% for the PT-TSCPC group and 79.6% for the SC-TSCPC group. It is also worth noting that, other than the success and response rates, factors such as the number of sessions, retreatment rates, and efficacy index may have to be considered. The retreatment rate in our study was significantly lower in the PT-TSCPC group (6.3%) compared to the SC-TSCPC group (22.4%). The cyclodiode efficacy index also detects this. 78% of patients in the pop technique achieved satisfactory IOP control after only a single treatment session. Whereas 61% of the SC-TSCPC group reached this target, in which a higher value indicated a greater sensitivity to treatment. The SC-TSCPC technique might need multiple treatment sessions and more cumulative laser energy to have more sustained long-term outcomes.

We found that the SC-TSCPC technique required more cumulative laser energy than the PT-TSCPC technique to achieve such success. The pop titrating energy technique requires fewer retreatment sessions to maintain the target IOP. The average total energy applied in the PT-TSCPC group was 78.3 joules, while in the SC-TSCPC group, it was 99.7 joules. The difference in the total energy applied between the groups is statistically significant. In the study on Caucasian eyes by Murphy et al., NVG accounted for about 46% of 263 eyes; the number and the energy per burn were titrated and varied from case to case21. The mean total energy delivered was 155.2 J (SD 106.7), and the mean number of cyclodiode treatment sessions was 1.5 (SD 0.9). The mean laser energy delivered was 104.1 ± 37.5 J per session. Mean IOP decreased significantly from 40.7 mm Hg before the therapy to 17.7 mm Hg (SD 10.9) post-treatment, a reduction of 53%. Following cyclodiode, 89% of patients achieved an IOP of less than 22 mm Hg or a greater than 30% drop in IOP. The NVG subgroup’s success and response rates were 72% and 88%, respectively. The retreatment rate was 30%. The cyclodiode efficacy index in treating NVG with standard pop-titration was 0.63. Their study has no report on the results of the slow-coagulation method21. This comparison probably illustrates that non-pigmented eyes require more energy and more retreatments for the same effect if using pop-titration. Duerr et al. performed a retrospective study to compare the outcomes of standard pop-titrated TSCPC (26 eyes) and slow-coagulation TSCPC (52 eyes) for treating glaucoma in Caucasians23. There was a statistically significant difference between the two groups in total energy applied during the TSCPC procedure [slow-coagulation 101.2 J (SD 28.8); standard pop 77.6 J (SD 34); P= 0.002]12. In contrast to our results, they reported no significant differences in the need for a second procedure (slow coagulation, 28.8%; pop-titration, 23.1%; P = 0.59). Less than 30% of all patients required a second TSCPC session or other procedure, with similar rates in both groups. The efficacious results of TSCPC have been reported to vary mainly due to the etiology of refractory glaucoma and the degree of pigmentation of the iris.

Kaushik et al. prospectively studied heavily pigmented Indian eyes with various causes of refractory glaucoma using a standard “pops” protocol with low energy and follow-up for a minimum of 1 year20. The mean energy delivered per eye was 87.8 ± 31.8 J, with a response rate of 88%, success rate of 78%, and 17% rate of retreatment. The mean IOP was reduced by 20.8 mmHg (57.1%) at the end of 1 year, and all patients were free of oral acetazolamide by 6 months following treatment. Fong et al. compared the TSCPC using slow coagulation and conventional techniques in patients with refractory glaucoma in Chinese eyes25. In 1 year, both procedures had similar success rates (IOP 6–21 mmHg without oral CAI) of 41% and 36% in the conventional technique group and slow coagulation group, respectively. Stevenson-Fernandez et al. prospectively compared the efficacy and safety of pop-titrated versus fixed-energy (2,000 mW for 2 s) diode TSCPC for refractory glaucoma in dark iris Mexican-mestizo patients with a follow-up period of 90 days10. Success was defined as IOP 5–22 mmHg, or a 30% reduction from preoperative IOP. No significant differences were found in the postoperative IOP outcome. The therapeutic success rate of eyes with preserved vision was 72% in the pop-titrated group compared to 52% in the fixed-energy control group (P = 0.23). The reduction of antiglaucoma medications was not different, 22% in the pop-titrated group, compared to 32% in the fixed-energy group. The fixed-energy group delivered a total amount of laser energy of 72 J per session/eye, significantly higher than the pop-titrated group (49 J, P< 0.0001)10. The slow coagulation in their study used the fixed higher energy level in a shorter duration than our study (1,250 mW for 4 s). Theoretically, delivering a high amount of laser energy over a short duration causes pain or discomfort during the procedure.

Laser cyclodestructive procedure has been reported to result in various complications, including pain during treatment, a reduction of visual acuity, prolonged inflammation, hyphema, hypotony, phthisis bulbi, atonic pupil, necrotizing scleritis, and sympathetic ophthalmia4. We found that heavily pigmented patients who underwent PT-TSCPC reported eye pain during treatment, with scores of 6 compared to 1.8 in the SC-TSCPC group (P < 0.001). However, both groups had low pain levels in follow-up visits and remained stable for up to six months after treatment. There was a paucity of data comparing pain during treatment between TSCPC techniques in the literature. One study by Duerr et al. also points out that the slow-coagulation group trended toward showing a lower incidence of pain related to the procedure. (P= 0.078)12. They did not report pain scores according to the numerical rating scale. Dull aching pain during hearing “pops” sounds in the PT-TSCPC group might be related to tissue explosion when delivering laser energy in a short duration.

After the 5-year follow-up, VA worsening (defined as a reduction of at least 0.2 log MAR) was found in 54.2% of PT-treated eyes and 51% of SC-treated eyes (P = 0.13). Five (10%) eyes in the PT-TSCPC and 2 (4%) eyes in the SC-TSCPC group had a loss of light perception (LP) vision, whose pretreatment VA was HM. The most common cause of decreased VA in our study was the progression of the underlying cause of NVG. Duerr et al. reported that VA remained better than light perception in patients with light-colored irises in 71% of those treated with slow coagulation and 65% with standard protocol (P= 0.59)12. Additionally, 42% of the patients who received slow coagulation did not experience a significant decrease in VA throughout the follow-up period. The reduction in VA was similar for both treatment groups, which aligns with the results of our study. Khodeiry et al. also found that in the NVG patients who underwent slow-coagulation TSCPC after 2 years, there was no change in the VA at the last visit from baseline VA in 31 (58.5%) eyes13. Seven (13%) patients had a decrease in BCVA at the final follow-up visit. Two (4%) patients had a loss of LP vision, whose pretreatment VA was LP. Rates of VA change after TSCPC vary in the studies due to the various underlying causes of refractory glaucoma. In many cases, the decrease in VA following TSCPC may not be a direct consequence of the laser itself but rather the deterioration of underlying ocular morbidities26.

In our dark iris color population, anterior chamber inflammation was the most common side effect related to the standard protocol. It was the second most common in the slow-burn group. In the PT-TSCPC group, 63% of patients had worsening uveitis activity, while only 16% of patients in the SC-TSCPC group did, which was statistically significant. Fong et al. studied the slow coagulation on the heavily pigmented eye and reported less prolonged anterior chamber inflammation than the standard protocol (P= 0.048)25. Duerr et al. highlighted that the standard pop technique showed a higher incidence of prolonged inflammation (73% vs. 34%; P= 0.002)12. The slow-coagulation technique, using a lower amount of energy applied over a longer duration, is theorized to decrease collateral tissue destruction and inflammation in the anterior chamber7. Eyes with worsening uveitis in our PT-TSCPC group had a longer time for the inflammation to be controlled (more than 3 months) than the slow-coagulation group. Khodeiry et al. reported that AC inflammation occurred in 9.4% of the treated patients using slow coagulation in the early postoperative period, and none had persistent iridocyclitis after the 3-month visit13. The uveitis after the standard protocol was comparable between Caucasian and Asian eyes. However, after the slow coagulation, we found in the hyperpigmented eyes that the incidence of uveitis might be higher than in the caucasian population. The occurrence of CME in our study was not different between the two protocols. This is lower than the results of previous studies, in which the average incidence of chronic uveitis after TSCPC was 10% of the patients13,23. CME complicating TSCPC may develop due to associated postoperative inflammatory processes and the underlying cause of NVG22. All our NVG eyes that have CME were pseudophakic diabetic retinopathy. Aggressive inflammatory control is critical for NVG eyes after TSCPC to reduce sight-affecting excessive cell and flare, as well as CME7.

Permanent destruction of the ciliary body after TSCPC could result in hypotony and phthisis bulbi27. After 5 years, 10% of dark-iris NVG eyes in our standard protocol experienced hypotony, while none in the slow-coagulation group did (P = 0.03). Kaushik et al. found that after using the pop-titration protocol with low total energy delivery per eye in an Asian-Indian population, 9% of refractory glaucoma (including NVG) had hypotony20. Ramli et al. evaluated risk factors associated with hypotony after using the standard TSCPC protocol in East Asian refractory glaucoma patients27. The mean total energy used was 83.3 ± 31.7 J. They found that the incidence of hypotony was 39%, significantly higher than our series27. Khodeiry et al. discovered that 3.8% of eyes with NVG experienced hypotony following slow-coagulation TSCPC13. None of the eyes in that study developed associated clinical complications from hypotony, such as maculopathy, choroidal effusion, or phthisis bulbi. Contradictory, the study in the dark iris Mexican-mestizo patients reported that 13% of eyes developed hypotony10. All belonged to the fixed-energy group (P = 0.048), compared to none from the pop-titrated group. There is no report of phthisis bulbi in either protocol. They hypothesized that the lower risk of hypotony in the pop-titrated eyes may be related to the total lower accumulated energy level used (49 ± 7 vs. 72 J in the fixed-energy group)10. Notably, the slow coagulation in their study used a fixed higher energy level in a shorter duration (2,000 mW for 2 s) compared to our study (1,250 mW for 4 s), in which the energy level was lower and applied in a longer duration per treatment site. The average total energy applied in our PT-TSCPC group was 78.3 J, while in the SC-TSCPC group, it was 99.7 J (P < 0.001). None of the eyes in our SC-TSCPC group had hypotony or phthisis bulbi. We hypothesize that cumulative energy usage might not be the cause but rather the duration of laser energy absorption by the melanin pigment at each contact site. The number of “pops” sounds during the procedure might be related to the occurrence of hypotony. However, we had not evaluated this proposed association in the current study. The higher total energy delivery likely plays a significant role in treatment success11. On the contrary, some studies reported that it is proportionally related to the risks of hypotony and phthisis22. Murphy et al. reported an overall 9.5% hypotony rate associated with high pre-treatment IOP, high energy delivered per session, and neovascular glaucoma (16% had hypotony)21. They suggested that applying lower energy in high pretreatment IOP and NVG may reduce the risk of hypotony. A safer approach might have been to apply a maximum of 60–80 J energy during each treatment session and give multiple treatments at 6–8 week intervals if retreatment was needed21.

The cumulative probability of success was varied in the published literature due to the variety of diagnoses of refractory glaucoma and the population in the study. We reported that the cumulative success probability at the 5-year follow-up was significantly higher in the SC-TSCPC group (69%) than in the PT-TSCPC group (49%). Failure criteria (hypotony, phthisis bulbi, visual acuity of NLP, or needing the oral CAI for IOP control) were met in 16.7% of PT-treated and 12.2% of SC-treated eyes. Khodeiry et al. found that the cumulative probability of success at 2 years was 59% in pseudophakic glaucoma after using slow-coagulation TSCPC without a comparative group24. In another study, Khodeiry used slow-coagulation TSCPC as an initial surgical intervention to treat Caucasian NVG patients with 2 years of follow-up. Treatment failure occurred in 19 eyes. The most common cause for failure was inadequate IOP reduction. The cumulative probability of success was 64% comparable to our study13. According to the multivariable-adjusted hazard ratio, we determined that worsening of uveitis after the laser, the standard protocol, and male gender were the significant risk factors for TSCPC failure in NVG, particularly in dark-colored eyes. Treatment failure was associated with male sex and low mean energy per treatment session in the previous study using the standard protocol21. In a study using the slow coagulation by Khodeiry, no predictive factors of laser treatment failure could be identified, including baseline demographics, age, race, gender, baseline IOP, previous ocular procedures, or the number of laser spots13. There is still a lack of evidence to determine the risk of laser failure in NVG patients. We could only point out the potential challenges in achieving successful outcomes in specific patient groups with refractory NVG, such as those with male gender receiving the pop-titrating protocol and having worsening anterior uveitis for more than 3 months after the procedure. We underscore the importance of individualized treatment approaches based on patient characteristics.

The present study is the first randomized trial that compares the efficacy and safety of diode TSCPC using the pop-titrated versus the slow-coagulation energy delivery technique to treat NVG in patients with dark irises over a five-year follow-up. There are several limitations. Our study population was restricted to dark-colored iris patients. Results cannot be directly applied to dissimilar patient groups. The patients and outcome assessors were masked to the randomized treatment assignment. Still, the physician who decided to retreat TSCPC and to adjust the antiglaucoma medication was not masked to the treatment group. This might be a source of bias. Further studies are warranted to investigate the utility of TSCPC using various protocols, especially in high-risk patients. Therefore, the choice of laser parameters and technique could be tailored based on the iris pigmentation and risks of failure.

In conclusion, the slow-coagulation TSCPC may achieve more efficacious IOP reduction and has better safety profiles than the pop-titrated technique for treating NVG patients with dark irises. Nevertheless, it requires more laser sessions and cumulative energy than the power titrating protocol. Treating male NVG patients who have dark iris color with a titrating protocol should be done with caution, especially if they develop worsening eye inflammation after the laser. Clinicians must carefully weigh these factors when deciding on the appropriate laser treatment approach for individual glaucoma patients.
