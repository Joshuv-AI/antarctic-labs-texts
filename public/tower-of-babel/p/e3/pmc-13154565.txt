Exploring psychological distress, appraisal, and coping during the scan result journey: a qualitative study of young and middle-aged Chinese adults with advanced cancer
Authors: Xingru Li, Youmin Lu, Yifan Fan, Wenguang xie, Chao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundScan-related distress is common in patients with advanced cancer. Young and middle-aged Chinese patients influenced by Confucian culture and identity stages may experience unique psychological distress following the scans.AimTo investigate the psychological distress and coping strategies in young and middle-aged Chinese patients with advanced cancer during the period from the scan result waiting phase to disclosure.MethodsA descriptive qualitative method guided by Lazarus and Folkman’s stress and coping theory was used. Nineteen young and middle-aged patients with advanced cancer participated in semi-structured interviews, and data were analyzed using direct content analysis.ResultsThe scan results journey was perceived as a threat by patients, generating various emotional distress, including “waiting anxiety,” confusion regarding imaging reports, and acute or chronic reactions. Resource assessment involves a tendency toward self-reliance, ambivalence toward family support, and a perception of the internet as an accessible yet untrustworthy resource. Ultimately, both emotion-oriented coping strategies, such as spiritual coping, cognitive reframing, and emotional suppression, and problem-oriented approaches, including seeking explanations and repeated confirmation, deliberately distancing themselves from result disclosure, and surrendering autonomy in scan management, were employed to alleviate psychological distress.ConclusionUnder the influence of Confucian philosophy and social identity, young and middle-aged patients demonstrated self-resilience in the scan results journey. While some coping strategies alleviated emotional distress in the short term, their prolonged use risks suppressing negative emotions and delaying treatment. Future initiatives should prioritize the early identification of scan-related emotional distress and the development of culturally sensitive psychological support interventions.

Introduction

China reported about 4,824,700 new cancer cases and 2,574,200 new cancer deaths in 2022 [1], with the number of advanced cancer patients increasing rapidly [2]. According to reports, 49% of patients experience moderate to severe fear of recurrence, and managing this fear remains an unmet need among cancer survivors [3]. Although imaging is an essential component of cancer surveillance, it can also lead to psychological distress, including fear of recurrence, stress, and anxiety [4]. The term “scanxiety” is often used to describe this scanning-related psychological distress [5]. It has a multidimensional nature, encompassing anxiety associated with waiting, fear of invasive procedures, and worries about further treatment and a limited lifespan [6, 7]. Scan anxiety is particularly evident among younger patients or those who lack adequate information and social support [8, 9].

Currently, most studies emphasize the psychological distress caused by the scanning procedure in cancer patients, which is influenced by the scanning environment, maladaptation of contrast agents, and lack of communication [10–12]. However, regular scans cause more psychological distress when preparing results [13] because the outcome may indicate the failure of the current treatment and the proximity of death [7]. Shah et al. [8] reported that 70% of cancer patients experience scan-related anxiety, which peaks during the waiting period for results. Similarly, in the study of Seibel et al. [14], patients with lung cancer reported anxiety triggered by waiting for follow-up scan results. Beyond anxiety during the waiting period, the results disclosure was described as “stressed” [15]. Chau [16] reported that cancer patients experienced increased anxiety when receiving abnormal results through patient portals. Therefore, distinguishing whether outcome-induced psychological distress or the scan itself is a future concern [17].

Distress is a multifactorial, unpleasant emotional experience of a psychological, social, spiritual, and physical nature that may interfere with coping ability [18]. Bui et al. [10] explored coping styles during computed tomography (CT) scans in patients with advanced cancer aged 46–82, including rationalization, seeking professional help, and alcohol consumption. In the report by Boele et al. [19], patients with primary brain tumors aged 40–75 adopted downward comparison, imagination, and avoidance strategies during MRI interval scans. In the study by Bellhouse et al. [20], visual imagery and relaxation techniques were the primary coping strategies employed by patients with lung cancer aged 54–89 during MRI scans. Existing research has primarily focused on scan-related distress within specific cancer populations (e.g., lung or brain cancer) and among middle-aged and elderly patients in Western cultures. Few studies have explored the psychological distress associated with preparing scan results among young Chinese patients with advanced cancer.

In China, the core tenets of Confucian culture, such as collectivism and filial piety, profoundly shape individuals’ cognitive, emotional, and behavioral patterns [21]. This influence may be particularly pronounced among young and middle-aged cancer patients. The primary developmental tasks for young and middle-aged adults are establishing careers, starting families, and fulfilling filial duties [22]. They tend to perceive themselves as “givers” and “protectors” for their families, rather than “takers” or “those who need protection.” When facing scan-related distress, their motivation to maintain an image of strength may be even stronger to avoid causing stress to their families. Therefore, influenced by their particular life stage and cultural norms, young and middle-aged cancer patients may exhibit differences in psychological distress and coping strategies compared to patients of other age groups [23, 24].

Lazarus and Folkman’s stress and coping theory [25] comprises stressors (referring to scan results in this study), cognitive assessments, and coping strategies. Cognitive assessment includes primary appraisal (i.e., determining whether the situation threatens oneself) and secondary appraisal (i.e., determining whether one has the resources to respond to the threat). The coping strategies include problem-oriented coping and emotion-oriented coping. The theory provides a framework for understanding how psychological distress (primary appraisal) during the scan results journey in young and middle-aged patients with advanced cancer, along with their evaluation of resources (secondary appraisal), contributes to the development of coping strategies.

Given above, the objective of this study was to explore the commonality in psychological distress and coping strategies in young and middle-aged Chinese patients with advanced cancer based on Lazarus and Folkman’s [25] stress and coping theory during the scan results journey, which represents the first investigation conducted within the Chinese cultural context. The research was guided by the following question: What is the commonality of psychological distress experienced by young and middle-aged Chinese adults with advanced cancer during the scan results journey, and how do they cope with it? It is hoped that the findings can help health providers to develop culturally sensitive, psychologically supportive protocols addressing scan-related distress across various cancer types.

Methods

Design

A descriptive qualitative design with semi-structured in-depth interviews was conducted, adhering to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [26]. This design allows us to understand and describe the psychological distress and coping styles related to scan results in young and middle-aged advanced cancer patients. Lazarus and Folkman’s [25] stress and coping theory was used as the theoretical framework to guide the interview outline and data analysis. The interview outline is shown in Table 1.

Study setting and recruitment

This study was conducted from December 2024 to February 2025 in the oncology departments of a teaching hospital in Anhui Province. A stratified purposive sampling method was used, enabling the findings to extend beyond specific cancer diagnoses and to be transferred across different cancer types. The researchers first recruited approximately 3–4 participants from the main advanced cancer categories in China (gastrointestinal, thoracic, head and neck, and pelvic tumors) to ensure diversity.

The criteria for qualified patients were young and middle-aged adults aged 18–59, diagnosed with advanced cancer within one year, and CT/MRI recipients within 4 months. Patients who underwent scans to diagnose were excluded. This study focuses on the 18–59 age group, as individuals in this range are in a developmental stage marked by career establishment, family formation, and fulfillment of filial responsibilities, and may therefore face distinct psychological distress and coping patterns.

Data collection

The first author contacted the corresponding nurses from oncology departments. The corresponding nurses were informed of the study’s purpose, content, and potential benefits, and then identified patients who were interested in the study. The patients’ contact details were provided to the first author, who contacted potential participants by phone, explaining the research and privacy protections. After obtaining verbal consent from the participants, they agreed on a suitable time to sign the informed consent form. After that, the first and third authors jointly evaluated the interview environment and ultimately chose a quiet room as the interview location. Then, the first researcher evaluated the recording device (a mobile phone) and extracted patients’ basic information from the hospital system. The face-to-face interviews were conducted by the first author and supervised by the third author. They recorded the patients’ facial expressions and body movements during the interview. Data saturation was determined through concurrent analysis and discussion, whereby no new themes emerged from the final three consecutive interviews. Each interview lasted 40–60 min. After all the interviews were completed, they were transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service.

Data analysis

All interviews were anonymized and stored on password-protected computers. NVivo software was utilized for code management. A directed content analysis approach was employed to analyze the data [27]. Transcriptions were initially read repeatedly by the first and second authors. At this stage, the two authors need to record their preliminary thoughts. Using Lazarus and Folkman’s (1984) stress and coping theory [25] as a framework, primary appraisal, secondary appraisal, and coping strategies were established as initial coding categories. Two researchers (LXR and LYM) independently identified meaningful text segments relevant to these categories and met regularly to discuss their coding. Subsequently, all codes under each category were clustered and analyzed to identify sub-themes reflecting specific experiences within each dimension. At this stage, the researchers focused on extracting recurrent and universal core patterns across participants. Concerns specific to particular diagnoses were not developed into independent themes. Then, the researcher discussed the differences between the coding and the corresponding theme. Any disagreements or amendments are resolved and updated through group discussion. Considering the uncertainty of the disease in advanced cancer patients, the final theme was not returned to the participants for confirmation.

Rigor and reflexivity

This study followed the strategy of ensuring the credibility of qualitative research [28]. Repeated immersion of the researcher in the transcript increased credibility; peer participation in supervision and discussion increased dependability; the combination of interview records with field records increased confirmability; and the maximum use of the patient’s original language for description increased transferability.

The first and fourth authors, doctoral students in nursing, possess extensive experience in qualitative research. The first three authors have clinical experience in caring for cancer patients, which informed the data analysis but may also have introduced potential over-interpretation bias. The fifth author, a nursing administrator, may have prioritized the feasibility of nursing interventions. To mitigate these potential biases, open-ended questions were employed, and participants’ original expressions were preserved as much as possible. All researchers need to reflect on their identities during the analysis.

Findings

Theme 1: primary appraisal-perceived psychological distress during the scan results journey

Psychological distress, as a direct product of primary appraisal (the appraisal of the situation as threatening), underwent a dynamic evolution from the waiting phase to results disclosure.

Sub-theme 1: pre-disclosure distress: “Waiting anxiety”

Despite completing the scan, the subsequent waiting period was described as uncertainty and anxiety. Even with relatively short turnaround times (1-2 days), the desire for immediate results was intense. For many, the distress of waiting was greater than that of receiving bad news.

“Waiting for the result made me feel anxious because no one can be sure of the result, and it will make me a little scared, nervous, just nervous.”[P17].

“Waiting for the result made me feel anxious because no one can be sure of the result, and it will make me a little scared, nervous, just nervous.”[P17].

“The waiting process is more anxious than receiving the result, and it felt like a black hole. I finished it yesterday. It is said that the report can be picked up after 10 o’clock today. Some patients said that they (scan results) can be found on their mobile phones in 2-3 hours. Anyway, I did not find it.”[P11].

“The waiting process is more anxious than receiving the result, and it felt like a black hole. I finished it yesterday. It is said that the report can be picked up after 10 o’clock today. Some patients said that they (scan results) can be found on their mobile phones in 2-3 hours. Anyway, I did not find it.”[P11].

Although the digital delivery of results is efficient, it can be counterproductive. Unexpected notifications may paradoxically exacerbate patients’ anxiety.

“When the official account sent out the (scan result) message prompt, I was very nervous. I thought it would be a few days before I received the results.”[P1].

“When the official account sent out the (scan result) message prompt, I was very nervous. I thought it would be a few days before I received the results.”[P1].

Sub-theme 2: post-disclosure distress: confusion and emotional reactions

When the patient receives the imaging report, the results become the primary source of threat, leading to confusion and acute or chronic reactions.

Confusion about imaging reports

While digital platforms have been acknowledged for enhancing efficiency and convenience, many patients reported confusion when interpreting their medical imaging reports. Limited medical knowledge and low educational levels hinder patients’ understanding of quantitative indicators such as tumor size, which lack additional explanation or comparison to relevant health standards. In some cases, patients expressed uncertainty and powerlessness because they received only an image with a QR code.

“There were quite a few things that I did not understand because the data above was just a number, 0.5cm… without any standards.”[P8].

“There were quite a few things that I did not understand because the data above was just a number, 0.5cm… without any standards.”[P8].

“Once, there was only an image without a report. I could not understand anything that way. Although the doctor told me you were fine and could be discharged, I was unsure of the result. There was only a QR code on it.”[P4].

“Once, there was only an image without a report. I could not understand anything that way. Although the doctor told me you were fine and could be discharged, I was unsure of the result. There was only a QR code on it.”[P4].

Acute and chronic reactions

Emotional breakdowns, tears, and sweating were immediate reactions triggered by unfavorable results. Some even questioned the meaning of treatment, equating a poor result to a “death sentence” [P5] and experiencing regret for past decisions. Beyond the acute emotional reaction, a consistent, chronic psychological distress was evident, such as loss of interest.

“The doctor prescribed Chinese medicine for me and let me take it for half a year to regulate my body. If I listened to him, my illness would not get worse.”[P10].

“The doctor prescribed Chinese medicine for me and let me take it for half a year to regulate my body. If I listened to him, my illness would not get worse.”[P10].

“After my test results came out, I lay in bed for a long time and did not want to do anything.”[P2].

“After my test results came out, I lay in bed for a long time and did not want to do anything.”[P2].

Theme 2: secondary appraisal-evaluation of coping resources

In response to the above psychological distress, patients engaged in a process of secondary appraisal, which is defined as the evaluation of available internal and external resources to manage the perceived threat.

Sub-theme 1: relying on oneself rather than psychological intervention

There was an evident tendency towards self-reliance. The psychological comfort offered by medical staff was viewed as formalism and an ineffective way by patients. They saw coping as an internal, personal journey of “self-redemption” [P1].

“The doctors and nurses are very nice and will encourage me, but these negative emotions can only be resolved on my own. Maybe it works for others, but for me, my mind is not easy to change.”[P7].

“The doctors and nurses are very nice and will encourage me, but these negative emotions can only be resolved on my own. Maybe it works for others, but for me, my mind is not easy to change.”[P7].

Some middle-aged patients, as the backbone of their families, perceive themselves as capable of bearing the burden of scan results on their own. As one 59-year-old breast cancer patient reported:

“I am no longer a child. I can bear any bad results alone.”[P5].

“I am no longer a child. I can bear any bad results alone.”[P5].

Sub-theme 2: family support as a paradoxical resource

When evaluating family as a resource, younger cancer patients (under 30 years old) perceived the companionship of family members as an important source of support. In contrast, some middle-aged patients expressed deep ambivalence. For some patients, particularly those with lower literacy levels, family members are regarded as an important resource for interpreting scan results. However, they also expressed that when family members are exposed to scan results, their emotional vulnerability may render them unable to cope with the associated stress, which further transforms this potential resource into feelings of guilt. A 45-year-old lung cancer patient whose daughter works in another city stated:

“My daughter’s ability to withstand pressure is poorer. If she falls, I will feel ashamed.”[P12].

“My daughter’s ability to withstand pressure is poorer. If she falls, I will feel ashamed.”[P12].

Another 59-year-old male patient expressed concern and shame regarding his wife:

“My wife is my strong spiritual pillar. But I feel like my wife is getting older and under too much pressure.”[P15].

“My wife is my strong spiritual pillar. But I feel like my wife is getting older and under too much pressure.”[P15].

Sub-theme 3: the internet as an accessible but untrustworthy resource

The internet was evaluated by some patients, particularly those around 30 years of age, as a readily accessible yet unreliable resource, as it often exposes them to information overload and even conflicting interpretations. This increased their uncertainty during the interval between receiving the results and consulting their physicians.

“… I didn’t know who was right or wrong. And I still had to wait for several more days before I could see my doctor.”[P1].

“… I didn’t know who was right or wrong. And I still had to wait for several more days before I could see my doctor.”[P1].

Theme 3: coping strategies derived from appraisal

Guided by their perception of psychological distress and the evaluation of available resources, patients ultimately employed both emotion-focused and problem-focused coping strategies to manage their psychological distress.

Sub-theme 1: emotion-oriented coping

These strategies aimed to regulate emotional responses in the face of threat.

Spiritual coping

Some patients prayed to heaven to empower themselves to alleviate “waiting anxiety,” and some adopted a philosophical stance on life and death to find peace and accept their situation.

“I prayed to God that he would give me strength and not let me fall easily.”[P16].

“I prayed to God that he would give me strength and not let me fall easily.”[P16].

“People are destined to die. For those like us who are diagnosed with lung cancer, I have already come to terms with it. At the moment of death, it is like this.”[P3].

“People are destined to die. For those like us who are diagnosed with lung cancer, I have already come to terms with it. At the moment of death, it is like this.”[P3].

Cognitive reframing

When waiting for their results, patients actively employed cognitive strategies to manage anxiety. They primarily used thought suppression to avoid catastrophic thinking and deliberately focused on hopeful outcomes. The downward comparison was also used by patients, who stated that the uncertainty surrounding scan results was insignificant compared to their past experiences of severe pain or major surgeries. Others performed self-reassurance by emphasizing the benefits of scan results for monitoring cancer progression. One patient also expressed the conscious self-suggestion before the results were disclosed.

“Although I was worried, I recalled that I had experienced many more severe things than this, such as surgeries, pain, and hair loss. This (adverse outcomes) would not defeat me.”[P14].

“Although I was worried, I recalled that I had experienced many more severe things than this, such as surgeries, pain, and hair loss. This (adverse outcomes) would not defeat me.”[P14].

“I need to be strong, as the result is something that there is no way to change.”[P11].

“I need to be strong, as the result is something that there is no way to change.”[P11].

In addition, during the waiting period for results, most patients often rationalized their clinicians’ silence; they frequently interpreted silence as implicit reassurance, perceiving the absence of proactive communication as indicative of favorable outcomes. As one patient said, “No notification is the best outcome.” [P6] The unexpected visit and contact of physicians after scanning accelerated the patient’s nervousness because they often associated such sudden interactions with adverse news.

“Once, when they (doctors) came to my ward early in the morning, I felt very nervous and thought there was something wrong. Instead of their visits, I felt at ease.”[P9].

“Once, when they (doctors) came to my ward early in the morning, I felt very nervous and thought there was something wrong. Instead of their visits, I felt at ease.”[P9].

Emotional suppression

As in the secondary assessment, when family is appraised as a source of ambivalent support, some patients often present as strong and calm in front of their loved ones, consciously suppressing their negative emotions. This coping style was used to protect family members from emotional distress and helps maintain family stability and harmony. Some patients expressed that they had been accustomed to this practice of “endurance.” Notably, this tendency toward emotional suppression was more evident among middle-aged male participants. For instance, a 56-year-old male lung cancer patient described how he maintained a facade of “everything as usual” in external settings, particularly in front of family members, to protect the psychological well-being of his children and wife:

“After returning from the hospital, my wife and son played in front of my house. When I went home, I greeted them with a smile. When I was eating, I shed tears alone.”[P3].

“After returning from the hospital, my wife and son played in front of my house. When I went home, I greeted them with a smile. When I was eating, I shed tears alone.”[P3].

In such contexts, some patients turn to diary recording, storing their restrained emotions in a private space, which not only allows them to release their emotions but also helps them maintain a calm appearance for their families.

“I bid farewell to myself. I realized I have negative emotions, but am used to forbearing. I write them (emotions) into my diary, put them in a storage box, and store them in a small corner.”[P17].

“I bid farewell to myself. I realized I have negative emotions, but am used to forbearing. I write them (emotions) into my diary, put them in a storage box, and store them in a small corner.”[P17].

Sub-theme 2: problem-oriented coping

These strategies aimed to manage the problem itself.

Seeking explanation and repeated confirmation

Upon receiving their scan results, some patients promptly seek explanations online to gain an immediate understanding. However, given the unreliability of Internet information, most patients perceive physicians’ additional verbal explanations as a guarantee of their safety. Even when the scan results were favorable, seeking repeated confirmation from their doctors can increase patients’ the stability and certainty.

“I will search online when I get the results, but I am still unsure. I want to find my doctor to tell me the final result in person.”[P1].

“I will search online when I get the results, but I am still unsure. I want to find my doctor to tell me the final result in person.”[P1].

“I checked the results on my mobile phone, and the results improved. I was pleased, but would still take the results to my attending doctor. I repeatedly checked whether I was getting better and better. Although I knew the words on the report, after all, I was a layman. After he told me, my heart would be stable.” [P11].

“I checked the results on my mobile phone, and the results improved. I was pleased, but would still take the results to my attending doctor. I repeatedly checked whether I was getting better and better. Although I knew the words on the report, after all, I was a layman. After he told me, my heart would be stable.” [P11].

This coping strategy reveals that the fundamental problem for patients in the digital age is not information deficiency but rather an absence of psychological safety due to information overload and unreliability.

Deliberate distance from result exposure

Most patients were afraid to face the bad outcomes, which made them suffer from acute and chronic emotional reactions. As such, patients consciously distanced themselves from the negative emotions associated with “bad news.” Some patients who received their reports on the same day intentionally delayed viewing them until the next day, which can allow more time for mental preparation. Others directly turned off mobile notifications around the expected results release time to prevent disruptions to their daily lives. Another more direct form of avoidance is that patients viewed the results themselves as a “burden” and consciously rejected imaging reports. Some cancer patients with a poor prognosis, such as those with brain tumors, hold less hope for treatment outcomes and, as a result, perceive obtaining scan reports as “unimportant” or “indifferent.”

“After I got it, I did not dare to watch it. I always saved it until the next day. I always wondered if it would be …”[P11].

“After I got it, I did not dare to watch it. I always saved it until the next day. I always wondered if it would be …”[P11].

“I do not want to watch it (report). It is useless for me to watch it, but it increases the burden… There are only disadvantages, but no benefits.” [P12].

“I do not want to watch it (report). It is useless for me to watch it, but it increases the burden… There are only disadvantages, but no benefits.” [P12].

While this avoidance strategy may reduce emotional distress in the short term, however, individuals who are indifferent about their scan results may not actively access their reports, and this may potentially affect timely medical decision-making.

Surrendered autonomy in scan management

The fear of adverse results leads patients to abandon their autonomy in future scans. They strictly follow doctor-recommended schedules and prefer that physicians control or reduce the frequency of scans, which can help reduce their risk of encountering “bad news”.

“I always follow the doctors’ arrangements and do not request a check-up because I am afraid… in case of any consequences.” [P1].

“I always follow the doctors’ arrangements and do not request a check-up because I am afraid… in case of any consequences.” [P1].

“Every time I have a check-up, I feel anxious for several days. If there are no special circumstances, I do not think going for a scan is necessary.” [P18].

“Every time I have a check-up, I feel anxious for several days. If there are no special circumstances, I do not think going for a scan is necessary.” [P18].

Similarly, this trust in physicians and relinquishment of personal autonomy may reduce outcome-related anxiety in the short term. However, in the long term, it carries the risk of becoming maladaptive, as prolonged avoidance may delay the initiation of treatment.

Based on the thematic descriptions above, when patients appraised the scan results journey as a threat, several psychological distresses appeared, including “waiting anxiety,” confusion regarding imaging reports, and acute or chronic emotional reactions. Next, patients assessed the resources available (secondary appraisal) and then developed coping strategies. To reduce “waiting anxiety,” patients primarily rely on themselves rather than psychological interventions, adopting strategies such as spiritual coping and cognitive reframing. When facing confusion about the results, patients evaluate family support and the internet as potential resources. However, this process generates feelings of burden and uncertainty, which drive them to seek explanations from healthcare professionals. During the process of coping with acute and chronic reactions induced by the results release, patients often deny the role of psychological support and consider family responsibilities. This leads to emotional suppression, avoidance of exposure to the results, and the transfer of their autonomy to subsequent scans. Fig. 1 describes the dynamic relationship among the themes.

Discussion

This study represents the first application of Lazarus and Folkman’s stress and coping theory [25] to examine the psychological distress and coping process among young and middle-aged Chinese patients with advanced cancer during the scan journey. Patients’ cognitive appraisals appear to be influenced by their developmental stage and cultural norms. Consequently, some external resources, such as professional psychological interventions and, at times, even family support, are perceived as unavailable or inappropriate to utilize, a perception that consequently shapes their adoption of emotion-focused and problem-focused coping strategies.

Although various forms of emotional distress were present during the primary assessment, most patients prefer to rely on themselves and deny the role of psychological interventions from medical staff. The reason may be that patients have limited access to such support in practice, thereby preventing them from recognizing the benefits of psychological assistance [14]. Another possible explanation is that young and middle-aged patients are at a critical life stage, taking on significant familial and professional responsibilities [29], and are expected to possess inherent resilience and self-regulatory capacity, described by participants as “self-salvation.” This expectation potentially contributes to a situation where patients may actively exclude professional psychological interventions from their available resources, even when healthcare providers offer such support. Furthermore, Chinese social norms emphasize collectivism over individualism, where seeking help may be perceived as imposing a burden on others. This cultural notion aligns with participants’ concerns about their family members. Even when family accompaniment and support were acknowledged, patients tended to perceive resources as inappropriate to utilize. In the context of this resource appraisal, spiritual coping, cognitive restructuring, and emotional suppression are further triggered.

Spiritual coping and cognitive restructuring have been proven to improve patients’ health behaviors, promote recovery, and reduce negative emotions [30]. At the same time, long-term reliance on this strategy may cause healthcare workers to ignore patients’ negative emotions, which may be deliberately hidden. In Western cultures, emotional suppression and resilience are primary manifestations of stoicism, associated with negative well-being outcomes [31]. However, in this study, the practice of emotional suppression aligns with the Chinese cultural value of “endurance”, which often represents maturity and social competence [32]. Participants’ narratives, such as “I am accustomed to enduring,” along with their efforts to maintain a calm exterior to protect their families, may illustrate how this cultural norm shapes coping behaviors. Notably, emotional suppression was more evident among middle-aged male cancer patients, which aligns with the findings of Chin et al. [33]. Men are typically associated with independence, strength, and resilience; as the primary providers and protectors of their families, they may be more inclined to suppress their emotions.

Some patients turn to online searching for further explanation after receiving their imaging reports. However, inconsistent and overloaded information increases their uncertainty, which indicates the drawbacks of digital tools and the lack of timely, accessible communication from healthcare providers. While seeking professional medical interpretation represents a problem-focused coping strategy, the repeated confirmation reflects a lack of psychological safety. In the future, the necessity of timely follow-up and the development of communication guidelines should be emphasized [34]. Contrary to actively seeking information and interpretation, some patients avoided contact with their results and requested a reduction in scanning frequency. Such behaviors have also been documented in Green et al. [35], where cancer patients avoided information to form emotional self-protection. However, long-term avoidance behavior may delay necessary medical interventions.

In our study, while some coping strategies alleviated patient distress in the short term, their long-term use increased risks of potential accumulation of negative emotions and treatment delays. According to the study by Skinner et al., the assessment of coping strategies should consider specific situations [36]. For patients who rely on avoidance strategies for a long time, healthcare providers should monitor their emotional distress closely.

Limitations

By design, the participant sample was heterogeneous, including various cancer types. However, the findings reveal common forms of psychological distress and coping that transcend specific diagnoses. Although stratified purposive sampling ensured that the identified themes were transferable across various cancer subgroups, subjective bias may have been introduced during the inclusion and exclusion decisions. To minimize this bias, clear inclusion and exclusion criteria were established through team discussions, and both department contacts and researchers double-checked participant enrollment. Next, given the small sample size and single-center study, the research findings may not be generalizable to other hospitals, where the medical resources may differ. Third, the interviews were conducted after patients received their scan results, which may have introduced recall bias (e.g., weakening the recall of acute emotional reactions). We included patients who had been scanned within 4 months to minimize recall bias. In the future, longitudinal interviews can be conducted at different scanning time points to capture more realistic psychological responses. This study may also have social bias. For example, under social norms that value “endurance” and “toughness,” participants might be more likely to report fewer negative emotions. Therefore, the researchers employed open-ended questions, rapport-building, and assurances of data confidentiality to encourage participants to express themselves honestly. Fourth, we did not consider metastasis and scan types in patients with advanced cancer. However, scan-related distress is unrelated to disease progression [12]. Future research can explore how advanced cancer patients cope with results related to specific scanning procedures. Finally, although our study discussed the influence of Confucian culture and identity stage on coping styles, the research design did not include questions that directly assessed the impact of cultural norms and identity on participants’ psychological distress and coping. Therefore, our interpretation remains an inference about participants’behavior. Future research could explore how cancer patients perceive the role of culture and identity in shaping their coping styles.

Implication

Given the commonality of emotional distress triggered by scan results, healthcare institutions could develop a core protocol for psychological support. This protocol should include rapid identification of psychological distress, pre-scan education on report interpretation, psychological preparation messages before result delivery, and the promotion of culturally adapted cognitive-behavioral interventions based on Chinese cultural contexts. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the gold standard for anxiety [37]. Moreover, by targeting the drivers of scan-related anxiety, such as intolerance of uncertainty and catastrophic thinking about disease progression, CBT serves as an intervention tool specifically designed to address maladaptive thought patterns [38]. Furthermore, patients, influenced by their life stage and cultural norms, tend to rely on themselves, suppress emotions, and employ unstructured cognitive strategies such as the rationalization of silence. Through cultural adaptation, CBT can guide patients toward appropriately seeking support. Finally, healthcare professionals should receive training in psychological support related to scanning, particularly in culturally sensitive communication skills, and integrate psychological interventions into routine workflows.

Conclusion

Young and middle-aged adult patients with advanced cancer experience psychological distress during the results journey. Influenced by the culture and identity stage, participants preferred self-reliance over external support. Some emotion-focused and problem-focused coping strategies can reduce emotional distress in the short term, but may delay treatment in the long run. These findings highlight the need for early recognition of psychological distress related to scan results and the development of culturally sensitive psychological support protocols.
