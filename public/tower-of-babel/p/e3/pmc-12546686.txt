Introduction

Reverse humeral avulsion of the glenohumeral ligament (RHAGL) is a rare cause of posterior shoulder instability. While humeral avulsion of the glenohumeral ligament (HAGL) is commonly linked to anterior instability and represents about 93 % of cases, posterior band involvement is significantly less frequent. This condition has only been intermittently documented in the literature [[1], [2], [3]]. Clinically, RHAGL presents substantial diagnostic challenges, as its nonspecific symptoms frequently mimic other intra-articular pathologies, including labral or rotator cuff injuries. Patients frequently experience posterior shoulder pain, mechanical symptoms, or instability after trauma, typically from dislocation or subluxation with the arm in flexion, adduction, and internal rotation [1,4]. Conventional radiographs often produce unclear results, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may miss lesions unless specifically targeted. Up to 50 % of RHAGL lesions can be overlooked on MRI, with definitive diagnoses typically made via arthroscopic surgery [4,5]. Notably, RHAGL may coexist with an anteroinferior Bankart lesion, recognizing this pattern is essential to guide comprehensive arthroscopic repair.

Excessive tensile force on the posterior band of the inferior glenohumeral ligament (IGHL) can cause it to avulse from the humeral neck, leading to a RHAGL. While RHAGL may occur alone, it often coexists with labral tears or capsular injuries. If untreated, it can result in chronic posterior instability, recurrent subluxations, and persistent pain, significantly affecting shoulder function and the patient's quality of life [1,3,4].

Arthroscopic repair is now considered the gold standard for treating RHAGL. It provides direct visualization and treats associated intra-articular issues with less morbidity compared to open surgery [1,3,6]. However, due to the rarity and diagnostic complexity of RHAGL, published evidence is limited to case reports and small series.

This article presents a rare case of a rotator cuff tear that went undetected on preoperative imaging and was discovered during arthroscopic exploration. We aim to clarify the condition's features, describe arthroscopic repair techniques, and discuss treatment outcomes, emphasizing the need for increased clinical vigilance and offering management recommendations.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist (SCARE 2025) [7].

Presentation of case

A 35-year-old right-handed man came in with pain and limited movement in his left shoulder. He had a motorcycle accident two months before he was admitted, and he got the dislocation after a fall right on his left shoulder. After being treated with reduction and immobilization in a Desault sling for two weeks, he continued to experience pain during overhead activities and mild weakness, with little relief from analgesics and home physical therapy.

At our orthopedic trauma clinic, the examination revealed no deformity, swelling, or bruising. Range of motion (ROM) with significantly increased ROM (external rotation) at 90 degree of abduction comparing to contralateral side. Positive O’Brien's and Biceps Load II tests suggested possible labral or posterior capsular injury, while the Apprehension test was negative for anterior instability.

Radiographs and Computed tomography (CT) showed a congruent glenohumeral joint, but an MRI indicated an anteroinferior labral tear and subtle posterior capsular changes (Fig. 1), with no definite HAGL or RHAGL lesions.

Anteroposterior radiograph (top left image), axial view MRI (top right image) and CT of the left shoulder (bottom image). Red arrow: Anteroinferior labral tear. Yellow arrow: Posterior capsular injury. (For interpretation of the references to colour in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the web version of this article.)

A diagnostic and therapeutic shoulder arthroscopy was conducted due to persistent symptoms and inconclusive imaging, confirming a complete avulsion of the posterior band of the IGHL, leading to a diagnosis of RHAGL, along with treatment for the anteroinferior labral tear.

The patient was under general anesthesia in the right lateral decubitus position, with three arthroscopic portals established: posterior, anterosuperior, and anteroinferior.

Arthroscopic Findings and Surgical Steps:

Step 1: The diagnostic arthroscopy revealed a tear of the anteroinferior labrum from the 4 o'clock to the 6 o'clock positions and no SLAP tear finding (Fig. 2). Further exploration through the anterosuperior portal showed a complete avulsion of the posterior band of the IGHL from its humeral attachment, indicating a RHAGL (Fig. 2). There were no associated bony fragments.

Posterior humeral avulsion of the glenohumeral ligament (RHAGL) identified during arthroscopy. HH: Humeral head. PC: Posterior capsule. IGHL: Posterior band of the Inferior Glenohumeral Ligament. RHAGL: Lesion location. Red arrow: No SLAP tear finding. (For interpretation of the references to colour in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the web version of this article.)

Step 2: The humeral footprint was prepared using the posterior working portal. A shaver and electrocautery debrided the avulsed area and prepared the anatomical insertion site of the posterior IGHL on the humeral neck, ensuring optimal healing conditions.

Step 3: Anchor placement and suture passage. A suture anchor was inserted at the anatomical footprint of the posterior IGHL. Using a Bird Beak suture passer, the sutures were passed through the avulsed ligament and the adjacent posterior capsule (Fig. 3).

Anchor placement at the humeral surgical neck for fixation of the posterior capsule and posterior IGHL (RHAGL repair). HH: Humeral head. PC: Posterior capsule. RHAGL: Posterior humeral avulsion of the glenohumeral ligament lesion.

Step 4: Capsuloligamentous Repair. The sutures were retrieved and tied extracapsularly, securing the posterior IGHL and capsule back to their anatomic position on the humerus.

Step 5: The anteroinferior labral tear was repaired with a suture anchor, securing the labrum to the glenoid rim (Fig. 4).

The anteroinferior labral tear was repaired and anchored to the glenoid rim.

After confirming the stability of the repairs via arthroscopy, the portals were closed, and a compression dressing was applied. The left shoulder was immobilized in a sling for four weeks, after which rehabilitation began with passive range of motion exercises and progressed to active motion as tolerated.

At the six-month follow-up, the patient exhibited full, pain-free range of motion in the left shoulder, showed no signs of recurrent instability, and successfully resumed daily and recreational activities. Clinical tests confirmed both anatomical and functional restoration were successful (Fig. 5).

Patient's range of motion at 6 months post-surgery. A: Full shoulder abduction. B, C: Full internal and external rotation of the shoulder.

The patient experienced significant improvement after surgery, with complete resolution of shoulder pain and restored function. He was satisfied with the outcome and could return to daily activities and sports without limitations, appreciating the thorough diagnosis and quick recovery from the arthroscopic treatment.

Discussion

RHAGL is a rare but clinically important cause of posterior shoulder instability that is often overlooked in both clinical and radiological practice. Most HAGL lesions affect the anterior band of the IGHL, leading to anterior instability, while posterior variants comprise less than 10 % of cases [1,3,4]. Despite their rarity, RHAGL lesions can result in persistent pain, mechanical symptoms, and progressive functional impairment if left untreated.

Clinical and diagnostic challenges

RHAGL typically presents with vague symptoms, such as posterior shoulder pain, clicking, or subjective instability, following trauma, often after a dislocation or subluxation event in flexion, adduction, and internal rotation. Physical examination findings are usually nonspecific, and conventional radiographs rarely reveal diagnostic clues unless associated with bony avulsion (“bony flake sign”) [4]. MRI, although the preferred imaging modality for soft tissue evaluation, is frequently insensitive to RHAGL, with studies showing up to 50 % of lesions missed on initial review, especially if the diagnosis is not actively sought or there are concurrent injuries such as labral tears or capsular disruptions [4,5]. In our case, both clinical assessment and MRI failed to clearly identify the RHAGL lesion, highlighting the limitations of non-invasive evaluation.

Role of arthroscopy

Diagnostic shoulder arthroscopy is the gold standard for diagnosing RHAGL injuries. It allows direct visualization of the capsuloligamentous anatomy and enables simultaneous treatment. In our patient, the procedure confirmed a complete avulsion of the posterior IGHL and facilitated the repair of both the RHAGL and an anteroinferior labral tear using a minimally invasive approach.

Surgical technique and outcomes

Arthroscopic repair of RHAGL, as described in the present case and supported by literature, is considered both effective and safe. It allows precise restoration of capsuloligamentous continuity with minimal morbidity, preserving surrounding soft tissues and enabling early rehabilitation. Arthroscopic repair has been shown to result in excellent outcomes, with most patients regaining full range of motion and returning to pre-injury activities without instability recurrence [4,8].

Our patient's postoperative course was uneventful, analgesia consisted of acetaminophen 1 g every 8 h for 3–5 days, NSAIDs for 5–7 days if no contraindication, and a short rescue course of tramadol only if needed, no opioids beyond 3 days, with full recovery at six months and no signs of residual instability.

Clinicians should have a heightened suspicion for RHAGL in patients experiencing persistent shoulder pain and mechanical symptoms after trauma, especially when initial imaging and examination results are inconclusive. MRI findings may be subtle or absent; careful review and correlation with clinical context are essential. Diagnostic arthroscopy should be considered early in the diagnostic algorithm if symptoms persist and non-invasive tests are nondiagnostic. Arthroscopic repair is currently the standard of care, offering reliable anatomic and functional restoration.

Given the rarity of RHAGL, current evidence is based primarily on case reports and small case series. Larger, multicenter studies are warranted to better define the epidemiology, optimal management strategies, and long-term outcomes of this uncommon but important injury.

Conclusion

RHAGL is a unique and critical cause of posterior shoulder instability that often goes undetected in imaging, highlighting the need for increased awareness and thorough examination. Clinicians should suspect this injury in patients with persistent shoulder pain after trauma and negative imaging. Early arthroscopic diagnosis and repair can restore stability and function, leading to excellent outcomes. Increased clinical awareness is essential to avoid missed or delayed diagnosis.
