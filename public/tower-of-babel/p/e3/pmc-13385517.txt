The First Documented Case of Congenital Drug‐Resistant Tuberculosis in Indonesia: A Case Report
Authors: Cynthia Centauri, Srisadono Fauzi Adiprabowo, Putri Maharani Tristanita Marsubrin, Irandi Putra Pratomo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCongenital drug‐resistant tuberculosis (DR‐TB) is a rare condition with no standardized treatment. Congenital TB itself is a life‐threatening condition. The clinical manifestations can be nonspecific, particularly in preterm infants, which can further reduce diagnostic awareness. We report a case of a 32‐week preterm baby with rifampicin‐resistant TB. She was born with respiratory distress and early‐onset jaundice, initially suspected of sepsis and admitted to the NICU. The infant initially showed clinical improvement at 3 days of life, but at 23 days of life, the clinical condition worsened despite a sterile blood culture. The mother was subsequently diagnosed with miliary TB with rifampicin‐resistant Xpert sputum. The infant's gastric lavage Xpert MTB/RIF confirmed rifampicin‐resistant TB. At 32 days of life, she received a five‐drug regimen, including bedaquiline. She completed 12 months of treatment with a favourable outcome. This case highlights the challenges of early recognition and treatment selection in congenital DR‐TB.

Introduction

Indonesia is a tuberculosis‐endemic country, ranked second globally in TB burden [1]. However, the incidence of congenital TB in Indonesia remains poorly documented. Congenital TB is defined as tuberculosis acquired in utero through haematogenous transplacental transmission or intrapartum through ingestion or aspiration of infected amniotic fluid or cervicovaginal secretions [2]. Presence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis resistant to one or more anti‐TB drugs at birth results in congenital DR‐TB. Diagnosis is challenging due to its nonspecific symptoms that often mimic neonatal sepsis, especially when maternal DR‐TB is unrecognized [3, 4, 5]. Furthermore, the management is complicated due to limited evidence guiding neonatal treatment. This report presents Indonesia's first known case of congenital DR‐TB.

Case Report

A female 32‐week preterm infant was delivered vaginally at the emergency department due to maternal vaginal bleeding and a low‐lying placenta. There was insufficient time to administer antenatal corticosteroids for foetal lung maturation. The neonate was born through meconium‐stained amniotic fluid with a birth weight of 1795 g and did not cry immediately, requiring bag‐mask ventilation. Respiratory effort subsequently improved, and bag‐mask support was replaced with CPAP. The APGAR score was 6 of 7. She was admitted to the NICU with respiratory distress syndrome, neonatal sepsis and low birth weight. Empiric ampicillin–gentamicin was started. CXR showed increased bilateral perihilar and paracardial markings (Figure 1A). Capillary blood gas demonstrated pH 7.304, pCO2 48.7 mmHg, pO2 38.2 mmHg, BE −2, and saturation 65.6% (CPAP 8/40%).

Serial chest x‐ray and CT imaging throughout the patient's treatment.

Surfactant was not administered, as the infant did not meet NICU criteria (gestational age > 28 weeks). Within 24 h, she developed jaundice requiring phototherapy. By Day 3, respiratory distress had improved, and CPAP was discontinued. On Day 4, echocardiography revealed a left‐to‐right PDA (1–1.5 mm) treated with paracetamol. After discontinuation of CPAP, she remained in the perinatology ward and maintained stable oxygen saturation on room air. Expressed breast milk was gradually introduced via nasogastric tube; no direct breastfeeding or kangaroo mother care was provided. Antibiotics were discontinued after sterile cultures.

On Day 22, the mother was readmitted to the hospital with respiratory failure due to miliary TB; sputum Xpert MTB/RIF confirmed rifampicin‐resistant (RR). Further history revealed that the mother had experienced an intermittent cough with recurrent febrile episodes treated symptomatically since the first trimester of pregnancy. On Day 23, the infant experienced fever and oxygen desaturation requiring CPAP. Chest radiography showed bilateral pulmonary consolidation (Figure 1B). Cefoperazone–sulbactam and amikacin were initiated for presumed sepsis. Neonatal gastric lavage Xpert MTB/RIF on day 28 also yielded RR‐TB, fulfilling revised Cantwell criteria for congenital TB. Abdominal ultrasonography was normal; umbilical cord histopathology was unavailable. Meropenem was used as bridging therapy before oral anti‐tuberculosis therapy (ATT) was initiated.

The patient was enrolled in the national TB program. On day 36, ATT was initiated with levofloxacin (Lfx 15 mg/kg/day), bedaquiline (Bdq 6 mg/kg/day for 14 days then 3 mg/kg/day; t.i.w), linezolid (Lzd 15 mg/kg/day), cycloserine (Cs 15 mg/kg/day) and clofazimine (Cfz 2 mg/kg/day; t.i.w). CPAP was weaned to nasal cannula oxygen on Day 44. The infant was transferred to the paediatric isolation ward on Day 53. She was able to tolerate full oral feeding and was discharged at 89 days (2650 g) on 0.5 L/min oxygen due to occasional desaturation to 89%. Contrast‐enhanced chest CT before discharge showed diffuse consolidations and nodular opacities across all lung segments (Figure 1C). On Day 103, the infant was successfully weaned off supplemental oxygen.

Baseline Xpert XDR from gastric lavage on Day 30 and drug susceptibility testing showed no resistance other than rifampicin. The baseline MGIT gastric lavage on Day 49 was positive, but all follow‐up cultures remained negative. Gastric AFB smears on Day 49 and all follow‐up AFB tests were negative. Ophthalmologic monitoring showed no abnormalities. During follow‐up, comorbid microcephaly and hypothyroidism were identified, despite a previously normal neonatal thyroid screening result. Appropriate weight gain was achieved after treatment for hypothyroidism and a high‐calorie formula. The infant completed a long‐term regimen of 12‐month ATT (6 Bdq‐Lfx‐Lzd‐Cfz‐Cs/6 Lfx‐Cfz‐Cs). Serial chest imaging demonstrated progressive resolution (Figure 1D,E). Both mother and infant completed treatment and were declared cured.

Discussion

Congenital DR‐TB is exceedingly rare, and current guidelines do not provide standardized management recommendations for neonates. Distinguishing congenital TB from postnatal infection relies on identifying a primary hepatic complex, caseating granuloma, or excluding postnatal TB exposure [2]. In this case, the absence of postnatal exposure strongly supported congenital transmission. The diagnosis of congenital DR‐TB became evident only after the mother was confirmed to have rifampicin‐resistant miliary TB. Similar case reports from Australia note delayed diagnosis due to the late recognition of maternal disease [4].

Treatment selection involved multidisciplinary consultation, including cardiology and an ophthalmology specialist. A 12‐month regimen including bedaquiline was chosen because of severe disease with extensive pulmonary involvement. Previous congenital MDR‐TB case reports have described the use of linezolid, cycloserine and levofloxacin [3, 5]. Another case report of congenital pre‐extensively drug‐resistant TB also included bedaquiline as part of the treatment regimen without observed cardiac adverse effects. Monthly ECG, CBC, liver function and renal function monitoring demonstrated no significant adverse effects. Although WHO permits bedaquiline use in young children, specific recommendations for neonatal congenital TB remain unavailable. This case suggests bedaquiline may be safe and effective for congenital DR‐TB, although long‐term multidisciplinary follow‐up remains important for growth and neurodevelopmental outcomes.
