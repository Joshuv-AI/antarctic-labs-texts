E3 ubiquitin-ligase Hakai regulates LRP4 stability and Wnt/β-catenin signalling in colorectal cancer cells
Authors: Andrea Rodríguez-Alonso, Lía Jove, Gloria Alfonsín, Marta Tasende, Ingrid Jordens, Madelon Maurice, Angélica Figueroa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe epithelial-mesenchymal transition is associated with the acquisition of cancer stem cell (CSC) traits that promote metastasis and therapy resistance. Here, we investigated the role of the E3 ubiquitin-ligase Hakai in regulating CSC-associated properties in colorectal cancer. Using tumoursphere models, loss-of-function approaches, proteomics, bioinformatics, and Wnt/β-catenin pathway studies, we found that Hakai depletion reduced tumoursphere formation, CSC marker expression, and Wnt target gene activation. Mechanistically, our findings support that Hakai regulates LRP4 stability, a negative regulator of Wnt signaling, consistent with ubiquitin-dependent mechanism. Consequently, Hakai enhances β-catenin/TCF transcriptional activity, nuclear accumulation of β-catenin, and attenuation of LRP4-mediated inhibition of Wnt signaling. Pharmacological inhibition of Hakai with Hakin-1, a selective inhibitor of its HYB domain responsible for E3 ubiquitin-ligase activity, reduced tumoursphere formation and promoted differentiation-associated features. Furthermore, these findings highlight Hakai inhibition as a potential CSC-directed therapeutic strategy in colorectal cancer.Keywords: E3 ubiquitin-ligase Hakai, LRP4, Wnt/β-catenin pathway, cancer stem cell, colon cancer

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide, characterized by high heterogeneity and frequent resistance to therapy. A growing body of evidence indicates that cancer stem cells (CSCs), a subpopulation of tumor cells with the ability to self-renew, differentiate, and tumor-initiating potential, play a critical role in tumor progression, metastasis, and resistance to therapy.1,2 Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms that regulate CSC properties is essential for developing more effective therapeutic strategies against CRC. Wnt/β-catenin signaling plays a critical role during homeostasis and in various diseases, including cancer. Indeed, over 90% of CRCs involve hyperactivation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling.3,4 The Wnt canonical pathway is dependent on β-catenin and TCF/LEF transcription factors, and primarily controls cell differentiation and proliferation. In the absence of Wnt ligands, the free pool of β-catenin is phosphorylated and incorporated into the β-catenin destruction complex (composed of APC, AXIN1/2, CK1, and GSK3β), leading to its degradation by the proteasome. In the presence of Wnt ligands, the Wnt pathway is activated through Wnt binding to Frizzled (FZD) and LRP5/6 receptors. This binding leads to the activation of the cytosolic effector protein DVL, leading to the inhibition of the destruction complex and thereby preventing β-catenin degradation. Subsequently, β-catenin accumulates in the cytoplasm and the excess translocate to the nucleus. Here, β-catenin activates TCF/LEF transcription factors, promoting the expression of Wnt/β-catenin target genes including C-MYC, CCND1, AXIN2, MMP7, or LGR5.5,6 The activation of Wnt/β-catenin target genes plays a crucial role in the initiation and maintenance of colorectal tumors and CSCs, making the Wnt/β-catenin pathway an attractive therapeutic target for CSC eradication.7 On the other hand, LRP4 (also known as MEGF7), a member of the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LRP) family, acts predominantly as a negative regulator of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, often antagonizing LRP6-mediated signaling.8 LRP4 function has been implicated in various neurological and developmental disorders. It plays a critical role in embryonic development, with loss-of-function mutations associated with malformations of limb, teeth, and kidneys.9,10,11 It modulates both Wnt and BMP pathways, notably through interactions with antagonists such as WISE, DKK1, and SOST.12,13 Beyond Wnt, LRP4 influences other pathways, such as the PI3K/AKT pathway in epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT).14 Importantly, while emerging data hint at its role in cancer biology, LRP4’s precise contribution to Wnt/β-catenin signaling in CRC and its specific effects on CSC are poorly understood.

Ubiquitination is the second most prevalent post-translational modification after phosphorylation. This process involves the ATP-dependent covalent attachment of ubiquitin, a conserved protein composed of 76 amino acids, to a substrate protein through a cascade of enzymatic reactions involving the E1 activating enzymes, E2 conjugating enzymes, and E3 ubiquitin-ligase enzymes. E3 ubiquitin-ligases are pivotal regulators of protein stability and function, orchestrating the final step of the ubiquitination cascade that targets specific substrates for proteasomal degradation or modulates their activity, localization, and interactions.15,16 Hakai was initially identified as an E3 ubiquitin-ligase that targets E-cadherin for ubiquitination, leading to its endocytosis and lysosomal degradation, a process that disrupts adherens junctions, facilitates cell-cell dissociation, and promotes EMT.17 This classic function positioned Hakai as a critical regulator of epithelial plasticity.18 Subsequent studies by our group and others have expanded its oncogenic profile, linking Hakai to enhanced tumor cell invasion, migration, and cellular plasticity across various cancer types.19,20,21 Structurally, Hakai contains a RING finger domain typical of E3 ligases and a conserved Hakai HYB (Hakai pTyr-binding) domain, which mediates the recognition of phosphorylated substrates such as E-cadherin. Notably, the development of Hakin-1, a selective small-molecule inhibitor of Hakai, has opened new avenues for therapeutic intervention by targeting its E3 ligase activity.22

The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is one of the key activators of the EMT, and it has been shown to play a role in the formation and maintenance of CSCs across various carcinomas.18,23 It plays a pivotal role in the development and regeneration of the small intestinal epithelium, including the differentiation of Paneth cells at the crypt base.24 In the intestinal and colonic epithelium, activation of Wnt target genes promotes the expansion of the stem cell compartment, whereas the attenuation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling facilitates cellular differentiation.25 A functional relationship has been established between EMT and the acquisition of CSC properties, which is closely linked to therapy resistance, metastasis, and tumor relapse. Despite these advances, the role of Hakai in regulating CSC properties and its potential functional interplay with the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway remain largely unexplored.

In the present study, by using a colon cancer tumoursphere culture grown under conditions that promote the induction of CSC-like properties, we demonstrate that silencing Hakai reduces both the size and number of tumourspheres, accompanied by a decrease in CSC markers. Through proteomic and computational analyses, we identify several CSC-related proteins, notably LRP4. We show that Hakai interacts with LRP4, promoting its ubiquitination and subsequent degradation. Furthermore, Hakai overexpression activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling and cooperates with LRP6 to overcome the antagonistic effect of LRP4. Together, our findings uncover a novel regulatory mechanism by which Hakai modulates Wnt signaling via LRP4 degradation, with potential implications for CSC maintenance and colon cancer progression. Moreover, treatment with Hakin-1, a pharmacological inhibitor of Hakai, modulated tumoursphere growth by reducing stemness-associated features, disrupting 3D structural integrity, and promoting cellular differentiation, further supporting the therapeutic potential of targeting Hakai in CRC.

Results

Hakai-silencing reduces colon cancer tumourspheres and modulates self-renewal features and differentiation-related properties

In order to investigate the mechanistic insights of Hakai in modulating colon CSCs, we used HT29 tumourspheres, a three-dimensional culture model derived from the human colon adenocarcinoma cell line HT29. This model has been extensively used to study CSCs within the context of CRC.26 HT29 tumourspheres serve as a valuable tool for advancing our understanding of CSC biology and developing novel therapeutic approaches for targeting CSCs in CRC and other tumor types. To validate the CSC-enriched nature of the tumourspheres under the experimental conditions used in this study, we assessed the expression of established colorectal CSC markers. Tumourspheres derived from HT29 cells showed increased expression of CSC-associated markers compared to their corresponding 2D monolayer cultures. Similar results were obtained in an independent CRC cell line (Caco-2), where Hakai silencing also modulated CSC-associated markers, further supporting the robustness of the tumoursphere model as a CSC-enriched system (Figure S1A). To determine the effect of Hakai silencing, Hakai depletion was induced in HT29 monolayer cultures using a previously reported doxycycline-inducible viral transduction system.27 After 72 h, HT29 tumourspheres were induced for 5 days in ultra-low attachment plates with a specific medium to enrich CSC growth, as shown in the schematic workflow (Figure 1A). This assay showed that Hakai-silencing reduces the number and size of the tumourspheres (Figures 1B–1D), further suggesting the impact of Hakai on cancer cells’ self-renewal capacity and proliferation. Notably, induction of Hakai silencing in pre-established tumourspheres also led to a marked reduction in tumoursphere growth (Figures S1B and S1C), demonstrating that Hakai is required not only for tumoursphere formation but also for the growth and maintenance of established CSC-enriched structures. Interestingly, similar inhibitory effects were observed in 2D monolayer cultures, where Hakai silencing also resulted in a significant reduction in cell viability (Figure S1D). While Hakai silencing affects cell viability in both 2D and 3D conditions, the effects on CSC properties are supported by additional CSC-specific readouts. In particular, Hakai depletion markedly impairs tumoursphere formation capacity and reduces the expression of established CSC markers, indicating a role beyond general effects on cell proliferation. Notably, these phenotypes are more pronounced under CSC-enriched conditions. Importantly, cleaved caspase-3 protein levels, a well-stablished apoptotic marker, revealed no increase under Hakai silencing conditions (Figures S1E and S2), indicating that apoptosis is not a major contributor to the observed effects. In accordance with previous reports,28 in 3D tumourspheres Hakai-silencing was accompanied by the downregulation of stem cell markers and Wnt/β-catenin target genes (Figures 1E–1G and S3), including LGR5, the best-established CSC biomarker for colorectal, LEF1, Ki67, CCND1 and the pluripotency-associated transcription factor NANOG. In contrast, MUC2, a key secreted mucin involved in intestinal epithelial differentiation and barrier function, showed a trend toward upregulation. These findings highlight the contribution of Hakai to the development of CSC tumour spheres, reinforcing its potential relevance as a therapeutic target in CRC.

Proteomic and bioinformatic analysis identifies novel cancer-related candidates regulated by Hakai

In order to investigate the extent to which Hakai-silencing may control altered protein expression in CSC, we decided to follow two different strategies: a proteomic study comparing Hakai-silencing in colon cancer tumourspheres to control conditions, and consulting the UbiBrowser database that is able to predict the proteome-wide human Hakai-substrate interaction network.29 For the proteomic analysis, we used nano-LC-MS/MS analysis coupled to a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer to identify novel Hakai-regulated proteins in colon CSC tumourspheres. The schematic workflow of the process is represented (Figure 2A). The confirmation of Hakai-silencing was shown at the protein level in three biological replicates per experimental group analyzed in the proteomic study (Figure S4A). Mass spectrometry analysis identified a total of 3,602 proteins with ≥2 peptides. A total of 103 proteins (2.85%) were specifically enriched in Hakai-silenced HT29 tumourspheres (+Dox), whereas 118 proteins (3.28%) were specifically enriched in control HT29 tumourspheres (-Dox) (Figure 2B). The principal component analysis (PCAs) and Volcano plot of differentially proteins identified are shown (Figures 2C and 2D). Notably, the sum of the principal components PC1 (PC1, X axis) and PC2 (PC2, Y axis) captured the majority of the variance, approximately 80%, providing a good representation of the variance in the samples (Figure 2C). The distinct clustering observed suggests significant differences in the proteome between the two conditions. We conducted a hierarchical clustering analysis of the differentially protein expression from three replicates comparing Hakai-silenced to control tumourspheres, which indicates protein expression was similar in each group (Figure 2D). As represented by black dots in the Volcano plot analysis (Figure 2E), eight proteins were significantly upregulated (Fold change ≥1.5 and p value <0.05) in Hakai-silenced tumourspheres, while three proteins were significantly downregulated. The boxplot represents the regulated proteins in Hakai-silenced tumourspheres compared to control tumourspheres (Figure 2F), including MUC2, DDX55, CLMN, C19ORF43, RABGGTA, SLC35F2, UBXN7, and UFSP2 that were significantly enriched in Hakai-silenced tumourspheres, whereas NIT1, PRPS2, and WDR26 were significantly depleted. The complete dataset from the proteomic analysis is shown in Table 1, including the biological functions of the identified proteins obtained from Uniprot. Interestingly, several identified proteins are cancer-related proteins involved in CSC or differentiation. However, due to the characteristics of the ubiquitination cascade, which involves transient interactions characterized by rapid kinetics and weak target affinities, in general, E3 ubiquitin-ligase substrates were not identified, making it challenging to identify and study their specific substrates. It is important to note that proteomic profiling of Hakai-silenced cells captures the integrated outcome of multiple regulatory layers, including transcriptional, post-transcriptional, translational, and post-translational mechanisms. Therefore, the differentially expressed proteins identified upon Hakai silencing may reflect both E3 ligase-dependent and -independent functions of Hakai. While the present proteomic analysis was not followed by systematic functional characterization of individual candidates, it provides an unbiased overview of proteins regulated upon Hakai depletion. To specifically explore the potential contribution of Hakai E3 ubiquitin-ligase activity, we complemented this approach with a bioinformatic prediction of Hakai-dependent ubiquitination targets, allowing us to focus on proteins that may be directly regulated at the post-translational level by Hakai. Therefore, we used the UbiBrowser platform, which allowed us to determine the predicted Hakai-substrate interaction network.29 By using UbiBrowser 2.0,30 116 proteins were identified as potential predicted substrates for Hakai-mediated ubiquitination (Table S1). We shortlisted the top 20 predicted novel substrates that could potentially be ubiquitinated by the Hakai protein (in blue). Moreover, 4 already known substrates were identified, shown in red (Figure 2G). Thus far, very few substrates for Hakai were biochemically or functionally reported, including E-cadherin, Cortactin, DOK1, or Ajuba and specific substrates of Hakai in CSC conditions remain unknown.17,20,31

Hakai interacts with LRP4 and regulates its stability

The predicted novel Hakai substrates identified by UbiBrowser included several cancer-related proteins. Given that previous studies have reported Hakai is involved in EMT and that our results support Hakai implication in CSC maintenance (Figure 1), we directed our focus toward proteins involved in CSC regulation. Interestingly, our top four protein, LRP4, is a member of the LRP and can act as a negative regulator of Wnt signaling.10 LRP4 is primarily known in neuromuscular junction development and bone formation;10,12 however, its significance in CSC is not well understood. To investigate the potential link between Hakai and LRP4, FLAG-Hakai was transiently transfected into HEK293 and colon cancer HCT116 cells, showing that Hakai overexpression reduced LRP4 protein levels in both cell lines (Figure 3A, right panel; Figures S5 and S6). In addition, Hakai knockdown in HT29 cells did not affect LRP4 mRNA levels but resulted in increased endogenous LRP4 protein levels, suggesting that Hakai regulates LRP4 at the post-translational level under physiological conditions (Figure 3A, right panel). Similar trends toward increased LRP4 protein levels were also observed in Hakai-silenced Caco-2 tumour spheres, although the magnitude of the response was more variable (Figures S4C and S13). Next, immunofluorescence analyses were conducted using HEK293 cells transiently overexpressing V5-Hakai in the presence or absence of overexpressed LRP4. Hakai overexpression alone was detected mainly in the nucleus but also in the cytosol, consistent with previous reports.32 LRP4 was located at the cell membrane, however, when both proteins were overexpressed, LRP4 expression was decreased (indicated by arrows) (Figure 3B), indicating that Hakai may regulate LRP4 expression. As previously mentioned, the absence of co-localization of both proteins may be attributed to transient interactions between Hakai and LRP4. Given that Hakai overexpression downregulates LRP4 protein levels and considering that LRP4 is a predicted new substrate for Hakai by UbiBrowser, we investigated whether we could detect a protein-protein interaction by immunoprecipitation. As co-immunoprecipitation using endogenous proteins was challenging, FLAG-Hakai, LRP4, and HA-ubiquitin were transiently transfected in HEK293T cells, and an LRP4 antibody was used for immunoprecipitation. As expected, co-immunoprecipitation was observed for Hakai and LRP4 when overexpressed, further confirming Hakai and LRP4 interaction (Figures 3C and S7). Based on previous studies identifying Hakai as an E3 ubiquitin-ligase that promotes substrate ubiquitination and subsequent degradation, we hypothesized that LRP4 could represent a novel Hakai substrate. To test this possibility, we performed ubiquitination assays under denaturing conditions using His6-tagged ubiquitin and Ni-NTA agarose resin to selectively isolate covalently ubiquitinated proteins. However, under the LRP4 overexpression conditions required for these ubiquitination assays, Hakai overexpression did not produce clear changes in either total LRP4 protein levels or the intensity of the ubiquitinated LRP4 signal (Figures 3D and S7). These experiments required strong exogenous LRP4 expression to enable efficient immunoprecipitation and detection, likely limiting the sensitivity to detect Hakai-dependent quantitative changes in LRP4 ubiquitination or degradation. Therefore, although the data support the presence of ubiquitinated LRP4 species, they do not demonstrate a robust increase in LRP4 polyubiquitination upon Hakai overexpression under these experimental conditions. To further explore the mechanism underlying Hakai-mediated regulation of LRP4, we analyzed the effect of the proteasome inhibitor MG132 on LRP4 protein expression. Under basal conditions, MG132 treatment produced only a modest and variable trend toward increased LRP4 protein levels, without consistent changes across biological replicates (Figures 3E and S8). Furthermore, no changes in LRP4 levels were observed upon treatment with the lysosome inhibitor chloroquine or the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA (Figures S4D and S8). This may indicate that Hakai-dependent regulation of LRP4 is influenced by additional cellular or signaling context, including the relative expression of LRP co-receptors. However, under Hakai-overexpressing conditions, MG132 partially restored the reduction in LRP4 levels induced by Hakai (Figures 3F and S9), supporting the involvement of the proteasomal pathway in Hakai-dependent regulation of LRP4 stability. Similarly, pharmacological inhibition of Hakai E3 ubiquitin-ligase activity also restored LRP4 expression levels in Hakai-overexpressing cells to those observed under control conditions (Figures 3G and S10). Together, these findings support a role for Hakai in the post-translational regulation of LRP4 protein stability, although the magnitude of this effect appears to depend strongly on the relative expression levels of Hakai and LRP4.

Hakai counteracts the antagonistic effect of LRP4 on LRP6-mediated Wnt pathway activation

LRP4 is a transmembrane protein of the LDL receptor family and functions as a negative regulator of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway by antagonizing the LRP6 receptor.8 Considering the crucial role of LRP4 in development and its implication in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, we aimed to investigate the potential mechanism by which Hakai may influence Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Moreover, the effect of Hakai on key Wnt-related proteins was extended to LRP6. First, we analyzed the effect on β-catenin-TCF transcriptional activity by using the TOP-Flash reporter gene, in which the luciferase gene is placed under the control of a promoter harboring ten copies of the TCF/LEF-1 consensus responsive sequence with a c-fos minimal promoter. As a negative control, a mutated form of this promoter (FOP-Flash) was used. Cells were transfected with either TOP-Flash or FOP-Flash plasmids, and the ratio of luciferase activity from TOP-Flash to FOP-Flash was calculated to obtain a measurement of Wnt-specific transcriptional activity. TK-Renilla was transfected as a transfection control. Cells were co-transfected with the Hakai and LRP4 or LRP6 plasmids (Figures 4A and 4B). To distinguish between effects on basal Wnt/β-catenin signaling and those occurring under ligand-induced pathway activation, cells were treated with either control L-cell conditioned medium (LCM) or WNT3A-conditioned medium (WCM). This experimental design allowed us to assess whether Hakai modulates Wnt signaling in a context-dependent manner, in the absence or presence of WNT ligand stimulation. As shown, overexpression of LRP4 significantly inhibited both basal WNT signaling as well as WNT3A-induced signaling, in line with LRP4 negatively regulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Whereas in basal conditions, overexpression of Hakai showed a trend but no significant effect, Hakai significantly potentiated WNT3A-induced signaling. However, Hakai was unable to fully rescue the inhibitory effect of LRP4 when both were co-transfected, although a small effect on basal LRP4-mediated suppression was observed (Figure 4A). On the other hand, we confirmed that LRP6 was able to significantly activate Wnt-pathway in both basal and WNT3A-stimulated conditions.33 Importantly, there was a synergistic effect observed when Hakai and LRP6 were co-overexpressed. Co-expression of LRP6 and LRP4 suppressed the LRP6-induced activation of the WNT pathway, suggesting that LRP4 functions as an antagonist of LRP6 (Figure 4B). Interestingly, co-expression of LRP4, LRP6, and Hakai showed that Hakai was able to partially rescue/counteract the negative effect of LRP4 on LRP6, potentially through the regulation of LRP4 protein stability (Figure 4B). To further evaluate whether Hakai modulates β-catenin signaling, immunofluorescence analyses were performed under Hakai overexpression conditions. Representative images together with quantitative analysis showed increased nuclear β-catenin intensity in Hakai-overexpressing cells compared with control cells, consistent with enhanced β-catenin/TCF transcriptional activity (Figure 4C). These observations provide complementary evidence supporting the activation of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway by Hakai.

Hakin-1 reduces tumourspheres formation and stemness features

Preclinical studies from our group have demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of targeting Hakai through its HYB domain with Hakin-1 in vitro and in vivo.22 Next, we investigated the potential effect of pharmacological inhibition of the E3 ubiquitin-ligase Hakai using Hakin-1 on tumoursphere formation. HT29-derived tumourspheres were treated with Hakin-1 for six days, concurrently with the induction of stemness culture conditions (Figure 5A). Phase-contrast microscopy revealed that this treatment phenotypically affected the tumourspheres (Figure 5B). Treated tumourspheres exhibited a less compact morphology and poorly defined 3D structures compared to the well-organized and dense tumourspheres observed under control conditions, suggesting a potential loss of stemness and increased cellular differentiation. In addition, Hakin-1 modestly, but significantly, reduced the number of tumourspheres formed (Figure 5C). Although the area of individual tumourspheres varied, Hakin-1 treatment significantly decreased the average size of tumourspheres compared to the control condition (Figure 5D). Importantly, similar effects were observed when Hakin-1 treatment was applied either before (Figures S11A–S11D) or after the induction of stemness (Figures S11E–S11H). To further investigate the effect of Hakin-1 on stemness and differentiation, we analyzed the expression levels of various markers for the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, stem cells, and differentiation in tumourspheres. Hakin-1 significantly reduced the expression levels of LEF-1 and TCF-1, both of which play pivotal roles in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway through their interaction with β-catenin. Consistent with this effect, Hakin-1 markedly reduced the expression of stemness-associated markers LGR5 and NANOG (Figure 5E). This was accompanied by a non-significant trend toward decreased proliferation, as indicated by reduced Ki67 expression. Moreover, while no overall reduction was observed across several downstream Wnt/β-catenin target genes commonly upregulated in CRC (CCND1 and C-MYC), a statistically significant decrease in AXIN2 and MMP7 was detected (Figures 5E and 5F). Taken together, Hakin-1 impairs Wnt pathway activation, particularly affecting genes related to stemness. At the protein level, we observed a slight upregulation of E-cadherin, accompanied by a downregulation of AXIN2, a marker of Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation that is typically elevated in cells exhibiting a mesenchymal-like phenotype,34 together with the reduced expression of Wnt-related stem cell markers LGR5 and NANOG (Figures 5G and S12). As expected, Hakin-1 treatment did not alter Hakai protein levels. These findings support the notion that Hakai inhibition alters the tumourspheres phenotype by reducing stemness-associated markers.

Hakin-1 modulates stemness and differentiation marker expression in tumourspheres

Given the observed effects on stemness, we next analyzed the potential impact of Hakin-1 on the subcellular localization of LGR5 and MUC2 in tumourspheres. Moreover, we examined the effect of Hakai inhibition on E-cadherin and LRP4 localization in tumourspheres using immunofluorescence, given that E-cadherin is a known target of Hakai and LRP4 is a novel Hakai-interacting protein linked to Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Hakin-1 treatment modestly increased E-cadherin levels (Figure 6A). Hakin-1 treatment resulted in a reduction in LGR5 levels (Figure 6B), suggesting a potential decrease in the stemness of cancer cells. As expected, LRP4, as a target for Hakai, showed increased expression levels upon Hakin-1 treatment (Figure 6C). On the other hand, Hakin-1 treatment increased the expression of the differentiation marker MUC2 (Figure 6D). Taken together, our results suggest that Hakin-1 reduces tumoursphere formation by reducing stemness-associated features, disrupting 3D structural integrity, and promoting cellular differentiation.

In conclusion, our findings identify Hakai as an important regulator of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in CRC as illustrated by the model in Figure 7. Our data support a role for Hakai in the post-translational regulation of LRP4 protein stability through mechanisms involving its E3 ubiquitin-ligase activity. Reduced membrane-associated LRP4 may relieve its inhibitory effect on Wnt signaling, thereby facilitating Hakai-mediated cooperation with LRP6 to enhance Wnt/β-catenin transcriptional activity and the expression of stemness-associated target genes in colon cancer cells. Pharmacological inhibition of Hakai may reduce stemness-associated features and promote cellular differentiation in CRC cells.

Proposed model for Hakai-mediated regulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling

In the absence of Hakai, LRP4 acts as an antagonist at the cell membrane, inhibiting Wnt/β-catenin signaling by interfering with LRP6 activity. As a result, cytoplasmic β-catenin is targeted for proteasomal degradation, and Wnt target genes remain transcriptionally repressed. In the presence of Hakai, LRP4 is ubiquitinated and degraded, thereby relieving its inhibitory effect on LRP6-mediated Wnt signaling. Discussion

In this study, we provide evidence that the E3 ubiquitin-ligase Hakai plays a pivotal role in regulating CSC properties in CRC. Using colon cancer tumourspheres as a model of CSC, we show that Hakai modulates self-renewal features, differentiation and Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Silencing Hakai markedly reduces in tumoursphere number and size (Figures 1B–1D) and leads to the downregulation of key CSC markers, including the transcriptional Wnt target gene LGR5 (Figures 1E and 1G).7,35 Importantly, although Hakai silencing also reduces cell viability under standard 2D conditions, the consistent effects on tumoursphere formation and CSC marker expression support a role in CSC-associated functions beyond general proliferation. These observations, together with the modulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling described later in discussion, suggest that Hakai contributes to the regulation of CSC properties through specific molecular pathways. HT29 and HCT116 cells were selected because they provide complementary advantages for inducible loss- or transient gain-of-function approaches, respectively. Importantly, similar effects of Hakai silencing on CSC-associated markers were also observed in Caco-2 cells, supporting the reproducibility of these findings in an additional CRC model, although the magnitude of the response varied between cell lines.

These results are consistent with our previous findings showing that tumourspheres derived from CRC cells exhibit increased CBLL1 (Hakai gene) and LGR5 mRNA levels compared to monolayer cultures.28 Although LGR5 emerged as a potential Hakai-associated candidate in the UbiBrowser-based prediction, our experimental data do not support LGR5 as a direct ubiquitination substrate of Hakai. Instead, the observed changes in LGR5 expression are consistent with its established role as a downstream transcriptional target of Wnt/β-catenin signaling, which is indirectly modulated by Hakai through the degradation of the Wnt inhibitor LRP4. Our mechanistic studies suggest that Hakai could promote the acquisition of CSC properties by enhancing Wnt/β-catenin transcriptional activity in colon cancer. We identified LRP4 as a candidate Hakai-regulated protein potentially involved in Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Although the role of LRP4 in CRC and CSC remains poorly defined, studies in developmental systems have demonstrated that LRP4 regulates Wnt/β-catenin signaling during limb and tooth development, bone homeostasis, and neuromuscular junction formation,8,9,10,13,36 where it functions as a context-dependent modulator, often antagonizing LRP5/6-mediated pathway activation. Given our biochemical studies and the important role of Wnt signaling in the development of CSCs, we hypothesize that Hakai’s effect on stemness is at least partly through the Hakai-regulated protein LRP4. Although the precise domains mediating the Hakai-LRP4 interaction were not mapped in this study, it is plausible that LRP4 interacts with Hakai through the HYB domain, which is required for Hakai’s E3 ubiquitin-ligase activity and has been previously shown to mediate substrate recognition, including E-cadherin.31 Notably, LRP4 was also identified as a putative Hakai substrate in UbiBrowser predictions, further supporting this hypothesis. This is the first study to implicate LRP4 in Wnt signaling within colorectal CSCs, expanding its role beyond its previously recognized function as a negative regulator during developmental processes. Hakai overexpression decreased LRP4 protein levels and its surface localization (Figures 3A and 3B), while Hakai depletion led to its accumulation. Co-immunoprecipitation assays confirmed the interaction between Hakai and LRP4, and ubiquitination studies support a role for Hakai in the post-translational regulation of LRP4 stability (Figures 3C–3F). Notably, Hakai-mediated regulation of LRP4 is likely to be context-dependent, which may limit detectable turnover under basal conditions while enabling more robust regulation upon pathway activation. These findings identify LRP4 as a novel Hakai-associated protein and suggest that Hakai may contribute to the modulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in CRC, at least in part, through the regulation of LRP4-associated inhibitory effects on LRP6. According to this, a recent study demonstrates that although WNT-induced FZD-LRP6 interaction occurs, this is not sufficient to initiate downstream Wnt/β-catenin signaling, indicating that additional regulatory mechanisms are required for full pathway activation.37 Moreover, the co-expression of CBLL1 and LGR5 was proposed for the identification of a subpopulation of patients with CMS2 with more aggressive biological characteristics.28 Notably, this may include cooperation with LRP6 to overcome LRP4-mediated inhibition, a mechanism consistent with previous reports of LRP4 antagonizing LRP6 signaling, especially in developmental contexts.9,10 We further evaluated the impact of pharmacological inhibition of Hakai using Hakin-1, a compound targeting the HYB domain responsible for its ubiquitin-ligase activity.22 Treatment with Hakin-1 significantly modulates tumoursphere growth and stemness features (Figures 5C–5G), while increasing differentiation markers such as MUC2, which is indicative of goblet cell lineage commitment.38 Importantly, Hakin-1 also exerted effects on pre-established tumourspheres, demonstrating its potential in both preventive and therapeutic contexts (Figures 5 and S11). These results were further corroborated by increased E-cadherin and LRP4 expression (Figure 6), consistent with the modulation of epithelial features and attenuation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling.

The interplay between Hakai and Wnt/β-catenin signaling refines our understanding of CSC regulation. While mutations in APC, CTNNB1, or AXIN frequently lead to Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation in CRC3,39,40 our findings suggest that such genetic alterations are not sufficient to induce a full CSC phenotype. Instead, CSCs may require additional signals or co-factors to enhance Wnt/β-catenin signaling activity. Only cells with the highest nuclear β-catenin activity exhibit CSC traits,5 highlighting the importance of post-translational regulators such as Hakai in enhancing Wnt/β-catenin pathway. In agreement with this, immunofluorescence analyses revealed increased nuclear β-catenin intensity in Hakai-overexpressing cells (Figure 4C). Indeed, multiple E3 ubiquitin-ligases modulate Wnt signaling through degradation of pathway components. For example, Mindbomb 1 enhances Wnt/β-catenin activity by targeting the non-canonical receptor RYK,41 while Itch ubiquitinates LRP6 to promote endocytosis and downstream signaling.42 Compared to these ligases, Hakai appears to occupy a unique niche by simultaneously targeting both E-cadherin and LRP4, thereby linking E-cadherin regulation and Wnt/β-catenin signaling within a common regulatory axis. In addition to modulating both signaling pathways, Hakai may also influence metabolic adaptations essential for CSC maintenance. Proteomic profiling revealed that Hakai silencing leads to a marked reduction in PRPS2 (Figures S4B and S13), a purine biosynthesis enzyme regulated by MYC signaling and previously implicated in supporting CSC survival and proliferation.43,44 Given that C-MYC was the first gene recognized as a target of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in CRC,45 this finding suggests that Hakai might promote metabolic activity indirectly through its impact on Wnt-driven C-MYC expression. These results are consistent with our previous results, which demonstrated an association between CBLL1 expression and the CMS2 subtype in patients with CRC, also characterized by Myc pathway activation.28 This opens the possibility that Hakai may exert a metabolic influence that complements its known effects on signaling and differentiation, suggesting a new direction for future investigation.

Mechanistically, our findings suggest that Hakai not only facilitates Wnt/β-catenin signaling by the regulation of LRP4 stability, but may also contribute to LRP6-mediated pathway activation. Reporter assays under WNT3A stimulation showed that co-expression of Hakai and LRP6 restored Wnt/β-catenin activity suppressed by LRP4 more effectively than either factor alone (Figure 4B). These observations imply that Hakai helps counteract LRP4’s inhibitory role, possibly by reducing inhibitory effects and thereby facilitating the assembly or function of activating complexes. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports describing the antagonistic role of LRP4 in modulating LRP6-mediated Wnt/β-catenin activation, particularly in developmental systems such as limb formation, bone development, and the neuromuscular junction,9,10,13,36,46 and aligns with models of Wnt/β-catenin signaling that emphasize the importance of the changing interactions and arrangements of receptors on the cell surface.47

Hakin-1’s therapeutic profile compares favorably with other agents targeting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. For instance, anti-LRP6 nanobodies and R-spondin 3 (RSPO3) inhibitors reduce tumor growth and induce differentiation by exhausting the CSC pool48,49 Similarly, APC restoration in inducible mouse models drives differentiation and suppresses tumor relapse.50 Like these agents, Hakin-1 promotes MUC2 expression and downregulates LGR5, suggesting a robust differentiation response. Notably, unlike broad Wnt inhibitors, Hakin-1 has not been associated with systemic toxicity, based on previous histopathological analyses in treated animals.22 However, the in vivo impact of Hakai’s pharmacological inhibition on the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade remains to be elucidated. Targeting E3 ubiquitin-ligases for cancer therapy is gaining traction due to their substrate specificity and regulatory capacity. While bortezomib and carfilzomib target the proteasome broadly, leading to side effects such as neuropathy and myelosuppression,51,52 E3 ubiquitin-ligase inhibitors such as MLN4924, which targets the NEDD8-activating enzyme to inhibit Cullin-RING ligases, and Hakin-1, demonstrate greater substrate specificity and improved lower toxicity.53,54 Hakin-1 treatment resulted in a marked reduction in CSC tumoursphere number and size. Although apoptosis was not directly assessed in the CSC tumoursphere model, previous in vitro and in vivo studies have consistently shown that Hakai inhibition does not induce apoptotic cell death but rather limits tumor growth by reducing proliferation and promoting tumor cell differentiation.22 Notably, Hakai-silencing shows a more pronounced effect on cell proliferation compared to the more limited impact of Hakin-1 targeting Hakai’s E3 ubiquitin-ligase activity. Importantly, although Hakai silencing reduced cell viability, this effect was not associated with increased apoptosis (Figure S1E). These findings support the notion that the observed reduction in tumoursphere growth reflects alterations in CSC-associated functional properties, including reduced proliferative capacity, rather than increased cell death. This is consistent with previous reports showing that full knockdown of Hakai markedly inhibits proliferation and decreases Cyclin D1 and Ki67 protein levels.22,32 In contrast, Hakin-1 treatment shows only a modest effect in proliferation in vitro and in vivo22 and does not significantly alter proliferation-associated targets such as C-MYC or CCND1 and Ki67 (Figure 5F). These findings support the hypothesis that other functional domains of Hakai may be responsible for its effects on proliferation, as the inhibition of the HYB domain, responsible for its E3 ubiquitin-ligase activity, shows only a limited impact when targeted alone. From a translational perspective, the ability of Hakin-1 to reduce stemness, enhance differentiation, and interfere with both Wnt/β-catenin signaling, together with the modulation of epithelial features, underscores its value as a therapeutic agent capable of targeting key mechanisms underlying cancer resistance and metastasis. Its impact on both the initiation and maintenance of tumourspheres, together with its favorable safety profile, positions it as a strong candidate for combinatorial regimens targeting CSCs alongside standard chemotherapy. While the present study is based on in vitro and cell culture models, the consistent genetic and pharmacological targeting of Hakai and its impact on CSC self-renewal and differentiation suggest that Hakai inhibition may represent an effective strategy to target CSC maintenance in vivo, warranting future validation in animal models.

In conclusion, our findings define a new functional role for Hakai in CRC, highlighting its involvement in CSC regulation and Wnt/β-catenin signaling through LRP4-mediated modulation. The development of Hakin-1 provides a promising therapeutic strategy to target CSCs by inhibiting Hakai E3 ubiquitin-ligase activity, enhancing differentiation, and attenuating Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity. These insights support further investigation of Hakai inhibitors as components of CSC-directed therapies in CRC and offer a rational approach for improving long-term treatment outcomes.

Limitations of the study

This study was primarily conducted using CRC cell lines and tumoursphere models that enrich for CSC-associated properties. Although these systems are widely used to investigate stemness and Wnt/β-catenin signaling, future studies using patient-derived organoids and in vivo models will be important to validate the role of Hakai in CSC properties. In addition, while multiple lines of evidence support a role for Hakai in the post-translational regulation of LRP4 stability, direct assessment of Hakai-dependent changes in LRP4 ubiquitination was limited by the experimental requirements of the assay. The endogenous levels of LRP4 were insufficient for reliable detection and immunoprecipitation under conditions in which Hakai reduced LRP4 abundance. Conversely, the exogenous overexpression of LRP4 required for these analyses likely masked quantitative effects on LRP4 ubiquitination and turnover. Finally, although Hakin-1 reduced stemness-associated features and promoted differentiation in CRC cells, the contribution of additional Hakai-dependent mechanisms beyond LRP4 regulation remains to be determined.

Cell culture

The human male colon cancer cell line HCT116 (ATCC#CCL-247TM) was obtained from the ATCC and were grown in DMEM. Embryonic HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) - High Glucose (Gibco). The human female colon adenocarcinoma HT29 cell line was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and cultured in Mc Coy’s Modified Medium (Gibco). The human colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line Caco-2 was cultured in DMEM supplemented as described later in discussion. HT29 cells with a doxycycline-inducible lentiviral system for shCBLL1 was previously established by our group.27 The doxycycline-inducible lentiviral system for CBLL1 (SMARTvector Inducible Lentiviral shCBLL1) was obtained from Dharmacon (Horizon PerkinElmer Group). Lentiviral particles encoding shRNA for CBLL1 or a non-targeting control (shCONTROL) were generated and propagated following established protocols for transduction into cancer cells. HT29 shCONTROL and HT29 shCBLL1 cells were cultured in Mc Coy’s Medium with doxycycline (1 μg/mL; Sigma- Aldrich) to induce shRNA expression. Following induction, the efficiency of Hakai (CBLL1 gene) knockdown was verified by Western blot analysis. All culture media were supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, 5000 U/ml) and 10% of Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, Corning) for monolayer cultures. Cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with a CO2 concentration of 5%. All cell lines were authenticated by the Genomic Platform of the Universidad Autómoma de Madrid through Short Tandem Repeat profiling, used from early passages and monthly tested for mycoplasma contamination. HT29 cells were selected for inducible lentiviral silencing due to their suitability for stable CSC-enriched tumoursphere assays, whereas other CRC cell lines showed variable transduction efficiency and/or target detectability under comparable conditions.

Method details

Tumoursphere assays for assessing stemness in vitro

Stemness induction in vitro was performed by culturing cells in Stem Cell Medium using flat-bottom ultra-low attachment plates to promote tumoursphere formation. Cells were seeded into ultra-low attachment 6-well plates (Costar 6-well Clear Flat Bottom Ultra-Low Attachment Multiple Well Plates, Corning) at a density of 10 × 104 cells per well in a Stem Cell Medium (20 ng/mL human EGF, 10 ng/mL human b-hFGF, 1× B27 and 1× GlutaMax in DMEM/F-12 Media), and then incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 from 3 to 5 days. Flat bottom plates were used to avoid the formation of cell aggregates instead of true tumourspheres structures. Tumourspheres formation was monitored and characterized by phase-contrast microscopy using an Eclipse-Ti microscope (Nikon). After the incubation period, images of tumourspheres formed in each well were captured. To analyze tumoursphere size, area of at least 15 tumourspheres per experiment was measured using the Freehand Tool in ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). To quantify the number of tumourspheres, self-renewal assays were conducted as follows: cells were seeded at a density of 500 cells/mL in 24-well low-attachment plates, as previously described. After 5 days of culture, tumourspheres were analyzed by microscopy, and the number of tumourspheres was counted. The size and number of tumourspheres were graphically represented using GraphPad software.

Cell viability assay

HT29 cells were seeded and treated with doxycycline to induce Hakai silencing. Cell viability was assessed 48 h post-treatment using an MTT assay. For tumoursphere assays, cells were seeded in ultra-low attachment in absence or presence of doxycycline to induce shCBLL1. Cells were incubated with 0.5 mg/mL MTT for 3 h at 37 °C, after which formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 μL DMSO. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm with a reference wavelength of 630 nm using an Infinite200 NanoQuant plate reader (Tecan). Data were obtained from six replicates, normalized to DMSO-treated controls, and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Western blot analysis

Cellular proteins were extracted using lysis buffer containing 1% Triton X-100, 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), and 150 mM NaCl, supplemented with protease inhibitors: 10 μg/mL aprotinin, 10 μg/mL leupeptin, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). To prevent the activity of deubiquitinating enzymes during ubiquitination and co-immunoprecipitation assays, 10 mM N-ethylmaleimide (Sigma-Aldrich) was added. Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein were resolved by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked for 1 h at RT under agitation in blocking buffer [5% non-fat dry milk (Sigma-Aldrich) in TBS-T (20 mM Tris base, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20, pH 7.4)]. After blocking, membranes were cropped and incubated with specific primary antibodies to the proteins of interest, followed by incubation with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signal detection was performed using the Luminata Crescendo Western HRP Substrate (Millipore) and visualized with the Amersham Imager 600 system (GE Healthcare). All experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates. Representative images are shown, and the full blots of all replicates are provided in the supplementary material (Figures S2–S10, S12, and S13). Quantitative densitometric analyses are reported in Table S2.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRI Reagent (Molecular Research Center, Inc.) and converted to cDNA using the NZY First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (NZYTech). qRT-PCR was performed using LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master (Roche) on an LightCycler 480 Instrument (Roche). Comparative CT method (ΔΔCT method) was performed to analyze qPCR data using β-ACTIN as a housekeeping control. Primer sequences are detailed in Table S3.

Proteomic analysis

Equal amounts of each sample were reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) for 1 h at 37 °C under agitation. Subsequently, alkylation with 50 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) was performed in the dark for 45 min at RT. Samples were digested with sequencing grade-modified trypsin (Promega) at 1:30 enzyme-to substrate ratio. After 17 h of digestion at 37 °C under agitation, samples were acidified with 10% trifluoroacetic acid to ∼ pH 3 to stop the reaction and then, samples were concentrated using an under speed-vacuum (SpeedVac, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and sonicated for 3 min. Subsequently, the digested peptides were desalted using in-house made stage tips (3M Empore SPE-C18 disk, 47 mm, Sigma-Aldrich). The dried eluates were reconstituted in water containing 0.1% FA for direct liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis. 200 ng of peptide mixture were loaded in a nanoElute (Bruker Daltonics) nano-flow LC that was coupled to a high resolution timsTOF Pro (Bruker Daltonics) with a CaptiveSpray ion source (Bruker Daltonics). Liquid chromatography was conducted at 50 °C with a constant flow of 400 nL/min on a reversed-phase column (15 cm × 75 m i.d.) with a pulled emitter tip, packed with 1.9 m C18-coated porous silica beads (Dr. Maisch, Ammerbuch-Entringen, Germany). Chromatographic separation was performed using a linear gradient of 5–35% Buffer B (100% acetonitrile and 0.1% FA) over a period of 30 min, followed by an increase to 95% within 2 min and maintaining at 95% for 8 min. To prevent the sample carryover, two blank injections were performed after each sample run to prevent any residual material. This involved a 30 min short gradient total run time (transitioning from 2% to 95% of Solvent B over 5 min; then from 95% to 5% B during another 5 min; maintaining 5% B for 5 min, reaching 95% at 25 min, and finally holding at 95% for 5 min to wash the column). Peptides underwent electrospray ionization (ESI) and were analyzed using data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode with parallel accumulation-serial fragmentation (PASEF) enabled. Raw data files were analyzed to obtain protein identifications using MaxQuant and LFQ Analyst was used for the analysis of Label-Free Quantitative (LFQ) proteomic datasets. Statistical analyses were carried out with an in-house R script using the “MaxQuant” “proteinGroup.txt” as the primary input file in conjunction with an experimental design text file, which describes conditions and replicates. Contaminant proteins, reverse sequences, proteins identified “only by site” or with “only by a single peptide” were removed. LFQ intensities data were converted to a log2 scale, and replicates were grouped by conditions based on the information provided in the “experimental design.txt” file. Missing values were imputed using the “Missing not At Random” (MNAR) method, which uses random draws from a Gaussian distribution left-shifted by 1.8 SD (standard deviation) with a width of 0.3. To determine significantly regulated proteins between conditions, a cutoff of an adjusted p-value cutoff of 0.05 (Benjamini−Hochberg method) along with a fold change of 1.5 and a requirement 74 of at least two peptides was applied.

Plasmid transfection

Cell lines were transfected with plasmids pcDNA3.1, pcDNA-FLAG-Hakai and pBSSR-HA-Ubiquitin which were kindly provided by Yasuyuki Fujita (Hokkaido University, Japan). pcDNA-LRP4 was a kindly gift of Tatsuo Suzuki (Shinshu University School of Medicine, Japan). pcDNA-His6-Ubiquitin was kindly provided by Carmen Rivas (Center for Research in Molecular Medicine and Chronic Diseases (CiMUS)).56 Plasmids for TOPFlash and FOPFlash reporter plasmids, Renilla luciferase plasmid pRLTK, pcDNA3-Myc-human LRP6, pcDNA3-MESD and pcDNA3-V5-Hakai were kindly provided by Madelon Maurice (Utrecht, Netherlands).55,57 The transfection experiments were performed using FuGENE 6 Transfection Reagent (Promega) and Opti-MEM Reduced Serum Medium (Thermo Fisher), following the manufacturer’s protocol. HT29 cells with a doxycycline-inducible lentiviral system for shCBLL1 was previously established by our group. Doxycycline-inducible lentiviral system targeting Hakai (SMARTvector Inducible Lentiviral shCBLL1) was obtained from Dharmacon (Horizon PerkinElmer Group). Lentiviral particles were produced following previously published protocols. Doxycycline (Sigma) was added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 1 μg/mL, and cells were maintained under induction conditions for 72 h. HT29 colon cancer cells were transduced with viral supernatants carrying either Hakai-specific shRNA or a non-targeting control shRNA. Stable clones were selected, and the efficiency of Hakai silencing was assessed by Western blot analysis.

Immunoprecipitation

For immunoprecipitation (IP) experiments, after indicated plasmid transfections, cells were lysed using lysis buffer containing 1% Triton X-100, 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), and 150 mM NaCl, supplemented with 10 μg/mL leupeptin, 10 μg/mL aprotinin, 1 mM PMSF and 10 mM N-ethylmaleimide for 30 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation at 18,000×g for 10 min, supernatants were immunoprecipitated with 2.5 μg of anti-LRP4 antibody or mouse IgG (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) at 4 °C for 2 h, bound to 60 μL protein G PLUS-Agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Immunoprecipitated proteins and input control protein lysates were then loaded into SDS-PAGE gels and analyzed by Western blot using the indicated antibodies.

Ubiquitination assays

Ubiquitination assays were performed under denaturing conditions. Briefly, cells transfected with the indicated plasmids were lysed in buffer containing 6 M guanidinium-HCl, 0.1 M Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, and 0.01 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), supplemented with 5 mM imidazole and 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 18,000 × g for 10 min, and supernatants were incubated with 100 μL of Ni2+-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific), pre-equilibrated in lysis buffer, for 2 h at room temperature. Beads were washed sequentially with the following buffers: (I) lysis buffer; (II) 8 M urea, 0.1 M Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, and 0.01 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) supplemented with 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol; (III) 8 M urea, 0.1 M Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, and 0.01 M Tris-HCl (pH 6.3) (buffer A); (IV) buffer A containing 0.2% Triton X-100; (V) buffer A containing 0.1% Triton X-100; and (VI) buffer A alone. Bound proteins were eluted in 50 μL of elution buffer containing 200 mM imidazole, 5% SDS, 0.15 M Tris-HCl (pH 6.7), 30% glycerol, and 0.72 M β-mercaptoethanol. Eluted proteins and input lysates were resolved by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by Western blot using the indicated antibodies.

TOPFlash luciferase reporter assay

HCT116 cells were seeded into 24-well plates and grown overnight. The next day, cells were transfected with 30 ng of the reporter plasmid TOPFlash or FOPFlash, 5 ng Thymidine Kinase (TK)-Renilla and 50–150 ng of each of the indicated plasmids. Plasmids concentrations were 150 ng pcDNA-V5-Hakai, 50 ng pcDNA-LRP4, 100 ng MYC-LRP6 + 25 ng MESD and 100 ng MYC-LGR5, respectively. All transfections were performed with FuGENE 6 Transfection Reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Six hours post transfection, cells were incubated with control L1-cell conditioned medium (LCM) or WNT3A-conditioned medium (WCM) overnight. LCM, the control conditioned medium derived from mouse L cells, was used to assess the effects under basal Wnt pathway conditions. In contrast, WCM, the conditioned medium produced by mouse WNT3A-expressing L cells, was used to evaluate the effect of each protein when the pathway was activated by WNT3A. LCM and WCM were produced as described57 After 24 h, total cell lysates were extracted with Passive Lysis Buffer (Promega) for 20 min at room temperature and levels of Renilla and Firefly luciferase was measured using the Dual-Luciferase kit (Promega) according to manufacturer’s instructions using a Centro LB960 luminometer (Berthold). TOP/FOP ratio was calculated to quantify Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway activity after normalization of the firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity.

Immunofluorescence assays

For immunofluorescence experiments of cells, HEK293T or HCT116 cells were seeded on laminin-coated glass coverslips in 24-well plates at a density of 0.25 cm2. After overnight incubation, cells were transfected with PCDNA-V5-Hakai, PCDNA-LRP4 and/or empty vector PCDNA3.1 for 24 h. The next day, cells were fixed in 4% PFA for 30 min at room temperature. After the incubation, the reaction was quenched in 0.05 M NH4Cl for 15 min. Cells were blocked in PBS containing 2% BSA and 0.1% Saponin. Primary and secondary antibody incubations were performed in blocking buffer for 1 h and 45 min at room temperature, respectively. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Life Technologies) 1:5000 diluted in PBS for 5 min and cells were mounted for microscopy using ProLong Gold antifade reagent (Life Technologies). For LRP4 cell-surface analysis, images were acquired using an LSM700 confocal microscope. For β-catenin localization analyses, images were acquired using an Olympus microscope and analyzed using Python 3.10 with NumPy and Matplotlib. Results were obtained from three independent experiments and expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was determined using an unpaired t test (∗p < 0.05).

To perform immunofluorescence assays of tumourspheres, tumourspheres culture were collected and centrifuged. After washing with cold PBS, tumourspheres of each ultra-low attachment 6-well were seeded in one well of an 8- well chamber (Millicell EZ SLIDE 8-well glass, Millipore) and they were fixed in 200 μL of 4% PFA in PBS for 40 min at room temperature, then permeabilized and blocked with PBD 0.2 T buffer [1% BSA, 1% DMSO, 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS] for 1 h. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 3 h in the dark. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Life Technologies) 1:5000 diluted in PBS for 5 min and tumourspheres were mounted for microscopy using ProLong Gold antifade reagent (Life Technologies). Images were acquired with a Nikon A1R confocal microscope and analyzed using Python version 3.10 with NumPy and Matplotlib to measure fluorophore intensities. Results were quantified from five pictures in three replicate experiments, expressed as mean ± SEM, and analyzed by t test for statistical significance.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Results are presented as mean ± SEM, as indicated in the figures. Statistical analysis and graphical representations were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 8, GraphPad Software). The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of the data and determine whether they followed a Gaussian distribution. Statistical significance was evaluated using a t test for comparisons between two groups, or ANOVA for comparisons involving three or more groups. If the ANOVA revealed significant differences, post-hoc tests were conducted to identify which groups differed significantly. Dunnett’s post-hoc test was used when comparing multiple treatment groups to a single control. The results are presented in the figures as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
