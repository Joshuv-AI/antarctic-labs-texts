Non‐Vital Decoronation of a Maxillary Central Incisor: A Case Report With 18 Months Follow‐Up
Authors: Maryam Enteghad, Zahra Lotf Haghpanah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn the present case, decoronation and root submergence of a necrotic maxillary central incisor were performed for a 13‐year‐old girl. The root canal was filled with mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA), which was covered with resin‐modified glass ionomer. Apexification with MTA was performed for her other maxillary central incisor, and the edentulous region was restored using a fiber‐reinforced composite bridge. Long‐term follow‐up indicated that decoronation might be a viable option to maintain the alveolar bone.

Introduction

Crown‐root fractures are characterized by the involvement of enamel, dentin, and cementum. These types of fractures are observed in approximately 5% of dental traumas among young children [1]. Managing these fractures in the aesthetic zone during growth presents significant challenges for pediatric dentists. According to the guidelines from dental traumatology and the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD), recommended treatment options include fragment reattachment, orthodontic or surgical extrusion of the apical segment and post‐placement, root submergence, and extraction [2]. Several factors affect the choice of treatment, including the extent and orientation of the fracture line, the involvement of the pulp, the maturity of the tooth, and the length of the remaining root in the alveolar bone [3, 4]. Among these, the location of the fracture line is the most critical, as it directly affects the restorability of the tooth. In cases of fractures that cannot be restored, potential complications from the early loss of the tooth, such as space loss, crowding, habits such as tongue thrusting, and significant loss of alveolar bone height should be considered. The treatment plan's objectives in such instances should focus on preventing space loss, addressing harmful habits, and preserving the alveolar ridge for future placement of a pontic or implant [5].

Decoronation and root submergence is a treatment method aimed at preserving alveolar bone [6]. This approach is generally more cost‐effective than alveolar ridge augmentation when vertical bone growth is necessary for future implant or prosthetic procedures. Typically, root submergence involves cutting the root 1 to 2 mm beneath the alveolar bone level and fully covering it with a gingival tissue flap [7]. Depending on the specific clinical scenario, vital or nonvital root submergence may be pursued. Vital submergence is particularly preferable due to its positive outcomes, particularly regarding the completion of root development [8]. Rodd et al. [9] found a 91% clinical success rate in a study involving 29 nonvital and 24 vital permanent incisor roots that were intentionally retained following crown‐root fractures in children. This treatment is suggested for teeth that have become ankylosed because of trauma and have experienced infraocclusion [10]. Most documented instances of decoronation in the literature aim to avert serious bony defects in younger patients, as their alveolar ridges continue to grow while the root stays ankylosed [11, 12, 13]. According to Andersson et al. [14], young patients with intentionally retained roots face a lower probability of needing bone grafting before getting implants because the volume of their alveolar ridge is preserved.

Limited research has been conducted on the outcomes of non‐vital root submergence in permanent teeth among children following fracture [1, 15]. However, there is limited knowledge regarding the effectiveness of this technique for a necrotic pulp long after a traumatic injury and in the presence of a periapical lesion. This report describes the successful management of anterior maxillary teeth in a 13‐year‐old girl following trauma, which included apexification of one tooth, conventional root canal treatment (RCT) of another, and root submergence of a non‐restorable tooth, thereby restoring both function and aesthetics in the anterior maxilla.

Case History/Examination

A 13‐year‐old girl visited the Endodontic Department at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences with a primary complaint of crown fractures in teeth #11, #21, and #22. The teeth were fractured after she fell while running. According to her parents, 3 months after the injury, she visited a dentist in Afghanistan, her home country, who performed a root canal treatment on tooth #11 and restored teeth #21 and #22.

When she came to the Endodontic Department, 2 years had passed since the treatment, and the restorations had fallen out (Figure 1). The orifices of the teeth were exposed, and the remaining structure showed signs of recurrent caries.

A baseline photograph of the patient shows severe carious lesions on the anterior maxillary teeth. The periapical radiograph reveals a gutta‐percha cone from the previously treated tooth #11. Additionally, periapical radiolucency is observed at the apex of teeth #11, #21, and #22.

All three teeth tested negative for the electric pulp test and the cold test. Tooth #11 was diagnosed with previously endodontically treated with an infected root canal system, and teeth #21 and #22 were diagnosed with pulpless and infected root canal systems. The teeth were not sensitive to percussion tests. Tooth #11 exhibited soft swelling and sensitivity to palpation, while teeth #21 and #22 showed no sensitivity. A pre‐apical radiograph revealed apical radiolucency in all three teeth (Figure 1). Due to the presence of soft swelling, tooth #11 was diagnosed with an acute apical abscess, while teeth #21 and #22, which did not have swelling, were classified as having chronic apical periodontitis. The probing depth was normal for all teeth.

After consulting with specialists in the restorative department, it was determined that tooth #21 was hopeless because the caries had extended into the root. However, teeth #11 and #22 were deemed restorable following RCT, although their prognosis was questionable. Given the immaturity of the root canal apex of tooth #11, apexification with the placement of an apical plug was recommended. For tooth #22, primary RCT was planned. After explaining the treatment plan to her parents, a written informed consent form was obtained.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment)

Tooth #11 underwent apexification in two visits. In the first session, local anesthesia was administered using an infiltration technique with 2% lidocaine and 1:80,000 epinephrine (Darou Pakhsh Pharmaceutical Manufacturing Co. Tehran, Iran). The tooth was then isolated with a rubber dam. Dental caries was excavated with a round bur, and a loose gutta‐percha cone was removed. The working length of the root canals was determined using an apex locator and confirmed by a radiograph. The root canals were then minimally prepared to remove the infectious smear layer from the root canal walls. During the instrumentation process, the canals were irrigated thoroughly with 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution, utilizing side‐vented irrigation needles that reached within 2–3 mm of the working length. Finally, the canals were rinsed with normal saline and dried using paper cones. Calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) was placed in the root canals for disinfection, and the tooth was restored temporarily with resin‐modified glass ionomer (RMGI; Fuji II LC, GC, Tokyo, Japan).

In the second session, the temporary filling material was removed. After thorough irrigation, a 3–5‐mm mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA; Angelus, Brazil) plug was placed into the root canal because the tooth apex was immature. The remaining length of the root canal was obturated by a thermoplastic gutta‐percha. Finally, the tooth was restored with a temporary filling material. Tooth #22 underwent primary RCT in the same session using an AH Plus sealer (Dentsply Sirona, Switzerland) and gutta‐percha cones (Figure 2).

Tooth #11 underwent apexification using an MTA plug, while tooth #22 received primary. To restore these teeth, Ribbond fibers were placed in a U‐shaped configuration to reinforce the cervical areas of the crowns. Subsequently, FRC posts were cemented within the fibers.

To restore the #11 tooth, a 2‐mm cervical gutta‐percha cone was removed. The tooth was etched, and two layers of bonding agent (All‐Bond Universal; Bisco Inc., IL, United States) were applied. After curing, a small amount of flowable bulk‐fill resin composite (Tetric N‐Flow Bulk Fill, Ivoclar Vivadent) was placed in the root canal, and the Ribbond fibers (Ribbond Inc., Seattle, WA) were placed in a U‐shaped configuration to reinforce the cervical area of the crowns of teeth #11 (Figure 2). After curing the composite resin, a fiber‐reinforced composite (FRC) post (White Post DC, FGM; Joinville; SC, Brazil) was fixed within the Ribbond fiber using flowable bulk‐fill resin composite. Then, the tooth was restored with composite resin (Tokuyama Asteria). Tooth #22 was restored using the Ribbond fibers and composite resin. Oral hygiene instructions were provided to the patient, along with a recommendation for regular follow‐up visits.

After 6 months, the patient returned, and her oral hygiene was satisfactory (Figure 3A). However, she expressed concern regarding tooth #21. Given the patient's young age, it was decided to maintain the alveolar bone associated with tooth #21 and restore the crown using restorative treatments like an FRC bridge. Therefore, decoronation of this tooth was recommended. Due to the necrosis of this tooth, it was necessary to clean and obturate the root canal.

(A) After 6 months, the patient returned to the Restorative Department, where the restorations were functional, and the gingival tissue appeared healthy. (B) Tooth #21 underwent decoronation and root submergence after root disinfection and obturation using MTA. (C) The edentulous region was restored by attaching Ribbond fibers to the abutment teeth.

In the first session of treating tooth #21, local anesthesia was administered. Using a scalpel blade #15, the intra‐sulcus incision was made around the tooth. The buccal mucoperiosteal flap was elevated, exposing the vestibular cortical bone of the teeth #11, #21, and #22. Using a surgical high‐speed drill and under irrigation with saline, the coronal remnants of the tooth were removed approximately 2 mm below the marginal bone. Then, dental caries and the necrotic pulp tissue were removed. The root canal was prepared, and the infectious smear layer was eliminated. After copious irrigation using 5.25% NaOCl solution, the root canal was filled with MTA. Then, MTA was covered with RMGIC. After irrigating the surgical field, the wound edges were approximated and sutured. Figure 3B shows a periapical radiography of tooth #21 after submerging.

After 5 months, the edentulous region was restored by placing an FRC bridge using Ribbond fiber (Figure 3C). A rubber dam was placed and wide boxes were prepared on the facial surface of the abutment teeth (#11 and #22). Then, they were sandblasted and thoroughly irrigated. A silane coupling agent (Bis‐Silane; Bisco, IL, USA) was applied, and two layers of bonding agent were applied to the teeth, and each layer was cured. Then, a small amount of flowable bulk‐fill resin composite was placed over the prepared boxes. A proper length of Ribbond fiber was placed over the flowable composite resin and formed. Then, the flowable composite resin was cured, and finally, the composite resin (Tokuyama Asteria) was placed in 2 mm layers over the Ribbond fiber, and each layer was cured.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient returned for a follow‐up appointment 18 months after the tooth restoration. Both teeth were asymptomatic, and a clinical examination showed no signs or symptoms of endodontic issues. A periapical radiograph indicated normal healing around the apices of all teeth, with a healthy lamina dura (Figure 4). There were no signs of resorption observed in the root of tooth #21. Additionally, the patient expressed satisfaction with the aesthetics of her teeth.

A photograph and radiograph of the patient after 18 months indicate a functional FRC bridge and bone healing around the submerged root. Teeth #11 and #22 exhibited normal periapical tissue, and the apical radiolucency had been resolved.

Discussion

The premature loss of primary anterior teeth can lead to reduced masticatory efficiency, speech difficulties, significant stress for children, and adverse psychological effects due to compromised aesthetics [16]. Additionally, bone resorption of the alveolar ridge is an unavoidable consequence following tooth extraction. Extracting tooth #21 would have resulted in notable bone loss, making future implant placement more complicated as the patient ages [5].

Having chosen to decoronate and submerge the root, it is essential to consider the management of the root canal system. Options include attempting to preserve the vitality of the submerged root, performing pulp extirpation (pulpectomy) while leaving the root canal empty, or placing an intraradicular dressing or obturation [17]. The management of the root canal system in a decoronated tooth lacks robust evidence, and various factors can influence decision‐making. These factors encompass the stage of root development, the extent of the injury, timing, clinical and radiographic indicators, symptoms of pulpal and periradicular disease, previous root canal treatment history, patient age, and compliance [17]. In this current study, the patient was referred several months post‐injury and previous restoration. The tooth exhibited necrotic pulp and signs of chronic apical periodontitis. Consequently, it was determined to proceed with nonvital root submergence to help preserve the alveolar bone.

Traditionally, the decoronation technique entails performing a coronectomy of the ankylosed tooth just below the cementoenamel junction (CEJ), removing the pulp, stimulating blood flow in the root canal by irritating the periapical area, and allowing the canal space to fill with blood, thus promoting gradual root resorption and bone replacement [18]. However, in this study, the goal was to retain the root. Therefore, MTA was chosen for canal obturation due to the infection present and its sealing capabilities, biocompatibility, and potential for bone induction [19, 20, 21]. After 18 months, the submerged root displayed successful healing, and the restorations functioned properly. Previous studies have also demonstrated non‐vital root submergence using MTA or gutta‐percha. Boyer and Ywom [19] detailed a case involving a middle‐aged woman where root submergence was performed on an unrestorable and infected maxillary central incisor through decoronation and root‐end surgery. Similar to this study, they utilized MTA for canal obturation and restored the edentulous spaces with a fixed partial denture (FPD). After a year, they observed signs of apical healing and reported high patient satisfaction. They covered the root with mineralized cortical particulate allograft and a resorbable collagen membrane. Moreover, Padmawar et al. [1] described root submergence in a 12‐year‐old healthy boy following an Ellis # III fracture related to the permanent mandibular right lateral incisor. They obturated the tooth with 6% gutta‐percha and Ah Plus (Dentsply) sealer, followed by restoration utilizing glass ionomer cement. Keçeli et al. [15] presented a case of non‐vital root submergence aimed at preserving alveolar bone growth after a cervical root fracture in an 8‐year‐old boy. They similarly opted for MTA to obturate the tooth due to the immature apex and the delayed referral following the injury. They also covered the MTA plug, while the access cavity was sealed with high‐strength glass ionomer cement. Glass ionomer cement has shown good compatibility with periodontal tissues, promoting cellular adhesion and enhancing mechanical strength [22]. Additionally, a study highlighted an 8 mm gain in clinical attachment and consistent bone regeneration surrounding GIC restorations over an 18‐month period [23]. GIC was also selected to prevent MTA washout from the root canal.

The management of the edentulous area following the root submergence of tooth #21 involved using a FRC bridge made with Ribbond. Ribbond, available since 1992, is constructed from pre‐impregnated, silanized, plasma‐treated, leno‐woven ultra high molecular weight polyethylene fibers [24]. The combination of flexibility and strength inherent in Ribbond supports effective retention of resin‐composite strip crowns, which enhances both aesthetic and functional results. The Ribbond bridge presents multiple advantages for anterior teeth in young children, including minimal tooth preparation that helps preserve healthy tooth structure. Its noninvasive approach allows for immediate aesthetic restoration. Moreover, the incorporation of fiber reinforcement offers added strength and durability, making it a dependable option for temporary restorations. Additionally, Ribbond bridges are cost‐effective and can be completed chairside [25]. The current study demonstrated that utilizing this method resulted in satisfactory outcomes for the patient and durable treatment lasting 18 months.

For tooth #11, an MTA apical plug was placed to address the issue of the immature apex. Treating immature root apices along with necrotic pulp poses significant challenges for clinicians due to potential issues with sealing, inadequate root canal instrumentation, and difficulties in establishing an accurate apical stop. Apexification is a traditional technique aimed at addressing immature, non‐vital teeth by inducing a calcified barrier in a root with an open apex or facilitating further apical development in teeth with incomplete roots and necrotic pulp. The apical plug technique proves to be an effective solution for managing immature teeth that present with pulp necrosis, as it creates an apical barrier that ensures a reliable seal while significantly reducing treatment time. Calcium silicate‐based materials have become the most commonly used options for apexification. MTA, which consists mainly of tricalcium silicate, tricalcium aluminate, tricalcium oxide, and silica, is made up of fine hydrophilic particles that generate a colloidal gel, which ultimately solidifies into a rigid structure [20]. MTA is considered the ideal material for the apical plug technique due to its various advantages. It can set effectively even in the presence of blood, maintains a high pH, and serves as a suitable apical plug for immature, non‐vital teeth by promoting the formation of cementitious tissue in the periradicular area [20]. The use of MTA as an apical plug encourages hard tissue development, resulting in a biological seal formed by cementum over the material, and successful healing was observed in this case.

Teeth #11 and #22 cervical regions were reinforced using a combination of Ribbond and a prefabricated glass fiber post. The use of an intracanal post is recommended for teeth that have lost more than 50% of their coronal structure while retaining at least 1 mm of tooth structure above the gingival margin [26]. FRC posts are particularly suitable when the focus is on stress distribution, aesthetics, and conserving the remaining tooth structure. FRC posts demonstrate positive flexibility and effective stress distribution along the root, boasting a success rate of around 93%. Their elastic modulus is similar to that of dentin, which facilitates more natural force absorption and minimizes stress concentration [27]. Additionally, the application of Ribbond posts significantly enhances the fracture resistance of teeth [28]. A critical factor for the success of customized ribbon fibers is ensuring they are positioned as close as possible to the cavity walls, enabling the fibers to absorb energy and improve resistance [28]. In this study, Ribbond fibers were arranged in a U‐shape to increase their proximity to all aspects of the tooth.

The long‐term objective of the treatment outlined was to maintain anterior maxillary teeth with uncertain prognoses, helping to prevent alveolar bone resorption of teeth deemed hopeless while restoring both function and aesthetics for the patient. Once the patient attains maturity, the submerged roots can be extracted, and an implant can be placed to optimize aesthetic and functional results, leading to a more predictable treatment [18]. Although the treatment strategy discussed can be viewed as a temporary solution for a missing tooth, it is an accessible, functional, aesthetic, and relatively cost‐effective response to the clinical issue at hand.
