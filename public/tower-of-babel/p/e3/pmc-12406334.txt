Health service needs, use, and barriers among formerly incarcerated women survivors of sexual violence
Authors: Mary Kathryn Allison, Marley F Fradley, Whitney K Norris, Melissa J Zielinski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Incarcerated women commonly report lifetime sexual violence victimization, have high rates of many physical and mental illnesses, and ultimately return to their communities with significant healthcare needs.Objective: This study qualitatively examined formerly incarcerated women sexual violence survivors’ health service utilization and perceived barriers to accessing health care – including primary care, mental health care, and substance use treatment services – three-to-five years post-release.Method: We conducted semi-structured qualitative interviews and administered self-report surveys with 65 women.Results: Interviews revealed some barriers that were consistent across healthcare service type, including health insurance coverage, healthcare costs, competing obligations, care interruptions, and intrapersonal factors; other barriers were unique to service type. Although women reported barriers to primary care engagement, most still reported engagement. However, relatively few were attending any form of specialty mental health care – ongoing psychiatric care was especially uncommon – despite that many participants were experiencing mental and/or behavioural health concerns. Very few had received evidence-based trauma therapy and treatment for sexual violence victimization specifically was rarely mentioned. Participants reported significant barriers to accessing therapy, including interpersonal factors and competing obligations that interfere with appointment attendance.Conclusions: This study reveals the need for behavioural healthcare – including treatment for sexual violence victimization – and healthcare navigation resources in the years following incarceration, as well as the need for new models of mental health care management, to improve healthcare access for this underserved population.KEYWORDS: Sexual violence, healthcare access, women’s health, primary care access, incarceration

Women who become incarcerated have experienced sexual violence at higher rates than community samples of women and men (Karlsson & Zielinski, 2020). In fact, past studies have revealed that 56–82% of incarcerated women report having experienced sexual victimization at some point in their lifetime (Karlsson & Zielinski, 2020). Sexual violence victimization sequelae, like mental illness (Dworkin et al., 2017; Hayes, 2015; Lynch et al., 2012; Villa, 2017) and substance use (Fazel et al., 2006), are also highly prevalent and may contribute to the co-occurring high rates of chronic illness (Binswanger et al., 2009; Knittel et al., 2021) poverty, unemployment, and violence victimization that are seen in this population and contribute to known health disparities when compared to women generally (Green et al., 2016; Karlsson & Zielinski, 2020; Kennedy et al., 2020; Moloney et al., 2009). These same (i.e. substance use, mental illness) and related (e.g. sex work) sequelae are also known contributors to incarceration and recidivism (Grella et al., 2005; Scott et al., 2014). For example, posttraumatic stress symptoms, commonly reported following sexual assault (Dworkin et al., 2023), are associated with drug use severity (Back et al., 2000; Mccauley et al., 2012). Drug use, even after treatment (Glasner-Edwards et al., 2013), is a predictor of post-release relapse and recidivism (Kubiak, 2004). Abrams and colleagues found that incarcerated women with histories of sexual violence victimization were more likely to use drugs and engage in sex work (Abrams et al., 2008). Despite this, not all carceral settings offer treatment for addressing sequelae associated with sexual violence victimization (Park, 2022) and thus women are likely to return to the community from incarceration with substantial mental, physical, and behavioural healthcare needs. However, little is known about women’s healthcare service access and utilization post-release, especially past the immediate post-release period – a gap that we aimed to fill in the current study.

Background

Health care during community reentry

The time immediately following release from prison or jail is known to be challenging due to issues accessing health care, including lack of health insurance, lack of adequate discharge planning, and issues with continuity of care (Wang et al., 2008). Many people leave prison without medical records, sufficient medication, or a referral to health care in the community (Wakeman et al., 2009). Incarcerated women in Besney et al.’s qualitative study expressed frustration in the prison system providing little transition planning and in having to constantly restart their pursuit of health care upon release into the community (Besney et al., 2018). In a study of New York jails, approximately half of women leaving jail lacked primary care and health insurance (Lee et al., 2006). Inadequate or lack of access to health care is not uncommon in the US; for example, nearly one-third of Americans do not have access to regular primary care, in part due to a shortage of providers in their local community (National Association of Community Health Centers, 2023). In fact, 7.3% of counties in the US do not have primary care physicians at all (HRSA Workforce, 2023). Having a primary care clinic immediately post-release from incarceration is important, given that it has been shown to reduce emergency department utilization (Wang et al., 2012) and may also improve linkage to care for medical, mental health, and substance use treatment (Morse et al., 2017). Access to primary and chronic care is critical since lack of engagement with such care can lead to worse health outcomes, including higher rates of emergency department utilization, hospitalization, or death post-release (Frank et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2013).

Longer-term healthcare challenges for formerly incarcerated women

Beyond the transition period immediately following release from prison or jail, formerly incarcerated people may continue to face barriers to care. Compared to the general population, formerly incarcerated people are significantly less likely to have a regular source of physical health care or to receive routine medical care (Kulkarni et al., 2010). Incarceration history contributes to disparities in access to physical and dental care for women post-release (Kulkarni et al., 2010), particularly for those also struggling with addiction (van Olphen et al., 2009). Formerly incarcerated women report experiencing discrimination and mistreatment in healthcare settings in the community (LeBel, 2011), particularly once history of substance misuse is disclosed (Marlow et al., 2009). A qualitative study by Colbert et al. (2016) found that women released from prison in the past year considered sobriety and addiction treatment a high priority upon release from prison; (Colbert et al., 2016) however, in a mixed methods study, Colbert et al. (2013) found that recently released women report that accessing health care and seeking out addiction treatment often function as competing needs and that mental health care, overall, is difficult to access (Colbert et al., 2013).

The current study

In sum, women who become incarcerated often enter the correctional system in poor health and with substantial needs related to sequelae of sexual violence; their physical and mental health care needs are also often not met while incarcerated (Ahmed et al., 2016; Young, 2000), and the limited data that is available suggests that they are likely to face substantial barriers to accessing care in the period immediately post-incarceration (Wang et al., 2008). Yet, little is known about women sexual assault survivors’ longer-term access to and utilization of healthcare services post-incarceration, the potential barriers to accessing care, and the resources needed to improve engagement with care. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to assess previously incarcerated sexual assault survivors’ long-term, post-release (1) health service utilization relative to need – including primary care, mental health care, and substance use treatment services, (2) health care access barriers and needs post-release, and (3) receipt of services for trauma sequelae.

Materials and methods

Participants

Participants included in the current study were 65 women survivors of sexual violence who had been incarcerated at a corrections centre in a Midsouthern US state. We focused on recruiting based on incarceration in this specific corrections centre because our study was nested within an investigation of the longer-term outcomes of a group therapy for sexual violence victimization (Karlsson et al., 2020) offered to residents in the centre starting in 2012.

Consistent with the demographics of the centre population, most participants were white (87.7%) and non-Hispanic (90.6%); average age was 37 years (range 20 to 55 years). More than half of the sample (55.4%)1 reported regularly working for pay or profit; of these, most (88.9%) reported working more than 20 h per week. Most reported having health insurance (80.3%).2 All participants who provided details on their criminal legal history3 reported having had multiple jail stays, and just over half (55.2%)4 had had multiple prison stays (M = 2.70, range = 1–7).At the time of study participation, most participants lived in the community (84.6%; n = 55), and some were reincarcerated (15.4%; n = 10).

Researcher description and positionality statement

In the spirit of self-reflexivity, we acknowledge our standpoint as four educated, female White Americans with no history of incarceration or justice involvement, and we acknowledge that our positionality and backgrounds may have influenced our interpretation in this project to some extent. We believe that our professional backgrounds and methodologic expertise enhance the research. The first author has experience and methodologic expertise in qualitative research methods; the second and third authors were trained in qualitative data collection and analysis for this study. The last author has content expertise in traumatic stress and treatment for trauma sequelae in carceral settings and extensive experience conducting research with justice-involved populations. The second, third, and last author have a background in psychology, and the third and last author have clinical mental health training. Study staff took reflexive notes throughout the research study.

Sampling and recruitment

The research team was provided with a cohort list of 712 women who were incarcerated at the correction centre between January 2012 and May 2017 to facilitate study recruitment which occurred between May 2019 and November 2020. Individuals on this list, who were 3–5 years post-release from their qualifying incarceration in the centre, were contacted using a combination of purposive sampling using publicly available contact information and snowball sampling. We intentionally sampled for approximately half of our sample to have participated in the exposure-based group therapy for sexual violence victimization called ‘Survivors Healing From Abuse: Recovery through Exposure’ (SHARE) (Karlsson et al., 2020) while incarcerated due to the goals of the parent study. We recruited women who were 3–5 years post-release because we were interested in identifying longer-term barriers to healthcare and health outcomes, as opposed to those more immediate following release from prison. Recruitment was done primarily via Facebook. Eligibility was assessed via a brief screener and included: being at least 18, English language proficiency, and self-reporting a lifetime experience of sexual violence victimization and having been incarcerated at the corrections centre 3–5 years prior. Eligible individuals were given additional information about the study and completed an informed consent process if they chose to participate.

Of the 94 individuals screened, 85 met the inclusion criteria. Of these, 70 individuals consented to participate. Four individuals were lost to follow-up or dropped out of the parent study, and one was excluded from the present analysis due to not having answered the focal questions for this study; therefore, our final sample consisted of 65 individuals. Women were compensated between $30-$50 for complete participation with a $5 compensation for travel if applicable. The participant compensation was raised from $30 to $50 halfway through the study to increase participation.

Procedure

Participants in this study completed (1) a semi-structured qualitative interview and (2) a questionnaire with self-report measures and open-ended questions.

Qualitative interviews

Qualitative data for this study were drawn from semi-structured interviews completed in person (23.1%) or by phone (76.9%), depending on the preference of the participant if they were living in the community; all incarcerated participants were interviewed in person in a private space with no carceral staff present. Interviews explored health service utilization, barriers to healthcare access, and needed resources. To assess healthcare barriers and needed resources for each service type, we asked questions such as, ‘Is there anything that gets in the way of you attending your [healthcare service] appointments or has stopped you from going as often as you think you should?’ or ‘What would be needed for you to be able to go to see a [healthcare provider type] if you decided you wanted to do so? (i.e. what resources).’ We also asked broad questions, including, ‘What would you say are the major challenges you have faced since release from the community corrections center?’ and ‘What sorts of resources have you needed – or do you still currently need – to overcome these challenges?’

Interviews lasted on average 51 min. All interviews were digitally recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using MAXQDA. We used a combination deductive and inductive approach, using Nowell et al. (2017)’s six phases of thematic analysis. The four authors served as the four coders. We first familiarized ourselves with the data (phase one) by reading transcripts and reflexive notes. The interview guide was used to deductively guide initial coding. We then further developed the codebook by collectively generating emergent codes (phase two) and organizing them into themes (phase three). In an iterative process of discussion and refinement, we reviewed the themes and subthemes (phase four), keeping detailed notes with definitions of codes and reasons for analytic choices. Through team consensus, we defined and named themes (phase five) and produced a report (phase six) detailing our coding, analysis, and audit trail.

Quantitative measures

Participants completed self-report measures to assess their levels of depression, anxiety, trauma exposure, PTSD symptoms, substance use history, and substance use disorder symptoms. Participants also provided basic sociodemographic information and details about their life history, including history of incarceration.

Depressive symptoms. We administered the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9; Spitzer et al., 1999; Cronbach’s α = 0.84) (Sun et al., 2022) to assess participants’ current depression symptoms. Example items include ‘little interest or pleasure in doing things,’ and ‘thoughts that you would be better off dead, or hurting yourself.’ Items are rated on a scale from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Nearly every day) with regard to how often in the past two weeks participants have experienced each symptom. Responses are summed to indicate the severity of depressive symptoms (range 0–27). Participants with a sum score of 10 or above were considered to have a positive screen for depression, consistent with standard scoring guidelines.

Anxiety symptoms. We administered the 2-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder-2 (GAD-2; Kroenke et al., 2007; α = 0.77) (Hughes et al., 2018) to assess participants’ current general anxiety symptoms. The two items ask participants to consider how bothered they have been by ‘feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge’ and ‘not being able to stop or control worrying’ over the past two weeks. Items are rated on a scale from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Nearly every day). Responses are summed (range 0–6) and participants with a sum score of 3 or above were considered to have a positive screen, consistent with standard scoring guidelines.

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms. PTSD symptom severity was assessed with the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; α = 0.94; Blevins et al., 2015) a self-report measure of PTSD symptoms with demonstrated reliability and validity (Blevins et al., 2015). The PCL-5 has twenty items and corresponds to the DSM-5 criteria for PTSD diagnosis. Participants rate the extent to which they have been bothered by each item in the past 30 days using a five-point scale (0 = ‘Not at all’ to 4 = ‘Extremely’). Total symptom severity scores range from 0 to 80, with scores of 33 and above considered clinically significant for PTSD symptoms (Bovin et al., 2016).

Substance use. We administered the Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test (AUDIT; de Meneses-Gaya et al., 2009; Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test, 2023; Saunders et al., 1993; α = 0.80) (Moussas et al., 2009) to assess for alcohol use problems and Drug Abuse Screening Tool (DAST; Skinner, 1982; α = 0.93) (Shirinbayan et al., 2020) for drug use problems. The AUDIT is a 10-item screening tool used to assess alcohol consumption, potential dependence on alcohol, and alcohol-related problems; scores range from 0 to 40, where zero indicates an abstainer who has not had any alcohol-related problems, 1 to 7 suggests low-risk consumption, 8 to 12 suggests harmful or hazardous drinking, and 13 or more in women suggests alcohol dependence (Saunders et al., 1993).

The DAST is a brief, 10-item self-report instrument assessing involvement with drugs, including the use of prescribed or over-the-counter drugs in excess of the directions and any nonmedical use of drugs, not including alcoholic beverages in the past year. Each item answered ‘yes’ receives 1 point, except for question 3, which a ‘no’ receives a point of 1. The range of possible scores is from 0 to 10 reflecting the degree of problems related to drug use, where 0 suggests no problems, 1–2 suggests a low level, 3–5 suggests a moderate level, 6–8 suggests a substantial level, and 9–10 suggests a severe level.

Results

Current mental and behavioural health

Of the participants who completed quantitative mental and behavioural health measures,5 36.5% scored above the clinical cutoff for depression, 44.4% scored above the clinical cutoff for anxiety, and 40.3% scored above the clinical cutoff for posttraumatic stress symptoms. Most participants reported no alcohol use or low risk drinking (27.4% and 56.5% respectively); however, a subset had responses consistent with current harmful/hazardous drinking (9.7%) or alcohol dependence (6.5%). Conversely, nearly half of participants (49.2%) reported illicit drug use in the past 12 months; all these participants reported at least some level of problems associated with their use, with 80% reporting substantial or severe problems (3.3% low, 16.7% moderate, 20.0% substantial, 60.0% severe).

In terms of potential comorbidity, 56.5% of participants were above the clinical cutoff on at least one of the three mental health measures; 12.9% on one measure, 21.0% on two measures, and 22.6% on all three measures. Among those who were above the clinical cutoff on at least one mental health measure, 82.4% also reported drug use problems and 20.0% also reported alcohol use problems.

Health service access and utilization

Primary care access and utilization

Of the 53 community-dwelling participants who provided information about their health service utilization, most reported having an established primary care physician (PCP; 69.8%). Among community-dwelling participants who reported having an established PCP, most had seen this provider within the last six months and said they saw their PCP regularly and/or when they got sick. Some participants did not see a need for having a regular primary care doctor because they ‘don't really get sick that often.’ Some participants denied having any barriers to PCP access; however, most participants reported barriers to attending these appointments, resulting in reduced appointment frequency.

Mental health care access and utilization

Few community-dwelling participants reported having a psychiatrist (13.2%) or mental health therapist (22.6%). Among the few that had a psychiatrist, most had seen their psychiatrist within the last six months and reported seeing them regularly. Among those that had a mental health therapist, most had seen their therapist in the last six months and reported seeing them weekly or monthly. Though some participants described barriers to having a psychiatrist, many denied barriers to psychiatry access and perceived no need for psychiatric medication. Some participants did not want to take psychiatric medications. Others denied a need for a psychiatrist because they were prescribed mental health medications by their PCP. Some described a need for a therapist and not a psychiatrist, while some said that they simply do not need a therapist because they were not currently struggling with mental health concerns. Others reported barriers to therapy for mental health care. Current mental health symptoms did not appear to be a driver of low utilization of mental health specialty services; three of the seven participants (42.9%) that reported having a psychiatrist and five of the 12 participants (41.7%) that reported having a therapist did not score above the clinical cutoff on any of the mental health measures.

Substance use treatment engagement and utilization

Approximately one-third (32.1%) of community participants said that they attend support groups, such as Alcoholics Anonymous (AA), Narcotics Anonymous (NA), or Celebrate Recovery, but few (7.5%) said they attend treatment specifically for drug or alcohol use problems. Many participants said that they did not need to attend substance use treatment because they ‘don't have a drug or alcohol problem.’ One participant said,

I've done drugs. I've never been addicted to any of it. I can take it or leave it. It doesn't bother me either way. I did it because it was there. And I was bored. And it was a time of my life at that exact moment. But at this exact moment, I don't drink. I don't do drugs, I have no desire to do drugs.

Some described other protective factors, like faith, coping skills they learned from treatment in prison or rehabilitation, or having a support system at home or from a support group. The most cited reason for not attending support groups was that participants reported that they do not like them; some participants said they did not like to hear about other people’s substance use or do not like to be around people who are high or actively using. Others did not like the group setting of support groups. For example, one participant said, ‘I just I have really bad social anxiety, and so being in like a group setting and talking about personal things is just really hard for me to do. I'm more of a one-on-one type person.’ Some participants reported barriers to attending support groups.

Treatment for traumatic stress sequelae

Of the 63 participants who answered questions about their receipt of trauma treatment, approximately three-fourths (74.6%) reported having received treatment specifically for trauma; however, only 33.3% of participants reported having received trauma treatment while living in the community. Thus, though we purposely sampled for approximately half of our sample to have participated in an exposure-based group therapy for sexual violence victimization (Karlsson et al., 2020) while incarcerated, most participants had not received any of the other six trauma therapy types we asked about, including Cognitive Processing Therapy, Prolonged Exposure Therapy, Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing, Beyond Trauma, Skills Training in Affective and Interpersonal Regulation, and Seeking Safety. Some participants said that the therapy methods or terminology sounded familiar and may have been part of therapy they had received but were unsure, and the vast majority did not think they had received one of the six therapies.

Barriers to care

The main barriers to health care access across service type – including primary care, psychiatric care, therapy, and support groups – were healthcare costs and issues related to health insurance, care being interrupted by a life stressor or transition, competing obligations that interfere with health care access, interpersonal factors that interfere with health care access, and stigma associated with being in recovery or being a felon (see Table 1).

Barriers to health care engagement and utilization by service type.

Healthcare costs and issues related to health insurance

Issues related to insurance, including no or poor health insurance coverage, were the most cited barriers to primary care and mental health care. Some had health insurance through their employment but struggled to pay copayments (i.e. fixed amounts paid for a covered health care service after a deductible has been paid) and out-of-pocket costs; some women described the cost of PCP visits to explain why they were not going as often as they thought they should. Others described having past medical bills to pay, which caused them to avoid any additional health care. Some had limited understanding of how insurance and the healthcare system works or did not know how to find a PCP that would be covered under their insurance plan.

The inability to pay for psychiatric medications was a common barrier to psychiatric care. When asked why they don’t have a psychiatrist, participants said things like, ‘I can’t afford one,' and ‘It costs too much on my insurance.’ Some said that finding a psychiatrist who would accept their insurance was a barrier to psychiatric care. Lack of insurance coverage for therapy and out-of-pocket costs were also reported barriers to therapy.

Transitions and life stressors

Care being interrupted by a transition or life stressor, such as reincarceration, relapse, moving to a new town, going through a divorce, or losing a job, was also described as a barrier to primary care. Some described insurance issues immediately following release from prison, which prevented them from having a PCP. Some said that they did not reestablish care with a new therapist. Some participants described prior bad experiences with therapy. Some participants did not want to start over with a new therapist following care disruptions or were concerned about finding the right one to address their needs and concerns.

Work and family obligations

Competing obligations, like work and issues with scheduling appointments, as well as family obligations and lack of childcare to attend appointments, were regularly reported as barriers to primary and mental health care. One participant said, ‘I'm a single mother and I work full-time. I go to school, and I have three kids. So yeah, sometimes that kind of stuff gets in the way.’ Some participants cited lack of transportation to get to appointments. One participant said, ‘We just recently were able to get a vehicle of our own, so, we didn't always have a vehicle to get to where I needed to be. Doctor, counselor, anything of that nature.’ Some struggled to find a good support group nearby their home or workplace. Some had busy lives and simply did not have time to attend regular therapy appointments and/or support groups or were unable to attend during the workday. Some said that taking care of children or addressing physical health care needs take priority over addressing their mental health concerns. One participant said, ‘The kids got to come first and food on the table and bills, so health needs come last.’

Intrapersonal factors

For some, intrapersonal factors like anxiety and motivation have prevented them from seeking primary and mental health care. Intrapersonal factors were the most cited barriers to having a psychiatrist. A few participants said that mental health symptoms themselves were barriers to seeking therapy. When asked what gets in the way of having a counsellor or therapist, one participant said, ‘Sometimes I get so depressed I don't want to go, I don't want to talk about it.’ Some participants said they avoid seeking help. Others described difficulty opening up to or trusting a therapist, and some said that they did not connect with the therapists they had seen. Some described being reticent to share personal information. Other participants reported struggling to find a therapist that they could relate to or who shared similar values, religious beliefs, or life experiences, such as being in recovery.

Stigma

Some participants described the stigma of being formerly incarcerated and/or being in recovery from addiction as barriers to primary care. One participant said, ‘They don't want to deal with people with drug addiction problems, or … Some of them are a little reluctant to have anything to do with you.’ Another participant described feeling judged by a primary care nurse for being in recovery and for requesting not to be given a narcotic.

Barriers to care reported by re-incarcerated women

Like participants living in the community, reincarcerated participants cited insurance and healthcare costs as barriers to primary care, but also commonly cited interruptions in care, like life transitions or stressors (e.g. moving, divorce, incarceration), that affected their re-engagement with primary care. They also were more likely to cite issues related to accessing care as a person with addiction, like discrimination from healthcare providers, as a barrier to primary care access. Regarding barriers to psychiatric care, incarcerated participants were more likely than community-dwelling participants to say that they had no need for a psychiatrist and felt ambivalent about psychiatric medication. They also cited cost and accessing care as a person with addiction as a barrier to psychiatric care. Regarding barriers to therapy, incarcerated participants more often cited intrapersonal factors, such as not connecting with a therapist, as a barrier to care. Incarcerated participants were more likely than community-dwelling participants to say that they did not have access to support groups. Few incarcerated participants reported barriers to substance use treatment.

Needed resources

For access to primary care, participants described needing health insurance and money to pay copayments for attending appointments. Some participants said they needed transportation to appointments. Others said they needed help knowing where to find a PCP or identifying one they can trust.

Participants needed similar resources to be able to see a psychiatrist, including better insurance coverage to cover psychiatric care, money to pay copayments or out-of-pocket costs, and knowledge of where to find the right psychiatrist to meet their needs. Some needed motivation or encouragement to see a psychiatrist; others needed transportation or time to attend appointments. Some needed a referral to a psychiatrist from their PCP. Others suggested wanting to receive mental health medication management and therapy in the same location.

To see a therapist, participants most often said they needed insurance to cover therapy or money to pay what insurance doesn’t cover. Several participants said they needed help finding a therapist, either from a PCP referral or from a resource list, or they needed to know which local therapists would accept their insurance. Some just needed more information about what type of mental health care, including therapy, would be covered under their insurance plan. A few participants said they wanted better therapist options, including clinics with flexible hours and those that are closer to home. Some participants needed help finding the right therapist, such as a religious or non-religious therapist. Others said they needed to find time in their busy schedules to go to therapy, while some needed motivation or encouragement to go. Questions about resources needed to attend support groups and substance use treatment were not asked in the interviews.

Discussion

This study examined need for mental and behavioural health care and ongoing barriers to healthcare access for formerly incarcerated women survivors of sexual violence three-to-five years post-release. A notable finding was the discrepancy between current mental and behavioural health needs and current service utilization. Specifically, over half of our participants reported symptoms of depression, anxiety, and/or posttraumatic stress, yet very few reported having a psychiatrist or therapist. Similarly, many of our participants endorsed items consistent with experiencing at least some level of drug and/or alcohol problems in the past 12 months, yet very few reported having received treatment for drug or alcohol use problems currently and even attendance at relevant support groups like AA or NA was limited. Together, these findings suggest that formerly incarcerated women’s need for mental and behavioural health care is going unmet.

Some of the barriers to care found in our study have been found within other previous work on the topic related to the short-term transition from prison to the community, such as lack of insurance (Lee et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2008), lack of transition planning and continuity of care (Besney et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2008), and competing priorities that interfere with healthcare access (Besney et al., 2018; Colbert et al., 2013), particularly mental and behavioural health care (Colbert et al., 2013). Some of these barriers also align with research on healthcare barriers among the US general population more broadly. Indeed, inadequate health insurance coverage is one of the most significant barriers to healthcare access in the US, with physician shortages and transportation issues also common barriers (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024; HRSA Workforce, 2023). However, our study also identified barriers survivors face several years post-release from prison, like understanding insurance benefits for primary care and mental health services, continued disruptions in continuity of care, and competing priorities, like busy work schedules and childcare needs. Our participants also report additional barriers unique to being formerly incarcerated, such as significant disruptions in care as a result of incarceration, moving to unfamiliar areas post-release, and stigma by providers when accessing healthcare as someone with a history of incarceration and/or addiction.

Our study also identified resources needed to overcome access barriers across primary and mental health care, like better insurance, money to cover healthcare costs, and transportation to attend appointments. Given primary care provider shortages in many rural areas of the US that create barriers to primary care access (HRSA Workforce, 2023; National Association of Community Health Centers, 2023), resources may be needed to help women identify primary care providers locally or attend some primary care visits virtually. Thus, resources to help formerly incarcerated women navigate the healthcare system, access local services, and identify supportive providers are needed. Resources to assist in identifying mental health providers that were accessible based on women’s insurance benefits, schedules, and preference for therapy versus medication were also requested.

Although our study did not reveal a theme about the healthcare needs of sexual violence survivors specifically, we find it particularly significant that although all participants had experienced sexual violence victimization and nearly half were experiencing ongoing clinically significant PTSD symptoms, few had ever received evidence-based trauma treatment while living in the community. This may be due to limited access to specialty mental healthcare in the community following incarceration, but also may be compounded by providers’ perceived barriers to implementing evidence-based trauma treatments (Finch et al., 2020).

Implications for policy and/or practice

Related to insurance-related barriers, there exists a strong need for efforts to improve health insurance enrolment and education among currently and formerly incarcerated US women to improve access to primary care in the community post-release. While insurance coverage influences access to health care for Americans broadly, our participants expressed needing help with enrolment prior to release from prison, as well as assistance post-release in understanding how health insurance functions within our complex healthcare system and information about benefits coverage. Patient navigation has been shown to improve healthcare access and quality for underserved populations (Natale-Pereira et al., 2011) and may be a promising intervention. Other resources could include case management or peer support before and after being released from incarceration. Consistent transition support and adequate resource information may greatly benefit this population in utilizing health insurance benefits.

Insurance coverage for mental health care was also cited as problematic for many of our participants. Some participants mentioned struggling to find mental health care providers who accept their health insurance, which may relate to the challenges many mental health providers experience when working with Medicaid (Isett et al., 2009). Much like they mentioned for PCP access, many participants reported high costs of mental health services as a barrier to mental health care, even when covered by insurance, including high copayments and medication costs. Exploring the discrepancy between physical health care and mental health care coverage and costs would be a worthwhile policy endeavour, especially as it relates to lower-income insurance programmes.

As mentioned by some participants, incarcerated women may benefit from prison staff helping them schedule appointments with in-network mental health providers in their community before leaving prison as a means to improve their likelihood of engaging in mental health care post-release. Because our participants reported needing resources to help find providers they can trust and those that will accept their insurance, this assistance navigating the healthcare system should continue beyond the immediate period post-release from prison and should be accessible in the years following incarceration. Given that most rural Americans have a smartphone and access to the internet at home (Vogels, 2021), web-based services for healthcare navigation and mental health care may improve access to care for this underserved population.

In addition to revealing challenges with identifying and scheduling healthcare visits in the community post-incarceration, our study revealed the stigma formerly incarcerated women face when accessing care in their communities, particularly among those with a history of addiction. People who use drugs are known to face significant barriers to accessing health care, including covering costs of healthcare services and finding transportation to healthcare facilities (Matsuzaki et al., 2018) – barriers that our study revealed may continue even when women are years into their recovery. Additionally, just over 40% of our study sample were currently experiencing posttraumatic symptoms placing them at or above the clinical off for PTSD. Like having a history of addiction, sexual violence victimization can also be stigmatizing, leading to symptoms like shame, self-blame, and poor self-esteem, which can contribute to self-medicating behaviours, reduced help-seeking, and reduced service utilization (Chivers-Wilson, 2006). This speaks to the need for community resources that help women identify providers that understand their unique needs as formerly incarcerated women with a history of addiction and sexual violence victimization. Also, healthcare providers need to be trained to help women who are formerly incarcerated and/or have a history of substance use to reduce provider stigma and discrimination and improve quality of care.

Because this population experiences unique barriers to care, there is a need for new models for mental health care management. Several participants cited a need for mental health clinics with more flexibility in scheduling to accommodate women’s work hours and childcare needs. Others mentioned a need for appointments outside of regular business hours, as well as same-day availability and flexible late cancellation/rescheduling policies. Some participants also mentioned that clinics offering both psychiatry and counselling services within the same clinic and scheduling window would improve their access to care.

In part due to the much higher rates of primary care access in our sample, this population may also benefit from integrated behavioural health and primary care models (Cook & Lancaster, 2018), especially considering how commonly women in our sample received psychiatric medication prescriptions from their PCP. Cobinna’s (2010) mixed methods study of 50 current and formerly incarcerated women also revealed that women experience competing demands post-release and may benefit from integrated services to address this challenge (Cobbina, 2010). Integrated or collaborative care models have been shown to improve mental and physical health outcomes for individuals with mental health disorders across a variety of healthcare settings, including primary care (Woltmann et al., 2012). Mental health medication management in primary care may be more feasible for this population than medication management by a psychiatrist. Future research should explore innovative models for mental health care management, including how management in primary care can improve mental health care access and outcomes for formerly incarcerated women with significant work and family obligations and competing priorities.

While no participants mentioned this option specifically, more availability of and coverage for teletherapy may provide a solution to some of these scheduling barriers, as well as some of the barriers mentioned regarding transportation and childcare. Research has demonstrated the effectiveness of treatment via telehealth as compared to in-person care for a number of mental health conditions, including PTSD (Turgoose et al., 2018), depression, anxiety, and adjustment disorder (Varker et al., 2019). To facilitate access to care during the COVID-19 public health emergency, state and federal regulatory agencies and insurance providers including Medicaid, Medicare, and private health insurers temporarily relaxed rules on telehealth. Some insurers made these temporary waivers permanent, thereby improving access to care for rural Americans. Access to specialty mental healthcare via teletherapy is particularly promising for improving access to care among rural survivors of sexual violence, as it could improve access to providers with specialized training in treating PTSD who are often located in urban areas, as well as address known barriers to in-person mental health care attendance, like transportation.

Limitations

There are several limitations to our study sample which should be considered when attempting to generalize results. First, we focused on women who had been incarcerated at a single community corrections centre within a single Midsouthern US state; individuals in other geographic regions and sociopolitical contexts may have different experiences than those voiced by the women in our study. This may be particularly true for individuals living outside of the US, considering the health insurance barriers unique to the US – the only developed nation without universal healthcare. Second, women self-selected to participate in this study, and therefore our sample may be limited to women who felt comfortable enough to disclose and discuss sensitive information about themselves and their past experiences of sexual victimization, substance use, mental health, and incarceration. Also, we relied on Facebook reach-outs to initiate contact with potential participants. Therefore, most of our sample was limited to those who have an active Facebook account post-incarceration.

Conclusion

Our study describes the significant barriers formerly incarcerated women survivors of sexual violence face accessing healthcare three-to-five years after release, and barriers to care were similar across service type, including primary care, mental health care, and substance use treatment services. This study also highlighted limited utilization of specialty mental health care, including psychiatry and therapy, for formerly incarcerated women. While formerly incarcerated women need assistance identifying and scheduling care in their communities immediately following release from prison, women also need continued help navigating our complex healthcare system in the years following their incarceration. Future research should explore effective strategies to promote healthcare utilization among formerly incarcerated women survivors of sexual violence.
