Research Mentoring During Times of Crisis
Authors: Jessica Ameling, Nathan Houchens, Martha Quinn, Rachel Ehrlinger, Sanjay Saint
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This qualitative study reports on strategies by successful mentors used to guide their mentees in times of crisis in academic medicine.

Introduction

Effective mentoring is foundational in developing the careers of faculty and staff members in academic medicine. During times of crisis, such as the current uncertainty and disruptions to US federal research funding,1,2 mentoring takes on greater importance as mentees may feel bewildered, vulnerable, and in need of additional guidance.3,4,5 While much has been written about the ideal characteristics of effective mentors,6,7,8,9,10 most previous work does not focus on the actions of award-winning research mentors. Furthermore, there is a paucity of recommendations based on empirical research on what approaches, techniques, and behaviors are used by these mentors to help mentees during times of crisis.3 We thus conducted a study to identify what outstanding research mentors currently do and recommend in mentoring relationships, and how such mentors assist mentees in navigating challenging times of uncertainty.

Methods

Study Design

Our exploratory qualitative study included in-depth interviews with award-winning research mentors (also described herein as accomplished, outstanding, notable, and gifted). Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was reviewed by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board and received an exemption determination because the study only involved an interview that posed minimal risk to the participants. In reporting this study, we followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting guideline. Participant quotations are presented verbatim from the interview transcripts.

Sampling and Recruitment

We conducted semistructured qualitative interviews with academic internal medicine physicians. Participants were selected from a cohort of University of Michigan Medical School faculty who had been recognized as exemplary research mentors with a formal mentoring award and had a primary appointment in the Department of Internal Medicine. Specifically, potential participants were identified from the list of winners of the Michigan Institute for Clinical and Health Research (MICHR) Mentoring Award from 2016 to 2024. These awardees are selected through a comprehensive 2-step process of nomination (nomination letter, letters of support, letter from a department or program chair or associate dean, nominee’s curriculum vitae, nominee’s list of mentees) and selection made by consensus of the MICHR Mentoring Award Review Committee and MICHR Director. Inclusion criteria consisted of the following: (1) age 18 years or older, (2) current or previous faculty member at the University of Michigan Medical School, (3) recipient of the MICHR Mentoring Award, and (4) an internal medicine physician.

Potential interviewees were approached via an email from the project manager on behalf of the principal investigator. Of the 19 potential participants contacted, 15 agreed to be interviewed, 2 declined, and 2 were unresponsive after 3 attempts. If the physician mentor agreed to participate, 2 authors trained in qualitative methods (J.A., M.Q.) interviewed them using virtual communication software (U-M Zoom) from July 14 through October 1, 2025. At the beginning of each interview, a study team member (J.A.) read a written consent statement that stated that we plan to audio-record, transcribe, analyze, and publish our findings and that all data would be deidentified and no names will ever be used in any study findings.

Data Collection

Interviews were audio-recorded, with the audio recordings transcribed and used to conduct the qualitative analysis. Interviews lasted a mean of 42.38 minutes (range, 23.63-56.73 minutes). Data collection continued until the research team determined that thematic sufficiency11 was achieved after the 15 interviews: that is, until the dataset was sufficiently robust and diverse to enable a comprehensive analysis. Participants received a $50 gift card for participation, although some participants declined. Key questions are listed in Box 1.

Reflexivity Statement

The research team used reflexivity to engage throughout the study, acknowledging how our professional backgrounds (public health, internal medicine, and health policy) and assumptions may have influenced the data collection, interpretation, and representation of findings. This process was aimed at enhancing credibility and interpretive rigor by fostering critical self-awareness and transparency.

Data Analysis

A descriptive content analysis was conducted using both an inductive and deductive approach to coding the qualitative data from deidentified interview transcripts.12 Three members of the study team (J.A., M.Q., and R.E.) began by independently reading a sample of interview transcripts to develop a preliminary coding scheme to help guide the analysis. A codebook was created that included both deductive codes identified prior to coding based on the categories most important to this study and inductive codes that emerged directly from the data. The 3 study team members used this codebook, which was revised periodically as additional codes emerged, to independently review and code all of the transcripts. The coders then met regularly in pairs to discuss and resolve any discrepancies in coding until agreement was reached. NVivo software version 15 (QSR International Pty Ltd) was used to manage and organize the qualitative data. Once all data had been coded and entered into NVivo, the 3 members of the study team (J.A., M.Q., and R.E.) met regularly to review the code reports and identify key themes. All recordings and transcripts were stored on a secure server, with access limited to only approved study staff.

Mentor Perspectives and Concerns About Current Challenging Times in Academic Medicine

Almost all of the interview participants stated that their mentees frequently voiced concerns about the current stressful climate in academic medicine, primarily related to research funding uncertainty. One participant reported, “I would say at least every other conversation, that topic comes up. I mean, that’s just the world we live in right now.” (Participant 11)

Most mentors said they shared similar concerns about the current climate and their peers did as well. These concerns were pervasive and sometimes unspoken. One participant reported, “I think we all perceive it whether we say it out loud or not. I think it’s the giant elephant in the room.” (Participant 10)

Variation existed in perceptions of whom these challenges affected the most, with some interviewees stating they heard the most concerns from their trainees and early-career faculty, and others stating that their midcareer faculty mentees seemed most affected.

Causes of Concern

Funding uncertainty was frequently cited as a cause of concern, including uncertainty about obtaining future grants, maintaining current grants, and the federal government’s current approach to funding research. Other causes of concern were feelings that the ground rules in academic medicine had changed and that the unprecedented nature of the times led to a lack of a clear roadmap for mentee career paths. One participant reported, “…[T]hings that were incredibly stable up to until a few years ago, you know, in terms of how to get established and how to get promoted … have all been disrupted.” (Participant 9)

Reactions to Challenging Times

Participants shared a wide range of reactions to the current climate that their mentees had expressed, from anxiety to feelings of futility to fear of the topic itself. Other mentors said their mentees were trying to stay optimistic and hoping for the best. One participant reported, “Right now, everyone is just hunkering down, doing the best they can and taking it day-by-day and week-by-week because there is only so much you have control over.” (Participant 10)

Downstream Effects

Downstream effects of the challenging times were commonly shared. Mentors said their mentees expressed concerns about the future of their careers in academic research. Mentors expressed concerns that promising mentees would be forced out of research due to difficulty obtaining research funding. One participant reported, “There are other people that I think are going to be pushed out of the research tenure track. I think it’s a terrible time. I think we’re going to lose the whole generation.” (Participant 14)

Domain 1: Tactical Mentoring Strategies and Advice During Challenging Times

Research mentors discussed many tactical strategies they used in their mentoring, as well as advice they would offer to other mentors. Themes within this domain were divided into funding strategies, consider a career change, and stay up to date.

Funding Strategies

Interviewees discussed several strategies to help their mentees obtain research funding when federal funding is uncertain, such as diversifying funding streams. Specifically, mentors advised mentees to explore options from industry, private donors, and foundations. Mentors also counseled mentees to seek assistance from university resources to refine their pitch to apply to these nonfederal funding entities.

Mentors advised some mentees to pivot to similar areas of research that are currently more likely to be funded but still align with their mentee’s career goals and values. Other interviewees suggested that their mentees leverage funding within their institution, such as internal bridge funding or pilot grants. Mentors also advised mentees to submit a higher volume of grant submissions than usual. Finally, some mentors encouraged their mentees to explore other ways to help fund their effort, such as teaching or editing an academic medical journal.

Consider a Career Change

Less commonly, some mentors advised their mentees to develop an alternative plan B and plan C in case they could not obtain funding to support their academic careers.

“…[S]o what we’re doing is hedging our bets in both ways. So she’s [mentee] applying for post-doc positions, but also, I connected a couple of pharma industry folks that I know, so she’s been exploring those options as well.” (Participant 11)

These back-up plans included the private sector, such as pharmaceutical companies or assuming a full-time clinical role. Mentors advised mentees to assess their own values and risk tolerance for the instability in academic medicine to help determine whether they should consider a career change.

Stay Up to Date

Mentors advised their mentees to stay current on activity and events within the federal government and their university’s response to changes. If mentees had a currently funded grant, mentors advised them to stay in regular contact with their project officer.

Domain 2: Psychological Mentoring Strategies and Advice During Challenging Times

Themes that emerged within psychological strategies included providing emotional support and empathy to mentees and coaching the mentee’s mindset.

Provide Emotional Support and Empathy

Research mentors expressed the importance of providing emotional support and empathy to their mentees.

“And the first thing to say is that ‘I’m sorry, I understand this has got to be terrible, you know? And it’s crazy, the world we live in today. It’s not about you, right? Your work is important and valued and I see and recognize that.’” (Participant 13)

Mentors also recommended that other mentors recognize and explicitly acknowledge mentee concerns. One participant reported, “…[W]e underestimate the amount of power there is in just voicing that and naming the concerns.” (Participant 7)

Interviewees emphasized the importance of acknowledging mentee concerns and not diminishing the seriousness of the challenges. They noted that emotional support can be provided through many channels including email, in-person conversation, and even departmental newsletters.

Coach the Mentee’s Mindset

Mentors emphasized the importance of coaching their mentees’ mindsets to help them thrive during trying times. The need to stay creative, flexible, and resilient was a common mindset endorsed by mentors. One participant reported, “All you can do is be resilient and adaptable. And that’s what makes you successful in a chaotic environment.” (Participant 15)

They also coached their mentees to put challenges in a larger context and be optimistic that “this too will pass.” Another mindset strategy was to focus on the work, not allowing a sense of feeling paralyzed by the environment. One participant reported, “But bottom line is the way we try to get around it is focus on today, focus on the experiments, you know, in the end, success is the best antidote for a lot of problems.” (Participant 11)

Domain 3: Effective Mentoring Practices

Research mentors described the philosophies and strategies they used in their own mentoring relationships.

Mentoring Philosophies

One common approach was to provide both content-focused and character-focused mentoring. Additionally, mentors strive for the right balance of structure and freedom for their mentee, described by an interviewee (participant 12) as a mix of “gardener and carpenter” approaches: gardeners “tend the field” to give their mentees the opportunity to flourish, while carpenters provide more structured mentoring. One participant reported, “… 90% of good mentorship is helping to find an environment and create a microenvironment in which the person in front of you will flower.” (Participant 8)

Other interviewees (participants 6, 8, and 11) said that their mentoring philosophies mirrored that of parenting. For example, effective mentors tailor their strategies to the individual mentee and adapt the relationship and support as the mentee matures.

Interviewees emphasized the need for these relationships to be mutually beneficial and reciprocal. One interviewee (participant 1) compared ideal mentoring structures with primary and specialty care: mentees have 1 primary mentor (primary care), but that person can connect the mentee to other mentors (specialists) for specific needs. We heard from interviewees that successful mentoring relationships are frequently lifelong relationships. One participant reported, “…[Y]ou know that mentorship key that you have, it’s good forever as long as I’m still around.” (Participant 1)

Mentoring Strategies

Interviewees shared many of their proven strategies for effective mentoring. First, to lay the foundation, a strong personal relationship should be developed so that the mentor can understand their mentees’ career goals. One participant reported, “…[A] good mentor also doesn’t have preconceived notions of what success looks like for their mentee.” (Participant 2)

Then, mentors can tailor their mentoring strategies to the mentee and their goals, assuring they are putting the mentees’ interests first.

Another strategy is to instill confidence in the mentee by providing support, encouragement, comfort, and belief in the mentee. One participant reported, “I think your mentees have to feel that you absolutely have their back and that you support them....” (Participant 14)

Mentors also emphasized the need to network for their mentee, serving in both the connector and sponsor roles.

“I specifically tell them [to attend] professional meetings. I will introduce them …take them to dinner with me when I can. If I can’t do a talk, I will refer it to them. Sometimes I will bow out of a manuscript opportunity and put them in instead.” (Participant 7)

Mentors should require accountability from the mentee with expectation setting and frequent check-ins. One participant reported, “I think you need to set expectations with your trainees that they need to show up, they need to bring agendas, they need to tell you what they need.” (Participant 2)

Another common strategy involves provision of honest, forthright, and timely feedback to mentees. Feedback should be both positive and constructive by suggesting ways they could have handled a situation differently or areas for improvement.

Mentors should be accessible to their mentees both to help with technical skills and to offer emotional support. Further, mentors said that this accessibility generally extended over a substantial period of time as they often engaged in longitudinal mentoring. One participant reported, “Some mentees have a long runway and when I see a mentor that has the patience to do longitudinal mentoring to bring that mentee up in a variety of ways, I appreciate that.” (Participant 3)

Finally, mentors emphasized the importance of leading by example and role modeling. They reflected on how their early mentors profoundly influenced the development of their mentoring skills, not through what they said but through the example they set with their actions. One participant reported, “…[Y]ou try to emulate the person that mentored you.” (Participant 15)

Discussion

Our qualitative study of award-winning research mentors from the University of Michigan Medical School identified strategies that accomplished mentors use to effectively mentor during challenging times in academic medicine. Several key themes emerged: mentors guided their mentees in identifying diverse funding sources and considering alternative career options or areas of research focus, which is especially important given the research funding concerns expressed by nearly all participants. Additionally, mentors provided emotional support by coaching their mentees to enhance their creativity, flexibility, and resiliency. Finally, mentors shared their overall mentoring philosophies and strategies for effective mentoring, such as developing a personal relationship with the mentee, providing honest and forthright feedback, and networking on behalf of the mentee. The suggested mentoring techniques and approaches we describe may be useful to mentors now and during future crises in academic medicine when their guidance is most needed.

Our findings align with studies of effective mentorship, in general and during crises, with the most recent examples of the latter arising from the COVID-19 pandemic. Our mentors’ tactical mentoring strategies align with evidence that effective mentors function as strategic advisors who actively facilitate research productivity and career advancement. The observation that mentors encouraged diversification of funding streams and consideration of alternative research directions or career pathways during uncertain periods extends this literature by highlighting how these strategic functions become especially salient during funding volatility. Consistent with COVID-19–era scholarship, our mentors supported deliberate evaluation of whether to pause, persist, or pivot research agendas, underscoring the importance of aligning short-term funding opportunities with long-term scholarly identity.13,14

The psychological strategies identified in our study corroborate literature emphasizing psychological safety and relational trust in effective mentoring. Core mentoring philosophies described in qualitative syntheses emphasize reciprocity, mutual respect, and a balance of support and challenge as foundational to mentee growth.10,15 During the COVID-19 pandemic, mentors were urged to listen more than usual, prioritize well-being over productivity, and explicitly address mentee distress.16 Our findings reinforce these recommendations, suggesting that, in challenging times, mentors’ roles as emotional anchors and mindset coaches are not ancillary but essential. Our data indicate that psychological support may sustain engagement and resilience during periods when external markers of success (eg, grants) are less predictable, thereby preserving long-term career trajectories.

Finally, our study’s emphasis on overarching mentoring philosophies and strategies (eg, authentic personal relationships, tailored and candid feedback, networking on behalf of mentees) is consistent with contemporary models that view mentorship as relational, individualized, and embedded within broader professional ecosystems. Several studies have found that effective mentorship often extends beyond the dyadic relationship and may involve networks of mentors addressing distinct domains of need.15,17,18

Mentors must approach each of these strategies with skill and emotional intelligence or risk deleterious effects on the mentee. For instance, an approach of reassuring mentees that all will be well if they just work harder (ie, toxic positivity) or encouraging mentees to submit more grant applications than usual may risk mentee burnout. Additionally, well-meaning encouragement to consider alternative career pathways during challenging times may inadvertently lead to attrition of talented mentees from scientific fields. Limitations

Our study should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, we selected participants who had won a research-based mentoring award at only 1 academic medical center, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. Second, we included only those whose specialty was internal medicine, so our findings may not apply to other disciplines. Third, as we gathered the perspectives of mentors only and not those of their respective mentees, we could not assess alignment or differences of opinion between mentor and mentee. Finally, our sample size of 15 mentors may also limit the generalizability of our findings. However, the aim of our examination was for depth rather than breadth.

Conclusions

This qualitative evaluation found that gifted mentors provided similar guidance on how best to mentor junior faculty in an academic setting, especially during times of uncertainty. Such guidance includes tactical strategies, psychological strategies, and relational mentoring philosophies and strategies. Prior crisis-era literature has largely been descriptive and has lacked rigorous outcome evaluation. Our findings thus contribute empirical support for integrating varied strategies into a cohesive mentoring framework during periods of uncertainty. We extend prior work by suggesting that effective mentoring in crisis is not a departure from established best practices but rather an intensification and intentional recalibration of them in response to contextual stressors.

Our findings may be used to elevate the strategies and approaches of mentors so that they may assist their mentees in navigating the current academic environment as well as prepare for potential future challenges. We believe that the practical principles and approaches espoused by our interviewees would transcend the exact cause of a crisis and be widely applicable during challenging times in academic medicine.
