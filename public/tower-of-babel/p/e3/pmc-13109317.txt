Design of a binocular multispectral stereo imaging system and its application in plant phenotyping
Authors: Wenxiu Wan, Zhiyuan Liu, Ziru Yu, Jiahui Wang, Xiangyang Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the continuous progress in micro-optical machine technology, miniature spectral imaging devices have been rapidly developed; however, three-dimensional (3D) imaging measurement technology has become increasingly mature and widely used. The evolution of these technologies has established a robust foundation for the integration of three-dimensional imaging and spectral information. To achieve accurate alignment between 3D data and spectral information to obtain a more comprehensive spectral representation of objects in 3D space, we developed a binocular multispectral stereo imaging (BMSI) system. This system acquires images in synchrony with a binocular multispectral imager, thereby ensuring accurate alignment between 3D data and spectral data at the pixel level and facilitating the construction of a four-dimensional (4D) dataset. The segmentation of leaf regions from shadow backgrounds in two distinct plant species was achieved through optimal band fusion and hue-saturation value (HSV) color space transformation, significantly improving the segmentation accuracy, processing efficiency, and robustness across different plant species. A systematic evaluation was conducted to quantify the reconstruction precision and system stability at different measurement distances. The designed system acquired 4D image spectral data with plants as the objects to be tested. The distribution characteristics of chlorophyll (Chl) on the 3D surface of plants were obtained by first-order derivatives of the spectral data and the normalized difference red edge (NDRE) index. This technique provides a new means for plant phenotyping research and a more effective technical approach for the digitalization and precision monitoring of the agricultural industry.Keywords: Binocular multispectral imager, Four-dimensional model, Multispectral images segmentation, Spectral analysis of plant

Introduction

In plant phenotyping research, two fundamental and indispensable techniques are the measurement of plant surface geometry through imaging techniques and the measurement of plant physicochemical characteristics using spectroscopic methods. Spectral imaging techniques, in which two-dimensional spatial images and spectral information are simultaneously obtained, facilitate the analysis and localization of biochemical properties and the two-dimensional spatial distribution of an object. These techniques have been widely employed in the measurement of chlorophyll (Chl) and nitrogen (N) contents in plants, in the detection of crop stress and disease [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]], and in mineral detection[[6], [7], [8]] and cultural heritage analysis [[9], [10], [11]]. Furthermore, information in the stereo spatial dimension is highly important in plant phenotyping studies. Binocular (multiocular) stereo vision [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]], time-of-flight (ToF) [18,19], and structured light projection [[20], [21], [22]] are among the optical three-dimensional (3D) imaging technologies that have been widely applied in recent years [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27]] and effectively meet the demand for 3D stereo imaging information. 3D spatial images facilitate a profound understanding of the spatial relationships and structural features of objects, offering precise depth information. These methods have been extensively employed to identify pivotal data, including dimensions, texture nuances, and microscopic imperfections of objects. However, in many application scenarios, the acquisition of both 3D spatial images and spectral information related to the corresponding spatial locations represents a dual requirement that cannot be fulfilled by a single spectral imager. To meet this need, a binocular multispectral stereo imaging (BMSI) system that enables the simultaneous measurement of 3D morphological and spectral properties is necessary.

The fusion of 3D and spectral information for four-dimensional (4D) inspection not only significantly enhances the capacity to comprehend and evaluate the characteristics of the object under examination but also paves the way for novel applications. Ali Can Karaca et al. [28] introduced a ground panoramic stereo hyperspectral imaging system composed of a hyperspectral stereo camera pair, a rotating axis, and a portable acquisition computer, which can collect images from two line-scan hyperspectral cameras while the platform rotates and can estimate depth maps. Pengyao Xie et al. [29] combined snapshot spectral imaging and RGB-D imaging technologies and used the speeded-up robust features (SURF) algorithm combined with the Demons algorithm to fuse depth images and snapshot spectral images acquired at close range for generating high-quality 3D multispectral point clouds of plants. Guoxiang Sun et al. [30] designed a system that includes a Kinect sensor, an SOC710 hyperspectral camera, an electric turntable, and other components for measuring greenhouse tomato plants and proposed a Fourier transform-based heterogeneous sensing image registration technique to assign hyperspectral reflectance to the Kinect depth image coordinate system. Huijie Zhao et al. [31] combined stereo vision and grating dispersion technology to acquire hyperspectral images through fiber-reformatting imaging spectroscopy (FRIS) and obtain 3D information through RGB camera binocular stereo vision technology. Jie Liang et al. [46] used hyperspectral imaging technology and structure from motion (SfM) technology to directly construct 3D models of plants and explored plant segmentation using K-means and SVMs on images in the near-infrared (NIR) band. Parsa Omidi et al. [32] introduced a multispectral 4-step phase-shifting fringe projection profilometry (FPP) technique using a multispectral filter array and a snapshot multispectral camera to generate a composite pattern containing four phase-shifted fringe projections, providing 3D imaging using a single acquisition. Fuhong Cai et al. combined a lightweight imaging spectrometer with an auxiliary camera to reconstruct 3D point clouds using binocular disparity [33] and SfM–MVS [34] methods and fused the data with spectral data to form a “spatial–spectral–depth” four-dimensional dataset, which can be used to solve the needs of environmental monitoring (vegetation classification and water quality assessment), facial forgery (in vivo spectral identification), and so forth at a low cost and in a lightweight manner.

Nevertheless, significant challenges remain in the fusion of 3D and spectral information into a unified 4D model, primarily due to the complexity of the system design and the need for precise registration and sophisticated algorithms to fuse 3D data and spectral data. The resolution of spectral data differs from that of 3D data, with the discrepancy manifesting primarily in the disparities in pixel values and distributions between spectral maps and RGB images or depth maps. Furthermore, the descriptors derived from spectral images are challenging to register with those extracted from conventional RGB images and depth maps, which hinders the relocation and registration of spectral images in 3D models. Additionally, the integration of 3D data and spectral data and their subsequent application to real-world scenarios represent significant current challenges.

In response to the existing technological challenges in fusing 3D information and spectral information into a 4D model, which is characterized by system design complexity and precise registration difficulties, this paper proposes a BMSI scheme. Taking plants as the target of measurement, we establish corresponding measurement schemes and computational methods. The main work of this paper includes the following points: a) We designed a BMSI system that consists of a binocular multispectral imager and a broadband light source and is capable of combining 3D point clouds and multispectral imaging techniques to form a 4D dataset of plants; b) we designed an image segmentation method based on the fusion of spectral images at an optimum band combination and the hue-saturation value (HSV) color space, with comparative analysis conducted on segmentation accuracy and computational efficiency across different plant species; c) a systematic evaluation was conducted to quantify the reconstruction precision and system stability with various measurement distances; and d) we performed first-order derivative and normalized difference red edge (NDRE) index analysis calculations on spectral data to assess the distribution of Chl in plants and reveal the distribution of Chl on the 3D surface of plants.

System design and methods

Experimental setup

The BMSI system comprises a broad-spectrum light source and binocular multispectral imager with parallel optical axes. Multispectral imaging and 3D stereo vision utilize the same light source, with the 3D data obtained through the computation of stereoscopic spectral image pairs. Additionally, the spectral data are also acquired from the multispectral image to ensure a one-to-one correspondence between the 3D data and spectral data of the measurement area. A schematic representation of the BMSI system, wherein a broad-spectrum tungsten halogen lamp serves as the light source and irradiates the object under measurement, is shown in Fig. 1 (a). The reflected light traverses a bandpass filter prior to being selected by a tunable Fabry–Perot interferometer (FPI) for a specific wavelength. The light is then focused by an imaging lens onto a CMOS sensor, thereby forming a multispectral image cube. The point P situated at the measurement object is selected, and its pixel coordinates imaged in the binocular multispectral imager are represented as pl(xl, yl) and pr(xr, yr), respectively. The 3D reconstruction coordinates of point P(Xw, Yw, Zw) are calculated based on the disparity between pl(xl, yl) and pr(xr, yr). The multispectral data Ip(λ0, λ1, …, λn) are recorded at pixel pl(xl, yl) on the left multispectral imager plane. Since there is a one-to-one correspondence between the multispectral data Ip(λ0, λ1, …, λn) recorded at the pixel pl(xl, yl) on the multispectral imager plane and the 3D spatial data P(Xw, Yw, Zw), we can combine the pixel-by-pixel data Ip(λ0, λ1, …, λn) of point pl(xl, yl) to the 3D point P(Xw, Yw, Zw) on a pixel-by-pixel basis. In this way, a 4D point containing 3D and spectral data is obtained and represented as (Xw, Yw, Zw, Ip(λ0, λ1, …, λn)). By processing all the pixel points of the multispectral imager in the same way, 4D data can be obtained for the whole measurement area, and the precise correspondence between the 3D information and the spectral information can be realized for high-precision data fusion.

BMSI: (a) schematic diagram of the working principle, (b) laboratory prototype, and (c) operational flowchart.

The BMSI prototype, which comprises an illumination source with four halogen lamps (Osram, 64832FL), a self-assembled binocular multispectral imager, and a tripod stand that maintains the imager's optical axis in a parallel configuration, is shown in Fig. 1 (b). The object to be measured is positioned on the measuring platform, and the measuring distance can be adjusted as needed. The operational flowchart of the BMSI system is shown in Fig. 1(c). Each binocular multispectral imager captures a spectral cube (Iλ0, Iλ1, …, Iλ9). By utilizing an optimal band combination selection algorithm, the optimal spectral image combination (Iλa, Iλb, Iλc) is selected from any three-band combination, such as (Iλ0, Iλ1, Iλ2), (Iλ1, Iλ2, Iλ3), …, (Iλ7, Iλ8, Iλ9). The three selected optimal spectral images are linearly superimposed to obtain the fused spectral image Im ​= ​Iλa ​+ ​Iλb ​+ ​Iλc. The fused spectral images are converted to the HSV color space, and spectral stereo image pairs are obtained after the plants and background shadows are segmented using saturation. Stereo matching is performed, and the 3D coordinates P(X, Y, Z) at each spectral image pixel coordinate p(x, y) are calculated. Since the spectral data I(λ0, λ1, …, λn) are recorded at pixel coordinate p(x, y) and there is a direct mapping relationship between the image pixel p(x, y) and the 3D point P(X, Y, Z), the spectral data I(λ0, λ1, …, λn) can be allocated to the 3D point P(X, Y, Z) on a one-to-one basis. In this way, we can obtain 4D points that include 3D stereo information and spectral data, represented as (X,Y,Z),I(λ0,λ1,…,λn). This method allows us to achieve a precise correspondence between 3D and spectral information across the entire measurement area, resulting in a high-precision fused 4D dataset of plants.

Multispectral imager

A multispectral imager with tunable filters based on MEMS–FPI was chosen because this solution ensures precise alignment between cross-band images. This means that spectral information in different bands can be read according to spatial pixel points, and 3D reconstruction can be performed directly from the stereoscopic spectral image. This approach avoids the need for additional complex algorithms to register spectral and 3D data while maintaining both spectral and spatial resolution and ensures data integrity and accuracy.

Fig. 2(a)–(b) illustrates the working principle and structure of the multispectral imager. Fig. 2(a) depicts the operating principle of the MEMS–FPI chip, which was first reported by Peleg Levin et al. [35]. The MEMS chip is based on the spectroscopic principle of the FP cavity and is composed of two semitransparent mirrors that form an optical resonator. The reflectivity of the mirrors determines the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the passband, and the distance between the mirrors determines the central transmission wavelength. The distance between the mirrors is adjusted based on the number of spectral channels and their central wavelengths. The MEMS–FPI was integrated into a conventional camera system in its entirety, forming a multispectral imager (Fig. 2(b)). To circumvent the potential interference of the harmonic wavelength light transmitted by the FPI with the spectral observation, a bandpass filter was positioned in front of the FPI. This configuration enables the selective passage of a specific range of wavelengths, thereby isolating a single wavelength and facilitating the efficient filtering of reflected light. The FPI was tuned with one mirror fixed, and the other mirror could be moved to adjust the length of the FP cavity within a set electrostatic drive range. As the mirrors are moved, light of a specific wavelength is transmitted through the imaging lens and imaged on the sensor. By adjusting the cavity length in a stepwise manner, a series of spectral image data can be acquired, and a complete spectral image cube can be constructed. The multispectral imager is capable of providing 10 different spectral channels in the red-edge (RE) and NIR ranges, each of which can acquire images with a resolution of 1280 ​× ​1024 pixels and a spectral resolution of 20 ​nm.

In the BMSI 3D reconstruction process, the 3D stereo coordinates of a point on the surface of an object are calculated on the basis of multispectral stereo images, whereby the coordinates are determined by the intersection of rays emanating from the camera and the surface of the object. The measurement process comprises two principal stages: camera calibration and 3D coordinate calculation. Prior to performing a 3D stereo measurement, an initial calibration must be performed to determine the global coordinates and to estimate the internal and external parameters of the camera. The objective of the calibration is to determine the camera's intrinsic and extrinsic parameters through the utilization of a pinhole camera model. The extrinsic parameters describe the relative orientation of the two camera coordinate systems through translation and rotation between the two projection centers. The intrinsic parameters include the camera constant, which is defined as the distance between the camera center and the image plane, as well as the coordinates of the principal point, which is the intersection of the optical axis of the lens and the image plane. Additionally, the intrinsic parameters encompass image distortions, which can be classified into two main categories: radial and tangential distortions.

After stereo calibration, the 3D coordinates of the measured object can be calculated based on the defined global coordinates. To achieve a rapid and robust measurement of the 3D shape of the object surface, we use the disparity between the stereo spectral images captured by the binocular multispectral imagers (i.e., the spatial displacement of the image points of the same object point) for 3D reconstruction. As shown in Fig. 2(c), for a global point P(Xw, Yw, Zw) on the measurement object, the corresponding coordinates of the captured light rays in the camera image coordinate systems are pl(xl, yl) and pr(xr, yr). Multispectral image segmentation and evaluation

When spectral image acquisition was performed, the dark current data from the camera and the multispectral image data from the white board, which were used as a standard reference with known reflectance, were acquired simultaneously. First, the average radiant luminance value of each band of the whiteboard image (Iwhite) is calculated and combined with the known reflectance of the whiteboard (ρ ​= ​90 ​%) to obtain the correction factor Co ​= ​ρ/Iwhite. This correction coefficient (Icorrected) is subsequently applied to target object (Iobj) measurements through Icorrected ​= ​Co ​× ​Iobj. This process attenuates errors caused by differences in sensor response and uneven illumination, thereby increasing the accuracy of the spectral image data.

Compared with RGB sensors with broad-band filtering (FWHM ​= ​80 ​nm), narrow-band filtered multispectral imaging systems with FWHM ​< ​40 ​nm have greater effective spectral resolution and less crosstalk between spectral images. The standard deviation of each spectral band in a spectral image reflects the amount of information in that band; the larger the standard deviation is, the greater the amount of information contained. When there is a high degree of information overlap between spectral image bands, these two channels can be combined into one or reduced to just one band to reduce data redundancy. In this paper, the optimal index factor (OIF) is used to select combinations of bands with high independence and low information redundancy and fuse them into a single spectral image with considerable information content. The OIF algorithm was proposed by Chavez et al. [36] in 1994 in the United States to assess the independence among various bands in k spectral images. Experimental demonstrations

This section provides a comprehensive account of the BMSI demonstration experiments. We present the effectiveness and evaluation metrics of spectral image segmentation across different plant species. A systematic assessment of reconstruction precision and system stability with different measurement distances is conducted, and the 4D plant models generated by the BMSI system demonstrate its ability to fuse stereoscopic and spectral information.

A calibration board with a uniformly distributed 11 ​× ​9 checkerboard pattern was employed, where the marker positions were precisely known. The calibration algorithm relies on the correspondences between the checkerboard positions and their coordinates on the image plane. Hyperspectral imaging systems (e.g., line-scan hyperspectral cameras) typically integrate two-dimensional spatial information with one-dimensional spectral data. These systems are often associated with extended acquisition times and substantial data volumes. In contrast, the imaging architecture proposed in this study demonstrates increased acquisition efficiency while simultaneously capturing 4D data (three spatial dimensions combined with spectral information). Compared with conventional 3D imaging modalities that acquire depth information (such as RGB-D cameras), our system extends functionality through integrated spectral analysis, showing distinctive advantages in applications ranging from 3D surface morphology reconstruction to Chl distribution analysis on plant surfaces. Compared with existing 4D imaging systems, our approach achieves direct spatial mapping between depth and spectral information without the need for cross-sensor registration algorithms while maintaining both spectral and spatial resolution fidelity. The BMSI system preserves relatively high spatial resolution, with experimental results demonstrating a single acquisition capacity of up to 1,310,720 four-dimensional data points.

Performance of the BMSI system

The performance of the BMSI system is systematically measured with the help of a series of evaluation parameters. These parameters are determined by using the BMSI system for 3D reconstruction of calibration plates placed at specific ranges and by generating point cloud data. The calibration plates were positioned at different measurement distances within a range of 700–1500 ​mm for the subsequent 3D reconstruction calculations. The collected data were entered into the BMSI system and underwent a series of preprocessing steps to increase the quality and accuracy of data reconstruction. These steps included the removal of noise and the correction of spectral information. The preprocessed data were then utilized for 3D reconstruction, thereby generating a point cloud model. The performance of the system was measured by evaluation parameters such as the plane fitting standard deviation.

The standard deviation reference measurements when the point cloud data are fitted to the plane at different distances are shown in 6(a). The vertical coordinates are goodness of fit (R2) on the left and root mean square error (RMSE) on the right. The data in Fig. 6(a) contain two curves, which indicate the relationship between R2 and RMSE and the distance. As shown in the figure, the R2 value remains within the range of 0.8–0.9 for distances between 785 ​mm and 1455 ​mm. These findings suggest that the fitting effect remains relatively stable and effective across different distances. In the close-range distance ( < 900 ​mm), the elevated RMSE stems primarily from residual lens distortion effects: higher-order aberrations degrade point cloud coplanarity, with persistent postcalibration distortions impacting RMSE accuracy. Within the 900–1220 ​mm working distance, a dynamic equilibrium between nonlinearly attenuated distortion and stereo matching noise drives a reduction in the RMSE (minimizing at 0.89 ​mm), indicating increased plane-fitting precision in this optimal range. Beyond 1220 ​mm, the RMSE increases monotonically to approximately 1.3 ​mm at 1455 ​mm. This degradation arises from disparate computation errors that increase with physical resolution limits, exhibiting near-linear error growth with increasing distance.

The performance of BMSI in the determination of plant material

The preceding section presented the utilization of the BMSI system with plants as the objects of measurement, resulting in 4D image spectral data. In this section, the first-order derivative and NDRE of the spectral data were calculated to obtain the distribution of Chl on the 3D surface of the plants.

Determination of the plant Chl content

Some studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between the reflectance at the RE and the leaf Chl content, with inversion results exhibiting superior precision compared with those of traditional Chl meters [[38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. The red edge position is well correlated with the Chl content. As the Chl content increases, the red edge position gradually shifts toward longer wavelengths, which is known as a redshift. Conversely, as the Chl content decreases, the red edge position moves in the shorter wavelength direction, which is known as a blueshift. The first derivative of spectral reflectance with respect to wavelength has the potential to highlight changes in the spectrum, eliminate the impact of background noise in spectral data, and aid in identifying the RE position of leaves.

NDRE is a vegetation index that is employed in remote sensing and plant physiology. This index employs the reflectance characteristics of plant leaves in specific spectral bands, notably the phenomenon in which reflectance is markedly increased in the RE region, which is highly beneficial for monitoring the Chl status in plants and environmental stress. It is employed to assess vegetation health and monitor crop growth conditions.

In the formula, Iλ(NIR) represents the reflectance in the NIR band, taken at the 851 ​nm band, and Iλ(RE) is the reflectance in the RE band, taken at the 713 ​nm band. The value of this index ranges from −1 to 1, with positive values typically associated with a healthy plant condition.

Distribution of Chl on the 3D surface of plants

The distributions of Chl on a 3D stereo model and spectral characterizations across two different regions are shown in Fig. 8(a)–(d), respectively. Fig. 8(e) and (f) illustrate the spectral response of the plant at different leaf positions and their corresponding first-order derivatives, which align with regions A and B, respectively, in Fig. 8(b). By analyzing the spectral reflectance, an examination of the changes and first-order derivatives for regions A and B reveals that the first-order derivative for region A reaches its maximum at 736 ​nm, whereas the first-order derivative for region B reaches its maximum at 759 ​nm. This indicates that the spectral response of region A is redshifted at the RE position, whereby the wavelength corresponding to the maximum value of the first-order derivative is longer. This phenomenon is typically linked to reduced Chl levels, indicating that plants in region A may have lower Chl concentrations and exhibit suboptimum growth than those in region B do. Conversely, the shorter wavelength corresponding to the maximum value of the first-order derivative in region B suggests that the Chl content in this region is higher, suggesting that the plants are thriving in healthier conditions.

Consequently, the use of BMSI to generate the distribution of plant Chl on 3D models provides a multiperspective and detailed representation of plant structure and growth. This level of detail is pivotal for applications such as precision agriculture and is anticipated to facilitate the visualization and measurement of plant growth patterns, thus providing robust data for supporting agricultural management decisions.

Conclusions and outlook

This paper presents the construction of a BMSI system, which addresses the need for the fusion of spectral imaging microdevices and 3D imaging measurement technology in the context of micro-optical machine technology development. The system achieves accurate registration of 3D data and spectral data at the pixel level by synchronously collecting stereo spectral images, calculating 3D stereo data from stereo spectral images, and collecting spectral information from multispectral images simultaneously. Furthermore, a 4D dataset is constructed. The system offers the advantages of rapid acquisition, straightforward composition and high integration, without the need for complex registration algorithms or sacrifices in spectral and spatial resolution. The optimal band combination fusion technique and HSV color space spectral image segmentation allow the spectral features of plants to be preserved effectively through the statistical analysis of spectral images in optimal band combinations, while accurate segmentation of plant leaf structure and background shadows is achieved. The image segmentation process is undertaken by utilizing a combination of Otsu thresholding, the K-means clustering method, and graph cutting and the watershed method. The efficacy of the segmentation algorithm for two distinct plant samples is significantly enhanced by this methodology, thereby substantiating the robustness of the method across diverse plant samples. The system is also evaluated for reconstruction accuracy and stability with different measurement distances. The system was used to acquire 4D image spectral data from plants, and the first-order derivatives and NDRE of the spectral data were employed for nondestructive assessment of the distribution of Chl content in plants, revealing the distribution characteristics of Chl on the 3D surface of plants. This achievement offers a novel technical approach for plant phenotyping research and provides a more efficient technical method for digitization and precision monitoring in the agricultural industry.

In future developments, the use of the BMSI system has the potential to expand the scope of spectral analysis; this could include the quantification of photosynthesis dynamics through chlorophyll fluorescence (ChlF) induced by ultraviolet (UV) and shortwave infrared wavelengths. The application of this technology could facilitate the capture of deep physiological indicators such as water stress and lignin content. Concurrently, the integration of scatter projection technology and binocular stereo vision increases the efficiency and precision of 3D reconstruction. In response to the challenges of field environment deployment, the BMSI system faces three core limitations. First, the dynamic characteristics of the light source are defined by the fact that solar irradiance fluctuates drastically from sunny to cloudy conditions, and the distribution of the light source varies at different times of the day [45]. Second, there is the spectral correction mechanism. The instability of the light source poses a challenge to spectral correction, and the complexity of the spectral correction operation is high every time, which makes it difficult to obtain valid and correct spectral information. The hardware adaptation bottleneck can be overcome by using a multidisciplinary approach in the development of the BMSI system. The hardware adaptability bottleneck is evident in the exposure time, which is approximately three times longer in cloudy conditions than in laboratory settings. The inability of the current system to cope with extreme low-light conditions is a significant challenge. In subsequent studies, we will explore the integration of polarimetric multispectral imaging with dynamic migration learning algorithms. This approach aims to enhance the spectral correction and extend the low-light adaptability of the system. In conclusion, this paper presents a novel approach that fuses multispectral imaging with 3D stereo data, thereby providing a powerful tool for plant phenotyping. In-depth analysis of plant structure, spectral features and Chl distribution has significant scientific implications and potential applications in precision agriculture and plant biology research. With the continuous advancement of technology, even stronger support for sustainable agricultural practices and food security is expected.
