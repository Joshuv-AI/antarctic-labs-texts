A Case of Acalculous Cholecystitis During Lenvatinib Plus Pembrolizumab Therapy for Advanced Renal Cell Carcinoma

Introduction

Lenvatinib is an oral multi‐targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) that inhibits vascular endothelial growth factor receptors (VEGFRs), fibroblast growth factor receptors, and other kinases [1, 2]. It is used in thyroid cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and renal cell carcinoma (RCC), as monotherapy or with pembrolizumab [1]. In advanced RCC, lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab is a standard first‐line regimen. Common adverse events (AEs) include hypertension, proteinuria, fatigue, and hypothyroidism, whereas hepatobiliary complications are generally uncommon [3].

However, acute cholecystitis has been recognized as a rare but potentially serious AE during lenvatinib‐based therapy and has been listed as a serious adverse reaction in Japan since 2018 [4]. Cholecystitis during lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab therapy is often acalculous, and pharmacovigilance reports and case series suggest an association between VEGFR‐TKIs and acute acalculous cholecystitis, sometimes complicated by gallbladder perforation [1, 2, 5]. A proposed mechanism is microvascular ischemia of the gallbladder wall due to VEGF pathway inhibition [1].

Importantly, early‐stage disease may lack typical elevations of cholestatic enzymes such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and γ‐glutamyl transpeptidase (γ‐GTP) [2, 6]. We report a case diagnosed promptly through careful reassessment and timely imaging before significant hepatobiliary enzyme elevation.

Case Presentation

A 60‐year‐old woman was transferred to our hospital in October of year X–1 because of progressive quadriparesis caused by a cervical spine metastasis (Figure 1A). Contrast‐enhanced CT after transfer suggested left renal cell carcinoma with multiple metastases to the C6 vertebra, lungs, liver, and lymph nodes (Figure 1B–E). Because left upper‐extremity paralysis progressed, dexamethasone was initiated, and palliative radiotherapy to the C6 lesion was administered. Renal biopsy demonstrated non–clear cell renal cell carcinoma with TFE3 positivity. She was diagnosed with cT1bN0M1, stage IV disease, and classified as poor risk by the International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium (IMDC) criteria (three risk factors).

Baseline imaging findings at diagnosis. (A) Sagittal T2‐weighted MRI showing a cervical spinal lesion with epidural extension. (B) Contrast‐enhanced CT demonstrating a left renal mass. (C) Contrast‐enhanced CT of the chest showing mediastinal lymphadenopathy. (D) Contrast‐enhanced CT demonstrating a hepatic lesion suspicious for metastasis. (E) Chest CT showing a pulmonary nodule consistent with metastasis. Yellow circles indicate the representative lesions.

In November of year X–1, treatment with lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab (cycle 1) was initiated. Lenvatinib was started at 8 mg, considering her clinical condition, and was escalated to 12 mg from cycle 2 onward. After cycle 3, CT showed shrinkage of the primary tumor and metastatic lesions (cervical spine, lungs, liver, and lymph nodes), and the response was assessed as a partial response according to RECIST (Figure 2).

Radiographic response to pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib. Representative CT images at baseline (upper panels) and after 3 cycles of pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib (lower panels). The circled lesions show a decrease in size, consistent with a partial response (PR) by RECIST.

In January of year X, 9 days after starting cycle 4 of lenvatinib (12 mg) plus pembrolizumab, she developed low‐grade fever and decreased appetite, followed 4 days later by right upper quadrant pain. CT revealed gallbladder distension and wall thickening, leading to a diagnosis of grade 2 acute cholecystitis (Figure 3). At that time, cholestatic enzymes were not elevated: γ‐GTP was 33 U/L (reference range, < 34 U/L) and ALP was 80 U/L (reference range, < 113 U/L). Given the temporal relationship and the possibility of lenvatinib‐associated cholecystitis, lenvatinib was immediately discontinued, and the patient was managed with fasting and intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam. Although delayed elevation of γ‐GTP/ALP prompted consideration of endoscopic ultrasound‐guided gallbladder drainage (EUS‐GBD), she improved with antibiotics and declined drainage. Oral intake was resumed as symptoms continued to improve, and abdominal pain resolved with improvement of inflammatory findings. Antibiotic therapy was discontinued after 10 days (Figure 4).

CT findings at the onset of acute cholecystitis. Plain abdominal CT demonstrating gallbladder enlargement with wall thickening and pericholecystic inflammatory changes (yellow circle), consistent with acute cholecystitis.

Timeline of pembrolizumab administration (Pem①–⑤), lenvatinib dosing (8–12 mg), and supportive interventions, together with serial laboratory markers (CRP, ALP, and γ‐GTP) plotted over hospital days. Lenvatinib was resumed at a reduced dose of 8 mg in combination with pembrolizumab (cycle 5) on day 23 after discontinuation. She has experienced no recurrence of acute cholecystitis and has continued treatment with sustained response through March of year X + 1.

Discussion

Acute cholecystitis has been reported with immune checkpoint inhibitor‐based therapy, although it appears uncommon. In major first‐line ICI combination trials for advanced renal cell carcinoma, including pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib, acute cholecystitis has not been highlighted as a common adverse event [3, 7, 8]. However, postmarketing safety data for lenvatinib have identified acute cholecystitis, including acalculous cholecystitis, as a clinically relevant adverse event [4]. Although its incidence during pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib therapy remains unclear, clinicians should be aware of it. In the present case, cholecystitis was diagnosed early despite normal γ‐GTP and ALP. Because biliary enzyme abnormalities may be absent in the early phase of lenvatinib‐associated (often acalculous) cholecystitis [6], prompt imaging should be considered when compatible symptoms or right upper quadrant tenderness occur, even if γ‐GTP and ALP remain within the normal range.

The temporal relationship between treatment exposure and symptom onset, imaging findings consistent with acute acalculous cholecystitis, and improvement after lenvatinib interruption supported a lenvatinib‐related etiology. Although pembrolizumab‐related biliary immune toxicity cannot be excluded, the overall course was suggestive of lenvatinib‐associated cholecystitis.

TKI‐associated cholecystitis has increasingly been recognized as a rare but clinically relevant adverse event. Although the mechanism remains incompletely understood, several pathophysiological pathways have been proposed. First, inhibition of the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling pathway may induce microvascular ischemia in the gallbladder wall. VEGF plays an essential role in maintaining vascular integrity, and its inhibition may lead to endothelial dysfunction, reduced capillary perfusion, and subsequent ischemic injury [9, 10]. Second, TKIs may impair bile transport and promote bile stasis, thereby predisposing the gallbladder to inflammation and secondary infection [11]. Third, the anti‐angiogenic effects of TKIs may directly injure the gallbladder mucosa, resulting in epithelial damage and local inflammatory change [12]. These mechanisms may contribute to cholecystitis during TKI therapy, as suggested by previous case reports and pharmacovigilance analyses [13]. In particular, lenvatinib has been associated with acalculous cholecystitis in several reports, raising the possibility of a drug‐specific risk related to its potent anti‐angiogenic activity [14]. Reported onset ranges from days to several months after treatment initiation, and our case falls within this range [1, 14].

This case was notable for diagnosis before cholestatic enzyme elevation, improvement after lenvatinib interruption, and no recurrence after reduced‐dose rechallenge. Because fasting and broad‐spectrum antibiotics were also administered, the observed improvement may not be attributable to drug interruption alone. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that early recognition may permit continued therapy without immediate invasive intervention.

Distinguishing TKI‐related toxicity from immune‐related adverse events is important during combination therapy. Given the shorter half‐life of lenvatinib than pembrolizumab, temporary discontinuation of lenvatinib may reasonably be prioritized when cholecystitis is suspected [5]. Improvement after lenvatinib interruption supports a TKI‐related mechanism and may allow continuation of pembrolizumab with careful monitoring. Conversely, persistent inflammation despite lenvatinib interruption should prompt reconsideration of the differential diagnosis, including immune‐mediated biliary toxicity, and escalation of management as clinically indicated.

Finally, a possible regional difference has been suggested, as many cases involved Asian patients, including Japanese cohorts [1, 15]. Whether this reflects true susceptibility or differences in exposure, monitoring, and reporting remains unclear [4, 15]. Overall, our case highlights that early imaging‐based diagnosis and temporary interruption of lenvatinib may enable cautious rechallenge and continuation of systemic therapy.

Conclusion

Acute acalculous cholecystitis is a rare but clinically important adverse event during lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab therapy and may present without early cholestatic enzyme elevation. Clinicians should maintain suspicion and obtain prompt imaging when compatible symptoms or right upper quadrant tenderness occur, even if γ‐GTP and ALP are normal. Temporary interruption of lenvatinib with antibiotics may allow cautious rechallenge and continuation of systemic therapy.
