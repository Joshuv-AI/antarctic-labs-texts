Artificial intelligence-based chatbots improve the efficiency of course orientation among medical students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Gergely H Fodor, József Tolnai, Ferenc Rárosi, Attila Nagy, Ferenc Peták
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLarge language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT offer new ways to improve academic and administrative workflows in medical education, particularly for students studying in a language that is not their native tongue. We set out to examine whether a custom-trained chatbot could better support course orientation compared to traditional text-based resources for an ESL student population.MethodsSeventy-five first-year international medical students at the University of Szeged, Hungary participated during the first session of an introductory medical informatics course to use either a custom-trained ChatGPT-based chatbot (AI group, n = 35) or the university’s standard e-learning platform (Traditional group, n = 40) to locate key course information. A six-item test based on the syllabus assessed information retrieval within a 10-min window. Test scores, time per question, and question revisits were compared using independent t-tests and two-way ANOVA.ResultsNo differences were found in previous use of AI-based tools between the two groups (Traditional: 90.0% vs AI group: 91.4%, P = 1). The AI group scored significantly higher (5.0 [4.8 − 6.0], median[IQR]) than the Traditional group (3.5 [2.8 − 4.3], P < 0.001). The majority of AI users (24/35) achieved 5 or more correct answers, compared to just 6/40 in the Traditional group. The lowest scores (0 points) were found exclusively in the Traditional group. No significant difference was observed in total test completion time (Traditional: 380.3 ± 158.6; AI: 433.7 ± 121.4; P = 0.10), time spent per question (P = 0.90), or question revisits (P = 0.99). Notably, the AI group outperformed on specific questions related to course logistics, such as attendance (62.9% vs 17.5% correct) and grading criteria (82.9% vs 55.0% correct) (both P < 0.001).ConclusionsArtificial intelligence-assisted information retrieval significantly improved students’ ability to locate and understand key course content. Despite comparable time-on-task and navigation behaviours between groups, students using the ChatGPT-based chatbot achieved markedly higher test scores, likely due to the simplified and focused responses it provided. The absence of zero scores in the AI group and enhanced performance on logistical questions suggest that conversational interfaces may improve comprehension and retention of procedural information. These results highlight the potential of LLM-based tools to support more efficient onboarding in medical education, with a potential for broader applications across educational settings.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Chatbot, Large language model, Medical education, Information retrieval

Introduction

The integration of generative artificial intelligence (AI) and digital technologies into daily life and medical education has gained significant attention in recent years, reflecting the evolving demands of an increasingly digital world. The rapid growth of large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT offers new opportunities to enhance learning by providing immediate access to information and personalized feedback.

An increasing number of studies have examined the use of LLMs for a variety of academic tasks in medical education. Advanced versions of ChatGPT have performed well on United States Medical Licensing Examinations (USMLE), often scoring at or above the passing threshold across multiple domains [1–5]. Moreover, ChatGPT has been shown to support students through personalized mentoring and self-assessment, including applications such as clinical case simulations [6], quiz generation on challenging topics [7–10] and the explanation or simplification of complex materials, particularly when using foreign-language resources [11].

These tools also offer significant potential for faculty by reducing the workload of exam creation, grading, and other administrative tasks. Although ChatGPT can generate exam questions efficiently, studies show that AI-generated items often lack the complexity of human-written ones and require additional fact-checking, highlighting human-AI collaboration rather than replacement [12–14]. Because LLMs perform strongly in text analysis [15], they can also support grading of written assessments [16, 17]. However, relying solely on AI to evaluate exams or theses raises ethical and legal issues [18].

While these applications show promise, they also have clear limitations. In contrast, using LLMs to support the course organization presents a lower-risk application with considerable potential to reduce faculty workload.

Furthermore, students in international programmes who study English as a second language (ESL) often face difficulties related to general language proficiency [19], and subject-specific vocabulary [20], which can affect their academic progression.

Therefore, this study evaluates the effectiveness of an LLM-based chatbot in supporting organizational tasks in medical education, specifically testing whether it improves information retrieval efficiency for ESL students during course orientation compared with traditional methods.

Methods

Participants

Participants were first-year students enrolled in English-language medical programme at the Albert Szent-Györgyi Medical School, University of Szeged, which primarily attracts international students from non-English-speaking countries. As a result, the student population consists mainly of students for whom English is a second language (ESL). A total of 75 medical students enrolled in the elective course Introduction to Medical Informatics, which includes both a lecture and a practical component, were included in the study. Students were randomly assigned to one of two groups: 35 to the AI group, and 40 to the Traditional group. Participants came from 7 of the 15 study groups for that academic year, with all students from the selected groups included. Students were given the option to opt out, however, none chose to do so. The study took place in September 2024, when a free ChatGPT account was still required to access the chatbot.

Protocol

In the first practical session, students were asked to extract key information from the course requirements. The Traditional group received the course syllabus (about two printed pages) via the university’s e-learning platform (Coospace, Dexter Kft, Pécs, Hungary). The AI group used a custom-trained ChatGPT-based chatbot (ChatGPT Custom GPT, model GPT-4o, OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA), designed to provide information on the course requirements.

The chatbot was trained with the course syllabus as embeddings, supplemented with manually added question–answer pairs refined through extensive testing by the course instructors to ensure accuracy. It was explicitly instructed to keep discussions strictly focused on course content. Because access to the chatbot required a free ChatGPT account, two students without an account were reassigned to the Traditional group before the test. As students used their own accounts, chat histories could not be analysed to examine interaction strategies.

To assess the effectiveness of each information retrieval method, all students completed a short test on course requirements using the university’s e-learning platform. Test questions were taken directly from the syllabus and from instructors’ feedback on common student queries. The test had a 10-min time limit. Students were not instructed to work as quickly as possible, but could submit their answers before the time expired. If the time limit expired, the platform closed the test automatically.

The test consisted of seven questions, the first of which recorded group allocation (all reproduced unchanged in Table 1). Four questions were multiple-choice, while two questions requiring exact numerical responses. One question had multiple correct answers, in this case, students assessed each option as true or false, with partial credit awarded. To minimise order effects, both the sequence of questions and the order of answer choices were randomised for each student. Individual work was enforced by the instructor during the test. Students were instructed to use only their assigned tool: the Traditional group used the course syllabus on the e-learning platform, while the AI group interacted with the chatbot. The test was administered in a computer-equipped classroom, however, AI group participants were allowed to access the chatbot on their smartphones via a QR code, provided they had the ChatGPT app installed.

The chatbot itself was created using ChatGPT’s custom GPT wizard. Uploading the syllabus and relevant materials as embeddings took less than half an hour, while iterative testing and refinement (adding targeted question–answer pairs and retesting) required an additional 3–5 h, performed primarily by the first author with brief contributions (10–15 min each) from the course instructors. Importantly, the creation process required a ChatGPT Plus (paid) account, although once deployed, the chatbot was accessible to students through free ChatGPT accounts.

Evaluation

Students received their total scores (maximum 6) immediately after completing the test. After the assessment, the correct course requirements were reviewed in detail for all students, with a focus on the tested information to ensure clarity and to reinforce learning. The chatbot was opened for unrestricted use by all students, regardless of group allocation or study inclusion. Test results were exported from the e-learning platform for analysis. The dataset included item-level responses, total test duration, time spent on each question, and the number of revisits per question. Data processing was done using Microsoft Excel.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.2, R Core Team, 2023) [21]. The primary outcome was the total test score. Differences in prior use of AI tools between groups were assessed with the chi-squared test. Total test scores and overall completion times were compared with two-sample t-tests. The number of correct answers, time spent per question, and question revisits were analysed with two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), using group allocation and question as factors. In cases of significant results, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with the Holm-Sidak correction for multiple testing. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Effect size for primary outcome was calculated as Cohen’s d and reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI) [22]. To assess whether the sample size provided adequate sensitivity, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the actual group sizes (n = 35 vs 40). At α = 0.05, the design had 80% power to detect a minimum standardised effect size of d = 0.66, as calculated by the online version of PS Power and Sample size at https://vbiostatps.app.vumc.org/ [23].

Results

To assess baseline informatics and AI literacy, students were asked after the test which AI tools they had used before the course. The proportion of students reporting no prior use of AI tools did not differ between groups (4 of 40 in the Traditional group vs. 3 of 35 in the AI group; χ2 < 0.001, P = 1). Among students who had previously used AI tools, commonly mentioned services were ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Microsoft Copilot. Although broader academic backgrounds were not formally assessed, end-of-semester course scores did not differ significantly (Traditional: 128.99 ± 32.75; AI: 124.07 ± 32.85; P = 0.53).

In the AI group, 24 of 35 students scored 5 or higher, while only 2 scored below 3. In contrast, only 6 of 40 students in the Traditional group scored 5 or higher, while 16 scored below 3. Discussion

This study investigated whether the use of LLM-based chatbots improve medical students’ information retrieval of course requirements. Students using the chatbot performed significantly better on the test than those relying on traditional text-based resources. There was no significant difference in test completion time, suggesting that the chatbot enhanced accuracy without increasing time demands. The largest performance gaps were on key questions about attendance requirements, passing scores, and grading criteria, suggesting that the chatbot may be particularly effective for clarifying structured academic information.

One possible explanation for group differences could be variation in AI adoption, such as general bias against AI (algorithm aversion [24]), or the real or anticipated presence of a social penalties of disclosing AI use [25, 26]. However, because no significant differences were observed in prior AI tool use between groups, the results are unlikely to be influenced by varying levels of AI literacy or technology aversion. End-of-course academic performance also did not differ significantly, suggesting comparable academic backgrounds. The observed benefits are therefore most likely attributable to the direct effects of chatbot use. Although not formally analysed, instructors reported receiving fewer course requirements-related questions during the semester than in previous years. Improved accuracy of information retrieval may have also helped prevent misunderstandings about course expectations.

Several factors may explain the key findings. First, language likely played a significant role. Most students in the English-language programme study English as a second language. The course requirements are drafted in a highly technical style, which aims for precision but often makes the text difficult to fully comprehend. In contrast, the chatbot presents the information in a simplified, structured format, facilitating comprehension of otherwise complex requirements. Evidence suggests that non-native speakers may find traditional text-based formats difficult to assimilate [20], making chatbots particularly beneficial by reducing cognitive load and enhancing knowledge retention [27, 28]. Furthermore, the chatbot supports follow-up questions, enabling dialogue where students can seek clarification or further explanation. This interactive approach contrasts with conventional resources, which lack an immediate feedback [29, 30].

A secondary finding was the near-significant trend toward longer total test times in the AI group. Although not statistically significant at the 5% level, this may reflect the additional time needed to type and phrase queries. Many students used the chatbot on their phones, where typing is slower, and may also have needed extra time to formulate and refine questions, including follow-up queries.

This study has some limitations. Because students used personal ChatGPT accounts, we were unable to analyse their prompt histories. Analysing individual dialogues could provide further insight into the mechanisms underlying chatbot benefits and help identify efficient and inefficient prompting behaviours. Furthermore, the questionnaire was relatively simple, making subtle differences harder to detect. However, the study focused on differences in key information extraction, and the questionnaire was based on instructor feedback and common student queries about course requirements. Although the sample size was moderate, sensitivity analysis showed that the study was adequately powered to detect effects of the observed magnitude in the primary outcome.

The reputation of generative AI tools like ChatGPT is mixed. Concerns include potential reductions in critical thinking [31], increased student stress [32, 33], and promotion of superficial learning that may lower productivity [34]. Conversely, recent evidence, including a meta-analysis, suggests that these tools can foster effective learning [35], leading to a deeper comprehension and greater engagement [36, 37]. Because course requirements typically do not demand deep, lifelong memorisation, chatbots may benefit students by reducing cognitive load and providing a personalised way to access this information [28, 38].

A key implication of our findings is that faculty should embrace LLM-based tools to improve the quality of course requirements and learning objectives [39, 40]. This would not only benefit students through clearer documents but could also reduce faculty workload by up to 50% [40].

Conclusions

This study showed that chatbots trained on course materials significantly improved students’ retrieval of course requirements compared to traditional methods, while the trend toward longer completion times in the AI group was not statistically significant. Because participants were predominantly ESL students, the findings highlight the value of chatbots in supporting orientation tasks involving complex technical texts. Such tools can enhance comprehension without increasing time burden and may also ease faculty workload by reducing routine administrative queries.
