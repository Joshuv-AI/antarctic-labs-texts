Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw: a retrospective single center study of recurrence-related factors after surgical treatment
Authors: Han-Jin Ruan, Meng-Yu Li, Zhi-Yuan Zhang, Hai-Long Ma, Yue He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo provide an overview of the features of patients with medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) and explore recurrence-related factors after surgery.Materials and methodsAll pathological records of patients diagnosed with osteonecrosis or osteomyelitis of the jaw were reviewed. Only patients who had a history of use of medication related to bone turnover were included. All demographic and clinical characteristics were collected during review. Univariate and logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between risk factors and recurrence. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance in all analyses.ResultsA total of 313 patients were ultimately included. Most patients (89.14%) underwent bone turnover-related treatment due to malignancy. The breast and prostate were the most common locations of primary tumors in females and males, respectively. Almost all MRONJ patients experienced inflammatory symptoms. Recurrence occurred in 55 patients at 60 locations. The total recurrence rate was 16.85%, with no significant differences between the maxilla and mandible. Extensive surgery and flap transfer were strongly related to a lower recurrence risk. Nearly 80% of patients had recurrence-related symptoms within 6 months.ConclusionWhen MRONJ is treated with surgical methods, extensive resection and flap transfer can reduce recurrence risk. Six-month follow-up is needed to exclude recurrence after surgery.Clinical relevanceThis study revealed the surgical-related risk factors, such as extensive surgery and flap transfer, when treating MRONJ patients, and 6-month follow-up is needed to detect recurrence. This could provide clinical guidance for head and neck surgeons.Keywords: Bisphosphonate-associated osteonecrosis of the jaw, Diphosphonates, Drug-related side effects and adverse reactions

Introduction

With the progress in immunotherapy and targeted therapy, the survival rate of patients with malignancies has increased significantly, but the incidence of metastatic bone disease is also increasing. Bone is the third most common site of tumor metastasis. Skeletal-related events (SREs) caused by bone metastasis, such as pathological fracture, decrease quality of life and lead to a poor prognosis [1]. On the other hand, with the aging of the population, which is becoming one of the biggest issues in the twenty-first century, the incidence of osteoporosis has increased rapidly. Poor bone quality and delayed healing in osteoporosis patients lead to a high risk of fracture. It has been reported that 33.2% of osteoporosis patients lose self-care ability after hip or vertebral fractures [2]. Therefore, to prevent or control SREs, antiresorptive drugs, such as zoledronate or denosumab, are increasingly used. However, with the application of these medications, adverse events related to drug administration are receiving increasing attention. In 2003, Marx reported avascular necrosis of the jaw related to pamidronate and zoledronate [3]. Since then, the use of more drugs, including bevacizumab [4], corticosteroids [5], and CDK4/6 inhibitors [6], has been found to be related to necrosis of the jaw. Therefore, the American Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons (AAOMS) proposed the concept of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) in 2014 [7] to describe this new phenomenon.

MRONJ refers to an adverse event that occurs after the exposure of antiresorptive medications, antiangiogenic medications or immune modulators in patients without a history of radiotherapy, manifested by consistent exposed bone or fistula for 8 weeks [8]. The incidence of MRONJ in cancer patients exposed to related medications ranges from 0 to 18%, whereas that in osteoporosis patients ranges from 0.02 to 0.05% [8]. Patients suffer from pain, trismus, decreased quality of life and mental health because of the disease [9], which not only affects their daily life but also hinders the treatment of the original disease. Notably, the incidence of MRONJ is high, but treatment strategies are limited. Although several studies have shown promising results for nonoperative therapies, such as hyperbaric oxygen therapy [10], ozone therapy [11] and teriparatide [12], surgery is still the main treatment method. Moreover, it was suggested early surgery should be performed to stop further disease progression [13]. However, not all lesions can be managed through surgery. The success rate for surgical management was only 61–89% in previous studies [14–19]. Sometimes, patients with recurrence need more extensive surgery during later reoperation, leading to a higher treatment cost and increased patient suffering. Therefore, to reduce patients’ pain and minimize the influence of the treatment of the original disease, it is important to understand and target recurrence-related factors.

To provide an overview of patient features, we performed a retrospective study on all pathologically diagnosed MRONJ patients in our hospital and systematically described their clinical characteristics. To further study the risk factors for recurrence, we divided the patients into two groups and found that extensive resection and flap transfer were related to decreased recurrence risk. We believe that this study will provide valuable guidance for the operative management of MRONJ.

Methods and materials

Patient selection

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, Shanghai JiaoTong University, School of Medicine (SH9H-2021-TK472-1). A retrospective review of records of patients who were diagnosed with osteonecrosis or osteomyelitis of the jaw at Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, School of Medicine, from January 1995 to May 2024 was performed. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) current or previous treatment with antiresorptive drugs, antiangiogenic drugs, glucocorticoids or other potential drugs related to bone turnover according to previous studies; and (2) patients who underwent surgery and were pathologically diagnosed with MRONJ. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who were diagnosed with chronic osteomyelitis or osteoradionecrosis; (2) patients who had previous radiotherapy in the maxillofacial region; (3) patients who had records related to a second surgery for recurrence; and (4) patients without clinical data.

Data collection

All demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, cause of drug administration, primary location of malignant disease, type of drug, route of drug administration, hypertension, diabetes, previous dental practices, clinical symptoms, osteonecrosis location and number of lesions, were collected. For patients with recurrence, surgical procedures, wound closure methods, number of reoperations, time to recurrence-related symptoms and time to reoperation were reviewed.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are described as means and standard deviations, whereas categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Statistical analysis was performed via IBM SPSS Statistics (ver. 26; IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA). Univariate analyses were performed with the Mann‒Whitney test, chi‒square test, Fisher’s exact test and logistic regression analysis to evaluate the associations between risk factors and recurrence. During the analysis, samples with incomplete information were removed. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance in all analyses.

Results

A total of 641 medical records related to osteonecrosis or osteomyelitis of the jaw were identified. Three hundred twenty-two patients were diagnosed with osteoradionecrosis or chronic osteomyelitis, had previous radiotherapy in the maxillofacial region, or underwent reoperation for recurrence. Among the remaining patients, six patients were excluded because of missing data. Ultimately, 313 patients were included in the study (Fig. 1).

Demographic and clinical characteristics of MRONJ patients

Causes of drug administration and primary location of malignant disease

Malignancy (89.14%) was the main cause of drug administration, followed by osteoporosis (7.03%) and rheumatoid diseases (2.56%). Notably, no male MRONJ patient had osteoporosis, which may be due to the low incidence of osteoporosis in males. Among the remaining 4 patients, one patient received bisphosphonate after knee replacement, one patient received denosumab due to a giant cell tumor of the bone, and one patient decided to take bisphosphonate on her own. The fourth patient did not use any commonly reported drugs, but he took lamotrigine and memantine to treat epilepsy. As a previous study indicated the potential role of lamotrigine in affecting bone metabolism [20], this patient was considered to have MRONJ (Fig. 2a).

Among all MRONJ patients who had malignancies, the lung (27.24%), breast (26.52%), prostate (21.51%), bone marrow (10.39%) and kidney (7.17%) were the five most common primary tumor sites. In females, the breast (54.81%), lung (25.18%) and bone marrow (11.11%) were the three most common primary sites, and the prostate (41.67%), lung (29.17%), kidney (13.19%) and bone marrow (9.72%) were most common primary sites in males. Among the remaining patients, 5 had colon cancer, 4 had thyroid cancer, 3 had lymphoma, and 2 had multiple primary malignant tumors. The remaining 6 patients had various types of cancer, including esophageal cancer, pancreatic cancer, liver cancer, testicular cancer, malignant tumor in the submandibular gland and malignant tumor near the aorta (Fig. 2c).

Types of drugs and routes of administration

A total of 69.97% of patients used bisphosphonates, followed by denosumab (4.79%), glucocorticoids (1.92%) and antiangiogenic agents (0.64%). Forty patients had a history of multiple drug use, including 6 with bisphosphonate and antiangiogenic agents, 21 with bisphosphonate and denosumab, 12 with bisphosphonate and glucocorticoids, and 1 with denosumab and glucocorticoids. Notably, a total of 12 patients never took those widely reported medications. In case we missed any new MRONJ-related drugs, we recorded drugs they had used. These drugs include alfacalcidol, calcitriol, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (pazopanib, axitinib, and imatinib), Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitors (zanubrutinib), cytarabine, bortezomib, anti-CD38 monoclonal antibodies, tocilizumab, methotrexate, leflunomide, abemaciclib, lenalidomide, pomalidomide and iguratimod. There was no type of drug listed in the medical records of the other 19 patients (Fig. 2b).

The number of patients who were administered drugs through intravenous injection (61.34%) was almost three times greater than the number of patients who were taking oral drugs (19.49%). All 15 patients who received denosumab received a subcutaneous injection (4.79%). Twenty-eight patients (8.95%) had multiple routes of drug administration; 6 were treated through intravenous and oral routes, 20 were treated through intravenous and subcutaneous routes, and 2 were treated through oral and subcutaneous routes. There was no route of drug administration listed in the medical records of the other 17 patients (Fig. 2d).

Previous dental practices

Tooth extraction was still considered a trigger of MRONJ, as 44.09% of the patients in this study had undergone this procedure. In addition, implant placement (0.96%) and root canal therapy (0.64%) might also be related to MRONJ. Six patients underwent multiple dental practices with any combination of tooth extraction, implant placement, root canal therapy, periodontal therapy or dental prosthesis. Although previous dental practices are considered important risk factors for inducing MRONJ [8], 52.4% of patients developed MRONJ spontaneously according to our research (Fig. 2e).

Clinical manifestations

To obtain a better understanding of the clinical characteristics of MRONJ, we collected data on the clinical manifestations, lesion locations and number of lesions from all records. Almost all MRONJ patients experienced inflammatory symptoms such as pain and swelling (91.05%). Pyorrhea (60.38%) and necrotic bone exposure (53.99%) were the second and third most common symptoms, respectively. Extraoral fistulas (16.29%) were less common than intraoral fistulas (22.68%). Symptoms including trismus (29.07%) and lip numbness (24.92%) should be paid attention to distinguish from other diseases, such as temporomandibular joint diseases and malignant diseases of the jaw (Fig. 2f).

Only 31 patients (9.90%) had lesions in both jaw bones. There were 206 patients with osteonecrosis in the mandible, which was almost three times greater than that in the maxilla (24.28%). Patients with multiple lesions (12.78%) were far less common than those with a single lesion (87.22%) (Fig. 2g h).

Recurrence-related factors of MRONJ

Similar to the surgical treatment of malignant diseases, surgery related to MRONJ can also recurrence. To identify the risk factors for recurrence, we regarded patients who underwent reoperations at the same location as the recurrence and divided all patients into two groups: those with or without recurrence. There were 55 patients who had recurrence, with 29 males and 26 females. There were no significant differences in age (p = 0.144), sex (p = 0.373), cause of drug administration (p = 0.156), hypertension (p = 0.902) or diabetes (p = 0.533). After all the necrotic locations were counted, a total of 356 lesions were identified. The total recurrence rate was 16.85%. The recurrence rate in the maxilla was 13.64%, which was lower than that in the mandible (18.29%) (Fig. 3a). However, no significant difference was detected between the maxilla and mandible (p = 0.278). Next, we explored surgery-related factors related to recurrence. Both partial resection and segmental resection were regarded as extensive surgery. Compared with curettage, extensive surgery was strongly correlated with a decreased recurrence risk (p < 0.001), with an HR of 0.16 (95% CI = 0.06–0.46). A significant difference was also observed in flap transfer (p = 0.013), which included both local flaps and free flaps. The HR of flap transfer with respect to recurrence was 0.34, with a 95% CI of 0.14–0.82. Interestingly, no difference in recurrence risk was observed between groups with and without direct suturing (p = 0.088), which was probably due to excessive tension applied when suturing the wound.

Clinical characteristics of recurrent sites

When reoperations were performed, 36.67% of sites underwent increased extent of surgery. The meaning of increased extent of surgery was curettage for the first time and partial resection, segmental resection or subtotal resection in later reoperations. Notably, the extent of the final surgery increased for 46.67% of sites which had recurrent mandibular lesions (Fig. 3b).

As follow-up time is one of the key factors in the clinic, we analyzed all of the reoperations and focused on the time to recurrence-related symptoms and reoperation. A total of 48.61% of sites experienced recurrence-related symptoms within 3 months, including 14 sites who had constant unhealing wounds since the first surgery. Nearly 80% of sites had recurrence-related symptoms within 6 months, which indicated the importance of follow-up within 6 months (Fig. 3c). With respect to reoperation, 54.17% of sites underwent surgery within 5 months. Only 2 sites underwent reoperation within one month (Fig. 3d). Discussion

Given the aging of the population and prolonged lifespan of patients with malignancies, more drugs are being used to prevent SREs. Therefore, the incidence of MRONJ has increased over the years [21, 22]. Patients with MRONJ experience more anxiety and depression than controls do [9]. However, although surgery has a significant effect on MRONJ treatment, the compromised general condition of patients and the obscure margin of these lesions increase the difficulty of disease control. As preventing disease progression was one of the goals of MRONJ treatment in the latest update of the AAOMS’s position paper [8], a better understanding of recurrence-related risk factors might reduce suffering in patients and aid in the treatment of primary disease. In our study, we systematically described the clinical characteristics of MRONJ patients and emphasized the potential factors related to recurrence. We found that more than 50% of patients developed the disease spontaneously. When MRONJ is treated with surgery, extensive resection and flap transfer are related to a lower recurrence rate.

Invasive dental practices, such as tooth extraction and implantation, are always considered important factors in the genesis of MRONJ. In a national retrospective study of 27,168 MRONJ patients, the hazard ratio of tooth extraction alone was 1.61, with a CI ranging from 0.74 to 3.52 [23]. Moreover, when teeth with previous inflammatory diseases are extracted, the risk of MRONJ development increases markedly [23, 24]. However, in our study, more than 50% of the patients did not receive any dental treatment. A similar result was reported in Soutome’s study, which revealed that even extracting teeth during antiresorptive therapy did not increase the risk of MRONJ development [25]. When oral or intravenous antibiotic regimens were used, only 7% of patients developed MRONJ after tooth extraction, with or without ongoing antiresorptive therapy [26]. These results indicate that focusing on invasive treatment alone may not be helpful in preventing MRONJ. In fact, more attention has been given to changes in the microenvironment, such as immune dysfunction [27, 28] and angiogenesis [29], after the use of zoledronate within the jaw. Breakthroughs in these fields may reveal promising targets for preventing MRONJ in the future.

While conservative treatment has a limited cure rate of only 50%, surgical treatment has a much better success rate of 90% [16]. In addition, early surgical treatment is considered beneficial for all-stage MRONJ patients [8]. However, the recurrence of MRONJ after surgery has been a difficult challenge in the clinic, with a recurrence rate of 11–39% [14–19]. This was consistent with our study of 16.85%. The difference in the recurrence rates was partly due to the inconsistent definitions of recurrence. In Chen’s study, recurrence was defined as symptoms (bone exposure, infection and dehisced tissue) that occurred within 3 months. To exclude metastasis, bone biopsies were performed in all patients [15]. Another study defined recurrence as infection or unrelieved pain after surgical treatment [14]. The other studies did not provide definitions [16, 17]. Although metastasis of the malignancy to the oral cavity is rare, the mandible is the most commonly affected site, accounting for 45.5% of all cases [30]. Therefore, in this study, we defined recurrence as reoperation at the same site, with pathological confirmation of osteonecrosis or osteomyelitis. No time limit was added in case of potential neglect. Although some cases may be missed if patients do not undergo a second surgery, we believe this definition is more accurate than definitions based only on clinical manifestations or radiographs.

As the accuracy of the histopathological margin determination is associated with recurrence [31], the most difficult part of surgery is defining the boundary between the normal and affected regions, especially when diffuse sclerosis occurs in 58.7% of MRONJ patients [32]. Although new techniques such as fluorescence-guided bone resection can increase the accuracy of detecting viable bone [33], no significant differences in outcome between this approach and conventional surgery have been observed [34]. To date, bone bleeding is still a quick and convenient method for defining viable bone during surgery.

As the gingival epithelium and angiogenesis can also be influenced after zoledronate usage [35], more attention should be given to the wound closure method. Primary closure blocks microbes and ensures an undisturbed environment for wound healing. Previous studies have indicated the relationship between primary healing and recurrence [14, 36]. However, this study did not reveal a significant difference in recurrence rate between patients with and without direct suturing. This result may be associated with tension when the wound is closed, which leads to wound dehiscence and delayed healing, allowing microbe invasion and infection. When surgery is performed in maxillary stage III patients with severe maxillary sinusitis, pocket infection can occur after Caldwell–Luc surgery. Both local flaps and free flaps can ensure tension-free closure, which was related to a lower risk of recurrence in our study. When flap transfer is not possible, other packing materials, such as autologous platelet concentrates, can provide a solid base and growth factors for epithelial healing [37], which might reduce the recurrence rate.

To ensure the efficacy of the operation, regular follow-up is needed for all patients. Although previous clinical studies have set follow-up endpoints at 6 to 24 months after surgery [19, 37–39], the rationale behind choosing these specific time points remains unclear. One study reviewed the healing time of MRONJ patients and suggested that the healing rate stabilizes 18 months postoperatively [40]. However, this study included very few cases, which decreases its universality. In this study, we analyzed the time to recurrence-related symptoms and reoperation after each surgery and reported that nearly 80% of patients experienced recurrence-related symptoms within 6 months, which indicated the need for a 6-month follow-up. As wound healing is truly difficult in MRONJ patients, sometimes clinicians may choose a “wait-and-see” approach when wounds have not yet healed. This may explain why only 54.17% of the patients underwent reoperation within 5 months. Future prospective studies are needed to determine the follow-up duration and observation window.

Conclusion

When MRONJ is treated via surgery, segmental resection and flap transfer can reduce the recurrence rate. Six-month follow-up is needed to exclude recurrence after surgery.
