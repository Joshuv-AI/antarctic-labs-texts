Artificial intelligence and radiomics biomarkers for treatment response prediction in advanced HER2-negative breast cancer
Authors: Laurent Dercle, Jeremy McGale, Binsheng Zhao, Julia Schmitt, Alexander Peltzer, Lawrence H Schwartz, Mario Amend
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy AimDespite the development of novel therapies, breast cancer mortality remains high. Improved treatment response assessment tools are needed. We aimed to test the transferability of externally validated AI/radiomics biomarkers for predicting treatment response in advanced, hormone receptor-positive (HR+), HER2-breast cancer treated with xentuzumab, exemestane, and everolimus.MethodsPatient data from a phase Ib/II trial (May 2014–October 2016) were analyzed retrospectively. Eight imaging biomarkers (liver and overall tumor volume, two radiomics features representing tumor heterogeneity at both baseline and week 8) were validated for predicting clinical benefit using AUC analysis. An ancillary AI analysis developed a signature for predicting best overall response using 40 variables (3 clinical and 37 imaging) in the same cohort.ResultsOf 106 patients with data available for analysis, 28 had no clinical benefit from treatment (Group A) vs. 78 with clinical benefit (Group B). Seven of eight imaging biomarkers demonstrated significant predictive value. Participants in Group B exhibited significantly lower baseline and follow-up measures of liver and overall tumor volume, alongside marked changes in tumor heterogeneity by week 8. In our ancillary AI/radiomics model, the dominant drivers of prediction were changes in both liver tumor and overall tumor volume during treatment and the number of osteoblastic lesions on baseline bone scans.ConclusionThis cross-cancer proof-of-concept testing study supports the feasibility of applying multimodal AI/radiomics biomarkers to predict treatment response in advanced HR+, HER2− breast cancer, laying the foundation for broader pancancer and pantreatment applications pending further validation.Clinical trial registration numberNCT02123823Keywords: AI-Radiomics, HER2, Breast cancer, Response, CT scan, Bone scan

Introduction

Breast cancer remains both the most prevalent cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide [1]. As the disease progresses and metastasizes, treatment options become limited, resulting in a steep drop-off in five-year survival rates for distantly disseminated cancer (31.9 %) versus loco-regional disease (86.7–99.6 %) [2]. New therapeutic approaches are urgently needed.

While targeted treatment options exist for both HR-positive and HER2-positive tumors, the former often leads to disease recurrence via primary or acquired endocrine resistance [3,4]. Various strategies, including cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) 4/6 and mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors, are being developed to treat or reverse endocrine resistance in HR-positive, HER2-negative disease [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. However, mTOR inhibitors specifically have generally failed to improve overall survival (OS), possibly due to concomitant upregulation of the IGF signaling pathway previously implicated in tumor proliferation, metastasis, and treatment resistance [9,10]. Preliminary studies neutralizing the IGF ligand with agents such as xentuzumab, a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody, have demonstrated both standalone antineoplastic activity and enhanced paired mTOR inhibitor efficacy, highlighting the pathway as a potential target for overcoming anti-estrogen resistance and improving existing treatments [[11], [12], [13]].

AI and radiomics are uniquely poised to accelerate the development and evaluation of novel therapies such as IGF inhibitors [14]. By analyzing vast arrays of medical imaging data, including radiomics features, AI-generated predictive models can improve non-invasive tumor phenotyping, prognostication, and response pattern differentiation, allowing clinicians to efficiently identify patients likely to benefit from specific treatments [15]. These models could refine therapeutic strategies by providing precise, real-time, imaging-guided insights into patient response.

Herein, we sought to Ref. [1] validate a series of eight previously investigated CT imaging biomarkers to predict treatment response in participants with advanced HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer undergoing treatment with exemestane and everolimus ± xentuzumab; and [2] develop a predictive model with a broader series of 40 biomarkers (derived from clinical characteristics, CT images, and bone scans) to identify the factors most strongly associated with survival. This work represents an intent to test the transferability of predictive biomarkers in a new cancer and treatment type and marks our first attempt at creating a multimodal AI/radiomics signature. Developing a single model based on multiple imaging modalities would be a significant advancement toward pancancer and pantreatment AI/radiomics biomarkers in oncologic imaging.

Methods

Participants

We retrospectively analyzed data from participants with advanced breast cancer recruited between May 2014–October 2016 to a randomized phase Ib/II clinical trial investigating the impact of exemestane and everolimus ± xentuzumab on median progression-free survival (NCT02123823) [16]. Tumor assessment followed standard RECIST 1.1 criteria, using contrast-enhanced CT scans of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis at baseline, week 8, and every 12 weeks up to 13 months. Participants also underwent a bone scan before treatment. Further study details can be found in the Supplemental Methods. Patients were included in the study if they had imaging available and interpretable at both baseline and week 8 follow-up (Fig. 1). The study protocol, approved by the Institutional Review Board with a consent waiver for use of de-identified retrospective data, adhered to HIPAA, Standards for Reporting Diagnostic accuracy studies (STARD), and Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines.

Flowchart of patient selection and classification.

CT imaging and bone scan features

Radiologists utilized a picture archiving and communications system viewing platform for semi-automatic volumetric segmentation of all detectable lesions at baseline and week 8 on every CT scan section. Imaging features were extracted from tumors at these two timepoints using previously established definitions of radiomics features [17]. The difference in radiomics features between baseline and week 8 post-treatment initiation (delta radiomics features, “_DELTA” suffix) was computed to characterize early changes. This lesion segmentation and feature extraction pipeline was previously peer-reviewed [18]. Radiologists also performed lesion segmentation of baseline bone scans. We used the number of lesions identified in these scans as a quantitative biomarker to indirectly assess the volume of osseous metastases.

Training data

Several prior studies have trained and validated AI/radiomics models for prognostication and treatment response prediction using variables derived from both baseline and first on-treatment imaging, summarized in Table 1 and Fig. 2. More details on model development are available in the Supplementary Methods. The training cohorts from these five trials, which demonstrated the predictive ability of individual imaging biomarkers across multiple cancers (e.g., non-small cell lung cancer [NSCLC], melanoma, colorectal cancer [CRC]) and treatment types, constitute the “training set.”

Primary objective

We sought to externally test the transferability a series of eight key imaging biomarkers derived from routine CT imaging that were previously identified as predictive of patient outcome in the training data above. These biomarkers were liver tumor volume (LTV), overall tumor volume (OTV), and two radiomics features (Components 4 and 6), each measured at baseline and first follow-up (week 8). The Supplemental Methods detail how the selected imaging features were first tested. The study cohort represents an external test set to test the transferability of biomarkers with a previously uninvestigated cancer and treatment type. Participants were categorized into two groups based on RECIST 1.1 objective response outcomes (progressive disease, stable disease, partial response, complete response). Performance of individual radiomics features in predicting clinical benefit was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for binary group classification as the primary endpoint. Although the predictive model in this study focuses on best overall response per RECIST 1.1, several of the included radiomics features were originally derived from signatures trained to predict overall survival, reflecting their established prognostic relevance across cancer types.

Ancillary AI/radiomics study

We also evaluated the predictive value of a broader series of 40 biomarkers—3 clinical features and 37 imaging features, one of which was the number of lesions identified on baseline bone scans—identified in the work outlined in Table 1, including those derived from clinical characteristics, CT imaging, and bone scans. This set of 40 variables was first individually applied to Groups A and B in a comparative study before being used to build an imaging signature. See Supplementary Methods for a description of the variables and model development methodology.

RECIST 1.1

Images were analyzed at our institution by two radiologists specializing in cancer imaging (PG, LD). These experts, blind to other clinical outcomes, assessed tumor response using RECIST 1.1 criteria at each evaluation point.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using MatLab version 9.5 (MathWorks), Excel version 2019 (Microsoft Corporation), and R version 3.6.2 (R Program for Statistical Computing) (LD). Statistical significance was defined as two-sided P < .05 (α = .05), with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Groups were defined by categories having occurred before the week 8 landmark and outcome events (BOR, OS) were only considered if they occurred after the landmark.

Results

Patients

A total of 140 patients with advanced breast cancer were recruited between May 2014–October 2016 for a combined phase Ib/II clinical trial investigating exemestane and everolimus ± xentuzumab. Of these, 106 had imaging data available at both baseline and week 8 follow-up; 34 patients were excluded due to missing transfer of source imaging data to the academic core. Patient selection details are shown in Fig. 1. Fifty-one patients were treated with exemestane (25 mg/day), everolimus (10 mg/day), and xentuzumab (1000 mg/week), and 55 patients were treated with exemestane + everolimus alone. Using RECIST 1.1 guidelines, 28 participants were classified as not experiencing clinical benefit from treatment (Group A; all progressive disease), while 78 had clinical benefit (Group B; 66 stable disease, 11 partial response, and 1 complete response). Seventy-six patients (71.7 %) were white and the mean age was 59.7 years (standard deviation: 9.3 years).

Biomarker performance

Eight radiomics features (LTV, OTV, Component 4, Component 6, LTV_DELTA, OTV_DELTA, Component 4_DELTA, and Component 6_DELTA) have previously been identified as predictive of treatment response in clinical trials examining patients with NSCLC, melanoma, and CRC (Table 1, Fig. 2). We sought to externally test the transferability of their predictive abilities in a new cancer and treatment type. All eight features were extracted or calculated from baseline and week 8 CT images of the 106 patients. Using AUC analysis, seven of the eight key imaging biomarkers demonstrated predictive value in differentiating Group A from Group B (defined as AUC≥50.0), with only Component 6_DELTA failing to show significance. LTV and LTV_DELTA demonstrated the highest AUC for prediction, followed by OTV_DELTA and baseline values of Components 4 and 6. Optimal thresholds, sensitivity, and specificity for each biomarker are detailed in Table 2.

AI/radiomics signature

In the ancillary AI/radiomics study of 40 features (Table 1, Fig. 2), our goal was three-fold [1]: perform a comparative analysis between the two treatment response groups [2], build a radiomics signature for best overall response prediction, and [3] within this signature, assess the relative importance assigned by AI to the eight variables examined in our primary objective and the incremental value of adding non-CT-derived features.

We first performed a comparative analysis of all 40 variables, applying them in the previously demarcated week 8 treatment response cohorts (Groups A and B) (Table 3). We found that 6 variables were significantly different between the two groups: OTV and LTV (measured in cm3) at baseline, delta measurements at week 8 follow-up, Component 4_DELTA, and baseline osseous lesion number as evaluated on bone scan. Higher baseline OTV and LTV, as well as more metabolically active bone lesions, were seen in Group A (no clinical benefit). A higher delta value for Component 4 was seen in Group B (clinical benefit). Notably, differences in baseline Component 4 values between the two groups approached significance, with a p-value of 0.055.

We then used data from the 106 participants to develop a radiomics signature using the 40 investigated biomarkers. A random-forest algorithm ranked the ability of all variables to estimate treatment response based on the four RECIST 1.1 categories. We established the final radiomics signature by including the top 10 ranked variables in our analysis (Table 2). The signature had an AUC of 75.5 for predicting clinical benefit in our participant cohort, better than any of the individual biomarkers explored in our primary objective. This finding, although beyond the main study aim, demonstrates that imaging biomarkers can be successfully integrated to predict outcomes across new tumor and treatment types.

This plot displays an unsupervised ranking of the top 10 features used in the multimodal AI/radiomics signature, based on their minimal depth across 500 decision trees in a random forest model. Each bar represents a single variable, with the color gradient indicating how often each feature is selected at different depths—shallower depths reflect greater predictive strength. Features like Liver_Delta and Overall_Delta frequently appear at depth 0, highlighting their strong contribution to model performance. In contrast, features like Component6_Delta and Component6_BL appear at greater depths, suggesting lower importance. Of note, the number of bone lesions from baseline bone scans—though not a conventional radiomic texture feature—ranked 6th, indicating moderate importance and supporting its inclusion as a pragmatic, clinically relevant surrogate of metastatic burden. This reinforces the value of a multimodal imaging approach even in an early-stage, proof-of-concept setting. The results reflect feature utility within a prototype model (Technology Readiness Level 3–4) developed for transferability testing, not for immediate clinical deployment.

Our machine learning algorithm assessed the rankings of individual variables within the AI/radiomics signature. The top 10 features were [1] LTV_DELTA, [2] OTV_DELTA, [3] LTV, [4] OTV, [5] Component 4, [6] number of osteoblastic bone lesions on bone scan, [7] Component I 4_DELTA, [8] age, [9] Component 6, and [10] Component 6_DELTA. Remarkably, all eight biomarkers from our primary objective were ranked among this group. Fig. 3 displays output of the unsupervised ranking process, focusing on variable importance based on the number of trees in a random forest algorithm that reaches a minimal depth when splitting the data. A variable with more trees reaching a depth of 0 signifies one that is more important to the predictive ability of the overall model. The top two variables, LTV_DELTA and OTV_DELTA, most frequently achieved shallow depths, indicating that they were the main drivers in prediction of treatment response. Notably, including the number of metabolically active bone lesions from bone scans added incremental value to the predictive model.

Variable importance ranking in the AI/radiomics model predicting best overall response.

As this model remains a proof-of-concept prototype (Technology Readiness Level 3–4), these results are intended to explore signal detection and feasibility rather than guide treatment decisions. Further validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is needed to confirm the clinical utility of such predictive visualizations.

Discussion

Our findings support the feasibility of using selected radiomic features—including baseline liver tumor volume (LTV), overall tumor volume (OTV), and CT-derived measures of tumor heterogeneity (Components 4 and 6), along with their early post-treatment changes—to predict overall response in patients with advanced HR+, HER2− breast cancer. While these biomarkers were originally developed in other tumor types and therapeutic settings, their transferability to this cohort highlights their potential general utility, albeit within the limitations of a retrospective, single-trial dataset. LTV and OTV remain intuitive indicators of disease burden and treatment susceptibility, particularly given the prognostic significance of visceral metastases—most notably in the liver—in shaping immune response [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. Moreover, early volumetric reductions and heterogeneity metrics capture dynamic and structural tumor traits that have previously been associated with outcome across cancers [[24], [25], [26]].

Overall, the results from our primary study support the hypothesis that AI/radiomics can significantly enhance treatment decision-making in advanced breast cancer. With CT and bone scans at baseline and first post-treatment follow-up, oncologists could estimate clinical benefit based on early changes in tumors on imaging, enabling rapid changes to therapy based on individual response, thus representing a potentially powerful tool in precision oncology.

The ancillary AI/radiomics study further corroborated the predictive abilities of the variables selected for the primary objective. Notably, the number of active lesions on bone scan also differed between the two groups at baseline. One of the most frequent metastatic sites of breast cancer is bone, and osseous metastases are associated with increased morbidity and decreased OS [27,28]. Mortality from breast cancer disseminated to the bone also increases with the number of bony lesions [29]. Thus, accurate identification of osseous lesions is critical for prognostication, with bone scans being the gold standard for detection [30,31].

Finally, we used machine learning to rank the 40 variables most strongly associated with best overall treatment response. All eight primary variables of interest were ranked among the top 10 features most predictive of outcome. Interestingly, the feature derived from baseline bone scans was also ranked in the top 10 variables and improved the model's predictive accuracy, supplementing our finding of its predictive/prognostic significance. While radiomics has previously been applied to bone scans—particularly in prostate cancer—this study represents, to our knowledge, the first integration of both CT and bone scan-derived features into a single multimodal radiomics signature for response prediction in breast cancer [32,33]. Given that bone scans remain the standard imaging modality for assessing osseous metastases, incorporating the number of bone lesions as a surrogate for metastatic burden was a pragmatic decision aimed at capturing clinically relevant disease extent. Although this variable is not a conventional radiomic texture feature and is semi-quantitative in nature, its inclusion aligns with our integrative modeling approach, which prioritizes leveraging all available imaging data to enhance prediction. In our model, this feature demonstrated moderate importance, contributing additional variance explained and supporting the added value of multimodal imaging in predictive modeling.

Our study has notable limitations. While the model shows promise for future integration into clinical decision-making, this study represents an early-stage, proof-of-concept effort focused on assessing the transferability of imaging biomarkers. As such, the model corresponds to a low Technology Readiness Level (TRL 3–4), emphasizing feasibility and prototype development rather than clinical implementation. Further validation in prospective, multicenter settings is required before it can inform treatment decisions. While it externally validates the potential transferability of a series of previously interrogated biomarkers, the study is limited by its retrospective nature (though the participant data was collected prospectively). The inherent focus on measurable tumor lesions, required for both radiomics and RECIST assessment, is also a limitation. By incorporating bone scan lesions, we hoped to provide a more generalized impression of tumor burden, though we were nevertheless limited by measurable lesions. Additionally, the AI/radiomics signature ancillary study was limited by cohort size. While nearly all individual radiomics features used for testing were trained in clinical trials, the signature herein was only trained on a cohort of 106 participants. Accordingly, there remains a significant possibility for overfitting; further testing on large, multicenter cohorts should be performed to ensure generalizability. Moreover, there is potential for decreased generalizability of the radiomics signature as it was trained only on participants with advanced breast cancer, though, as previously stated, the individual biomarkers have been identified as predictive in a number of other cancer and treatment types. Lastly, while we utilized imaging at baseline and week 8 to validate biomarkers and develop a radiomics signature, future studies should examine follow-up imaging at earlier timepoints to identify the first possible actionable changes in tumor imaging phenotype.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of applying previously validated radiomics biomarkers to predict treatment response in patients with advanced HR+, HER2− breast cancer using routine CT and bone scans. While the results are promising, they represent a proof-of-concept effort focused on testing biomarker transferability across cancer and treatment types. The resulting multimodal AI/radiomics model remains a prototype (Technology Readiness Level 3–4) and is not yet mature for clinical use. Nevertheless, the ability to leverage standard imaging modalities underscores the practical potential of this approach. We are actively pursuing external validation efforts across independent, multicenter cohorts to assess generalizability and refine model robustness. Future work will also aim to integrate these predictive tools into prospective clinical workflows, in coordination with automated lesion detection and radiomic harmonization strategies. Ultimately, scaling radiomic biomarker deployment will require alignment with real-world clinical needs, ongoing collaboration with industry and academic partners, and continued validation across diverse patient populations to advance the goal of precision oncology in routine practice.
