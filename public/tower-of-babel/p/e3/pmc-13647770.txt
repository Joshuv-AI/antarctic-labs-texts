A novel face-level blast mechanism: CT findings of neuro-ocular injury from a mass-casualty event
Authors: Christel Tamer, Diana Abou Ltaif, Taima Ghalayini, Omar El Sardouk, Joe Rizkallah, Farah Abou Zeid, Aya El Harake, Hassan Cherri, Alain Abi Ghanem, Roula Hourani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOn September 17, 2024, thousands of handheld communication devices detonated simultaneously across Lebanon, producing a mass-casualty event with an unprecedented close-proximity blast mechanism. Because the devices were held at face level, victims sustained highly localized craniofacial and hands, and to a lesser extent, neck injuries. This article aims to characterize the spectrum, severity, and distribution of intracranial, craniofacial, orbital, and neck injuries among victims presenting to a tertiary trauma center. To our knowledge, this study represents the first radiological characterization of neuro-ocular injury patterns resulting from synchronized handheld device detonations.MethodsThis retrospective study included all patients presenting to the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC) within 24 hours of the event who underwent head, neck, and/or orbital imaging. Patients without blast-related injuries or without neuro-ocular imaging were excluded. Computed Tomography (CT) findings were recorded using standardized neuro-ocular trauma categories.ResultsOf 177 patients presenting after the blast, 153 had blast-related injuries, and 103 underwent brain and orbit CT scan. Head injuries were highly prevalent (93.2%). Intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) occurred in 39.8%, most commonly hemorrhagic contusions (22.3%), and subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) (21.4%). Skull and facial fractures were present in 65%. Orbital injuries occurred in 82.5%, including orbital wall fractures (54.4%). Ocular trauma was severe, with globe rupture in 56.3% of patients (24.1% bilateral), penetrating globe injuries in 28.2%. Vitreous hemorrhage was the most common posterior segment injury (35.9%). Neck injuries occurred in 13.6% of patients, most commonly retained shrapnel and subcutaneous emphysema, with occasional extension into deep neck spaces and one case of laryngeal injury. Shrapnel was predominantly localized to the face (89.3%) and orbits (74.8%). Incidental intracranial findings were identified in approximately 1–2% of imaged patients.ConclusionsThe pager detonations produced an extraordinary concentration of craniofacial, orbital, and neck trauma, with rates of ICH, skull fractures, and globe rupture exceeding those reported in previous civilian mass-casualty events. The face-level, close-contact blast mechanism explains the intense neuro-ocular injury burden. Rapid CT imaging proved essential for identifying injury severity, guiding surgical intervention, and supporting coordinated ophthalmologic–neurosurgical management. These findings underscore the need for specialized preparedness in future unconventional blast scenarios.Keywords: Blast injuries, Computed tomography, Intracranial hemorrhage, Orbital injuries, Globe rupture, Neck injuries, Foreign bodies

Background

On 17 September 2024, Lebanon experienced a highly unusual mass-casualty event when thousands of defective handheld communication devices detonated almost simultaneously across the country. The synchronized on-person blasts produced more than 2,800 injured individuals nationwide, many of whom presented to emergency departments within minutes of the explosions [1]. The American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC), a major tertiary teaching hospital in Beirut, received a substantial proportion of these victims. Unlike large-scale stationary explosions such as the 2020 Beirut Port blast—which generated widespread polytrauma due to blast wind, shattered glass, and building collapse [2, 3]—the pager detonations represented a unique close-contact mechanism. Because the devices were held in hands or positioned in pockets near the torso and waist, injury patterns were profoundly localized. At the time of the incident, the pagers emitted a sound to encourage users to pick the devices up and lift them to their heads to read the message on the screen, and they detonated when the user pressed a button to clear or read the message.

Understanding these injuries requires revisiting the established framework of blast trauma, which categorizes damage into primary (overpressure), secondary (shrapnel and debris), tertiary (acceleration and blunt impact), and quaternary mechanisms (burns and chemical exposure). Each category carries distinct implications for neuro-ocular structures [4, 5]. In small, close-proximity detonations, primary and secondary mechanisms tend to dominate, producing a unique combination of neuro-ocular trauma [6–8].

Given the sudden influx of patients and the highly focal nature of the injuries, rapid and systematic imaging becomes essential. In mass-casualty settings, structured Computed Tomography (CT) pathways accelerate diagnosis, facilitate surgical prioritization, and support coordinated neurosurgical and ophthalmologic intervention [9, 10].

Prior civilian blast literature, ranging from terrorist bombings to industrial explosions, has documented intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), skull fractures, globe rupture, vitreoretinal injury, and retained craniofacial shrapnel as key features of blast-related neurotrauma [6, 11, 12]. However, no studies to date have described facial and neuro-ocular imaging findings resulting from synchronized handheld detonations occurring at face level, and existing literature rarely reports this extent of localized neuro-ocular injury.

In this context, this study aims to characterize the spectrum, severity, and distribution of neuro-ocular, craniofacial and neck injuries among victims presenting to AUBMC following the September 17 detonations. By establishing imaging patterns and injury correlates unique to handheld, on-person explosive devices, this work provides essential insights for radiological triage, acute clinical management, and preparedness planning for future unconventional blast events.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at AUBMC, a tertiary academic hospital in Beirut, Lebanon. The study reviewed imaging and clinical records of all patients who presented to the Emergency Department or outpatient trauma pathways within 24 hours of the September 17, 2024 pager detonations. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained, including a waiver of informed consent due to the emergent nature of the event.

Patient selection

We included all patients who presented to our institution within 24 hours of the incident and underwent head, neck or orbital imaging for suspected trauma. Patients with non–blast-related conditions were excluded from the analysis.

Data collection

Two trained research fellows independently reviewed electronic health records and the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). Epic was used to extract demographics, mechanism (device holder vs. in vicinity), and clinical disposition. PACS was used to extract radiologic findings from CT head, CT neck, CT maxillofacial, and CT orbits, as well as any supplemental imaging performed during initial evaluation.

Imaging interpretation and injury classification

For the purposes of this study, “neuro-ocular injury” was defined as the spectrum of intracranial, craniofacial, orbital, ocular, and related cervical injuries resulting from the face-level blast mechanism. These injuries were evaluated through the acute head, orbit, maxillofacial, and neck imaging workflow.

Statistical analysis

A unique identifier was assigned to each patient. All data were tabulated in Microsoft Excel and analyzed using descriptive statistics. Frequencies and percentages were used for categorical variables and mean or range values were used for continuous variables. The denominator for all neuro-ocular injury percentages was the 103 patients who underwent head and neck imaging.

Results

Patient demographics

Within the first 24 hours after the blast, 177 individuals presented to AUBMC. Of those, 24 were found to have conditions not directly related to the incident itself (e.g accidental trauma like an ankle dislocation sustained after being run over by a responding ambulance). The remaining 153 patients comprised the primary blast cohort. Within this group, 50 patients did not undergo head and neck imaging and were therefore excluded from the analysis. Consequently, the final study population for neuro-ocular assessment consisted of 103 patients (Table 1).

Head injuries

Head injuries were exceedingly common, affecting 96 patients (93.2%). In this cohort, ICH was observed in 41 patients (39.8%), with the most frequent subtype being hemorrhagic contusions (22.3%, Fig. 1), followed by SAH (21.4%), subdural hematoma (14.6%), and epidural hematoma (1%) (Fig. 2). Only one patient demonstrated severe ICH (1%), including subarachnoid, subdural, and intraparenchymal hemorrhage with associated contusions. Pneumocephalus was identified in 14 patients (13.6%), while brain edema was reported in 11 individuals (10.7%). Less common findings included one patient with an infarct (1%). Skeletal trauma was also prominent, with skull and facial fractures identified in 67 patients (65%) (Table 2). Subgaleal hematoma was detected in 58 patients (56.3%) (Table 2).

Orbital and ocular injuries

Orbital injuries were observed in 85 patients (82.5%), with periorbital hematomas (76.7%) and orbital wall fractures (54.4%) being the most common findings. Eyelid lacerations were present in 11 cases (10.7%).

Ocular globe injuries were severe and widespread; globe rupture was noted in 58 patients (56.3%), including 44 unilateral (75.9%) and 14 bilateral globe ruptures (24.1%, Fig. 3). Penetrating globe injuries occurred in 29 patients (28.2%, Fig. 4), almost all of which were unilateral (96.6%). Other ocular trauma include lens dislocation/subluxation 11 (10.7%, Fig. 5), corneal lacerations 5 (4.9%), scleral lacerations 7 (6.8%), anterior chamber injury with decreased anterior chamber depth (ACD) in 13 patients (12.6%, Fig. 6) and traumatic hyphema 3 (2.9%) (Table 2).

Posterior segment involvement was significant, with predominance of vitreous hemorrhage 37 (35.9%). Retinal hemorrhage or detachment were observed in 8 cases (7.8%). Less frequent findings included subretinal hemorrhage (1.9%), choroid detachment or effusion (1.9%), and optic nerve injury (2.9%) (Table 2).

Neck injuries

Neck trauma was seen in 14 patients (13.6%). The most common finding was retained shrapnel within the cervical soft tissues, observed in all cases. These fragments were predominantly located within the subcutaneous tissues of the anterior and lateral neck, with occasional extension into deeper neck spaces.

Subcutaneous emphysema was frequently observed (9.7%). In several cases, air dissected into deep neck spaces (4.9%), including the parapharyngeal and retropharyngeal spaces. Associated soft tissue injuries, including lacerations, edema, and fat stranding, were frequently present within the superficial cervical fascia and adjacent soft tissues (5.8%).

In one patient (1%), severe laryngeal injury was identified, including cricoid cartilage fracture and cricoarytenoid joint dislocation, accompanied by extensive soft tissue emphysema and airway compromise (Fig. 7).

Discussion

The September 17, 2024 pager explosions in Lebanon resulted in a disproportionately high rate of craniofacial and hands injuries. This distribution is attributable to the mechanism of injury, as most individuals were holding the devices in close proximity to their faces to read a message at the moment of detonation. The direct blast effect, combined with penetrating shrapnel, produced extensive craniofacial trauma, including ocular injuries, ICH, and skull and facial fractures. The clustering of injuries in this anatomical region highlights both the vulnerability of the head and neck to explosive mechanisms when protective barriers are absent, and the unique circumstances of the event, where the explosive source was positioned at face level.

Mechanistic insights and pathophysiology

The clustered pattern of neuro-ocular trauma in our cohort illustrates the interplay of multiple blast injury mechanisms. Primary blast effects, mediated by overpressure waves, can transmit through the skull and the globe, producing subtle but significant tissue deformation in the absence of external trauma [5]. Computational modeling studies have shown that rapid intraocular pressure spikes during blast events surpass thresholds known to cause globe rupture, providing a biomechanical explanation for the high incidence of ocular disruption in our cohort [6]. Similarly, skull flexure induced by blast waves can deform the cranial vault, serving as a mechanism for diffuse brain injury independent of penetrating fragments [7].

Secondary blast effects, particularly shrapnel and device fragments [5], likely accounting for the high rate of craniofacial and ocular trauma. Tertiary mechanisms, including blunt acceleration and body displacement, may further exacerbate traumatic brain injury (TBI) by amplifying shear forces within the brain and ocular structures [5]. Taken together, these mechanistic pathways provide a cohesive explanation for the combined intracranial, facial, and ocular injury spectrum witnessed.

Neurotrauma in blast settings

We identified ICH in 39.8% of patients, with SAH and hemorrhagic contusions representing the most frequent subtypes. This is consistent with reviews of blast-related head traumas, where a complex interplay of primary pressure waves, skull flexure, and secondary fragment penetration produce diffuse injury and hemorrhagic patterns [5, 8]. In our series, pneumocephalus (13.6%) and cerebral edema (10.7%) were common, reflecting this complex pathophysiology. Subgaleal hematomas were also frequent, reflecting extensive scalp and soft-tissue energy transfer associated with blast exposure.

Civilian blast case reports also illustrate comparable patterns, with documented ICH, skull fractures, and retained foreign bodies, underscoring the devastating potential of primary and secondary blast effects [13]. Broader reviews confirm that blast-related TBI differs fundamentally from conventional impact TBI, often producing widespread hemorrhage, edema, and long-term neurocognitive sequelae [14, 15].

The high rate of skull and facial fractures (65%) underscores the destructive energy transfer of the blast. Fractures not only mark high-energy trauma but also offer a route for intracranial bleeding and brain injury. The role of skull flexure from blast waves, where the blast waves cause minimal but significant deformation of the cranial vault, has been proposed as a mechanism of brain injury even in the absence of overt fragment penetration [7]. Prior mass-casualty explosions have reported cranial injuries in up to one third of survivors, with skull fractures representing a major cause of fatal injury [16, 17]. Imaging studies after the Beirut Port explosion demonstrated head and neck involvement, mainly due to blast wind and falling debris rather than close-proximity fragment impact [3]. In contrast, the markedly higher frequency of combined hemorrhage and skull/facial fractures in our cohort reflects the unique mechanism of handheld pager detonation in immediate proximity to the head and torso.

Ocular and orbital injury patterns

While trauma to the head, orbits, and hands represented the most frequent injury sites in our cohort, the present analysis specifically focuses on the neuro-ocular manifestations. Ocular injuries were strikingly severe, with globe rupture in 56.3% of patients, including a notable proportion of bilateral cases (24.1%). By comparison, reports following the Beirut Port explosion described open-globe injuries in 20.8% of eyes, predominantly due to shattered glass and debris [12]. The higher frequency of globe rupture in our series highlights the devastating impact of small, localized explosive devices carried at the level of the head. Penetrating globe injuries were also frequent, largely unilateral (96.6%), reflecting the role of directed shrapnel trajectories.

The mechanisms underlying globe rupture are well established: sudden intra-ocular pressure spikes or direct eyewall violation by foreign objects, conditions intrinsic in blast settings [18, 19]. Multiple reviews confirm that such injuries portend poor outcomes, particularly when accompanied by posterior segment involvement, such as vitreous hemorrhage, or poor visual acuity [20, 21]. In our cohort, globe rupture (56.3%) and posterior chamber hemorrhage (35.9%) represented the first and third most frequent ocular injuries, both associated with a high risk of unfavorable visual outcomes and worse prognosis.

In addition to direct transglobe penetration, secondary blast fragments may reach the intracranial compartment through orbital and skull base pathways. Potential routes include the superior orbital fissure or optic canal, as well as penetration through the orbital roof or superior orbital wall, particularly when high-velocity fragments are directed toward the orbital apex. This mechanism has been described in penetrating orbital trauma and may explain the close association between orbital foreign bodies, globe disruption, skull and facial fractures, pneumocephalus, and intracranial hemorrhage observed in our cohort [22].

Anterior chamber injuries such as decreased ACD and traumatic hyphema were also common and reflect direct globe disruption and internal structural damage. CT evaluation of ACD and lens position is considered a valuable adjunct in blast-related ocular trauma assessment [13].

Orbital wall fractures were frequent (54.4%), often co-occurring with ocular injuries, a rate substantially higher than that described in conventional orbital trauma literature [13]. Studies of maxillofacial trauma in war zones confirm that facial fractures, especially of the zygomatic and orbital bones, show high rates of ocular injuries including globe laceration, rupture, and hemorrhagic injuries [23]. Though blow-out fractures classically result from blunt orbital trauma, the presence in a blast setting suggests combined mechanisms: fragment impact, blunt energy transfer from blast wave, and secondary collisions [24]. This emphasizes that ocular and craniofacial injuries in blast victims are rarely isolated but rather part of a broader pattern of complex craniofacial trauma. The very high prevalence of periorbital hematomas further underscores the magnitude of soft-tissue injury accompanying orbital and ocular trauma in this cohort. The rate of eyelid lacerations detected on CT in our cases was low, likely because such injuries are more accurately diagnosed through clinical examination and CT imaging has limited sensitivity and may underestimate their presence.

Neck trauma

Although less frequent than cranial and orbital injuries, cervical trauma in our cohort demonstrated characteristic patterns consistent with penetrating blast mechanisms. Retained shrapnel within the neck soft tissues was observed in all patients with neck trauma, reflecting the predominance of secondary blast injury. Similar distributions of fragment-related injuries have been reported in wartime cohorts, where the head and neck sustain a disproportionate share of shrapnel wounds due to limited protective shielding [25].

Subcutaneous emphysema and air tracking along cervical fascial planes were common findings in several patients, and in some cases extended into the parapharyngeal and retropharyngeal spaces. These findings are well-recognized radiologic indicators of penetrating neck trauma and may suggest aerodigestive tract involvement or injury to deep cervical spaces [26, 27].

One patient demonstrated severe laryngeal injury with cricoid cartilage fracture and cricoarytenoid joint dislocation. In this case, the device reportedly detonated while the patient was raising the pager to inspect it, exploding at neck level before reaching eye level. This observation highlights the vulnerability of the laryngeal framework to high-energy penetrating trauma. A similar pattern of laryngeal trauma has been described in penetrating neck injuries, including cases of laryngeal cartilage fracture requiring urgent airway and multidisciplinary management to prevent compromise [28, 29].

Vascular injury

In our cohort, only one patient had ischemic injury to the brain. In particular, this patient presented with an acute infarction involving the body of the right caudate nucleus and the right internal capsule. CT angiography demonstrated a metallic shrapnel fragment located in the right subinsular region and extending into the right Sylvian fissure, where it was found to impinge upon the right middle cerebral artery (MCA) and its lenticulostriate branches (Fig. 9). Given that the body of the caudate nucleus is supplied by the lenticulostriate arteries, this vascular compromise likely explains the observed acute infarction.

Shrapnel distribution, mechanism, and implications

Our findings that shrapnel was most frequently localized to the face (89.3%), then orbits (74.8%), underscores the vulnerability of the craniofacial region. Similar observations have been reported in wartime cohorts, where the head and neck consistently sustain a disproportionate share of wounds in explosive and shrapnel trauma compared with posterior or more protected regions [24].

Open-globe injuries from the Port explosion were largely secondary to glass rather than direct fragment impact [12]. Imaging analyses from the same event further demonstrated that extremity injuries predominated, with comparatively fewer orbital cases [2]. Although hand injuries were also common in our cohort and were analyzed separately, this distribution contrasts with the Beirut Port blast, in which extremity injuries, particularly limb trauma from shattered glass predominated [3].

This unique clustering links mechanism to injury pattern: close-proximity detonation concentrated fragment trajectories toward the face and orbits, whereas larger stationary blasts typically disperse energy to the extremities and trunk [30]. The detonation mechanism in our event instead placed the face and orbits at the “front line” of fragment exposure. The result was an unusually high burden of facial, orbital, and ocular trauma, consistent with biomechanical models showing that blast-induced intraocular pressure spikes exceed rupture thresholds [6].

Radiologic insights

Radiologic evaluation was central in defining the breadth and severity of neuro-ocular trauma in our cohort. Rapid non-contrast CT served as the frontline imaging modality, enabling detection of globe rupture, intra-orbital foreign bodies, orbital wall fractures, ICH, and other pathologies. Imaging of orbital soft tissue trauma might further clarify the trauma pattern and degree of injury beyond clinical examination solely in the setting of orbital and ocular trauma [13]. The high prevalence of periorbital and subgaleal hematomas highlights the value of CT in characterizing both osseous and soft-tissue injury burden in blast victims. In penetrating neck trauma, CT enables detection of retained foreign bodies, soft-tissue disruption, and air tracking within cervical fascial planes, findings that help delineate injury trajectories and guide surgical management [25]. It also plays a central role in evaluating deep cervical spaces such as the parapharyngeal, retropharyngeal, and carotid compartments, where injuries may otherwise remain clinically occult [31].

An educational review recognized CT as the gold standard in orbital trauma settings [32]. In our experience, thin-section axial and multiplanar reconstructions were essential for detecting subtle globe disruptions that guided urgent ophthalmologic intervention. Fractures of the orbital rim and walls are most effectively evaluated through high-resolution axial CT, with spiral CT providing optimal results when using 1 mm slices and reconstructions [33].

Beyond simply detecting injuries, radiologic findings help differentiate blast-specific patterns from conventional blunt or penetrating trauma [34]. The simultaneous presence of extensive facial and orbital shrapnel burden alongside ICH and skull fractures is distinctive of blast mechanism. In mass-casualty settings, the speed and capacity of imaging become integral to triage, surgical planning, and allocation of resources [9]. Finally, radiologic protocols developed for blast and mass-casualty exposure highlight the value of structured imaging workflows and dedicated triage pathways [9, 10].

Clinical and public health implications

The scale of globe rupture, frequent bilateral involvement, and concomitant neurotrauma portends profound long-term disability. Compared with the Beirut Port blast cohort, in which 14.5% of injured eyes had a final visual acuity worse than 20/200 [12], our findings suggest a substantially higher burden of severe ocular injury. Although detailed ophthalmologic examination data were not available, visual outcomes can be approximated from imaging findings. Assuming that globe rupture results in complete vision loss, at least 35% of patients in this cohort would be expected to have vision loss. Evidence from open-globe injury literature indicated that outcomes are often poor even with prompt surgical repair [35].

From a neurotrauma standpoint, blast-related TBI also carries significant long-term morbidity. Case reports of severe blast TBI demonstrate the requirement for both neurosurgical and neurorehabilitation pathways [11]. In our series, the high rate of head and neck imaging abnormalities suggests that trauma systems must anticipate combined ocular–neuro injury patterns, rather than treating these entities in isolation. Therefore, effective preparedness requires integrated ophthalmology and neurosurgery trauma teams, efficient triaging, and long-term rehabilitation.

Incidental findings

Beyond the acute traumatic sequelae, a small subset of patients demonstrated incidental intracranial findings. These included benign lesions such as arachnoid cysts, meningiomas, and lipomas, with a prevalence of approximately 1–2% among those imaged. In our department, the radiologists made every effort to detect and report all findings, even the ones that were unrelated to the blast injury itself. Recognition of incidental abnormalities is important in disaster radiology literature, as they may carry clinical significance in long-term follow-up or influence decisions unrelated to the acute event [36].

Limitations and future directions

This study is retrospective and limited to patients undergoing imaging at a single center, introducing potential selection bias. Ophthalmologic examination data were incomplete in the acute setting, likely underestimating subtle anterior or posterior segment injuries. Clinical outcomes could not be reliably predicted from radiologic findings alone, as morbidity and mortality are influenced by additional factors including Glasgow Coma Scale, hemodynamic status, associated systemic injuries, and pre-existing comorbidities. Furthermore, temporal bone and otologic injuries, recognized manifestations of primary blast injury, were not systematically evaluated or recorded, and dedicated temporal bone CT examinations were not routinely performed, limiting assessment of blast-related auditory injuries. Moreover, detailed assessment of vascular injuries was not mentioned as it would have required CT angiography, which was not feasible during this mass-casualty event because of time constraints and the need to prioritize rapid evaluation and management of a large number of patients.

Finally, findings are specific to the unique mechanism of the pager blast and may not generalize to other blast contexts. Future prospective studies should incorporate standardized ocular and otologic assessments, dedicated temporal bone imaging, long-term functional outcome measures, and uniform imaging protocols to better characterize the full spectrum and clinical impact of blast-related injuries.

Conclusions

The September 17, 2024 pager explosions produced a distinctive pattern of severe neuro-ocular trauma characterized by high rates of ICH, skull and facial fractures, and globe rupture. This injury distribution reflects the unique face-level, close-proximity blast mechanism. CT imaging played a critical role in injury assessment and triage, highlighting the importance of specialized preparedness for future unconventional blast events.
