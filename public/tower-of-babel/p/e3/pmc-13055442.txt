The nociceptor primary cilium
Authors: Geoffrey K Ganter, Hope C Reveche, Lindsey A Fitzsimons, Eugen V Khomula, Jon D Levine, Kerry L Tucker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe primary cilium, a single microtubule-based organelle protruding from the surface of cells, uses intraflagellar transport to establish and maintain its structure and function. It is well-established to guide development and function of the nervous system through signalling pathways identified by expression of molecular markers such as ADP-ribosylation factor-like protein 13 (ARL13B) and type 3 adenylyl cyclase (AC3). Its dysregulation results in ciliopathies, clinical syndromes affecting a broad range of organ systems. Recently, it has been established in several species that nociceptors have a primary cilium, which regulates their excitability, contributing to acute and chronic pain. Since primary cilium-dependent signalling, such as Hedgehog (Hh) and Wingless (Wnt) pathways, have been implicated in nociceptor excitability and pain syndromes, interventions targeting this organelle may provide novel treatments for pain.

Primary cilium

Cilia, microscopic hair-like protrusions located on the plasma membrane of most vertebrate cell types,1 are classified as primary (non-motile) or motile. Motile cilia are responsible for directional movement of bodily fluids, while primary cilia, considered sensory antennae, are implicated in signalling pathways necessary for organismal development and cell function, acting as specializations for sensory transduction.2 While both types of cilia have a central axoneme, a microtubular structure anchored to the plasma membrane by a basal body,3 their unique compositions allow for their very different functions. Motile cilia, which have nine microtubule doublets surrounding a central doublet, the ‘9 + 2’ configuration, actively move mucus and fluid through conductive respiratory structures (e.g. nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, trachea and bronchi) and brain ventricles.4 In contrast, the primary cilium, of which there is only one per cell, lacking the central doublet, to give it a ‘9 + 0’ configuration, is non-motile.2 Anchored into the cytoplasm by a basal body, and separated from the cytoplasm by a transition zone that acts as both a soluble diffusion gate as well as a membrane diffusion barrier,5-7 both types use intraflagellar transport (IFT) to traffic proteins into and out of the cilium, to establish its structure and function.8 This anterograde and retrograde transport is carried out by motor proteins, kinesin-II and dynein-II, respectively,9 which interact with intraflagellar scaffolding protein complexes, IFT-B and IFT-A, respectively.10 IFT is responsible for the trafficking of proteins important for primary cilium-dependent signal transduction cascades, as exemplified by Hedgehog signalling1 (Fig. 1A).

In patients with ciliopathies, defects occur in genes encoding proteins that localize to the primary cilium or its basal body.11,12 While there are currently 247 known genes implicated in ciliary dysfunction, both primary and motile, it remains unclear as to how dysfunctional cilia affect the structure and function of individual organs, to generate observed clinical syndromes.13 For example, mutations in CEP290, a centrosomal protein, can result in five different clinical conditions: Bardet–Biedl syndrome, Joubert syndrome, Meckel syndrome, nephronophthisis and Senior–Loken syndrome. Factors including type of allele, overlapping ciliary protein function and timing of dysfunction during development contribute to elusive mechanisms by which discrete genotypes determine clinical phenotypes. Complicating matters, some ‘ciliary’ proteins can also localize to and function outside the cilium.14

Ciliopathies are further classified as first- or second order. First-order ciliopathies are associated with mutations in genes that encode for proteins that localize to the cilium, whereas second-order ciliopathies relate to changes in proteins that do not localize to the cilium but indirectly impact its function.12,13 The most common first-order ciliopathy, polycystic kidney disease (PKD), has been studied extensively and reproduced in animal models, such as the Oak Ridge Polycystic Kidney (Tg737Orpk) mouse. Like patients with PKD, mice with a mutation in the Ift88 (Intraflagellar transport 88 kDa) gene, which encodes Ift88, an IFT-B protein,15-17 have renal cysts and liver and pancreatic defects, as well as polydactyly. As a result of this mutation, dysfunctional IFT machinery fails to transport proteins into and out of the primary cilium, disrupting normal development.18,19

Neuronal primary cilium

The neuronal primary cilium, located on its cell body, subserves a variety of functions including regulating axon guidance during development, modulation of synaptic function and sensory transduction.20,21 It contains receptors and signalling cascades that are isolated from the contents of the cell body by a transition zone.20,22 The length, ultrastructure and orientation of the cilium vary depending upon the anatomical location of the neuron in the nervous system. For example, the cilium on cortical and hippocampal projection neurons are oriented toward the pial surface, while neurons in subcortical brain regions and in peripheral ganglia demonstrate an irregular orientation, with cilium length varying from 2 to 10 μm.23-27 Region-specific variation is also seen in the molecules expressed in the cilium, such as ADP-ribosylation factor-like protein 13 (ARL13B), adenylyl cyclase 3 (AC3), GPCR somatostatin receptor 3 (SSTR3), 5-hydroxytryptamine receptor 6 (HTR6) and melanin-concentrating hormone receptor (MCHR).20,25 For example, in hippocampal and cortical projection neurons, HTR6 plays a role in determining the length of the primary cilium.28-30 SSTR3 is localized to excitatory cortical neurons, with rare expression in inhibitory neurons.31 Of note, neuronal primary cilia can exhibit changes in length within minutes to hours of changes in their environment.21,32,33 Circadian rhythms and nutritional status, both resulting in exposure to various molecules such as fatty acids and hormones like insulin, also impact its length.26,34-36

Although they do not form traditional synapses, primary cilia extend into neuropil and contact axons, dendrites and glial processes.21,24 ‘Axo-ciliary’ synapses were recently discovered between brainstem serotonergic axons and the primary cilium of CA1 hippocampal pyramidal neurons, which play a role in chromatin remodelling.37 Using metabotropic receptor-dependent mechanisms, primary cilia regulate Sonic hedgehog (Shh) and cAMP signalling, impacting neuronal development, excitability and plasticity.38 In primary cilia, cAMP signalling is thought to involve the AC3 isoform, an established marker for neuronal primary cilia.39 While knowledge of the function of AC3 in neurons remains fragmentary, clinically it has been implicated in obesity and major depressive disorder, and is also thought to be important in olfaction.38 Understanding the spatiotemporal distribution of ciliary markers may help reveal how primary cilia are critical for development of and function in different cell types.

During development, axonal guidance relies heavily on the primary cilium.40-42 ARL13B contributes to the structure and integrity of the cilium as well as intracellular signalling pathways necessary for growth cone behaviour.42,43 Its deletion disrupts the formation of the polarized radial progenitor scaffold, leading to the inability of cilia to respond to extracellular signals, a loss of sensory (‘antennal’) function.43 Aberrant commissural axon projections and midline crossing were observed in mice with an ARL13B mutation; while axons travelled normally along the periphery of the spinal cord, these projections were observed to invade the motor column in a disorganized fashion.41 Mutations, such as those that encode proteins of the primary cilium and impact ARL13B localization to the primary cilium, result in ciliopathies such as Joubert syndrome, which is characterized by abnormalities in brain development.44,45 Furthermore, Dumoulin et al.40 were able to show cell-autonomous regulation of Ift88, specifically within embryonic spinal cord neurons, by demonstrating that long-range, primary cilium-dependent intracellular signalling is required for axon guidance. Their study also highlighted a role of Smoothened (Smo), a key Shh pathway signalling protein, as a component of the cilium, necessary for axon guidance.40 As seen in Joubert syndrome, because of the widespread involvement of the cilium in detecting extracellular signals and regulating intracellular pathways, failure of its normal function can lead to developmental, psychiatric and sensory disorders.46,47

Nociceptor primary cilium

Nociceptors, sensory neurons innervating skin, muscle, fascia, bone, joints and viscera, transduce noxious mechanical, thermal and chemical stimuli. They are sensitized by diverse molecules, cytokines, lipids and neuropeptides, acting at receptors on their peripheral terminals, to lower the activation threshold and amplify response to noxious stimuli,48-50 producing, respectively, allodynia and hyperalgesia. While acute nociceptor sensitization occurs as a protective response to tissue injury, sustained sensitization may occur in the setting of chronic pain.49

During embryonic development, shortly after elaborating axons, dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons display a primary cilium.51 While they are also likely present at postnatal stages, at least in some DRG neurons,51,52 until recently it was unclear as to whether they are expressed by and functional in adult nociceptors. A primary cilium on the nociceptor cell body has, however, now been established in several species (rat, mouse and Caenorhabditis elegans).23,53,54 Analysis of rat DRGs, using antibodies that label components of the primary cilium, provide support that a diversity of nociceptor subtypes, including IB4-positive, TrkA-positive and CGRP-positive sensory neurons, elaborate a primary cilium. Because the numerous satellite cells surrounding each DRG neuron are ciliated,23 definitive assignment of cilia to neurons proved difficult in situ.23 However, analysis in dissociated DRG neurons, plated at low density to produce clear separation between cells, has allowed for definitive identification of a primary cilium on more than 80% of small-diameter neurons23 (Fig. 1B and C).

In Drosophila it has been reported that only some cell types are ciliated, including type I sensory neurons.55 While previous studies failed to detect a primary cilium on dendritic arborization sensory neurons,56-58 using confocal microscopy we have recently provided evidence for a primary cilium on the type II class IV dendritic arborization neuron, a thermal and mechanical nociceptor.23 Furthermore, evidence suggesting that DmGucy2d, an IFT88 cargo, may be expressed in class I and class III dendritic arborization neurons59 supports the notion that sensory neurons, including nociceptors not previously characterized as ciliated, do in fact contain canonical ciliary components.

Primary cilium functions can be disrupted by targeted inactivation of genes encoding proteins essential for maintenance of its structure, such as Ift88,60-62 a critical component of the IFT-B complex.63 Using intrathecal administration of antisense or small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting Ift88, a role of the primary cilium in nociceptor function has recently been demonstrated.23 In this study, nociceptor primary cilia were found to control the pain threshold, as indicated by an elevated mechanical nociceptive threshold. Rats administered anti-Ift88 siRNA also showed attenuation of the hyperalgesia induced by prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), whose synthesis is the target of the clinically important non-steroidal anti-inflammatory analgesic drugs (NSAIDs), as well as in a preclinical model of paclitaxel chemotherapy-induced painful peripheral neuropathy. A similar alleviation of the pain associated with paclitaxel neuropathy was observed when another component of the IFT-B complex, Ift52,64 was targeted with siRNA.23

When patch clamp electrophysiology was performed on nociceptors cultured from Ift88 siRNA-treated rats, a large increase was observed in rheobase, the minimum intensity of a prolonged current stimulus required to excite a neuron, indicative of an increase in action potential threshold.23 This increase in rheobase is associated with a selective increase in peak outward current.23 To pursue a mechanistic explanation for these primary cilium-dependent nociceptor functions, a reasonable starting point would be to explore the specific ion channel responsible for the increase in outward current as well as the role of molecules concentrated in, or whose signalling is dependent on, the primary cilium, which have been established to be important for nociceptor function and in pain syndromes [e.g. Shh and Wingless (Wnt), see later].

Location and function of the nociceptor primary cilium

As the nociceptor primary cilium is located on its cell body, what remains to be established is how an organelle at this location impacts the transduction of noxious mechanical stimuli and its sensitization, effects occurring remotely at nociceptor peripheral terminals. A precedent for primary cilium-dependent long-range control of biological events at terminal processes, remote to its cell body, is afforded by studies examining embryonic axonal pathfinding. During development, axonal growth cones crossing the ventral midline of the spinal cord employ retrograde transport of midline-derived Shh to activate Hh signalling cascades at the primary cilium on the soma, hundreds of cell body diameters away.40 However, the timeframe of the modulation of nociceptive function seen following peripheral application of Shh and cyclopamine, a steroidal alkaloid that disrupts Hedgehog signalling, would seemingly preclude the involvement of such retrograde signalling in sensory transduction in nociceptors.23 This would not, however, rule out changes in nociceptor terminals secondary to antisense-induced changes in primary cilium function. Alternatively, Shh has been shown to enhance calcium oscillations in neurons.65 And calcium signalling can propagate rapidly along an axon66,67 to the soma and its primary cilium, a specialized calcium signalling organelle.68-70 It also remains to be determined if the nociceptor primary cilium contributes to the detection of other stimulus modalities, such as noxious heat and cold, and pain produced by chemicals such as capsaicin and serotonin.

Shh signalling in nociception

Shh signalling, which is strongly primary cilium dependent18,71,72 (Fig. 1A), plays a vital role in development and maintenance of the nervous system, disease progression and pain processing.73-75 Shh signalling comprises a variety of transmembrane proteins, transcription factors and diverse signalling molecules.75 Its canonical pathway begins with Shh, a morphogen that binds to and inactivates its primary receptors, Patched1 (Ptch1) and Patched2 (Ptch2).76 These receptors inhibit the activity of Smo, a seven-transmembrane helical protein required for Shh signalling, though itself not an Shh protein receptor.77 Inactivation of Ptch allows Smo to be translocated to the primary cilium, an essential step in this signalling pathway that distinguishes between vertebrates and many cells in invertebrates.78,79 Downstream cascades lead to the activation of Shh target genes by three Gli transcription factors,80,81 which exhibit a mix of activator and repressive effects: Gli1 acts mainly as an activator of transcription, Gli3 mainly as an inhibitor and Gli2 displays both functions.82,83

Recombinant Shh is pro-nociceptive in the rat when injected via intradermal or intra-ganglionic routes of administration, the latter is in the vicinity of the nociceptor primary cilium.23 This pro-nociceptive effect is markedly attenuated in rats treated intrathecally with siRNA targeting Ift88. Hedgehog signalling did not, however, impact the mechanical nociceptive threshold as cyclopamine, the prototypical inhibitor of Hh signalling, which both blocks Smo and attenuates cancer chemotherapy-induced neuropathic pain, does not affect the nociceptive threshold.23 In contrast, anti-Ift88 siRNA, which attenuates the nociceptor primary cilium, produces an elevation of the mechanical nociceptive threshold, supporting the presence of an Hh-independent primary cilium mechanism capable of regulating the nociceptive threshold. The potential importance of Shh signalling in pain can also be seen in various disorders, including opioid-induced hyperalgesia and cancer-related pain.

Opioid-induced hyperalgesia

Repeated opioid administration can produce hyperalgesia as well as analgesic tolerance, the net effect being enhanced pain and reduced pain relief.84 While the mechanism underlying opioid-induced hyperalgesia is incompletely understood, repeated exposure to the opioid analgesic morphine has been shown to increase the expression of Shh, Ptch1 and Gli1 in DRG.85 Additionally, cyclopamine reduces morphine-induced hyperalgesia and analgesic tolerance, mediated by brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF),85,86 which has a crucial role in regulating Shh signalling.87,88 Thus, inhibition of Shh signalling has the potential to alleviate opioid side effects and enhance its analgesia.

Cancer-associated pain

Pain management in oncology patients remains a major unmet medical need. As an example, pancreatic cancer, known for its poor prognosis, is associated with debilitating, difficult to treat, abdominal pain.89,90 Pancreatic tumour cells have been found to secrete Shh, which activates signalling in pancreatic stellate cells, upregulating nerve growth factor (NGF),91 which can sensitize nociceptors.92,93

Common types of cancer, such as prostate, breast and lung, which have a predilection to metastasize to bone, release mediators that can damage nerve fibres and produce hyperalgesia.94 In preclinical models of bone cancer pain, increased levels of Shh pathway components are found within the DRG and spinal cord, while cyclopamine, an inhibitor of Shh signalling reduces neuronal excitability and pain.95 Although these findings support a contribution of Shh signalling to bone cancer pain, its dependence on the primary cilium remains to be established.

Lessons from Drosophila Hedgehog in nociception

Drosophila has been used as a model organism for the study of Hedgehog (the Drosophila orthologue of Shh) signalling pathways in peripheral pain mechanisms. Hh signalling is necessary for injury-induced pain sensitization (allodynia and hyperalgesia) in Drosophila86 (Fig. 2). In addition, impairment of Hedgehog signalling by nociceptor-specific under expression of Smoothened or overexpression of Patched attenuates injury-induced allodynia.86 While several cell types in Drosophila do not appear to express a primary cilium, we have recently provided evidence that the Drosophila nociceptor has a primary cilium.23 In vertebrates, Hedgehog signalling has been established to be primary cilium dependent.18,72,96,97 And, while not universally dependent on the primary cilium, in Drosophila a cilium-dependent Hh pathway has been demonstrated in olfactory sensory neurons.98

In Drosophila, under expression of components in the Bone Morphogenetic Protein (BMP) pathway, downstream of Hedgehog, attenuates allodynia. For example, Decapentaplegic (BMP2/4),86 and its receptors, Thick Veins and Saxophone (TGFβ type 1) and Punt (TGFβ type II) are necessary for nociceptor sensitization, and the transducer, Mothers Against Decapentaplegic (SMAD), is phosphorylated upon injury.99 Another BMP, Glass Bottom Boat (BMP5/6/7/8), and its receptor Wishful Thinking (BMPR2), have likewise been implicated in injury-induced allodynia,100 as have the ligand-availability-regulating glypicans Dally and Dally-like Protein.101 The Brinker transcription control system,102 activated by BMP signalling, also plays a role in nociception. Of note, BMP signalling has been shown to affect calcium levels in nociceptor axon terminals.103 These studies of Shh signalling in the Drosophila nociceptor (Fig. 2) provide a road map for future studies of its role in mammalian nociceptors.

Wnt signalling in nociception

The primary cilium plays a complex role in signalling by Wingless/Wnt, a phylogenetically conserved family of glycolipoproteins. The IFT-A complex is necessary for canonical Wnt/β-catenin signalling,104,105 and a non-canonical Wnt-planar cell polarity (PCP) pathway regulates the location of the primary cilium on a cell although a divergent branch also impacts ciliary function.106 In neurons, the primary cilium influences the balance between canonical and non-canonical Wnt signalling.46 In a preclinical model of neuropathic pain, activation of Wnt/β-catenin has been detected in DRG and pain in this model is attenuated by Wnt/β-catenin pathway inhibitors.107-109 However, as for Shh, whether the contribution of Wnt signalling to pain is primary cilium dependent remains an open question.

Lessons from Drosophila Wnt in nociception

Wingless/Wnt signalling, implicated in mammalian models of neuropathic pain,107-109 is also emerging as important in Drosophila nociception. The central Wingless regulator Armadillo (β-catenin) has been shown to affect nociceptor sensitivity/pain threshold in that under expression was associated with hyposensitivity, while overexpression produced hypersensitivity.110 Furthermore, the Wnt receptor, Arrow (LRP5/6) and signalosome components Gilgamesh (casein kinase 1-γ) and Disheveled (Dvl), implicated in assembly of the primary cilium basal body,111 have pro-nociceptive properties (unpublished observations). Thus, studies of the role of Wingless/Wnt, as well as Hedgehog, in Drosophila nociception, provide blueprints for studies of their role in nociceptor function in vertebrates.

Other pain- and analgesia-related molecules in primary cilia

That the primary cilium is in a position to influence pain and analgesia is further supported by the localization to this organelle of other molecules strongly implicated in nociceptor function. For example, the adenylyl cyclase-cAMP signalling pathway, well-established to play a role in nociceptor sensitization by diverse pro-nociceptive inflammatory mediators including prostaglandins, whose synthesis by cyclooxygenase (COX) is the therapeutic target of NSAIDs, a class of analgesics used extensively for the treatment of both acute and chronic pain.112-114 However, while type 3 adenylyl cyclase (AC3), a well-established molecular marker for primary cilia,115-117 is found in nociceptors,118 along with AC1, AC5 and AC8,119-121 the role of this adenylyl cyclase isoform in nociceptor function remains to be established.

Mu-opioid receptor (MOR) agonists, the most important class of opioid analgesics, are widely used to treat moderate-to-severe pain.122,123 MOR is found on nociceptors,124-128 where it contributes to analgesia,126,129 by inhibiting adenylyl cyclase.130 While MORs have recently been demonstrated in the primary cilium of small-diameter mouse DRG neurons,53 the function of the nociceptor primary cilium MOR, and AC3, are not known. Other receptors involved in nociceptor function have also been found in primary cilia. For example, the EP4 prostaglandin131 and 5-HTR6 serotonin receptors, which have been found in primary cilia,37,132,133 are implicated in nociceptor sensitization and/or pain syndromes.134-138 While these molecules, critical for pain and analgesic mechanisms are found in nociceptors, where they exert pro- or anti-nociceptive effects, and are present in primary cilia, how their presence in this organelle on the nociceptor cell body contributes to nociceptor function, occurring at its peripheral (sensory transduction) or central (neurotransmission) terminals, in health and disease, remain to be addressed.

Clinical impact of the nociceptor primary cilium

While ciliopathies are well described in diverse clinical syndromes affecting the nervous system (Fig. 3), in patients with most of these syndromes there exists limited information regarding pain phenotypes. However, compatible with our recent preclinical observation that attenuation of the nociceptor primary cilium, using siRNA to target gene expression of intraflagellar transport protein Ift88, produces an elevation of the mechanical nociceptive threshold23 (Fig. 3), attenuated pain and enhanced pain tolerance have been reported in patients with Bardet–Biedl syndrome and their relatives.52 In agreement with these findings, a mouse model carrying mutations in Bbs1 and Bbs4, which are necessary for primary cilium function, produced impaired nociception.52 Furthermore, in Joubert and Meckel syndromes, ciliopathies caused by biallelic transmembrane protein 128 (TMEM128) variants, elevated pain tolerance has also been reported.139 Another important clinical phenotype of several ciliopathies (e.g. Bardert–Biedl and Joubert syndromes and mutations of CILK1), is the occurrence of epilepsy, and several antiepileptics, such as carbamazepine, gabapentin, topiramate and lacosamide, have provided a novel class of treatments for neuropathic pain. These preclinical and clinical findings, taken together, support the suggestion that attenuation of the function of the nociceptor primary cilium could be used as a novel treatment modality for pain syndromes that are dependent on activity in nociceptors. For example, since function of the primary cilium positively correlates with its length,32,140 interventions that shorten the nociceptor primary cilium might prove effective for the treatment of these pain syndromes.28,32,141 ,142
