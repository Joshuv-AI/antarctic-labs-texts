Engineered Nestin+ TSPC‐Derived Exosomes Promote Tendon Repair Via Metabolic Reprogramming
Authors: Linxiang Cheng, Junyu Guo, Junshu Zhang, Qiangqiang Zheng, Zhixiong Zhang, Jingyao Chen, Jinhan Zhang, Xiao Chen, Zi Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTendon repair remains a substantial clinical problem with limited treatment options. While Nestin+ tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs) are known to regulate tendon healing, their clinical translation faces challenges. Here, we show that exosomes derived from engineered Nestin‐overexpressing TSPCs (Nes‐EVs) significantly ameliorate tendinopathy in multiple mouse models. We isolated Nes‐EVs following lentiviral Nestin overexpression and demonstrated their capacity to reduce cellular senescence and promote tenogenic differentiation in vitro. In collagenase‐induced (WT and Nes‐CreERT2;iDTR) and naturally aged murine tendinopathy models, Nes‐EVs robustly restored motor function, improved tissue architecture, reduced senescence markers and restored collagen homeostasis. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling revealed a hallmark enrichment of the pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis pathway in both Nestin+ TSPCs and their exosomes. Mechanistically, Nes‐EVs enhanced mitochondrial function in tenocytes, increasing ATP production and diminishing oxidative stress thereby revitalizing cellular metabolism in damaged tendons. Our findings unveil a fundamental mechanism where Nes‐EVs coordinate tendon metabolism and homeostasis, offering a promising acellular therapeutic strategy for tendinopathy.

Introduction

Tendons are fibrous connective tissues connecting muscles to bones, serving as a vital component of the musculoskeletal system. Their primary function is to transmit mechanical loads, enabling normal bodily movement(Leong et al. 2020; Millar et al. 2021; Traweger et al. 2025). Tendinopathy is a common tendon disorder frequently occurring during intense or prolonged physical activity, accounting for over 50% of all sports‐related injuries(Yu et al. 2020). It is widely recognized that aging is a primary risk factor for the development of tendinopathy, which is characterized by pain, localized edema, compromised tissue integrity and functional impairment(Gehwolf et al. 2025; Kwan et al. 2023). Current management relies on conservative treatments and surgical intervention, yet their therapeutic efficacy remains unsatisfactory(Aicale et al. 2020). In recent years, novel strategies for tendinopathy, such as stem cell therapy are being explored, showing considerable promise for tendon regeneration(Yin et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2018a).

Nestin, a type IV intermediate filament protein enriched in stem cells across various tissues(Bernal and Arranz 2018; Lai et al. 2026; Wiese et al. 2004), exhibits significant antioxidant capacity and contributes to tissue repair processes(Chen et al. 2025a; Wang et al. 2019). Our previous work identified Nestin‐positive tendon stem/progenitor cells (Nestin+ TSPCs) as a potent tendinogenic population essential for tendon development and repair(Yin et al. 2016). These findings suggest that enhancing Nestin expression represents a promising therapeutic strategy for tendinopathy. However, the precise mechanisms through which Nestin+ TSPCs mediate tendon repair remain incompletely elucidated. Furthermore, the clinical translation of Nestin+ TSPC‐based therapies faces substantial challenges, including potential immune rejection, tumorigenicity risks and logistical complexities in cell processing.

Exosomes are extracellular vesicles with diameters ranging from 40 to 160 nanometres(Kalluri and LeBleu 2020). These vesicles function as key mediators that transport bioactive components—proteins, nucleic acids and metabolites including lipids(Keerthikumar et al. 2016; Lai et al. 2022; Pathan et al. 2019; van Balkom et al. 2015)—and facilitate intercellular communication(Cossetti et al. 2014). Their attractive biophysical properties, including excellent biocompatibility, stability, low immunogenicity and the ability to cross biological barriers, make them ideal therapeutic vehicles(Chen et al. 2025b; Jin et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2020). Furthermore, exosomes inherit specific biological characteristics from their parent cells, which confers inherent homing capabilities and enhances their therapeutic potential(You et al. 2023). These properties support their emerging applications either as innate therapeutic agents or as engineered drug delivery systems(Kalluri and LeBleu 2020; Zhang et al. 2024). Notably, mesenchymal stem cell‐derived extracellular vesicles (MSC‐EVs) have demonstrated significant therapeutic effects across various disease models, including pulmonary fibrosis(Li et al. 2025; Mizenko et al. 2024), liver disease(Lu et al. 2025; Luo et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2025) and cardiovascular disorders(Bellin et al. 2019). Based on these advantages, we propose that exosomes derived from engineered Nestin+ TSPCs represent a particularly promising cell‐free therapeutic strategy for tendinopathy.

In this study, we establish that Nestin is essential for tendon repair in mice and identify exosomes derived from engineered Nestin‐expressing cells (Nes‐EVs) as key mediators of this process. Using comprehensive in vitro models, naturally aged mice and collagenase‐induced tendinopathy in both wild‐type and Nes‐CreERT2;iDTR mice, we demonstrate that Nes‐EVs effectively attenuate cellular senescence and promote tendon regeneration. Mechanistically, Nes‐EVs enhance mitochondrial function in TSPCs while reducing oxidative stress. Our findings reveal a previously unrecognized pathway wherein Nestin+ TSPCs facilitate tendon repair through exosome‐mediated metabolic reprogramming, presenting a clinically translatable strategy for tendinopathy treatment.

Materials and Methods

Reagents and Antibodies

The following reagents and antibodies were used: Collagenase Type I (Gibco, 17100017); SA‐β‐Gal Assay Kit (Solarbio, G1073‐100T); CCK‐8 assay kit (Beyotime, C0037); ATP assay kit (Beyotime, S0026); Beyo3D Calcein/PI/Hoechst Cell Viability Assay Kit (Beyotime, C1375S). For immunofluorescence (IF): Nestin (Merck, N5413), COL1A1 (Abcam, ab34710), COL3 (Proteintech, 22734‐1‐AP), Lamin B1 (Abcam, ab16048), p21CIP1 (CST, 2947S), p16INK4a (Servicebio, GB111141‐100), γ‐H2AX (Abcam, ab81299), Tomm20 (Abcam, ab78547), TMRE (Beyotime, C2001S), mitoSOX (Beyotime, S0061S) and Dihydroethidium (DHE, Beyotime, S0063). For flow cytometry: DCFH‐DA (Beyotime, S0033S). For Western blot: Tsg101 (Proteintech, 28283‐1‐AP), Alix (Proteintech, 12422‐1‐AP), GM130 (Proteintech, 11308‐1‐AP), Calnexin (Proteintech, 66903‐1‐AP), MIC60 (Proteintech, 10179‐1‐AP), Cox IV (Proteintech, 11242‐1‐AP), Tomm20 (Abcam, ab78547) and Actin (Abcam, ab8226).

Cell Culture and Lentiviral Infections

Tendon‐derived stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs) were isolated from human tendon tissue. Briefly, minced tissue was digested in serum‐free, low‐glucose DMEM containing 0.2% collagenase type I at 37°C for 2–4 h. The digest was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min to pellet the cells (TDSC), which were then expanded using a low‐density seeding method to obtain a homogeneous TSPC population.

For lentiviral transduction, a CMV‐enhanced MCS‐3FLAG‐sv40‐puromycin construct (Shanghai Genechem) was used. One day prior to transduction, TSPCs were seeded in 6‐well plates at 1 × 105 cells/well. At 30%–50% confluence, the medium was replaced with 1000 µL fresh complete medium and lentivirus carrying the Nestin gene or control vector was added at the predetermined optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI). After 4 h, 1000 µL of fresh medium was added. The virus‐containing medium was replaced with fresh complete medium at 24 h post‐transduction. Transduction efficiency was assessed by fluorescence microscopy at 48–72 h and stably transduced cells were selected using puromycin.

Isolation of TSPC‐Exosomes

Exosomes were isolated from conditioned medium (CM) by differential ultracentrifugation according to established protocols(Fafián‐Labora et al. 2020). Briefly, 1 × 106 TSPCs were cultured in a 10 cm dish with exosome‐depleted serum medium for 3–4 days. The CM was collected and sequentially centrifuged at 2000 × g for 20 min to remove cells and debris, followed by filtration through a 0.2 µm filter. The filtrate was then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 1 h to remove larger vesicles and the resulting supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 80 min to pellet exosomes for the subsequent experiments.

Exosome Characterization

1) Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA): Purified exosomes were diluted in PBS and analyzed using a Flow NanoAnalyzer to determine particle size distribution and concentration. Data from three 60‐s measurements were averaged.

2) Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM): Exosomes were applied to a carbon‐coated copper grid for 10 min, negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid (pH 7.0) and air‐dried. Morphology was examined at 80–120 kV. Typical cup‐shaped vesicles of 40–160 nm in diameter were observed.

Western Blotting

Cells were lysed in SDS‐PAGE Sample Loading Buffer (Beyotime, P0015A) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, P0012). Proteins were denatured, separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with skim milk, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (Tsg101 1:1000; Alix 1:5000; GM130 1:5000; Calnexin 1:5000; MIC60 1:5000; Cox IV 1:5000; Tomm20 1:1000; Actin 1:1000) overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies. Blots were developed using a chemiluminescence detection kit (Fdbio science, FD8020).

QPCR Analysis

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol and reverse‐transcribed into cDNA. qPCR was performed on a CFX96 system (Bio‐Rad) using SYBR Green mix (Takara, RR420A). Gene expression levels were normalized to Gapdh. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

SA‐β‐Gal Staining

Cells were washed with PBS twice, fixed for 15 min at room temperature and incubated with β‐galactosidase staining solution overnight at 37°C in a dry incubator. Images were acquired the following day.

Cell Counting Kit‐8 Experiment

TSPCs were seeded in 96‐well plates at 2000 cells/well. To determine the optimal dosage, cells were treated with varying concentrations (0, 10, 20, 40 and 80 µg/mL) of Con‐EVs or Nes‐EVs for 4 and 7 days. For subsequent functional assays, cells were treated with a fixed concentration of 40 µg/mL in serum‐free medium for 48 h. CCK‐8 solution (10 µL) was added to each well and after 1 h of incubation, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured.

Live/Dead Staining

TSPCs were seeded and treated with 40 µg/mL of Con‐EVs or Nes‐EVs. At days 1, 4 and 7 post‐treatment, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with a staining solution containing Calcein‐AM, Propidium Iodide and Hoechst for 20 min at 37°C. The samples were then imaged using a confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000). The percentage of live cells (green fluorescence) was quantified using ImageJ software based on five random fields per group.

Scratch Test

TSPCs were seeded in 6‐well plates at 1.5 × 105 cells/well and grown to 90%–100% confluence. A uniform scratch was created using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. After washing twice using PBS, cells were treated with serum‐free medium containing Nes‐EV, Con‐EV, or an equal volume of PBS. Images were captured at 0 and 24 h. The migration rate was calculated as: [(Area at 0 h—Area at 24 h) / Area at 0 h] × 100%.

ATP Detection

TSPCs were seeded in 6‐well plates at 1.5 × 105 cells/well. After removing the medium, TSPCs were lysed with ATP lysis buffer and the lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was mixed with ATP detection working solution (100 µL) in a white opaque 96‐well plate and luminescence was measured using a luminometer. ATP concentration was calculated based on a standard curve.

Cellular Immunofluorescence Staining

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X‐100 and blocked with 1% BSA. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C (Nestin 1:500; Tomm20 1:500; COL1A1 1:200; COL3 1:1000; Lamin B1 1:1000; p21CIP1 1:400), followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000) and analyzed with ImageJ.

Mitochondrial Function Assays

1) Membrane Potential (TMRE): Cells were incubated with TMRE working solution at 37°C for 30 min. After washing with PBS, images were acquired immediately by confocal microscopy. Data are analyzed using ImageJ.

2) Mitochondrial Superoxide (MitoSOX): Cells were incubated with MitoSOX reagent for 30 min. After washing with PBS, images were acquired immediately by confocal microscopy. Data are analyzed using ImageJ.

3) Intracellular Total Superoxide (DHE): Cells were incubated with DHE working solution for 30 min. After washing with PBS, images were acquired immediately by confocal microscopy. Data are analyzed using ImageJ.

Flow Cytometry

Cells were incubated with DCFH‐DA working solution for 30 min, trypsinized and resuspended in PBS with 2% FBS. Analysis was performed on a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter).

Bulk RNA‐seq

For bulk RNA sequencing, RNA was extracted from Nestin+ TSPC and Control TSPC cells using Trizol reagent. After verifying the quality of the extracted RNA, it was reverse transcribed into a cDNA library for sequencing (Guangzhou OZ Biotech Co., Ltd.).

Metabolite Analysis

Metabolites from Nes‐EV and Con‐EV were extracted and analyzed by liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC‐MS) at OE Biotech (Shanghai). Samples were purified by solid‐phase extraction, separated on a UPLC HSS T3 column and analyzed using a Thermo Q‐Exactive mass spectrometer in both positive and negative ion modes with data‐dependent acquisition.

Mouse Models

1) Nes‐CreERT2;iDTR Mice: Nes‐CreERT2 mice (Shanghai Model Organisms Centre) were crossed with iDTR mice (gift from Dr. Wen‐biao Gan, Peking University Shenzhen Graduate School). To ablate Nestin+ cells, 12‐week‐old Nes‐CreERT2;iDTR mice received intraperitoneal tamoxifen (100 µg/g/day for 5 days) followed by diphtheria toxin (20 ng/g/day for 5 days).

2) Animal Experiments: All procedures were approved by the Zhejiang University Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee (ZJU20250812). For aged tendinopathy model, 20‐month‐old male C57BL/6J mice received weekly intratendinous injections of 10 µL PBS, or 10 µL PBS containing 5 µg of Con‐EVs or Nes‐EVs (based on total protein concentration), for 6 weeks (n = 5/group). For collagenase‐induced tendinopathy model (Nes‐CreERT2;iDTR mice and WT mice), 12‐week‐old male mice received a single intratendinous injection of 20 µL 2% collagenase type I. One week post‐injury, mice received weekly intratendinous injections of 10 µL PBS, or 10 µL PBS containing 5 µg of Con‐EVs or Nes‐EVs, 6 weeks (n = 5/group). Behavioural experiments were performed and mice were euthanized to collect Achilles tendons for subsequent analysis.

Histology and Immunofluorescence Assays

Tendon samples were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 48 h, followed by decalcification for one week. The tissues were then dehydrated, embedded in paraffin and serially sectioned at a thickness of 5 µm using a microtome. Sections were floated in a water bath, mounted onto glass slides and dried overnight at 60°C to ensure adhesion.

For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Subsequently, they were stained with hematoxylin for 10 min, briefly differentiated in 1% acetic acid and counterstained with eosin for 30 s. Finally, the sections were dehydrated through an ethanol series, cleared in xylene and mounted with a neutral medium. Histological scoring was performed on a scale of 0–18 according to established criteria(Chen et al. 2020).

For immunofluorescence staining, antigen retrieval was performed on heat‐fixed sections. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti‐COL1A1 (1:200), anti‐COL3 (1:500), anti‐p16INK4A (1:500), anti‐γ‐H2AX (1:250) and anti‐Lamin B1 (1:500). After washing, sections were incubated with fluorophore‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature, followed by counterstaining with DAPI to visualize nuclei. Stained sections were mounted and imaged using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Olympus FV3000, Japan).

Micro‐CT

Formalin‐fixed hindlimbs were scanned using a U‐CT‐XUHR system (50 kV, 0.17 mA, 75 ms exposure). Heterotopic ossification volume in the Achilles tendon was quantified using Imalytics Preclinical software.

Open Field Test

Following a 24‐h acclimation period in a quiet environment, mice were individually placed in the centre of an open field arena (45 × 45 × 45 cm). Their activity was recorded for 10 min and the total distance travelled was automatically quantified using ANY‐maze software.

Wire Hanging Test

After 24‐h acclimation, mice were individually placed on a standard metal wire (40 cm in length, 2 mm in diameter). The latency to fall was recorded. Each mouse underwent three independent trials and the average latency was calculated.

Treadmill Performance Test

Mice were acclimated for 24 h prior to the test. Exhaustion exercise was performed on a motorized treadmill (SANS Bio, SA101) set at a 5° incline with a speed acceleration of 2 m/min. An electrical stimulus (1.2 A) was applied to encourage running. The test was terminated when a mouse remained on the shocker plate for more than 10 s. The total running time, distance covered and maximum speed achieved were recorded.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (v.8.0). All data are presented as the mean ± SEM. For comparisons among multiple groups, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed. Detailed statistical parameters for each experiment are included in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Characterization of Nestin+estin+ TSPCs and Exosomes

Building upon our previous work identifying Nestin+ TSPCs as a pivotal population in tendon repair, we sought to further characterize their therapeutic potential. We established a stable Nestin‐overexpressing TSPC line via lentiviral transduction, which was confirmed by significantly elevated Nestin expression (Figure 1a). By utilizing this upstream genetic engineering approach, we bypassed the need for complex post‐isolation modifications while generating vesicles with robust regenerative potential. Transcriptomic profiling comparing Nestin+ and Nestin− TSPCs revealed 46 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with 28 upregulated and 18 downregulated in the Nestin+ population (Figure 1b, c). A volcano plot confirmed the successful enrichment of NES transcript, validating the transduction efficiency (Figure 1d, e). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis associated Nestin+ TSPCs with key biological processes, including cell adhesion, regulation of multicellular organismal process, regulation of cell population proliferation, regulation of substrate adhesion‐dependent cell spreading, cell population proliferation and animal organ morphogenesis, suggesting enhanced capacities for tissue organization and growth (Figure 1f). KEGG pathway analysis further indicated significant enrichment in pathways related to longevity regulating pathway—multiple species, pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis and signalling pathways regulating pluripotency of stem cells, consistent with a role in promoting regeneration (Figure 1g). To translate these findings into a cell‐free therapeutic strategy, we isolated and characterized exosomes from Nestin+ TSPCs (Nes‐EVs) and Nestin‐ TSPCs (Con‐EVs). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed that both Nes‐EVs and Con‐EVs exhibited the classic cup‐shaped morphology with a bilayered membrane (Figure 1h). Nanoparticle tracking analysis confirmed a typical exosome size distribution peaking at 60–100 nm (Figure 1i). Western blot analysis verified the presence of exosomal markers Tsg101 and Alix and the absence of the cellular contaminants GM130 (Golgi) and Calnexin (endoplasmic reticulum), confirming the purity of the exosome preparations (Figure 1j).

Nes‐EVs Promote Functional Repair of TSPCs in Vitro

To assess the therapeutic potential of exosomes derived from Nestin+ TSPCs (Nes‐EVs), we first investigated the dose‐response relationship of Con‐EVs and Nes‐EVs on TSPC proliferation. TSPCs were treated with a concentration gradient (0, 10, 20, 40 and 80 µg/mL) and cell viability was assessed at 4 and 7 days. Both groups exhibited dose‐dependent promotion of proliferation, reaching a saturation plateau at 40 µg/mL (Figure S1). Notably, Nes‐EVs demonstrated higher potency than Con‐EVs at the same concentrations. Based on these findings, a dose of 40 µg/mL was selected for all subsequent in vitro functional and mechanistic experiments. To ensure the biosafety of the selected exosome concentration, we evaluated the potential cytotoxicity of Nes‐EVs using Live/Dead staining. TSPCs treated with 40 µg/mL of Nes‐EVs maintained high cell viability at days 1, 4 and 7, which was comparable to the PBS and Con‐EV groups (Figure S2). The results confirmed that Nes‐EVs possess excellent biocompatibility and exert no cytotoxicity at the therapeutic dosage. Following this, we treated TSPCs isolated from tendinopathic tendons with PBS, Con‐EVs or Nes‐EVs. Nes‐EV treatment significantly reduced cellular senescence, as evidenced by decreased ratio of SA‐β‐gal staining (Figure 2a, b). Regarding SASP‐related transcripts, Nes‐EVs markedly suppressed the expression of IL1A, IL1B and IL6, whereas Con‐EVs did not. For CCL20, IL8 and MMP3, both exosome treatments led to significant reductions compared to PBS, with Nes‐EVs exerting a stronger inhibitory effect than Con‐EVs (Figure 2c–h). Evaluation of senescence markers revealed that both exosome treatments increased Lamin B1 and decreased p21CIP1 expression, indicative of attenuated senescence; however, Nes‐EVs elicited more pronounced effects than Con‐EVs (Figure 2i–l). In scratch wound assays, Nes‐EVs enhanced cell migration over 24 h compared to PBS, whereas Con‐EVs showed no significant improvement over PBS (Figure 2m, n). Similarly, CCK‐8 assays revealed that Nes‐EVs significantly promoted cell proliferation after 48 h of treatment (Figure 2o). All of the above experiments demonstrate that Nes‐EV alleviates senescence in pathogenic cells and promotes cell proliferation. We next evaluated tenogenic differentiation factors using qPCR. Nes‐EVs significantly upregulated the expression of key tenogenic genes—COL1A1, ERG1, MKX and SCX—compared to Con‐EVs (Figure 2p–s). Although TNMD expression did not differ significantly between Con‐EV and Nes‐EV groups, both treatments elevated TNMD levels relative to the PBS control (Figure 2t). Immunofluorescence staining further corroborated these findings. Nes‐EVs substantially increased Collagen I (COL1A1) expression relative to both PBS and Con‐EV treatments (Figure 2u, v). In contrast, while Con‐EVs did not significantly alter Collagen III (COL3) levels, Nes‐EVs led to a notable reduction—consistent with a restorative phenotype in tendinopathy (Figure 2w, x). In summary, although both Con‐EVs and Nes‐EVs enhanced TSPC proliferation, tenogenic differentiation and resistance to senescence, Nes‐EVs consistently demonstrated superior reparative efficacy in vitro.

Nes‐EVs Mediate the Reparative Function of Nestin+estin+ TSPCs in Tendon

To determine whether Nestin+ TSPCs promote tendon regeneration through exosome secretion, we employed Nes‐CreERT2;iDTR transgenic mice for inducible ablation of Nestin+ cells. As outlined in Figure 3a, Nestin+ cell depletion was achieved via 5‐day tamoxifen (TAM) administration followed by 5‐day diphtheria toxin (DT) treatment. Tendinopathy was then induced by intratendinous injection of 2% collagenase I. Beginning at the time of modelling, mice received weekly local injections of PBS or Nes‐EVs for six weeks, with analysis conducted one week after the final injection. Behavioural assessments confirmed that collagenase‐induced injury significantly compromised motor function, as indicated by reduced total distance travelled in the open field test, decreased latency to fall in the wire hang test and diminished running duration, maximum speed and distance covered in the treadmill test (Figure 3b–e). Notably, Nes‐EVs treatment effectively reversed these functional impairments and restored motor performance (Figure 3b–e). Micro‐CT (µCT) analysis revealed spontaneous ectopic ossification in Nes‐CreERT2;iDTR mice, highlighting the essential role of Nestin+ cells in maintaining tendon integrity (Figure 3f, g). Collagenase‐induced injury further exacerbated heterotopic bone formation, an effect that was significantly attenuated by Nes‐EV treatment (Figure 3f, g). Importantly, Nes‐EVs not only counteracted collagenase‐driven ossification but also exhibited a trend toward reducing ectopic bone below baseline levels. These findings strongly support the conclusion that exosome secretion constitutes a primary mechanism through which Nestin+ TSPCs promote tendon repair. Consistent with µCT findings, histological analysis indicated that Nestin+ cell ablation predisposed tendons to partial chondrogenic differentiation, while collagenase injury further disrupted tendon architecture in Nes‐CreERT2;iDTR mice. In contrast, Nes‐EV treatment promoted substantial structural restoration (Figure 3h, i). At the cellular level, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that collagenase‐induced injury in Nestin+ cell‐deficient mice resulted in pronounced cellular senescence, characterized by elevated p16INK4A and reduced Lamin B1 expression. These senescence markers were significantly ameliorated following Nes‐EV administration (Figure 3j–m). Further evaluation of collagen composition confirmed that injury reduced COL1A1 while increasing COL3 expression in Nes‐CreERT2;iDTR mice, alterations that were effectively reversed by Nes‐EV treatment (Figure 3n‐q). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Nestin+ cell depletion predisposes tendons to degeneration, which is exacerbated by collagenase‐induced injury. Nes‐EVs significantly mitigate these pathological changes, establishing exosome secretion as a key mechanism through which Nestin+ TSPCs mediate tendon repair.

Nes‐EVs Ameliorate Age‐Related Tendinopathy in a Natural Aging Mouse Model

We next evaluated the therapeutic potential of Nes‐EVs in a natural aging model of tendinopathy. Twenty‐month‐old mice received weekly local injections of PBS, Con‐EVs or Nes‐EVs for six weeks, with analysis performed one week after the final injection (Figure 4a). Behavioural assessments revealed that Con‐EVs did not significantly improve motor function compared to PBS across multiple parameters, including total distance travelled in the open field test, latency to fall in the wire hang test and running duration, maximum speed and distance in the treadmill test (Figure 4b–e). In contrast, Nes‐EVs produced significant functional recovery relative to PBS in all measured endpoints (Figure 4b–e). µCT imaging confirmed the presence of ectopic ossification in aged tendinopathic tendons (Figure 4f–g). Con‐EVs showed a non‐significant trend toward reducing heterotopic bone volume compared to PBS, whereas Nes‐EVs significantly attenuated pathological ossification (Figure 4f–g). Histological analysis of H&E‐stained sections indicated that Con‐EVs did not markedly improve tendon architecture relative to PBS. In contrast, Nes‐EVs promoted substantial restoration of tendon histology, demonstrating more robust reparative effects than Con‐EVs (Figure 4h, i). At the cellular level, immunofluorescence analysis of senescence markers showed that both Con‐EV and Nes‐EV treatments reduced p16INK4A and γ‐H2AX expression in aged tendons. Nes‐EVs elicited a more pronounced reduction in γ‐H2AX compared to Con‐EVs and similarly decreased p16INK4A levels, although the latter did not reach statistical significance (Figure 4j–m). Evaluation of collagen composition further demonstrated that Con‐EVs did not significantly alter COL1A1 or COL3 expression relative to PBS (Figure 4n–q). Nes‐EVs, however, markedly increased COL1A1 while reducing COL3 levels (Figure 4n–q). Together, these data indicate that Nes‐EVs significantly ameliorate age‐related degenerative tendinopathy, attenuating cellular senescence and promoting functional and structural restoration, whereas Con‐EVs exhibit limited therapeutic efficacy under the same conditions.

Nes‐EVs Promote Functional and Structural Recovery in Collagenase‐Induced Tendinopathy

We further evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of Nes‐EVs in an acute collagenase‐induced tendinopathy model. 12‐week‐old mice received a local injection of 2% type I collagenase followed by weekly treatments with PBS, Con‐EVs, or Nes‐EVs for six weeks, with analysis performed one week after the final injection (Figure 5a). Behavioural assessments demonstrated that Con‐EVs did not significantly improve motor function compared to PBS in the open field or wire hang tests, whereas Nes‐EVs significantly increased total distance travelled and prolonged hang time (Figure 5b–d). In the treadmill test, both Con‐EVs and Nes‐EVs enhanced running performance (duration, maximum speed and distance), with Nes‐EVs producing significantly greater improvement than Con‐EVs (Figure 5e). Micro‐CT imaging revealed ectopic ossification in injured tendons. Con‐EVs showed a non‐significant trend towards reducing ossification volume, while Nes‐EVs significantly attenuated heterotopic bone formation (Figure 5f, g). Histological analysis of H&E‐stained sections indicated that both Con‐EVs and Nes‐EVs improved tendon architecture relative to PBS, with Nes‐EVs promoting more substantial tissue restoration than Con‐EVs (Figure 5h, i). Immunofluorescence analysis of senescence markers showed that both exosome treatments significantly reduced p16INK4A levels, with Nes‐EVs exerting a stronger effect (Figure 5j, k). While Con‐EVs did not significantly alter Lamin B1 expression, Nes‐EVs markedly increased it, confirming enhanced anti‐senescence activity (Figure 5l, m). Evaluation of collagen composition revealed that Con‐EVs did not significantly elevate COL1A1 expression above PBS levels, whereas Nes‐EVs substantially enhanced it (Figure 5n, o). Both treatments reduced COL3 expression, with Nes‐EVs demonstrating a more pronounced decrease than Con‐EVs (Figure 5p, q). In summary, although Con‐EVs provide moderate reparative effects, Nes‐EVs exhibit superior efficacy in mitigating cellular senescence, restoring tendon matrix composition and promoting functional recovery in collagenase‐induced tendinopathy.

Metabolomic Profiling Reveals Nes‐EV‐Specific Enrichment in Energy Metabolism Pathways

To elucidate the metabolic basis of Nes‐EV bioactivity, we conducted untargeted metabolomic profiling comparing Nes‐EVs and Con‐EVs (Figure 6a). Analysis identified 56 significantly differentially abundant metabolites, with 44 upregulated and 12 downregulated in Nes‐EVs relative to Con‐EVs (Figure 6b–d). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these metabolites demonstrated significant overrepresentation of the ‘Pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis’ pathway (Figure 6e). This metabolic signature aligns with our initial transcriptomic findings in Nestin+ TSPCs (Figure 1g). Given the established function of pantothenate and CoA in cellular energy metabolism and ATP production, together with concurrent enrichment of amino acid metabolism and TCA cycle pathways, we propose that Nes‐EVs enhance tendon repair through potentiation of cellular energy metabolism and ATP generation.

Nes‐EVs Enhance Mitochondrial Biogenesis and Function While Attenuating Oxidative Stress

To test our hypothesis, we first evaluated mitochondrial content by Tomm20 immunofluorescence. Nes‐EV treatment significantly increased Tomm20 signal intensity compared to both PBS and Con‐EV groups, indicating enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis (Figure 7a, b). Western blot analysis further confirmed the upregulation of key mitochondrial proteins, including Tomm20, Cox IV and MIC60, in Nes‐EV‐treated cells relative to Con‐EV controls (Figure 7c). This expansion of mitochondrial mass was functionally coupled with a substantial increase in intracellular ATP production (Figure 7d). We next assessed mitochondrial quality by measuring membrane potential (ΔΨm) using TMRE staining. Both Con‐EV and Nes‐EV treatments elevated ΔΨm compared to PBS controls, yet Nes‐EVs generated a significantly stronger enhancement than Con‐EVs, indicating superior preservation of mitochondrial health (Figure 7e, f). Furthermore, Nes‐EVs demonstrated pronounced efficacy in counteracting oxidative stress. Mitochondrial superoxide levels, detected by MitoSOX Red fluorescence, were significantly reduced following Nes‐EV treatment compared to PBS controls, whereas Con‐EVs showed no statistically significant effect (Figure 7g, h). Consistent with this observation, cellular ROS levels were markedly attenuated by Nes‐EV administration, as verified through both DHE staining (Figure 7i, j) and DCFH‐DA‐based flow cytometric analysis (Figure 7k, l). In summary, Nes‐EVs enhance mitochondrial biogenesis and quality, leading to increased ATP production that meets cellular energy demands. Concurrently, they effectively reduce oxidative stress at both mitochondrial and cellular levels, thereby promoting metabolic homeostasis.

Discussion

The effective treatment of tendinopathy remains a significant challenge in orthopaedics and sports medicine(Millar et al. 2021). This study demonstrates, for the first time, that exosomes derived from Nestin‐overexpressing TSPCs (Nes‐EVs) function as potent acellular therapeutic agents. They significantly promote structural and functional repair in both collagenase‐induced acute injury and naturally aged chronic degeneration mouse models. The core mechanism involves the unique metabolite cargo of Nes‐EVs, which reshapes tendon cell metabolism by enhancing mitochondrial function, alleviating oxidative stress and reversing cellular senescence to promote regeneration. Consequently, this work reveals that the reparative function of Nestin+ TSPCs is mediated through their secreted exosomes and elucidates the underlying therapeutic mechanism at the metabolic level, opening new avenues for tendinopathy treatment.

Current tendinopathy management relies predominantly on conservative treatments(Cheng et al. 2024) including rest(Traweger et al. 2025), physical therapy (e.g., eccentric exercises)(Murphy et al. 2018), nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs(Irby et al. 2020) and extracorporeal shockwave therapy(Korakakis et al. 2018). When these approaches fail, more invasive interventions such as platelet‐rich plasma injections (Zhang et al. 2018b) local corticosteroid injections are employed, though their efficacy remains debated(Gross et al. 2013; Kongsgaard et al. 2009). Refractory cases often require surgical debridement and repair (Andres and Murrell 2008). Critically, these existing strategies focus on alleviating symptoms (e.g., pain and inflammation) or restoring macroscopic structure, but they fail to reverse the underlying biological degeneration or promote functional regeneration. This fundamental limitation results in high recurrence rates and suboptimal long‐term outcomes(Millar et al. 2021).

Cell‐based regenerative strategies, particularly stem cell therapies, represent a promising pathway for addressing the root pathology of tendinopathy(Yin et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2018a). Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), known for their robust self‐renewal and multipotent differentiation capabilities(Heo et al. 2016), can regenerate damaged tendon cells(Bi et al. 2007) and modulate the local microenvironment via paracrine signalling to promote repair(Lee et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024; Long et al. 2025). Preclinical investigations support the potential of stem cells in enhancing tendon healing and functional recovery(Goldberg et al. 2024). However, their clinical application faces challenges, including potential immune rejection, tumorigenic risks and complexities in cell storage and transportation(Yamanaka 2020). Furthermore, the instability of transplanted cell survival and homing efficiency adds uncertainty to therapeutic outcomes(Hu et al. 2023).

Recent research increasingly indicates that the therapeutic effects of stem cells are mediated primarily through paracrine mechanisms rather than direct differentiation(Trigo et al. 2025; Yuan et al. 2025). Exosomes, nanoscale extracellular vesicles carrying progenitor cell‐specific bioinformation (proteins, nucleic acids, metabolites), have thus emerged as a highly promising acellular therapeutic platform(Lotfy et al. 2023). They inherit the therapeutic potential of stem cells while offering distinct advantages: low immunogenicity, no tumorigenic risk, high stability and ease of storage and standardized production(You et al. 2023). Our data confirm that Nes‐EVs successfully retain the potent reparative functions of their parent Nestin+ TSPCs. In vitro, Nes‐EVs outperformed control exosomes (Con‐EVs) by more effectively mitigating cellular senescence, promoting TSPC proliferation and migration and upregulating key tenogenic markers (e.g., SCX, COL1A1). In vivo, Nes‐EVs significantly improved tendon tissue architecture, reduced heterotopic ossification and restored motor function in both acute injury and natural aging models. Notably, while Con‐EVs showed limited efficacy in the aging model, Nes‐EVs produced comprehensive therapeutic benefits. This suggests that exosomes derived from specific, highly efficient stem cell subpopulations may offer unique advantages for treating age‐related degenerative pathologies.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism of Nes‐EVs, we integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. Strikingly, both datasets consistently identified the ‘Pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis’ pathway as a key target. CoA is a crucial cofactor in cellular metabolism, playing a vital role in the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and energy production(Felix et al. 2021). We therefore hypothesized that Nes‐EVs act by reprogramming cellular metabolism. Supporting this, we found that Nes‐EVs significantly increased both mitochondrial quantity (upregulated Tomm20 expression) and quality (enhanced membrane potential) in tendon cells, concomitant with improved mitochondrial function (increased ATP production). Nes‐EVs also effectively alleviated oxidative stress at both the mitochondrial and cellular levels. These results suggest that Nes‐EVs reprogram the metabolic state of dysfunctional tendon cells, potentially by modulating acyl‐CoA synthetase‐related activities, shifting them from an energy‐depleted senescent state to an energy‐rich regenerative state. This metabolic shift provides a critical bioenergetic foundation for tendon repair.

Given the strict compartmentalization of cellular CoA, it is critical to consider how the cytosolic delivery of pantothenate and CoA by Nes‐EVs augments mitochondrial function. Mitochondria lack the enzymatic machinery for de novo CoA synthesis and must rely on active transport mechanisms. In the pathological microenvironment of tendinopathy, the tightly regulated, rate‐limited endogenous synthesis of CoA may struggle to meet the high bioenergetic demands of tissue repair. By directly delivering pantothenate and CoA, Nes‐EVs bypass these biosynthetic bottlenecks to rapidly supplement the cytosolic pools of TSPCs. This enriched cytosolic CoA is subsequently imported into the mitochondrial matrix via specific mitochondrial carriers, predominantly SLC25A42 and SLC25A16 (Fiermonte et al. 2009; Liu et al. 2025). This active import restores the mitochondrial CoA pool required to fuel central carbon metabolism, providing a biologically plausible mechanism for the observed recovery of mitochondrial function in Nes‐EV‐treated tenocytes.

Previous studies have established a correlation between tendon pathology and altered metabolic state(Cheng et al. 2024; Dai et al. 2020), accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction and accumulated oxidative stress(Kroemer et al. 2025; Shen et al. 2022). Our study provides the first mechanistic insight by linking Nestin+ TSPC‐derived exosomes to downstream pathways that regulate mitochondrial metabolism, offering a novel perspective on tendon regeneration. Although exosomes from generic MSCs have demonstrated therapeutic potential, our findings indicate that exosomes sourced from a key, highly regenerative stem cell subset (Nestin+ TSPCs) exhibit superior efficacy. This points the way toward the future development of ‘engineered’ or ‘customized’ exosome therapies.

In summary, this study confirms the remarkable therapeutic efficacy of Nes‐EVs for tendon pathologies and reveals their core mechanism: regulating cellular metabolism and counteracting senescence via the ‘pantothenate‐CoA‐mitochondria’ axis. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the biological mechanisms of tendon regeneration but, more importantly, establish a cell‐free therapeutic strategy with significant clinical translation potential. Future research will focus on identifying the key bioactive molecules within Nes‐EVs, exploring their integration with biomaterials for local sustained‐release systems and ultimately advancing this therapy toward clinical application.
