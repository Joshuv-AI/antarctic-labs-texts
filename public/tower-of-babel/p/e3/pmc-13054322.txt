A case of severe carotid blowout syndrome successfully treated with endovascular parent artery occlusion: Additional N-butyl cyanoacrylate embolization under flow control after incomplete coil embolization
Authors: Kazuki Shida, Hiroshi Matsuyama, Kohei Shibuya, Kenichi Morita, Shino Murai, Yoko Nakayama, Kimihiko Nakamura, Junki Fukumoto, Yusuke Sakata, Jun Takashima, Akihiko Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Carotid blowout syndrome (CBS) is a potentially fatal complication associated with surgery and irradiation. Carotid artery rupture can cause life-threatening hemorrhage. We report a case of massive hemoptysis due to the rupture of an infected pseudoaneurysm following cervical irradiation for Hodgkin’s lymphoma, which was successfully treated with embolization using coils and N-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA).Case Description: A 50-year-old man who had undergone surgery and chemoradiotherapy for Hodgkin’s lymphoma of the left neck 35 years earlier developed massive hemoptysis with transient cardiopulmonary arrest during hospitalization for a deep neck abscess. Cerebral angiography immediately after resuscitation revealed a pseudoaneurysm originating from the left common carotid artery (CCA) extending into the internal carotid artery; however, there was no evidence of contrast extravasation, leading to conservative management. However, the following day, he developed another episode of massive hemoptysis, resulting in shock. Cerebral angiography confirmed contrast extravasation from the CCA, prompting parent artery occlusion (PAO). Although 43 platinum coils were deployed, residual blood flow to the aneurysm persisted. Therefore, 50% NBCA was injected under flow control of the CCA to achieve hemostasis. No new neurological deficits were observed post-treatment.Conclusion: Achieving hemostasis is the highest priority in cases of massive hemorrhage from CCA rupture caused by CBS. After confirming the collateral blood flow from the contralateral intracranial circulation, immediate PAO should be performed. Additional NBCA embolization under parent artery flow control is a valuable option in cases where hemostasis is difficult to achieve using coil embolization alone.

INTRODUCTION

Carotid blowout syndrome (CBS) is a complication of head-and-neck cancer treatment that can lead to fatal outcomes due to carotid artery rupture.[9] CBS develops due to factors such as early or late radiation-induced damage, surgical trauma, or cancer invasion. In 80–90% of CBS cases, a history of radiation therapy is present, which manifests acutely with symptoms such as cervical swelling, bleeding from the skin, and pharyngeal hemorrhage.[10] Massive hemorrhage often leads to cardiopulmonary arrest (CPA), and the prognosis is extremely poor. Therefore, prompt diagnosis and therapeutic intervention are crucial. However, CBS may present with diverse symptoms depending on the rupture site, potentially leading to diagnostic delays. Treatment options for CBS include surgical ligation, parent artery occlusion (PAO), and covered stent placement. Historically, surgical ligation has been the mainstay of treatment;[12] however, it is associated with a high risk of neurological complications, infections, and coagulopathy.[4] At present, less invasive endovascular techniques are being used more frequently.[10] We report a case of an infected pseudoaneurysm that developed in the common carotid artery (CCA) 35 years after surgery and chemoradiotherapy for a cervical tumor. The patient experienced massive hemoptysis due to the rupture and was successfully treated with PAO, using coils and n-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA).

CASE REPORT

A 50-year-old man presented to our otolaryngology department with progressive cervical swelling, fever, sore throat, and dysphagia. Laboratory test findings revealed elevated levels of inflammatory markers, and computed tomography (CT) revealed an abscess in the left deep neck region [Figure 1a], leading to hospital admission.

(a) A 50-year-old male patient who underwent radiation therapy 35 years ago. A deep cervical abscess was observed at the previously irradiated site on the left side. Cervical computed tomography revealed an abscess (red arrowheads) near the left common carotid artery, with communication with the piriform sinus. (b) Laryngeal fiberoptic examination revealed purulent discharge from the left piriform sinus (black arrow).

Thirty-five years prior, he had undergone tumor resection and chemotherapy for Hodgkin’s lymphoma in the left cervical region. One year later, the patient underwent 110 Gy of radiation therapy, receiving 60 Gy from the upper edge of the first cervical vertebra to the middle level of the twelfth thoracic vertebra and 50 Gy from the lower edge of the skull to the upper edge of the fifth cervical vertebra. Consequently, the patient developed radiation-induced skin fibrosis and contractures in the left cervical region.

On admission, he was alert but had severe induration in the left cervical region with brownish skin discoloration. Cervical extension and left upper-limb elevation were also impeded. Mild hoarseness was noted, and copious sputum production was observed, some of which contained blood. Laryngoscopic examination revealed purulent discharge from the left piriform sinus [Figure 1b]. A neck CT revealed an abscess near the left CCA communicating with the piriform sinus [Figure 1a].

Laboratory findings revealed a white blood cell count of 12,930/mm3 and a C-reactive protein level of 23.69 mg/dL. The patient received intravenous fluids and meropenem. On the 4th day of hospitalization, the patient suddenly experienced massive hemoptysis and CPA lasting several tens of seconds. Immediate endotracheal intubation and intravenous epinephrine administration were performed, which led to the return of spontaneous circulation. His consciousness improved, and hemoptysis ceased spontaneously.

Cerebral angiography was performed to identify the source of bleeding, revealing a fusiform dilation suggestive of a pseudoaneurysm along the wall of the left CCA extending into the internal carotid artery (ICA) from the upper edge of the third to that of the fifth cervical vertebra, but without contrast extravasation [Figure 2a and b]. The Matas test confirmed good cross-flow through the anterior communicating artery [Figure 2c]. Although a pseudoaneurysm in the left CCA was suspected to be the source of bleeding, spontaneous hemostasis had already occurred. Given the lack of definitive evidence that the hemoptysis originated from the pseudoaneurysm in the left CCA, considering the risks of PAO in the ICA, we opted for conservative management with continued intubation, close observation in the intensive care unit, and serial imaging to monitor changes in the pseudoaneurysm.

(a and b) During the initial episode of massive hemorrhage, cerebral angiography revealed a fusiform dilation (red arrow) in the wall of the left common carotid artery, suggestive of a pseudoaneurysm; however, no contrast extravasation was observed. (c) The Matas test demonstrated good cross-flow through the anterior communicating artery. (d) During the second hemorrhagic episode, cerebral angiography confirmed contrast extravasation from a pseudoaneurysm in the left common carotid artery. (e) After coil embolization, angiography performed without deflating the OPTIMO balloon showed continued extravasation from the aneurysm but no antegrade flow into the internal carotid artery. (f) When 50% N-butyl cyanoacrylate was injected, the lesion progressed into a pseudoaneurysm (red arrow), achieving complete occlusion. (g) Postoperative computed tomography (CT) revealed no new abnormalities, such as cerebral infarction. (h) CT angiography confirmed the complete occlusion of the common carotid artery.

However, the following day, the patient experienced another episode of massive hemoptysis and transient CPA. Despite extensive gauze packing in the oral cavity and manual compression, hemostasis could not be achieved, necessitating repeated cerebral angiography while maintaining manual compression.

Cerebral angiography confirmed contrast extravasation from a pseudoaneurysm in the left CCA [Figure 2d]. Given the previously confirmed good crossflow from the contralateral ICA, we decided to proceed with an immediate PAO of the left CCA. An 8Fr OPTIMO balloon-guiding catheter (Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) was placed in the CCA and inflated to achieve a flow control. Coiling was initiated from the normal segment distal to the pseudoaneurysm in the cervical ICA, followed by coiling of the external and common carotid arteries.

After deploying 36 platinum coils, we deflated the OPTIMO balloon and performed angiographic evaluation; however, complete hemostasis was not achieved. Seven additional coils were placed, resulting in a total of 43 coils. However, even when the balloon was inflated, slight contrast extravasation persisted from the pseudoaneurysm, although antegrade blood flow into the ICA was not observed [Figure 2e]. Given the high-flow shunt nature of the lesion and fragile vascular wall of the pseudoaneurysm, we determined that coiling alone was insufficient to achieve complete hemostasis.

Therefore, we decided to use a liquid embolic material. Under balloon flow control, we carefully injected 0.33 mL of 50% NBCA (Braun Aesculap Japan Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) while preventing influx into the ICA and successfully achieved complete occlusion of the pseudoaneurysm [Figure 2f]. Final angiography of the right ICA confirmed good blood flow to both the middle and anterior cerebral arteries, which allowed us to complete the procedure.

A postoperative head CT revealed no evidence of intracranial hemorrhage or ischemic stroke, and no new neurological deficits were observed upon awakening [Figure 2g]. Postoperative CT angiography confirmed complete occlusion of the left CCA [Figure 2h].

DISCUSSION

We encountered a case of life-threatening hemorrhage due to the rupture of an infected pseudoaneurysm of the CCA after neck irradiation, which was successfully treated with PAO using coils and high-concentration NBCA. Endovascular therapy is the standard treatment for CBS, and coil embolization is commonly performed when PAOs are performed. However, because the CCA is a high-flow vessel, and pseudoaneurysms lack a true arterial wall, complete occlusion with coils alone is sometimes insufficient.[6] Furthermore, in cases of consumptive coagulopathy, the thrombogenic effects of the coils can be attenuated. In such situations, the adjunctive use of high-concentration NBCA under flow control can enhance the hemostatic efficacy.

NBCA is a medical adhesive that is widely used worldwide for hemostasis in skin wounds and vascular embolization. When injected into a vessel, NBCA polymerizes in response to the anions in the blood, forming a cast or thrombus that occludes the vessel.[11] In neurosurgery, NBCA is commonly used to treat arteriovenous malformations. However, inadvertent embolization of normal intracranial vessels may lead to cerebral infarction and neurological deficits.

To prevent distal embolization, we inflated a balloon proximally in the CCA to achieve flow control and injected 50% NBCA. NBCA was propagated only toward the bleeding site without extending to the distal vessels, successfully achieving hemostasis.

Similar studies have been published. Kolu and Yıldırım reported that inflating a balloon proximally in the CCA and injecting 10% NBCA could reduce the risk of distal embolization.[8] Similarly, Hanyu et al. reported on PAO for giant ICA aneurysms using coils in combination with 25% NBCA under flow control.[7]

In the present study, a higher concentration of NBCA was used compared to previous reports. A higher concentration of NBCA has the advantage of remaining in lesions located close to the catheter because of its reduced propagation speed. However, there is also a risk that it may solidify before reaching the target lesion and may be ineffective. Our therapeutic experience, together with these reports, strongly suggests that adding NBCA to coil embolization may be a valuable option for PAO in high-flow vessels.

Endovascular approaches can be broadly divided into PAO, which sacrifices antegrade intracranial blood flow, and covered stents, which preserve antegrade blood flow. Although PAO is less invasive than surgical ligation, its successful treatment requires sufficient collateral circulation to prevent ischemic complications.

When considering PAO, preoperative cerebral angiography should be performed, and intracranial hemodynamic status should be evaluated using a balloon occlusion test (BOT). The BOT allows for the evaluation of ischemic tolerance, and if sufficient collateral circulation is confirmed, PAO can generally be performed safely. However, the sensitivity of the BOT is approximately 85%, and false-negative results may occur; thus, careful interpretation is required.[5]

When the ischemic tolerance is insufficient, a covered stent may preserve intracranial perfusion. Covered stents offer the advantage of maintaining the parent artery and cerebral blood flow, distinguishing them from PAO and ligation. However, in cases of rupture, covered stents may not provide sufficient hemostasis.[3] Additionally, some reports indicated higher mortality (12% vs. 3%) and rebleeding rates (34% vs. 17%) with covered stents than with PAO.[1]

In emergency situations requiring definitive hemostasis and resuscitation, PAO may be unavoidable. In cases of uncontrollable active bleeding, PAO must be performed promptly, even in the absence of collateral circulation, to prevent exsanguination.

This case was successfully managed in the acute phase. However, several problems, in addition to concerns regarding collateral circulation, were involved in the selection of PAO as a treatment for CBS.

First, the timing of the intervention. CBS is classified into three subtypes according to severity: threatened, upcoming, and acute CBS.[10] Acute CBS is defined as an uncontrollable hemorrhage due to complete arterial rupture, in which immediate resuscitation and stabilization are necessary before definitive treatment can be initiated.

Upcoming CBS was defined as a state of impending rupture with brief episodes of bleeding that may stop spontaneously or with minimal interventions.

Threatened CBS is a condition in which the carotid artery is exposed to the external environment. Although a rupture has not yet occurred, it is highly likely.

The more severe the subtype, the more urgent the need for intervention. Emergency PAO may be unavoidable in life-threatening situations. In our case, the initial bleeding episode was consistent with an upcoming CBS and rapidly progressed to acute CBS. Intervention during the early phase of minor bleeding may improve prognosis and reduce complications,[10] suggesting that earlier treatment may have been preferable in this case.

CBS can present with various bleeding symptoms depending on the rupture site, including epistaxis, cutaneous bleeding, and oropharyngeal hemorrhage. These diverse presentations may not be initially recognized as indicative of major vascular injury, potentially resulting in a delayed diagnosis. Clinicians must remain vigilant for such early warning signs, particularly in patients at high risk of CBS.

Second, the risk of infection must be considered. Coil embolization involves the placement of foreign material into an infected wound, which carries the risk of persistent infection in cases of infected pseudoaneurysms. Indeed, there have been reports of cerebral abscess formation following such treatments.[2] Both surgical ligation and endovascular treatments involving foreign material carry an inherent risk of infection, and this issue remains controversial. In the present case, surgical treatment was not feasible in our case because of radiation-induced fibrosis and skin rigidity. Moreover, active bleeding persisted, necessitating urgent hemostasis; therefore, endovascular therapy was selected. Nevertheless, close long-term follow-up is essential. The patient showed no signs of infection recurrence or rebleeding for 6 months.

Thirdly, irradiation and the extent of embolization should be considered. Radiotherapy can cause long-term fragility of the vascular wall, necessitating careful consideration of both the indications and extent of PAO. As pseudoaneurysms lack a normal vascular wall, tight coil packing may be impossible; therefore, we included angiographically normal-appearing segments in the embolization zone. Furthermore, evidence regarding CBS occurring many years after radiation therapy remains limited, which may introduce uncertainty into treatment strategies.

In our patient, the underlying disease was controlled, allowing for long-term observation, which may provide important insights into the management challenges.

CONCLUSION

We reported the case of a patient with massive hemoptysis due to CBS, who was successfully treated with PAO of the CCA using coil embolization, combined with high-concentration NBCA. CBS presents with diverse symptoms and may occasionally cause massive hemoptysis, potentially resulting in death before a definitive diagnosis can be established. Therefore, early diagnosis and recognition are critical for improving patient outcomes. When PAO with coil embolization alone is insufficient, NBCA embolization under balloon-controlled flow is an effective alternative. Given the limited number of cases at a single institution is limited, additional accumulation of cases and multicenter collaborative studies are warranted to determine optimal treatment strategies for CBS.
