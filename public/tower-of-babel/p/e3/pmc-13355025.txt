CD8+CD38+ T cells identify functional high-risk multiple myeloma after autologous transplant: BMT CTN 0702 correlates∗
Authors: Nezar Mehanna, Andriy Derkach, Billel Gasmi, Theodora Anagnostou, Heather J Landau, Oscar B Lahoud, Michael Scordo, Gunjan L Shah, Hani Hassoun, Kylee Maclachlan, Malin Hultcrantz, Neha Korde, Sham Mailankody, Urvi A Shah, Carlyn R Tan, Saad Z Usmani, Sergio A Giralt, Alexander M Lesokhin, David J Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•PD1+ bone marrow T cells portend ER independent of cytogenetic risk, maintenance therapy, or treatment response.•Circulating CD38-expressing CD8 T cells segregate ER from prolonged remission with >80% area under the curve in a risk-stratification model.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) treatment has improved significantly over the past decades, but patients with early relapse (ER) or high-risk (HR) disease, defined by plasma cell cytogenetic aberrations identified by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), remain a therapeutic challenge.1,2 Currently, induction therapy followed by autologous stem cell transplant (ASCT) and maintenance is the usual treatment approach.3 Lenalidomide maintenance is used for patients with standard-risk disease, whereas a proteasome inhibitor in combination with lenalidomide is used for patients with HR disease defined by cytogenetics.4, 5, 6, 7 Evolving paradigms are also incorporating anti-CD38 therapies into the maintenance setting.8 Although some patients achieve long-term remission (LR) with extended disease-free intervals after ASCT, a subset of patients experience ER, defined by disease recurrence within 24 months of initial treatment, with shorter overall survival. Notably, this ER group is not limited to those with conventional HR cytogenetic features assessed by FISH,9,10 representing a functionally HR (FHR) population for which biomarkers of risk are not well defined. The early identification of FHR patients could inform treatment decisions to improve outcomes and represents an unmet medical need.

With the advent of immunotherapy for MM, including bispecific antibodies and engineered T cells, mounting evidence for the central role of the immune tumor microenvironment (TME) in MM progression has spurred efforts to identify immune parameters for predicting outcomes.11 Decreased naïve CD4 and CD8 populations, loss of effector CD4 subsets, and increased programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (TIM-3) expression by circulating T cells are associated with progressive disease.12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 However, validated and predictive immune biomarkers to identify immune HR (HR-I) patients among the FHR group have not been established.

Defining HR-I biomarkers of FHR MM would enhance risk stratification for predicting ER and identify potential targets for intervention to refine maintenance strategies. In this study, we first evaluated an exploratory cohort of patients using bone marrow (BM) immunohistochemistry (IHC; IHC cohort) on day +100 samples. These initial findings informed the design of subsequent clinically comparable but distinct validation cohorts (Blood and Marrow Transplant Clinical Trials Network [BMT CTN] and Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center [MSKCC] cohorts, respectively), in which peripheral blood collected at day +100 after ASCT was analyzed by high-dimensional flow cytometry to identify immune signatures of early progression. Using samples enriched for ER and LR patients, we performed multivariate logistic regression, controlling for HR cytogenetics, treatment response, minimal residual disease (MRD), and age, to identify T-cell profiles segregating clinical outcomes.

Methods

Patients

This study evaluated 3 cohorts of patients with MM. The first cohort (IHC cohort) included individuals who had their first ASCT at MSKCC between 2001 and 2013, along with a post-ASCT day +100 BM biopsy sample available for analysis. Baseline patient characteristics and clinical variables are summarized in supplemental Table 1.

The second cohort (BMT CTN cohort) included individuals treated from 2010 to 2013 on BMT CTN 0702 study, in which patients were randomly assigned to post-ASCT consolidation with either tandem ASCT, 2 cycles of RVd (Revlimid, Velcade, and dexamethasone), or no consolidation followed by lenalidomide maintenance.18 The control group received lenalidomide alone. Peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples were collected at post-ASCT day +100 for patients receiving a single transplant or at day +100 from the second ASCT for patients receiving tandem ASCT. Patients were grouped into ER, defined as disease recurrence within 2 years of transplant, or LR, defined as >4 years of progression-free survival (PFS). Additional clinical variables included HR cytogenetics (t(4;14), t(14;16), del17p), consolidation or maintenance treatment, premaintenance MRD, response status before ASCT, and post-ASCT response status. Baseline patient characteristics and clinical variables are summarized in supplemental Table 2.

The third cohort (MSKCC cohort) included patients with serial PBMC samples from diagnosis, pre-ASCT, and post-ASCT day +100 collected between 2015 and 2019 at MSKCC. Patient and clinical variables are summarized in supplemental Table 3. The study was approved by the institutional review board of MSKCC.

IHC

IHC was performed in formalin-fixed, decalcified, paraffin-embedded sequential 4 μm cuts of archived BM specimens using an anti-human CD3 monoclonal antibody (mAb) (Dako; clone F7.2.38) and an anti-human PD-1 mAb (Cell Marque; catalog no. 315M-95). CD3 and PD-1 IHC stains were graded as negative (<5% for CD3 and < 1% for PD-1) or positive (≥5% for CD3 and ≥1% for PD-1). Paraffin-embedded tissue was obtained from the MSKCC tissue registry.

PBMC sample collection

For the BMT CTN cohort, PBMC samples were collected, processed, and cryopreserved per BMT CTN 0702 protocol guidelines and stored in the BMT CTN biorepository. After approval of a research proposal (principal investigator, D.J.C.; co-principal investigator, A.M.L.) by the BMT CTN executive committee, these samples were shipped to MSKCC and stored in liquid nitrogen.

For the MSKCC cohort, peripheral blood was processed within 24 hours of collection. Samples were centrifuged over Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare), the serum was decanted, and white blood cells were separated by pipette from the underlying red blood cell layer. Separated white blood cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline, resuspended in fetal bovine serum with 10% dimethyl sulfoxide, cryopreserved by controlled-rate freezing, and stored in liquid nitrogen for future analysis.

Antibodies for flow cytometry and IHC

Antibodies were stored at −4°C and used according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The BMT CTN and MSKCC cohorts were stained with an identical 28-marker panel. A complete list of antibodies, fluorochromes, and clones is available in supplemental Table 4.

Flow cytometry acquisition

Flow cytometry was performed on batch-thawed PBMCs. Samples were stained with a 28-color panel inclusive of cell lineage and activation markers. Cells were fixed and permeabilized with the FoxP3/Ki-67 fixation/permeabilization concentrate and diluent (eBioscience) for intracellular staining with antibodies to LAG-3, Ki-67, FoxP3, CTLA-4, T-bet, and granzyme B, in the presence of brilliant stain buffer plus (BD Biosciences). Before and after staining cells were washed with 1% bovine serum albumin in phosphate-buffered saline and spun at 1500g for 2 minutes. Stained cells were acquired on a BD Biosciences FACSymphony.

High-dimensional cytometry analysis

FCS 3.0 files were imported and analyzed with R (4.2.1) using the packages flowWorkspace, ggcyto, and flowCore. Files were compensated, arcsinh transformed, and gated on live, singlet, CD3+ lymphocytes. All files were merged, and unsupervised clustering was performed with the FlowSOM algorithm using tools and functions within the CATALYST package.19 Clustering was performed without down-sampling; the x and y dimensions were set at 10, with a maximum of 25 clusters. We selected 10 flow markers of T-cell lineage (cell type) and 15 markers of activation status (cell state). Expression of CD3, live/dead, CD14, and CD19 was not considered for downstream analysis. Dimensionality reduction visualization was performed using uniform manifold approximation and projection, with 500 events from each file randomly selected for visualization. The original 25 cell clusters were manually merged based on expert opinion of surface expression profiles into 19 separate T-cell subsets. The original cluster annotation is displayed in supplemental Figure 1.

Statistical analysis

For the BMT CTN cohort, clinical and patient characteristics were compared between post-ASCT relapse status by χ2 test. For comparison of IHC staining positivity with PFS, the Cox proportional hazards model was used.

To detect differences in cluster abundance and cluster protein expression between post-ASCT relapse status, we performed moderated Bayes t test with a false discovery rate of 5% using the Benjamin-Hochberg method. For the 285 cluster and surface protein expression combination variables, logistic regression was used to further select 22 variables while controlling for potential confounders such as age, sex, batch, cytogenetics, and maintenance arm. Using backward logistic regression based on Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values, 8 model variables were selected, of which 2 were manually removed based on expert assessment of a variance inflation factor. A fivefold crossvalidation was performed to evaluate the model selection method.

To assess the accuracy of predictions for our selected protein expression marker, the area under the curve (AUC) for receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves was calculated, and confidence intervals (CI) were generated based on bootstrap. To identify optimal cut points, accuracy was measured using the sum of specificity and sensitivity. To compare the accuracy of 2 ROC curves, significance testing was performed with the bootstrap method to test for a difference in the AUC.

The study was approved by the institutional review board of MSKCC, and all patients provided informed consent.

Results

Elevated PD-1 expression by T cells after ASCT correlates with inferior PFS

We identified 341 patients who underwent ASCT for MM at our center between 2001 and 2013 to investigate whether the BM microenvironment at day +100 after ASCT predicts outcomes. This time frame was selected to include patients before and after incorporating lenalidomide maintenance into our institutional standard-of-care treatment approach. Formalin-fixed, decalcified BM samples were available from 122 post-ASCT patients. Of the 122, a total of 12 were excluded due to a lack of clinical follow-up data, leaving 110 patients in this cohort for post-ASCT IHC analysis.

We assessed the presence of T cells in the BM using CD3 and PD-1 staining to determine whether activated T cells correlate with inferior outcomes. Samples were dichotomized based on >5% CD3 staining and >1% PD-1 staining to indicate the presence or absence of these cell populations (Figure 1A). The presence of CD3+ cells trended with inferior PFS (Figure 1B), and the presence of CD3+ PD-1+ cells was associated with early PFS events (Figure 1C), independent of lenalidomide maintenance, complete remission at day +100, or cytogenetic risk on multivariate analysis (Figure 1D). Together, these findings suggested antigen-experienced T cells, identified by CD3+ PD-1+ IHC as a biomarker of ER after ASCT. Additionally, the inverse association between higher CD3 presence and lower PFS provided a rationale for further investigating the relationship between immune profiles and clinical outcomes.

Loss of activated EM CD4 populations in ER

Recent studies comparing BM and peripheral blood immune phenotypes have demonstrated concordance.20 Because peripheral blood is more readily accessible and clinically practical for routine monitoring than BM, we surveyed peripheral blood samples for T-cell immunophenotypes associated with inferior PFS to refine our initial IHC-based BM findings (Figure 1). Specifically, we performed in-depth immunophenotyping of the circulating T-cell compartment using day +100 post-ASCT peripheral blood samples from 96 patients selected from the BMT CTN 0702 trial (STaMINA; NCT01109004). To enhance the identification of immune-based differences, patients were selected based on divergent PFS, including a FHR group with ER (n = 45), defined as relapse within 24 months of ASCT, and a long-term responder cohort (LR; n = 51), defined as patients who remained relapse free >4 years from ASCT (Figure 2A). Supplemental Table 2 summarizes the characteristics of the patients from the BMT CTN cohort. There was no statistical difference between the 2 groups for male sex (ER 65% vs LR 49%), HR cytogenetics (ER 53% vs LR 66%), or >65 years of age (ER 7% vs LR 15%). For treatment regimens, 43% of patients received ASCT followed by maintenance lenalidomide, 27% received ASCT followed by RVd consolidation and maintenance lenalidomide, and the remaining 30% received tandem ASCT followed by maintenance lenalidomide. Importantly, treatment variables, including response to ASCT and MRD status before initiation of maintenance therapy, showed no statistical association with ER or LR (supplemental Table 2), underscoring the limits of current risk assessment parameters.

All patient samples were manually gated on live, CD3+ events (8.35 million cells) and concatenated, followed by unsupervised clustering using FlowSOM on mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) values. A full list of antibodies is shown in supplemental Table 4. Highly similar clusters were manually merged for a final output of 19 T-cell subsets (Figure 2B). The expression of CD45RA and CCR7 was used to identify T-cell clusters as follows: CD45RA+CCR7+ naïve cells (naïve), CD45RA˗CCR7− effector memory (EM), CCR7+CD45RA− central memory (CM), and CD45RA+CCR7− EM reexpressing CD45RA (TEMRA; Figure 2C).21 We also identified regulatory T-cell (Treg) populations based on CD25 and FoxP3 expression and follicular T helper cells based on CXCR5high expression.

The final annotation of the 19 clusters included 6 CD8 T-cell clusters, 12 CD4 T-cell clusters, and a CD4/CD8 double negative (DN) cluster. The largest CD8 cluster was “CD8: TEMRA” (17.03% of total CD3+ cells). There were 2 naïve CD8 clusters, one with a classical phenotype and a smaller secondary population with increased expression of CD25, Eomes, and Tbet, possibly representing a transitional state of early differentiation of naïve T cells after T-cell receptor (TCR) engagement.22 The largest CD4 cluster was “CD4: Naïve CD25˗” (19.91% of total CD3+). There was also a smaller naïve CD4 cluster that was CD25+. The EM and CM CD4 populations formed clusters based on the differential expression of CD25 and CCR4. A minor “CD4: TEMRA” cluster was also detected. Additionally, there were 2 Treg clusters (CCR4+/−), 1 follicular T helper cell cluster, and 1 cluster that was CD4/CD8 DN but CD45RA+Tbet+Eomes+ (Figure 2D). This DN subset clustered closely with the CD8: TEMRA, suggesting a state of CD8 downregulation after antigen exposure.23

When testing for differences in the frequency of T-cell subpopulations associated with ER, we identified statistically significant dissimilarity in 4 of the 19 clusters (Figure 2E). For ER patients, both CD4: CM CD25+ and CD4: EM CD25+CCR4+ cells were significantly reduced (adjusted P = .01 and P < .001, respectively), whereas CD4: EM CD25˗CCR4˗ T cells were increased (adjusted P = .018). CD25 and CCR4 expression is associated with T-cell activation,24,25 and in our cohort, the presence of highly activated EM and CM CD4 T cells was associated with LR after ASCT. There was also a significant difference in the frequency of naïve CD8 T cells, with a small naïve CD8 cluster (“CD8: Naïve CD25+”) with CD25high expression demonstrating a ninefold greater frequency in the ER cohort (0.01% vs 0.09%; adjusted P < .0001). Overall, the most notable difference in T-cell subpopulations between ER and LR patients was the reduced abundance of EM and CM CD4 T cells expressing CD25 and CCR4 in ER patients.

Exhausted T-cell phenotype in relapsed patients

To further characterize T-cell phenotypes between ER and LR patients and to identify predictive immune biomarkers, we next examined the expression patterns of positive and negative costimulatory molecules by MFI. We interrogated the expression profiles of 15 distinct markers within our annotated 19 cell clusters (Figure 3A). Moderated Bayes t tests using Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate method identified 93 significant results (supplemental Table 5). Among the ER cohort, multiple CD4 and CD8 T-cell clusters had lower GzmB, ki67, and CD27 expression with concurrent higher expression of TIGIT, CD38, and PD-1 than the LR group (Figure 3B). In aggregate, there was a signal of exhaustion at day +100 after ASCT within both the CD4 and CD8 subsets among ER individuals, indicative of an immune phenotype of FHR disease biology.

Because multiple proteins were differentially expressed in numerous cell subsets, we refined our analysis to identify variables most predictive of ER. We first performed multivariate analysis controlling for the clinical covariates of age, cytogenetics, and maintenance treatment for each variable. This yielded 25 variables independently associated with ER risk (Figure 3C). We assessed the 25 variables in a backward stepwise logistic regression model to identify the strongest predictors of ER. The resulting model yielded 8 variables, including 3 highly colinear CD38 variables (supplemental Table 6). We retained the one CD38 variable with the largest coefficient. The 6 variables selected for the final model are displayed in Figure 3D. The strongest positive predictor of relapse risk was TIGIT expression among the “CD4: EM CD25+ CCR4+,” whereas high expression of CD27 among “CD4: Tregs CCR4˗” was associated with LR. A fivefold crossvalidation of our variable selection methodology using an 80 of 20 spilt of the BMT CTN cohort had an average AUC of 88%, indicating strong performance (Figure 3E).

Evaluation of clinically applicable flow cytometry analysis approaches

Although MFI offers an objective measure for assaying potential immune biomarkers by flow cytometry, it is subject to interexperimental and machine variability. In addition, granular clustering from high-dimensional approaches may be difficult to replicate, thus limiting utility in clinical applications. Therefore, we performed manual gating on the BMT CTN cohort, recapitulated our clustering output, and used percent positive gates to evaluate the 6 variables from our final logistic regression model. In parallel, we also manually gated a separate MSKCC cohort of patients who met our ER (n = 8) and LR (n = 10) definitions and had samples collected at diagnosis, before ASCT, and day +100 after ASCT (supplemental Figure 2A). This comparative analysis yielded high concordance between the algorithm-based clustering and the populations resolved by manual gating (supplemental Figure 2B-E).

In the BMT CTN cohort, the upregulation of TIGIT, CD38, and HLA-DR in the ER group was reproduced in their respective subpopulations by manual gating. The loss of CD27 expression on Tregs among ER patients showed a moderate effect size that reached statistical significance (P = .049). Changes in the expression of CTLA-4 and granzyme B between ER and LR groups were not replicated (Figure 4A-B). Using an identical manual gating strategy, we explored the same 6 variables for concordance within the MSKCC cohort at the post-ASCT time point to validate our findings. In the MSKCC cohort, CD38 upregulation on Eomes+ CD8 EM cells demonstrated statistical significance with a robust effect size, similar to that observed in the BMT CTN cohort. Loss of CD27 among CCR4− Tregs was also significant in the MSKCC cohort (P = .0004). There was a trend toward HLA-DR upregulation in the second cohort that did not reach statistical significance (P = .77). Upregulation of TIGIT among CD4 EM T cells in ER patients, which was reproduced by manual gating of the BMT CTN cohort, was not observed in the MSKCC cohort (Figure 4C-D). In summary, loss of CD27 expression among CCR4− Tregs and upregulation of CD38 on Eomes+ CD8 EM T cells demonstrated the greatest potential for discriminating ER from LR patients, based on validation across 2 distinct clinical cohorts.

Identification of a validated predictive T-cell phenotype

A higher ratio of naïve-to-effector T-cell subsets has been proposed as a favorable prognostic parameter for newly diagnosed MM and before chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy.16,26, 27, 28 We postulated that CD27 upregulation in LR signifies a naïve T-cell phenotype, whereas increased CD38 and HLA-DR expression indicated terminal differentiation or activation, respectively. We sought to build a flow-based prediction score using ratios of the expression of CD27 to CD38 and HLA-DR by calculating a CD27/CD38+HLA-DR score, which is the ratio of percent CD27+ among CD3+Foxp3+CD25+CCR4− cells divided by the sum of percent CD38+ among CD3+CD8+CD45RA−CCR7−Eomes+ cells and percent HLA-DR+ among CD3+CD4+CD45RA−CCR7−CD25+CCR4+ cells. After calculating this score, we assessed its predictive power among both the BMT CTN and MSKCC cohorts at the post-ASCT time point. We compared this score to other ratios using the same 3 markers, including CD27/C38, CD27/HLA-DR, and CD38+HLA-DR, as well as each marker alone (supplemental Figures 3 and 4).

In the BMT CTN cohort, the CD27/CD38+HLA-DR score had the highest AUC (AUC, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.80-0.95), but it was not significantly different from CD38 alone (AUC, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.78-0.93; P = .43; Figure 5A). Evaluation of CD38 expression among Eomes+CD8+ EM T cells in the MSKCC cohort at the post-ASCT time point demonstrated similar overall accuracy (AUC, 0.83; CI, 0.62-1.0; Figure 5B). In the BMT CTN cohort, the optimal cutoff for CD38 staining to discriminate ER from LR patients was >40% positivity, yielding a sensitivity and specificity of 71% and 92%, respectively. Importantly, this difference in CD38 surface expression between ER and LR patients was significant regardless of HR cytogenetics (del17p, t[4;14], t[14;16]; supplemental Figure 5), premaintenance MRD status (supplemental Table 7), or treatment response before or after ASCT (supplemental Table 8).

CD38 in EM CD8+Eomes+ T cells demonstrates predictive power across both BMT CTN and MSKCC cohorts at multiple time points. (A-D) ROC curves estimating the sensitivity and specificity of CD38 expression by Eomes+ CD8 EM T cells for delineating ER vs LR in post-ASCT time point (A) in the BMT-CTN cohort (n = 96); post-ASCT time point (B) in the MSKCC cohort (n = 17); diagnosis time point (C) in the MSKCC cohort (n = 17); and pre-ASCT time point (D) in the MSKCC cohort (n = 18).

Using the MSKCC cohort, we next analyzed whether CD38 expression in Eomes+CD8+ EM T cells could separate ER (n = 8) and LR patients (n = 10) at the pre-ASCT and diagnosis time points. CD38 expression was consistently elevated among ER patients but was only significantly different at the post-ASCT time point. ROC curves demonstrated 76% AUC (CI, 53.5-100.0) at the diagnosis time point, followed by an AUC of 73% (CI, 46.3-99.7) for the pre-ASCT time point (Figure 5C-D). Although this cohort was not sufficiently powered at diagnosis and after induction, similar trends in CD38 expression in EM CD8+Eomes+ cells segregating LR from ER patients were observed at all time points. These findings suggest that further validation in larger sample sets may support the integration of this immune signature into an HR-I framework for identifying FHR MM.

Discussion

Defining novel prognostic factors of functional HR MM is essential to guide preemptive measures to maintain disease control after first-line therapy.29 At the same time, defining patients who achieve long-term disease control with standard lenalidomide maintenance evokes questions about strategies for maintenance therapy discontinuation. The expanding role of immunotherapies in MM highlights the importance of T-cell surveillance in disease control, but clinically actionable immune biomarkers are lacking. We found that antigen-experienced CD3+ PD-1+ T cells in the BM microenvironment are associated with ER independent of clinical response, maintenance therapy, or HR cytogenetics. Subsequent in depth exploration of T-cell phenotypes in patients with ER and LR after ASCT across 2 clinically comparable but distinct patient cohorts identified a circulating immune phenotype of CD38-expressing CD8 T cells that distinguishes HR-I biology, independent of HR cytogenetics (HR-FISH) or MRD status.

Our algorithmic approach in the discovery BMT CTN 0702 study cohort identified 6 key variables highly associated with the risk of ER. However, 4 of the 6 markers (TIGIT, HLA-DR, CD38, and PD-1), which increase with T-cell exhaustion and correlate with worse outcomes in MM,13 did not maintain significance after crossvalidation. Instead, we identified CD27 downregulation by CD3+CD4+Foxp3+CD25+CCR4− cells and upregulation of CD38 by CD8+CD45RA−CCR7−Eomes+ cells as reproducible markers of relapse risk across 2 independent cohorts (BMT CTN 0702 and MSKCC). CD38 expression alone segregated ER from LR with >90% specificity and 70% sensitivity at 3 months after ASCT in the BMT CTN cohort. Not surprisingly, CD38 expression was not uniform across time points, with CD38 expression lower after ASCT, likely due to treatment-related effects on the immune environment. Incorporating CD38 expression status to identify patients with a suppressed or impaired immune milieu early after ASCT may enhance risk-stratification assessment.

CD38 is highly expressed by malignant plasma cells, and the CD38 mAbs daratumumab and isatuximab have significant anti-MM efficacy.30 CD38 is also a marker of T-cell activation,31 and the clinical activity of CD38 mAbs is in part due to the depletion of immunosuppressive subsets, primarily myeloid and Treg populations, which increases functional helper and cytotoxic T cells.32 For the ER patients in our study, we observed increased CD38 expression within all major T-cell subsets, except for naïve CD4 and naïve CD8 T cells. Moreover, this finding was most pronounced among Eomes+ EM CD8 T cells. CD8+ CD38+ T cells are hypoproliferative and expand in states of repeated antigen exposure, such as chronic viral infections.33 Within EM T-cell populations, high expression of CD38 may signify an accumulation of dysfunctional T cells, impaired antitumor immunity, and increased risk of relapse. This is relevant to the ongoing evolution of MM induction, consolidation, and maintenance strategies incorporating CD38 mAbs, as supported by the recently published CASSIOPEIA, GRIFFIN, and PERSEUS trials.8,34,35 Our finding that CD38 expression is higher among ER patients at multiple time points, in conjunction with clinical data demonstrating the benefit of CD38 mAbs during induction and maintenance treatment phases, raises the possibility that CD38+Eomes+ EM CD8 T cells do not merely reflect an aberrant immune TME but may facilitate MM disease progression. Furthermore, our study raises the possibility of defining patient subsets for whom CD38 mAbs have the greatest clinical benefit, thus enabling biomarker-driven therapeutic decision-making. Additional mechanistic and clinical studies are warranted to investigate this hypothesis, because all the patients in our study were treated before the adoption of CD38 mAbs into induction and maintenance therapy. Further investigation is also needed to determine whether CD38+CD8+ T cells contribute to disease progression or whether these cells are a consequence of a more aggressive disease phenotype.

This study has limitations that merit attention in future investigations. High-dimensional analysis techniques are prone to false positives and can identify minor differences in expression despite stringent P value adjustment. In this study, stepwise logistic regression allowed for the identification of key variables that best predict relapse, and cross-validation protected against model overfitting. The sample size of our MSKCC cohort was relatively small, but the consistent trend observed across all 3 time points provides proof of principle and lends merit to confirmation with additional sample sets. Furthermore, this study is T-cell focused and does not address the contribution of other cell populations or factors within the peripheral blood and TME. Because our patient cohorts were preselected for extremes in clinical outcomes, further studies across larger unselected patient samples are needed to confirm the positive predictive value of CD38-expressing CD8 T cells for identifying early relapse. Although functional characterization could not be performed in this study due to limited sample availability, our findings provide a rationale for such future analyses to elucidate the biological relevance and mechanistic underpinnings. Although our flow cytometry analysis was performed on batch-thawed PBMCs to minimize variability, potential batch effects from sample collection across multiple sites cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, the use of cryopreserved samples, rather than fresh real-time samples, may have resulted in attenuated antigen expression signals.

In summary, our immune profile modeling highlights differences in ER and LR patients based on CD38 upregulation by an EM CD8 T-cell subpopulation. Incorporating this immune-based biomarker distinguishing ER from LR populations would complement standard tools of disease prognostication by providing more precise risk stratification, leading to tailored treatment approaches based on relapse risk to optimize clinical outcomes in frontline therapy for MM.
