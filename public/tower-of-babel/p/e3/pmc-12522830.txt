Immediate implant placement for single failed maxillary anterior teeth after trauma: a case-series study
Authors: Zinan Yang, Xiao Chen, Jingyun Chen, Yinyin Li, Qian Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose of this retrospective evaluation was to assess the treatment outcomes following immediate implant placement for single failed maxillary anterior teeth after trauma. Moreover, both objective and subjective esthetic assessments were conducted.MethodsImmediate implant placement was performed on thirty-two patients who had a single untreatable, traumatic tooth in the anterior maxilla. At the one-year mark following the final restoration, marginal bone changes, pink/white esthetic scores (PES/WES), and patient satisfaction data were collected. Factors such as age, sex, gingival biotype, thickness of the buccal bone wall, and size of the jumping gap were considered potential influences on the clinical results, with their relationships being examined.ResultsThe implant survival rate was 100% after one year of final restoration., with marginal bone changes measuring − 0.17 ± 0.31 mm mesially and − 0.22 ± 0.28 mm distally. Additionally, the PES averaged 10.7, whereas the WES averaged 7.3. On the patient side, esthetic satisfaction scored an average of 8.89 on the visual analog scale. Interestingly, no significant correlations were found between potential factors and esthetic scores or patient satisfaction.ConclusionsIn the initial year following the final restoration, immediate implant placement for single failed maxillary anterior teeth post-trauma demonstrated promising clinical outcomes both objectively and subjectively.Keywords: Dental implants, Esthetic region, Immediate implant, Dental trauma

Background

Injuries to the teeth in the anterior maxilla are commonly observed. In some cases, teeth that have been severely damaged and are unable to be restored may need to be removed and replaced by a dental implant. Experiencing the loss of a tooth in the esthetic area can be emotionally challenging for individuals, particularly in cases of dental injuries where patients are caught off guard. Hence, individuals in such circumstances are often eager for implant placement and restoration. Nevertheless, the challenge of maintaining esthetics becomes more pronounced due to the gradual loss of soft and hard tissue after tooth extraction.

The timing, in terms of implant placement post-tooth extraction categorization, can be classified into four types: immediate placement (type 1), occurring within 24 h following extraction; early (type 2), occurring after 6–8 weeks; delayed (type 3), occurring after 3 months; and late (type 4), occurring after more than 6 months. The advantages of immediate dental implant placement are evident compared with delayed implant placement, offering streamlined surgical interventions, reduced treatment duration, and preservation of surrounding soft and hard tissues [1–3].

Numerous investigations have documented favorable outcomes with regards to the placement of dental implants immediately after the extraction of teeth in the anterior maxilla [4–7]. On the other hand, the immediate placement of implants comes with a set of restrictions and dangers, including the possibility of implant failure, infection, peri-implantitis, and issues related to esthetics [8, 9]. Furthermore, few studies have explored the topic of the immediate placement of dental implants after dental injury. Hence, further investigations should be conducted to determine the critical details of the therapeutic process.

Our department has conducted immediate placement of dental implants for maxillary anterior teeth that have been compromised due to trauma over the course of the previous 8 years. The aim of this study was to assess the efficacy, peri-implant tissue response, esthetic results, and patient satisfaction following immediate dental implantation subsequent to trauma in the anterior region of the maxilla while examining potential factors influencing the clinical results.

Methods

Patients and study design

A review of the medical records of individuals who underwent immediate placement of dental implants following injuries to their teeth was performed. During the period spanning from January 2014 to December 2021, patients who participated in this research were treated at the Department of Periodontology and Oral Implantology, Stomatological Hospital, Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China). The patients were selected according to specific inclusion and exclusion criteria.

All study participants underwent immediate dental implant placement in the esthetic zone, with a portion of them receiving immediate temporary restorations. Following a healing period of 6 months or more, the final restorations were carried out. All patients provided detailed informed consent before treatment

Clinical procedure

During the initial visit, a cranial cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan was conducted to assess the injured teeth and rule out any potential bone fractures. The subsequent evaluation of the injured tooth involved consultation with an oral surgeon, prosthodontist, and endodontist to determine its preservation feasibility.

If the wounded tooth could not be kept and the patient Chose to have an oral implant, the patient received standard treatment planning and consented to the treatment. In the case of a damaged tooth where the buccal bone wall remains intact and there is sufficient residual bone, minimally traumatic extraction was carefully carried out, and the implant was immediately inserted into the fresh extraction socket in a flapless manner. All implants were placed 1–2 mm below the edge of the socket, to compensate the potential resorption of the buccal wall. The specific implant system utilized in this study was the Zimmer Biomet TSV (Warsaw, Indiana, U.S.). The diameter of the implant was 3.7 mm in all patients. Bone particles (Bio-oss, Geistlich Pharma AG, Wolhusen, Switzerland) were used to fill the space between the implant and the buccal wall.

Patients were instructed to use the broad-spectrum antibiotics cefuroxime (0.25 g, twice per day for 3 days) or roxithromycin (0.15 g, twice per day for 3 days) in the case of cefaclor allergy. 0.5% chlorhexidine was utilized for mouthwash three times daily during the first 2 weeks. The patient received one dose of dexamethasone (5 mg) orally post-surgery. The ice packs were used in the first 48 h after surgery. Patients were advised to consider nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs in cases of pain. Sutures were removed after one week.

For some of the patients with an insertion torque greater than 35 N×cm, a temporary restoration was prepared and placed immediately after surgery. Some temporary restorations were prepared with the fractured crown of the patient before surgery (see Fig. 1). In cases where immediate provisionalization was not performed, prefabricated healing abutments were placed on the implants. The clinical outcomes were subsequently analyzed and compared between patients who received immediate provisionalization and those who did not. A minimum of four months after the surgery date, the final implant impression was taken using polyether silicone rubber (Minnesota Mining and Manufacturing Company, 3 M, Maplewood, Minnesota, U.S.). A definitive customized crown with gingival emergence established by the provisional restoration was fabricated via Hex-Lock Contour Abutments (Zimmer Biomet). The definitive abutment was torqued to 30 N/cm (manufacturer’s recommendation, Zimmer Biomet). The zirconia coping and zirconia crown, which are highly esthetic and natural-looking, were cemented (RelyX U200, 3 M).

Data collection

All examinations and data collection were performed by the same examiner. Assessments were conducted at the time of implant insertion (T0), upon completion of the final restoration (T1), and twelve months after final restoration (T2). The recorded variables included the success or failure of the implant, the marginal bone level, pink/white esthetic scores (PES/WES), and the satisfaction of patients with esthetics. The buccal bone wall thickness and jumping gap distance were measured at the level of implant shoulder through CBCT images at T0.

Marginal bone change

Sequential periapical radiographs and the long cone parallel technique were utilized to assess the changes in the marginal bone. To achieve standardization in angulation and film position to the X-ray beam, an occlusal jig was employed. Marginal bone levels of the implants were measured on the mesial and distal aspects at T1 and T2 under 10x magnification, with accuracy to the nearest 1 mm. The reference point for measurement was established at the apical corner of the implant shoulder, and subsequently, the alterations were calculated. A positive value is denoted when there is more coronal implant–bone contact in comparison to the reference point; conversely, a negative value is assigned when implant–bone contact is more apical than the reference point.

Esthetic assessment

The esthetic scores were objectively evaluated by two blinded prosthodontists who were not involved in the treatment.

Pink esthetic score

The pink esthetic score [10] was used to assess all patients, and consisted of seven variables: the mesial papilla, distal papilla, soft tissue level, soft tissue contour, alveolar process deficiency, soft tissue color, and soft tissue texture. The scoring system assigned a value of 0, 1, or 2 to each factor. The scale ranged from 0, representing the least favorable outcome, to 2, indicating the most favorable outcome for every variable; hence, achieving a total of 14 would signify optimal peri-implant soft tissues. The threshold for clinically acceptable soft tissues was set at 8. A score of 12 or higher was considered to indicate almost perfect peri-implant soft tissues, as previously described.

White esthetic score

The White Esthetic Score [11] was utilized to evaluate all patients, encompassing five different variables: the general shape of the teeth, the contour of the teeth, the color of the teeth (including hue and value), the texture of the tooth surface, and the level of translucence. A scoring system of 0, 1, or 2 was applied to each parameter. The scores ranged from the least favorable outcome, represented by 0, to the most favorable outcome, represented by 2, for every variable. The esthetics of the implant-supported tooth were compared with those of the contralateral reference tooth. When the closest resemblance to the contralateral tooth was achieved, a maximum score of 10 was awarded. The thresholds for a clinically acceptable or an almost perfect implant crown were set at 6 and 9, respectively.

Patient esthetic satisfaction

During the one-year follow-up evaluation, an evaluation was conducted to assess the satisfaction of each patient with the overall implant treatment through a subjective outcome questionnaire. The questionnaire comprised five inquiries and a visual analogue scale (VAS) spanning 10 centimeters, ranging from 0 (representing the least favorable outcome) to 10 (representing the most favorable outcome) for evaluating patient content. Each patient’s response to the listed inquiries was used to compute an average VAS score.

Data analysis

Means and standard deviations were computed for each clinical parameter during each specific timeframe. GraphPad Prism (version 10, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA) was utilized to analyze the data. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was adopted to measure the marginal bone changes in the first year. Two-tailed t tests for independent samples were used to define the relationships between potentially relevant factors (i.e., age, sex, gingival biotype, buccal bone wall thickness, jumping gap and immediate provisionalization) and esthetic scores. The Spearman correlation coefficient was used to assess the association between objective esthetic scores and patient esthetic satisfaction (VAS). The Mann‒Whitney test was used to compare the VAS scores of patients in different groups according to their potentially relevant factors. Statistical significance was denoted when P < 0.05.

Results

From 2015 to 2021, 32 patients with single traumatized teeth in the esthetic area underwent immediate implant placement. There were 17 males and 15 females, with a mean age of 36.0 years (range 18–67). Thirty central incisors and 2 lateral incisors were included. Thirty-two threaded hydroxyapatite-coated tapered implants (Zimmer Biomet, TSV) were immediately inserted within two weeks after trauma. Six of them had immediate provisionalization. At least one year after the final restoration (ranging from 12.1 to 73.1 months, average 21.3 months), all 32 implants exhibited stability. The overall cumulative implant success rate was 100%, with no instances of osseointegration loss. Furthermore, the average buccal bone thickness was 0.83 mm, and the average jump gap between the bone and the implant was 2.38 mm. Marginal bone changes

Statistically significant marginal bone changes were noted after 1 year, with mean changes of −0.17 ± 0.31 mm mesially (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 0.004; median = 0.00, range − 0.84 mm to 0.20 mm) and − 0.22 ± 0.28 mm distally (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 0.0058; median =−0.10, range − 0.92 mm to 0.20 mm).

Esthetic outcomes

At the patients’ one-year revisit, the esthetic outcomes, including the PES and WES, were evaluated. The PESs of 30 cases are acceptable or almost perfect, and the WESs of 27 cases are acceptable or almost perfect. The average PES was 10.7 (ranging from 4.5 to 14, standard deviation ± 5.44, median 11), and the average WES was 7.3 (ranging from 3.0 to 10, standard deviation ± 4.05, median 7.75), with a mean total esthetic score of 18.0. Discussions

For severely traumatized teeth that cannot be saved in the anterior maxilla, dental implants are one of the options. Dental injury in this area is often accompanied by lip swelling, gingival laceration and dental alveolar fracture. Implant surgery should be performed after the swelling has subsided and the gingiva has healed. Many patients facing this situation prefer to extract the traumatized teeth and insert the implant in one surgery to reduce the number of visits and shorten the period of tooth loss. However, a survey revealed that very few patients were prepared to accept a greater risk of implant failure to reduce the treatment time [12]. Hence, it is crucial for dentists to have a thorough understanding of the advantages and disadvantages associated with the immediate placement of implants in cases of single failed maxillary anterior teeth following trauma before proposing this course of action to their patients.

The success rate was 100% after one year of final restoration in this study. A 5-year, multicenter, prospective evaluation by Cooper et al. [13] reported that the survival rate was 94.6% in immediate implant cases and 98.3% in delayed implant cases; this difference was not statistically significant. The authors also noted that all the failures occurred in the first year. A literature review evaluated immediate implant placement and provisionalization with a single crown in the esthetic area and reported a 97.96% success rate [14]. Additionally, a meta-analysis including 8 studies and a follow-up period between 12 and 96 months revealed that implant survival was 94.9% after immediate placement, demonstrating a notable decrease compared with delayed placement, which was 98.9% [15]. Considering the literature and our results, we believe that immediate implantation for trauma to a single tooth in the esthetic area can achieve a satisfactory success rate through the use of appropriate guidelines and careful patient selection.

Throughout this investigation, the marginal bone change following the placement of the implant until one year after the final restoration was found to be −0.17 mm mesially and − 0.22 mm distally. A one-year prospective study on the immediate placement and provisionalization of maxillary anterior single implants reported that the marginal bone change one year after implant placement was − 0.26 mm mesially and − 0.22 mm distally [16]. These findings align with those of a previous study, indicating that immediate implant placement for single failed maxillary anterior teeth after trauma did not lead to more marginal bone loss.

The outcomes from the analysis of both PES and WES in this study were found to be satisfactory. The mean PES in this study was 10.7, which is comparable to that reported by Cosyn et al. [17] (mean PES = 10.7) and higher than that reported by Altay et al. [18] (mean PES = 9.9). The mean WES in this study was 7.3, which is close to the result of Cosyn et al. [17] (mean WES = 7.8) and that of Angkaew et al. [19] (mean WES = 7.5) but lower than the result of Altay et al. [18] (mean WES = 8.6).

The esthetic outcomes of immediate implant placement and provisionalization are influenced by several intrinsic and extrinsic factors [20]. Intrinsic factors are patient dependent, and extrinsic factors are clinician dependent. In accordance with previous studies [5, 21–23], patient age, sex, gingival biotype, buccal bone wall thickness, jumping gap and immediate provisionalization were chosen as potentially relevant factors that may influence objective and subjective esthetic outcomes.

The PES is significantly higher in cases which had immediate provisionalization in this study. Immediate implant placement and loading is an effective way to preserve the existing gingival architecture, which positively influences the esthetic result [24]. Furthermore, immediate provisionalization facilitates the immediate sealing of the extraction socket and implant, potentially contributing to enhanced osseointegration and mucosal integration. Recent literatures demonstrated in anterior maxilla, the esthetic outcomes are comparable between immediate implant placement and provisionalization cases and immediate implant placement and delayed provisionalization cases [5], or early implant placement with guided bone regeneration and delayed loading cases. That is to say, immediate provisionalization itself doesn’t increase the risk of soft tissue recession. Therefore, we believed immediate provisionalization after immediate implant placement for single failed maxillary anterior teeth can help to increase the final Pink esthetic outcomes.

Age, sex, gingival biotype, buccal bone wall thickness and jumping gap are not correlated with the esthetic scores. Nonetheless, only two individuals displayed poor PESs due to significant soft tissue recession around their implant during the follow-up examination. These two patients had both thin gingival biotype and buccal bone wall thickness less than 1 mm. The thin gingival biotype is widely accepted as a risk factor for soft tissue recession in immediate implants [21, 25]. Angkaew et al. [19] reported that patients with a thick gingival biotype presented significantly higher PES/WES scores than did those with a thin gingival biotype. At the same time, it has long been widely accepted that a buccal wall thickness < 1 mm is a risk factor for gingival recession after immediate implantation [26]. However, opposite opinions have also been proposed. Altay et al. [18] indicated that the gingival biotype might not be a crucial factor in the esthetics of single-implant restorations. Meanwhile, Chen et al. [27] suggested that performing buccal bone augmentation at the same time as immediate implant placement can help ensure consistent buccal bone thickness in the anterior maxilla with a thin buccal plate. Considering the previous studies and our clinical data, special care must be taken for patients with thin gingival biotype or buccal bone wall thickness less than 1 mm before performing immediate implant insertion, especially in cases with combined factors. A systematic review revealed that a combination of an immediate implant and a connective tissue graft can thicken the peri-implant soft tissue and prevent soft tissue recession [28]. This treatment plan may be recommended for patients with thin gingival biotype and buccal bone wall thickness less than 1 mm.

The patient’s VAS score was 8.89 on average in this study. Similarly, Angkaew et al. [19] reported that the mean VAS score was 9.15, and Altay et al. [18] reported that the mean VAS score was 8.54. Considering these previous studies and our results, we believe that immediate implant placement for single failed maxillary anterior teeth after trauma can achieve satisfactory results for patients. We analyzed the correlations between potentially relevant factors and the patient’s VAS scores and found no significant results. Moreover, we analyzed the relationship between the PES/WES/ES score and the VAS score, and no significant differences were detected, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [18, 19]. In contrast, Cho et al. [29] reported a statistically significant correlation between ES and the VAS score. In our study, the lowest VAS score was 5.2, and the PES/WES score of this patient was 11/9.5. Moreover, one of the patients had a PES/WES score of 4.5/3, but his VAS score was 7.8. The difference between the PES/WES and VAS may be attributed to the subjective nature of esthetics and the incompatibility of the patient perspective. Therefore, the previous literature and our results indicate that patients’ satisfaction levels may not comply with objective evaluations from experts. Some patients may feel unsatisfactory for an implant with perfect pink and white esthetic scores. In contrast, some patients may feel good about having an implant, regardless of its appearance. Overall, we suggest that patients’ expectations and subjective evaluations of esthetics should be focused on during the whole therapy process.

The potential limitations of this study include the limited sample size and the bias inherent to the retrospective nature of the study. To validate our conclusions regarding immediate implant placement after dental trauma, future studies should involve larger sample sizes and extended follow-up periods. Moreover, exploring a prospective randomized clinical trial focusing on various timeframes for implant placement following dental trauma could enhance our ability to make informed decisions regarding the incidence of maxillary anterior tooth failure subsequent to trauma.

Conclusions

immediate implant placement for single failed maxillary anterior teeth after trauma can achieve both objectively and subjectively good clinical results in the first year after final restoration. The success rates and marginal bone loss rates of these cases are comparable to those reported in previous studies of immediate implants in the esthetic area. This study indicates that, with proper indications, immediate implantation is an effective way to treat traumatic single tooth loss in the anterior maxilla, which can shorten the treatment process.
