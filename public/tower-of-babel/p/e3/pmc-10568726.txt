1,1,6-Trimethyl-1,2-dihydronaphthalene Content of Riesling Wines in Hungary
Authors: Eszter Antal, Zsuzsanna Varga, Miklós Kállay, Szabina Steckl, Péter Bodor-Pesti, István Fazekas, Annamária Sólyom-Leskó, Barnabás Zoltán Kovács, Balázs Nagy, Áron Pál Szövényi, Diána Ágnes Nyitrai-Sárdy
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Abstract1,1,6-Trimethyl-1,2-dihydronaphthalene (TDN) is known to give a petrol note when smelling and tasting wine, which is most pronounced in Riesling varieties. Its increasing concentration has been linked to climate change. In the present work, a gas chromatographic method was used to quantify free TDN in “Italian Riesling”, “Rhine Riesling”, and “Kéknyelű” wines from Hungary. From the vintages 2010 to 2020, 39 bottles of wine from different wine regions were evaluated by instrumental analysis and sensory evaluation. Our aim is to determine the extent to which the Riesling wines in Hungary show a petrol character. The other objective was a comparison of sorts to see if there is a difference in TDN production potential between Italian Riesling and Rhine Riesling. We also aimed to clarify the question whether the Hungarian variety Kéknyelű is also capable of developing a petrol character. The wines we tested were corked and screw-locked. This allowed us to compare the difference in TDN concentration variation over time between the two closure methods.

Introduction

Riesling has been grown in Europe for over 600 years. It is considered one of the noblest wine grape varieties because of its particularly high cold tolerance, drought resistance, and great adaptability to a wide range of soil types. Riesling wines are produced on all wine-producing continents and are excellent for translating terroir, soil type, and style into an aroma profile. In Hungary, when we talk about Riesling wines, we think of three varieties: the Italian Riesling, the Rhine Riesling, and the Riesling Silvaner (a. k. a. Muller-Thurgau). In Hungary, the fifth most common variety is Italian Riesling, which is grown on 3000 ha, while Rhine Riesling and Riesling Silvaner are grown on 1000–1000 ha1 (Central Statistical Office, 2022) (Figure 1).

Area of the most common grape varieties in Hungary in 20201 (Central Statistical Office, 2022).

Riesling wines are described in a wide variety of ways as fresh, elegant, spicy, or very fruity and floral. These organoleptic characteristics depend on the wine region and vintage and are due to the relatively high monoterpene content in young wines and the volatile norisoprenoids in mature wines.

Aroma is a key element in the enjoyment of wine. The organoleptic appreciation of wine is very complex as wine contains a very large number of flavor and aroma compounds. In most wines, only a relatively small proportion of the more than 800 identified aroma compounds are responsible for the varietal aroma in the berries. These compounds, which are predominantly in the ppb-ppt range, are mainly monoterpenes, C13-norisoprenoids, thiols, and pyrazines. Terpenes, esters, methoxypyrazines, and aldehydes provide the floral, fruity, peppery, green, and woody flavors of wine.2

1,1,6-Trimethyl-1,2-dihydronaphthalene (TDN) is one of the key aroma components of Riesling wines, belonging to the group of C13-norizoprenoids (Figure 2).

1,1,6-Trimethyl-1,2-dihydronaphthalene (own editing).

At lower concentrations, this compound contributes to the characteristic kerosene-like aroma of aged Riesling wines, but at high concentrations, it can lead to the formation of an undesirable aroma, the so-called ″petrol″ or ″kerosene″ note, which can mask the fruity character of the wines. TDN is known to exist in very low concentrations in grapes and young wines and is released later during fermentation and storage from acid-sensitive glycosidic bonds in the precursors. These precursors are not organoleptically relevant but form a continuous reserve in the wine for the release of TDN.3

With the kerosene/petrol flavoring, TDN is considered controversial in terms of consumer preference. Low to medium TDN concentrations contribute to the complexity of wine aroma, but high TDN concentrations often create negative impressions in consumers due to the strong kerosene/gasoline fragrance dominance.4

Several cool climate wine-growing areas are affected by global warming. Rising temperatures and changing rainfall patterns affect the composition and style of the wines. The expression of TDN is influenced by the growing conditions of the grape. Wines made from Riesling grapes grown in warmer climates have higher concentrations of TDN. Therefore, “TDN management” will be one of the most challenging tasks in viticulture in Riesling growing areas, especially in cool climate regions.5

European Riesling wines usually contain between 1 and 50 μg/L TDN, while Australian wines can contain up to 250 μg/L or more. The organoleptic threshold for TDN in wine has been determined in several studies to be in the range of 2–20.6 μg/L.4 Simpson gave the taste threshold as early as 1978 as 20 μg/L, but no details of the organoleptic experiment were described.6 A few decades later, the TDN odor detection threshold (ODT) was set at a significantly lower level of 2 μg/L.7

In a 2020 study, Tarasov and colleagues found that the increased free sulfur dioxide content of wines may have some masking effect on TDN aroma perception and also showed that lower consumption temperatures of around 11 °C facilitated TDN aroma perception compared to wine samples served at room temperature.4

The concentration of TDN in wine increases during bottle aging due to the conversion of carotenoid precursors from the grape. One such carotenoid-type compound is lutein. In 1992, Marais demonstrated in an experiment that the TDN responsible for the kerosene-like flavor of Riesling wines can only come from lutein. Carotenoids are unstable compounds that readily degrade into various products in the presence of oxygen and light and at high temperatures.8 Thus, the transformation of precursors in the bottle is facilitated by higher storage temperatures, closure type, oxidation, high acidity, and bottle age.9

The amount of TDN precursor formation depends on viticultural practices such as defoliation, soil fertilization, irrigation, and selection of grape clones. Global climate change and warmer temperatures in the future may all increase the formation of TDN in Riesling wines.4 Already in 2015, Schüttler et al. thought that a viable approach to consider was the treatment of the grape canopy and the selection of the right yeast strain for alcoholic fermentation.5

The chemical composition of the wine is influenced by the way the grapes are grown, the cultivation technology, the yeast used for fermentation, aging, and storage. The primary aim of the viticultural practices is to produce quality grapes that reflect the flavor and aroma of the variety and are characterized by the specific character of the region. This involves choosing the right time to harvest the grapes at the right stage of ripeness, depending on the style of wine to be made. The use of specific processing techniques and fermentation strategies after harvest also plays a key role in shaping the aroma and flavor of the wine. The final aroma and flavor profile of a wine also depends on post-fermentation treatments such as filtration or aging.2

The selection of yeast strains may also influence TDN formation in wines.4 Yeast and its metabolites contribute to the sensory profile of wine and consumer preferences. During the second half of the 20th century, the use of selected yeasts Saccharomyces cerevisiaevar. cerevisiae and var. bayanus became widely accepted as starter cultures, reducing the risk of formation of undesirable aroma compounds and providing a high degree of fermentation.10

Yeast strains also influence the TDN potential of wines. It can be assumed that yeast strains have different enzymatic glycosidase activity during fermentation and are therefore more efficient in releasing aglycones from glycoside precursors. Yeast strain selection has an effect on the TDN potential of Riesling wines and is therefore considered an effective tool to manage TDN potential and therefore important for the aroma development of Riesling wines during bottle aging.5

In a long-term experiment published in 2007, Linsenmeier and Löhnertz found that TDN concentrations were lower in cooler years with fewer hours of sunshine. They also observed that grapes exposed to direct sunlight also produced wines with higher TDN concentrations than shaded grapes. The influence of climate on TDN formation was also found to be important. In Riesling wines produced in cooler climatic zones, TDN is only detectable after four to six years of bottle aging. In wine-producing regions with hot climates, TDN can be formed much earlier and in much larger quantities.11

Ziegler and colleagues have studied different Riesling clones. They investigated the effect of the rootstocks on the volatiles in the finished wines, in particular, on TDN. They observed that genetically determined loose grape clusters favored the development of TDN. However, berry size had no relevant effect on TDN formation. Low pH also increases TDN concentration in wines.12

The fruity, fresh aroma of Hungarian Riesling wines is threatened by climate change. Global warming is expected to lead not only to higher alcohol and lower acidity levels but also to changes in flavor, including increasing concentrations of TDN. We are the first in Hungary to investigate the concentration of TDN in Hungarian Riesling wines. The aim of the work was to evaluate the analytical and sensory state of the kerosene/gasoline taste problem in the present Hungarian Riesling and Italian Riesling wines, and to compare the varieties with other Hungarian grape varieties. By analyzing wines made from Kéknyelű grapes, our aim was to estimate the susceptibility of this grape variety to this problem. Kéknyelű, the grape variety used in the study, is an ancient Hungarian grape variety. The variety takes its name from the bluish-reddish tint of its leaf stalk. Its main production area is the Badacsony wine region, with an area of almost 46 hectares.

The measured TDN levels of 39 wines (Italian Riesling, Rhine Riesling, and Kéknyelű) from the vintages 2010 to 2020 were summed up per vintage, and the results were presented in graphs. The varieties were compared to see if there is a significant difference in their TDN production capacity. Differences in concentration between years were also investigated, looking for a correlation between the amount of global radiation and TDN concentration. These comparisons are illustrated in boxplot diagrams. We compared whether there is a significant difference between TDN concentrations measured after bottle dismantling and TDN concentrations measured after 6 months of storage by wine type. We also had the opportunity to compare whether the evolution of TDN concentrations at baseline and after 6 months of storage was influenced by the way the bottle was closed. In 6 bottles from the same winery, samples of wine from different vintages showed a tendency for older wines to have higher TDN concentrations than younger wines.

Material and Methods

Chemicals and Materials

The following chemicals were used for the analyses: ethanol absolute, sodium chloride (≥99%), L(+)-tartaric acid (p.a.), and potassium hydroxide (p.a.) were purchased from Reanal Laborvegyszer Kft. (Késmárk u. 9. 1158 Budapest, Hungary). Analytical standards were TDN, purity of 80% (CAS No. 30364-38-6, BOC Sciences Inc., 45-16 Ramsey Road, Shirley, NY 11967, USA); 4-methyl-2-pentanol was employed as internal standard (IS), purity of 98% (CAS No. 108-11-2, from Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH, Germany) and 20 mL headspace vial with a magnetic cap was used (purchased from Fisher Scientific, Germany).

Samples

Thirty-nine wine samples were analyzed (Table 1). All of them were commercial samples purchased in Hungary from different wine regions. The samples were Italian Riesling (bottle of 20), Rhine Riesling (bottle of 14), and Kéknyelű (bottle of 5) wines from different vintages (2010–2020) and from different wineries. Table 1 shows that most of the samples come from the Balaton wine region. The reason for this is that compared to other Hungarian wine regions, this wine region has the largest production area in terms of the three grape varieties we examined.

For the Italian Riesling and Rhine Riesling wines, the large variation is due to the mix of sweet wines and dry wines among the samples.

The wines tested were sealed with cork and screw caps. For sampling, the cork was not removed from the bottle. To sample wines sealed with cork, we used a special argon gas wine pouring device designed to allow the wine to be poured from the bottle without removing the cork. The device uses a thin, hollow needle to pierce the cork. As the wine flows out through the needle, pure argon gas flows into place, ensuring that the oxygen in the air does not reach the wine remaining in the bottle. The argon gas prevents oxidation of the wine caused by air entering the bottle when it is opened. The degradation of carotenoids is facilitated by the presence of oxygen. The TDN of the wines was measured immediately after opening the bottle and again after 6 months. The bottles with screw caps were resealed. All wines included in the study were stored in the same room and protected from sunlight at 22 °C until re-analysis. Since the external conditions were identical except for the way the wines were closed, we could test whether the presence of air made a difference to the amount of TDN produced.

Sensory Analysis

The wines were also rated on a 0–5 point scale, with 0 points for the wine that tasted the least of the TDN kerosene flavors and 5 points for the wine that tasted the most. The qualified tasting panel was made up of seven members (four women and three men, aged between 30 and 72) with several years of experience in wine evaluation. In order to identify the kerosene flavor and to detect the different concentrations, the panel members tasted various concentrations of TDN (2, 10, 20, 40, and 100 μg/L) in spiked white wines before evaluating the wines. The concentration of the spiked wine (formerly blue tongue) was previously checked by gas chromatography, and no TDN was found.

The tasting panel assessed the 39 bottles of wine by variety on three consecutive days. For each wine, clean, standard white wine glasses were used, and the wines were slightly chilled (15 °C). During the test series, the wines were subjected to sensory analysis once, immediately after the first opening.

Preparation of Standard Solutions

Individual TDN and 4-methyl-2-pentanol (IS) stock standard solutions were prepared by weight in 10 vol % ethanol. Working solutions used in further studies were prepared by diluting different amounts of the TDN stock standard solution in a model wine. A model wine was prepared with about 10 vol % ethanol, 3 g/L tartaric acid, and pH adjusted to 3.0 with a 1 N sodium hydroxide solution.

Instrumental Analysis

For sample preparation, automated headspace-solid phase microextraction (HS-SPME) analysis was used. Analysis was performed by gas chromatography with flame ionization detection (FID). 10 mL of wine samples was pipetted and placed into a 20 mL headspace vial with 1.2 g of NaCl. Each sample was spiked with 30 μL of a solution of 4-methyl-2-pentanol (2.252 g/L in distilled water containing 15% v/v of ethanol). The HS-SPME extraction procedure was carried out with a Combi PAL autosampler using an Acrylate SPME fiber of 1 cm in length and 85 μm in thickness both from CTC Analytics AG (Zwingen, Switzerland). Sample conditioning, extraction, and headspace sampling were conducted using the agitator of the Combi PAL autosampler. The samples were incubated for 10 min at 40 °C followed by an extraction time of 20 min at 40 °C at a stirring rate of 400 rpm. Desorption was performed in the split/splitless injector of the gas chromatograph (GC) at the temperature of 220 °C. Cleaning and conditioning of the fiber was conducted at 280 °C for 5 min under a nitrogen flow of 6 mL/min in the conditioning station of the autosampler, before and after each analysis. Gas chromatographic analyses were conducted with a Shimadzu-2030 with FID detection. The separation column used was a 30 m × 0.25 mm i.d. fused silica capillary, coated with a 5% diphenyl containing polydimethylsiloxane stationary phase of 0.25 μm (SH-Rxi-5 ms from Simkon Kft., Hungarian distributor of Shimadzu Europa GmbH.). The GC was used in splitless mode (splitless time in 2 min). The carrier gas was helium at a flow rate of 0.86 mL/min. The detector temperature was 320 °C. The GC oven was programmed as follows: the initial temperature was 60 °C, then ramped at 11.5 °C/min to 110 °C, after that ramped at 6.1 °C/min to 170 °C, finally ramped at 66.5 °C/min to 290 °C, and held for 3.4 min.

The TDN was quantified by comparison with a calibration curve, obtained using the relative peak area in relation to that of 4-methyl-2-pentanol, the internal standard.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical evaluation of the measurement results was performed using the ANOVA program of Microsoft Excel (version 18.2106.12410.0, license: Microsoft Corporation). The analytical results were evaluated by one-factor analysis of variance. Differences between varieties were tested at the 95% (p = 0.05) significance level as well as whether the method of closure (stopper or screw cap) caused a difference in whether TDN concentrations increased during storage. The one-factor analysis of variance indicates whether there is a statistically significant difference among the means of three or more independent groups but does not tell us which groups differ from each other. Statistical analysis was performed using the ANOVA program in Microsoft Excel. In order to find out exactly which wine groups differ from each other, a post hoc test was performed. The most commonly used post hoc test is the Tukey–Kramer test, which compares the means of pairwise combinations of groups. This test was used to determine which wine varieties had significant differences in TDN content.

Various thresholds have been identified in the literature;6,7 Tarasov et al. in 2020 identified three sensory thresholds: the detection threshold (DT: about 4 μg/L), the recognition threshold (RT: 10–12 μg/L), and the rejection threshold (RejT: 71–82 μg/L).4 After re-measurement after 6 months of storage, we found that 17 of the 39 bottles of wine we tested had TDN concentrations above the DT defined by Tarasov et al. This is 44% of the total number of wines tested. Only two bottles of the tested wines reached the DT during 6 months of storage.

Figure 3 shows the TDN concentrations in a variety comparison. Analyzing the data with a one-factor analysis of variance between the wine types, we found that there were significant differences at the 95% significance level (p = 0.05) based on the measured TDN concentrations. Investigating further, using the Tukey–Kramer test, we found that the difference in TDN concentrations measured in the Italian Riesling and the Rhine Riesling wines is statistically significant. The same conclusion can be made when comparing the TDN concentrations in Rhine Riesling and Kéknyelű wines. However, the difference in measured TDN concentrations is not statistically significant for Italian Riesling and Kéknyelű wines. The same can be said for the TDN concentrations measured at the second measurement time although the average TDN concentrations increased in all three varieties during storage. The highest TDN concentrations were measured in Rhine Riesling wines. In two cases, TDN concentrations even reached the rejection threshold for this variety.

Figure 4 shows a boxplot of the wines analyzed for the vintages 2010–2020. The wines from the 2020 vintage had concentrations between 0.2 and 9.2 μg/L at the time of measurement, summing the two measurement dates. The highest TDN content in the 2019 vintage wines was 19.7 μg/L, while in 2017, TDN levels reached over 25 and 79.5 μg/L (outlier). The explanation for the high TDN content in 2019 compared to other years is that the summer was warmer than usual in this year compared to the previous (2018) and subsequent (2020) years, with higher global radiation. Marais and colleagues in 1992 and Gerdes and colleagues in 2001 found that overexposure of grapes to sunlight leads to high TDN concentrations.13,14 In 2019, the summer global irradiance in Hungary typically fell between 190 and 210 kJ/cm2 in most parts of the country. The same global irradiance was lower in 2018 and 2020, falling between 180 and 200 kJ/cm2.16

The effect of vintage on the concentration of TDN in wines seems to be much stronger than the age of the wines. The vintage has an effect on grapes, which respond to extreme weather conditions by producing TDN precursors. More precursors will subsequently result in higher TDN concentrations in the finished wines. In previous years, TDN was measured at low concentrations. The DT was reached in samples measured in 2017, 2018, and 2019. The DT was exceeded by the wine samples measured in 2019. None of the TDN content of the wines we measured reached the rejection threshold, except for two wines (2017 and 2018) whose TDN concentration was exceptionally high.

The wines with high TDN concentrations observed in 2017 and 2018 (Figure 4) were all Rhine Rieslings. They came from different wineries and different wine regions (Table 4). Since the wine samples were commercial samples and not from the wineries, we have no information on the viticultural and winemaking techniques that influenced TDN formation. However, it can be noted that all four wines exceeded the sensory threshold range given by Tarasov et al. at the first measurement, and two wines reached the rejection threshold (RejT: 71–82 μg/L) during 6 months of storage.4

Wines with Outstandingly High TDN Concentration

Comparing the scores given in the sensory evaluation with the measured TDN concentrations, it can be observed that the concentration of the compound does not always follow the sensory appearance. The matrix may blunt or highlight the detectability of the compound. This is observed for all three wine types (Figures 5, 6, 7). An extreme case can also be observed where the concentration of TDN remains below the DT, but the tasting panel still detected a kerosene taste. The reverse was also the case, where the TDN concentration of the wine reached the rejection threshold and yet the tasting panel detected little taste reminiscent of kerosene.

The TDN content of the wines was measured again after 6 months. The measurement conditions were the same as in the first series of measurements. Until the re-measurement, the wines were stored in a sealed room at 22 °C. The results obtained are shown in Figures 5, 6, 7.

Of the 20 bottles of Italian Riesling wine analyzed, all but one (sample 3, vintage 2017) showed a significant increase in the amount of TDN measured over time at the 95% (p = 0.05) significance level. The highest measured concentration (12.1 μg/L) was measured in a 2018 wine (sample 8), which exceeds the DT. Only the TDN content of 6 wines exceeded the DT. The largest change, with a 2.3-fold increase in TDN content, was also found in a 2018 wine (sample 19).

For the 14 bottles of Rhine Riesling analyzed, no significant increase in TDN content was detected at the 95% (p = 0.05) probability level after 6 months. In the 2010–2017 vintages (batches 1–6 in the figure), TDN concentrations remained practically unchanged. For these wines, we tested wines closed with roughly the same amount of corks (6 bottles) and screw caps (8 bottles), and therefore we examined whether the method of closure made a difference to the increase in TDN concentration. As the cork was not removed from the bottle, the wine remaining in the bottle was not exposed to air. When tested at the 95% (p = 0.05) probability level, it can be concluded that there is a significant difference in the change in TDN concentration depending on whether the bottle was fitted with a cork stopper or a screw cap (whether or not the wine remaining in the bottle after opening was in contact with air). If our wine remaining in the bottle was in contact with air, the amount of TDN increased over the 6 months (lots 7–13 in the figure). The concentration of TDN in lots 7 (2017) and 9 (2018) of wine reached the rejection threshold by the time of the second measurement. The concentration of these wines was then 79.5 μg/L (2017) and 76.2 μg/L (2018). Thus, the presence of oxygen in the bottle also has an effect on TDN formation (and other deleterious processes in wine, which are not the subject of this research) by promoting the degradation of the precursor carotenoid compounds. Even brief exposure of wine in the bottle to air can result in an increase in TDN. The acidity and pH of the wine as a matrix, in addition to the oxygen in the air, promote the release of TDN from the precursor molecules. Wine 14 had a TDN concentration of only 0.2 μg/L at the time of both measurements, yet the sensory assessors gave this wine a high score, indicating that it was considered to have a strong kerosene taste. This may have been due to the combined confounding effect of the relatively high total sulfuric acid content (170 mg/L) and alcohol content (15.5% by volume).

In Kéknyelű wines, we have found that this variety is not prone to TDN formation. All but one sample of the lots tested had TDN levels close to the detection limit. In the case of the 2012 batch, the TDN concentration (5.2 μg/L) is also just above the DT.

Six of the wine samples were made during different vintages at the same vinery. As the samples came from the same winery, difference in technology can be excluded. The older vintages showed higher levels of TDN than the younger vintages. In the context of the effect of climate on TDN formation, Rapp found that TDN is only detectable in Riesling wines produced in cooler climate zones after four to six years of bottle aging. In wine-producing regions with hot climates, TDN can be formed much earlier and in much larger quantities.15 This finding is consistent with the TDN concentrations in the wines we studied. The TDN concentrations of the 6 wines from the same wineries studied show a decreasing trend. We measured the highest TDN concentration (4.9 μg/L) in the 2010 vintage wine and the lowest (0.2 μg/L) in the 2020 vintage wine (Figure 8).

The aim of the work was to investigate the analytical and organoleptic characteristics of the kerosene in Hungarian Rhine Riesling and Italian Riesling wines of different vintages and origins. A further aim was to compare the varieties in terms of which Riesling variety has a higher concentration of TDN. An ancient Hungarian grape variety, Kéknyelű, was also included in the study to assess whether the Hungarian varieties could be characterized by a kerosene taste and whether TDN concentrations could be measured at the same concentration as in wines made from Riesling varieties. In the present work, only the free TDN concentration was considered.

It is important to mention that no previous results on TDN concentration of Hungarian Rhine Riesling, Italian Riesling, and Kéknyelű wines have been published. The wines studied were average wines in terms of other analytical parameters (alcohol content, residual sugar, titratable acidity, and pH).

European Riesling wines generally contain between 1 and 50 μg/L of TDN.7 The TDN concentrations in the Hungarian wines we studied were comparable to these values. The results clearly showed that it is relevant to address the issue of the petrol character of Riesling wines in Hungary. It can be said that after two to four years of storage, TDN concentrations reached or even exceeded the detection threshold (ODT: 2 μg/L7 and DT: 4 μg/L4), but concentrations rejected by consumers (RejT: 71–82 μg/L) were measured in only a few cases (Figure 6). These individual outliers above 50 μg/L were even on par with wines from significantly warmer producing countries, such as Australia, in international comparisons.

Comparison showed that the tested grape varieties demonstrated different abilities to produce TDN (Figure 3). In the wine of the Hungarian variety Kéknyelű, which was tested in addition to Italian Riesling and Rhine Riesling, TDN concentrations did not even reach the detection threshold, except for one. The results of the total TDN determination confirm the TDN formation tendency of Hungarian Riesling and Italian Riesling.

In 6 bottles of Rhine Riesling wines from the same winery of different vintages, it was observed that the TDN concentration was higher in the wines after 4–6 years of aging. The oldest vintage had the highest TDN content, while the youngest wine had the lowest.

To our knowledge, this is the first publication that has studied the TDN content of Hungarian Riesling wines (Italian Riesling and Rhine Riesling) and a Hungarian variety, Kéknyelű. The study, which includes 39 wines from 2010 to 2020, shows that kerosene traits are common in wines of Hungarian Italian Riesling and Rhine Riesling varieties but do not differ from the European average. Of all the wines tested, 44% reached the detection threshold for TDN as established in the literature. The threat of global warming may increase the formation of TDN in Hungary, both in wines of Riesling varieties and in wines of other grape varieties. As climate change and warming will increase the potential for TDN formation during winemaking and wine storage, viticultural and oenological measures to reduce TDN formation and release should be investigated and implemented in order to avoid a higher risk of consumer rejection of Riesling wines in the future.
