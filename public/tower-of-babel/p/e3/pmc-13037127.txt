Ferroptosis-autophagy crosstalk in bladder cancer: mechanisms and therapeutic implications
Authors: Youzhi Wang, Ning Wu, Shudong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBladder cancer (BC) continues to be a prevalent malignancy within the urinary tract, characterized by high rates of recurrence, metastatic progression, and resistance to therapy, highlighting the importance of developing treatments that target regulated cell death pathways. Ferroptosis is a regulated form of cell death that depends on iron and is caused by excessive lipid peroxidation, whereas autophagy is a conserved catabolic process that can either buffer cellular stress or contribute to cell demise depending on context. Emerging evidence indicates that ferroptosis and autophagy intersect through shared metabolic and signaling nodes, including iron handling, glutathione and lipid metabolism, and stress-response pathways. In this narrative review, we summarize bladder-cancer-specific studies linking ferroptosis and autophagy, integrate mechanistic insights with evidence from patient cohorts and public datasets, and discuss translational opportunities and limitations for targeting this crosstalk in BC.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-026-02608-7.Keywords: BC, Ferroptosis, Autophagy

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC) is the tenth most prevalent type of cancer globally, and around 573,000 newly diagnosed cases and 212,536 fatalities recorded each year [1–3]. It has a higher prevalence among males, and its incidence increases with age [4–6]. Notably, more than 90% of cases originate from the transitional epithelium [7–9]. Cisplatin has served as a fundamental component in BC treatment for over four decades; however, only about half of patients are eligible for cisplatin-based therapies [10–12]. The emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has transformed the treatment paradigm for advanced BC, providing efficacy and manageable safety profiles [13–16]. Nevertheless, there remains an unmet need for novel therapeutic alternatives to address the diverse needs of BC patients [17–19].

Ferroptosis, introduced by Stockwell and colleagues in 2012, refers to a regulated form of cell death that is triggered by an excess of lipid peroxides on cellular membranes and is dependent on the presence of iron [20–22]. In contrast to apoptosis, ferroptotic cells do not exhibit the typical characteristics of apoptosis; instead, they present with smaller mitochondria that have reduced cristae [23–26]. The key distinguishing feature is the lethal accumulation of lipid peroxides when the mechanisms promoting ferroptosis surpass the cellular antioxidant defenses [27–29]. This characteristic differentiates ferroptosis rely on specific proteins for execution [30, 31]. Experimental research has confirmed the potential for developing innovative therapeutic strategies against cancer by targeting the associated genes [32–34]. Autophagy, a conserved cellular degradation process that involves the formation of autophagosomes [35–37]. Its regulation is precisely controlled through specific signaling events, and this complex process depends on autophagy—related (ATG) genes [38, 39]. Although autophagy shares the commonality of being a mode of cell death with ferroptosis, the intrinsic relationship between autophagy and ferroptosis remains unclear. Therefore, further exploration is required to improve treatment options [40].

This study aims to examine the correlation between ferroptosis and the progression of BC and to explore the interaction of signaling pathways related to ferroptosis and autophagy. Moreover, potential strategies for overcoming resistance in advanced BC by targeting these mechanisms will be deliberated.

Autophagy in BC

Beyond its canonical role in cellular quality control, autophagy has been increasingly implicated in BC pathobiology [41–43]. Studies in BC models suggest that autophagy is frequently engaged as an adaptive response to oxidative stress, metabolic rewiring, and therapeutic pressure, supporting tumor cell survival and drug tolerance; in other contexts, excessive or dysregulated autophagy can facilitate cell death and enhance treatment responses [44–47]. Autophagy regulation in BC has been shown to involve multiple signaling axes, including nutrient- and energy-sensing pathways, stress-responsive regulators, and redox-associated transcriptional programs [48–50]. Key contributors include PI3K-AKT-mTOR and AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling, p53 and HMGB1-mediated stress responses, as well as NRF2-driven antioxidant networks [51–53]. Notably, several of these regulators overlap with canonical ferroptosis checkpoints (e.g., iron storage/handling, the glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) axis, and lipid peroxidation control), providing a rationale for considering ferroptosis-autophagy crosstalk as a coordinated stress-response network in BC rather than as two isolated processes [54–56].

Metabolic and molecular regulators of ferroptosis in BC

A characteristic feature of ferroptosis is its capacity to propagate sequentially from one cell to another [57, 58]. Electron microscopy enables the observation of mitochondrial morphological abnormalities during ferroptosis, which shows alterations in size, density, and the disruption of the outer membrane [59, 60]. Metabolic processes related to iron, glutathione, and lipids are crucial in initiating ferroptosis [61]. The subsequent discussion will explore the metabolic perspective to investigate the relationship between ferroptosis and BC (Fig. 1).

Metabolic pathways implicated in ferroptosis in BC. Ferroptosis is mainly caused by iron-related lipid peroxidation, and the absorption, storage, utilization and export of iron in iron metabolism will affect ferroptosis. STAMP1-mediated ferric iron reduction can increase labile iron pools to sensitize cells to ferroptosis via the Fenton response. The classical inhibitory pathway of ferritin metabolism is mainly uptake of Cys by the cystine-glutamate antiporter (system xc −), which is used for the synthesis of GSH. GPX4 utilizes GSH as a cofactor to reduce lipid hydroperoxides to lipid alcohols. In addition, the activation of ACSL4 and lncRNA RP11-89/miR-129-5p/PROM2 in the lipid metabolism pathway can promote the peroxidation of PUFA and induce ferroptosis. Conversely, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is able to promote Fin56, bupivacaine and FGFR3/SCD1-mediated MUFA formation and convert MUFAs to their acyl-CoA esters under the action of ACSL3 for incorporation into membrane phospholipids, thereby protecting cancer cells from ferroptosis

Iron metabolism in BC

Cells obtain iron through transferrin-mediated and lactoferrin-mediated uptake mechanisms [62, 63]. Once these proteins bind to iron, they enter cells via the corresponding receptor/non-receptor pathways. In BC, the upregulation of the transferrin receptor (TFR) promotes the endocytic uptake of iron-bound transferrin, thereby enhancing iron acquisition for cancer cell metabolism and growth [64]. TFR, which is a downstream target of the MYC oncogene, acts as an indicator of the MYC gene status [65]. Imaging techniques, such as positron emission tomography (PET) using a TFR-specific radiotracer and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) that amplifies TFR with specific promoters, have been effective in detecting abnormal oncogene signals in BC precursors [66].

In BC cells, internalized transferrin is localized throughout the cytoplasm and extended cellular processes, differing significantly from its distribution in non-malignant cells [67]. This distinct spatial pattern suggests that malignant cells have evolved to meet elevated iron demands [68]. Crucially, the V-ATPase-mediated release of iron from endosomal compartments plays a vital role in this process [69]. Moreover, the protein six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of the prostate 1 (STEAP1), which participates in iron uptake. Targeting STEAP1 has demonstrated efficacy in T cell-based immunotherapy for BC [70].

Ferroportin (FPN) functions as the principal transporter for cellular iron export [71]. However, in BC, the expression of FPN is down-regulated [72]. Regulating intracellular iron levels via the modulation of FPN expression can inhibit the proliferation of BC cells [73]. Hepcidin, which regulates the activity of FPN, shows increased levels in BC [74]. It is hypothesized that hepcidin may act through autocrine mechanisms to reduce iron excretion rates. Maf bZIP transcription factor G (MAFG), has been associated with the suppression of ferroptosis in BC cells [75]. Baicalin and talaroconvolutin-A can induce ferroptosis in BC. The former acts by targeting ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1). Abietic acid selectively induces ferroptosis in BC cells, and it demonstrates chemotherapeutic agents and inhibits tumor growth in vivo [76].

Glutathione metabolism in BC

The liver synthesizes glutathione (GSH), a principal antioxidant that consists of cysteine, glutamate, and glycine. A reduction in GSH levels leads to a decline in GPX4 activity, thereby accelerating ferroptosis [77]. Cancer cells, including those in BC, experience heightened oxidative stress induced by reactive oxygen species (ROS) [78]. These cells depend on the antioxidant properties of GSH for their proliferation and growth. In studies where cysteine was depleted using cysteinase in mice with BC xenografts, a significant inhibition of tumor growth was observed [79]. Cysteine, a critical precursor for GSH production, is primarily obtained via the system xc-, a transporter that facilitates cystine import in exchange for glutamate export [80]. This transport system consists of two subunits: solute carrier family 3 member 2 (SLC3A2) and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), where SLC7A11 directly mediates the reciprocal transport of cystine and glutamate across the cell membrane [81, 82].

Phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH), frequently overexpressed in BC patients, modulates the expression of SLC7A11. MAFG, known to inhibit ferroptosis in BC cells, binds to Poly(rC)-binding protein 2 (PCBP2) to enhance the stability of SLC7A11 mRNA [83]. Downregulation of epithelial membrane protein 1 (EMP1) promotes tumor metastasis by increasing peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARG) levels, activating phosphorylated FAK at tyrosine 397 (pFAK Y397), and upregulating SLC7A11 [84]. Additionally, heat-shock protein family A member 5 (HSPA5) suppresses ferroptosis through the p53/SLC7A11/GPX4 signaling axis [85]. P53 activates arachidonate 15-lipoxygenase (ALOX15B), thereby inducing ferroptosis in BC cells [86]. In cisplatin-resistant BC cells, miRNA—27a targets SLC7A11, which contributes to resistance [87].

The antioxidant enzyme GPX4 inhibits iron-mediated peroxidation, protecting cell membranes [88]. Fin56 induces ferroptosis and autophagy in BC cells by promoting GPX4 degradation [89]. Proteasome non-ATPase regulatory subunit 14 (PSMD14), highly expressed in BC tissues, influences GPX4 expression [90]. In BC, bupivacaine enhances Fe2 + and ROS levels while suppressing xCT and GPX4. Glutathione transferase zeta1 (GSTZ1) overexpression decreases GPX4 and GSH, activating ferroptosis in BC cells [91]. Evodiamine (EVO) inhibits BC cell growth by reducing GPX4 levels. Staphylococcal nuclease domain containing 1 (SND1) stabilizes GPX4 mRNA, and APS inhibits BC progression by reducing GPX4 expression.

The intricate network of molecular mechanisms, including GSH synthesis, cystine transport, and GPX4 regulation, underscores the dynamic interplay between ferroptosis and BC progression [92].

Lipid metabolism in BC

Phospholipids within cellular membranes, which contain polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), are highly prone to lipid peroxidation, a crucial process in ferroptosis [93, 94]. The content of PUFA in cell membranes serves as a significant determinant of ferroptosis susceptibility [95, 96]. In contrast, competitive inhibition of PUFA-associated ferroptosis can be accomplished by modulating cell membrane components [97–99].

Fibronectin leucine-rich transmembrane protein 2 (FLRT2) has been associated with the modulation of multiple tumor processes, where it upregulates ACSL4 expression, thereby increasing lipid peroxidation and sensitizing human BC cells to ferroptosis [100–102]. Conversely, fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) activates stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1), contributing to the progression of bladder tumors [103–105]. Additionally, the long non-coding RNA RP11-89 promotes tumorigenesis and confers resistance to ferroptosis in BC by acting as a sponge for microRNA-129-5p (miR-129-5p), which in turn alleviates miR-129-5p–mediated suppression of key ferroptosis regulators such as ACSL4 and prominin-2 (PROM2). By modulating these targets, miR-129-5p plays a pivotal role in controlling ferroptotic cell death [106–108].

The combination of Fin56 and Torin 2, a highly effective mTOR inhibitor that induces autophagy, demonstrates synergistic cytotoxicity against BC cells, thereby enhancing the potential for therapeutic intervention [109–111]. Bupivacaine suppresses the growth of BC, induces apoptosis and ferroptosis, and inhibits the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in xenografted tumors [112, 113]. The intricate interplay of these molecular mechanisms emphasizes the multifaceted strategies for modulating ferroptosis in BC [114].

Predicted targets associated with ferroptosis

Beyond protein-coding genes, increasing evidence suggests that RNA-mediated regulation represents an important layer of control over ferroptosis-related pathways in BC [115]. In addition to lncRNAs, multiple classes of RNA molecules, including miRNAs, circular RNAs (circRNAs), and messenger RNAs (mRNAs), have been implicated in the regulation of redox homeostasis, lipid peroxidation, and iron metabolism, which are central processes governing ferroptotic cell death [116–118].

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are currently the most extensively studied RNA regulators of ferroptosis in BC [119–121]. Several lncRNAs have been reported to influence ferroptosis-related processes by modulating antioxidant defenses, iron homeostasis, or lipid metabolism, thereby affecting tumor cell sensitivity to oxidative stress [122–125]. However, it should be noted that most available evidence is derived from in vitro experimental models, and the functional relevance of many lncRNAs in clinical BC specimens remains to be further validated [126, 127]. Given the growing association between ferroptosis and BC, statistical analyses on screened targets and their correlation predictions are crucial [128]. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) also play an important role in ferroptosis regulation by targeting key mRNAs involved in lipid metabolism, redox balance, and iron handling [134, 135]. In BC–related studies, dysregulated miRNAs have been associated with altered expression of ferroptosis-related genes, suggesting that post-transcriptional regulation may contribute to the fine-tuning of ferroptotic susceptibility [136, 137]. At the mRNA level, transcriptomic analyses and public datasets such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) have identified multiple ferroptosis-associated genes whose expression patterns correlate with disease progression and patient outcome, highlighting the potential relevance of RNA-level regulation in this context [138, 139].

Emerging evidence further suggests that circRNAs may participate in ferroptosis regulation, primarily through interactions with miRNAs or RNA-binding proteins [140, 141]. Although direct experimental evidence for circRNA-mediated ferroptosis regulation in BC remains limited, mechanistic insights from other cancer types indicate that circRNAs may act as important regulatory nodes, warranting further investigation in BC–specific settings [142, 143].

Collectively, these observations support the concept that competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks may serve as integrative regulatory frameworks linking lncRNAs, circRNAs, miRNAs, and mRNAs in the control of ferroptosis-related pathways [144]. Rather than functioning in isolation, different RNA species may cooperatively modulate ferroptotic responses by converging on shared molecular targets. Nevertheless, most proposed ceRNA interactions are based on computational predictions or limited experimental validation, underscoring the need for systematic, context-specific studies to delineate functional RNA regulatory networks in BC [145].

Core signaling intersections between ferroptosis and autophagy in BC

PI3K/AKT/mTOR and autophagy

Autophagy is a highly conserved and tightly regulated process, mediated by a core set of autophagy-related proteins [146, 147]. In yeast, approximately 40 essential Atg proteins have been identified, whereas mammalian cells harbor a more complex and expanded autophagic machinery [148, 149]. In human cells, autophagy initiation is orchestrated by the ULK1 kinase complex, which is functionally analogous to the yeast Atg1 complex [150]. ULK1 activity is dynamically controlled by nutrient- and energy-sensing pathways, primarily through mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) and AMPK [151]. Under nutrient-rich conditions, activated mTORC1 suppresses autophagy by phosphorylating ULK1 and ATG13, thereby attenuating ULK1 kinase activity [152]. In contrast, inhibition of mTORC1 or activation of AMPK promotes ULK1 activation and autophagy induction [153]. Following ULK1 activation, downstream events facilitate autophagosome nucleation through the engagement of Beclin 1 (BECN1)–VPS34 lipid kinase complexes. ULK1-mediated phosphorylation of BECN1 enhances the stability and activity of ATG14L-containing VPS34 complexes, promoting phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate production at nascent autophagic membranes. In addition, the exocyst component Exo84 has been shown to interact with RalB, providing a scaffold that recruits ULK1 and catalytically active BECN1–VPS34 complexes, thereby linking membrane trafficking machinery to autophagosome formation [154].

In cancer cells, autophagy can be activated through a wide array of oncogenic and stress-responsive signaling pathways, reflecting its context-dependent role in tumor biology [155]. These pathways include Notch1/Hes-1, JNK/c-Jun, ERK-AKT-mTOR-STAT3-Notch signaling, Hedgehog signaling, AMPK-mTOR-p70S6K, EGFR–RAS–MEK–ERK, p38 MAPK, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways [156]. Among them, the PI3K-AKT-mTOR axis represents one of the most extensively studied regulatory hubs, functioning both as a major suppressor of autophagy and as a therapeutic target for autophagy modulation in cancer. Collectively, these interconnected signaling networks underscore the complex and multilayered regulation of autophagy in malignant cells [157].

The impact of three metabolic pathways on ferroptosis in BC is visually presented in Fig. 1. A comprehensive analysis of these pathways and their targets indicates that the regulation of autophagy within the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway involves multiple targets that influence metabolism during ferroptosis (Fig. 3). Inhibition of PHGDH suppresses the invasion and migration of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) cells and affects PDAC autophagy via the AMPK-Beclin 1 pathway [158, 159]. Arginylation and subsequent ubiquitination of HSPA5 lead to the degradation of AKT through autophagy flux [160]. Endogenous HMGB1 interacts with Beclin 1, displacing Bcl-2 and regulating autophagy. Knockout of HMGB1 reduces the expression of microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3-II (LC3-II) and Beclin 1 in BC, suggesting decreased autophagy [161]. TGF-β1 mediates its atrophic effect on denervated skeletal muscle through the HMGB1/autophagy pathway. The activator MUL1 promotes the lysosomal degradation of AKT via R-HSPA5-induced autophagy. Bupivacaine upregulates the expression of microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3B-II (LC3B-II)/microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3B-I (LC3B-I) and Beclin-1, downregulates sequestosome 1 (p62), and attenuates the phosphorylation of AKT and mammalian target of mTOR in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells [162].

In glioblastoma (GBM) cells, silencing E-twenty-six-specific sequence variant 4 (ETV4) leads to inhibition of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascade, which in turn enhances autophagy and apoptosis by decreasing transcriptional activation of EMP1 [163]. Conversely, miR-129-5p attenuates autophagy and apoptosis in H2O2-treated H9c2 cells by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and downregulating ATG14 expression. In gallbladder cancer, the inhibition of exportin 1 (XPO1) using KPT-330 activates the p53/mTOR pathway, leading to autophagy-dependent apoptosis. Extracellular vesicles derived from rheumatoid arthritis (RA)-associated fibroblast-like synoviocytes target the p53/mTOR signaling pathway in vascular endothelial cells via miRNA-1972 to promote angiogenesis [164]. The downregulation of functional p53 attenuates non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) by promoting HMGB1-mediated autophagy induction. AMPK regulates autophagy and exerts an impact on mTOR, playing a crucial role in autophagy regulation. Betulinic acid induces apoptosis through autophagy in human BC cells via the Bmi-1/ROS/AMPK-mTOR-ULK1 pathway [165]. Vitamin K2 enhances glycolysis, resulting in AMPK-dependent cell death through autophagy in BC cells. Sodium butyrate inhibits migration and induces autophagy that is dependent on the AMPK-mTOR pathway, accompanied by ROS-mediated apoptosis regulated by the miR-139-5p/Bmi-1 axis in human BC cells [166].

Other pathways that regulate autophagy and ferroptosis

In addition to the well-established PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, there is a close correlation between the regulation of autophagy and targets implicated in the metabolic pathway of ferroptosis in BC (Fig. 3).

Linagliptin, an inhibitor of DPP4, restores glomerular autophagy in db/db mice, a well-established model of type 2 diabetes [167–169]. Similarly, sitagliptin, another member of the DPP4 inhibitor class, exerts protective effects in acute lung injury associated with severe acute pancreatitis by activating the p62-Keap1-Nrf2 signaling cascade, which reduces oxidative damage and normalizes dysregulated autophagy [170–172]. Additionally, gemigliptin alleviates nonalcoholic steatohepatitis through autophagy pathways involving ULK1, independent of AMP-activated protein kinase activation [173–175]. Mutations in HSPB1 that lead to neuropathy impair autophagy by interfering with the assembly of SQSTM1/p62 aggregates [176, 177]. Furthermore, the P2RX7-MAPK1/2-SP1 signaling axis suppresses HSPB1-dependent astroglial autophagy in an MTOR-independent manner [178, 179]. PCBP2 stabilizes glutathione Peroxidase 2 (GPX2) to induce autophagy, which safeguards Het-1A esophageal cells against apoptosis and inflammation [180, 181]. Concanamycin A, a V-ATPase inhibitor, obstructs lysosomal acidification, thus hindering the degradation of autophagic bodies [182, 183].

The activation of the p62-Keap1-Nrf2 pathway via autophagy inhibition safeguards HepG2 cells against alcohol-induced ferroptosis [184, 185]. The suppression of autophagy elevates p62 expression, which promotes Nrf2 translocation into the nucleus and upregulates FTH, FPN, and HO1 [186, 187]. CQ inhibits autophagy in liver cancer stem cells in response to metabolic changes induced by desferrioxamine (DFO) administration [188, 189]. Exposure to DFO increases the phosphorylation of AMPK in p53S/S cells [190]. TRAF6 suppresses ZEB1 expression, thereby inhibiting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through catenin beta 1 (CTNNB1) [191]. Silencing ATG7 mitigates the effect, suggesting the involvement of autophagy in TRAF6-mediated EMT [192]. LncRNA MAFG-AS1 contributes to breast cancer development by modulating autophagy through its interaction with miR-3612 and FKBP4 [193]. Autophagy facilitates ferroptosis triggered by Fin56 via the degradation of GPX4, thereby enhancing the cytotoxic effects of mTOR inhibition in breast cancer cells [194]. Additionally, a protein complex involving short coiled-coil protein (SCOC), fasciculation and elongation protein zeta 1 (FEZ1), ULK1, and the autophagy receptor NBR1 has been shown to associate with miR-129-5p [195]. Overexpression of miR-129-5p leads to decreased expression of key autophagy-related proteins. Notably, activation of miR-129-5p stimulates autophagy, which in turn alleviates hypoxia-induced apoptosis in rat H9c2 cardiomyocytes [196].

Importantly, the tables summarized here do not represent isolated observations, but instead reveal recurring regulatory patterns linking ferroptosis and autophagy. Many of the listed genes and compounds influence shared biological processes, including redox homeostasis, lipid metabolism, and lysosomal function, suggesting that ferroptotic and autophagic responses are often coordinately regulated rather than acting independently [266]. In BC, such coordinated regulation may play a critical role in tumor cell adaptation to metabolic and oxidative stress, thereby affecting disease progression and therapeutic responsiveness. Nevertheless, it should be noted that much of the current evidence is derived from in vitro experiments or preclinical models, underscoring the need for further validation in clinically relevant bladder cancer settings [267].

Challenges for clinical translation

Several limitations of the current literature and of this review should be acknowledged. Despite the increasing number of compounds reported to regulate autophagy in BC, several major challenges remain that may limit their clinical translation. These challenges include inefficient drug delivery, limited targeting specificity, potential off-target toxicity, and inconsistent therapeutic efficacy across heterogeneous tumor contexts. Many autophagy-modulating agents exert pleiotropic effects, raising concerns about unintended disruption of physiological autophagy in normal tissues and associated safety risks.

Moreover, the context-dependent role of autophagy—as either a cytoprotective or pro-death mechanism—adds an additional layer of complexity to therapeutic targeting. Without reliable biomarkers to guide patient stratification, indiscriminate activation or inhibition of autophagy may lead to variable or even opposing outcomes. Together, these considerations highlight the need for improved delivery strategies, more selective modulators, and rational combination approaches when developing autophagy-based therapies for BC.

Future research directions

Future research should increasingly view ferroptosis and autophagy as interconnected stress-response pathways rather than independent cell-death mechanisms. A key priority will be to clarify the context-dependent roles of autophagy during ferroptosis in BC, particularly the molecular conditions under which autophagy shifts from a cytoprotective response to a pro-death process. Defining these conditions will be essential for the rational design of therapeutic strategies targeting ferroptosis and autophagy crosstalk.

From a translational perspective, future studies should move beyond single-pathway modulation and explore strategies that simultaneously target both ferroptosis and autophagy. The development of dual-pathway modulators, together with biomarker-guided patient stratification based on molecular subtypes, may help identify subsets of bladder cancer most likely to benefit from such interventions. In parallel, the establishment of clinically relevant experimental models, including patient-derived organoids and immune-competent systems, as well as prospective validation in clinical settings, will be critical for translating mechanistic insights into effective and safe therapeutic applications.

Conclusions

The investigation of the metabolic pathways related to ferroptosis in various cancer types is a rapidly evolving and extensive research area. This review offers a comprehensive overview of the diverse metabolic pathways that affect ferroptosis in BC by concentrating on their relevant targets. Within the framework of BC's ferroptosis metabolic pathway, several interrelated regulatory metabolic targets, such as HSPB1, PCBP2, Fin56, multiple lncRNAs, and pharmaceutical agents, have been identified. Moreover, there is substantial interest in employing a gene-based molecular typing model to predict prognosis and assess drug resistance associated with ferroptosis. Through the analysis of ferroptosis gene data obtained from BC patients in TCGA and GEO databases, a risk score model has been developed that can effectively predict prognosis, levels of immune infiltration, and molecular target species in BC. Additionally, it is remarkable that nine genes exhibited the highest duplication rate.

Owing to the intrinsic resistance of cancers to programmed cell death, there is an increasing interest in employing non-apoptotic regulated cell death (RCD) as an innovative strategy for cancer treatment. Recent studies have clarified the complex interaction among different non-apoptotic RCD mechanisms in combating tumors. As a result, targeted therapies that either induce or inhibit ferroptosis and autophagy possess considerable potential to exert potent antitumor effects. Remarkably, a significant overlap has been detected between autophagy and the associated targets of ferroptosis. This overlap involves various metabolic pathways and predicted targets related to ferroptosis, such as the PI3K/AKT/mTOR, miRNA, lncRNA, AMPK, GPX4, HO1, and other signaling pathways. These findings imply that the simultaneous regulation of both ferroptosis and autophagy pathways may have significant implications for the treatment of BC. Further research on ferroptosis and autophagy, combined with rigorous clinical trials, is essential to deepen our understanding of these vital processes and rationally manipulate their activity during BC.
