Spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) is a severe medical emergency that affects patients across all ages, but its frequency and etiology differ substantially between adults and children. Adults most commonly experience spontaneous ICH due to hypertension, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, and anticoagulation therapy.1 In contrast, pediatric spontaneous ICH often reflects structural or developmental pathology as opposed to degenerative disease, and it is approximately 30 times less common in children than in adults.1–3

Large studies have shown that vascular malformations account for approximately 78% of pediatric spontaneous ICH cases, making them the most common etiology.4 Additional contributors include coagulopathies, hematological disorders, and neoplasms.4 Despite comprehensive neuroimaging, angiographic, and hematological evaluation, up to 20% of pediatric spontaneous ICH cases can remain unexplained and are often written off as idiopathic.5

In these unexplained cases, genetic small-vessel diseases affecting the structural integrity of type IV collagen represent an important diagnostic category. Type IV collagen is the principal structural protein of the vascular basement membrane, forming a supportive meshwork that provides stability and mechanical strength to small blood vessels.6 Mutations in COL4A1 and COL4A2 are well-established risk factors for childhood spontaneous ICH and porencephaly due to basement-membrane fragility.7,8 These genes encode essential structural components of type IV collagen, and pathogenic variants disrupt collagen assembly, weakening the basement membrane and predisposing small vessels to rupture.7,8 These collagenopathies typically manifest during the perinatal or early childhood periods.7

By comparison, COLGALT1-related pathology is exceptionally uncommon. Biallelic variants in the COLGALT1 gene, which encodes collagen β(1-O)-galactosyltransferase 1, have been reported in only a small number of patients with congenital cerebral small-vessel disease characterized by developmental delay, epilepsy, and periventricular hemorrhagic infarction.9 Unlike COL4A1 and COL4A2 pathologies, which directly disrupt the structural composition of type IV collagen, COLGALT1 is required for its posttranslational glycosylation—an essential step for proper folding and assembly of the collagen type IV network.9 Loss of this enzymatic function results in underprocessed collagen and secondary basement-membrane weakness, similarly predisposing small vessels to rupture.9 The clinical spectrum of COLGALT1-related disease remains extremely limited.9–11

Here, we present the case of an adolescent female with a spontaneous atraumatic basal ganglia hemorrhage with intraventricular extension, unrevealing angiography, and an unrevealing hematological evaluation, occurring in the setting of a known biallelic COLGALT1 variant.

The goal of this report is to expand the clinical spectrum of COLGALT1-associated disease and underscore the importance of considering genetic etiologies in pediatric patients who require urgent neurosurgical management for spontaneous ICH without identifiable vascular or hematological causes.

Illustrative Case

A 14-year-old female with a complex past medical history of spastic cerebral palsy, microcephaly, global developmental delay, Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, and a documented biallelic COLGALT1 variant (c.1051C>T; p.Arg351Trp) presented to an outside hospital after several episodes of emesis followed by a prolonged seizure without return to neurological baseline. Both parents were also confirmed to be genetic carriers of the pathogenic allele. At the outside hospital emergency department, she had a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 8 and was intubated. CT of the head without contrast revealed a left basal ganglia intraparenchymal hemorrhage with extensive intraventricular extension and obstructive hydrocephalus (Fig. 1). She was also noted to be COVID-19 positive. The patient was transferred to a pediatric tertiary care center for definitive neurosurgical management.

A–F: Sequential noncontrast axial CT images of the head demonstrating a large left basal ganglia intraparenchymal hemorrhage involving deep brain structures with blood layering throughout the lateral ventricles, resulting in obstructive hydrocephalus and associated mass effect.

On arrival, she was sedated and mechanically ventilated. Her pupils were equal, round, and reactive to light, and she withdrew all extremities to noxious stimuli without new focal asymmetry compared to her known baseline deficits, indicating salvageable neurological function despite significant mass effect and obstructive hydrocephalus. There was no clonus. Prior to this event, her baseline neurological function included feeding oneself and being awake, alert, smiling, and interactive. She was inconsistently verbal and had a limited vocabulary, but she was able to use an augmentative and alternative communication application on a tablet to communicate with family members. Her motor function included right-sided antigravity strength, left-sided hypertonia and weakness, and a nonambulatory status.

Given her examination and the extent of mass effect present on imaging, she was deemed a candidate for emergency left decompressive hemicraniectomy (DHC) and right external ventricular drain (EVD) placement. Because the etiology of the hemorrhage was unknown at the time of the surgery, and a macrovascular lesion had not been ruled out, the intraparenchymal hematoma was not aggressively evacuated. The operative strategy prioritized safe decompression and CSF diversion while avoiding manipulation that could precipitate rebleeding from a potential vascular lesion. Postoperative CT demonstrated the expected decompression and appropriate EVD positioning (Fig. 2).

Postoperative noncontrast axial CT image of the head demonstrating the left DHC with relief of mass effect and appropriate positioning of a right frontal EVD within the lateral ventricle.

Postoperatively, she was admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) for neurocritical care and continuous EEG monitoring. Continuous EEG monitoring demonstrated diffuse slowing with intermittent epileptiform discharges and one focal motor seizure, which was managed with optimization of her antiseizure regimen. Epileptic activity stabilized in the ensuing days, and continuous EEG monitoring was discontinued on postoperative day (POD) 7. Intracranial pressures (ICPs) remained stable at 7–18 mm Hg, with the EVD set at 15 cm H2O, and CSF output ranged from approximately 50 to 200 mL/day.

In the absence of an immediately identifiable cause of hemorrhage, an extensive postoperative workup was undertaken. Serial neuroimaging demonstrated stable intraparenchymal hematoma morphology with gradual clearance of intraventricular blood. A left-sided subdural hygroma developed around the time of EVD removal. It displayed enlargement on interval imaging and was managed electively at the time of subsequent cranioplasty without associated neurological decline (Fig. 3). Digital subtraction angiography and MR angiography showed no aneurysm, arteriovenous malformation, or other macrovascular lesion (Fig. 4). Hematological studies, including coagulation profile, fibrinogen, and platelet function analyses, were unremarkable. Multiple CSF samples obtained from the EVD showed normal indices and repeatedly negative cultures. Despite persistent fevers and leukocytosis, blood and respiratory cultures remained negative. A concurrent Escherichia coli urinary tract infection was treated with antibiotics, and COVID-19 testing remained positive throughout her PICU course.

Over the ensuing PODs, the patient demonstrated gradual neurological improvement. She was successfully extubated on POD 6 and began following simple commands and engaging with family. After tolerating a clamp trial with stable ICPs, her EVD was removed on POD 12. She was stepped down from the PICU on POD 16 and was discharged to an inpatient rehabilitation facility on POD 18, where she continued to make steady functional gains. Approximately 10 weeks after initial DHC, the patient underwent an elective cranioplasty with a custom implant and was discharged on POD 3. At outpatient follow-up, she had returned to her prehemorrhage neurological baseline in terms of motor function, communication, and behavior. Her only persistent change was the need for continued gastrostomy tube feeding rather than oral intake.

Discussion

Observations

Spontaneous ICH in children remains an uncommon entity, and the diagnostic workup can prove challenging when no angiographic or hematological etiologies are evident. In this case, an adolescent with previously stable neurological function developed an acute deep intraparenchymal hemorrhage with intraventricular extension and obstructive hydrocephalus. Although the patient carried a known COLGALT1 variant, its relevance to spontaneous hemorrhage in later childhood was uncertain at the time of presentation. Accordingly, she underwent a standard, comprehensive evaluation to exclude more common etiologies, as macrovascular lesions are responsible for almost 80% of these conditions.4 In the absence of any alternative explanation, attention turned toward her underlying collagenopathy as a potential contributor to microvascular fragility leading to spontaneous hemorrhage.

To date, COLGALT1-associated disease has been described in a small number of patients across three publications, encompassing a limited clinical spectrum. Miyatake et al. reported 2 pediatric patient cases of COLGALT1-related congenital small-vessel disease, with one child experiencing fatal multifocal intraparenchymal and intraventricular hemorrhages following influenza infection in middle childhood.9 Teunissen et al. described a neonate with severe congenital porencephaly attributed to presumed in utero hemorrhage.10 More recently, an adult patient with COLGALT1 variants was reported with a chronic cerebral small-vessel disease phenotype marked by recurrent ischemic events, leukoencephalopathy, and lacunar infarcts, without large parenchymal hemorrhage or mass effect.11

Previously published cases provide biological and molecular evidence supporting an underlying cerebral microangiopathy. In the pediatric cases reported by Miyatake et al., functional in vitro analyses demonstrated markedly reduced COLGALT1 protein expression and enzymatic activity in patient-derived cells, with impaired collagen type IV processing and secretion, consistent with basement-membrane vulnerability.9 In the other pediatric case, Teunissen et al. identified a homozygous COLGALT1 splice-site variant and confirmed aberrant splicing by reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction and Sanger sequencing, producing a frameshift transcript predicted to substantially reduce COLGALT1 function.10 Collectively, these functional and molecular findings provide mechanistic support for COLGALT1-related microvascular fragility and strengthen the biological plausibility of spontaneous hemorrhagic events in the absence of alternative vascular or hematological etiologies.

Across these reports, hemorrhagic manifestations were either perinatal, multifocal, or limited to microbleeds within a chronic small-vessel disease phenotype. Neurosurgical management was not described in these cases.9–11 In this context, the present case provides clinically relevant insight into how genetic microvascular pathology may inform diagnostic evaluation and operative decision-making when standard vascular and hematological etiologies for pediatric ICH are not identified.12

The operative considerations in this scenario highlight broader principles relevant to the urgent management of deep ICH when the underlying etiology is unknown. Evidence to date has not consistently demonstrated improved functional outcomes with aggressive clot removal compared to decompression with EVD placement alone.13 Accordingly, this conservative approach represents a prudent initial strategy, prioritizing the rapid reversal of mass effect and control of ICP while minimizing disruption of the hematoma cavity in the case of unknown etiology.

Although COLGALT1-related disease alone may have been sufficient to account for the spontaneous hemorrhage, the patient’s concurrent COVID-19 infection is a notable aspect of her presentation. Inflammatory cytokines released during viral infection can impair normal endothelial function, increasing the risk of hemorrhagic events.14 In this patient, elevated C-reactive protein levels support a systemic inflammatory response with potential cytokine-mediated endothelial dysfunction.15 In individuals with structurally normal vasculature, these effects may be limited; however, in patients with comorbidities, such as basement-membrane instability in the present case, systemic inflammation may further destabilize already fragile vasculature.16 To reemphasize, one of the children described in the Miyatake et al. series experienced fatal multifocal hemorrhages in the setting of an influenza infection,9 suggesting that viral illnesses may serve as physiological stressors capable of precipitating or amplifying vascular instability in genetically vulnerable individuals. Cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) is another genetic small-vessel disease with literature relevant to this case. CADASIL mutations disrupt smooth muscle cells in cerebral small vessels, resulting in luminal thickening and predisposing individuals to ischemic events.17 Mizutani et al. described a case of an adult patient who experienced multiple watershed cerebral infarcts in the setting of an influenza infection and was found to harbor a CADASIL mutation.18 While pathophysiologically distinct from the COLGALT1-related disease in the present case, it provides further context into how viral infections may exacerbate cerebral small-vessel disease.

In summary, COLGALT1-related vascular pathology can present in childhood with a discrete, focal ICH, differing from the diffuse or predominantly perinatal hemorrhagic patterns previously described. Although hemorrhagic complications are well recognized in other type IV collagen disorders such as COL4A1 and COL4A2-associated disease, detailed discussions of operative management remain limited across collagen-related small-vessel disorders.19 In this context, the present case provides practical neurosurgical insight by illustrating that COLGALT1-associated pathology may represent a manifestation in patients presenting with spontaneous deep ICH requiring urgent intervention. This reinforces the importance of considering genetic small-vessel etiologies in pediatric patients with unexplained hemorrhage after standard vascular and hematological evaluations are unrevealing.

Lessons

Although limited to a single report, this case illustrates that when pediatric ICH remains unexplained after standard vascular and hematological evaluation, genetic small-vessel disease should be considered in the differential diagnoses. Recognition of these disorders has practical implications for neurosurgeons, as underlying basement-membrane fragility may support a conservative operative approach focused on decompression rather than hematoma evacuation. Genetic diagnoses may also inform family counseling regarding recurrence risk, future surgical considerations, and potential vulnerability during systemic inflammatory stressors such as viral infections.

The patient’s family is grateful for the multidisciplinary care that their child has and continues to receive.
