Factors influencing antenatal care utilisation in Madang Province, Papua New Guinea: a qualitative study
Authors: Elvin Lufele, Sophie Pascoe, Paula Tesine, Alma Auwun, Nalisa Neuendorf, Maria Ome-Kaius, Moses Laman, Kamala Thriemer, John W Bolnga, Stephen J Rogerson, Holger W Unger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntenatal care (ANC) provides an opportunity to prevent or minimise adverse pregnancy outcomes and enhance women’s health by providing essential interventions including malaria prevention measures. In Papua New Guinea (PNG), three quarters of women commence ANC after the first trimester of pregnancy and one in five women receive no ANC. The present qualitative study aimed to explore determinants of ANC utilisation in the context of a clinical trial of intermittent preventative treatment of malaria in pregnancy in PNG.MethodsA cross-sectional qualitative study was conducted at four health facilities in Madang Province, PNG, between 17 April and 6 September 2023. Data were collected through individual in-depth interviews, focus group discussions (FGDs) and observations of ANC proceedings at clinics. Study participants included 79 pregnant and 5 postpartum women, 15 healthcare providers, and 13 representatives of district, provincial and national health authorities involved with the programming of ANC. In-depth interviews and FGDs were audio-recorded and transcribed. Transcript data were coded using deductive and inductive approaches. Emerging themes from interviews and FGDs were triangulated with data from field notes and observations.ResultsWe identified four main themes in relation to ANC utilisation: accessibility of ANC; attitudes towards ANC; interpersonal factors; and timing of ANC. Accessibility of ANC services related to costs associated with ANC, healthcare service delivery challenges, proximity to clinics, and rigid clinic schedules. Attitudes toward ANC, including the perceived benefits and disadvantages, knowledge of ANC interventions and access to education, influenced utilisation. In terms of interpersonal factors, positive relationships and communication between women and healthcare providers, and community or family members participation, were enablers of ANC engagement, while negative healthcare provider attitudes and fear of pregnancy disclosure were identified as potential barriers. Timing of ANC contacts were influenced by personal choice, a desire to avoid many clinic visits, community attitudes, prior pregnancy experiences, distance to health facilities, anticipated travel and clinic costs, attitudes of community members around ANC timing and lack of awareness of pregnancy or gestational age. Most women considered ANC important and recognised the need for ANC to ensure safe pregnancy and delivery.ConclusionsHealthcare benefits of ANC were perceived as an important part of ensuring a safe pregnancy and delivery outcome. ANC initiation and utilisation were enhanced through clinic fee remission, ANC content, and community engagement provided in the context of a clinical trial. These findings highlight opportunities for strategic programmatic investments that could lead to improved ANC utilisation in Madang Province.Keywords: Antenatal care, Malaria, Papua New Guinea, Pregnancy, Qualitative

Background

Globally, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 287,000 women die from pregnancy-related causes each year, and maternal mortality remains a major public health concern [1]. Maternal deaths can be a consequence of direct pregnancy-related causes, such as postpartum haemorrhage, or indirect causes, such as severe malaria. Most maternal deaths in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) primarily occur from direct causes [1], but are often compounded by other comorbidities, such as malaria infection and/or maternal anaemia. The clinical knowledge and antenatal interventions to reduce the incidence of maternal deaths and adverse pregnancy outcomes have existed for a long time. However, some of these services are often not available, not accessible or may not be implemented properly, especially in LMICs [2]. Antenatal care (ANC) is most commonly provided through clinics at health facilities, with the aim to optimise maternal and foetal health well-being and prepare for safe birth [3]. ANC provides a critical opportunity to identify risk factors, establish corrective measures, manage complications, and implement preventative strategies to avoid or reduce maternal and newborn mortality and morbidity [2, 4–6]. To reduce risks and improve outcomes, ANC needs to be available, accessible, acceptable and of good quality and received in a timely and regular manner [4, 5].

The WHO now recommends a minimum of eight ANC contacts, with the first clinic visit to take place in the first trimester of pregnancy (8–12 gestational weeks), and two further visits in the second trimester of pregnancy, at 20 and 26 gestational weeks [7]. WHO guidance emphasises the need for provision of preventative measures, including iron and folate supplementation to prevent anaemia, intermittent preventative treatment in pregnancy (IPTp) for malaria, immunisation against tetanus and tuberculosis, health education on nutrition, screening for chronic diseases, and detecting warning signs of pregnancy-related complications [2, 7–9]. Many of the WHO-recommended interventions delivered as part of ANC, including IPTp, have substantially reduced maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality [2, 10, 11].

Late initiation of ANC may lead to critical delays with the diagnosis of complications and the implementation of preventative measures, such as IPTp for malaria. Additionally, late and poor ANC is associated with higher maternal mortality and morbidity and poor pregnancy outcomes such as low birth weight (< 2500 g), stillbirth, premature labour (< 37 gestational weeks) and foetal growth restriction [8, 12]. Studies from LMICs have reported a range of factors influencing indicators of ANC utilisation, the timing of first ANC contact, and frequency of ANC contacts [13, 14]. Factors identified included maternal education and employment, partner education, family support, cost of clinic visits, household income, history of obstetric complications, media exposure, and previous experiences with the healthcare system [13–17]. In addition, provider-side factors identified from these settings that affect ANC utilisation, including clinic availability, lack of local ANC policy and guidance, user fees, medical supply stock-out, and suboptimal healthcare provider performance [18, 19].

Papua New Guinea (PNG) is an LMIC with one of the highest maternal mortality rates in the Asia-Pacific region, at approximately 200 maternal deaths per 100,000 livebirths [20, 21]. Major causes of maternal deaths in PNG include obstetric haemorrhage, sepsis, venous thromboembolism, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, unsafe abortion and infection [20, 22–24]. Stillbirth and neonatal mortality rates are also high, as is the incidence of low birth weight [20, 25, 26]. Malaria in pregnancy is among the significant causes of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality in PNG [25, 27]. These outcomes are associated with low rates of access to ANC in our study setting [23, 25].

In many LMICs endemic to malaria including PNG, antenatal clinics are the principal platform of distribution of IPTp and insecticide-treated bed nets for the prevention of malaria in pregnancy [28–30]. In these settings, uptake of malaria in pregnancy interventions strongly depends on ANC utilisation, which in turn is influenced by healthcare system, individual, socio-cultural and environmental factors [18, 19]. In PNG, it is estimated that less than a quarter of women initiate ANC in their first trimester and a similar proportion do not attend clinic at all, while only half of women had four or more ANC contacts [17, 31]. Previous studies in PNG have explored factors influencing ANC utilisation [3, 17, 32, 33]. In Madang Province, three principal determinants of timing and frequency of ANC contacts were identified, namely, accessibility of ANC (distance to clinic and cost of clinic visit), attitudes toward ANC (influenced by previous experiences of ANC and perceptions around ANC), and interpersonal factors (fear of pregnancy disclosure, interactions with family members and healthcare providers) [3]. In other areas of PNG, healthcare providers and other stakeholders have identified inadequate staffing and limited resources as negatively influencing ANC utilisation [34, 35].

As PNG is restructuring its health system towards a decentralised model and local governance, provincial health authorities require up-to-date setting-specific data on factors influencing ANC utilisation to inform programmatic decision-making and prioritisation. The present qualitative study draws on the perspectives of women and additionally complements their experiences with experiences from healthcare providers and policy makers, with the aim to identify and characterise current facilitators and barriers of ANC utilisation in Madang Province, PNG, where ANC utilisation continues to be suboptimal. As this study was undertaken in the context of a clinical trial, the findings offer an opportunity to contrast routine care with enhanced ANC services within a research setting, helping to identify key enablers and barriers to ANC utilisation.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional qualitative study was conducted in PNG involving pregnant and postpartum women; healthcare providers including nurses, community health workers, midwives, health extension officers, doctors and administrators; and representatives of district, provincial and national health authorities involved in programmatic aspects of ANC provision. Women were informed about the purpose of the study through information sessions at the clinics. All participants were provided with study information sheets. Participants were invited to take part in the study following provision of written informed consent. Women who were receiving or had recently received ANC at participating health facilities were eligible, along with healthcare providers and representatives of district, provincial and national health authorities involved with the programming or administering of ANC at health facility, district, provincial or national levels.

Study setting

The study was conducted at four health facilities located in the Sumkar and Madang Districts of Madang Province on the northern coast of mainland PNG. These included Madang Provincial Hospital, Town Urban Clinic, Yagaum Rural Hospital, and Mugil Health Centre. The provincial capital, Madang, is a port township in the Momase region of PNG and shares boundaries with the Highlands and New Guinea Islands regions. The province has a population of approximately 600,000, comprised mainly of subsistence farmers who reside in rural areas [36]. In Madang township, people come from many different ethnic and language groups due to migration and intermarriage [3].

ANC in Madang Province is provided by government or faith-based institutions through the provincial hospital, several district or rural hospitals, health centres, community health posts and urban clinics [37, 38]. Community health posts and urban clinics provide outpatient services only, while health centres and district hospitals provide outpatient and basic inpatient services for deliveries and minor ailments requiring observation. The provincial hospital provides more advanced clinical support services including pharmacy, laboratory and radiology [37].

Madang Provincial Hospital and Town Urban Clinic are government-run and located within Madang township (Fig. 1). Mugil Health Centre is managed by the Catholic mission and located approximately 50 km north of Madang township. Yagaum Rural Hospital is operated by the Lutheran mission and located approximately 10 km south of Madang township. Alexishafen Health Centre, operated by the Catholic mission and located 15 km from Madang township, also provides ANC and delivery care, but was closed at the time of the study. At the time of the study, all the above-mentioned health facilities levied once-off charges at ANC initiation, ranging from 1 to 15 PNG Kina (~ 0.25 to 3.5 US$). Unless already available from a previous pregnancy, women were also required to purchase a pregnancy hand-held record (“mama book”, covering all pregnancies), at a cost of ~ 7 PNG Kina (~ 1.6 US$). At Town Urban clinic, initial antenatal clinic visit (“booking visit”) and subsequent antenatal clinic visits were scheduled once a week on the same day, whilst the remaining health facilities scheduled initial and subsequent antenatal clinic visits on different weekdays. All health facilities cater for over 200 antenatal clinic registrations per annum.

Nurses, midwives, community health workers and/or health extension officers run antenatal clinics and attend to deliveries at Mugil Health Centre, Alexishafen Health Centre, Yagaum Rural Hospital and Madang Provincial Hospital. Doctors attend to higher-risk pregnancies at Madang Provincial Hospital. The maternal mortality ratio (363 per 100,000 livebirths) in Madang Province remains high [23, 39]. According to national sector performance annual review, between 2019 and 2023, about a quarter of pregnant women attended at least four ANC contacts, and a quarter had supervised deliveries at health facilities in Madang Province [40]. There is year-round moderate-to-intense transmission of Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax malaria [41], and malaria in pregnancy contributes substantially to adverse maternal and pregnancy outcomes in this setting [27, 42].

Clinical trial context

The qualitative study was nested into activities of a double-blind randomised controlled clinical trial comparing sulphadoxine-pyrimethamine plus dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine compared to sulphadoxine-pyrimethamine for IPTp (NCT05426434 registered on 16 June 2022, referred to as parent trial), as described previously [43]. The recruitment sites of the parent trial included Mugil Health Centre, Alexishafen Health Centre, Town Urban Clinic, Madang Provincial Hospital, and Yagaum Rural Hospital (Fig. 1). Alexishafen Health Centre was temporarily closed when this qualitative study was conducted so was not included in data collection. As part of the parent trial recruitment process, community reporters, who are based within catchment areas of each health facility raised awareness about the trial, assisted unbooked pregnant women to attend clinic, and encouraged women who already enrolled in the clinical trial to re-attend subsequent clinic visits.

All pregnant women presenting at the clinics were then screened for eligibility to enrol in the parent trial. HIV-negative women between 12 and 26 gestational weeks were invited to participate in the trial. Following informed written consent, participants were randomised to take IPTp with sulphadoxine-pyrimethamine plus dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine or sulphadoxine-pyrimethamine [43]. IPTp was given monthly from 16 weeks until delivery and trial participants were followed up until at least six weeks after birth. All pregnant women, irrespective of whether they joined the clinical trial or not, were registered for ANC, in accordance with the PNG national guidelines [29]. Clinical trial staff worked alongside local health facility staff at the clinics. Pregnant women who participated in the trial additionally received antenatal ultrasound, coverage of clinic and delivery fees, medications, and support from community reporters to enable re-attendance at clinics.

Study participants and procedures

Data for this qualitative study was collected between 17 April to 6 September 2023. The study included pregnant women (trial and non-trial participants), women who had recently delivered (trial participants), healthcare providers, and decision-makers, i.e., staff involved in programming ANC (Table 1). Participants were informed about the purpose of the study and provided written informed consent. Women who were receiving or had recently completed ANC and enrolled in the parent trial were invited to participate in in-depth interviews (IDIs) or focus group discussions (FGDs). IDIs were more broadly focused on acceptability of the clinical trial so recruitment was limited to participants enrolled in the trial. Women not enrolled in the parent trial but attending clinics at the above-mentioned health facilities were invited to join FGDs (Fig. 2). One FGD was conducted per health facility. IDIs were also conducted with healthcare providers including nurses, community health workers, midwives, health extension officers, doctors, and administrators. Decision-makers involved in programming ANC services were also invited to participate in IDIs – these included staff working across district, provincial and national health authority levels. Study participants were selected through a combination of purposive and snowballing sampling. In respect of local gender norms, a trained female research assistant conducted IDIs and FGDs with women while a trained male researcher conducted IDIs with healthcare providers and decision-makers [43].

IDIs and FGDs: pregnant and postpartum women

Data were collected through IDIs, FGDs and observations [43]. Semi-structured topic guides for IDIs and FGDs were designed to explore factors affecting ANC utilisation more broadly, and acceptability of intermittent preventive treatment of malaria in pregnancy [44]. These guides, written in English, were tested through mock interviews to refine questions and develop probes that allowed this study to assess factors affecting utilisation of ANC. Three women with lived experience of pregnancy and engagement with ANC and health services in Madang participated in initial interviews to test and refine interview guides. Trial participants were identified purposively by clinical trial staff at either first antenatal visit, subsequent antenatal visits or postnatal visits (within three months of delivery), to reflect experiences at different time points in relation to ANC utilisation. Pregnant women who were not enrolled in parent trial but attending study clinics at the health facilities the trial was conducted were selected through purposive and/or snowball sampling. This approach to sampling ensured that participants met the inclusion criteria for the study and that engagement in the research was relational – based on connections with health staff, researchers or other participants and willingness to engage in the study.

IDIs were held in a separate room at each health facility. The responses obtained from each participant varied, and IDIs lasted up to 45 min, as guided by study participants. FGDs were conducted at each health facility to engage women with diverse ANC experiences and residing at various localities (rural, peri-urban or urban), as well as to triangulate and validate views gathered from IDIs. A total of four FGDs were held. Each FGDs comprised 6–10 participants, with similar proportions of trial and non-trial participants (Fig. 2). This group size allows for active participation and interactive in-depth discussions. Participatory methods of brainstorming (open discussions), storytelling (individual story and open discussions), fishbowls (small group discussions), and target evaluation (group activity) were utilised to enable effective engagement of women in FGDs [45, 46]. The participatory methods were designed to explore women’s experiences of pregnancy, ANC, malaria, and IPTp to draw a wide range of perspectives relevant to ANC utilisation and content. FGDs were held in a conference room or outdoors near the clinic. FGDs lasted up to 67 min. IDIs and FGDs for women were aligned with ANC contacts and were held in a local lingua franca (‘Tok Pisin’). IDIs were numbered as IDI 01–52, and FGDs as FGD 01–04.

IDIs: healthcare providers and decision-makers

Healthcare providers and decision-makers involved in the provision and programming of ANC were invited to participate in IDIs. Data collection followed semi-structured topic guides that included enablers and barriers to ANC utilisation and intersections between healthcare policy and practice in PNG. Healthcare providers were selected through purposive and/or snowball sampling from the health facilities where the parent trial was conducted and included trial and non-trial staff. Decision-makers working across national, provincial and district health authority levels were selected through purposive and/or snowball sampling at the National Department of Health in Port Moresby and health authorities in Madang Province. They included directors, co-ordinators, supervisors, and programme managers. All IDIs for healthcare providers and decision-makers were held in ‘Tok Pisin’ or English, as per interviewee’s preference, and at a location and time of their choice. IDIs lasted up to 53 min. Data saturation was determined to have been reached when no new information was generated from IDIs or FGDs [47]. IDIs were numbered as IDI 01–15 for healthcare providers and IDI 01–13 for decision-makers.

Observations

Observations were documented as field notes to describe the context and physical setting of the clinics as well as the interactions between pregnant women and healthcare providers. Direct observation of activities relating to provision of ANC, including information processes, enrolment of women in antenatal clinics, and care provided at subsequent clinic visits, were undertaken (by AA and EL). Data from the field notes and observations were used to triangulate findings from IDIs and FGDs.

Data management and analysis

All IDIs and FGDs were recorded with a digital audio recorder. Recordings were transcribed verbatim in the originally spoken language and coded to maintain the anonymity and confidentiality of participants. IDIs and FGDs held in ‘Tok Pisin’ were translated into English. Back-translation was conducted by a co-investigator (NN) and the qualitative research team at the PNG Institute of Medical Research for a subset of interview translations to ensure the translations were accurate. IDIs conducted in English were transcribed using Otter.ai (Otter.ai, Inc., California, USA) and edited for accuracy. Transcriptions and translations were verified by the principal investigator (EL) for quality assurance and detection of unintended discrepancies in meaning.

Finalised transcripts of IDIs and FGDs were manually coded (by EL and SP) thematically using both deductive and inductive approaches. Pre-defined themes were identified through the review of the literature and deductive application of the framework on ANC utilisation in Madang Province. We followed Andrew et al.’s framework for assessing accessibility, attitudes to ANC, and interpersonal issues impacting ANC attendance and timing [3]. Concomitant use of deductive and inductive approaches allowed the research to contrast and compare findings with previous research from the study area and other settings in PNG [3, 32–34], while remaining open to new themes to emerge from this specific study and context. Any differences in coding between coders and approaches were discussed until a consensus was reached. Quotes in the results are indicated in italics. Context and additional information provided in the quotes are indicated with [square brackets] whereas omitted non-relevant sections of the text are denoted as […]. All data (recordings, transcripts, and analysis files) were stored and secured on a password-controlled and access-monitored server at Menzies School of Health Research, Australia.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 112 participants were enrolled in the qualitative study, including 15 healthcare providers, 13 decision-makers, and 79 pregnant and 5 postpartum women (Table 1). Of the 80 IDIs conducted, 15 were with healthcare providers (including four clinical trial staff), and 13 were with representatives of district, provincial and national health authorities.

IDIs were undertaken with trial participants at different stages of ANC engagement, including currently pregnant women (n = 45), pregnant women who had withdrawn from the trial (n = 2), and women who had delivered and attended postpartum (n = 5). FGDs involved both trial (n = 14) and non-trial (n = 18) participants with experience of ANC (Table 1). The characteristics of women participating in IDIs and FGDs are outlined in Table 2. Most were younger than 30 years of age (n = 60, 71%), married (n = 77, 92%) and had already given birth to one or more children (n = 54, 64%). Only five women (6%) had no or limited level of primary education. Almost three quarters of women had attended more than one antenatal clinic visits (n = 54, 64%) at the time of their participation in this study. Most women were from Madang Province (n = 57, 68%), some were from Sepik or Morobe (n = 10, 12%) or other provinces (n = 17, 20%), and the majority resided in rural areas (n = 59, 70%).

Theme 1: accessibility of ANC

Accessibility of ANC services was a key theme that emerged from our analysis and related to costs associated with ANC, healthcare service delivery challenges, proximity to clinics, and rigid clinic schedules. As ANC services were provided at no cost to women who were enrolled in the clinical trial, women saw clinic and delivery fee remission as a key enabler for them to access ANC. Interview participants explained they were also attracted by the opportunity to have a foetal ultrasound scan and receive medicines and clinic books at no cost at each ANC contact. Women with experiences of ANC received outside of the clinical trial setting reported clinic-related fees as barriers to healthcare service utilisation. Women across health facilities shared similar sentiments about free healthcare and how it encourages clinic attendance. A pregnant trial participant stated, “I think that at Yagaum [Rural Hospital], they don’t get medicine for free at routine clinic. That’s what I’ve seen. I see that with IMR [Institute of Medical Research], it’s free. Everything is free, that’s why I like it, and I came.” [IDI 05, pregnant woman]. A pregnant woman not enrolled in the trial shared similar views in a FGD, “Okay, they [trial participants] said that when they went for the [ultrasound] scan, it was free. And for us, if we want to get a [ultrasound] scan […], we have to pay for it [at private clinic or hospital] that’s why, I am happy that they are doing this. I am happy that IMR [Institute of Medical Research] does scanning for free whereas for us at the regular [clinic], we have to pay.” [FGD 01, participant 3]. Women highlighted potential benefits of ultrasound scans for clinical management, but scans were generally not available at routine clinics and had to be obtained at private clinics. A pregnant woman not involved in the trial and attending a routine clinic stated in a FGD, “My view would be the normal clinic is okay. But like as for me, [my] child is sleeping normal, some mothers we come together to the [normal] clinic here, I don’t see them anymore. Some say there is fluid in the womb, some the child sleeps across, so for me, maybe if the [normal] clinic has [ultrasound] scan would be better, to scan such mothers and can see [their health issues].” [FGD 03, participant 5]. As these quotes highlight, free ANC and provision of additional investigations such as obstetric ultrasound, which was not routinely available at recruitment sites, may incentivise utilisation, and is viewed as an integral but lacking component of ANC.

Accessibility of ANC was also affected by local healthcare service delivery challenges, including limited staff capacity and competing activities that disrupted antenatal clinic schedules. Field observations revealed that a polio vaccination programme for children led to cancellation of some clinic sessions at some health facilities. Staff shortage at health facilities meant that it was not possible to simultaneously provide ANC and community vaccination. Other service delivery challenges encountered at health facilities included power outages, water supply disruptions, community conflicts, and public holidays, all of which were observed to affect clinic accessibility during the data collection period. At one of the health facilities, a healthcare provider not engaged in the clinical trial stated, “The other thing is when the antenatal clinics are shut down on peak period especially during the festive periods; December and January, that’s when we miss providing treatments [to pregnant women].” [IDI 07, healthcare provider].

Healthcare providers and decision-makers described geographical challenges impacting on ANC accessibility. Some healthcare providers and decision-makers interviewed were concerned that community engagement activities led by health facilities to increase awareness of ANC were limited to areas in the vicinity of health facilities, and there was a risk that these activities would not reach remote-dwelling women. Similarly, concerns were voiced that ANC was not delivered at more remote community health posts. A health authority representative stated, “Within the city or town, it’s very easy, because more than 70 to 80% of our patients [women] attend antenatal clinic, but it’s more difficult outside in the rural areas.” [IDI 03, decision-maker]. This was substantiated by a healthcare provider working at a health facility, “Now that we do these antenatal services, our coverage is not really okay. Because we don’t cover the rural people properly. We concentrate in town areas, areas where its accessible to the [healthcare] services.” [IDI 09, healthcare provider].

The rigidness of antenatal clinic schedules also affected accessibility. During interviews, healthcare providers expressed that some women could not make it to the clinic on the dedicated day or allocated time and thus missed out on ANC. A healthcare provider at a routine clinic stated, “Because you know, when we run this MCH [Maternal and Child Health] clinic, it’s only once a week. Yeah, only once a week we attend to ANC. The rest of the time, we let them [pregnant women] go free. We send them and they went, and they never come back.” [IDI 09, healthcare provider]. Some healthcare providers were concerned about this and envisaged ANC to be available throughout the week, akin to birthing services, which would enable pregnant women to access ANC more easily. A healthcare provider at routine clinic stated, “There must really be a centre available on its own and they must provide the [ANC] services every day, Monday to Friday it must be accessible to the women.” [IDI 09, healthcare provider]. Healthcare providers were also concerned about an increasing number of pregnant women, translating into higher numbers of women that needed to be seen at clinics, thereby increasing workload for a limited number of clinic staff.

Theme 2: attitudes toward ANC

Attitudes toward ANC, including the perceived benefits and disadvantages, knowledge of ANC interventions and access to health education, influenced utilisation. Factors associated with positive attitudes toward an ANC included women’s perceptions on the importance of health check-ups, a desire to have safe pregnancy and delivery, desire to take medicines and receive health education, and an interest in involvement in pregnancy-related health research. On the other hand, women were concerned about not being provided with adequate medicine supplies at every clinic appointment, based on experiences from routine clinics and worried about the impact of this on their health and the health of their babies. A FGD participant not enrolled in the trial stated, “For me, the ANC is okay, but when it comes to getting medicines, we used to come to the clinic, and we sometimes don’t usually get enough medicines.” [FGD 02, participant 5]. These women indicated that frequent medicine shortages affected their attitudes toward ANC and subsequent attendance.

Overall, women expressed a strong desire to attend clinic to have a safe pregnancy and prepare for delivery. Women were keen to learn of the baby’s position, heartbeat, weight and the opportunity to detect any concerns over the foetus’ well-being or emerging risks for delivery. An interviewed pregnant woman involved in the trial stated, “I want to know that my baby is well, does not have sickness [condition in the womb] or such, and the baby must grow well, that’s why I was going to the clinic, for the wellness of my baby and myself as well.” [IDI 11, pregnant woman]. In a FGD, similar views were shared by a pregnant woman not enrolled in the trial, “I felt that for us pregnant women, we need to come [to clinic] so that the nurses are going to find out about how our baby is sleeping, because many times, when mothers give birth, the [baby’s] leg normally comes [first] or the hands does, that’s why we need to come so that they are going to check and see how the baby is sleeping.” [FGD 02, participant 7]. A few women specifically mentioned the risks of contracting malaria during pregnancy and the importance of attending clinic to protect their baby. Others were keen to attend clinic to obtain health education on best approaches to support the growth and development of their baby. A pregnant woman involved in the trial stated, “I came early [to clinic] so I must get advice [on] how I will take care of myself and this unborn child inside, what type of food is best for me to eat to [help] grow this child.” [IDI 50, pregnant woman]. Of note, women with previous experience in health research or clinical trials appeared to have more positive attitudes toward ANC. The healthcare received and the perceived positive health outcomes seen through study participation had encouraged ANC attendance in their subsequent pregnancies.

Women further demonstrated a strong understanding of the importance of other health checks provided for the mother and unborn child through clinics, including measurement of body weight and temperature, body mass index, blood tests to check for malaria parasites and blood levels (haemoglobin), ultrasound scans to date their pregnancy, and vaginal examinations where indicated. In addition, women expressed that healthcare providers usually provided helpful information about medications, mosquito nets, and nutrition during clinic visits. Women involved in the trial noted the value of this information and advice, but participants also mentioned that sometimes this education can be lacking. In a FGD, a pregnant woman not enrolled in the trial stated, “I think IMR [Institute of Medical Research] gives a lot of information to the mothers who come to attend with them. And we at the normal clinic, we miss out on many information that talks about how to look after ourselves with the baby and the kind of medicine we get together.” [FGD 03, participant 5]. Some women wanted healthcare providers to continuously provide such information at each clinic visit.

While healthcare providers explained that they conduct community awareness activities to encourage women to seek antenatal check-ups at the nearest health facility, it was highlighted these activities were dependent on the availability of resources and staff capacity, which varies between health facilities. The present analysis highlights the interdependence between attitudes to and knowledge of ANC, and perceptions around the quality and extend of service provision, and utilisation of ANC services.

Theme 3: interpersonal factors

A range of interpersonal factors can positively or negatively influence clinic attendance. In the present study, positive relationships and communication between women and healthcare providers, and community or family members participation, were reported by women to enhance clinic attendance, while negative healthcare providers’ attitudes and fear of pregnancy disclosure were identified as potential barriers. During field observation, friendly relationships were noted among the women and healthcare providers in the context of clinical trial participation. This was substantiated in interviews; one pregnant woman with the parent trial stated, “When we go there, they are open, and they talk openly to us, and they explain things to us.” [IDI 04, pregnant woman].

Although women expect ANC to be delivered with care and respect, some healthcare providers (notably in routine clinics outside of the trial setting) were reported as being aggressive in their approaches to women when providing ANC. Furthermore, multigravid mothers recalled having encountered dismissive behaviour of healthcare providers in their previous pregnancies at routine clinics. In a FGD, such experiences were shared by a pregnant woman attending routine clinic, “There are few nurses that talk nicely to us, some they don’t treat us well. They are rough and aggressive to the mothers.” [FGD 04, participant 1]. Positive healthcare provider attitudes towards pregnant women facilitated engagement with ANC and the clinical trial. An interviewed pregnant woman enrolled in the clinical trial stated, “I see that they [healthcare providers engaged in the trial] are also friendly, they will not be harsh on you, they will be soft, so I was interested to join [the parent trial].” [IDI 11, pregnant woman].

Participation of family or community members emerged as an enabling factor for clinic attendance. Women described how having support from a family member, or someone from their village, encouraged and helped them to attend clinic. This indicates the role that family and community members play in fostering healthcare seeking behaviour during pregnancy. Some women mentioned that they were encouraged to attend clinic by their husbands or mothers-in-law. A pregnant woman participating in trial stated, “I wasn’t supposed to come here [clinic], but my in-law told me to come and [enrol] with the IMR [parent trial], so I came. She brought me here herself and enrolled me here.” [IDI 02, pregnant woman]. Similar experiences were shared in an FGD by a pregnant woman attending routine clinic, “As for me, with my first [antenatal] visit, my husband and I came to the clinic.” [FGD 01, participant 9]. These interpersonal relationships appear to be factors affecting ANC utilisation.

In cases of teenage or unplanned pregnancy or when women lacked support from a partner, concerns around pregnancy disclosure were identified as potentially delaying clinic attendance. Fear of being stigmatised or reprimanded within the family, community or even at the health facility influenced ANC utilisation. An interviewed pregnant woman involved in the trial stated, “I was scared to tell my father about it [being pregnant], so I kept it to myself, and I was just there [at home].” [IDI 36, pregnant woman]. Some healthcare providers also expressed concerns over teenage pregnant women not seeking ANC because they do not want to disclose pregnancy.

Theme 4: timing of ANC initiation

Among women, timing of the first antenatal clinic visit was influenced by personal choice, a desire to avoid many clinic visits, community attitudes, prior pregnancy experiences, distance to health facilities, and anticipated travel and clinic costs. Most women who participated in interviews reported that they initiated ANC at four to five months of pregnancy. Many said that there was ‘no reason’ as to why they waited until this time to seek ANC, which indicated that there was a lack of awareness of the importance of early clinic attendance. A pregnant woman enrolled in the trial stated, “That is my own wish which I have decided to come on the fourth month [of pregnancy], so I make my way to the clinic. On the first, second and third month [of pregnancy], I stayed back just because I just want to stay [at home].” [IDI 01, pregnant woman]. Another pregnant trial participant stated, “There are no thoughts or such. I just stayed and like I said I wanted to go to the clinic, that’s why I came on my fifth month [of pregnancy].” [IDI 06, pregnant woman]. Other trial participants explicitly acknowledged that they wanted to avoid a high number of clinic visits, which influenced their decision about the timing of their first attendance. An interviewed postpartum woman involved in the trial stated, “I was thinking that I will stay until I am six months. I start to go back and forth to shorten my time here [clinic] until I deliver.” [IDI 09, postpartum woman]. A few participants explained that they waited until their ‘belly grows bigger’ to indicate they were pregnant before initiating ANC.

Attitudes of other community members also played a role in decision-making about initiating ANC. A pregnant woman involved in the trial stated, “I was just thinking, let me stay a bit first. Also, most people said, your belly is not showing out and how could you go quickly [to clinic]?” [IDI 34, pregnant woman]. Similar views were also shared from pregnant women attending routine clinics in an FGD, “The mothers on the street and my colleagues said, when you go early at three or four months it’s like you are wasting the time to go back and forth, too many [clinic] attendances. You just stay, and at six, seven, even eight months [of pregnancy] you just go for clinic, and it’s a short clinic and you deliver.” [FGD 03, participant 5]. This echoed reports from other women stating that delaying ANC initiation was due to the advice they received from other women with pregnancy experiences. A pregnant woman enrolled in the trial stated, “So, it was not my idea, but I follow advice from some experience mothers, I wait till five months, and I came [to clinic].” [IDI 50, pregnant woman]. A notable finding was that community awareness activities undertaken as part of the clinical trial appeared to trigger earlier clinic attendance for some women. Communication relating to the clinical trial being conducted at the clinics reached women in their communities, triggering some women to attend clinics early. Observations from fieldwork during this study further revealed that community volunteers involved with the clinical trial supported women to attend clinic earlier and re-attend subsequent clinic appointments. This showed the importance of community support to enable earlier clinic attendance.

Some women miscalculated their gestational age or were unaware of their pregnancy, which delayed ANC initiation. Some of those who were not sure of their pregnancy attended clinic early, but some healthcare providers postponed ANC initiation as they were unable to confirm the pregnancy due to lack of pregnancy tests or ultrasound equipment. A pregnant woman participating in the trial stated, “Because I was not sure and they [healthcare providers] were not sure, the nurses at the clinic said to go back and do a test or such to confirm it then come [back to clinic].” [IDI 45, pregnant woman]. A few of the women attending clinic within the first three months of pregnancy did so because they contracted malaria or were sick. A pregnant trial participant stated, “The first time was when I was in my early stage of pregnancy and I felt this fever so, when I went to the hospital, […] when they [healthcare providers] tested me for malaria, it was positive.” [IDI 46, pregnant woman].

There were differences between women in their first pregnancy and those with previous pregnancy experience in terms of the timing of ANC contacts. Women with two or more pregnancies appeared less likely to initiate ANC early, except for those who had experienced an adverse pregnancy outcome previously. A first-time pregnant woman involved with the trial stated, “Yeah, at the first month, I just went to find out why I was not having my menstruation, I went, and the doctors told me that, ‘oh you are pregnant now’.” [IDI 11, pregnant woman]. On the contrary, a multigravid postpartum woman involved in the trial stated, “I was thinking that I will stay till I am six months […], going back and forth [to clinic] every month, I am feeling tired.” [IDI 09, postpartum woman].

Location and distance to the nearest health facility were further identified as potential factors that deter women to initiate ANC early. A postpartum woman involved in the trial stated, “I was in a remote area, so when I came [to Mugil Health Centre], I arrived late. There was no staff there [at the local community health post] who could help me.” [IDI 27, postpartum woman]. Lack of funds to pay for bus fares or clinic fees also delayed ANC initiation. Whilst some women said they were keen to attend clinic early during pregnancy, they had to delay ANC initiation until they had saved up sufficient funds to pay for travel and clinic books. An interviewed pregnant trial participant stated, “I have no bus fare, and I did not come. I stayed on until I got money then I came.” [IDI 17, pregnant woman]. These factors influencing timing of ANC initiation are aligned with accessibility, attitudes and interpersonal factors that can enable or constrain clinic attendance throughout pregnancy.

Discussion

The present study highlights the range of factors that influence ANC utilisation in Madang Province in PNG. These factors were related to healthcare service delivery, accessibility, past experiences and perspectives of ANC, relationships with staff, support from family and community members, and personal issues (Fig. 3). Building on previous foundational research into ANC utilisation and timing of first clinic attendance in this region [3], the present study contributes to understandings of how accessibility, attitudes to ANC, and interpersonal factors intersect to enable or constrain ANC uptake. Improving awareness, access, and healthcare content of ANC through trial activities appeared to enhance utilisation and attendance earlier in pregnancy. The study findings expand understandings to include perspectives of healthcare providers and decision-makers.

Summary findings of factors influencing antenatal care timing and frequency among women attending clinics in Madang Province

The present study indicates that removing clinic fees may enable and motivate women to attend clinic earlier and more frequently, increasing ANC utilisation. Free maternal healthcare services or healthcare incentives such as vouchers or cash transfer, provided to pregnant women as part of a government policy or health research in other LMICs have been shown to significantly increase ANC utilisation in some circumstances [48–51]. In LMICs including PNG, costs incurred to seek maternal healthcare services appear to be an important barrier to ANC utilisation and early ANC initiation [3, 48, 52–54]. Similarly, cost was reported to contribute to delaying ANC initiation in the present study. These findings warrant consideration of implementing free maternal healthcare services policy in LMICs, including PNG, to mitigate the costs for socio-economic disadvantaged women and increase access to healthcare [38].

The current study further suggests that enhancing the content and quality of ANC may improve ANC utilisation, which supports findings from earlier research [32, 35]. Women valued ANC education and appreciated the additional interventions, such as ultrasound, medicines and blood tests, that the concurrent clinical research trial provided. Trial activities further appeared to mitigate the limited or interrupted availability of medical supplies and equipment in clinics at some health facilities. Similarly, at some rural health facilities in Uganda, the integration of ultrasound scans as a component of ANC significantly increased early ANC initiation and frequent attendance among women [55]. However, large clinical trials conducted in Democratic Republic of Congo, Guatemala, Kenya, Pakistan, and Zambia suggest that introducing this intervention to ANC has limited impacts on attendance and did not reduce maternal deaths and near-misses, stillbirth or neonatal deaths [56]. The present qualitative research provides insights into the potential benefits of enhancing the healthcare content and quality of ANC, which was enabled through a clinical trial.

This study draws attention to the differences in ANC services provided within a clinical trial context and through routine non-trial ANC services. In the context of a clinical trial, ANC was free, women received medicines free-of-charge, ultrasounds, and additional tests such as haemoglobin checks, which together appeared to enhance ANC utilisation. Removing financial barriers, and providing tests that women may expect, but often do not yet receive as part of routine ANC, could incentivise timely initiation and retention in ANC. In interviews and FGDs, women drew comparisons between the care they received within the clinical trial and at routine clinics, particularly in terms of healthcare provider attitudes, communication and availability of medication and other resources. For example, respectful relationship with healthcare providers and effective provider-patient communication enabled ANC utilisation while negative experiences with healthcare providers were barriers to ANC utilisation. Limitations in ANC services provision have been identified in PNG and other LMICs [3, 32, 33, 45, 57]. This study indicates opportunities for strengthening ANC and increasing utilisation but flags potential issues around sustainability and continuity of care when healthcare services can no longer be supplemented and supported through pregnancy-related research activities.

A key finding of this study is the centrality of relationships for healthcare access and utilisation within the clinical trial setting – relationships with healthcare providers, family and community engagement and support for ANC are crucial to foster timely utilisation. The enabling factors that more broadly increased ANC utilisation were family support and community engagement in ANC, which included connections to staff or community volunteers involved in the clinical trial. Women with relatives or community members engaged in the trial, as staff or community volunteers, were encouraged to attend clinic while previous experiences in health research for some women complimented their engagement. Raising community awareness of the benefits of ANC and antenatal malaria preventative measures provided through community volunteers engaged as part of the clinical trial was deemed an important facilitator for early ANC initiation in this study. In cohort studies from Africa, community volunteers or healthcare providers engaged in promoting the benefits of early comprehensive ANC and referral of pregnant women to clinics also appeared to increase early and regular ANC attendance as well as improve IPTp uptake [58, 59]. In support of these findings [58, 59], the present study indicates the value in identifying, training, and incentivising community members to promote maternal healthcare services and encourage women to access ANC and birthing services. Partners and mothers-in-law were also identified as important influences on decision-making to attend clinic, whilst lack of family support delayed ANC utilisation, which supports findings from earlier research [3, 60]. On the contrary, advice from relatives and other women about waiting to visit clinics contributed to delays with ANC initiation.

In the present study, healthcare providers and decision-makers added important insights regarding barriers and enablers of early and frequent ANC. Clinic staff and decision-makers highlighted that most women in the rural areas in PNG had limited access to ANC because of the distance to health facilities. Distance to health facilities, and difficulties accessing and financing transport to clinics that provide ANC, have long been identified and pose ongoing challenges for women in PNG and elsewhere [3, 17, 45, 61]. Some healthcare providers further suggested that services may need to be tailored to remote-dwelling women who may not always reach the health facility at the time or day of the scheduled clinic. In addition, structural changes could be considered to allow more flexible clinic schedules to provide every woman with opportunities to be seen by a healthcare provider, irrespective of when they attend, rather than sending them away either because they attend on the wrong day or at the wrong time. However, competing activities at health facilities and staff shortages in the study area already impact on the ability to provide ANC as is, and this has been an ongoing issue in this province and other regions in PNG [32, 35]. Staff shortages and lack of pregnancy tests or ultrasound may also have led to some women being turned away from clinics because they attended ‘too early’ in their pregnancy, or over uncertainty over pregnancy status. Outreach healthcare services, such as mobile ANC, may be a suitable option to address this challenge. Likewise, strengthening community-based approaches, such as community IPTp, may reduce malaria-associated morbidity and may increase the number of clinic visits [62, 63]. These approaches to ANC through community engagement could enhance local knowledge about the importance of pregnancy care and could facilitate early and regular ANC utilisation.

Experiences and perspectives of women reinforced the importance of early ANC in malaria-endemic regions and opportunities for education on malaria in pregnancy to foster ANC utilisation. Some multiparous women in the current study had experienced pregnancy-related complications including stillbirth and miscarriage or morbidity attributed to malaria and/or other causes in their previous pregnancy, which are common phenomena among women in PNG [20, 25, 27]. Malaria preventative strategies and treatments are delivered through ANC to safeguard the health of the mother and their babies [3, 7, 30], but most women attended clinics late which often leave them and their foetus vulnerable to malaria-associated morbidity and mortality [8, 12]. While previous experience of poor pregnancy outcomes influenced early ANC utilisation, multiparous women who had not experienced complications were more likely to delay ANC initiation, which is consistent with other studies in this setting [31]. In line with earlier research [3], some women in the present study wished to avoid multiple clinic visits and delayed early ANC initiation, which may exacerbate their risk of, e.g., contracting malaria and render them more vulnerable to adverse pregnancy outcomes. Health education about preventative medicine, diseases and other pregnancy-related complications could be prioritised to improve knowledge and benefits of ANC utilisation in LMICs.

Although a large sample of patients, healthcare providers, and decision-makers were engaged to evaluate barriers and enablers of ANC in rural PNG, there are some limitations to this research. The main limitation of this study is that it did not include the perspectives or experiences of women who had not utilised ANC at all. In addition, it was not possible to fully separate women’s experiences of routine non-trial ANC from their experiences of ANC within a clinical trial, hence, the findings have revealed interesting intersections between health research, accessibility and utilisation of ANC. This study has a specific regional focus and predominately involved women from Madang Province; other regions in PNG may experience different barriers and enablers to ANC utilisation. Further studies are required to explore these factors. As a qualitative study focused on rigour and trustworthiness rather than generalisability, the findings in the present study are grounded in the local context, but there may be parallels with other regions and LMICs. To assess rigour, we followed Finlay’s focus on clarity – asking does the research make sense and has the research been systematically worked through and explained – and credibility – asking do the findings match the evidence and are they convincing [64].

Conclusions

Removing clinic fees, enhancing healthcare content of ANC, and increasing flexibility around ANC provision may improve ANC utilisation. Experience in previous health research, engagement of relatives or community members together with health education more broadly encourage ANC utilisation in this setting. Educational awareness is required during clinics and in communities to improve ANC knowledge and understanding of women, particularly in relation to malaria in pregnancy and the need for early and timely ANC. Healthcare system barriers need to be addressed by relevant health authorities to improve ANC accessibility and utilisation in this setting.
