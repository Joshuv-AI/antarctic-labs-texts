The power of phytochemicals in cancer chemoprevention through multi-target modulation of oncogenic signaling pathways
Authors: Chandni, Sheikh Showkat Ahmad, Kritika Pandit, Anantika Suri, Muzafar A. Macha, Satwinderjeet Kaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The conventional cancer therapies like synthetic drug treatment, radiotherapy, and surgery are often associated with serious side effects including cancer recurrence and drug resistance. In contrast, plant-derived compounds offer a multitargeted and potentially less toxic approach that may complement traditional cancer therapies. Ethnopharmacological evidence, together with preclinical and clinical studies, has demonstrated substantial cytotoxic potential of plant extracts and isolated compounds against different human cancer cell lines. Medicinal plants are a rich source of bioactive compounds, including alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids and polyphenols which may inhibit angiogenesis, induce apoptosis, inhibit cell proliferation, and cell cycle progression, reverse drug resistance, interfere with microtubule formation, act on topoisomerase targets and modulate key signaling pathways like JAK–STAT, NRF2-KEAP1 and Wnt/β-catenin. Furthermore, these compounds can activate the immune system while also inducing epigenetic alterations like histone acetylation and DNA methylation to restore normal gene expression. The review examines recent literature (2020–2025) on phytochemicals derived from botanical sources that have demonstrated significant anticancer effects with particular emphasis on their molecular mechanism of action to understand the implications of these findings in the future treatment and prevention of cancer. Although there is an interest in the field of cancer chemoprevention using natural compounds because of the phytochemicals intriguing anti-cancer properties, comprehensive mechanistic investigations and well-designed clinical research is required to support the compounds therapeutic usage for chemoprevention.

Introduction

Cancer is a broad category of illnesses in which the body’s immune system cannot keep up with the rapid proliferation of aberrant cells, which ultimately results in metastasis, the primary cause of cancer-related deaths [1]. Somatic mutations, reactive oxygen species, ionizing radiations, and several chemical and biological factors are identified as the primary causes of cancer development by the World Health Organization (WHO). Ionizing radiation can alter the molecular structure of nucleic acids by breaking hydrogen bonds that hold them together, thereby altering the normal DNA expression [2]. Consequently, carcinogen exposure is considered a risk factor for cancer initiation and contributes significantly to the increasing incidence of cancer [3]. Carcinogenesis is a complex and multistep process through which a healthy cell is transformed into a cancerous cell. It is categorized into three stages viz., initiation, promotion and progression [4]. The first stage that is tumor initiation is a rapid and irreversible process triggered by exposure to several carcinogenic agents causing non-lethal mutations in the DNA of a healthy cell. Tumor promotion is characterized by selective proliferation of initiated cells, allowing further mutations to accumulate. Ultimately, these mutations result in an invasive, distinct cellular phenotype capable of metastasis and tumor progression; the final stage of neoplastic transformation occurs [5].

The widespread occurrence of cancer and resistance to standard cancer treatments like surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation along with the numerous side effects and limited therapeutic efficacy, has highlighted the urgent need for safe antitumor therapeutic research that can selectively target tumor cells and prevent cancer cell proliferation [6]. Natural products have consistently been a key focus in the search for alternative agents against synthetic anticancer drugs [7]. Globally, the potential of phytochemicals that are naturally found in fruits, vegetables, spices, roots, leaves, tubers, rhizomes, flowers, flavouring agents and other herbal supplements to prevent the development of cancers in humans is growing [8–12]. The phytochemicals such as capsaicin (Capsicum sp.), curcumin (Curcuma longa), resveratrol (Vitis vinifera), epigallocatechin-3-gallate (Camellia sinensis) and several other bioactive compounds, especially from cruciferous vegetables, such as 3,3’-diindolylmethane, phenethyl isothiocyanate, indole-3-carbinol and sulforaphane are widely acknowledged for their anticancer properties in different studies (both in vitro and in vivo). Phytochemicals from botanical sources exhibit multi-target effects upon cancer cells by interacting with numerous pathways and molecular targets. Phytochemicals have been shown to influence transcription factors, kinases, ion channels, growth factors, inflammatory mediators, pro-apoptotic proteins, anti-apoptotic proteins and microRNAs that ultimately impact a range of cellular activities. Some of these phytocompounds also trigger specific biological responses that are helpful in combating and preventing cancer by regulating various proteins and enzymes involved in cancer development and progression [13, 14]. The use of agents such as medications or supplements to prevent or slow down the cancer onset, progression, or recurrence is referred to as chemoprevention [15]. Chemopreventive drugs can be divided into three groups according to the stage at which they work. Preventing cancer in high-risk and other healthy individuals is the first type of chemoprevention, often known as primary chemoprevention which is achieved by stopping the emergence of cancer biomarkers. Secondary chemoprevention encompasses the prevention of premalignant lesions from becoming invasive cancer, as shown in Fig. 1. The administration of drugs that prevent the recurrence of invasive cancer is categorized as tertiary chemoprevention [16]. Shifting the focus from treating end-stage cancer to controlling carcinogenesis has proven to be a more effective and promising approach. Research on cancer chemoprevention has grown substantially because of the ability of phytochemicals to modulate molecular targets at multiple stages of carcinogenesis to prevent its altered mechanism [17]. In this context, the current review focuses on revealing the most recent developments in utilizing natural phytochemicals as cancer chemopreventive agents. The molecular mechanisms through which different phytochemicals affect key signaling pathways such as NRF2-KEAP1, JAK–STAT, Wnt-β catenin pathway, ferroptosis, cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, inhibition of cell migration, redox homeostasis, and epigenetic modulation have been discussed in this review. This study aims to summarize existing evidence to demonstrate the therapeutic potential of phytochemicals and to guide future investigations into specific cancer preventive techniques in order to counteract the fatal effects of cancer.

Anticancer potential of phytochemicals: an overview

Medicinal plants constitute a prolific source of natural products that are structurally diverse and highly specific, providing a rich platform for the discovery of novel compounds with anticancer properties [18]. The development of anticancer agents using phytochemicals involves the extraction, isolation and purification of bioactive constituents. Notably, several plant-based compounds, including cinchonine, paclitaxel (Taxol), vincristine, and camptothecin derivatives have gained clinical approval and remain important components of modern cancer chemotherapy. Conventional chemotherapeutic drugs, despite being the first line of treatment, are associated with adverse side effects and dose-limiting toxicities which has intensified the interest of scientists in search for safer medications. Phytochemicals used in combination with standard therapeutic drugs have shown promising results in cancer treatment with reduced toxicity [19]. However, the clinical translation of phytochemicals is limited by rapid metabolic degradation, poor oral bioavailability and limited intestinal absorption. These limitations have led to the development of nanotechnology-based delivery systems that offer reduced systemic toxicity and improved drug bioavailability [20]. For instance, carrier-free nanoparticles of gambogic acid and glycyrrhizic acid formulated through self-assembly demonstrated enhanced anti-tumor effect and reduced toxicity to normal cells [21].

Mechanisms underlying anticancer effects of phytochemicals

Phytochemicals influence various pathways within cancer cells to produce their therapeutic effects. These actions include preventing angiogenesis, promoting apoptosis and ferroptosis, limiting cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, regulating non-coding RNAs, targeting cancer stem cells, reducing inflammation, and inducing genetic and epigenetic changes, as discussed in Table 1. They also impact tumor metabolism. A single phytochemical can activate multiple mechanisms to exert its anti-cancer effects. Additionally, combinations of different phytochemicals can interact synergistically to strengthen their overall anti-cancer activity [22, 23].

Phytochemicals as cell cycle modulators

The cell cycle is a tightly regulated process through which a cell grows, duplicates its genetic material, and divides into two genetically identical daughter cells. Progressions through four phases, viz. G1, S, G2, and M of the cell cycle is controlled by an intricate network of proteins, primarily cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) [24]. Cyclin expression fluctuates throughout specific phases of the cell cycle to drive orderly cell cycle transitions, while CDK activity regulates phase-specific transcription. After binding, cyclins (A2, B1, B2, D1, D2, D3, E1, E2, and G1) and CDKs (2, 4, and 6) work together to phosphorylate key members of the pocket protein family including p130, p107 and retinoblastoma (Rb) [25]. When these pocket proteins like Rb are phosphorylated, they are released from chromatin, enabling the transcription of E2F target genes involved in DNA replication and facilitating the transition into the S phase [26]. The CDK/Cyclin activity is regulated by CDK inhibitors (CDKIs) such as p16, p21, p27, and p57 [27]. CDKIs and Cyclins compete for binding to CDKs: for instance, the CDK2-Cyclin E complex promotes the cell cycle progression [28], while the CDK4/6-p16 complex induces cell cycle arrest [25]. Cell cycle checkpoints including the DNA damage checkpoints (DDCs), the replication stress checkpoint (RSC), and the spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC), act as surveillance mechanisms that detect errors and either pause the cell cycle for repairs or trigger cell death if damage is irreparable. These checkpoints preserve genomic integrity and prevent the propagation of mutations [29, 30]. Cancer can result from disruption of cell cycle control, frequently caused by mutations that inhibit apoptosis, block cell cycle exit or promote unregulated cell cycle progression. These defects permit unchecked, continuous cell division, underscoring the critical function of the cell cycle system in maintaining regular cellular activity [25, 29, 31, 32]. Given the important role of cell cycle dysregulation in cancer cell progression, regulating cell cycle progression or inducing cell cycle arrest has emerged as a crucial strategy for cancer prevention and treatment. Several studies have demonstrated that phytochemicals can effectively induce cell cycle arrest frequently leading to the activation of apoptotic cascades and ultimately causing apoptosis. By focusing on important cell cycle regulatory sites these naturally occurring substances have offered considerable potential to reduce tumor growth and enhance therapeutic outcomes [33].

Betulinic acid (BA), a pentacyclic triterpene obtained mainly from the bark of birch trees, has shown significant anticancer potential by targeting multiple cellular pathways, including cell cycle regulation, angiogenesis inhibition, and apoptosis induction [34]. In colorectal cancer cells (CRC), BA modulated cell cycle progression by reducing the proportion of cells in the G1 phase and elevating the number of cells in G2 phase. This G2 phase accumulation was associated with the upregulation of cyclin-dependent kinases CDK2 and CDK4 as well as increased phosphorylation of the retinoblastoma protein (pRb) in CRC cell lines such as HT29 and SW480. Notably, knockdown of metallothionein 1G (MT1G) attenuated these effects, suggesting that MT1G is crucial for BA-mediated G2/M phase arrest via modulating CDK2, CDK4, and pRb phosphorylation [35].

Furthermore, BA demonstrated a strong anti-proliferative effect against Caki-1 renal carcinoma cells by inhibiting cell growth in a dose-dependent manner. This effect was mediated through activation of apoptotic pathway, notably through caspase-3 activation. BA induced G2/M phase arrest in Caki-1 cells by downregulating cyclin A, cyclin B1 and upregulating the tumor suppressor p53 protein and the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors p21 and CIP1. Elevated p21 enhanced its binding to cyclin-dependent kinases, further contributing to cell cycle arrest [36]. Collectively, these findings indicate betulinic acid’s potential as a natural substance that targets important cell cycle and apoptosis regulators, providing intriguing treatment options for cancer.

Kaempferol, a prominent flavonoid aglycone widely distributed in various vegetables, fruits, and herbs such as tomatoes, grapes, broccoli, and tea, has been extensively studied for its modulatory effects on cancer pathways, including induction of cell cycle arrest [37]. Recent studies have shown differential cytotoxic and antiproliferative effects of kaempferol on genetically distinct triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells. Kaempferol significantly suppressed cell growth in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells, with higher potency in MDA-MB-231 cells (IC₅₀:43.86 µM) than MDA-MB-468 cells (IC₅₀:53.47 µM). Treatment with kaempferol induced cell cycle arrest at the S-phase of the cell cycle in both cancer cell lines, with MDA-MB-231 cells responding at lower concentrations (6.25 µM and 12.5 µM) and MDA-MB-468 cells requiring higher concentrations (25 µM and 50 µM) to show similar effects. Furthermore, kaempferol modulated CDKs expression, key regulators of cell cycle progression, thereby contributing to its antiproliferative effects [38].

In addition to its effects on breast cancer cells, kaempferol has demonstrated significant antiproliferative activity in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) models, where it promoted G2/M phase arrest. Mechanistically, kaempferol treatment caused marked downregulation of critical cell cycle regulatory genes and proteins, including CHEK2, CCNB1, ATM, CDK1, KNL1, CDC25C, MPS1, and Bub1. Notably, kaempferol co-treatment with ATM inhibitor (KU55933), resulted in enhanced anticancer response against HCC cells. Bioinformatics analyses further supported these findings by revealing that several of these genes, CDK1, CHEK2, ATM, CDC25C, and KNL1, are highly expressed in HCC patient tissues, highlighting their potential as diagnostic biomarkers [39]. These findings suggest that kaempferol acts as a promising phytochemical capable of modulating cell cycle checkpoints and suppressing cancer cell proliferation.

Apigenin (4′,5,7-trihydroxyflavone), a natural flavonoid has gained significant attention as a health-promoting compound owing to its low toxicity and selective cytotoxicity toward cancer cells compared to normal cells, distinguishing it from other structurally related flavonoids [40]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that apigenin induces G2/M phase arrest in various cancers including prostate cancer [41], renal cell carcinoma [42], cervical cancer [43], and breast cancer [44], highlighting its broad anticancer potential through regulation of cell cycle. Furthermore, apigenin together with acacetin exhibited selective cytotoxicity against U87 glioblastoma cells, with IC50 values of 48.18 ± 1.37 µM and 43.73 ± 1.19 µM, respectively. Treatment with these compounds induced G2/M phase arrest and increased the sub-G1 cell population, which was mediated by the upregulation of p21 and the downregulation of CDK1, Cyclin-A1, and Cyclin-B1 expression [45].

Similarly, esculetin (6,7-dihydroxycoumarin), a naturally occurring coumarin derivative found in plants such as Aesculus hippocastanum, Fraxinus rhynchophylla, and Artemisia capillaris has been shown to interfere with cancer cell cycle progression. Esculetin induced cell cycle arrest at both the G0/G1 and S phases in CMT-U27 and CF41.MG cell lines. These effects were supported by western blot results which showed an increase in cleaved caspase-3 expression, a hallmark of apoptosis, along with a decrease in CDK4 and cyclin D1 levels, critical regulators of the G1/S transition [46].

Collectively, the studies mentioned above indicate that phytochemicals can inhibit cancer cell proliferation effectively by targeting key regulators of cell cycle progression. They modulate the expression of cyclins, CDKs, tumor suppressor proteins such as p53 and p21 and checkpoint kinases which leads to cell cycle arrest at different checkpoints, including the G0/G1, S, and G2/M phase. These alterations restrict the cancer cell growth by disrupting the orderly progression of the cell cycle. Moreover, the modulation of the cell cycle frequently leads to the activation of apoptotic cascades and triggers apoptosis. Given the significance of the cell cycle in cancer progression, its modulation by phytochemicals represents a promising strategy for cancer chemoprevention.

Phytochemicals-mediated modulation of epigenetic targets in cancer

Epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation, histone post-translational modifications, chromatin remodeling and expression of non-coding RNAs (microRNAs and long non-coding RNAs) plays significant roles in the regulation of gene expression [47, 48]. Covalent histone modifications such as methylation, ubiquitination, sumoylation, acetylation and phosphorylation of specific residues on histone N-terminal tails are also involved in regulating fundamental biological processes [49]. Alterations in these epigenetic modifications can cause significant cellular damage that contributes to cancer development [50].

The multifaceted chemopreventive roles of phytochemicals have been extensively investigated. These roles vary from induction of DNA demethylation [51], modulation of expression of chromatin modifiers and inhibition of activity of DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) [52], histone deacetylases (HDACs) [53], histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone methyltransferases (HMTs) [54]. Recently, several therapeutic strategies have focused on the use of molecules known as epidrugs which target specific epigenetic modifiers for cancer treatment [55]. Among these molecules, several natural bioactive compounds have been identified from different medicinal plants including cannabinoids, carotenoids, chalcones, fatty acids, lignans, polysaccharides, saponins, secoiridoids, steroids, tannins, and tanshinones, as shown in Table 2 [55]. Although there are very few clinical validations, these compounds are known to regulate the expression of miRNAs, reduce DNA methylation, restore expression of tumor suppressor genes, and decrease cell proliferation both in vivo and in vitro models in a dose-dependent manner [56–58]. Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) is a major catechin or an active phenolic compound of green tea that has been studied for its anti-tumor and anti-carcinogenic properties by targeting molecular signaling pathways, including epigenetic modifications such as DNA methylation, miRNA silencing, and histone modifications [59, 60]. EGCG has been reported to block cell proliferation, colony formation, and induce apoptosis along with epigenetic modifications in various cancer cell lines Several studies have demonstrated the anticancer efficacy of EGCG when administered alone or in combination with two other phytochemicals, namely, amarogentin and eugenol. A recent study demonstrated that EGCG inhibited the tumor growth of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) by downregulating the expression as well as the activity of DNMTs. The EGCG treatment decreased DNMTs protein levels and reactivated tumor suppressor genes, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation [59].

Several studies have documented the role of withaferin A, a dietary steroidal lactone isolated from the roots of Withania somnifera, as an anticancer drug [61–64]. Therefore, it is widely used as a chemopreventive and therapeutic agent. Different molecular targets for withaferin A have been well characterized in breast cancer including p53, ERα, STAT3, ERK, JAK, FOXO3a, IAP and NF-κB [65]. Withaferin A has been reported to modulate the expression of several miRNAs in breast cancer-derived mammospheres. Although treatment with withaferin A leads to differential expression profiles of several miRNAs a significant increase in miR-let‐7c‐5p, a known tumor suppressor was noticed in breast cancer cells-derived mammospheres [63]. The anticancer activity of withaferin A is attributed to its inhibitory effects on the epigenome including its ability to inhibit and reduce the activity of the family of DNMTs [66]. A recent study evaluating withaferin A in combination with another phytochemical phenyl isothiocyanate (PEITC), found in cruciferous vegetables such as broccoli, watercress, cabbage, demonstrated the anti-cancerous properties of these compounds, particularly as epigenetic modifiers. Since PEITC can act as HDAC inhibitor, its combination with with withaferin A, synergistically inhibited cell proliferation, induced apoptosis and suppressed tumorigenicity in breast cancer cells through alteration in DNA methylation, histone acetylation pathways and miRNome profiles [65]. Further studies on withaferin A revealed that methylation of the p21 promoter is a vital step underlying its anticancer property [66].

Cannabinoids are a group of bioactive compounds abundantly present in the Cannabis sativa plant [67]. Tetrahydrocannabinol is a cannabinoid that has been extensively researched for its anti-inflammatory and anti-cancer effects [55]. Additionally, a recent review highlighted the impact of cannabidiol on epigenetic therapies and its potential to enhance chemotherapy efficacy [68]. Cannabinoids have been demonstrated to affect several epigenetic mechanisms, including RNA interference and DNA methylation making them crucial regulators of inflammation and cancer therapies [69, 70]. Recent research has also revealed several long-lasting epigenetic modifications associated with cannabinoid receptor function including DNA-associated histone proteins, DNA methylation and RNA regulatory networks [71]. These modifications may pave the way for the emergence of new chemotherapy approaches.

Several other phytochemicals, including curcumin, quercetin, resveratrol, oleanolic acid, genistein, artemisinin, and artesunate have also been reported to function as epigenetic modifiers. Curcumin suppresses human gastric cancer cell growth by inhibiting cell proliferation and migration through DNA demethylation [51]. A recent study revealed that sulforaphane and iberin affected the acetylation and methylation status of particular lysine residues on histones 3 and 4 in malignant melanoma cells [54]. A curcumin derivative, CU17, exhibited stronger HDAC inhibition than curcumin inducing dose-dependent histone H3 hyperacetylation in A549 cells [57]. Resveratrol inhibited the invasion and migration of prostate cancer cells by enhancing the demethylation of TIMP 2 and TIMP3 [56].

Taken together, these studies indicate that phytochemicals can influence various epigenetic mechanisms associated with tumor initiation and progression. They restore normal expression of tumor suppressor genes and suppress the oncogenic signaling pathways through` alteration of DNA methylation patterns by regulating DNMTs, histone modifications by altering the expression of histone-modifying enzymes including HDACs, HATs and HMTs and modulation of non-coding RNA expression. However, despite promising preclinical research, more studies and planned clinical trials are required to demonstrate their exact mode of action, validate their efficacy, identify the optimal dose, and evaluate their long-term safety and translational potential in cancer treatment.

Phytochemicals as ferroptosis inducers

Ferroptosis is a form of non-apoptotic cell death characterized by glutathione depletion, iron accumulation, and excessive ROS generation, which leads to lipid peroxidation and rupture of the plasma membrane [72–74]. Ferroptotic cells exhibit morphological alterations at both cellular and ultra-micro levels. These alterations include loss of plasma membrane integrity, cytoplasmic swelling (oncosis), and reduction in mitochondrial size as compared to normal cells. Additionally, rupture of outer mitochondrial membrane, disappearance of mitochondrial cristae, and increased membrane density is observed in ferroptotic cells. However, despite these changes, the nuclei of ferroptotic cells maintain their structural integrity without showing any signs of condensation or chromatin margination [75].

Ferroptosis can be activated through intrinsic or extrinsic pathways. The intrinsic pathway is triggered by the suppression of intracellular antioxidant enzyme activity such as glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). Meanwhile, the extrinsic pathway is initiated by inhibition of the amino acid antiporter system Xc − or activation of iron transporters such as transferrin and lactotransferrin [76].

Furthermore, intracellular iron intake, storage and outflow regulate tumor cell sensitivity to ferroptosis by influencing intracellular iron homeostasis [77]. The transferrin receptor (TFR) mediates the uptake of circulating ferric ion (Fe3+) which are subsequently converted into the ferrous ion (Fe2+) in the endosome. Excess Fe2+ ions in the cell combines with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in the Fenton reaction and generates the most reactive form of activated oxygen, hydroxyl radicals [78]. These hydroxyl radicals induce lipid peroxidation leading to ferroptosis [79, 80].

Lipoxygenases, particularly 12- and 15-lipoxygenases, catalyze the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) under physiological conditions. The resulting lipid hydroperoxides are generally neutralized by the lipid repair enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), which utilizes glutathione (GSH) as a cofactor to reduce lipid peroxides and prevents toxic accumulation. Ferroptosis is triggered when this protective system is disrupted. A key initiating event is the inhibition of the cystine–glutamate antiporter, known as System Xc⁻ [81].

System Xc⁻ comprises of two essential subunits: solute carrier family 3 member 2 (SLC3A2) and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), and facilitates the 1:1 exchange of extracellular cystine for intracellular glutamate. Upon cellular uptake, cystine is rapidly reduced to cysteine, a precursor required for GSH biosynthesis, catalyzed by glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC) and glutathione synthetase (GSS) [82].

Disruption of this system impairs GSH production, compromises GPX4 activity, and results in the accumulation of lipid peroxides, ultimately driving ferroptotic cell death [78, 81]. The ferroptotic cell death mechanism has been illustrated in Fig. 5. Numerous studies indicate that ferroptosis plays a significant role in tumor cells elimination and tumor growth suppression [83–85]. It has been associated with cell death in cancer types including non-small cell lung cancer, breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma.Therefore, selective induction of ferroptosis has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy for cancer treatment [86].

The role of phytochemicals as ferroptosis modulators has been highlighted by recent studies. These compounds can enhance the expression of antioxidant enzyme such as GPX4, decrease the availability of iron for Fenton reactions, scavenge ROS, inhibit lipid peroxidation-catalyzing enzymes like lipoxygenases and control gene expression all of which improve overall health [87]. Furthermore, natural products can modulate ferroptosis sensitivity through interactions with lipid metabolism regulators such as ACSL3 and ACSL4 [88].

Curcumin, a naturally occurring phenolic compound used as food coloring agent, spice and in traditional medicine, is widely known in treatment of various diseases due to its diverse biological activities [89]. Emerging evidence suggests that curcumin’s potential to induce ferroptosis contributes significantly to in its anticancer effects. In breast cancer models, curcumin increases glutamine transporter SLC1A5 expression, which improves glutamine uptake and promotes ferroptosis. Administration of curcumin at a dose of 30 mg/kg/day significantly reduced tumor volume and weight in vivo [90]. In colorectal cancer (CRC) cells curcumin suppressed colony formation, migration and proliferation and prevented the growth of xenograft tumors by modulating SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 signaling axis thereby triggering ferroptosis in CRC cells [91, 92]. Curcumin has also been shown to suppress lung cancer progression via ferroptosis induction. In A549 and H209 lung cancer cell lines, treatment with 35 µM curcumin for 24 h significantly reduced cell viability, proliferation, invasion and migration, while enhancing cell death. These effects were associated with increased levels of MDA, Fe²⁺, decreased levels of GSH, superoxide dismutase (SOD) and GPX4 levels and enhanced nuclear translocation of NRF2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) and increased HO-1 (Heme Oxygenase 1) expression. The ferroptosis inhibitor liproxstatin-1, and the NRF2 inhibitor ML385 partially reversed these effects confirming the involvement of this pathway [93]. Further, curcumin inhibits JNK (c-Jun N-terminal kinase) signaling, thereby contributing to the induction of ferroptosis in CRC (SW-480) cells [94].

Common barberry (Berberis vulgaris) fruits are rich in berberine, an alkaloid known for its beneficial effects against metabolic syndrome and cancer, making it a promising part of cancer chemoprevention strategies [95]. Berberine inhibited the expression of several ferroptosis regulators in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) cells, including GPX4, SLC7A11, and SLC3A2. This resulted in increased lipid peroxidation and ROS accumulation, thereby promoting ferroptotic cell death. Moreover, molecular docking experiments demonstrated a high binding affinity between berberine and GPX4, indicating that berberine-induced ferroptosis is partly caused by direct suppression of GPX4 activity [96]. Another study reported that berberine, when combined with ferroptosis inducers, exerted synergistic cytotoxic effects against non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells by elevating ROS levels, impairing antioxidant defences and inducing mitochondrial dysfunction.These changes collectively promoted ferrroptotic cell death. This combination treatment also markedly inhibited tumor growth in NSCLC allograft mouse models [97]. Moreover, berberine inhibited cell proliferation and significantly reduced the expression of TYMS, AURKA, CCNB1, and CDK1 in LNCaP and PC3 prostate cancer cells. Berberine had cell-line-specific effects, suppression of ICAM1 in PC3 cells, and downregulation of AR expression in LNCaP cells. Overall, berberine may suppress prostate cancer growth through modulation of ferroptosis-related genes including AR, TYMS, CDC42, CCNB1, CDK1, ICAM1, NTRK1 and AURKA [98].

Quercetin (3,3′,4′,5,7-pentahydroxyflavone) is a polyphenol commonly found in a wide variety of fruits and vegetables, with the highest concentrations found in red onions and shallots. Other rich sources include broccoli, apples, cherries, tomatoes, berries, tea, kale, red wine and red grapes [99]. Quercetin demonstrates a wide range of pharmacological properties including antidiabetic, anticancer, antioxidant, cardioprotective, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial and neuroprotective activities [100]. Numerous animal studies have demonstrated an increase in the antioxidant levels including Vitamin C, GSH, and Vitamin E, etc., following quercetin treatment [101]. Recent research has shown that quercetin can induce ferroptosis in cancer cells thereby contributing to its anticancer activity. The ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) reversed the effect of quercetin treatment on cell viability in gastric cancer models, suggesting that ferroptosis plays a role in quercetin-mediated cytotoxicity. Additionally, quercetin treatment increased intracellular iron levels which returned to normal following Fer-1 administration. Molecular analysis further revealed that quercetin decreased the expression of important ferroptosis regulators including xCT (SLC7A11) and GPX4 indicating that its ferroptosis-inducing effects are mediated through inhibition of the xCT system and suppression of GPX4 [102, 103]. Quercetin’s significance in inducing ferroptosis as a chemoprevention strategy is further supported by research conducted on endometrial cancer (HEC-1-A) cells, which revealed that quercetin administration increased intracellular ROS levels and decreased GPX4 expression [104]. Similarly, isoquercitrin, also known as quercetin-3-O-glucoside, inhibits the proliferation of lung cancer cells by enhancing ferroptosis, triggering NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent pyroptosis and inducing apoptosis. Ferroptosis and pyroptosis mediated by isoquercitrin were reduced by inhibiting NLRP3 or treatment with the caspase-1 inhibitor VX-765. Furthermore, in vivo studies demonstrated that isoquercitrin (1 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg), dramatically decreased tumor development, apoptosis, ferroptosis, NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis and ROS production in tumor tissues. Overall, isoquercitrin suppresses lung tumor progression through induction of ROS/NLRP3-driven pyroptosis and ferroptosis while ROS further contributes to apoptosis induction [105, 106].

Sulforaphane (SFN), an isothiocyanate abundant in broccoli and other Brassica species, has been identified as a potent inducer of ferroptosis in osteosarcoma, as confirmed by various cell death inhibitors. SFN-mediated ferroptotic cell death is characterized by increased ROS generation, enhanced lipid peroxidation, and GSH depletion, along with a marked downregulation of SLC7A11 expression. Mechanistically, SFN directly interacts with p62, strengthening the p62/SLC7A11 interaction and promoting the lysosomal degradation of SLC7A11, ultimately triggering ferroptosis [107]. In small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) and multidrug-resistant H69AR cells, SFN significantly inhibited cell growth and induced ferroptosis, as confirmed by the reversal of these effects with ferroptosis inhibitors and by the accumulation of lipid ROS, which was associated with significant downregulation of SLC7A11 at both mRNA and protein levels [108].

In addition to its anticancer properties, SFN also protects non-cancerous cells. By preventing oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and apoptosis, SFN has been demonstrated to mitigate the lung damage caused by cigarette smoke in human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) [109]. Besides its regulation of the p62/SLC7A11 axis, SFN also modulates other ferroptotic signaling pathways. SIRT3, a mitochondrial deacetylase implicated in various clinical disorders is often dysregulated in cancer and has been demonstrated to influence the tumor initiation and progression. Notably, SIRT3 can activate the AMPK/mTOR pathway, a crucial regulator involved in development and metabolism of cancer cells. Treatment of HCT-116 colorectal cancer cells with sulforaphane (4–16 µM for 12–48 h) significantly decreased cell viability in a concentration and time-dependent manner. The expression of SIRT3, AMP-activated protein kinase and mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin) was all elevated by sulforaphane whereas the expression of SLC7A11 was downregulated in a dose-dependent manner. These findings suggest that sulforaphane may induce ferroptosis in colorectal cancer cells through activation of SIRT3/AMPK/mTOR signaling axis [110]. Furthermore, SFN reduces the development of cancer resistance by triggering alternative death inducing pathways, inducing apoptosis at lower concentrations whereas ferroptosis occurs at higher concentration levels [111].

The pentacyclic triterpenoid corosolic acid (CA), also known as 2α-hydroxyursolic acid is predominantly found in the leaves of the Banaba tree (Lagerstroemia speciosa). It was first identified for its antidiabetic properties but it is also recognized for its anticancer potential [112]. In lung cancer cells, CA induces ferroptosis by inhibiting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and regulating the antioxidant defense system. Mechanistically, CA does not act directly on GPX4; rather it suppresses glutathione synthetase (GSS) expression by inhibiting the activity of Yes-associated protein (YAP) leading to decreased intracellular glutathione (GSH) levels, enhanced ferroptosis and further inhibited EMT [113]. Additionally, corosolic acid isolated from the roots of Actinidia valvata Dunn. was found to promote ferroptosis in liver cancer cells by increasing the expression of homocysteine-inducible ER protein with a ubiquitin-like domain 1 (HERPUD1). This process impairs synthesis of GSH and triggers ferroptotic cell death. These findings were further supported by in vivo studies, which demonstrated CA suppressed tumor growth by enhancing HERPUD1-mediated ferroptosis [114]. In Capan-1 and BxPC-3 pancreatic cancer cells, CA treatment suppresses proliferation and migration while promoting cell death in a concentration-dependent manner. In vivo research revealed that CA reduced pulmonary metastasis by decreasing the number of metastatic foci. Proteomic analysis revealed that CA induces SAT1-dependent ferroptosis, and co-treatment with ferrostatin-1 reversed CA-induced apoptosis, highlighting ferroptosis as a key mechanism for its anticancer property [115].

Collectively, these studies highlight induction of ferroptosis in various cancer types by phytochemicals through effective modulation of multiple ferroptosis-related signaling pathways. Phytochemicals including berberine, sulforaphane, quercetin, curcumin, and corosolic acid induce ferroptotic cell death by promoting iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and reactive oxygen species generation. They also disrupt cellular antioxidant defense system of cancer cells through the regulation of key proteins or transcription factors including GPX4, SLC7A11, GSH, NRF2, and associated signaling networks. These findings demonstrate the diverse roles of natural products in ferroptosis regulation and offer encouraging approaches for cancer treatment. However, further studies are required to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms and validate their clinical efficacy and safety in human cancers.

Modulation of JAK–STAT pathway by phytochemicals

Numerous growth factors and cytokines can activate the JAK/STAT pathway, a crucial signaling cascade that regulates gene expression and affects cell activation, proliferation, and differentiation. Cellular receptors, JAK, and STAT proteins are the three main components of this pathway [116]. There are four members of the human JAK (Janus Kinase) family: TYK2, JAK1, JAK2, and JAK3. A Src homology 2 (SH2) domain, a pseudokinase domain, an N-terminal FERM domain, and a conserved protein tyrosine kinase (PTK) domain are among the various unique domains found in each JAK protein. The STAT (signal transducer and activator of transcription) family includes seven members including STAT1, STAT2, STAT3, STAT4, STAT5a, STAT5b and STAT6. These STAT proteins are structurally similar and play a prominent role as transcription factors in cancer [117].

Binding of cytokine-related glycoproteins, including growth factors, interleukins, and interferons with target cells cytokine receptors (CRs) promote JAK proteins activation by transphosphorylation. Activated JAKs then phosphorylate tyrosine residues on CRs, providing docking sites for STAT proteins. Once recruited, STATs are phosphorylated, phosphorylated STATs form homo- or heterodimers in the cytoplasm, and translocate to the nucleus. Upon nuclear translocation, STAT dimers bind with DNA and regulate transcription of genes including myc, p21, and Bcl-xl. Unphosphorylated STATs can also translocate to the nucleus, interact with heterochromatin protein 1 (HP1), and promote heterochromatin formation, influencing gene transcription as depicted in Fig. 6. Although STATs mainly activate gene expression, their unphosphorylated forms can suppress expression of certain gene by interacting with HP1α and may function as tumor suppressors [118]. Constitutive phosphorylation of STAT1, STAT3, and STAT5 has been observed in different cancer types including breast, lung, liver, and head and neck, largely driven by elevated cytokine levels acting through autocrine and paracrine signaling, along with increased expression of cytokine receptors. Constitutively active STAT proteins are recognized as key oncogenes consequently their regulation has emerged as a promising strategy for developing new cancer therapies [119, 120]. Several phytochemicals including resveratrol, curcumin, diosgenin, and andrographolide effectively block JAK1/2 and STAT3/5 activity, thereby exhibiting promising anticancer potential [121, 122]. The chemical structures of some of the phytochemicals that have shown chemopreventive potential, discussed in this review has been provided in Figs. 7 and 8.

Cucurbitacins are a group of tetracyclic triterpenoids widely distributed in plants and classified into 12 types, from cucurbitacin A to T. Among them, cucurbitacin B (CuB) is commonly isolated from members of the Cucurbitaceae family. Structurally, CuB is characterized by a single bond between carbon atoms C-1 and C-2, and an acetyl group at the C-25 position [123, 124]. CuB exhibits potent anticancer activity against colorectal cancer (CRC) cells through apoptosis induction. CuB inhibits the phosphorylation of JAK2 and STAT3 and their nuclear translocation. Binding studies revealed direct interaction between CuB and STAT3. Beyond its effects on cancer cells, CuB also modulated the tumor microenvironment by suppressing M2 macrophages polarization through downregulation of JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. Importantly, CuB-induced reprogramming of M2-like macrophages leads to decreased migration potential of CRC cells, suggesting its dual antitumor action through both direct tumor targeting and modulation of the tumor microenvironment [125]. In CD133⁺ HepG2 liver cancer stem cells (CSCs), CuB reduced cell viability, suppressed sphere and colony formation, and induced cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase. These effects were associated with downregulation of the expression of key regulatory proteins, including cyclin B1, CDK1, CD133, phosphorylated JAK2 (p-JAK2), and phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3), suggesting that its antitumor activity is mediated through inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway [126]. Moreover, in H358 non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells, CuB suppressed STAT3 phosphorylation. Silencing STAT3 silencing amplified CuB-induced ferroptosis while STAT3 overexpression reversed these effects, which indicates that CuB promotes ferroptosis by targeting STAT3 [127].

Cucurbitacin I (CuI) suppressed the proliferation of four human pancreatic cancer cell lines (SW 1990, ASPC-1, BXPC-3, CFPAC-1) significantly by inducing apoptosis and G2/M phase cell cycle arrest. These effects were mediated by activation of the caspase cascade and inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. Moreover, in hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG2) cells, CuI inhibited the expression of STAT3, MAPK, JAK2, mTOR and Akt (protein kinase B) in a dose-dependent manner that are involved in multiple oncogenic pathways including MAPK, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and JAK2/STAT3 [128, 129].

Caffeic acid (3,4-dihydroxycinnamic acid) isolated from the bark of Eucalyptus globulus and from the herb Dipsacus asperoides is a bioactive polyphenolic compound [130] with numerous bioactive properties owing to its unique chemical structure which includes a double bond in the carbon chain and phenolic hydroxyl groups in a catechol ring. These characteristics make caffeic acid (CF) a strong pro-oxidant and antioxidant. Since ROS can damage proteins, lipids, and DNA, CF mainly functions as an intracellular antioxidant [131]. CF demonstrates strong anticancer effects against prostate cancer cells by enhancing apoptosis, inhibiting proliferation, and suppressing migration and invasion in prostate cancer PC-3 cells. These effects are associated with the downregulation of STAT3 expression and anti-apoptotic protein expression [132]. Furthermore, CF treatment suppressed the growth of both PC-3 and LNCaP cells in a concentration-dependent manner by inducing cell cycle arrest, ROS production and apoptosis induction. CF also downregulated IL-6 (interleukin-6), JAK1 and p-STAT3 expression resulting in reduced levels of CDK1, cyclin D1 and cyclin D2. These findings suggest that CF exerts its anticancer effect primarily through inhibition of the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway [133].

Ailanthone (AIL), a water-soluble pentacyclic diterpene lactone obtained from the bark of Ailanthus altissima, is another intriguing natural compound with anticancer potential. By targeting multiple signaling pathways and molecular regulators, AIL demonstrates strong anticancer efficacy [134]. AIL significantly suppressed cell proliferation and migration in bladder cancer cell lines (5637 and T24) while promoting apoptosis in a concentration-dependent manner. These effects were accompanied by an elevation of apoptosis-related protein expression and a reversal of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), suggesting its role in suppressing tumor invasiveness. Additionally, in vivo research demonstrated that AIL treatment reduced tumor growth and decreased transcription levels of JAK/STAT pathway activators including IL-6, IL-10, and IL-23. Notably, AIL inhibited JAK1, JAK2 and STAT3 phosphorylation in tumor tissues without altering total protein expression, suggesting that pathway activation was disrupted instead of protein expression. These findings demonstrate that AIL suppresses phosphorylation cascades involved in tumor progression, thereby inhibiting JAK/STAT3-mediated oncogenic signaling [135]. Apart from its effects on bladder cancer, AIL also suppressed the proliferative potential of colorectal cancer cell lines (HCT116 and SW620) by induction of cell cycle arrest. This effect was mediated through the JAK3/STAT3 pathway inhibition and downregulation of G2/M phase regulators including CDK1 and cyclin B1 [136]. Importantly, Ailanthone complies with Lipinski’s rule of five, indicating favourable oral bioavailability and reinforcing its potential as a strong oral anticancer drug candidate [137].

Significant anticancer potential has also been demonstrated by naringenin, a naturally occurring flavanone commonly present in citrus fruits, including grapefruit, tomatoes, cherries, and also in a variety of medicinal herbs [138]. Naringenin was found to target the STAT3-MGST2 signaling pathway in osteosarcoma thereby inhibiting tumor progression. Microsomal glutathione S-transferase 2 (MGST2), which is highly expressed in osteosarcoma cells, confers resistance to ferroptosis and encourages cell invasion, migration, and proliferation. Naringenin reduced MGST2 expression by blocking STAT3 activation. Additionally, the effects of naringenin were reversed by treatment with a STAT3 agonist (Colivelin) establishing STAT3 as a crucial mediator of its anticancer activity [139]. Furthermore, naringenin stimulates apoptosis in HepG2 cells by regulating ROS-mediated JAK2/STAT3 signaling and activating caspase-3. These findings indicate that naringenin is a viable multifunctional drug for cancer therapy since it induces both apoptosis and ferroptosis through the inhibition of important oncogenic pathways [140]. Beyond its anticancer activity, naringenin also displayed anti-inflammatory effects. In a mouse model of atopic dermatitis, it inhibited the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway resulting in decreased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-γ, IL-12, and IL-5) [141].

Naringin, the glycoside form of naringenin also shows significant anticancer properties [142]. Naringin co administration with doxorubicin synergistically suppressed the proliferation and migration of MCF-7 breast cancer cells through inhibition of important survival and angiogenic proteins including STAT3, JAK1, Bcl-2, Survivin and VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factoral and increased Bax expression [143]. In oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (Eca109) cells, Naringin treatment significantly inhibited cell proliferation, colony formation and invasive capacity and promoted apoptosis. These effects were associated with upregulation of the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins such as Bax, cytochrome c (Cyt c), caspase-3 and caspase-9, and suppression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2. Additionally, Naringin modulated several key signaling molecules, including increased levels of p-STAT3, p-AKT, AKT, and p-JAK2, suggesting a complex regulatory role. These findings further reinforce naringenin’s multifaceted anticancer potential through the modulation of apoptosis-related pathways among various cancer types [144].

Among the phytochemicals discussed in this section, most have been reported to act on this pathway by directly inhibiting the STAT3 activation along with the downregulation of JAK2 activity. Moreover, these phytochemicals modulate the STAT3-regulated gene expression, including cyclin D1, Bcl-2, surviving, VEGF and MCL-1 while activating caspase-3, caspase-7 and caspase-9 expression. They may also influence the JAK/STAT signaling pathway indirectly by downregulating the expression of upstream activators such as interleukins. However, most of the current studies on the effects of phytochemicals on JAK/STAT signaling in cancer are restricted to in vitro studies. Thus, additional in vivo investigations are needed to validate their therapeutic potential in cancer treatment through inhibition of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway.

Natural compounds combating cancer cell migration

Cancer cells have potential to migrate from their original location to other parts of the body, a process known as metastasis [145]. In most instances, metastatic cancer is difficult to cure, making it the primary cause of cancer-related deaths and accounting for more than 90% of such fatalities [146]. There are two main types of cell migration: individual cell movement and collective cell movement [147]. Tumor progression involves several stages, beginning with the migration and invasion of cancer cells into the extracellular matrix (ECM). This invasion allows them to penetrate surrounding tissues and access both the bloodstream and lymphatic vessels. The process is tightly regulated and is impacted by numerous factors such as matrix-degrading enzymes, integrins, adhesion molecules and communication signals exchanged between cells within the tissue environment. An important event in migration is the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) which triggers cell mobility and ultimately facilitates the spread of cancer to distant organs [148, 149]. Cells change from a structured epithelial form to a more mobile mesenchymal state during EMT. E-cadherin and occludins are examples of adhesion proteins that hold epithelial cells together. Downregulation of these proteins causes the cells to separate from the surrounding matrix and from each other. Mesenchymal markers, including vimentin and N-cadherin are then expressed by them supporting their new more adaptable and migrating behaviour [150]. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) play an important role in the progression of cancer by promoting tumor invasion and metastasis [151]. These enzymes aid cancer cells in overcoming the structural barriers that hold them in place by breaking down the components of the extracellular matrix. MMP-2 and MMP-9 contribute to the breakdown of type IV collagen and the basement membrane, two important components that typically prevent the spread of cancer. This degradation allows tumor cells to enter surrounding tissues, invade the circulatory or lymphatic systems and eventually establish secondary tumors in distant organs [152]. Interestingly, phytochemicals which are bioactive substances present in plants disrupt important stages of metastasis including EMT, ECM breakdown and MMP activity. This suggests that phytochemicals may be used to prevent and inhibit the spread of cancer [153].

The well-known natural oxycoumarin auraptene (7-geranyloxycoumarin), present in citrus fruits, is known to possess immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [154]. According to recent research, Auraptene (AUR) (20 µM concentration), efficiently prevented the migration and invasion of B16F10 melanoma cells without significantly affecting healthy fibroblasts. Furthermore, AUR inhibited MMP-2 and MMP-9 two important matrix metalloproteinases that are essential for cancer cell invasion and metastasis. In-silico study further supported these results, which showed that auraptene exhibited stable interactions with the active sites of both wild-type and mutant MMP-2 and MMP-9. These findings are consistent with those of gelatin zymography, which supports auraptene’s ability to inhibit these proteases [155]. According to scratch wound healing and Transwell assays, AUR significantly decreased U87 glioblastoma cell invasion and migration at sublethal concentrations of 12.5 and 25 µg/ml respectively. qRT-PCR and zymography tests showed that AUR decreased mRNA expression and activity of MMP-2 and MMP-9. Western blot research further revealed that auraptene-treated cells had lower levels of MMP-2 protein and phosphorylated p-JNK and p-mTOR two proteins linked to metastasis. Molecular docking studies consistently demonstrated that AUR exhibits a significant affinity towards MMP-2/-9, the ATP binding site of mTOR, and JNK1/2/3 [156]. A combination of alpha-lipoic acid and AUR effectively inhibited the migration and metastasis of U87 cells, as indicated by the reduced MMP-2/-9 expression both at mRNA and protein levels [157]. Another study demonstrated that AUR inhibited the proliferation and migration of CT26 (colorectal carcinoma) cells by modulating the expression of MMP-2, MMP-9, E-cadherin, VEGF-A, and VEGFR1 genes. Accordingly, the expression levels of MMP-2 and MMP-9 proteins were significantly reduced [158].

Galangin (3,5,7-trihydroxyflavone), a flavonoid present in linden, propolis, Alpinia officinarum, and various other medicinal plants, demonstrates considerable anticancer efficacy via chemopreventive and therapeutic pathways against several cancers, including renal, hepatic, and gastric malignancies [159]. In glioblastoma cells, galangin (GLN) inhibited cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by downregulating the expression of Skp2 (S-phase kinase associated protein 2) and key mesenchymal markers, viz. Zeb1, N-cadherin, Snail, and Vimentin. Overexpression of Skp2 attenuated these effects, suggesting its critical role in GLN-mediated suppression of EMT [160]. Another study reported that in cervical cancer (HeLa) cells, GLN (40 µM) significantly reduced cell viability, migration, and invasion. It upregulated E-cadherin and downregulated N-cadherin, indicating EMT inhibition. Transcriptomic and protein analyses further revealed that GLN suppressed tumor progression by modulating the Hippo/YAP signaling pathway, as evidenced by increased p-YAP and reduced total YAP levels [161]. GLN has also shown potent anti-metastatic activity in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells by reversing EMT and promoting apoptosis. It also inhibited the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway and enhanced PTEN (Phosphatase and Tensin Homolog) expression, thereby reducing cell proliferation [162].

Loureirin A and Loureirin B, flavonoids isolated from the Thai traditional medicinal plant Dracaena loureiri, have demonstrated notable anti-metastatic properties in cancer research. These natural compounds markedly inhibited the migration and invasion of non-small cell lung cancer cell lines (A549 and H1299). Mechanistically, they exerted these effects by suppressing the enzymatic activity of matrix metalloproteinases MMP-2 and MMP-9, and downregulating the expression of critical invasion-related proteins including urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA), its receptor (uPAR), and membrane-type 1 matrix metalloproteinase (MT1-MMP). Furthermore, Loureirin A and B effectively attenuated EMT by reducing the expression of mesenchymal markers including N-cadherin, vimentin, and fibronectin. These compounds inhibited the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway by downregulating the phosphorylation of ERK (extracellular signal-related kinase), JNK, and p38 proteins, highlighting their potential in targeting metastatic signaling cascades [163]. Loureirin also plays a role in targeting multiple metastatic mechanisms across various cancer types, as evidenced by its ability to suppress the invasive and migratory potential of human melanoma cell lines A375 and A875. This suppression may be achieved by inhibiting the protein kinase B (AKT)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway [164].

Complementing these findings, anthocyanins (Delphinidin, Petunidin, Pelargonidin, Malvidin, and Cyanidin) have emerged as another class of natural compounds with potent anti-cancer properties [165, 166]. These compounds are known to inhibit the progression of several human cancer types, including lung [167], hepatocellular [168], and cervical carcinoma [169]. These effects are mediated through numerous mechanisms, including stimulation of apoptosis, suppression of angiogenesis, and reduction in invasivenes [165, 166].

Among these, cyanidin-3-glucoside (C3G), an anthocyanin isolated from the Thai black rice Luempua cultivar, has been demonstrated to modulate the EMT process in prostate cancer (PC3) cells. The anti-cancer effect of C3G is mediated by inhibiting cytoskeletal-associated proteins, thereby limiting cancer cell motility. Additionally, C3G delays tumor progression by promoting E-cadherin production and inhibition of the Smad/Snail signaling pathway. Furthermore, cancer cell invasion and migration are suppressed by downregulating the expression of MMP-9 and NF-κB [170]. Although C3G at concentrations up to 50 µM has no effect on metabolism or cell viability, it markedly lowers the p-NFκB/NFκB ratio in LPS-stimulated leukemic cells and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-8, and CCL2. Furthermore, by downregulating Rho-GTP expression, it prevents cell migration, indicating that it can regulate inflammatory and migratory responses in cancer [171]. Chemo-resistant colorectal cancer cells (HCT-116-ROx) exhibit enhanced migratory potential and a mesenchymal phenotype. C3G treatment reversed EMT in these cells through the downregulation of vimentin and N-cadherin expression and the restoration of E-cadherin expression [172], . These findings support the medicinal potential of anthocyanins in metastasis prevention.

Resveratrol, chemically known as 3,5,4′-trihydroxystilbene, is a polyphenolic stilbene found in a variety of plant-based foods including peanuts, blueberries, grapes, cranberries, rhubarb, beans, and eucalyptus. Its widespread occurrence in nature allows for enables its easy isolation in purified form. Besides its known benefits in metabolic disorders, such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases, resveratrol has demonstrated significant anti-cancer effects in various experimental models [173]. In a study, resveratrol inhibited migration and invasion of cisplatin-resistant oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cells within a non-toxic dose range (25–75 µM) treatment, resulting in a concentration-dependent suppression of these metastatic behaviors. A notable finding was the reduction in the phosphorylated forms of ERK and p38 proteins, along with a decreased MMP-2 and MMP-9 expression, key molecules involved in extracellular matrix degradation and metastasis. Importantly, total levels of ERK, p38, and JNK remained unaffected, suggesting that resveratrol specifically disrupts their activation rather than their overall expression. Since ERK phosphorylation plays a critical role in promoting cancer cell metastasis, its inhibition is considered a viable therapeutic approach [174].

In addition, resveratrol encapsulated in nanoparticle formulations has shown similar anti-metastatic effects in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) by downregulating of MMP-2 expression and interfering with ERK signaling [175]. Further evidence from studies on cervical cancer cell lines (HeLa and SiHa) revealed that resveratrol inhibits cell proliferation and metastasis by preventing STAT3 activation at the Tyr705 residue. This effect was associated with reduced levels of mesenchymal markers, including N-cadherin, vimentin, MMP-3, and MMP-13, and increased E-cadherin expression, indicating a reversal of EMT [176]. Altogether, resveratrol exerts multifaceted anti-metastatic effects by modulating signaling pathways such as MAPK and STAT3, as well as suppressing matrix-degrading enzymes.

These studies combinedly demonstrate that phytoconstituents can significantly affect the cancer metastasis process by multiple mechanisms, including upregulation of epithelial markers (E cadherin), downregulation of mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin, Fibronectin), suppression of matrix metalloproteases (particularly MMP2 & MMP9), and modulation of other signaling pathways affecting tumor cell mobility like MAPK, ERK, and mTOR, etc. By targeting these different markers or proteins, phytochemicals limit the tumor invasion and migration, underscoring their potential role in the regulation of metastatic progression.

Redox modulation in cancer cells by phytochemicals

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are highly reactive, partially reduced forms of oxygen that have been extensively studied for their roles in various human diseases, particularly cancer. The term “ROS” collectively refers to molecules such as singlet oxygen (1O2), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hypochlorous acid (HOCl), superoxide anion (O2⁻), and hydroxyl radicals (⋅OH) [177]. ROS are generated through multiple cellular processes, particularly during metabolism. A major source of ROS is the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Other endogenous sources include oxidases, peroxisomes, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, metabolites, and immune cells like phagocytes. External factors including air pollution, cigarette smoke, alcohol consumption, and exposure to ultraviolet (UV) radiations can also increase ROS production either by introducing ROS directly or stimulating their formation inside cells [178]. Under normal physiological conditions, cells maintain redox homeostasis to regulate ROS levels [179]. This balance is achieved through a complex antioxidant defense system. Enzymatic antioxidants including superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), glutathione reductase, and catalase, together with non-enzymatic antioxidants like glutathione (GSH) and vitamins C, D, and E, work to neutralize excess ROS and prevent cellular damage [180].

Nrf2-ARE signaling pathway

One of the major protective systems against oxidative stress and harmful xenobiotics is the Keap1-Nrf2-antioxidant response element (ARE) signaling pathway [181]. Nrf2 activity is primarily regulated through its interaction with Keap1. Under normal conditions, Keap1 functions as a substrate adaptor for the CUL3-based E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, targeting Nrf2 for rapid degradation by the 26 S proteasome [182]. Upon exposure to oxidative stress, Nrf2 dissociates from Keap1 and translocates to the nucleus. Elevated nuclear levels of Nrf2 enable its binding to the antioxidant response element (ARE), located in the promoter regions of genes involved in xenobiotic metabolism, antioxidant defense, and other metabolic processes such as heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), glutamate-cysteine ligase (GCL), GSH, and GPx [183, 184]. These enzymes help eliminate ROS and damaged cell components, thereby reducing oxidative stress and inflammation [185]. Nrf2 activation enhances detoxification and antioxidant defense system, and plays a vital role in protection against carcinogenesis [186]. Plant-derived polyphenols have emerged as potent modulators of the Nrf2 signaling pathway, a central regulator of cellular antioxidant defense mechanisms, with strong implications for cancer chemoprevention [187, 188]. The number and position of hydroxyl groups on the phenolic structure play a critical role in determining their bioactivity [189]; compounds with multiple hydroxyl substitutions exhibit superior antioxidant properties compared to those lacking such groups [190]. Quercetin forms four hydrogen bonds with KEAP-1 and modulates the interaction between Nrf2 and Keap1, leading to Nrf2 activation [191]. Similarly, caffeic acid and protocatechuic acid, both rich in hydroxyl groups, significantly upregulated the expression of Nrf2-regulated genes, including HO-1, glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC), and glutamate-cysteine ligase modifier subunit (GCLM), and enhanced NQO1 activity in HCT-116 colorectal cancer cells. Furthermore, intraperitoneal administration of these compounds to Ehrlich ascites carcinoma-bearing mice resulted in significant suppression of tumor growth and angiogenesis, demonstrating their in vivo efficacy [192]. Similarly, resveratrol and gallic acid exhibited concentration-dependent inhibition of cell proliferation in PC3 and DU145 prostate cancer cell lines. In PC3 cells, both polyphenols lowered ROS levels, whereas in DU145 cells, only gallic acid significantly decreased ROS levels. Furthermore, intracellular glutathione (GSH) levels were increased, both resveratrol and gallic acid enhanced the activities of antioxidant enzymes including activities, such as SOD, GR, GPX, and catalase (CAT). The expression of Nrf2 and HO-1 genes was also elevated in both cell lines, confirming their role in enhancing the antioxidant capacity. By modifying Nrf2 signaling and related antioxidant enzymes, these results collectively demonstrate the anticancer potential of hydroxy-substituted phenolic compounds [193].

Nigella sativa seeds, commonly known as black cumin are a source of bioactive phytochemical Thymoquinone (TQ), which is known to prevent inflammation and oxidative stress by modulating two signaling pathways, namely Nrf2 and NF-κB [194]. In triple-negative breast cancer cell lines, viz. MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468, TQ treatment significantly upregulated the expression of genes associated with the oxidative stress response, such as PRNP, NQO1, and GCLM. Higher expression levels were observed in MDA-MB-468 cells as compared to MDA-MB-231 cells, indicating a differential cell line-specific response. An increase in Nrf2 mRNA and protein levels was also observed following TQ treatment, corroborating its role in activating the Nrf2 pathway [195]. Similarly, 4,4′-dimethoxychalcone (DMC), a methoxylated chalcone derivative, has demonstrated the ability to modulate Nrf2 signaling through a different mechanism. DMC was found to inhibit ferrochelatase (FECH) activity while promoting the proteasomal degradation of Keap1, the negative regulator of Nrf2, leading to enhanced nuclear translocation of Nrf2 and subsequent upregulation of HO-1, a downstream target gene of Nrf2. The combined effect of FECH inhibition and HO-1 induction triggered iron accumulation and ferroptotic cell death, suggesting that DMC induces ferroptosis through coordinated activation of the Keap1/Nrf2/HO-1 axis [196].

Dihydrotanshinone I (DHT I), a phenanthraquinone compound derived from Salvia miltiorrhiza Bunge, has demonstrated promising anti-cancer effects across various tumor types. It induces ferroptosis and apoptosis, inhibits cancer cell proliferation, and suppresses tumor invasion and metastasis [197]. In gallbladder cancer cells, DHT I exerts its anti-tumor effects by modulating Nrf2 activity through a dual mechanism, either by enhancing Keap1-mediated degradation of Nrf2 or by concurrently inhibiting Nrf2 phosphorylation. This results in decreased expression of Nrf2-regulated antioxidant genes, thereby promoting oxidative stress, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis [198]. Similarly, in ovarian cancer (HO8910PM and ES2) cells, DHT I inhibited cell viability by increasing ROS accumulation and inducing oxidative stress. This was achieved by suppressing Nrf2 nuclear translocation and downregulating the expression of Nrf2 and p62 while elevating Keap1 expression. DHT I treatment effectively delayed tumor growth in HO8910PM and ES2 xenograft nude mice, reduced HE4 and CA125 levels, and restored normal expression of Ki67, Nrf2, p62, Keap1, Bcl-2, Cyclin B1, Cdc2, SOD, and CAT in vivo [199].

Similarly, xanthohumol (XN), a prenylated flavonoid predominantly found in the flowers of the hop plant (Humulus lupulus), has gained attention for its potent antioxidant properties and applications in both dietary supplements and cosmetics [200]. In pancreatic cancer cell lines PANC-1 and Mia-Pa-Ca-2, XN has shown anti-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic activities through the activation of Nrf2 and the upregulation of downstream antioxidant and detoxifying related genes including SOD, NQO1, and GSTP [201]. Moreover, combined treatment with XN and phenethyl isothiocyanate has been shown to induce apoptosis in PSN-1 pancreatic cancer cells by modulating Nrf2 and concurrently inhibiting key survival pathways, including NF-κB, STAT3, and Akt/P70S6K. Interestingly, this combination also demonstrated a cytoprotective effect in non-cancerous (MS1) endothelial cells through Nrf2 activation, suggesting a selective action of these phytochemicals combination in targeting cancer cells while preserving normal cellular function [202].

Collectively, the studies highlight the potential of phytochemicals in modulating the Keap1–Nrf2–ARE signaling pathway through multiple molecular mechanisms. These mechanisms include promotion of Nrf2 nuclear translocation, disruption of the Keap1–Nrf2 interaction, modulation of oxidative stress responses and regulation of antioxidant and detoxification enzymes. Several phytochemicals, including dihydrotanshinone I, thymoquinone, dimethoxychalcone, polyphenols, and xanthohumol, have been reported to modulate Nrf2 signaling, resulting in decreased oxidative damage, cancer cell growth suppression, apoptosis or ferroptosis induction, and inhibition of tumor development and progression. These findings support the potential of Nrf2 as a promising target for cancer chemoprevention and therapeutic intervention.

Phytochemicals as apoptosis inducers

Apoptosis is a form of programmed cell death that occurs in both healthy and cancerous cells, alongside other regulated forms of cell death such as necroptosis, ferroptosis, autophagy, and pyroptosis. This process is characterized by distinct features, including membrane blebbing, externalization of phosphatidylserine, and the activation of caspase family enzymes (cysteine-aspartic proteases). Apoptosis is primarily controlled through two signaling pathways: the extrinsic and intrinsic pathways [203, 204]. The extrinsic pathway of apoptosis is initiated when ligands such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF), TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL), or FAS ligand (also known as APO-1) bind to their respective surface receptors—TNFR, TRAILR, or FAS [203]. Following ligand binding, death receptors form homotrimers and initiate the assembly of cytoplasmic signaling complexes through their intracellular death domains (DDs). These domains attract adaptor proteins, specifically TNFR1-associated death domain protein (TRADD) for TNF receptors and FAS-associated death domain protein (FADD) for FAS receptors. These adaptor proteins possess a death effector domain (DED), which facilitates the recruitment of inactive initiator caspase-8. This recruitment leads to the formation of the Death-Inducing Signaling Complex (DISC), where caspase-8 is activated through oligomerization. Upon activation, caspase 8 and caspase 10 trigger the activation of caspases 3, 6, and 7, the downstream effector caspases [205, 206].

However, the intrinsic pathway relies on mitochondria dependent signals rather than surface receptors to induce apoptosis. These signals include both positive stimuli, such as radiation, hypoxia, poisons, free radicals, and viral infections, as well as negative stimuli including absence of hormones or growth factors. These stress signals stimulate the release of cytochrome c from the mitochondria by altering the inner mitochondrial membrane. Cytochrome c subsequently binds to procaspase-9 and apoptotic protease-activating factor 1 (APAF-1), leading to activation of caspase-9 and ultimately inducing cancer cell death [207, 208].

The Bcl-2 protein family, comprising of proapoptotic proteins (Bax, Bak, Bad) and antiapoptotic proteins (Bcl-2, Bcl-xl, MCL-1), plays a pivotal role in regulating the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. Proapoptotic proteins promote cell death by permeabilizing the mitochondrial outer membrane, leading to the release of cytochrome c, whereas antiapoptotic members inhibit apoptosis process [206]. Cancer cells may avoid apoptosis by disrupting the balance of Bcl-2 family proteins, either through upregulation of antiapoptotic proteins or suppression of proapoptotic proteins. Therefore, to counteract apoptosis evasion in cancer cells, potential approaches include modulation of Bcl-2 family proteins which can be accomplished through post-translational changes, transcriptional control, or by specifically targeting and blocking the activity of antiapoptotic proteins [209].

The rich pharmacological properties of andrographolide, a labdane diterpenoid molecule isolated from Andrographis paniculata, have drawn a lot of attention, particularly for its potential application in cancer treatment [210]. One of the key mechanisms through which andrographolide exerts its anticancer effects is the induction of apoptosis in cancer cells. Andrographolide inhibits the growth of gastric cancer cells (SGC7901 and AGS) in a dose-dependent manner, with IC50 values of 38 µM and 44 µM, respectively. This inhibition is associated with the suppression of colony formation in SGC7901 cells, via p53-dependent pathway. Furthermore, andrographolide induces apoptosis through intrinsic pathway, as evidenced by caspase-9 and − 3 activation, PARP cleavage, and a decrease in the Bcl-2 protein levels [211].

Strong apoptotic effects of andrographolide have also been observed in breast cancer. Research has shown that andrographolide reduces the viability of triple-negative (MDA-MB-231) and estrogen receptor-positive (MCF-7) breast cancer cells in a concentration and timedependent manner. It induces apoptosis in these cell lines through the downregulation of anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 expression and the increase of pro-apoptotic protein Bax expression at both mRNA and protein levels [212]. Similarly, in colon cancer cells, andrographolide exhibits anticancer effects by inducing cell cycle arrest and suppressing Hedgehog (Hh) signaling, a pathway often implicated in cancer progression. The underlying mechanism involves ROS generation, disruption of mitochondrial membrane potential, caspase activation, and changes in the expression of Bcl-2 family proteins. The anticancer potential of andrographolide is further revealed by the fact that it induces cell death by both extrinsic (death receptor-mediated) and intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathways of apoptosis [213].

A significant advancement in the delivery of andrographolide is the development of andrographolide loaded phytosomes (AG-PTMs). These phytosomal formulations significantly enhance the bioavailability of andrographolide and have been shown to suppress the growth of HepG2 liver cancer cells with an IC50 value of 4.02 µM. The modified formulation significantly improves the cellular uptake of AG-PTMs by HepG2 cells and increases apoptosis, particularly in the pre-G1 phase. Along with the upregulation of Bax and the downregulation of Bcl-2 proteins, these effects were associated with the induction of oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. Furthermore, AG-PTMs elevated caspase-3 expression, suggesting apoptosis induction via the mitochondrial pathway [214].

Ferulic acid (FA), a bioactive compound found in traditional medicinal plants such as Spargani rhizome, red root, and Ligusticum chuanxiong, has drawn considerable interest due to its potential therapeutic use. It is known for preventing coronary heart disease, reducing oxidative stress, and skin whitening [215]. In cancer research, FA has demonstrated significant effects on inhibiting CT26 cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis. This process is mediated through the phosphorylation of ERK and JNK, leading to the enhanced expression of key apoptotic proteins like Bax [216]. FA also reduces the viability of breast cancer cells, particularly MDA-MB-231 cells, without harming normal cells. Moreover, FA increases ROS levels in MDA-MB-231 cells, triggering mitochondrial-dependent apoptosis through the MAPK/STAT3/NF-κB signaling pathways [217]. Similarly, Phaeanthine, a bisbenzylisoquinoline alkaloid isolated from Cyclea peltata (Lam) Hook. f & Thoms., has shown selective cytotoxicity toward cervical cancer cells (HeLa), with an IC50 value of 8.11 µM. In vitro analysis confirmed that phaeanthine induces apoptosis through the mitochondrial intrinsic pathway, primarily by downregulating Akt and p-Akt expression. Additionally, phaeanthine decreases the levels of other antiapoptotic proteins, including MCL-1, IGF-2, and XIAP. Mitochondrial membrane depolarization and cytochrome c release further corroborate the involvement of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway in this process [218]. Genistein, a naturally occurring isoflavone found in soybeans, has demonstrated potent anticancer effects primarily through the induction of apoptosis and inhibition of oncogenic pathways across various cancer models [219–221]. In prostate cancer DU145 cells, genistein significantly reduced cell viability and induced apoptosis, as evidenced by an increased sub-G0/G1 cell population and AnnexinV/PI-positive staining by flow cytometry. These changes were associated with elevated levels of ROS, mitochondrial depolarization (indicative of mitochondrial dysfunction) and marked inhibition of ATP production. At the molecular level, genistein treatment upregulated p53 expression at both mRNA and protein levels and activated caspase-3/7, accompanied by the cleavage of Bax, procaspase-3, and PARP. Increasing concentrations of genistein led to a rise in both sub-G0/G1 and G2/M phase cell populations, effects that were attenuated by the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD, indicating caspase-dependent cell death. Importantly, genistein exhibited minimal cytotoxic effects on normal prostate epithelial (HPrEC) cells [222].

In colorectal cancer models involving SW480 and SW620 cells, genistein in combination with 5-fluorouracil enhanced TRAIL-induced apoptosis by upregulating death receptor 5 (DR5) gene expression and surface receptor activation. These combinatorial treatments also significantly downregulated the anti-apoptotic genes DcR1 and XIAP compared to TRAIL monotherapy. Mechanistically, triple combinations of these compounds resulted in a pronounced loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and increased activation of caspases 3, 8, and 9, particularly in SW480 cells [223]. Moreover, in HPV-positive HeLa cervical cancer cells, genistein displayed both antiviral and pro-apoptotic effects. It suppressed the expression of viral oncogenes E6 and E7, which are known to inactivate the tumor suppressors p53 and pRB. Consequently, genistein treatment decreased cell proliferation, induced G0/G1 phase cell cycle arrest, and upregulated p53 and pRB mRNA expression. These effects were accompanied by activation and cleavage of caspases 3, 8, and 9, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic candidate for HPV-associated cervical cancers [224].

Collectively, the investigations discussed above suggests that phytochemicals can trigger apoptosis effectively in different cancer types through the modulation of both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways of apoptosis. These compounds induce apoptosis in cancer cells by regulating key molecular mediators of apoptosis, including death receptors, Bcl-2 family proteins, cytochrome c, caspases and p53. Additionally, several phytochemicals demonstrate pro-apoptotic effects by influencing upstream signaling pathways including Akt, MAPK, NF-κB, and TRAIL signaling. Since apoptosis resistance is a hallmark of cancer, the ability of phytochemicals to modulate multiple apoptotic regulators underscores their potential as adjuvant agents in cancer therapy.

Wnt/β-catenin pathway regulation by phytochemicals

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays an essential role in controlling cell growth and is involved in multiple biological functions, including cell renewal, embryo development, sustaining stem cell properties, tissue repair, and ensuring the stability of adult tissues [225]. Wnt signaling is broadly categorized into canonical (β-catenin-dependent) and non-canonical (β-catenin-independent) pathways. In the absence of Wnt ligands, the cytoplasmic β-catenin levels remain low due to the activity of the β-catenin destruction complex, which comprises Glycogen Synthase Kinase 3-β (GSK3β), Casein Kinase 1α (CK1α), APC (Adenomatous Polyposis Coli), and the scaffolding protein Axin. Under these conditions, TCF/LEF (T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer-binding factor) proteins, along with other factors, function as repressors that inhibit transcription of wnt target genes [226]. When Wnt ligands are present, they bind to the Frizzled (FZD) receptors and the LRP5/6 co-receptors and trigger the activation of the FZD receptor on the receiving cell [227]. This activation leads to the recruitment of Dishevelled (Dvl), which prompts the relocation of the destruction complex to the receptor. Consequently, β-catenin accumulates in the cytoplasm and subsequently translocate into the nucleus, where it interacts with TCF/LEF transcription factors to initiate gene expression [226]. This interaction promotes the expression of key Wnt target genes including c-Myc, survivin, c-Jun, cyclin D1, and Wnt1-inducible signaling pathway protein 1. Aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway can lead to the nuclear accumulation of β-catenin, resulting in uncontrolled cell proliferation [228]. Given its significant role in cancer development, efforts have been made to develop pathway-specific inhibitors for effective cancer treatment. Remarkably, many of these Wnt-targeting anticancer compounds are phytochemicals [229]. Figure 11 provides an overview of phytochemicals-mediated regulation of the Wnt signaling pathway.

Capsaicin, a compound isolated from Capsicum, is a bioactive member of the capsaicinoid/vanilloid family that functions as an agonist of the TRPV1 receptor. Structurally, capsaicin consists of three parts: a vanillyl group, an amide linker, and a hydrophobic side chain. The vanillyl group is critical for its activity, providing hydrogen bond donor and acceptor sites that facilitate its strong agonistic binding to TRPV1, a tetrameric ion channel with intracellular N- and C-termini [230]. Beyond its well-known sensory effects, capsaicin exhibits promising anticancer properties. In prostate cancer stem cells (CSCs), including PC-3 and DU145 lines, capsaicin downregulates key CSC markers and inhibits cell growth. Mechanistically, capsaicin suppresses components of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway by reducing the expression of Wnt-2, phosphorylated GSK3β, β-catenin, and downstream target genes such as c-Myc and cyclin D1. Activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by LiCl (inhibitor of GSK3β) reverses these effects, highlighting the central role of this pathway in the anticancer activity of capsaicin [231]. Similarly, in breast cancer cells (MDA-MB-231), capsaicin reduces cell viability, induces G2/M cell cycle arrest, decreases CDK8 expression, and inhibits the phosphorylation of key survival kinases PI3K and Akt. Additionally, it downregulates Wnt and β-catenin expression, thereby disrupting proliferative signaling [232].

Dihydrocapsaicin (DHC), another major capsaicinoid found in Capsicum, has also demonstrated potent anticancer effects. In melanoma cell lines including A375 and MV3, DHC significantly inhibited proliferation, migration, and invasion. DHC suppressed tumor growth and lung metastasis in a NOD/SCID mice model. This effect was associated with downregulation of β-catenin, a key regulator of cell proliferation and epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and its downstream targets, viz. cyclin D1, c-Myc, MMP2, and MMP7. Mechanistically, DHC promotes β-catenin degradation by enhancing its ubiquitination through upregulation of the β -transducin repeat containing E3 ubiquitin protein ligase. Overexpression of β-catenin partially reversed the inhibitory effects of DHC, confirming its critical role in mediating DHC’s anticancer activity [233].

Silibinin, a flavonolignan derived from milk thistle (Silybum marianum), inhibited proliferation and migration of HCT-116 cells, induced apoptosis, and suppressed cancer stemness markers, primarily through the downregulation of β-catenin expression [234]. Similarly, diallyl disulfide, an oil-soluble organosulfur compound present in garlic, demonstrates potent anticancer effects [235]. In gastric cancer cells, diallyl disulfide reduces the expression of Wnt1, β-catenin, TCF-4, and their downstream targets (Axin, c-Jun, c-Myc), along with intranuclear and phosphorylated β-catenin expression. Overexpression of retinoid-related orphan receptor α (RORα) further enhanced diallyl disulfide -mediated suppression of the Wnt1/β-catenin pathway, leading to G2/M phase arrest and reduced tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo, whereas RORα knockdown mitigated these effects. Additionally, it inhibited EMT, migration, and invasion by downregulating vimentin, snail, and MMP-9 expression, while elevating E-cadherin and TIMP3 (tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases) expression [236].

Emodin, a naturally occurring anthraquinone, exerts strong anticancer effects through multiple cellular mechanisms. In HepG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma) cells, it inhibited cell proliferation, induced S and G2/M phase cell cycle arrest, triggered apoptosis, and suppressed cancer cell migration and invasion. It also promoted autophagy, leading to degradation of Snail and β-catenin, thereby blocking epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT). These effects were mediated by the inhibition of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and Wnt/β-catenin pathways [237]. Aloe-emodin, a structural analog, showed similar anticancer activity in melanoma and DU145 prostate cancer cells by inducing apoptosis, inducing cell cycle arrest at the G2 phase, and downregulating cyclin D1, c-Myc, Bcl-2, and Wnt signaling components. Molecular docking supported its strong interaction with Wnt2 and β-catenin [238, 239]. Together, these findings highlight the potential of emodin and aloe-emodin as multitargeted anticancer agents that regulate the cell cycle, apoptosis, autophagy, and key oncogenic pathways.

Collectively, these studies highlight the potential of phytochemicals to modulate Wnt/β-catenin axis while simultaneously modulating apoptosis, cancer stemness, EMT, cancer cell proliferation and metastatic progression. Additionally, the phytochemicals target the Wnt/β-catenin axis at various levels such as they reduce the expression of Wnt-2, phosphorylated GSK3β, and β-catenin. They also modulate the expression of downstream targets, such as cyclin D1, c-Myc, MMP2, and MMP7, which highlights their potential as useful candidates for adjuvant therapy and cancer chemoprevention.

A primary barrier lies in the identification, isolation, and characterization of the bioactive phytoconstituents from the vast chemical diversity of plants. The type of extraction technique, environmental factors like altitude, temperature, seasonal variations, and plant variety greatly influence the phytochemical composition, which may lead to inconsistency in dose standardization and complicate reproducibility. Therefore, standardized extraction protocols and regulatory frameworks are crucial for ensuring the uniformity of the product [240, 241].

Another critical challenge is the reduced therapeutic efficacy resulting from poor bioavailability, rapid clearance, and low solubility, as demonstrated in multiple clinical trials. For instance curcumin exhibits an oral bioavailability of less than 1% because of poor absorption and substantial glucuronidation, whereas resveratrol undergoes more than 75% first pass metabolism resulting in low plasma levels [242, 243]. These pharmacokinetic restrictions highlight the discrepancy between limited clinical results and encouraging experimental efficacy.

One potential strategy to overcome these limitations is the discovery of nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems including metallic nanoparticles, dendrimers, liposomes, lipid-based carriers, and polymeric miscelles, which improve the pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic profiles of natural compounds by integrating nanotechnology with phytochemicals. Phytochemicals encapsulated nanoparticles deliver the drug to tumor site directly via active or passive mechanisms, show enhanced systemic retention, improved stability, and increased bioavailability [244, 245]. Several nanoparticle formulations incorporating bioactive phytochemicals have been developed, including curcumin solid lipid nanoparticles [246], berberine-loaded magnetic nanoparticles [247], resveratrol-loaded cationic liposomes [248], and surface-modified genistein phytosome [249]. These formulations show enhanced bioavailability and better target site accumulation, making them promising candidates for cancer drug discovery. Even though nanoparticle-based formulations have demonstrated promise in addressing the pharmacokinetic limits of phytochemicals, insufficient human trials, safety, and regulatory issues continue to hinder their clinical application.

Another important bottleneck in the clinical application is the safety issues that may arise from the interaction of phytoconstituents with drug-metabolizing enzymes and drug transporters. Numerous phytochemicals have been demonstrated to modify cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYPs: a superfamily of hemoproteins containing monooxygenase enzymes involved in the metabolism of chemical compounds and drug detoxification), altering the metabolism of co-administered medications and raising the possibility of toxicity or therapeutic failure. Garlic, for instance, has an inherent antiplatelet property, which may increase bleeding risk when used in conjunction with anticoagulant medications. Green tea catechins have been found to reduce the bioavailability of simvastatin and nadolol by interacting with intestinal transporters. These findings highlight the significance of thoroughly assessing phytochemical-drug interactions before recommending phytochemicals as adjuncts to conventional cancer therapies [250–252].

Conclusion and future perspectives

Phytochemicals are a class of multitargeted agents that modulate various hallmarks of cancer, such as cell proliferation, metastasis, ferroptosis, apoptosis, redox balance, and epigenetic regulation. Among the different classes of compounds discussed, curcumin, resveratrol, berberine, quercetin, and genistein emerge as the promising candidates based on preclinical evidence, mechanistic diversity, the development of new formulations, and the availability of early-phase clinical data. These phytocompounds have generally demonstrated favorable safety profiles in preclinical studies and early clinical investigations and can modulate various carcinogenic signaling pathways.

Despite the significant potential, several critical bottlenecks continue to hinder the clinical translation of phytochemicals. Many natural compounds exhibit limited bioavailability and rapid breakdown, which ultimately restrict their therapeutic efficacy and systemic exposure. Furthermore, high-quality clinical data is limited and the majority of the evidence still comes from in vitro and animal research. Clinical translation is hindered by variations in phytochemical sources, extraction techniques, dosage schedules, and study designs, which further contradict cross-study comparisons.

To facilitate the translation of phytochemicals from bench to bedside, future studies should mainly focus on well-planned, randomized clinical trials employing standardized and bioavailable formulations. Further, combinatorial therapeutic approaches that include integrating phytochemicals with traditional chemotherapy, radiation therapy, or targeted agents should be systematically investigated to maximize efficacy and reduce toxicity. Finally, it is crucial to validate and identify predictive biomarkers for patient stratification and to identify which population is most likely to benefit from phytochemical-based therapies.

In conclusion, phytochemicals represent promising candidates for cancer chemoprevention and adjunctive therapeutic strategies; however, their clinical translation requires further validation through rigorous translational and clinical research. To determine the future role of phytochemicals in precision oncology, translational integration, strategic prioritization of lead compounds, and rigorous clinical evaluation is essential.
