Bone marrow evaluation in neuroblastomas: bone marrow aspirates have little additional value over trephine biopsies
Authors: R S Bruinsma, A van Barneveld, B W Meussen, M Fiocco, A M Heijlaerts-Klever, G den Engelsman, M P Dierselhuis, G A M Tytgat, M M van Noesel, M H W A Wijnen, R R de Krijger, A F W van der Steeg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone marrow involvement is essential for staging, treatment evaluation, and progression/relapse detection in neuroblastoma patients. We investigated the diagnostic accuracy of trephine biopsies (TBs) and the additional value of bone marrow aspirate (BMA) cytomorphology, considering clinical implications.MethodsData were collected from patients diagnosed at our centre over a seven-year period. Complete bone marrow evaluation (bilateral TBs and BMAs) was performed at diagnosis and at predefined timepoints. Cohen’s kappa was used to compare test results. Representativeness was assessed for each TB and BMA and the presence or absence of tumour infiltration was reported.ResultsIn total, 197 neuroblastoma patients underwent bone marrow evaluation, yielding 1880 biopsies (947 procedures) and 1856 aspirates (934 procedures). TBs were representative in 94.9%, BMAs in 76.8% and complete bone marrow evaluation in 98.4%. Bone marrow infiltration was reported in 40.1%. TBs reported infiltration in 35.5% and BMAs in 19.7%. There was almost perfect agreement between TBs and complete evaluation (κ = 0.901, p < 0.001).ConclusionTBs showed higher sensitivity and representativity than BMA cytomorphology. In 4.6% of procedures, BMAs were positive while TBs were negative, but this never led to treatment alteration. These findings suggest that bilateral TBs alone may be sufficient to detect bone marrow involvement.

Introduction

Neuroblastoma is known for its clinical and molecular heterogeneity, and patient outcome varies tremendously [1, 2]. The presence of metastatic disease, along with age and MYCN-amplification, is one of the most powerful predictors of poor outcome [3]. Therefore, the presence of metastatic disease is of paramount importance in the International Neuroblastoma Risk Group (INRG) stratification system [4]. The most common site of metastatic disease is the bone marrow [5, 6]. The 5-year overall survival (OS) of neuroblastoma patients with bone marrow infiltration at diagnosis is 64.6%, compared to 93.4–98.3% for patients without bone marrow infiltration [7]. Furthermore, persistent bone marrow infiltration during high-risk treatment was associated with worse prognosis [8], and by far the most frequent site of relapse is the bone marrow [5, 9]. Bone marrow evaluation is thus essential for staging [4], evaluating treatment response in high-risk patients, and detecting progression or relapse in all neuroblastoma patients [10]. Presence of bone marrow infiltration is assessed using a combination of I-MIGB scintigraphy and morphological examination, with morphological examination being the gold standard [3, 5].

Since 1977, standard practice for bone marrow evaluation has been the combination of bilateral trephine biopsies (TBs) and bilateral bone marrow aspirates (BMAs). Several studies have investigated the accuracy of both techniques, all of which concluded that TBs were more reliable than BMAs, though most still recommended a combination of both techniques [11–15]. Favrot et al. (1986) detected bone marrow infiltration in 38 procedures, with 63% detected exclusively by TBs, 24% by both analyses, and 13% solely by BMAs [12]. Franklin et al. (1983) found TBs to be more effective in 20% of cases, compared to aspirates in just 7% [13]. Mills et al. (1986) detected bone marrow infiltration in 20 patients, in which only one aspirate was positive when the biopsy was negative [14]. Aronica et al. (1998) observed a small difference in reliability, with 8% detected solely by TBs and 6% by BMAs only [11]. More recently, Rastogi et al. (2014) found biopsies and aspirates to be equally reliable [15]. However, most of these studies were conducted in the previous century and examined fewer than a hundred patients, with the exception of Aronica et al. (1998). Furthermore, none of these studies examined the clinical implications of relying solely on TBs [11–15]. Also, diagnostic accuracy of TBs has improved with the addition of the neuroendocrine immunohistochemical (IHC) markers (synaptophysin and chromogranin A) [16, 17], both of which are endorsed by the International Neuroblastoma Response Criteria Bone Marrow Working Group (INRC-BM-WG) [5]. None of the studies mentioned above performed IHC staining using these markers [11–15].

Alternatively, more sensitive methods for the detection of bone marrow infiltration may be used. The INRC-BM-WG recommends immunocytology (IC) on BMAs using GD2 antibodies, ideally combined with a second antibody, e.g. anti-CD56 or anti-PHOX2B [5]. Several studies have demonstrated increased sensitivity of IC compared to cytomorphology of BMAs, especially when combined with fluorescence in situ hybridisation [18–21]. In contrast, studies comparing IC using GD2- and CD56-antibodies with standard of care (cytomorphology and histology) reported higher sensitivity of the latter [18, 19]. Notably, these studies did not report the use of neuroendocrine IHC markers, which may further improve the sensitivity of histology [5, 16, 17]. However, IC might provide additional information when combined with standard practice [5, 16–18].

The INRC-BM-WG also advocates reverse transcriptase–quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RTqPCR) to quantify neuroblastoma-associated mRNAs, particularly tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) and PHOX2B, in clinical trials [5]. Subsequent studies have shown that TH, PHOX2B, and other mRNA markers are of prognostic value and enable detection of minimal residual disease [22–27]. Multiplex RTqPCR, in which multiple molecular markers are combined, may help to address tumour heterogeneity in neuroblastomas [18, 28–30]. However, further research is required before these methods can be implemented into standard clinical practice [5, 18, 31].

To the best of our knowledge, no studies have compared bilateral TBs using neuroendocrine IHC stains with the results of cytomorphology of BMAs. Moreover, no studies have investigated the clinical implications of relying solely on TBs for the bone marrow evaluation of neuroblastoma patients. We aim to evaluate our current standard clinical practice by evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of histological examination of bilateral TBs and cytomorphology of bilateral BMAs, considering the clinical implications of relying solely on TBs. We hypothesise that, given the improved accuracy of TBs using IHC staining, the cytomorphology of BMAs holds less value in the bone marrow evaluation of neuroblastoma patients.

Methods

Patient cohort

Data were obtained from all patients diagnosed with a neuroblastoma in the Princess Máxima Centre for Paediatric Oncology in the Netherlands between November 2014 and December 2021. All bone marrow evaluations performed on these patients during the course of their disease before April 2024 were included. This study was performed following approval by the local biobank committee.

Procedure of bone marrow evaluation

TBs and BMAs were obtained under general anaesthesia or sedation from both sides of the pelvic bone, according to the recommendations of the International Neuroblastoma Response Criteria Bone Marrow Working Group [5, 10]. In addition to HE staining of TBs, immunohistochemical staining was performed with chromogranin A and synaptophysin. Further processing of TBS and BMAs is described in Appendix A.

Bone marrow evaluation

Complete bone marrow evaluation (combined bilateral TBs and bilateral BMAs) was performed at diagnosis, at set timepoints during treatment, progression or relapse, and until the bone marrow was devoid of malignant cells. This is in accordance with the adapted GPOH protocol [10, 32]. Each TB and BMA was assessed for representativeness and tumour infiltration.

Evaluation of TBs

TBs were deemed representative when at least one side was considered representative. This is the case when a minimum of five millimetre hematopoietic bone marrow was available after processing or when tumour infiltration was observed [33].

Tumour infiltration was scored positive when one or both TBs showed tumour infiltration. The extent of bone marrow infiltration was estimated by an experienced pathologist (R.d.K.) and reported using categories with a 5% range: 0%, <5%, 6–10%, etc [5].

Evaluation of BMAs

A single aspirate was deemed representative, partially representative, or non-representative as per the criteria below. When at least one side was considered representative, the BMAs were classified as representative. When both sides were deemed non-representative, they were classified as non-representative. BMAs were considered partially representative if one side was partially representative and the other side non-representative, or if both sides were partially representative.

Aspirates were deemed representative when [1] bone marrow fragments with stromal cells (e.g. osteoblasts, fibroblasts and histocytes) and [2] trilinear hematopoiesis were present. The latter criteria were also met in hypocellular samples, if they were in accordance with the peripheral blood count. Furthermore, in case of massive tumour infiltration (> 30%) an aspirate was considered representative even if not both criteria were met. In other cases, partially representative was given if not both criteria were met. In case of a poor quality aspirate (e.g. clotting in the tube) it was also reported as partially representative [34]. Representativeness was determined by one of the authors (B.W.M.). A senior morphologist was consulted in case of ambiguity.

Tumour infiltration was scored positive when one or both BMAs showed tumour infiltration. Infiltration was only reported when at least three tumour cells were present in a nest or clump. The extent of bone marrow infiltration in aspirates was scored by an experienced morphologist and categorised using the following categories: 0%, <1%, 1–30% and >30% [34]. Tumour percentage could not be determined in partially representative aspirates.

Complete bone marrow evaluation

Complete bone marrow evaluation combines the results of TBs and BMAs for both representativeness and tumour infiltration. To combine results, partially representative BMAs were reclassified as representative when tumour cells were present and as non-representative when no tumour cells were present to reflect clinical decision-making.

Representativeness of complete bone marrow evaluation was classified in two categories: representative and non-representative. A complete evaluation was deemed representative if either biopsies or aspirates were deemed representative.

Tumour infiltration of complete bone marrow evaluation scored positive if either biopsies or aspirates showed tumour infiltration.

Clinical implications

When tumour infiltration was detected by BMAs only, clinical implications were assessed: did tumour infiltration detected by BMAs alone influence clinical decision-making? These investigations were conducted by consulting medical records. When medical records were ambiguous, an experienced paediatric oncologist (M.D.) was consulted.

Statistics

Patients were categorised into two age groups: infants (<12 months) and children (≥12 months). Pearson’s chi-square test was used to compare the representativeness of TBs and BMAs across age groups. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cohen’s kappa (κ) was used to compare the results of TBs and BMAs with those of complete bone marrow evaluation. Κ was interpreted as follows: poor agreement (0.00–0.20), fair agreement (0.21–0.40), moderate agreement (0.41–0.60), substantial agreement (0.61–0.80) and almost perfect agreement (0.81–1.00) [35]. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The bootstrap method was used to provide 95% confidence intervals (CI) [36]. After correcting for multiple testing, a p-value < 0.025 was considered statistically significant [37]. SPSS for Windows, version 29.0.1. (IBM, Armonk, New York) was used to perform statistical analyses.

Results of complete bone marrow evaluation.

When comparing results of TBs with complete bone marrow evaluation, there was almost perfect agreement (κ = 0.901, 95% CI (0.871–0.930), p < 0.001). In contrast, the results of BMAs showed moderate agreement with complete bone marrow evaluation (κ = 0.532, 95% CI (0.477-0.585), p < 0.001).

When comparing solely TBs and solely BMAs with the findings of complete bone marrow evaluation, bone marrow infiltration would have been missed in 193 bone marrow evaluations (20.7%) when using solely BMAs, in 44 bone marrow evaluations (4.6%) from 35 different patients, infiltration was detected by aspirate alone and would have been missed when using TBs only. In five of these cases, the TBs were non-representative. The other 39 trephine biopsies were representative but negative (Table 3).

Seven of these 44 discordant TBs were performed at diagnosis, and the patient was classified as high-risk based on other risk factors. Of the remaining 37 TBs, BMAs detected a tumour percentage <1% in 23 procedures, which did not lead to treatment changes. In 13/44 cases, the percentage of infiltration was not reported, and in one BMA, the tumour infiltration reported was 1–30%. In twelve cases, the positive BMA(s) had no clinical consequences. In two cases, it concerned a bone marrow evaluation after relapse, in which the positivity of the aspirate(s) had no consequences regarding therapy, but follow-up evaluation was deemed necessary. In both cases, the follow-up evaluation was negative in both biopsies and aspirates. In the context of this study, we reevaluated tumour infiltration percentage when not reported. In 7/13 cases, tumour percentage was <1%. In six cases, a percentage could not be determined due to partial representativeness (n = 3) or unavailable BMAs (n = 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the current standard clinical practice in our national care centre by evaluating the diagnostic yield of bilateral TBs and BMAs for detecting bone marrow infiltration in neuroblastoma patients. Our findings show that bilateral TBs demonstrated considerably higher sensitivity for detecting bone marrow infiltration in comparison to BMAs and were more often representative (94.9% vs. 76.8%). Bone marrow infiltration was detected only in TBs in 20.7% and solely by BMAs in 4.6%. TBs were in almost perfect agreement with complete bone marrow evaluations (κ = 0.901, p < 0.001), whereas BMAs showed only moderate agreement (κ = 0.532, p < 0.001).

TBs are known to be more reliable than BMAs in the detection of tumour infiltration in bone marrow of neuroblastoma patients, although a combination of both techniques is still recommended [5, 11–15]. Our findings align with previous studies investigating the diagnostic accuracy of TBs and BMAs [11–14]. However, the detection yield by TBs in our study was notably higher. This difference is most likely due to the addition of neuroendocrine IHC markers in the histological analysis of TBs [16, 17]. The sensitivity of BMAs in our study was comparable to the sensitivity found in older studies, but two more recent studies have found BMAs to have a higher sensitivity [11–15]. The study of Rastogi et al. (2014) solely included bone marrow evaluations at diagnosis. This may have influenced the result, since the quality of the aspirates is most optimal at diagnosis when it is not affected by chemotherapy, which is known to lead to hypocellular bone marrow. Studies have also shown that the operator’s experience is of paramount importance for the quality of aspirates, but this has not been investigated in this study [38]. However, since our centre is a National Centre for Paediatric Oncology with a relatively high number of these procedures and a limited number of specialised nurses who perform these procedures, we believe this factor is unlikely to negatively affect the sensitivity of our aspirates.

In addition, this is the first study to investigate the potential clinical implications of relying solely on TBs for bone marrow assessment in neuroblastoma patients. In our study, no treatment strategies were altered based solely on positive aspirate findings. In two cases, a follow-up evaluation was deemed necessary, which yielded negative results. Thus, exclusion of BMAs for bone marrow evaluation of neuroblastoma patients would not have negatively impacted clinical outcomes in this study. Eliminating BMAs would not be expected to influence complication rates and post-procedure pain, as puncture sides for TBs and BMAs are the same. However, it would decrease procedure time, material usage, number of analyses, and personnel costs. Cost savings are estimated at €532,66 per bilateral BMA, corresponding to a total of €497.504,44 for all 934 procedures. Furthermore, it would result in a reduction of 10 minutes of anaesthesia time and six hours and forty minutes labour-time per patient (Appendix B). Our findings suggest that this time- and resource-consuming analysis can be excluded without compromising patient outcomes.

The greatest strength of this study is the high number of included procedures. Previous studies have examined fewer than a hundred bone marrow evaluations, with tumour cells detected in 20–40 cases [12–15]. The only exception is a study by Aronica et al. (1998), who included 289 procedures, detecting bone marrow infiltration in 139 [11]. In contrast, our study included 932 procedures (from 197 patients), detecting tumour infiltration in 380 analyses. Another strength is the use of neuroendocrine IHC staining for histological examination, thereby improving the diagnostic accuracy of TBs [16, 17].

The retrospective cohort design of this study allowed us to gather a large cohort of patients, but this study design also has limitations. The biggest limitation of this study is that we did not investigate BMAs utilising IC with GD2 antibodies or methods that are more sensitive in detecting minimal residual disease, such as RTqPCR [3, 5, 39]. Since these methods have not yet been incorporated into standard clinical practice, we were unable to compare their results with those of morphological examination. Furthermore, we have studied the clinical implications of relying solely on TBs by reviewing the medical records and consulting an experienced paediatric oncologist. However, our approach may not have fully captured the decision-making process in real-time clinical scenarios. Moreover, since all bone marrow evaluations included in this study were performed using a combination of TBs and BMAs, we could not compare long-term outcomes and evaluate potential risks of eliminating BMAs. The retrospective design also made it impossible to perform blinded analysis of TBs and BMAs. Another limitation of this study is that it is a single-centre study, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to broader clinical settings. Nevertheless, we believe that the size of the cohort justifies our conclusions.

To address the questions raised by our study, future studies should compare the results of TBs with IHC staining to those of BMA's cytomorphology complemented by IC and RTqRCR. We recommend a multicenter randomised controlled trial, in which data regarding treatment strategy are prospectively gathered and long-term outcomes are evaluated. This trial should control for operator experience and include blinded analyses of TBs, BMAs cytomorphology, IC and RTqPCR. Furthermore, different patient groups might benefit from different evaluation strategies. Therefore, future studies should include subgroup analyses based on age, disease stage, and genetic markers.

In summary, our findings show that TBs outperform cytomorphology of BMAs in detecting bone marrow infiltration in neuroblastoma patients. Our research suggests that limiting bone marrow evaluation to TBs with neuroendocrine staining results in sensitive detection of bone marrow metastases. We conclude that in this series and in the context of the current workflow, cytomorphology of BMAs has little additional value in bone marrow evaluation of neuroblastoma patients.
