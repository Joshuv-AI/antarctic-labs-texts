A Case of Anti-SRP Immune-mediated Necrotizing Myopathy Following Suspected Coxsackie B4 Infection: Exploring a Viral Trigger Hypothesis
Authors: Marwan Ahmed, Ruba Salameh, Soziema Salia, Brent T. Harris, Christopher J. Haas, Anees Siddiqi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is a rare subtype of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies that can present with rhabdomyolysis. We report a 43-year-old woman with rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness following a flu-like illness. Laboratory evaluation revealed markedly elevated creatine kinase (22,207 U/L) and transaminases, with positive anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) and Coxsackie B4 antibodies. Muscle biopsy demonstrated extensive necrosis, regeneration, and endomysial lymphocytic infiltration, confirming IMNM. The patient was treated with intravenous methylprednisolone, IVIG, and rituximab, followed by oral steroid taper and mycophenolate. At six-month follow-up, she showed significant clinical and biochemical improvement. This case highlights a potential viral-triggered autoimmune mechanism, suggesting that Coxsackie B4 infection may initiate or amplify anti-SRP-mediated immune muscle injury. Greater awareness of viral associations may aid early diagnosis and targeted therapy of IMNM.

Introduction

Rhabdomyolysis was first clearly characterized as a clinical syndrome during the London Blitz in 1941, when Eric Bywaters and Desmond Beall described crush injury–associated muscle necrosis leading to acute renal failure.1 Despite being a well-established diagnosis, the etiology of rhabdomyolysis remains diverse and involves complex pathophysiologic mechanisms. A 2005 study by Melli et al. identified 17 triggers, with prescribed medications, alcohol, and illicit drugs accounting for 46% of cases; notably, 60% of patients had multiple contributing factors.2 Rhabdomyolysis can be broadly classified as acquired (traumatic, exertional, and non-exertional causes such as drugs, infections, and inflammatory conditions including idiopathic inflammatory myopathies) or genetic (disorders of lipid metabolism, glycogen storage, and mitochondrial function).2

Although muscle pain, weakness, and dark urine are commonly cited as the classic triad, this constellation is observed in fewer than 10% of patients.3 Large muscle groups such as the thighs and calves are most frequently affected,3 and severe cases may progress to complications including compartment syndrome requiring urgent surgical intervention.4 Diagnosis is established through clinical evaluation and laboratory findings, particularly elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels.3 Early recognition and aggressive intravenous hydration are essential to prevent acute kidney injury.3

Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is a rare and severe subtype of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that can present with profound muscle necrosis and, in rare instances, fulminant rhabdomyolysis.5 Although anti–signal recognition particle (anti-SRP) antibodies are strongly associated with IMNM, the environmental or infectious triggers that precipitate immune activation remain incompletely understood. In this report, we describe a case of anti-SRP–positive IMNM following Coxsackie B4 infection, raising the possibility of a viral trigger and contributing to the evolving understanding of immune-mediated muscle injury.

Case description

A 43-year-old previously healthy Latin American woman presented with a three-week history of bilateral lower limb pain, weakness, and swelling. Four weeks prior, she had self-limited fevers, myalgias, and upper respiratory symptoms. Soon after, she developed restless leg symptoms, followed by progressive weakness and pain that limited ambulation. One week before admission, she tested positive for COVID-19 with mild respiratory symptoms.

On presentation, she was afebrile, hypertensive (164/112 mmHg), and tachycardic (124 bpm) with normal oxygen saturation. Examination revealed bilateral calf tenderness, grade 2 pitting edema, and 4/5 lower limb strength with preserved reflexes and pulses. Laboratory testing showed markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) (22,207 U/L), AST (706 U/L), and ALT (589 U/L), with normal renal function. High-sensitivity troponin was elevated (579 ng/L); however, the patient denied chest pain and had no electrocardiographic or echocardiographic evidence of ventricular dysfunction. Cardiac MRI was not performed, and subclinical myocarditis could not be definitively excluded. The patient was admitted for rhabdomyolysis management with IV fluids and analgesia.

She denied trauma, exertion, substance, or medication use, and had no family history of muscle disease. Despite supportive care, CK remained elevated (~17,000–19,000 U/L), and MRI revealed multifocal myositis. Given persistently elevated CK levels despite aggressive hydration and MRI findings consistent with inflammatory myositis, rheumatology initiated IV methylprednisolone 60 mg daily and IVIG for four days and pursued an extended myositis antibody panel as part of the diagnostic evaluation for suspected idiopathic inflammatory myopathy. Infectious and metabolic evaluations did not reveal evidence of active systemic infection. However, serologic testing demonstrated positive Coxsackie B4 antibodies. Additionally, the extended myositis antibody panel revealed positive anti–signal recognition particle (anti-SRP) antibodies. Muscle biopsy demonstrated perivascular and endomysial lymphocytic infiltration, variable fiber sizes, and scattered necrosis—consistent with autoimmune necrotizing myopathy [Fig. 1].

She received rituximab and began a gradual prednisone taper from an initial dose of 60 mg daily. Given the anticipated prolonged high-dose corticosteroid therapy in combination with additional immunosuppressive agents, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis was initiated. She also received recommended immunizations for immunocompromised, including influenza, COVID-19 vaccines, and shingles.

At peak disease severity, she was unable to ambulate on admission due to marked proximal lower extremity weakness. Upper extremity strength remained intact throughout her hospitalization, and there was no evidence of respiratory muscle involvement or dysphagia. She was discharged in a wheelchair.

By hospital day 15, CK declined to approximately 7400 U/L with modest improvement in strength [Fig. 2]. In two months post discharge, CK had decreased to 2600 U/L with partial functional recovery. In six months, she was able to ambulate with a cane; CK was 789 U/L and liver enzymes had normalized. Ongoing therapy included prednisone 15 mg daily, mycophenolate mofetil 500 mg twice daily, and monthly IVIG.

Discussion

Research of IIMs has been ongoing in recent years and has been broadened to include subtypes such as inclusion body myositis, dermatomyositis, polymyositis, anti-synthetase syndrome, and IMNM as distinct entities.5 IMNM is characterized by the microscopic findings of necrosis, regeneration, myophagocytosis (the engulfment of muscle fibers by macrophages), and intramuscular lymphocytes.6 IMNM is a rare cause of myopathy with a debatable prevalence.7

IMNM is classified based on autoantibody status into anti–signal recognition particle (anti-SRP), anti–hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl–CoA reductase (anti-HMGCR), and seronegative subtypes.8 While muscle biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard, myositis-specific antibodies are highly specific for IMNM and are detected in approximately 70–80% of cases, with anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP accounting for the majority and 20–30% of patients remaining seronegative.9–11

The prominent clinical picture of IMNM is usually proximal muscle weakness, mainly involving the hips more than the arms.12 It is noteworthy that anti-SRP myositis tends to have more prevalent muscle symptoms, including severe muscle weakness, neck weakness, dysphagia, and muscular atrophy than anti-HMGCR myositis.13 Cardiac involvement has been reported in IMNM and may include conduction abnormalities, arrhythmias, cardiomyopathy, and, less commonly, myocarditis.14 In a recent cohort study, subclinical or clinical cardiac abnormalities were observed in approximately 52.6% of patients.14 Notably, our patient did not demonstrate overt clinical or echocardiographic abnormalities despite elevated troponin levels, underscoring the heterogeneity of cardiac manifestations in this condition.

Prior studies suggested that certain human leukocyte antigen (HLA) alleles may confer genetic susceptibility to IMNM, while observed seasonal patterns raised the possibility of viral triggers; in addition, statin exposure has been proposed as a contributing factor, particularly in anti-HMGCR–associated IMNM.10,15 There are incidental seasonal patterns noted in IMNM diagnosis that have led to the notion that viruses may also be a potential trigger.16

Our patient fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for seropositive IMNM: elevated serum CK levels, proximal muscle weakness, and presence of anti-SRP or anti-HMGCR autoantibodies.9 Muscle biopsy is usually not necessary for diagnosis except in seronegative IMNM.12 In our case, muscle biopsy findings showed scattered areas of myocyte necrosis and regeneration as described in [Fig. 1]. Additionally, MHC1 immunostaining was evident within inflamed areas and around many fibers [Fig. 3]. The pattern of inflammation was compatible with immune mediated processes and against inclusion body myositis or dermatomyositis.

Our case demonstrates a rare entity of IMNM with SRP positive antibodies leading to rhabdomyolysis. Our patient lacked risk factors or medical history that could explain her severe muscle injury. Notably, she experienced flu-like symptoms one week prior to the onset of muscle symptoms, which could be linked to her positive Coxsackie B4 antibody titer.

To date, only four published reports have described Coxsackie B4–associated rhabdomyolysis17–20. Most reported cases involved male patients with extremely elevated CK levels and biopsy findings consistent with acute viral necrotizing myopathy, reflecting direct viral muscle injury. One of these reports described immune-mediated inflammatory myopathy in a patient with underlying cirrhosis, although extended myositis antibody testing was negative.19 Another case involved a middle-aged woman with rhabdomyolysis and cardiac involvement.20 In contrast, our patient was a previously healthy 43-year-old woman with persistent CK elevation, MRI-confirmed myositis, biopsy-proven immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, and positive anti-SRP antibodies requiring multimodal immunosuppressive therapy. To our knowledge, anti-SRP–positive IMNM temporally associated with Coxsackie B4 infection has not been previously reported.

Coxsackie B viruses are non-polio enteroviruses within the Picornaviridae family and are transmitted primarily via the fecal–oral route.21,22 The majority of symptomatic infections occur in infants, children, and adolescents, with adult infection being relatively less common.21,22 Gastrointestinal and upper respiratory symptoms frequently precede systemic manifestations.21,22 According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), reverse transcription–polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) is the preferred diagnostic modality for enteroviral infections, whereas serologic testing and viral culture may provide supportive evidence in selected cases.22

It has also been established that Coxsackie virus can cause IIMs especially dermatomyositis.23 An animal study done by Jongen et al. in 1994 indicated that Coxsackie B1 virus plays a pathologic role in the induction of chronic myositis in mice.24 Complications from Coxsackie B infections typically include aseptic meningitis, myopericarditis, and pancreatitis.17 Our patient had elevated troponin level, which could be attributed to viral myocarditis17 or SRP antibody-related myocarditis.25 In our patient, diagnosis was supported by serologic testing, as RT-PCR was not performed at the time of presentation.

Pharmacological treatment depends on the auto-antibody type and consists of induction phase and maintenance phase.12 For patients with anti-SRP IMNM, steroids are the first choice of treatment with adjuvant methotrexate and or rituximab within the first month.12 For anti-HMGCR and seronegative IMNM, steroids are also usually used with adjuvant methotrexate and/or IVIG.12

The maintenance phase targets gradual taper of steroids to the least effective dose and taper of other medications after 2 years of remission.12 Physical therapy also plays a significant role in the road of recovery.9 It is also worth noting that triple therapy, with steroids, IVIG, and rituximab is a new strategy to treat severe anti-SRP IMNM.26

We suspect that the autoimmune inciting event in our case could be linked to the Coxsackie B4 infection. This hypothesis is consistent with existing evidence implicating Coxsackie virus as a potential trigger for autoimmune conditions. For instance, Coxsackie virus B1 has been shown to induce pancreatic beta cell autoimmunity, predisposing individuals to type 1 diabetes.27

Similarly, an experimental murine model demonstrated that a cardiotropic strain of Coxsackie virus B3 can induce an autoimmune response directed against murine cardiac myosin, supporting its role in virus-mediated autoimmunity.28

The pathogenesis of IMNM remains incompletely understood, underscoring the importance of clarifying the precise roles of anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR antibodies.13 Evidence from multiple studies suggests that these autoantibodies contribute to disease mechanisms by activating the classical complement pathway and promoting sarcolemmal immunoglobulin deposition, thereby emphasizing the pivotal role of humoral immunity in IMNM pathogenesis.9,14

Further investigation by rheumatology experts and researchers is warranted to explore a potential link between IMNM and viral infections especially Coxsackie virus. Early identification of subtle prodromal symptoms prior to the onset of muscle damage may be critical for timely diagnosis and intervention.

Conclusion

The rarity of IMNM poses challenges to advancing our understanding of its pathogenesis and etiology. This case highlights the importance of thorough clinical evaluations including detailed history-taking and broadening serological testing in patients with severe muscle injury. Our case may provide early insight into the pathogenesis of virus-triggered immune-mediated myositis.

Experimental models, particularly those involving Coxsackie virus, may further elucidate mechanisms by which viral infections initiate or propagate autoimmune muscle damage. Clinical vigilance is warranted to recognize IMNM as a rare but important potential cause of rhabdomyolysis, and clinicians should maintain a low threshold for expanding serological investigations when patients lack standard risk factors for such a debilitating condition.
