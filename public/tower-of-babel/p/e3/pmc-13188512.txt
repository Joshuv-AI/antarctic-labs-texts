The impact of Vaginal Flora Screening in the third trimester on pregnancy outcomes in primiparas undergoing vaginal delivery: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Bin He, Wenyao Chen, Qin Fang, Haojing Ma, Yuyan Zhou, Xu Wang, Jing Xu, Ting Zou, Tianyi Ye, Zhangye Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe necessity of third-trimester vaginal flora screening remains debated. This study aims to evaluate the association between third-trimester vaginal flora screening and pregnancy outcomes among primiparous women undergoing vaginal delivery, as well as neonatal health outcomes.MethodsThis study enrolled 1,380 primiparous women with singleton pregnancies who underwent GBS screening alone or combined GBS plus vaginal flora screening at 35–37 weeks of gestation. Participants were categorized into two cohorts: Group A (GBS screening only; n = 880) and Group B (GBS plus vaginal flora screening; n = 500). Group B was further stratified into Group C (vaginal flora–negative; n = 229) and Group D (vaginal flora–positive; n = 271). After excluding 40 women with positive GBS results from Group D, the remaining 231 women constituted Group E. Clinical data were collected for each group, and intergroup differences were analyzed.ResultsCompared with Group B, Group A exhibited significantly higher rates of intrapartum fever, cesarean delivery, and premature rupture of membranes (p < 0.05). Neonatal outcomes were also less favorable in Group A, with higher NICU admission rates and a greater incidence of pathological jaundice, as well as lower Apgar scores than in Group B (all p < 0.05). Within Group B, Group D had significantly higher rates of intrapartum fever, abnormal routine blood test results, meconium-stained amniotic fluid (≥ grade Ⅲ), and postpartum hemorrhage than Group C (p < 0.05); however, no significant differences in neonatal outcomes were observed between Groups C and D (p > 0.05). After excluding GBS-positive cases, Group E likewise showed higher rates of intrapartum fever, abnormal routine blood test results, and postpartum hemorrhage compared with Group C (p < 0.05), while neonatal outcomes did not differ significantly between Groups C and E (p > 0.05).ConclusionsAbnormal vaginal flora colonization is associated with an increased risk of maternal perinatal infection, without a corresponding increase in neonatal infection. These findings support the implementation of third-trimester vaginal flora screening and appropriate treatment for pregnant women planning vaginal delivery.

Introduction

During pregnancy, women undergo profound metabolic, immunologic, and endocrine adaptations [1]. Rising estrogen and progesterone levels promote vaginal glycogen deposition, while the vaginal mucosa becomes hyperemic and edematous with increased epithelial permeability—conditions that may facilitate the growth and proliferation of nonresident or opportunistic microorganisms. Virulence factors produced by pathogenic bacteria can trigger host infection and stimulate the release of proinflammatory cytokines, thereby disrupting vaginal microbial homeostasis and increasing susceptibility to lower genital tract infections, which may ultimately contribute to adverse pregnancy outcomes [2].

At present, routine screening at 35–37 weeks of gestation in most countries primarily targets Group B Streptococcus (GBS), and intrapartum penicillin prophylaxis is recommended for individuals with GBS colonization. However, whether abnormal vaginal flora in late pregnancy—including Mycoplasma spp., Candida spp., and Escherichia coli—should be routinely screened remains controversial. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) has stated that current evidence is insufficient to support routine vaginal flora screening [3]. In contrast, Eszter H, Weihong W, and colleagues reported that screening for and identifying abnormal vaginal flora may reduce adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as preterm birth and premature rupture of membranes (PROM) [4–6]. PROM may facilitate ascending migration of pathogenic microorganisms into the uterine cavity, leading to intrauterine infection. Moreover, exposure to contaminated amniotic fluid or passage through an infected birth canal during delivery can increase the risk of neonatal complications, including pathological jaundice and neonatal sepsis, and in severe cases may result in neonatal death, thereby posing substantial threats to maternal and neonatal health.

However, to date, most studies have focused on bacterial or fungal vaginitis among symptomatic pregnant women, with comparatively limited investigation in asymptomatic individuals, nontraditional vaginal microorganisms, and their associations with pregnancy outcomes. Accordingly, the present study implements routine late–third-trimester vaginal flora screening in primiparous women to evaluate its relationship with pregnancy outcomes and to further clarify the clinical utility and necessity of vaginal flora screening in late pregnancy.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This was a retrospective cohort study. In this design, exposure and outcome have already occurred at the time of study initiation, and both are ascertained retrospectively from existing medical records. Accordingly, in the present study, exposure (screening group) and outcomes (maternal and neonatal outcomes) were determined by reviewing medical records. This study was conducted at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University, with data collected between January 2023 and December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Approval No. 2023-K-50-01). All participants received routine antenatal care throughout pregnancy, including first-trimester ultrasonography nuchal translucency (NT), prenatal screening and/or diagnostic testing, detailed anatomic ultrasonography, an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), mid-trimester laboratory assessments, and GBS screening, and subsequently completed delivery at our institution. Participants were followed through 42 days postpartum.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: primiparous women with a singleton pregnancy, cephalic presentation, spontaneous onset of labor, and a gestational age ≥ 35 weeks; completion of GBS screening at 35–37 weeks of gestation; no use of systemic antibiotics or intravaginal suppositories within 2 weeks prior to specimen collection; and availability of complete prenatal examination data.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: contraindications to vaginal delivery; a scarred uterus; major fetal congenital anomalies; severe maternal comorbidities (e.g., significant cardiac, hepatic, or renal disease) or pregnancy-related complications (e.g., preeclampsia, acute fatty liver of pregnancy); underwent labor induction with dinoprostone; and suspected intrauterine infection prior to delivery, such as fever and leukocytosis.

Grouping and intervention

Between January 2023 and December 2024, a total of 2,126 primiparous women received antenatal care and delivered at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. Of these, 726 women were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. The remaining 1,380 eligible participants were enrolled at 35–37 weeks of gestation and underwent routine GBS screening. With informed consent, participants who agreed additionally received vaginal flora screening.

Based on screening strategies, participants were categorized into two cohorts: the GBS screening group (Group A, n = 880) and the GBS plus vaginal flora screening group (Group B, n = 500). Within Group B, women with no abnormal microorganisms identified on vaginal flora culture were classified as the flora-negative subgroup (Group C, n = 229), whereas those with any abnormal microorganisms detected were classified as the flora-positive subgroup (Group D, n = 271). Among Group D, 40 women with concurrent GBS-positive results were excluded; the remaining 231 women constituted the vaginal flora–positive, GBS-negative subgroup (Group E, n = 231). Sample collection, cultivation, and treatment

GBS sampling, culture, and intrapartum prophylaxis: At 35–37 weeks’ gestation, obstetricians collected vaginal and rectal specimens using sterile cotton swabs. Samples were transported to the laboratory within 30 min of collection. Specimens were inoculated onto 5% sheep blood agar and incubated in 5% CO₂ at 37 °C for 24–48 h. Presumptive β-hemolytic colonies were subcultured for isolation, followed by Gram staining and biochemical identification using the WalkAway-96 Plus automated microbial identification system to confirm GBS colonization. For women with confirmed GBS colonization, intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis was administered with intravenous penicillin: an initial dose of 5 million units (Ruiyang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.; H20067324) at the onset of labor or 4 h prior to amniotomy, followed by 3 million units every 4 h until delivery.

Other vaginal flora sampling, culture, and treatment: At 35–37 weeks’ gestation, the vulva was cleansed, and a disposable sterile speculum was inserted to fully visualize the vagina. Vaginal secretions were collected from the posterior fornix and lateral vaginal wall using a sterile cotton swab. A second sterile swab was then inserted approximately 1.5 cm into the endocervical canal, rotated for four full turns, and held in place for 10 s. Both swabs were placed into sterile tubes, sealed, and immediately transported for microbiological testing.

The following microbiological analyses were performed:

Bacterial vaginosis (BV)

Diagnosed using Amsel criteria (at least three of: homogeneous discharge, vaginal pH > 4.5, positive whiff test, and clue cells > 20% of epithelial cells on saline wet mount microscopy).

Candida species

Detected by microscopy (10% KOH preparation) and fungal culture on Sabouraud dextrose agar incubated at 35 °C for 48–72 h. Species identification was performed using CHROMagar.

Mycoplasma species

Detected using commercial culture kits for Ureaplasma urealyticum and Mycoplasma hominis. Testing for Mycoplasma genitalium was not performed.

Aerobic bacteria (e.g., Escherichia coli): Cultured on MacConkey agar at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Suspected colonies (e.g., lactose-fermenting colonies on MacConkey agar) were identified by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS).

Chlamydia trachomatis

Not routinely tested in this study.

Treatment was provided according to microbiological diagnoses. Prophylactic antifungal therapy was administered after confirmation of vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC), primarily using clotrimazole vaginal suppositories (500 mg) every 3 days for two doses. For mycoplasma infection, clindamycin was prescribed at 300 mg orally twice daily for 7 days. For bacterial vaginosis (BV), patients received standardized anti-anaerobic treatment: oral metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 7 days. For patients with contraindications to metronidazole, oral clindamycin 300 mg twice daily for 7 days was prescribed.

Observational indicators

The following variables were collected: vaginal flora culture results; indicators of maternal intrapartum and postpartum infection and neonatal infection, including intrapartum and postpartum body temperature and complete blood count parameters; severity of maternal perineal lacerations; degree of meconium-stained amniotic fluid; neonatal Apgar scores; neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admissions attributable to suspected infection or jaundice; neonatal birth weight; rate of intrapartum conversion from planned vaginal delivery to cesarean section; duration of labor; and the interval from rupture of membranes to delivery.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Data normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables are summarized as median (interquartile range). Between-group comparisons of continuous variables were conducted using the independent-samples t test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. For comparisons across multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Due to the exploratory nature of this study, no adjustment was made for multiple comparisons.

Subgroup analysis according to pathogen type

To further investigate the association between specific pathogens and pregnancy outcomes, a stratified analysis was performed among women with positive vaginal flora screening (Group D, n = 271). Based on the pathogens identified, women in Group D were classified into mutually exclusive groups: Candida species (Candida group), Mycoplasma species (Mycoplasma group), Group B Streptococcus (GBS group), mixed infections (presence of two or more pathogens), and others (e.g., Escherichia coli). Differences in maternal and neonatal infection-related outcomes across these groups were compared using Fisher’s exact test.

Pregnancy outcomes

The incidences of PROM, intrapartum fever, and intrapartum conversion from planned vaginal delivery to cesarean section were significantly higher in the GBS screening group (Group A) than in the GBS plus vaginal flora screening group (Group B) (30.6% vs. 24.0%, 28.3% vs. 23.4%, and 7.3% vs. 2.4%, respectively).

Among the 64 intrapartum cesarean deliveries in Group A, 6 were attributed to suspected maternal infection (intrapartum fever and/or elevated inflammatory indices on complete blood count) and 20 to protracted labor. In Group B, 12 intrapartum cesarean deliveries occurred, including 6 for protracted labor, with no cases attributable to maternal infection or intrapartum fever.

No significant between-group differences were observed in the proportion of women with a white blood cell count ≥ 15 × 10^9/L, neutrophil percentage ≥ 85%, postpartum hemorrhage, use of epidural analgesia, prevalence of gestational diabetes mellitus or gestational hypertension, total duration of labor, or postpartum perineal infection (all p > 0.05). However, the rate of meconium-stained amniotic fluid (≥ grade Ⅲ) was significantly higher in Group B than in Group A (3.2% vs. 1.0%, p < 0.01).

Neonatal outcomes

The rates of NICU admission and pathological jaundice were significantly higher in the GBS screening group than in the GBS plus vaginal flora screening group (18.5% vs. 13.2% and 16.6% vs. 12.0%, respectively). In addition, the 1-min Apgar score was slightly but significantly lower in the GBS screening group compared with the combined screening group (9.89 ± 0.36 vs. 9.93 ± 0.31). No significant between-group differences were observed in NICU admissions attributable to sepsis/infection, the incidence of low birth weight (LBW), fetal distress, the proportion of neonates with a white blood cell count ≥ 15 × 10^9/L, or the rate of preterm birth (all p > 0.05) (Table 2).

The detection results of vaginal flora during late pregnancy

The GBS colonization rate was 7.4% (65/880) in Group A (GBS screening only) compared to 8.0% (40/500) in Group B (combined GBS and vaginal flora screening), with no significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.697). Vaginal flora screening identified at least one pathogen in 271 women, corresponding to an overall positivity rate of 54.20%. Specifically, 67 women were positive for Candida spp. (13.40%), 222 for Mycoplasma spp. (44.40%), and 2 for Escherichia coli (0.40%). Co-infection with two or more pathogens was detected in 41 cases, accounting for 8.20% of the cohort.

Pregnancy outcomes

In the vaginal flora–positive group, the incidences of intrapartum fever, leukocytosis (white blood cell count ≥ 15 × 10^9/L), neutrophilia (neutrophil percentage ≥ 85%), severe meconium-stained amniotic fluid (≥ grade III), and postpartum hemorrhage were significantly higher than those in the vaginal flora–negative group (31.0% vs. 14.4%, 17.71% vs. 11.35%, 5.17% vs. 1.75%, 4.8% vs. 1.31%, and 8.86% vs. 3.49%, respectively). The prevalence of gestational diabetes mellitus was also significantly higher in the vaginal flora–positive group compared with the negative group (20.3% vs. 8.3%). In addition, total labor duration was longer in the vaginal flora–positive group (12.70 ± 4.77 h vs. 11.47 ± 4.94 h). No significant between-group differences were observed in the prevalence of gestational hypertension, PROM, intrapartum conversion from planned vaginal delivery to cesarean section, or the rate of lateral episiotomy (all p > 0.05).

Neonatal outcomes

No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in overall NICU admission, NICU admission attributable to sepsis/infection, incidence of pathological jaundice, LBW, fetal distress, the proportion of neonates with a white blood cell count ≥ 15 × 10^9/L, preterm birth, or 1-min Apgar score (all p > 0.05). Notably, among neonates admitted to the NICU, infection accounted for 4 admissions in the negative group (11.76% of NICU admissions) and 10 admissions in the positive group (31.35% of NICU admissions) (Table 4).

Pregnancy outcomes

After excluding the potential influence of GBS colonization, the vaginal flora–positive group exhibited significantly higher rates of intrapartum fever, leukocytosis (white blood cell count ≥ 15 × 10^9/L), neutrophilia (neutrophil percentage ≥ 85%), and postpartum hemorrhage compared with the vaginal flora–negative group (32.9% vs. 14.41%, 19.05% vs. 11.35%, 5.19% vs. 1.75%, and 8.66% vs. 3.49%, respectively). The prevalence of gestational diabetes mellitus was also significantly higher in the vaginal flora–positive group (20.4% vs. 8.3%). In addition, total labor duration was longer among women in the vaginal flora–positive group (12.84 ± 4.51 h vs. 11.47 ± 4.94 h). No significant between-group differences were observed in the prevalence of gestational hypertension, severe meconium-stained amniotic fluid (≥ grade Ⅲ), PROM, cesarean delivery due to labor dystocia, or episiotomy rate (all p > 0.05).

Neonatal outcomes

No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in overall NICU admission, NICU admission for sepsis/infection, incidence of pathological jaundice, LBW, fetal distress, the proportion of neonates with a white blood cell count ≥ 15 × 10^9/L, preterm birth, or 1-min Apgar score (all p > 0.05). Among neonates admitted to the NICU, infection accounted for 4 admissions in the negative group (11.76%) and 8 admissions in the positive group (30.77%) (Table 6).

Discussion

Evidence suggests that the progression from lower genital tract infection to ascending intrauterine infection is an important precipitating pathway for adverse pregnancy events [7]. In the present study, women in the combined GBS and vaginal flora screening cohort received targeted management when abnormal vaginal flora was identified in the third trimester. In contrast, participants in the GBS-only screening cohort did not undergo vaginal flora testing and therefore did not receive prophylactic intervention for dysbiosis. We observed a lower incidence of PROM among women who underwent vaginal flora screening than among those who did not, consistent with prior reports [8]. A plausible explanation is that ascending colonization by dysbiotic microorganisms may compromise membrane integrity and promote premature rupture.

Given that abnormal genital tract flora may ascend after membrane rupture or during labor and, in some cases, be vertically transmitted to the neonate, the clinical question of whether routine antepartum screening and prevention are warranted remains highly relevant. In our cohort, vaginal dysbiotic colonization was associated with a higher-risk profile for peripartum infection. Intrapartum fever—defined as a maternal temperature ≥ 38 °C during labor or within 24 h postpartum [9]—is a recognized risk factor for adverse perinatal outcomes [10]. After accounting for differences in total labor duration and the interval from rupture of membranes to delivery, the incidence of intrapartum fever remained higher in women who did not undergo vaginal flora screening (28.3%) than in those who did (23.4%). Moreover, within the screened cohort, intrapartum fever, leukocytosis (white blood cell count ≥ 15 × 10^9/L), and neutrophilia (neutrophil percentage ≥ 85%) were more frequent in women with positive vaginal flora results than in those with negative results. Although GBS is commonly implicated in maternal sepsis [11], these associations persisted even after excluding GBS-positive cases, indicating that non-GBS dysbiotic colonization may independently increase the risk of intrapartum infection [10].

Prior work has shown that, among women planning vaginal birth, azithromycin administration can reduce the risk of maternal sepsis or death [12]. In our study, women in the vaginal flora screening cohort received organism-directed therapies after abnormal findings were identified (e.g., clotrimazole for VVC, macrolides for Mycoplasma/Chlamydia colonization, and metronidazole or clindamycin for BV). Collectively, these measures may have contributed to a reduction in maternal genital tract infection indicators during late pregnancy, supporting the potential utility of third-trimester screening coupled with targeted treatment for preventing maternal peripartum infection.

We also found a higher rate of intrapartum conversion to cesarean delivery among women who did not undergo vaginal flora screening. In the GBS-only cohort, of 64 conversions to cesarean delivery, 6 were attributed to suspected maternal infection (intrapartum fever and/or elevated inflammatory indices) and 20 to protracted labor. By contrast, among women who underwent vaginal flora screening, only 12 conversions occurred, 6 due to protracted labor, and none attributable to maternal infection or fever. Within the screened cohort, vaginal flora–positive women experienced longer labor and higher rates of intrapartum fever and severe meconium-stained amniotic fluid (≥ grade Ⅲ), factors that may increase the likelihood of operative delivery. Additionally, prolonged labor can exacerbate maternal fatigue and predispose to uterine atony, thereby increasing the risk of postpartum hemorrhage.

Regarding neonatal outcomes, NICU admission was lower among neonates born to women who underwent vaginal flora screening (13.2%) than among those whose mothers received GBS screening alone (18.5%), with a notable reduction in pathological jaundice. The 1-min Apgar score was also modestly higher in the screened cohort (9.93 ± 0.31 vs. 9.89 ± 0.36). These observations align with findings from Jing Huang et al. suggesting that maternal vaginal dysbiosis may adversely affect neonatal well-being [13]. Intrapartum fever has been associated with unfavorable neonatal outcomes, and higher maternal temperatures are linked to increased risks of NICU admission and neonatal sepsis [14]. Neonatal sepsis often results from vertical exposure to contaminated amniotic fluid or microorganisms within the birth canal during vaginal delivery [15, 16]. Pathological jaundice accounts for a substantial proportion of neonatal jaundice cases [17], and prior studies indicate that elevated maternal inflammatory indices and PROM are associated with an increased risk of neonatal pathological jaundice [18]. In this context, maternal infection or inflammatory activation may contribute to neonatal hemolysis or impaired bilirubin handling, providing a biologically plausible link between maternal peripartum status and neonatal jaundice.

Notably, despite these differences in NICU admission and jaundice, we did not detect significant between-group differences in neonatal infection indicators regardless of whether mothers underwent vaginal flora screening or had positive screening results, consistent with observations reported by Alon et al. [19]. Thus, our data suggest that abnormal vaginal flora colonization is associated with an increased risk of maternal peripartum infection but does not correspond to a higher neonatal infection rate. While intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis reduces early-onset neonatal disease, it may be less effective for preventing maternal infections [20]. In contrast to Hoffmann et al. [21], we found no significant association between abnormal vaginal flora and preterm birth or neonatal birth weight in this cohort.

We further estimated the prevalence of vaginal dysbiosis in singleton pregnancies. The overall positivity rate for any dysbiotic microorganism among women undergoing vaginal flora screening was 54.2%, with Mycoplasma spp. being most prevalent (44.4%), followed by Candida spp. (13.4%); Escherichia coli was uncommon (0.4%). In addition, dysbiotic colonization was more frequent among women with gestational diabetes mellitus than among normoglycemic women, potentially reflecting increased glycogen availability in the vaginal epithelium and diabetes-associated changes in the local microenvironment [22, 23].

The pathogen-specific analysis, although not reaching statistical significance, revealed clinically relevant trends: women with GBS alone had the highest rate of intrapartum fever, and mixed infections were associated with higher NICU admission rates. These findings suggest that both the type and number of pathogens may influence maternal and neonatal morbidity, warranting further investigation in larger cohorts.

Notably, among the 40 GBS-positive women identified in Group B (8% of the 500 women undergoing combined screening), only 14 (35%) had GBS as the sole pathogen, while the remaining 26 (65%) had co-infections with at least one additional organism. This high proportion of polymicrobial colonization among GBS-positive women has important clinical implications. Routine GBS screening alone would fail to detect these coexisting pathogens, potentially underestimating the infectious risk and missing opportunities for intervention [24]. Our findings highlight the value of comprehensive vaginal flora screening in identifying a broader at-risk population.

This study has several limitations. First, as an observational study rather than a randomized controlled trial, selection bias cannot be entirely excluded. Second, the single-center design and restricted cohort may limit generalizability. Third, our Mycoplasma testing did not distinguish between Ureaplasma urealyticum and Mycoplasma hominis in the outcome analysis, nor did we perform antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Given the variable sensitivity of U. urealyticum to clindamycin [25], treatment efficacy may have differed across patients. Fourth, BV diagnosed clinically by Amsel criteria was not systematically recorded, precluding analysis of its prevalence in this retrospective cohort. Fifth, we did not perform test-of-cure after treatment, which may have led to underestimation of the true effect of screening and treatment. Sixth, given the exploratory nature of this study, no adjustment was made for multiple comparisons. Larger, multicenter, prospective studies incorporating post-treatment reassessment are warranted to validate our findings and clarify causal relationships.

In conclusion, abnormal vaginal flora colonization is associated with an increased risk of adverse maternal peripartum outcomes and may also be linked to less favorable neonatal outcomes (notably NICU admission and pathological jaundice). These findings support the clinical value of third-trimester vaginal flora screening and organism-directed treatment in women planning vaginal delivery.

Conclusion

Abnormal vaginal flora colonization is associated with an increased risk of maternal perinatal infection but does not appear to increase the risk of neonatal infection. Third-trimester vaginal flora screening with subsequent targeted treatment may reduce the incidence of PROM, intrapartum conversion from planned vaginal delivery to cesarean section, and maternal infectious morbidity among primiparous women undergoing vaginal delivery. Moreover, it may decrease neonatal NICU admissions and the incidence of pathological jaundice. Collectively, these findings support the implementation of third-trimester vaginal flora screening and appropriate treatment in pregnant women planning vaginal delivery.
