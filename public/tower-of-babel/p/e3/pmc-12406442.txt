Dynamic birth position, awareness and practice by midwives in Ethiopia: a parallel mixed-methods study
Authors: Osman Yimer Mohammed, Kerstin Erlandsson, Tewodros Seyoum, Solomon Hailemeskel, Lemma Derseh, Helena Lindgren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRespectful maternity care, as recommended by the World Health Organization, includes allowing women to give birth in the position of their choice. Dynamic birth positions, which enable women to adopt various positions during labor and delivery, are associated with improved obstetric outcomes. However, the extent of knowledge and practice regarding dynamic birth positions among Ethiopian midwives remains poorly understood.ObjectiveTo assess midwives’ awareness and practice of dynamic birth positions and explore their perspectives and experiences.MethodsA convergent parallel mixed-methods design was employed to evaluate midwives’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding dynamic birth positions. Quantitative data were analyzed using stepwise backward elimination logistic regression to identify key predictors, while qualitative data were examined through thematic analysis.ResultsThe study included 483 midwives, with 473 participating in the quantitative survey and 10 in the qualitative interviews. Fewer than half of the midwives demonstrated good levels of knowledge (45%) and attitude (49.5%), while only 22.6% reported regular practice of dynamic birth positions. Participation in training emerged as the strongest predictor of knowledge, attitude, and practice. Thematic analysis revealed six key themes: routine practices, awareness, clinical implementation, barriers, facilitators, and user needs.ConclusionAwareness and practice of dynamic birth positions among Ethiopian midwives are notably low. Key barriers include limited awareness and suboptimal working conditions, whereas targeted training significantly enhances all assessed domains. To promote the adoption of dynamic birth positions, efforts should focus on increasing awareness, modifying delivery equipment, and improving the clinical environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08051-8.

Introduction

In Ethiopia, only about 48% of births occur in health facilities [1]. Several barriers contribute to this low utilization, including a lack of respectful maternity care particularly the inability to choose birthing positions poor quality of care, limited involvement in care planning, restricted mobility during labor, and shortages of skilled healthcare providers [2, 3]. Promoting maternal mobility and allowing women to adopt birthing positions of their choice such as standing, squatting, sitting, side-lying, kneeling, or being on all fours is associated with improved obstetric outcomes [4–6].

Dynamic birth position refers to an individualized, fluid progression through different laboring and birthing postures, guided by the woman’s instinctive responses to her body’s needs for comfort and ease. It integrates periods of movement and rest and supports a natural, self-directed birthing process [7]. This approach encompasses any non-supine position that facilitates pelvic mobility, including standing, kneeling, squatting, lateral positions, use of a birth seat, or the all-fours position each chosen freely by the woman throughout labor and delivery [7–10].

Using dynamic birth positions has been proven to have many benefits. Women who assume a non-supine position for birth have fewer perineal tears, less vulvar oedema, less blood loss, shorter second stages, fewer abnormal fetal heart rates, lower caesarean section rates, and a higher rate of spontaneous vaginal birth (SVB) [11–14]. This is due to the upright birthing position significantly expanding women’s pelvic dimensions and facilitating the descent of the baby’s head [15, 16]. Furthermore, upright and other flexible sacrum positions significantly reduce women’s pain and increase their satisfaction, positively impacting the birth experience [17–19]. Despite the evidence, in Ethiopia and most other low-income countries, almost all women giving birth in health facilities are in a supine or lithotomy position during the second stage of labour [20]. Giving women the option to assume different birth positions and respecting their choice is part of respectful maternity care [5]. Several studies have been conducted to assess the advantages and disadvantages of different birthing positions [21, 22]. Women’s health conditions during pregnancy, the relationship with care providers, fetal heart rate monitoring, the use of continuous cardiotocography, amniotomy, the use of episiotomy, the use of operative vaginal delivery, epidural anaesthesia, and disagreement with co-workers are barriers to the utilisation of dynamic birth positions [23–25]. There is shortage of evidences from Sub-Saharan Africa and only two studies from Ethiopia and Uganda reported similar findings on the benefit of dynamic birth position [9, 14]. Factors including knowledge of health professionals, job satisfaction, years of experience, and institutional policies are reported to be facilitators for the use of different birth positions [23–25].

Knowledge and attitude are key determinants of professional behavior and practice [26, 27]. In the context of maternity care, midwives with sound knowledge and positive attitudes toward evidence-based practices are more likely to implement those practices in clinical settings [28, 29]. Despite growing global advocacy for respectful maternity care including the use of dynamic birth positions there remains a lack of evidence on how well midwives understand, value, and apply these practices in their day-to-day work.

In Ethiopia, where health system challenges already limit women’s autonomy during childbirth, little is known about midwives’ awareness, attitudes, and actual use of dynamic birth positions. Understanding these factors is essential for designing effective interventions to promote women-centered care and improve birth outcomes.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess midwives’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to dynamic birth positions and to explore their experiences and perspectives to inform future training and policy strategies.

Method

Study design

An institution-based mixed-method study (a concurrent cross-sectional and qualitative descriptive) was conducted in North Shoa, Amhara National Regional State, Ethiopia, from October to November 2024. North Shoa zone comprises 24 Woredas and 3 city administrations. There are around 11 hospitals and 91 health centres in the study area.

Study participants

All midwives working in public health facilities in the study area were included if they had more than one year of work experience. A total of 473 midwives participated in the quantitative survey, and 10 midwives participated in the in-depth interviews for the qualitative part of the study. The department heads were approached to identify qualitative interviewees who have diverse experience and exposure in their profession and delivery-related routine activities.

Data collection

Quantitative data were collected using a structured self-administered questionnaire. There was no existing suitable assessment tool. Therefore, a tool for the assessment of knowledge and attitude was developed using the three-step modified Delphi method [30]. Knowledge about dynamic birth positions was assessed using a 12-item tool with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89, and the attitude was assessed using a 10-item question with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.77. The knowledge section had three response alternatives (yes, no, and I do not know) scored as 3, 2, and 1 point respectively. The correct response for all items except for items 3 and 5 was “Yes”; however, for items 3 and 5, the correct response was “No” and thus those were reversed during analysis. One point was scored for items answered correctly and zero for items with wrong answers for the final scaling of the correct response. The total scores were summed and scored against the 12 total scores. Individuals who scored 50% and above out of the 12 expected points were considered to have good knowledge [24].

The attitude section was scaled using a five-level Likert scale with 1 being strongly disagree and 5 being strongly agree. The scores for negatively scored items (3, 4, 9, and 10) were reversed during the analysis stage to ensure uniform scoring. The total score was summed and categorised based on the mean score. Individuals were considered to have a favourable attitude if they scored more than the mean score [24].

Eight positions, six non-supine (standing, squatting, side-lying, sitting on the birth seat, standing on all fours, and kneeling), and two supine positions (lying on the back or lithotomy, and semi-sitting), were included to assess the level of practice. Midwives were asked to mark a position if they had ever practised it. Midwives who practised three or more non-supine birth positions were considered to have a good practice level, while midwives who used two or fewer non-supine positions were considered to have a poor practice level [9, 10].

A pilot test was conducted on 5% of the study participants in a different area. Six BSc midwives were trained for one day on data collection using the tool, and two MSc midwives were assigned to supervise the data collection. Supervisors checked the completeness of the data on a daily basis and addressed issues in a timely manner. An open-ended interview guide was developed in English and translated into Amharic for the qualitative data collection. The interview was conducted for an average duration of 30 min with each interviewee. The principal investigator conducted all the interviews. All interviews were audio recorded and field notes were taken with the help of an assistant. Sustained researcher engagement, iterative questioning, and maximum variation sampling techniques were used to maintain trustworthiness [31, 32].

Data analysis

The survey data were entered into Epidata version 4.6 and exported to STATA 17 for statistical analysis. Summary statistics (Proportion, Mean, SD, and Ranges) were used to report the descriptive summary of the data. A stepwise backward elimination logistic regression model was used to identify factors associated with the knowledge, attitude, and practice of midwives on GBP, and statistical significance was declared at a p-value of less than 0.05. Regarding the qualitative data, the interviews were transcribed verbatim and translated into English. The six-step thematic analysis was used for data analysis [33]. Accordingly, codes were developed inductively by the main researcher and shared with the co-authors. Confirmatory feedback was received from all authors on the developed codes, and slight modifications were made based on comments from one researcher after a thorough discussion. Then, the codes were categorised into themes, which were named and defined. Quotes were extracted and presented along with the summary of the themes to support the evidence.

The principal investigator (OY) was a midwife tutor and PhD student with more than 17 years of academic, research, and leadership experience. He is also familiar with the subject of DBP from the capacity building training and involved in the provision of training on DBP. However, he has minimal engagement in clinical practice setting. Other member of research team have PhD degree. Two of the members (HL & KE) were experienced in qualitative study and in the subject of DBP; and they are the one who introduced it in the form of capacity building training for midwives in the study area. The principal investigator had the capacity building training on DBP, and participated in different activities in the subject matter. I, the principal investigator, have a relativist ontological view which consider reality of DBP as relative to each midwife apart aspects that can be measured objectively. Therefore, the principal investigator took a constructivist research paradigm by which the reality needs to be interpreted. The development of code and interpretation would be affected by my experience. However, every coding and interpretation was presented for the research team and adequate feedback was taken to avoid bias.

Results

Quantitative findings

This study recruited a total of 473 participants: 252 (53.3%) female and 221 (46.7%) male midwives. The mean age ± SD of the participants was 29.43 ± 3.8 years, with a range between 22 and 48 years. Most midwives, 292 (91.72%), were between 26 and 30 years old. Nearly half, 251 (53.1%), of the midwives had a bachelor’s degree in midwifery. Most midwives, 453 (95.8%), served as clinical service providers, and 451 (95.3%) were permanent staff. More than half, 271 (57.3%), of the midwives worked in health centres, with the labour ward being the main working unit, 380 (80.3%) (Table 1).

Midwives’ awareness level of dynamic birth position

The mean cumulative correct score of knowledge was 5.33 ± 3.15; the minimum score was 0, and the maximum score was 12. About 213 (45%) of the midwives had good knowledge, with a mean score above or equal to the mean score. In identifying birth positions, side-lying was the most frequently identified dynamic birth position, 281 (59.4%); followed by squatting, 265 (56.0%); and kneeling, 238 (50.3%). However, the two supine positions, semi-sitting and lying on the back, were incorrectly identified as dynamic birth positions by 338 (71.5%) and 247 (52.2%) participants, respectively. Among the four comprehensive items on knowledge, 277 (58.56%) identified that dynamic birth position is a component of physiologic birth, and 262 (55.39%) participants identified that dynamic birth position increases the pelvic diameter (Table 2).

Source of information

Midwives reported that they learned about dynamic birth positions from different sources. A total of 194 (41%) midwives reported that they had learned at university or college, 97 (20.5%) learned through training, 41 (8.7%) from co-workers, and 14 (3%) from media. However, a significant number of midwives, 127 (26.8%), have never heard about dynamic birth positions.

Attitude of midwives towards dynamic birth position

The minimum cumulative score of attitudes was 18 and the maximum was 50; the mean attitude score was estimated to be 35.91. Accordingly, 234 (49.5%) of the midwives have a good attitude towards dynamic birth positions with a score equal to or above the mean score. In total, 256 (54.1%) of midwives strongly agree that they need to receive training, and 180 (38.1%) and 169 (35.7%) of midwives agree and strongly agree that using dynamic birth positions means that women are allowed to give birth in a position of their choice. More than 60% of midwives agree that dynamic birth positions enhance spontaneous vaginal delivery, reduce operative delivery, and shorten the second stage of labour. About two-thirds, 142 (30.0%) and 146 (30.9%) of the midwives strongly disagree and disagree, respectively, with the statement that dynamic birth positions do not improve fetal outcomes. About 116 (24.5%) and 58 (12.3%) of midwives agree and strongly agree that dynamic birth positions are difficult to implement (Table 3).

Practice of dynamic birth position

The most frequently used positions were lying on the back and semi-sitting positions, accounting for 82.25% and 81.8%, respectively. Standing on all fours was the least practised position. The practice of midwives regarding the six non-supine positions was minimal compared with the two supine positions (the semi-sitting and lying on the back).

Less than one in four midwives, 107 (22.6%), assisted women giving birth in three or more non-supine positions, while the remaining 366 (77.4%) assisted in two or fewer non-supine positions. Similarly, less than one in four midwives, 103 (21.8%), have never assisted a woman giving birth in a non-supine position.

Factors associated with the knowledge, attitude, and practice level of midwives on dynamic birth positions

Participants’ sex, age, education level, employment type, work experience, source of knowledge, facility type, and working units were included primarily in the backward elimination logistic regression methods.

Through stepwise elimination, education level and source of knowledge remained important predictors of knowledge. Accordingly, midwives who reported learning from training were 10.67 times more likely to have good knowledge compared with midwives who had never heard about it. Similarly, midwives who reported learning from school and co-workers were 3.12 and 4.13 times more likely to have good knowledge, respectively. Regarding the level of education, though it is not statistically significant, midwives with an MSc are 2.05 times more likely to have good knowledge (p-value = 0.065) (Table 4).

Theme 1: routine work

Midwives’ routine work included assessing labouring women, admitting them to the labour ward, following the labour, attending the labour, counselling the woman, providing postnatal care, and consulting and referring when the case is beyond their scope.

“As soon as a woman arrives, we check whether there is labour or not. She will be admitted with us, and then we will follow her every hour; if she enters the active stage, we check every 30 minutes; if she enters the second stage, we will check her every 15 minutes, and then if she delivers, we will provide postnatal care.” (Midwife 1).

“As soon as a woman arrives, we check whether there is labour or not. She will be admitted with us, and then we will follow her every hour; if she enters the active stage, we check every 30 minutes; if she enters the second stage, we will check her every 15 minutes, and then if she delivers, we will provide postnatal care.” (Midwife 1).

Midwives use the partograph as a tool while following labouring women. The follow-up includes taking vital signs, assessing labour progress, and assessing fetal condition.

“After admission, we assess the vital signs according to the partograph; we assess the fetal heartbeat and temperature. We do vaginal examinations every 4 hours; we may put them on an IV line if necessary. We do everything following the partograph.” (Midwife 2).

Theme 2: awareness

Awareness explores midwives’ understanding of dynamic birth positions, and it has three subthemes.

Subtheme 1: conceptualization

Midwives describe dynamic birth positions as not sticking to a single position, allowing a woman to assume a position of her choice and comfort, and giving birth outside of supine positions.

“I define it as not being fixed in one position. To keep moving, to keep changing positions as she wishes, to keep her in a position of her choice.” (Midwife 3).

“I define it as not being fixed in one position. To keep moving, to keep changing positions as she wishes, to keep her in a position of her choice.” (Midwife 3).

Dynamic birth positions are understood as positions that ensure safety and avoid any form of harm to the woman. Maternal comfort and choices are central to the concept of dynamic birth positions.

“Dynamic birth positions are based on the women’s preference and are comfortable for her. They are positions that allow the women to give birth without facing any psychological or physical harm and that ensure her comfort.” (Midwife 4).

“Dynamic birth positions are based on the women’s preference and are comfortable for her. They are positions that allow the women to give birth without facing any psychological or physical harm and that ensure her comfort.” (Midwife 4).

Subtheme 2: alternative positions

Midwives reported that continuous ambulation, being in the left lateral position, sitting with a birth seat, squatting, kneeling, and standing are alternative dynamic birth positions.

“Side lying, kneeling, and on all fours positions are better. We sometimes use them; the birth seat is very preferable and better. These are the positions I know.” (Midwife 5).

“Side lying, kneeling, and on all fours positions are better. We sometimes use them; the birth seat is very preferable and better. These are the positions I know.” (Midwife 5).

The presence of special birth support, such as a birth seat and rebozo, is reported to be supportive for the practice of dynamic birth positions like squatting. They also consider all fours, standing, and kneeling to be alternative positions.

“The main positions we are using include squatting, which we widely use here in our hospital since we have the magic chair (birth seat); side lying (left lateral); and the others are on all fours, standing, and kneeling positions. We also use rebozo.” (Midwife 3).

“The main positions we are using include squatting, which we widely use here in our hospital since we have the magic chair (birth seat); side lying (left lateral); and the others are on all fours, standing, and kneeling positions. We also use rebozo.” (Midwife 3).

Subtheme 3: benefit

Reduction in the episiotomy rate, reduction in tear occurrence, correction of malposition, shortening of labour duration, reduction in maternal pain, and improvement in maternal satisfaction and fetal conditions are reported as benefits of dynamic birth positions. Most midwives reported that the supine positions slow down labour progress.

“The episiotomy rate is decreased; the duration of the second stage is also shortened. Labour progress is good when we put them in the left lateral position; however, the contraction ceases when we take them to the couch and put them in the supine position. It may need further study; I have seen the difference that the second stage is very short when we attend them in the lateral position; I have witnessed it. Thirdly, I have not faced any complications so far; both the fetal and maternal outcomes were very good.” (Midwife 1).

It may need further study; I have seen the difference that the second stage is very short when we attend them in the lateral position; I have witnessed it. In addition to the decrease in contraction, the supine position causes vulvar dryness, which leads to an increased risk of tear and bleeding. As a dynamic solution, midwives delay taking women to the delivery couch until the head becomes visible after they enter the second stage of labour.

“There is a significant difference; vulvar dryness is common when we keep them in the lithotomy position, and this aggravates the risk of tear. The second is that it reduces the episiotomy rate.” (Midwife 3).

“There is a significant difference; vulvar dryness is common when we keep them in the lithotomy position, and this aggravates the risk of tear. The second is that it reduces the episiotomy rate.” (Midwife 3).

Theme 3: practice of dynamic birth positions

Midwives manage the first stage of labour more in dynamic birth positions, allowing free ambulation and using alternative birth positions. However, all the midwives reported that they manage the second stage of labour on the labour couch in lithotomy positions.

“They can stand; they can also squat if they have something to stand against. Some women bring a kind of stick to lean against and sometimes kneel down. However, the position we can use for the second stage is lithotomy. They can be in any position they wish except during the second stage.” (Midwife 6).

“They can stand; they can also squat if they have something to stand against. Some women bring a kind of stick to lean against and sometimes kneel down. However, the position we can use for the second stage is lithotomy. They can be in any position they wish except during the second stage.” (Midwife 6).

Most midwives reported that they have used the lateral position on some occasions. They use the lateral position when labour is precipitated. In addition, they prefer the lateral position for primiparous women since these women have a tested perineum (gave birth before), and the risk of tear is minimal. Some midwives use the squatting position with the birth seat.

“I remember around three cases managed in the lateral position. One of them was multiparous, and it was precipitate labour. She delivered while we were waiting for the head to engage; we were unable to take her to the couch. We attended her in the lateral position, holding one of her legs, and she had a tear. The other two were primiparous.” (Midwife 7).

Theme 4: barriers

Having clinical conditions like premature rupture of membrane or fetal distress, ruptures of the membrane, fear of tear, fear of accidents, risk of contamination, and fear of complications were stated as barriers for the practice of dynamic birth position. Facility related factors like shortage of staff, non-supportive setup, non-supportive couch, and the need to be in an awkward position to attend were among the barriers. Professional related factors like poor attitude, lack of decision-making power, lack of knowledge, and lack of skill were also mentioned as barriers to the practice of dynamic birth positions. Midwives reported that attending birth outside of the delivery couch causes a lot of pain and discomfort. They reported experiencing severe back pain when trying to attend a birth with the birth seat or in other birth positions.

“There are many challenges when we try to practice dynamic birth positions. The first is the environmental condition; the setup here does not invite practicing dynamic birth positions; it is very crowded and difficult to maintain privacy. The second problem is the attitude of the professionals; their attitude toward dynamic birth positions is poor. The third is a lack of skill. There may be some fear. People may fear that a tear or other complications may happen.” (Midwife 1).

The second problem is the attitude of the professionals; their attitude toward dynamic birth positions is poor. The third is a lack of skill. There may be some fear. People may fear that a tear or other complications may happen.” (Midwife 1).

The major decision-makers in the labour ward are the senior physicians, and midwives have limited decision-making power. This condition has limited midwives’ motivation to fully engage in the practice of dynamic birth positions.

“It is not convenient to support the perineum. That is why we fear attending in other positions; we are told that we will be accountable if a third or fourth-degree tear occurs. It is believed that you have supported when you attend on the couch. A third-degree tear may happen while you attend on the couch; however, you will not be blamed since it is presumed to have been supported. When it is on the birth chair, you are supposed not to have supported since it is not comfortable.” (Midwife 3).

Theme 5: facilitators

The need for pain relief and shortening of labour, maternal preference, good skill, good clinical judgment (fetal weight, pelvis diameter), maternal condition, having more staff, reducing the workload, and having a modified couch were mentioned as facilitators for dynamic birth positions. Midwives particularly reported that they learned about dynamic birth positions from the training provided by the MIDWIZE (an acronym for the project coined by combining the two terms Midwife and Wise) project [8].

“The training has contributed significantly. No one allowed them to move out of bed before the training. Currently, we have mobile CTG, so we call them after one hour for evaluation. They may get to bed by themselves when they feel tired. No one forces them to sleep currently; previously, we would have to get them to the bed once they were 4 cm.” (Midwife 8).

They may get to bed by themselves when they feel tired. No one forces them to sleep currently; previously, we would have to get them to the bed once they were 4 cm.” (Midwife 8).

Availing birth support that enables attending different positions is considered a facilitator for the practice of dynamic birth positions by most midwives.

“If there were comfortable couches to support lateral and kneeling positions. If it has to be on the floor, a higher mattress-like object. If such things are made accessible, it will be easy to try these positions. In addition, the number of staff has to be increased, and it is important.” (Midwife 9).

Theme 6: users’ requirements

What midwives consider to be components of an ideal setup to practice dynamic birth positions is seen under this theme. Accordingly, a modified hybrid couch that supports different birth positions with an improved workstation (height), a wide room, legal enforcement (guidelines and policy), good staff attitude, and an adequate number of staff were mentioned as requirements for the proper implementation of dynamic birth positions.

“The couch is fixed and not comfortable to manage. If the couches were modified to be manipulable; for example, some couches can serve as both a bed and a couch; if the couches were like that, and if they could be adjusted as you wish, you may not have problems practicing.” (Midwife 4).

“The couch is fixed and not comfortable to manage. If the couches were modified to be manipulable; for example, some couches can serve as both a bed and a couch; if the couches were like that, and if they could be adjusted as you wish, you may not have problems practicing.” (Midwife 4).

The use of this parallel mixed method enable to explain the findings of the survey with the qualitative data. The level of knowledge measured is further elaborated by the conceptualization of DBP by midwives. Attitude estimated quantitatively but also its role in making the practice was explored qualitatively.

‎The knowledge, attitude and practice were very much affected by capacity building training. The qualitative data explained how midwives come to know about DBP from the training, the MIDWIZE project specifically. Low practice level was explained by the factors stated under barriers while having capacity building training was seen as a positive predictor from the quantitative data. Potential solutions were explored under facilitators and users requirement. Generally the use of the mixed method help to see the different aspects of dynamic birth position, and enable us make a more complete view of the subject.

Discussion

This concurrent mixed-methods study assessed midwives’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to dynamic birth positions (DBPs), along with associated factors. The findings revealed that fewer than half of the participants demonstrated good knowledge, held favorable attitudes, or regularly practiced DBPs. The study identified the source of knowledge as a significant factor associated with knowledge levels. Additionally, both facility type and source of knowledge were significantly linked to attitudes toward DBPs, while gender, source of knowledge, and level of knowledge were significantly associated with the practice of DBPs.

About 45% of the midwives have good knowledge of dynamic birth positions. Side-lying, squatting, and kneeling are the most frequently identified dynamic birth positions. This is almost similar to a study in Nigeria [23]. However, it is lower compared to a study in Italy [24]. This may be due to the differences in the working environment and resources between developed and Sub-Saharan African countries.

Midwives describe dynamic birth positions as positions based on maternal choice and comfort; it is also described as not being in one position and giving birth in non-supine positions. This understanding aligns with the WHO labour care user’s guide and the hybrid concept analysis on the understanding of dynamic birth positions [5, 7].

Reducing labour duration, providing pain relief, and decreasing episiotomies and tears are among the benefits reported by midwives. This view matches the findings of several randomised controlled trials (RCTs) on different birthing positions in Turkey, Egypt, and Spain [19, 22, 34]. This may be due to the fact that non-supine positions open up the pelvis and facilitate labour [15].

Midwives who reported having learned about dynamic birth positions from training are more likely to have good knowledge. This might be due to the effect of in-service training in improving professionals’ knowledge [35]. Midwives who have learned from school and co-workers also demonstrate good knowledge. This might be due to the positive influence of peers on staff members [35].

A positive attitude is reported as a reason for good practice, and about 49.5% of midwives have a good attitude towards dynamic birth positions. About 54.1% of midwives strongly agree that midwives need training, and more than 60% of midwives agree that dynamic birth positions enhance spontaneous vaginal delivery. This aligns with findings established by several RCT studies in Turkey, Egypt, and Ethiopia [14, 19, 34].

Lying on the back or in the lithotomy position is the most frequently used position. The practice of dynamic birth positions is very limited, with only 22.65% of midwives reporting having ever used three or more non-supine positions during delivery. Side-lying was the most used dynamic birth position apart from lithotomy. This is similar to studies in Nigeria and Kenya but lower than a study in Italy [23, 24, 36]. This may be due to differences in the quality of care between developed and developing Sub-Saharan African countries. Midwives also reported in the in-depth interviews that side-lying is what they frequently use when they assist in labour outside of the couch. This may be due to the ease of using the side-lying position on the bed.

The use of the supine position on the delivery couch was reported to interfere with the frequency and intensity of uterine contractions, prolonging the duration of labour. This may be due to a reduction in blood flow to the uterus, which also reduces the amount of oxytocin that has to reach the organ and diminishes the Ferguson reflex or utero-pituitary reflex, which becomes very active when there is pressure on the cervix while women assume a non-supine position [37, 38]. Taking the dynamic solution to keep women on the bed until the baby’s head crowns and becomes visible might serve a purpose in such situations.

Non-supportive work environment, staff shortages, lack of decision-making power, the need to be in awkward positions to assist, poor attitudes, lack of knowledge, and lack of skill were among the reasons mentioned as barriers to the practice of dynamic birth positions. A study from South Africa reported similar barriers, including lack of knowledge, skill, and non-supportive setup [39]. A similar study from Australia reported that midwives’ practices are significantly affected by the condition of the delivery room [40]. This may be due to the similar requirements for providing care globally. Hence, modifying the delivery couch and the setup, providing training, increasing staff numbers, and legal enforcement with guidelines and policies are reported as necessary to enhance the practice of dynamic birth positions.

One of the key strengths of this study is the development of a new survey tool to assess midwives’ knowledge and attitudes toward dynamic birth positions, guided by the modified Delphi method. The study’s trustworthiness was further enhanced through prolonged researcher engagement as the principal researcher work all the way from interview guide development, conducting the interview, transcribing, translating to coding and writing the final report; iterative questioning was used by adjusting the questions based on the responses obtained, and maximum variation sampling was used by recruiting midwives from maintaining the gender balance, work experience and types of health facility, which strengthened the credibility and depth of the qualitative findings [31, 32]. The use of a mixed-methods design allowed for a comprehensive exploration of both measurable trends and nuanced insights. Additionally, conducting a census of all midwives in the study area improved the representativeness of the sample and increased the statistical power of the findings.

However, the study did not include the perspectives of other key stakeholders such as physicians, nurses, or facility administrators, which may have limited the understanding of broader institutional or interdisciplinary factors affecting the use of dynamic birth positions. Midwives in this study were from health facilities in 24 districts and two city administration; and the representativeness may be limited. Therefore, the generalizability and the interpretation needs to take this fact in consideration. Additionally, the birth positions investigated in this study were only those which can be used in the study setting, and we would like to inform our reader the list might be different for a resourceful work environment.

Conclusion

The Knowledge and practice of dynamic birth positions among midwives were very low. Training was found to be the common predictor for midwives’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices. The knowledge level was a key determinant of practice.

Pain relief, shortened labor duration, reduced episiotomy and perineal trauma, improved maternal and fetal outcomes, and increased maternal satisfaction were reported to be benefits of dynamic birth positions. However, lack of supportive infrastructure, staffing shortages, limited decision-making autonomy, insufficient knowledge and skills, and unfavorable attitudes among midwives identified as barriers for the practice.

Therefore, it is commendable to expand training opportunities, encourage peer learning, and develop clear guidelines supported by legal and institutional frameworks. In addition, modifying delivery rooms including redesigning birth couches and increasing the workforce could enhance the uptake and consistent practice of dynamic birth positions in health facilities.
