Robust Chiral High‐Q Response Driven by Quasi‐Bound Flatband in the Continuum
Authors: Lei Huang, Weiqing Wang, Han Shen, Jing Chen, Shifeng Li, Biao Liu, Fuhuan Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChiral bound states in the continuum (BICs) with infinite radiation quality factor (Q‐factor) and topological robustness have provided a new paradigm for enhancing chiral light–matter interaction. However, the strong momentum‐space dispersion leads to a rapid collapse of the Q‐factor when the resonance deviates from the ideal BICs condition, severely limiting its practical applications. Here, by strategically engineering the interaction among four counterpropagating folded guided modes, we theoretically propose and numerically realize the chiral flatband quasi‐BICs. The pronounced chiroptical response arises from the destructive interference of two antiphase oscillating intrinsic modes, whereas the flatband characteristic originates from the interactions among opposite curvature hybrid guided modes. Compared with conventional BICs featuring strongly dispersive bands, our dispersionless BICs provide stronger light field confinement, enabling highly efficient third‐harmonic generation (conversion efficiency up to 2.3 × 10−3 under a pump intensity of 1 MW/cm−2). Our work establishes a new benchmark for miniaturized nonlinear chiral sources and may pave the way for the development of novel chiral optical device.Keywords: chiral bound states in the continuum, guide mode, light‐matter interaction, photonics flatband

Introduction

Chirality, arising from the breaking of mirror or rotational symmetry, is a basic property in nature with significant implications across multiple scientific and technological fields [1, 2]. In optics, chiral phenomena originate from the selective interaction of a chiral object with the spin angular momentum of photons and are quantitatively characterized by circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy [3, 4]. In general, the CD response in natural materials is weak due to the scale mismatch between chiral molecules and optical wavelengths [5]. Artificially engineered chiral metasurfaces have been developed to enhance these weak chiroptical signals. However, their practical application in chiral optics remains limited by significant ohmic and scattering losses within supported optical modes, which typically result in a low‐quality factor (Q‐factor) [6].

Bound states in the continuum (BICs) are nonradiative eigenmodes embedded inside the radiation continuum, which emerges as Bloch resonances in periodic lattice structure, and have been demonstrated as a revolutionary solution for the significant problems of chiral photonics [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Despite possessing an infinite radiation lifetime, the applications of BICs remain limited due to their intrinsic angle‐dependent dispersion, which comes with a rapid collapse of the Q‐factor that is unavoidable during fabrication and measurement processes [12]. Merging multiple BICs in momentum space to form super‐BICs offers a feasible approach to alter the scaling rule and boost Q‐factors as coupling among multiple BICs around a single BIC effectively reduces radiation and scattering losses [13, 14]. However, to achieve further control over light–matter interaction and enhance Q‐factors, novel physical mechanisms still need to be explored in BICs research. Engineering the energy‐momentum dispersion of photonic structures is at the heart of contemporary optics research. Among these, the weakly dispersive or even dispersionless (flat) bands have garnered significant attention because their vanishing group velocity and high density of states (DOS) can significantly enhance light–matter interactions and reduce radiation or scattering loss, offering great potential for applications in nonlinear optics and laser [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Recently, moiré superlattice and disorder‐induced band‐folding approaches have been proposed to achieve flatband BICs, but their experimental fabrication remains challenging [20, 21], and the associated chiral or nonlinear properties, as well as the underlying mechanisms, have not yet been thoroughly investigated.

Here, by leveraging the interaction among four counterpropagating folded guided modes, we achieve the nondispersive high‐Q chiral quasi‐BICs over a wide incident angular range of ± 5° (Q‐factor exceeding 20,000 and circular dichroism > 0.6). The strong chiral response is demonstrated to arise from the intrinsic destructive interference of two antiphase oscillating polarization modes. While the flatband is revealed to originate from the interactions among opposite curvature hybrid guided modes. Furthermore, leveraging these unique field confinement characteristics of the flatband BICs, we demonstrate highly efficient chiral third‐harmonic generation (THG) with conversion efficiencies up to 2.3 × 10−3 under a pump intensity of 1 MW/cm−2. This work opens a new route toward practical chiral nanophotonic devices, with promising applications in chiral lasing and advanced nonlinear polarization imaging.

Results and Discussion

Figure 1a depicts the schematic diagram of our designed flatband chiral metasurface, with its detailed geometry parameters illustrated in the inset (see more information in caption). A chiral cross metasurface with C 2 rotational symmetry but no in‐plane mirror symmetry is constructed onto a SiO2 substrate. The calculated band structure and Q‐factor in k‐space are presented in Figure 1b,c. Three eigenmodes with distinct properties are identified within the investigated energy range. Both modes 1 and 2 exhibit divergently high Q‐factors at the Γ‐point, confirming their nature as symmetry‐protected BICs (SP‐BICs). In contrast, the Q‐factor of mode 3 remains nearly constant with increasing in‐plane wavevector (k x), identifying it as a guided mode. Besides, compared with two emerged BICs modes, mode 2 exhibits a higher Q‐factor with slower decay rate than mode 1, which indicates they have intrinsically distinct radiation decay nature.

The far‐field eigenpolarization maps with the Q‐factor distribution for mode 2 are given in the inset of Figure 1c. Both an ultrahigh Q‐factor and a polarization singularity (V‐point) emerge at the Γ point, further confirming that mode 2 is an SP‐BIC. Additionally, the chiral property of mode 2 is demonstrated by the far‐field eigenpolarization map in Figure S1 (Supporting Information S1: Note 1), where breaking the C 2 symmetry causes the V point to split into two C points away from the Γ point, giving rise to a chiral quasi‐BIC with nonvanishing helicity. The simulated transmission spectra for different polarization components T ij under LCP and RCP normal illumination for mode 2 at α = 10 nm are shown in Figure 1d. The subscripts “i” and “j ”in T ij represent the polarization states of the transmitted and incident light, respectively. An ultranarrow band chiral resonance with transmittance peak T rl = 0.95 and transmittance dip T rr = T lr = T ll ≈ 0 are observed at the wavelength of 1022 nm, which demonstrates the chiral quasi‐BICs characteristics. Besides, the emerged chiral quasi‐BICs possess an ultrahigh Q‐factor of ∼20,000 with a CD peak of 0.91 under the large asymmetry parameter (α = 10 nm), derived from Q = λ 0/Δλ and CD=Trr+Tlr−Tll+TrlTrr+Tlr+Tll+Trl (λ 0 is the resonance wavelength, Δλ is the full width at half maximum), which confirms the intrinsically low radiation decay nature of the quasi‐BICs.

To reveal the underlying physics origins of the high‐Q response, the zoomed‐angle‐resolved LCP and RCP transmission and CD spectra are simulated in Figure 2a–f. Here, for mode 2, a flatband dispersion with a missing point at the normal direction (Γ point) supported by the SP‐BICs is further demonstrated in the transmission or CD as shown in Figure 2d–f, and comparing with the parabolic‐like energy band presented in mode 1 (shown in Figure 2a–c), the dispersionless behavior constitutes the physical basis for the high‐Q response resulting from the reduced radiation and scattering loss. To clearly explain these reasons, we calculate and compare the magnetic field distributions of mode 1 and mode 2 at their respective resonance wavelengths with the same α = 10 nm as shown in Figure 2i,j, respectively. It is clearly seen that mode 1 is an electric dipolar resonance, which possesses a weak optical field localization, thus resulting in the strong scattering and radiation loss at the edge of configuration. Although mode 2 is a typically magnetic dipolar mode, exhibiting a strong optical field localization within the configuration due to the nearly zero group velocity and infinitely large effective mass, and thus results in the low radiation and scattering loss at the edge of configuration. Obviously, in contrast to strong dispersion for mode 1, the flatband BICs exhibits a much narrower linewidth and higher Q‐factor. The red‐shifted guided modes along the y‐direction modulate the negative‐curvature state at off‐Γ points and ultimately hybridize with the positive‐curvature state at Γ points, leading to the formation of flatband [24, 25]. Figure 4a shows the calculated results of mode volume and field enhancement factor for the dispersive and nondispersive BICs. The effective mode volume V mode is obtained by integrating the energy density W over the space on cross structures, and normalizing it to the maximum value of W at each wavelength: Vmode(λ)=∫W(r,λ)d3rmax[W(r,λ)] [28]. Clearly, the nondispersive BICs exhibit stronger localized field and smaller mode volume, which is expected to possess great potential for chiral nonlinear generation and conversion, since the nonlinear conversion efficiency scales with V mode according to the equation QVmode2·Ipump2, where I pump is pump intensity [29]. Figure 4b presents the simulated THG spectra for the two BIC modes. As expected from theory predictions, the THG signal from the nondispersive BICs is pronouncedly stronger than that from the conventional dispersive BICs, and the nonlinear conversion efficiency is evaluated to be 2.3 × 10−3 under a pump intensity of 1 MW/cm2, based on the equation: ηTHG=PTHGPFW (where P THG and P FW are the radiated power of the third harmonic wave and fundamental wave, respectively). It should be noted that, for practical experimental measurements, the actual conversion efficiency is lower than the calculated value due to material absorption, fabrication imperfections, and thermal effects. The log‐log plot of emission power as a function of pump power is further given in Figure 4c. Linear fitting yields slopes close to 3 for both modes, confirming the cubic power scaling expected for THG. Conclusion

In summary, by tuning the interaction between four counterpropagating guided modes in a chiral measurface, we theoretically propose and numerically demonstrate nondispersive chiral BICs. In contrast to conventional BICs, we verify this nondispersive nature can sustain an effective light‐field confinement, enabling a robust chiral resonance with a high Q‐factor (> 20,000) and efficient third‐harmonic generation (conversion efficiency up to 2.3 × 10−3 under a pump intensity of 1 MW/cm−2) across a wide incident angular range. Our findings establish a new paradigm for developing high‐performance, miniaturized chiral, and nonlinear photonic devices with wide‐angle operation.
