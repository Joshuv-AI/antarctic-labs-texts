Psychometric validation of a knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) scale for breast cancer screening among Chinese women
Authors: Yang Liao, Suhaily Mohd Hairon, Najib Majdi Yaacob, Tengku Alina Tengku Ismail, Li Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer screening participation remains suboptimal in China, partly due to psychological and behavioral barriers. A validated instrument is needed to assess knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) related to screening behaviors among Chinese women.MethodsA cross-sectional study with a multistage sampling approach was conducted in Guizhou Province, China. A total of 611 women aged 18–70 participated, with samples allocated for exploratory factor analysis (EFA: n = 314) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA: n = 297). The KAP questionnaire, adapted from existing instruments and culturally tailored, included 24 knowledge items, 12 attitude items, and 8 practice items. Psychometric evaluation involved EFA, CFA, reliability testing, and measurement invariance (MI) analysis across residential settings.ResultsEFA revealed a five-factor structure encompassing three domains: knowledge (symptoms and screening/treatment), attitude (screening self-efficacy), and practice (breast self-examination and mammography utilization). CFA confirmed good model fit (CFI = 0.978; TLI = 0.976; RMSEA = 0.045 [90% CI: 0.038–0.051]; SRMR = 0.082). Internal consistency, composite reliability, and convergent validity were acceptable across all subscales (ω = 0.703–0.919; CR ≥ 0.70; AVE ≥ 0.50). Measurement invariance was supported across residential groups.ConclusionsThe validated KAP scale demonstrates strong psychometric properties and is suitable for assessing breast cancer screening behaviors in Chinese women. It provides a reliable tool for both research and practice, facilitating the identification of women at risk of low screening participation and informing tailored intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24775-w.

Introduction

Globally, breast cancer (BC) remains a major public health concern and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women [1]. Despite the well-established benefits of early detection through screening programs, participation rates remain suboptimal, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as China [2]. In China, BC incidence has been steadily increasing, yet screening participation remains inconsistent across different regions [3]. According to national health statistics, screening rates in urban areas are significantly higher than in rural regions, where limited access to healthcare facilities and lower health literacy contribute to reduced participation [4]. Unlike in high-income countries with structured screening programs, breast cancer screening (BCS) in China remains largely opportunistic, with substantial variations in accessibility, affordability, and awareness [5].

Structural barriers such as healthcare accessibility, financial constraints, and lack of organized screening programs have been studied extensively. Among them individual psychological and behavior factors are becoming more recognized as they key influences on screening decision [6]. Such factor is breast cancer screening hesitancy (BCSH). BCSH is determined by reluctancy, delay, and avoidance of screening despite its availability [5]. BCSH is influenced by factors such as low health literacy, fear of cancer diagnosis, mistrust in healthcare providers, and perceived screening risks [7]. Despite this, research into these psychological determinants remain relatively scarce, especially within China [2].

Within the context of BSC, the Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) framework is widely used as the theoretical basis for understanding health behaviors and their psychological determinants [8]. The KAP framework comprises three sections: Knowledge refers to awareness and understanding of breast cancer risk factors, symptoms, and available screening methods; Attitudes encompass perceptions, beliefs, self-efficacy, and trust towards BCS procedures and outcomes; Practices involve engagement in screening behaviors such as breast self-examinations (BSE) and mammography [3, 9].

In previous studies, it is established that inadequate knowledge and negative attitudes might significantly reduce screening participation. This is established by utilizing descriptive statistics or regression analyses on KAP factors, and screening behaviors [10]. This highlights the utility of the KAP framework in identifying modifiable psychological factors that influences BCS behaviors [11]. However, despite widespread application of KAP in BCS research, few studies have conducted rigorous psychometric validations of KAP instruments, particularly among Chinese populations [4]. Validation should be done by systematic evaluations of the structural validity, reliability, and cross-group comparability (measurement invariance) of these instruments [11]. Without thorough validation, the reliability and generalizability of KAP measures across different cultural contexts will be limiting [12].

In China, BCS programs lack standardization, and public awareness campaigns are not as widespread as in Western countries [3]. Furthermore, individual factors such as financial constraints, fear of screening results, and mistrust in medical institutions contribute even more to hesitancy in screening participation [13]. Due to the cultural differences affecting these factors KAP instrument validation is necessary to accurately measure how these factors influence the screening participation [14, 15].

This study seeks to validate KAP measurement instrument for BCS among Chinese women. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted to identify the underlying factor structure, followed by Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) to assess the structural validity and reliability of the instrument. Multi-group CFA was employed to examine measurement invariance across subgroups (urban vs. rural). By creating a validated instrument, a more standardized and culturally relevant tool will be available for assessing psychological and behavioral determinants of breast cancer screening.

Method

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study using a multistage sampling approach was conducted in Guizhou Province, China. Participants were recruited from both the provincial capital and other cities across the province to capture demographic variation, including urban–rural diversity, socioeconomic differences, and varying levels of accessibility to BCS services. In each wave of data collection, cities were selected, followed by random selection of two districts within each city, and then random sampling of participants from those districts.

Participants were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) Women aged 18 to 70 years; (2) Residents of Guizhou Province for at least one year; (3) Chinese citizens; (4) Able and willing to provide informed consent.

A total of 611 participants were recruited across two independent samples. After excluding ineligible and incomplete responses, 314 participants were included in the dataset for EFA, and 297 participants in the dataset for CFA.

The KAP questionnaire comprised 44 items. Psychometric guidelines recommend a minimum participant-to-item ratio of 5:1 for factor analysis [16], suggesting that at least 220 participants were required. In addition, a sample size of approximately 300 has been considered as adequate for stable factor solutions [16]. Both the EFA and CFA datasets met or were very close to these recommended thresholds, supporting the adequacy of the sample sizes [17].

Instrument development and measures

The questionnaire items used in the present study were adapted from the instrument developed [18], which investigated determinants of barriers to breast self-examination among Eastern Chinese women. Permission to use and adapt the original items was obtained from the corresponding author. To ensure linguistic and conceptual equivalence, the questionnaire underwent a translation-back translation process following World Health Organization guidelines for cross-cultural adaptation of survey instruments [19]. The English version was first translated into Chinese, and an independent bilingual expert then back-translated it into English to ensure consistency with the original meaning.

Following the translation process, content validity was assessed by a panel of five experts in public health and oncology, who evaluated the clarity, cultural relevance, and appropriateness of each item. Minor modifications were made based on their recommendations to enhance contextual relevance.

Additionally, face validity was tested among 10 native Chinese speakers representative of the target population. Participants completed the questionnaire and provided qualitative feedback regarding item clarity, comprehensibility, and cultural appropriateness. Minor revisions were made accordingly to improve readability and ensure respondent understanding. The English version of the final adapted questionnaire is available in Supplementary File 1.

The questionnaire consisted of three subscales: (1) Knowledge (24 items): Assessed awareness of breast cancer risk factors, symptoms, and screening methods. Responses were dichotomous (Yes/No/Not Sure) and coded as 1 = Correct, 0 = False. Negatively worded items were reverse-scored to maintain consistency. (2) Attitudes (12 items): Measured perceptions, beliefs, and willingness to undergo breast cancer screening using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree). Negatively worded items were reverse-scored for consistency. (3) Practices (8 items): Evaluated actual screening behaviors, including mammography, clinical breast exams, and self-breast exams. Responses were dichotomous (Yes/No/Not Sure) and coded as 1 = Good, 0 = Poor. Negatively framed items were reverse-coded for consistency.

The final version of the questionnaire was subjected to EFA to examine its underlying structure, followed by CFA to validate its structural validity within the target population.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected from in two waves using an online self-administered questionnaire. The first wave was conducted from August to October 2024, and the second wave from February to May 2025. Participants were recruited through community health centers and social media platforms to ensure broad outreach. The questionnaire was hosted on a secure survey platform, and participants could access it via a link or QR code.

Before starting the survey, participants were provided with an informed consent form explaining the purpose of the study, confidentiality measures, and their right to withdraw at any time. Only those who consented could proceed with the questionnaire. To minimize social desirability bias, the survey was anonymous, and no personally identifiable information was collected.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics and responses to KAP questionnaire. For continuous variables, means and standard deviations were calculated, while categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. EFA, CFA, and Measurement Invariance (MI) Analysis were conducted using R version 4.4.2 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) within RStudio (Posit, Boston, MA, USA), utilizing the lavaan package [20] and semTools packages to assess the factor structure, validity, and cross-group comparability of the KAP model. A conceptual path diagram representing the final CFA model was manually constructed using IBM SPSS Amos 28.0 (Analysis of Moment Structures) to illustrate the relationships between latent variables and their corresponding observed indicators.

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

EFA was conducted to examine the dimensional structure of the KAP questionnaire. EFA was performed separately for each subscale to ensure an accurate factor structure.

Given that the Knowledge and Practices subscales contained dichotomous response options, tetrachoric correlation matrices were used for factor extraction. In contrast, the Attitudes subscale, which was measured using a Likert scale, was analyzed using Pearson correlation-based factor extraction. Factor extraction was conducted using the minimum residual (minres) estimation method with oblimin rotation, allowing for factor correlations.

The suitability of the dataset for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. The KMO measure was used to evaluate sampling adequacy, while Bartlett’s test was conducted to determine whether the correlation matrix was appropriate for factor analysis [21]. Factors were extracted based on multiple criteria, including eigenvalues greater than 1, visual inspection of the scree plot, and a minimum factor loading cut-off of 0.40 for item retention [22]. In addition, Model fit was evaluated using the Root Mean Square Residual (RMSR), with values below 0.08 indicating an acceptable fit [23].

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

CFA was performed to validate the proposed factor structure following EFA. Given that the KAP questionnaire is a well-established instrument, EFA was conducted as a preliminary check to confirm the suitability of its factor structure within the study population rather than to explore new factor structures. CFA was then conducted on an independent dataset collected in the second wave, using the weighted least squares mean-variance (WLSMV) estimator to accommodate categorical and ordinal data.

Model fit was evaluated using several fit indices. The recommended cutoff values for model fit indices are: Comparative Fit Index (CFI) ≥ 0.95, a Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) ≥ 0.95, a Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.06, and a Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) ≤ 0.08 [24].

Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega, with values of 0.70 or higher considered acceptable [25]. Convergent validity was evaluated using composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE), with thresholds of ≥ 0.70 for CR and ≥ 0.50 for AVE [25]. The validated factor structure provided a robust measurement framework for assessing KAP related to breast cancer screening.

Measurement invariance analysis (MI)

To ensure the validity and cross-group comparability of the KAP model, MI was assessed across residential settings using MG-CFA. Because the number of suburban participants was very small (n = 6), they were combined with urban participants, resulting in two groups: Urban (n = 100) and Rural (n = 197).

MI was tested sequentially at four levels: configural, metric, scalar, and strict [26]. Configural invariance evaluated whether the same factor structure was consistent across groups, metric invariance tested the equivalence of factor loadings, scalar invariance assessed the equality of item intercepts, and strict invariance examined whether item residual variances were equivalent [27]. Invariance was determined based on changes in model fit indices, with thresholds of ΔCFI ≤ 0.01, ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015, and ΔSRMR ≤ 0.03 [26]. If full scalar invariance could not be established, partial invariance was examined by relaxing constraints on non-invariant items while maintaining equality constraints for the remaining parameters [27].

Result

The majority resided in urban areas (70.4%), held at least a bachelor’s degree (63.7%), and were employed (54.8%). Most participants were married (56.4%), and 89.5% reported no chronic diseases. Regarding breast cancer screening behaviors, 30.9% reported performing breast self-examinations (BSE) regularly, while 53.8% had never undergone a mammogram. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

EFA was performed separately for each subscale (Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices). The overall Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.85, indicating strong sampling adequacy, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 6115.094, p < 0.001), confirming data suitability for factor analysis. The subscale-specific KMO values were all acceptable (Knowledge = 0.91, Attitudes = 0.80, Practices = 0.61), confirming the suitability of the data for factor analysis [28].

The EFA identified a five-factor structure: [1] K1: Symptoms Knowledge (10 items), which reflects recognition of common symptoms such as lumps and nipple retraction; [2] K2: Screening and Treatment Knowledge (6 items), which reflects awareness of screening methods such as mammography and treatment modalities; [3] A: Screening Self-Efficacy (6 items), which reflects confidence in performing self-examinations and seeking clinical screenings; [4] P1: Breast Self-Examination (4 items), which reflects past and intended self-examination practices; and [5] P2: Mammography Utilization (4 items), which reflects mammography screening behaviors and future screening plans, with one negatively worded self-examination item (Q41) also loading on this factor, likely due to method effects rather than conceptual overlap. Notably, Symptoms Knowledge (K1) and Screening and Treatment Knowledge (K2) exhibited a strong correlation (r = 0.732), suggesting potential conceptual overlap, which was further examined in CFA [28]. Standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.414 to 0.973 (Table 3).

Discussion

Inter-Factor correlation analysis

High inter-factor correlation between K1 and K2 subscales

The correlation between Symptoms Knowledge (K1) and Screening & Treatment Knowledge (K2) was notably high (EFA: r = 0.732; CFA: r = 0.758), initially suggesting potential conceptual overlap. However, Conceptual redundancy is typically assumed when factor correlations exceed 0.85 [30], the observed correlation in this study remains below that threshold. Moreover, exploratory attempts to merge these factors into a single dimension resulted in significantly poorer model fit (CFI and RMSEA substantially worsened), clearly indicating each factor represents distinct cognitive domains. Specifically, K1 emphasizes symptom recognition, whereas K2 addresses knowledge regarding screening and treatment options. Maintaining these as separate constructs therefore enhances both theoretical precision and the practical applicability of the instrument.

Associations between breast self-examination (P1) and other constructs

Breast Self-Examination (P1) showed a moderate correlation with Screening Self-Efficacy (A) (r = 0.623), whereas its associations with Symptoms Knowledge (K1) (r = 0.339) and Screening & Treatment Knowledge (K2) (r = 0.169) were relatively weaker. This pattern suggests that self-efficacy may play a more central role in motivating self-examination behaviors, as women with greater perceived capability are more likely to engage in such practices. In contrast, the weaker correlations with knowledge imply that knowledge alone may be insufficient to ensure consistent practice. This interpretation is consistent with prior evidence indicating that adequate knowledge does not necessarily translate into health behaviors [31], and that self-efficacy has frequently emerged as a critical determinant of breast self-examination within health belief frameworks [32]. Accordingly, the results for the P1 subscale support its construct validity while also highlighting the importance of interventions that strengthen self-efficacy alongside knowledge promotion.

Independence of the mammography utilization (P2) subscale

Mammography Utilization (P2) showed negligible or even negative correlations with all other constructs (r range − 0.121 to 0.032). This finding suggests that prior mammography behaviors are relatively weakly related to knowledge, attitudes, or self-efficacy, and are instead more strongly shaped by external contextual factors. For example, previous research has indicated that the direct associations between mammography uptake and knowledge or attitudes are weak, whereas structural barriers such as financial burden, insurance status, and healthcare accessibility exert a more substantial influence [33]. Rather than being primarily determined by cognitive or motivational factors, mammography utilization appears to depend more heavily on structural conditions, including the availability of screening facilities, physician recommendation, and economic or logistical constraints [5, 33]. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of addressing systemic barriers in efforts to increase screening uptake, while also underscoring the unique role of P2 within the overall measurement framework. This interpretation is further consistent with health behavior theories, which emphasize that resource-dependent health practices are often constrained by external accessibility and structural conditions rather than being determined solely by knowledge or attitudes [34].

Measurement issues in the practices subscale

The Practices (P) subscale exhibited several measurement challenges, including the highest RMSR (0.122) among all subscales, indicating greater residual variance. This increased variance likely stems from inherent variability in self-reported screening behaviors, influenced by external factors such as healthcare accessibility, socioeconomic conditions, and individual screening intentions [35]. Despite this elevated RMSR, CFA model fit indices (CFI = 0.978, RMSEA = 0.045) demonstrated acceptable overall fit, affirming the subscale’s structural robustness and practical utility [36].

In terms of internal consistency, Cronbach’s α was 0.644 for Breast Self-Examination (P1) and 0.658 for Mammography Utilization (P2). These relatively modest values may be attributable to item heterogeneity and the limited number of items, both of which commonly lead to underestimation of reliability in small subscales. Complementary indices, however, indicated more favorable psychometric properties: McDonald’s ω was 0.799 for P1 and 0.703 for P2, composite reliability (CR) values were 0.861 and 0.722, respectively, and average variance extracted (AVE) exceeded the 0.60 threshold for both subscales (P1 = 0.764; P2 = 0.601). Furthermore, CFA confirmed the structural validity of both subscales, with all factor loadings ≥ 0.653. Taken together, while Cronbach’s α may underestimate internal consistency, the overall psychometric evidence still supports the reliability and construct validity of the Practices subscale [29].

Beyond these subscale-specific issues, the relatively low Cronbach’s α coefficients observed for both P1 and P2 may reflect item design characteristics. On the one hand, several items adopted compound wording, simultaneously addressing both past behaviors and future intentions within a single statement, which could confuse respondents and lower inter-item correlations. For instance, items Q39/40 (“I have never done breast self-examination, but I intend to do it”) exemplify such compound wording. On the other hand, negatively or compound-negative worded items, such as Q41 (“I have been doing BSE, but I am not going to do it again”) and Q44 (“I had a mammogram before, but I don’t plan to repeat it”), may have further undermined internal consistency by increasing respondent confusion and reducing inter-item correlations. Together with the limited number of items, these design characteristics likely explain the internal consistency limitations.

Future revisions of the instrument may consider the following strategies: first, decompose compound items into single-dimension statements that assess behaviors and intentions separately; second, add more positively worded intention items to balance the influence of negatively worded ones; and third, modestly expand the number of items to enhance construct coverage of both behaviors and intentions. Implementing these improvements may further strengthen the internal consistency and reliability of the Practices subscale.

Impact of dichotomous and likert scale items on model fit

The CFA indicated overall acceptable model fit (χ²/df = 1.60, CFI = 0.978, TLI = 0.976, RMSEA = 0.045 [90% CI: 0.038–0.051]), though the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR = 0.082) slightly exceeded the conventional threshold of 0.08. This minor deviation likely arose from combining dichotomous response items (Knowledge and Practices), which are known to increase SRMR in CFA models [37], particularly when mixed with Likert-scale items (Attitudes). This combination may inflate residual variances and subsequently impact SRMR values. Although the WLSMV estimator was employed specifically to mitigate such effects, minor residual correlations inevitably persisted. Nonetheless, the model demonstrated strong interpretability and overall structural validity, with opportunities for refinement in future studies.

Measurement invariance and applicability across residential settings

Measurement invariance across residential settings (urban [including suburban] vs. rural) was successfully established, indicating that the KAP scale operates equivalently across diverse populations. This ensures that observed variations in screening attitudes and behaviors reflect genuine differences rather than measurement artifacts. Given the pronounced disparities in healthcare accessibility and cultural norms across geographic and demographic groups, particularly in the Chinese urban and rural context, confirming invariance significantly strengthens the generalizability and practical utility of the KAP scale in designing targeted, evidence-based public health interventions and screening campaigns [38]. In particular, the established invariance supports its application in identifying women with insufficient knowledge or negative attitudes in community and rural healthcare contexts, as well as in assessing the effectiveness of tailored interventions within these populations [39–41].Based on subscale scores, targeted educational strategies can be developed; for example, enhancing symptom knowledge among women with low knowledge scores and addressing fear and misconceptions among those with low attitude scores [42].

Limitations and future directions

This study still has several limitations, although the robustness of the KAP scale has been confirmed. First, validation is needed to explore other sociodemographic groups, such as those with different educational levels and access to healthcare. Second, although measurement invariance was successfully established, direct examination of external influences such as healthcare policies and cultural norms on screening behaviors remains necessary. Third, a further limitation concerns the relatively modest internal consistency of the Practices subscales. This issue may be partly attributable to item design characteristics, such as compound wording that simultaneously addresses both behaviors and intentions, the inclusion of negatively phrased items, and the limited number of items in each subscale. Future revisions should aim to refine item wording, balance negative and positive phrasing, and modestly expand the number of items to strengthen the reliability of the instrument.

Future research should not only extend the validation to other demographic factors and incorporate longitudinal designs to track changes in screening behaviors [42, 43], but also examine how knowledge, attitudes, and practices evolve with policy shifts, public health campaigns, and personal experiences [3]. Additionally, integrating the KAP scale into cohort studies may reveal critical determinants for sustained screening adherence, providing essential guidance for dynamic, population-level interventions [44, 45]. Finally, future studies may explore how the validated KAP dimensions relate to constructs such as breast cancer screening hesitancy (BCSH) [5]. Understanding how these components interact with hesitancy could support the development of integrated models and strengthen the predictive value of behavioral frameworks in cancer screening research.

Conclusion

This study creates a Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) scale tailored for assessing breast cancer screening among Chinese women that is validated and psychometrically robust. The five-factor structure shows strong reliability, convergent validity, and measurement invariance, proving its effectiveness in accurately measuring factors for screening-related behaviors across diverse populations.

The cross-population measurement invariance highlights the KAP scale’s utility for future public health research targeted at specific demographics. The information gathered from this research could help create better specialized, and evidence-based strategies for breast cancer screening.

The validated KAP scale with its robust psychometric properties is a valuable assessment tool for both epidemiological studies and practical health promotion interventions. Future research should further analyze the longitudinal stability, validate adaptations in varied cultural and socioeconomic contexts, and explore its integration with digital health platforms to maximize outreach and participation in breast cancer screening initiatives.
